Early detection of chronic kidney disease based on a SURD-enhanced machine learning model
Authors: Ningning Xue, Tiantian Bai, Xianjie Jia, Xing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major global health burden, and early, reliable risk prediction remains clinically challenging. This study proposes a CKD prediction framework that integrates machine learning with Synergy–Unique–Redundant Decomposition (SURD) from causal information theory to enhance both predictive performance and interpretability. Ten classification models were developed using the UCI-CKD dataset (n = 400). Missing values were handled using multiple imputation via chained equations, and class imbalance was addressed with the synthetic minority oversampling technique. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To rigorously assess generalizability and mitigate concerns regarding overfitting, extensive external validation was conducted using a large-scale real-world electronic health record cohort from the MIMIC-IV database (n = 27,834). While several models achieved near-perfect performance on the internal dataset, the Random Forest model demonstrated superior generalization in the external cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.990 (95% CI 0.989–0.991), compared with an AUC of AUC: 0.914 (95% CI0.912–0.916) for the baseline Decision Tree. SURD-based causal decomposition and feature importance analyses consistently identified clinically established predictors, including serum creatinine and hemoglobin. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition characterized by high prevalence and mortality1. Due to its insidious onset, early-stage CKD often lacks obvious clinical symptoms, leading to delayed diagnoses and eventual progression to end-stage renal disease2. While the standard diagnostic criterion involves an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m23, early identification through routine blood and urine tests remains a more practical screening approach for general populations4,5.

While machine learning has significantly improved CKD prediction accuracy, model complexity often compromises clinical transparency. Existing XAI tools typically evaluate features in isolation, which fails to account for the redundant or synergistic interactions between clinical variables6. Because statistical correlations fail to identify the causal structure of CKD, we utilize a causal framework to resolve the high redundancy and interactions among clinical features.

Traditional causal discovery methods, including constraint-based and hybrid algorithms7–10, have laid the foundation for identifying directional dependencies. However, established techniques such as Granger Causality (GC)11, Convergent Cross-Mapping (CCM)12, and Transfer Entropy13—while effective in specific domains—often treat information flow as an aggregate signal. These methods, along with recent conditional independence tests like PCMCI14, struggle to handle the non-linearities and unobserved confounders typical of physiological data.

To address these limitations, this study utilizes Synergy-Unique-Redundant Decomposition (SURD)15. Unlike standard causal frameworks, SURD facilitates a granular decomposition of causal relationships, allowing us to isolate independent predictors from overlapping clinical signals. This approach aligns with the recent emphasis on hybrid XAI and data balancing in medical diagnostics16,17, moving beyond associative patterns to provide a more biologically plausible explanation for model decisions.

XAI provides a framework for interpreting machine learning decisions, addressing the opacity often caused by complex clinical correlations. While standard ML models excel at identifying associations, they lack the capacity to elucidate causal mechanisms. By applying causal inference, this study investigates the underlying causal relationships between CKD markers to complement the findings of traditional associative models. To this end, we investigate the following research questions:

Related works

In previous studies, causal inference and machine learning have been increasingly combined to support disease diagnosis, risk prediction, and treatment evaluation. MINDMerge18 was proposed to address inconsistency across causal discovery algorithms by fusing multiple causal models with credibility weighting and loop-elimination, and was validated on synthetic networks and ICU databases (eICU and MIMIC-III), showing improved causal graph recovery and strong performance in AKI risk prediction. Other studies integrated causal discovery (e.g., LiNGAM) with conventional predictive models19, or introduced temporal causal reasoning by combining VAR with GNNs to enhance discrimination across standard classifiers20. Beyond causal discovery, observational EHR-based designs have been used to emulate trial-like inference: regression discontinuity coupled with dynamic Bayesian networks revealed key drivers of renal function (e.g., eGFR) and the long-term effects of modifiable interventions21. Longitudinal targeted maximum likelihood estimation further quantified the attributable mortality of time-varying AKI severity, highlighting the importance of temporal dynamics for ICU kidney protection strategies22.

In parallel, a large body of work has evaluated feature selection and predictive modeling for CKD and related outcomes. Studies have explored correlation- or sparsity-based selection (e.g., LASSO) and compared classical ML/DL classifiers and ensembles on benchmark datasets23–25. Automated model selection and multimodal learning have also been investigated for cardiovascular risk stratification, with interpretability tools (e.g., SHAP) used to identify prognostic factors, while time-censoring and survival bias remain challenges for clinical translation26–28. Moreover, causal machine learning frameworks have been applied to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects and quantify drug-related risks, including comparisons between causal forests and penalized regression29, GAN-based causal inference for treatment effects30, and broader causal reasoning frameworks for pharmacovigilance31.

However, in early CKD risk assessment, clinical variables often exhibit substantial redundancy and complex dependencies. Traditional feature selection methods based on mutual information, information gain, or linear correlation can rank importance but cannot distinguish redundant, unique, and synergistic contributions, limiting interpretability and generalizability. To address this gap, SURD decomposes causal information into redundant, unique, and synergistic components, enabling more principled feature selection and interpretable modeling in high-dimensional clinical data. Applying SURD to multimodal CKD variables can identify independently informative indicators and synergistic feature sets that only become discriminative jointly, providing a solid foundation for the modeling tasks in this study.

Data processing

Due to the significant amount of missing values in the dataset, as shown in the table, traditional methods such as the mean, median, mode, logistic regression, and KNN imputation may exacerbate issues with missing data issues. Given the substantial number of missing values, it is not feasible to simply delete rows or columns to handle missing data, primarily because the data includes both numerical and categorical types. Two methods can be employed for filling in the missing values. This study first cleans the outliers in the categorical variables. To ensure the objectivity of model evaluation, the dataset is divided in an 8:2 ratio, using stratified sampling to maintain consistency in class distribution. For numerical features, the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) strategy is adopted, which fits the IterativeImputer on the training set and is then applied to both the training and testing sets. For categorical features, the mode imputation (SimpleImputer) is applied to the training set, and after fitting, it is utilized on both datasets for the imputed categorical variables, which are then converted into numerical vectors using One-Hot Encoding. The encoder is fitted on the training set and similarly applied to the testing set to ensure consistency in dimensionality. Although the class distribution between CKD and non-CKD samples is moderately imbalanced, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to the training set to mitigate potential bias toward the majority class and to enhance the robustness of minority-class recognition34. All pre-processing operations were strictly fitted on the training set and subsequently applied to the testing set to avoid data leakage, thereby ensuring the reliability of subsequent model training and evaluation.

Implementation details and data discretization

The study was conducted on the original dataset. To ensure the reproducibility of the MI estimation and subsequent decomposition, we employed an equal-frequency binning strategy for data discretization. Each continuous biomarker dimension was divided into 50 non-overlapping bins, a parameter selection based on prior stability sensitivity analysis which demonstrates that 50 bins achieve a robust balance between computational efficiency and the preservation of system dynamics15. In this context, the clinical dataset is interpreted as a stationary realization of a multivariate probability space. From the perspective of Partial Information Decomposition(PID), the informational interaction—specifically how diagnostic information is shared or uniquely contributed—is an intrinsic property of the joint distribution and is not strictly constrained by temporal evolution.This discretization transforms continuous observations into a finite state space,enabling the precise calculation of joint and marginal probabilities.

Model training and testing

The feature subsets were identified using the training set through SURD-based causal information decomposition and then evaluated on the independent test set across ten classifiers35. Given the high dimensionality and non-linear nature of clinical chronic kidney disease (CKD) data, we selected models across a spectrum of computational paradigms, focusing on the trade-off between interpretability and predictive depth.

Standard learners, including Decision Trees (DT)36, Naive Bayes (NB)37, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)38, and Support Vector Machines (SVM)39, were utilized to establish a performance baseline. These algorithms are prioritized in clinical informatics for their transparency, which is essential for verifying that the model’s decision logic aligns with established physiological markers of renal decline. To address potential noise and complex feature interactions within the observational trial data, we leveraged ensemble architectures—Random Forest (RF)40, XGBoost41, and AdaBoost42. These models provide a robust assessment of feature stability through bagging and boosting mechanisms, which mitigate the risk of overfitting inherent in small-to-moderate clinical datasets.

While the UCI-CKD dataset is primarily static and non-temporal, we intentionally incorporated Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)43, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)44, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)45 as high-capacity benchmarks. The inclusion of sequential architectures (RNN and LSTM) serves as a rigorous stress test for our proposed framework; we aimed to empirically determine whether the increased architectural depth of deep learning offers any incremental advantage over more efficient, traditional models in a static tabular context. This comprehensive benchmarking ensures that the selected feature set maintains strong discriminatory power regardless of the underlying algorithmic complexity.

Furthermore, We introduced LASSO46 and the PC algorithm47 as baselines to isolate the impact of the SURD framework. While LASSO represents a purely statistical approach to feature sparsity, the PC algorithm serves as a representative of classical constraint-based causal discovery. This comparison allows us to verify whether the predictive gains stem from the causal information-theoretic decomposition itself, rather than from a specific model architecture or standard statistical regularization.

Model evaluation metrics

Model performance was validated through fivefold cross-validation to ensure the generalizability of the results across diverse patient subsets. In accordance with established clinical reporting standards, we quantified model efficacy using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC)48–50.

Model hyperparameters

In this study, grid search combined with fivefold stratified cross-validation was employed to optimize the hyperparameters of the classifiers. To address potential class imbalances and ensure model robustness, the Area Under the ROC Curve was utilized as the primary optimization criterion to select the best-performing configurations. Both the predefined search ranges and the resulting optimal hyperparameters for the 10 models used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Experimental setup

All studies were conducted on Python 3.12 on an Apple M4-chipped MacBook Pro with 16 GB of RAM, and the operating system was macOS Sonoma 15.3.

Comparative analysis of feature selection frameworks

To validate SURD’s advantages, we compared its performance against LASSO and the PC algorithm (detailed results in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6; visualizations in Figures S1 and S2).

The results indicate that while all methods achieved high accuracy, SURD demonstrated superior algorithmic robustness. Specifically, under the PC-derived feature set, the NB classifier’s accuracy dropped to 0.907, and under LASSO, its AUC was 0.963. In contrast, the SURD-based U + S combination maintained near-optimal performance across all models, including NB (AUC = 0.997).

This discrepancy suggests that traditional methods like PC and LASSO may fail to capture the synergistic dependencies essential for stable prediction. The observed performance saturation (AUC > 0.99) across paradigms confirms that the predictive ceiling is driven by causal feature quality rather than model complexity, justifying our focus on information decomposition over black-box architectures like GNNs.

To further ensure a fair comparison and evaluate whether model performance was biased by complexity, we analyzed the learning curves for the classifiers Figure S3. Taking the Random Forest model as a representative case, the training score and cross-validation score converge effectively as the number of training examples increases beyond 100. This convergence indicates that the high performance is a result of effective learning of the underlying data patterns rather than overfitting or unfair complexity advantages.

External validation

To evaluate the robust generalizability of our model, we conducted an independent external validation using the MIMIC-IV database (n = 27,834)51. The author, Ningning Xue, has completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI Program) and received the corresponding certification (record number:14856183). Despite the inherent differences in baseline characteristics between the two datasets, our model demonstrated robust generalizability. The externally validated ROC curve for the Random Forest model yielded an AUC of 0.990 (95% CI 0.989–0.991), as illustrated in Figure S4, which significantly outperformed the Decision Tree baseline (AUC: 0.914, 95% CI0.912–0.916).

Model explanation and feature importance analysis

This study employs SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) to characterize the contribution of individual features to model predictions. SHAP values quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction outcome and provide a model-specific view of feature relevance. In this study, SHAP was used to rank feature importance for the RF model, thereby highlighting variables that exert strong influence on predictive outputs. To complement this model-based interpretation, SHAP results were examined alongside SURD-based information decomposition to further explore the structural roles of features.

The SHAP analysis identified hemo, sg, sc, htn, and dm as the most influential features in CKD prediction. These variables consistently showed substantial contributions to model outputs. SHAP values were computed for both the RF and DT models; due to space limitations, only the RF results are presented in Fig. 6, while the corresponding DT results are provided in Figure S5.

To quantitatively assess the relationship between SHAP-based feature attribution and information-theoretic relevance, we computed Spearman rank correlations between SHAP global importance scores and SURD-derived relevance measures (U + S). A moderate correlation was observed (Spearman’s ρ = 0.50, p = 0.0577), indicating partial concordance between the two approaches. This result suggests that SHAP and SURD capture overlapping but distinct aspects of feature importance.

From the SURD decomposition, most features with high SHAP importance (with the exception of sc) were primarily characterized by synergistic information, indicating that their predictive contributions arise mainly through interactions with other variables. In contrast, sc exhibited a dominant unique information component, consistent with its role as an independent indicator of renal function in clinical practice.

Overall, SHAP highlights features that strongly influence model predictions, whereas SURD provides complementary insight into whether such influence is driven by unique or interaction-based information structures. The combined analysis therefore supports a multi-level interpretation of feature relevance rather than a direct equivalence between the two methods.

Conclusion

This study proposes an information-theoretic framework that combines SURD-based information decomposition with machine learning for chronic kidney disease risk assessment. By decomposing clinical variables into unique, synergistic, and redundant components, this method provides a structured perspective on feature causality, surpassing traditional correlation-based feature selection strategies and offering deeper insights into the informational roles of clinical variables.

Using the UCI-CKD dataset as a benchmark, the results indicate that SURD-informed feature groupings support stable discrimination across multiple representative machine learning models. Rather than emphasizing absolute performance values, these findings highlight the consistency and interpretability of the feature space induced by information decomposition across different model families.

To examine robustness beyond a single cohort, the framework was further evaluated on the MIMIC-IV database (n = 27,834), where it achieved a stable AUC of 0.990 (95% CI 0.989–0.991). This result offers supporting evidence that the feature hierarchies identified by SURD remain informative across datasets with differing institutional characteristics, while not constituting definitive clinical validation.

With respect to interpretability, the observed Spearman rank correlation between SHAP-based feature attribution and SURD-derived relevance (ρ = 0.50, p = 0.0577) indicates partial concordance. This suggests that model-based importance and information-theoretic structure capture complementary, rather than redundant, aspects of feature relevance.

This study constructs an analysis framework based on the combination of SURD information decomposition and a prediction model, which demonstrating methodological value for structured feature analysis and interpretable risk modeling. Future research can further expand the following aspects.

First, time series data can be introduced within the existing framework, and by combining dynamic Bayesian networks or time-cause modeling methods based on deep learning, the temporal change patterns and potential causal paths between variables can be thoroughly explored. This will help to more comprehensively understand the interactions and masking trends of key clinical indicators at different time points, thereby enhancing the model’s perception and interpretation of dynamic features.

Secondly, it is recommended to apply this analysis framework to larger-scale and more diverse sample populations, especially in combination with real-world clinical electronic health record (EHR) data, further to verify its applicability and robustness in different clinical scenarios. This can not only enhance the practical application capabilities of the model but also help promote the widespread promotion of information decomposition methods in the field of medical health.

Overall, this study demonstrates the methodological potential of combining information decomposition with machine learning for structured feature analysis and interpretable risk modeling.
