Prenatal psychological distress and neurodevelopmental trajectories in the first 3 years: a systematic review
Authors: Carmela De Domenico, Andrea Calderone, Desirèe Latella, Rosaria De Luca, Francesco Corallo, Francesca Cucinotta, Davide Cardile, Angelo Quartarone, Angela Militi, Rocco Salvatore Calabrò
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis systematic review aims to examine the association between maternal psychological distress (specifically perceived stress, clinical anxiety and depressive symptoms), measured exclusively during pregnancy, and child neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed within the first 3 years of life (0–36 months), including cognitive, language, socioemotional and behavioural development.DesignThe review was conducted in line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024599742). It focused exclusively on studies assessing maternal distress during the prenatal period and its impact on cognitive, language, socioemotional and behavioural outcomes in infancy and toddlerhood.Data sourcesA comprehensive search of six databases, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, EBSCOhost and PsycINFO, was conducted up to 10 April 2025, using structured combinations of keywords related to maternal stress and child development.Eligibility criteria: Studies were included if they assessed psychological distress during pregnancy with validated tools and evaluated neurodevelopmental outcomes in children aged 0–36 months using standardised measures. Excluded were studies measuring distress only postnatally, animal models, non-original articles and studies without neurodevelopmental endpoints.Data extraction and data synthesisData were extracted and reviewed independently by two authors using predefined criteria, with a third reviewer resolving disagreements. Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Exposures tool for non-randomised studies and Cohort Studies. Given study heterogeneity, a structured narrative synthesis with standardised effect summaries was used.Results44 studies met the inclusion criteria. Across these, small, correlational associations linked higher maternal distress during pregnancy with modest differences in cognitive and language scores and with elevated risks of behavioural and socioemotional difficulties. Children exposed to higher distress more often showed attention problems, greater negative emotionality, lower verbal ability and weaker emotion regulation, with effects frequently attenuated after adjustment and selective attrition.ConclusionsMaternal psychological distress during pregnancy is a context-sensitive correlate, not a proven cause, of early neurodevelopmental differences across cognitive, emotional and behavioural domains.

Introduction

The initial 3 years of life are a sensitive and critical period in brain development when the foundation is laid for cognitive, language, motor, social, behavioural and emotional skills.1 2 At this young age, the brain is highly plastic and susceptible to genetic and environmental influences.3 Of the prenatal contributors to this early neurodevelopmental trajectory, one of the larger focuses of attention has been maternal psychological distress during pregnancy.4 Variables like women’s self-reported stress, preadult anxiety and depressive symptoms have been associated with differences in child development, not just short-term but also long-term, impact on development.5 6 These conditions are common, impacting a large part of the population and can potentially affect outcomes not only for the mother’s health but also for fetal and early childhood development.7 Prevalence of clinical anxiety and depression in pregnancy globally is 20.7%, increasing with gestation advancement.8 9 The fetus can be a possible target for multiple insults, such as external and internal, throughout the course of pregnancy, and the maternal psychological well-being has been associated with affecting the early neurodevelopmental trajectory.10 11 To encompass these varied experiences, we use psychological distress in pregnancy as an umbrella descriptor for stress, clinical anxiety and depressive symptoms occurring during the prenatal period.12 13 It also encompasses antenatal psychosocial risks, collectively referred to here as ‘adversity’, such as financial hardship, relationship conflict and a history of trauma.14 15 Such heightened stress would not merely bear significance for the mental and physical well-being of the mother, as stress exposure in pregnancy could exert its adverse influence deleteriously on the biological maturation of the fetus.16 17 Mothers’ own mental illness and chronic stress can trigger the body’s stress response, increasing production of stress hormones like cortisol.18 19 These hormones could cross the placenta and alter uteroplacental blood flow, and any change in nutrient and oxygen delivery may disrupt important fetal brain development pathways.20 21 Furthermore, inflammatory stress responses associated with pregnancy may also interfere with fetal neurodevelopment.22 23 Among the range of maternal psychological disturbances, anxiety is especially common and may range from mild fears for the mother’s and baby’s health and the birth to severe, enduring anxiety disorders, including panic and post-traumatic stress disorders.24 Similarly, prenatal depression has been identified as a common form of maternal distress with significant implications, for example, feeling sad all the time, feeling hopeless and not enjoying things that the pregnant woman would normally enjoy.25 26 Severe depressive symptoms may interfere with maternal self-care and exacerbate threats to fetal well-being and fetal emotional and behavioural regulatory capacities longer term.27 28 Infants exposed in utero to maternal depression may also experience difficulties related to emotional attachment and regulation and higher levels of mood problems such as anxiety and depression during childhood.29 30 As these maternal psychological disturbances may influence fetal development, examining genetic and epigenetic mechanisms underlying child neurodevelopment and their susceptibility and fetal vulnerability to such disruptions is of critical importance. Child neurodevelopment is a complex and dimensioned process that is the result of the interplay between genetic and environmental factors and of the emerging importance of gene–environment interactions.31 32 Genomic risk factors can have major effects on the susceptibility to the impact of prenatal maternal psychological distress.33 Polymorphisms of the genes implicated in stress response, neurotransmitter systems and neurodevelopment interfere with the influence of maternal distress on the developing fetus.34 35 In addition, epigenetic mechanisms, which involve changes to gene expression that do not involve changes to the DNA sequence itself, are also increasingly being recognised as guiding these interactions, offering a basic biological mechanism for how environmental conditions, such as in utero stress in the mother, can imprint on and stay with a child’s own developmental path.36 37

Despite increasing interest in this topic, a need persists for a complete, systematic review of the literature specifically investigating the relationship between maternal psychological distress assessed during pregnancy and child neurodevelopmental outcomes consisting of psychological and behavioural development in toddlerhood. Most published reviews either combine postnatal with antenatal distress or generally describe overall developmental outcomes, so there is a need to specifically examine the distinct impact of maternal distress during pregnancy on early child development. Therefore, this systematic review aims to examine the association between maternal psychological distress (specifically perceived stress, clinical anxiety and depressive symptoms), measured exclusively during pregnancy, and child neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed within the first 3 years of life (0–36 months), including cognitive, language, socioemotional and behavioural development. Materials and methods

Inclusion criteria

We limited our systematic review to stringent and novel human studies in which maternal psychological distress (regardless of whether it was reported as self‐reported perceived stress, clinically significant anxiety or depressive symptoms) was assessed exclusively in the prenatal window. Essentially, we mandated that such studies use well‐validated, standardised measures of both maternal distress (eg, Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), State‐Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)) and child neurodevelopment (eg, Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, MacArthur–Bates Communicative Development Inventories (MCDI), Child Behaviour Checklist, CBCL). We also specified that results on the offspring are up to age 3, as brain plasticity propels rapid brain maturation during the first years of life, rendering children particularly responsive to early effects, and that effect measures (effect sizes, regression coefficients, ORs and correlations) were reported, enabling the integration of results across various cohorts. In order to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence, this review included a wide variety of study designs, including longitudinal studies, non-randomised controlled trials (RCTs), observational studies, retrospective studies and prospective studies. Last but not least, only full-text articles published in peer-reviewed journals written in English were included in this systematic review, in the interest of rigour.

Exclusion criteria

To preserve focus on the unique influence of prenatal maternal distress, we excluded any investigations in which psychological symptoms were measured only after birth or beyond 36 months, as well as those that conflated prenatal and postnatal assessments without temporal separation. Studies whose primary outcomes lay outside neurodevelopment, such as obstetric complications, birth weight, or maternal physical health, were also set aside, as were non‐original works (reviews, meta‐analyses, study protocols, case reports) and preclinical animal models. By limiting our pool to peer‐reviewed, full‐text articles available in English, we ensured both methodological rigour and practical accessibility. Finally, to safeguard against confounding and bias, we gave preference to prospective cohort designs with adequate sample sizes, clear adjustment for socioeconomic and perinatal covariates, and formal assessments of study quality or risk of bias.

Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome evaluation

In addressing the existing knowledge gap, this systematic review uses a clear Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome framework. In this systematic review, we defined our Population (P) as pregnant individuals whose levels of psychological distress had been rigorously assessed during pregnancy. Our Exposure (E) of interest was elevated maternal distress, whether captured as perceived stress, clinical anxiety or depressive symptoms, measured exclusively with validated instruments such as the PSS, the STAI or the EPDS. We compared these exposed mothers and their children to a Comparator (C) group in which maternal distress scores had remained below established clinical or subclinical thresholds. Finally, our Outcomes (O) comprised children’s neurodevelopmental performance across cognitive, language, socioemotional and behavioural domains up to 36 months of age, as evaluated by standardised assessments like the Bayley Scales of Infant Development (BSID), the MCDI and the CBCL.

Search strategy

This systematic review used an established approach to assess research studies and included a time search conducted from 5 March 2025 to 10 April 2025. Importantly, no predefined time range determined the search to guarantee all relevant evidence would be collated. The April 2025 endpoint ensures that the appraisal captures the latest relevant content available at the time of the search. To minimise time-restriction bias, we intentionally set no lower date limit; to manage heterogeneity introduced by instrument/version changes over 2001–2025, we harmonised outcomes to common domains, prioritised validated and, where feasible, assessor-administered subscales and did not pool non-commensurate effect metrics. An extensive literature search was conducted through PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, EBSCOhost and PsychINFO databases using the keywords of (“maternally”[All Fields] OR “maternities”[All Fields] OR “maternity”[All Fields] OR “mothers”[MeSH Terms] OR “mothers”[All Fields] OR “maternal”[All Fields]) AND (“stress”[All Fields] OR “stressed”[All Fields] OR “stresses”[All Fields] OR “stressful”[All Fields] OR “stressfulness”[All Fields] OR “stressing”[All Fields]) AND ((“toddler”[All Fields] OR “toddler s”[All Fields] OR “toddlers”[All Fields]) AND (“develop”[All Fields] OR “develope”[All Fields] OR “developed”[All Fields] OR “developer”[All Fields] OR “developer s”[All Fields] OR “developers”[All Fields] OR “developing”[All Fields] OR “developments”[All Fields] OR “develops”[All Fields] OR “growth and development”[MeSH Subheading] OR (“growth”[All Fields] AND “development”[All Fields]) OR “growth and development”[All Fields] OR “development”[All Fields])).

To ensure that there is a complete and non-biased view of the literature on prenatal maternal psychological distress and child neurodevelopment in infants and toddlers, we searched six different databases. We selected PubMed because of its broad coverage of biomedical and life‐science research, which encompasses developmental neuroscience and paediatric psychiatry. Web of Science was also used to maximise its multidisciplinary coverage and its citation tracking capabilities, which refine search results. The gold standard compendium of systematic reviews and RCTs is the Cochrane Library, which is one of the strictest repositories of evidence about the efficacy of interventions. We chose Embase because of its strong coverage of clinical and pharmacology studies, useful for discovering research on biological stress markers with fetal outcomes that may not be included in PubMed. To access specialised nursing, midwifery and allied health literature on perinatal mental health and early developmental screening, we searched the EBSCOhost database. We searched PsycINFO because of its comprehensive coverage of psychological and behavioural science to be as certain as possible that we captured studies that measured maternal anxiety, depression and stress. In doing so, we minimised the potential risk of missing important evidence, ensured different methodological approaches were considered, and optimised the trustworthiness and credibility of our systematic review. Data extraction

Two reviewers (CDD and AC) performed independent searches to improve transparency and accuracy in locating pertinent studies. Each search strategy was iteratively refined by testing combinations of keywords, Boolean operators and controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) to achieve a balance between maximum sensitivity and specificity. The process for selecting studies was illustrated by using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow chart (identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion), which is presented in figure 1.46 Additionally, two researchers (CDD and AC) screened all articles based on titles, abstracts and full texts, conducting independent data extraction, article gathering and cross-validation to minimise bias risks (eg, missing results bias, publication bias, time lag bias, language bias). For each study, the same two authors (CDD and AC) extracted information independently on study design, sample characteristics (size, maternal age, socioeconomic status), measure of prenatal distress (eg, PSS, EPDS, STAI), time frame of distress assessment, child neurodevelopmental outcomes (cognitive, language, socioemotional, behavioural), measure and age of outcome and follow-up age. The researchers (CDD and AC) reviewed complete text articles considered suitable for the study, and if there were disagreements regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a final decision was reached by a third researcher (RSC). Discrepancies between reviewers during the screening or data extraction process were also resolved through discussion, with unresolved cases adjudicated by a third reviewer (RSC). Additionally, the concordance between the two evaluators (CDD and AC) was evaluated through the kappa statistic.47 48 The kappa score, which has a recognised threshold for significant agreement established at >0.61, was understood to indicate substantial alignment among the reviewers. This standard guarantees a strong assessment of inter-rater reliability, highlighting the attainment of a significant degree of consensus in the data extraction procedure. Microsoft Excel was used to extract and organise the data, which greatly reduced the human error margin in the process. The software lent itself to handling large datasets, allowing reviewers to systematically record study characteristics, risk of bias assessments and outcome data. Previous inclusion/exclusion criteria allowed for consistency and use of custom extraction sheets built into the software. It also provided tagging, filtering and sorting features that helped close discrepancies and speed up the cross-validation of accounts. The articles were then narrowed to be relevant, assessed and summarised with major topics identified from the summary based on the inclusion/exclusion criteria. This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO under the following number: CRD42024599742. Registration on PROSPERO ensures transparency and accountability by providing a publicly accessible record of the review protocol prior to its execution.49 This step minimises the risk of selective reporting and allows for external validation of the review’s methodology. By adhering to PROSPERO standards, this review aligns with best practices for systematic reviews and reinforces its methodological rigour.50

Data synthesis

We used narrative synthesis to synthesise results across the studies, reflecting the heterogeneity of the study designs and the maternal distress measures used and the child neurodevelopmental outcomes evaluated.51 All studies were appraised for quality and risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Exposures (ROBINS-E) tool for observational cohort studies.52 53 Our review has presented a structured narrative synthesis with standardised effect summaries that revealed common and divergent findings across key thematic patterns such as consistent relationships between heightened prenatal cortisol and cognitive deficits and divergent findings in language and socio-emotional functioning. Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment were conducted by three independent reviewers in a multidisciplinary team54 (CDD, AC and RSC), with any disagreement resolved through discussion. Weekly team meetings promoted consensus in interpretation and the situating of findings in the larger developmental and clinical context. We intentionally set no lower date limit to minimise time-restriction bias and capture the full evolution of this literature; to address temporal heterogeneity in measurement, we harmonised outcomes to common domains and prioritised validated/assessor-administered subscales. Towards the same end, this integrative approach results in an inclusive and well-balanced overview of the extent to which children’s neurodevelopment at up to 36 months of age is influenced by maternal psychological distress during her pregnancy, especially considering the natural complexity of the data and the quality of the evidence base. The methodological framework used in this systematic review is summarised in online supplemental file 2.

Patient and public involvement

This study is a systematic review of published, deidentified data. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, selection of outcomes, data collection, analysis or interpretation of this research, and no participants were recruited or contacted; therefore, assessment of participant burden and compensation was not applicable. The research question, eligibility criteria and outcomes were determined from the scientific literature and methodological guidance for systematic reviews rather than through patient consultation.

Results

A comprehensive literature search was carried out using six electronic databases. This initial search yielded a total of 885 records. Before formal screening commenced, 260 duplicate records were identified and removed, along with 5 non-English articles. This left 620 records for title and abstract screening. Following this initial screening phase, 13 records were excluded based on inadequate study design, leaving 607 reports for full-text retrieval. However, despite efforts to obtain these articles (eg, emailing corresponding authors, consulting libraries, exploring open-access sources, checking institutional resources and using research networks), 2 reports could not be retrieved. The remaining 605 reports underwent a thorough assessment for eligibility. During this stage, 511 reports were further excluded based on title screening, and an additional 50 were excluded after abstract screening. This rigorous selection process resulted in a final set of 44 studies5598 that met the predefined inclusion criteria and were therefore included in this review (figure 1).

Quality of included studies: risk of bias

To ensure a thorough evaluation of methodological quality, risk of bias was assessed with a single, exposure-focused tool applied across all observational cohort designs.5598 This systematic approach provided a robust framework for identifying potential limitations and inconsistencies in research methods, enabling a transparent and reliable comprehension of the findings within the broader context of evidence.

Cochrane ROBINS-E

For all included studies,5598 we applied the ROBINS-E tool across seven domains: confounding; selection of participants; classification of exposures; deviations from intended exposures; missing data; measurement of outcomes; and selection of the reported result, judging each study against a hypothetical well-conducted randomised trial (figures2 3). We used conservative decision rules: the overall rating equalled the highest domain, and insufficient information was assigned when protocols or analysis plans were unavailable or when key details could not be verified. To make judgements transparent, we integrated the catalogue of bias into domain ratings (online supplemental file 3): classical confounding (notably incomplete capture of paternal/familial and maternal-mental health/history, caregiving/home environment and environmental coexposures) mapped to D1; selection mechanisms at enrolment and attrition/missing not at random mapped to D2 and D5; information/measurement bias, including common-rater and mood-congruent reporting when the same mother reported exposure and socioemotional outcomes, mapped to D6; exposure misclassification (single late-pregnancy assessment or retrospective disaster measures) mapped to D3; and analytical flexibility/selective reporting mapped to D7. Deviations from intended exposures (D4) were minimal, as expected in observational cohorts.

The resulting profile shows that most studies carry a high overall risk of bias, concentrated in confounding (D1), selection of participants (D2), missing data (D5) and measurement of outcomes (D6). Across cohorts, confounding was pervasive: paternal mental health and family history, caregiving quality and home environment, and relevant coexposures (eg, tobacco, environmental toxicants, obstetric complications) were rarely captured comprehensively, leaving substantial residual confounding even in models with multiple sociodemographic covariates. This pattern is evident in large population cohorts and smaller clinical or disaster samples alike, including Wesselhoeft et al,55 Lin et al,63 Zhu et al,60 Stroustrup et al,84 Palmer et al,93 Brand et al95 and Bhat et al.98 Selection and attrition further compromised internal validity. Disaster-exposed cohorts (Laplante et al58 81 97) and several convenience or subsampled studies showed recruitment and retention processes plausibly related to both exposure and outcome, often without inverse-probability weighting, multiple imputation or missing not at random sensitivity analyses; the consequence is a credible risk of biased estimates where the most distressed dyads are also the least likely to remain in follow-up. Outcome measurement introduced additional systematic error, especially for socioemotional endpoints. Many associations rested on single-informant maternal reports, the same informant who provided the exposure, creating common-rater and mood-congruent reporting bias and prompting high ratings in D6 for a substantial proportion of studies (eg, Brand et al,95 Frigerio and Nazzari,75 Stroustrup et al,84 Palmer et al,93 Buthmann et al,96 Bhat et al98). When outcomes were examiner-administered with blinded assessors (eg, Bayley), D6 was typically Low or Some concerns, and estimates tended to be smaller and less variable. Other domains contributed but were less determinative. Classification of exposures (D3) was usually at some concerns, reflecting single time point assessment late in pregnancy or retrospective capture in disaster designs; repeated antenatal measures and triangulation with biomarkers were uncommon. Deviations from intended exposures (D4) were consistently low, as expected in observational cohorts. For selection of the reported result (D7), preregistered protocols and analysis plans were rarely available and multiplicity (multiple outcomes, time-windows and interaction tests) was frequent; in these circumstances, we recorded No information rather than inferring low risk. Mechanistic cohorts using MRI or inflammatory/endocrine markers (eg, Spann et al,90 Wu et al76) were informative for biological plausibility but small and selective, with high risk from confounding (D1) and missingness/selection (D2/D5); these are best regarded as exploratory. Collectively, the ROBINS-E assessments indicate that a non-trivial fraction of observed associations may be distorted by uncontrolled confounding, common-method variance and selective retention, and that certainty depends heavily on outcome ascertainment and follow-up integrity. In the synthesis and interpretation, we therefore give greater weight to studies with examiner-based or multi-informant outcomes and more complete covariate frameworks, and we treat disaster-exposed and small mechanistic cohorts as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Demographic and aetiological characteristics: age, sex, diagnosis, observation periods and their geographical distribution

Assessment time points in the 44 included studies ranged from the neonatal period to early childhood, clustering at approximately 3 weeks,56 6–12 months,60 62 74 18 months61 73 78 79 and 24–30 months.575963 64 80 81 93 Follow-up intervals varied meaningfully (SD from 1 to 7 months), which introduces potential distortions when comparing trajectories across cohorts. Sex distributions were typically balanced, though several samples skewed slightly male; for example, 52% boys in New York62 and in the Iowa flood cohort,81 and a minority did not stratify by sex, limiting inference on sex-specific vulnerability.71 Across studies, assessments most commonly occurred in late infancy and the second year of life, with fewer data points beyond 30 months, which constrains trajectory modelling over the full 0–36 month window. Most exposure profiles centre on perceived stress, anxiety or depressive symptoms (PSS, STAI, EPDS), commonly measured once or twice in pregnancy.5559 6163 Exposure capture was typically mid-to-late gestation, with repeated trimester-specific measures less frequent, and a subset of disaster cohorts using retrospective exposure ascertainment anchored to event timing. A smaller set combined questionnaires with biological or environmental indicators, hair/salivary cortisol, inflammatory markers, Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAH)/Particulate Matter≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) models or metals, providing partial triangulation of stress biology with self-report.62 65 67 79 84 These multimodal designs offered partial triangulation of prenatal distress with stress biology or coexposures, although biomarker sampling windows and environmental models varied across cohorts. Geographically, evidence is concentrated in high-income settings across North America and Europe,555961 with important but fewer cohorts from East Asia,60 63 65 77 Scandinavia,83 Suriname,80 South Africa89 and Georgia.68 69 Most samples were drawn from antenatal clinics or population registers in high-income settings; this, together with outcome instruments standardised primarily on Western norms, may limit generalisability to lower-resource contexts and to populations with different cultural–linguistic adaptations of child measures. Considering study quality, inference in this review is anchored to the ROBINS-E judgments. Within that uniform overall rating, there was heterogeneity in the dominant drivers: population-based cohorts with examiner-administered or multi-informant outcomes and more complete covariate frameworks showed fewer and more contained high-risk domains and yielded smaller, more stable estimates,597779 84 86 whereas quasi-natural disasters, small neuroimaging/endocrine samples and single-informant designs accumulated high risk from confounding, selection/attrition and outcome measurement, resulting in low to very low certainty despite occasional statistical significance.5871 72 81 90 9597 Persistent mother–teacher discrepancies further indicate measurement error and reduce confidence in parent-only socioemotional outcomes.55

Study design, research methods, data collection tools and safety and adverse events

Most investigations were prospective longitudinal cohorts with repeated assessments from mid-gestation through 6–36 months, using validated maternal scales (PSS, STAI, EPDS) and standardised child tools (BSID/Bayley-III, MCDI, CBCL/Infant–Toddler Social and Emotional Assessment, Neonatal Behavioural Assessment Scale, Gesell).565862 64 66 71 73 Several integrated objective exposures (eg, PM2.5 models, lead/mercury biomarkers) or stress biology (cortisol, inflammatory cytokines), as well as fetal/neonatal MRI, strengthen internal validity within the limits of observational data.62 64 65 67 72 76 79 84 Modern analytic strategies, mixture and interaction models, careful confounder adjustment and sensitivity analyses were common in the stronger cohorts.62 64 65 77 84 Updated bias appraisals nonetheless temper certainty: single time-point exposure capture, parent-reported outcomes, incomplete follow-up in smaller imaging or disaster cohorts and co-occurring postnatal adversity remain the dominant threats to validity.5558 7173 81 90 92 97 Parent report predominated for socioemotional endpoints, with teacher/caregiver reports available in a subset55, and examiner-administered assessments more common for cognitive/motor domains. Mode of administration varied (in-person vs questionnaire), and the proportion of multi-informant datasets was smaller than single-informant datasets, which is reflected in wider variability for socio-emotional outcomes.

Effect size and certainty of evidence

Effect sizes were typically small to modest, with mean differences of a few standardised points, regression coefficients near zero but non-null, or ORs around 1.3–2.0, linking prenatal distress with early cognitive, language and socioemotional/temperament outcomes.555860 62 64 66 Cognitive findings most often showed small decrements in standardised scores among offspring of more stressed or anxious mothers, with several cohorts reporting attenuation after robust adjustment, consistent with residual confounding in observational designs.59 61 63 66 Temperament and early behaviour studies showed similarly small coefficients for negative affect, diminished regulation or internalising symptoms, and additive or interactive effects where maternal distress co-occurred with environmental toxicants (lead, PM, PAH).62 64 65 84 In very preterm infants, maternal psychological risk was associated with larger clinical contrasts, for example, higher odds of dysregulated neonatal neurobehaviour and severe motor delay at 24 months, although even these estimates were moderate in magnitude.88 Applying a single, consistent risk appraisal across designs (ROBINS-E), overall certainty was moderate at most for small effects in larger, well-characterised cohorts,597779 84 86 91 and low to very low for disaster-exposed samples and small neurobiological studies; these latter findings are treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.5871 72 81 90 9597 Across the corpus, no study reported assessment-related adverse events.

Perinatal distress and subsequent cognitive and language performance

Longitudinal data generally point to subtle cognitive and language disadvantages after higher prenatal distress, with heterogeneity by design quality and covariate control. In a healthy cohort, late-pregnancy anxiety predicted lower Bayley Mental Developmental Index (MDI) at 24 months and attentional differences at 3 weeks and 12 months, although sample size limits precision.56 Natural-disaster exposure in Québec was associated with lower MDI and poorer receptive and productive language at 2 years, explaining 11%–17% of variance with stronger effects for earlier gestation; given design limitations and incomplete control of selection/missingness, these estimates are considered hypothesis-generating with very low certainty.58 A large US cohort found that an unadjusted 3.8-point Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test deficit at age 3 largely attenuated after controlling for sociodemographic factors, suggesting confounding explains much of the crude association; certainty is best characterised as moderate for a near-null effect on receptive vocabulary and visual-motor skills.59 Population cohorts from Greece and China linked antenatal depressive symptoms and prenatal stress with modest reductions in cognitive and motor domains at 18–30 months, again with small absolute differences and low certainty due to self-reported exposures, screening-tool outcomes and non-trivial attrition.61 63 Environmental coexposures appear to amplify risk: prenatal lead under high maternal stress was associated with poorer language and adaptive/social scores, and another study predicted lower receptive/expressive communication, whereas ‘probable depression’ tracked with weaker motor scores; overall certainty for these toxicant–distress combinations is low, contingent on sample size and adjustment.65 80 Motor rather than cognitive/language differences was the most consistent signal after antidepressant exposure in pregnancy, with small reductions at age 2 and moderate certainty for no meaningful cognitive or language deficit.91 Emerging work suggests prenatal emotion dysregulation and maternal physiology may map to language via postnatal stress pathways (smaller expressive vocabularies at 18 months), but single-time point language measurement and potential socioeconomic confounding keep certainty low.70 Taken together, when reappraised with a uniform approach, the balance of evidence supports at most small or null differences in early cognition and language, often attenuated by rigorous adjustment; certainty ranges from moderate in larger cohorts to low/very low in disaster-based or small mechanistic studies.565861 63 65 70 80 81 91

Linking prenatal adversity to child socioemotional and behavioural development

Associations between prenatal distress and early socio-emotional or behavioural risk are more consistently observed than for cognition, though effect sizes remain small and sensitive to context and coexposures. Combined prenatal PAH and maternal stress predicted poorer orienting/regulation at 4 months and lower competence with more behaviour problems at 12 months, with an indirect path via reduced surgency/extraversion; certainty is low because exposure was modelled, outcomes were parent-reported, and the cohort was modest in size.62 Flood-related hardship was linked to higher internalising via toddler negative reactivity, but mediation for anxiety was not robust and discrepancies between teacher and mother report with attrition warranted very low certainty.71 Antenatal major depressive disorder was linked to neonatal social-orientation differences and heightened cortisol reactivity at 12 months without clear Bayley-III deficits; biological measures strengthen plausibility, but small samples limit confidence.74 Elevated prenatal and postnatal EPDS scores tracked with higher internalising at 18 months in a small French cohort (very low certainty).73 Larger studies report clearer patterns: maternal prenatal anxiety predicted a persistently ‘difficult’ temperament trajectory through 18 months in a Swedish cohort with repeated measures83; depressive symptoms and lead exposure independently and additively predicted difficult temperament at 24 months in Mexico City.84 Parenting context and caregiving quality modify risk: responsive caregiving buffered the association between maternal stress and developmental delay at 12 months in a very large Chinese cohort,77 and higher maternal sensitivity altered links between antenatal anxiety and toddler externalising in Italy (low certainty).75 Sex-specific or physiology-anchored pathways are plausible but not definitive: interactions with cortisol and female vulnerability at 24 months,67 sex differences in cortisol reactivity after disaster exposure,82 and inhibitory-control mediation of aggression, particularly among girls, suggest nuanced mechanisms with low certainty.67 82 85 Neuroimaging cohorts provide convergent biological signals, prenatal depression aligning with uncinate fasciculus fibre density and later socioemotional difficulties, altered corpus callosum microstructure predicting toddler behaviour, and fetal cortical/hippocampal markers mediating infant cognition and socioemotional outcomes, but small samples and complex models keep certainty low to very low.72 76 79 Community cohorts reiterate the role of maternal stress and sociodemographic adversity in 2-year socioemotional risk, with precise estimates and moderate certainty.78 93 Overall, prenatal distress is described as a correlate of early socioemotional dysregulation and difficult temperament, often amplified by environmental toxicants and mitigated by caregiving quality, with moderate certainty in larger cohorts and low/very low certainty in disaster-based and small mechanistic studies.627184 87 Online supplemental file 4 contains a summary table of the included studies.

Discussion

Qualitative analysis

Maternal prenatal psychological distress is modestly associated with offspring development in the first 3 years and is best interpreted as correlational rather than causal, with a relatively consistent pattern showing less than ~2 months difference (in terms of standardised scores) on birth-to-age-3 developmental assessments across cohorts. Consistent signals are seen for socioemotional functioning (overall lower; higher negative affect) and temperament (decreased orienting/effortful control); cognitive and language findings are more variable and attenuated with adjustment for socioeconomic and familial factors and are also the most vulnerable to single-informant reporting and selective follow-up.5961 64 66 7779 84 93 This pattern argues for domain selectivity rather than a global developmental decrement, and it is strengthened when exposure timing is considered. Three advances extend prior literature. First, several studies report modest, model-sensitive amplification when psychosocial distress co-occurs with environmental toxicants. Prenatal PAH/PM2.5 combined with higher maternal stress predicted poorer infant orienting/regulation and later socioemotional difficulties; prenatal lead showed stronger adverse associations with language, social and adaptive skills under higher maternal stress.62 64 65 80 84 These mixture results are consistent with, but do not prove, a ‘double-hit’ model whereby chemical and psychosocial stressors converge on shared neurobiological pathways. Second, modifiable buffers emerge: responsive caregiving, greater maternal sensitivity and partner support consistently attenuate risk, reframing prenatal distress as a context-sensitive susceptibility rather than a deterministic cause.75 77 87 Third, convergent biological readouts link distress to fetal/infant brain organisation (hippocampal volume, cortical folding, uncinate and callosal microstructure) and to endocrine/immune markers (cortisol, inflammatory cytokines), with downstream behaviour associations, offering mechanistic plausibility despite modest samples, but these mechanistic findings are treated as exploratory given small samples, selective participation and model complexity.72 74 76 79 90 Timing and population context matter. Multiple cohorts indicate stronger effects with early-gestation exposure, particularly for cognitive and language outcomes, implicating first/second-trimester sensitivity windows, though estimates are imprecise in disaster and convenience samples.58 60 81 99 Designs that separate prenatal from postnatal symptoms suggest prenatal distress relates more to cognition, while postnatal symptoms align more with temperament and behavioural problems, sharpening the case for time-specific pathways and interventions.63 100 In very preterm infants, maternal prenatal psychological risk marks substantially higher odds of dysregulated neonatal neurobehaviour and severe motor delay by 24 months, highlighting a subgroup in whom antenatal distress may ‘matter more’ clinically.88 By contrast, in large, well-adjusted cohorts of term births, prenatal depressive symptoms show near-null associations with receptive vocabulary and visual-motor skills once confounding is addressed.59 For medication exposures, prenatal antidepressants carry small reductions in fine/gross motor scores at age 2 without consistent cognitive or language deficits after adjustment, useful for balanced risk–benefit counselling when treating maternal depression.91 Effect sizes are generally modest: differences of a few standardised points on Bayley-type indices. Alone, these are unlikely to be clinically salient for most children, but they can accumulate alongside poverty, environmental exposures or biological vulnerability.64 78 84 88 93 Informant effects are non-trivial: teacher–mother discrepancies and higher symptom reporting among mothers with postnatal depression suggest common-method variance that can inflate associations when outcomes rely on a single, non-blinded informant, so parent-only socioemotional outcomes are downweighted.55 Several studies also hint at sex-sensitive and self-regulatory mechanisms, for example, cortisol-linked behaviour problems more pronounced in girls at 24 months, stronger stress reactivity after disaster exposure among girls, and mediation via early inhibitory control, though these interactions need replication in larger, well-powered samples.67 82 85 Emerging pathways from prenatal emotion dysregulation and maternal physiology to toddler vocabulary via heightened postpartum stress further illustrate the intertwining of prenatal and early caregiving influences.70 Confidence in estimates tracks a uniform, exposure-focused appraisal applied across studies: disaster-exposed and small mechanistic cohorts are treated as hypothesis-generating with low to very low certainty for larger effects.5860 71 81 9597 Conversely, larger, lower-bias population cohorts with examiner-administered or multi-informant outcomes, prespecified confounders and principled handling of attrition support at most small associations, especially for socioemotional/temperament domains and in mixtures with environmental exposures.5657 59 61 62 64 66 7679 84 86 91 93 94 Mixture studies with modelled air pollution or metals typically sit at low to moderate certainty owing to reliance on modelled exposures and residual confounding despite conceptual strength.62 64 65 80 84 Practice implications are calibrated rather than prescriptive: routine antenatal care should integrate mental health screening and brief supports early in pregnancy, with referral pathways for moderate-to-severe symptoms, while definitive causal inference awaits quasi-experimental/within-family designs. Where chemical coexposures are likely, coupling psychosocial care with exposure reduction (air quality, lead mitigation) is rational given interaction evidence.62 64 65 80 84 Because postnatal caregiving quality buffers risk, scalable supports, responsive-care coaching, stress management and partner engagement are logical complements that align with observed moderation.75 77 87 For very preterm infants and families facing combined psychosocial and environmental adversity, stepped-care models and earlier developmental surveillance focused on socioemotional regulation and motor development are warranted.77 88 Priorities for research include dense, multitime point exposure sampling across pregnancy; multi-informant, assessor-blinded outcomes at harmonised ages; quasi-experimental/within-family designs to probe causality; mixture/exposome models to quantify joint effects; and adequately powered biomarker/neuroimaging studies to test mediational pathways. Trials that integrate antenatal mental health treatments with postnatal caregiving supports, especially in early gestation and high-exposure settings, would directly test the modifiability these observational data imply.

Integrating genetic and environmental contributions to toddler neurodevelopment

Development of offspring, especially before and soon after birth, is strongly influenced by the complex interplay between genetic endowment and environmental exposures.101 Although the review primarily addresses other aspects rather than focusing specifically on genetic and environmental factors, existing literature consistently highlights that these influences significantly shape early child neurodevelopment. Genetic polymorphisms specifically within catecholamine-related genes, such as DRD4, DAT1 and COMT, are considered key factors in determining an infant’s vulnerability or resilience to prenatal stress.102 103 For example, children with specific genetic variations, including the DRD4 7-repeat allele, may be at increased risk for negative outcomes, such as behavioural and emotional dysregulation, in the context of high maternal prenatal stress.104 Yet, the same genetic profiles can also be associated with benefits in supportive and nurturing postnatal environments, pointing at differential susceptibility to environmental context effects.105 Maternal antenatal stress may additionally exert effects on the child through epigenetic modifications. It can lead to epigenetic changes in fetal gene expression, particularly in genes related to stress or HPA regulation.106 These epigenetic alterations have been associated with enduring changes in the stress system and higher susceptibility to childhood cognitive and emotional difficulties.107 108 For example, offspring exposed prenatally to increased maternal cortisol continue to have changes in stress responsivity, attention regulation and emotional well-being.109 Environmental risk factors such as maternal tobacco and alcohol exposure, pollutants and socioeconomic adversity might interact in a synergistic fashion with genetic predispositions, in many cases exacerbating strongly adverse developmental outcomes.110 111 One such example is prenatal tobacco exposure, which has been repeatedly linked with epigenetic changes negatively affecting neurodevelopment and leading to decreased cognitive functioning and increased behavioural difficulties.112 Jointly, these results highlight the need for the consideration of genetic as well as environmental influences in early development. Acknowledgement of the interplay of genetic susceptibility, as well as prenatal environmental influences, can provide cues to specific interventions that might enhance developmental resiliency and result in more favourable long-term child outcomes.

Strengths, limitations and scientific significance: a reflective evaluation of the systematic review

This review’s principal strength is methodological coherence. We applied a single, exposure-focused risk appraisal consistently across all observational designs and embedded those judgements within the synthesis so that certainty explicitly reflects confounding, selection/attrition, exposure ascertainment and outcome measurement. The analysis was quality-weighted by design: larger, characterised cohorts with repeated antenatal assessment, examiner-administered or multi-informant child outcomes, prespecified confounders and principled handling of missing data were given greater inferential weight, whereas disaster-exposed samples, small mechanistic cohorts and studies relying on parent-only socio-emotional outcomes were down-weighted and treated as hypothesis-generating. We enforced a strict prenatal exposure window (separating antenatal from postnatal phenomena) and anchored interpretation to validated instruments commonly used in this field, which improved internal consistency across heterogeneous settings. Mechanistic findings (endocrine/inflammatory markers, fetal/neonatal MRI) were retained for biological plausibility but not used to elevate certainty, acknowledging sample size, participation selectivity and model complexity.

Important limitations remain and temper generalisability. The evidence base is observational; residual and unmeasured confounding, particularly paternal/partner mental health, caregiving quality and environmental coexposures, cannot be excluded even in stronger cohorts. Exposure measurement was often sparse (one or two antenatal time points) and socioemotional outcomes frequently relied on single, non-blinded informants, increasing the risk of common-method variance and informant discrepancies. Attrition and selective follow-up were common in several cohorts, with incomplete use of modern missing-data methods, thereby risking biased estimates and reduced precision. Outcome heterogeneity (instruments, ages, scoring) and non-standardised effect reporting precluded a defensible meta-analysis, necessitating narrative synthesis. External validity is constrained by the predominance of high-income settings and by reliance on instruments standardised primarily in Western populations. Finally, mixture and mechanistic evidence is uneven: chemical exposures were often modelled rather than personally monitored, and mediation claims rest on complex, underpowered models that require replication.

Scientifically speaking, this review provides a tempered, evidence-aligned reading of the results. First, associations are small and domain-specific: they are most consistent for early socioemotional/temperament phenotypes, whereas cognitive/language differences are smaller and frequently attenuate with rigorous adjustment; in stronger cohorts, certainty is capped at moderate. Second, risk is context-dependent: coexposure to chemical stressors and psychosocial distress yields modest amplification, while responsive caregiving, maternal sensitivity and partner support attenuate risk, underscoring actionable levers in routine care. Third, signals of greater sensitivity in early gestation and greater clinical impact among very preterm infants refine translational targets but should be interpreted cautiously. Progress towards causal inference will require quasi-experimental and within-family designs; dense, multitime point exposure assessment (including biomarkers and personal monitoring); harmonised, assessor-blinded outcomes at shared ages; preregistered analysis plans; and adequately powered tests of mechanistic and mixture pathways.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review indicates small, context-sensitive correlations between prenatal maternal psychological distress (stress, anxiety and depression) and early child neurodevelopment, rather than demonstrating causal effects. The overall pattern reflects domain-specific associations, with signals most frequently reported, but also the most vulnerable to single-informant reporting and attrition, were in socioemotional/temperament domains; these were down-weighted accordingly. Clinically, implications are calibrated: routine antenatal mental health screening and timely, evidence-based support are sensible, particularly early in pregnancy, without implying causality. In higher-risk contexts, pairing psychosocial care with exposure-reduction strategies and targeted developmental surveillance is warranted, especially for very preterm infants or families facing concentrated adversities. No assessment-related harms were reported, underscoring feasibility. Future work should increase temporal density of prenatal exposure measurement, adopt assessor-blinded, multi-informant outcomes at harmonised ages, and implement quasi-experimental or within-family designs to strengthen causal inference. Greater inclusion of under-represented populations and adequately powered mechanistic studies, ideally with individually monitored exposures, will improve generalisability and clarify modifiable pathways to healthier developmental trajectories.
