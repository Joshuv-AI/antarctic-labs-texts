Decomposition analysis of coverage of cervical cancer screening among Indian women within reproductive age-group: distribution and determinants of socioeconomic inequalities using a nationally representative survey
Authors: Pritam Halder, Divya Sharma, Ashish Behera, Arun Kumar Aggarwal, Anshul Mamgai, Pulak Syal, Seema Chopra, Tanvi Kiran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCervical cancer (CC) screening promotes early identification and treatment. Increasing participation in screening is difficult because of socioeconomic and cultural impacts. The objective was to estimate distribution and factors contributing to socioeconomic inequalities in CC screening across wealth index among women aged ≥ 30 at the national and subnational levels.MethodsSTATA-v17 was used to analyse the data from the National Family Health Survey-5 in India to estimate the coverage of CC screening among Indian women aged ≥ 30. Concentration index highlighted socio-economic disparities across states and union territories (UTs) based on wealth. Screening inequalities were recorded, stratified by residence, area, states, and UTs. Spatial map was used to depict the difference (richest-poorest) in coverage of CC screening (%) for states/UTs. Erreygers method was performed for further decomposition.ResultsThe overall coverage (1.96%) of CC screening raised as the wealth quintile increased (0.99 to 2.45%). Both high overall coverage and disparity (richest-poorest) was reported in Chandigarh, Punjab, Manipur, Mizoram, Maharashtra, and Kerala. The Inequalities of CC screening was concentrated among the wealthy: overall concentration index- 0.011 (0.010 to 0.013)). The north (0.009 (0.007 to 0.010)) and north-east (0.009 (0.007 to 0.010)) have the highest inequality. BMI (6.15%), number of living children (7.15%), education (9.17%), residential variance (14.17%), regional (21.37%), and non-exposure to media (49.21%) positively contributing to disparities, whereas regular fruit consumption (-7.25%) and caste variation (-10.99%) were negatively influencing factors.ConclusionIndia has a low overall screening adoption percentage. The socioeconomic disparities at different levels can be effectively addressed by early and regular screening, especially for the poor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02121-y.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cancer screening, Decomposition, Factors, Distribution, Determinants, India, NFHS, Socioeconomic inequality, Screening

Introduction

Gynaecological cancers are rising worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Cervical cancer is India’s second most common cancer and ranks fourth globally in terms of incidence and mortality [1, 2]. Nearly 90% of cervical cancer-related fatalities occurred in developing nations. In India, cervical cancer contributes to 10% of all female cancers, despite declining incidence rates. Over three-quarters of cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage [3]. The age-standardized incidence rate for cervical cancer in India was 9.0%, with a mortality rate of 8.7% [4]. A greater prevalence of the human papillomavirus (HPV) and lower socioeconomic position are associated with cervical cancer [5]. HPV 16 and 18 are the leading strains in India’s cervical cancer cases; prevention includes hygiene, barrier contraception, and vaccination [5, 6].

As part of its national health programs, India has set ambitious targets for cervical cancer screening. The Operational Guidelines of National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD: 2023–2030) state that all women between the ages of 30 and 65 should undergo a visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) screening through the network of Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centers at least once every five years [7].

Despite having a dedicated National Program, namely; NP-NCD, India still accounts for more than 25% of cervical cancer mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) revealing lack of proper nationwide screening [3, 6]. Despite of the National Cancer Control Programme’s (NCCP) current guidelines, screening coverage in India remains inadequate [8]. The fourth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-4) documented poor coverage of (29.8%) cervical examination in women aged 30–49, which dropped down to 1.9% in the fifth round of NFHS [9]. This might be due to taking proxy of cervical examination as cervical cancer screening in NFHS-4 while NFHS-5 conducted proper cervical cancer screening.

Timely detection of cervical cancer is impeded by insufficient screening services and a lack of knowledge [10]. Screening systematically identifies individuals at risk for disease before symptoms appear, enabling early intervention or further testing. It plays a vital role in reducing cervical cancer mortality and improving overall health through timely detection and prevention [11].

In 2016, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) recommended VIA screening every five years for women aged 30–65 under National Health Mission (NHM). Despite community outreach, cervical cancer screening in India remains opportunistic, lacking nationwide implementation and consistent accessibility [12]. Invitations to the eligible population are utilised in High income countries/HICs to conduct screening. On the contrary, it is dependent on individuals’ willingness to disclose risk factors to healthcare workers and avail screening facilities in nearby health clinics [13].

Low cervical cancer screening in India is influenced by psychological, structural, sociocultural, and knowledge-related barriers. As per Fundamental Cause Theory, socioeconomic status plays a key role—those with greater resources are more health-aware and proactive, leading to healthier choices and lower disease risk [14]– [15]. Socioeconomic disparity in cervical cancer screening in India remains underexplored. Despite increased coverage in NFHS-5, underlying factors and wealth-based inequalities across states and UTs lack detailed analysis, particularly using advanced methods like decomposition. Thus, we have conducted this research with objectives to estimate the distribution of coverage of cervical cancer screening among Indian women within reproductive age-group across wealth index, stratified into residence, region, states and union territories, to determine the socioeconomic inequalities of cervical cancer screening across wealth index and to estimate the contribution of different demographic, socioeconomic and health related factors to the socioeconomic inequalities.

Methods

Study design

The data was extracted from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-2019–2021) [16]. This nationally representative cross-sectional survey was carried out using a multi-phase, stratified cluster sampling methodology. Data on emerging family and health-related issues are gathered for the survey which provides solid evidence to support, monitor and evaluate ongoing national programmes and opens new avenues for finding unmet needs in the population [16].

Data source

The NFHS-5 survey collects essential data on family and health-related issues, helping monitor and evaluate national programs while identifying unmet needs in the population. It employs four types of questionnaires—Household, Woman’s, Man’s, and Biomarker—using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) in local languages. The Household Schedule gathers information on socioeconomic status, health insurance, sanitation practices, and mortality within the last three years. The Woman’s Schedules address topics such as marriage, fertility, contraception, healthcare, nutrition, sexual behavior, and gender roles. The Biomarker Questionnaire records physical measurements like height, weight, haemoglobin levels, blood pressure, and random blood glucose for children and women aged 15–49 years. The data from ‘Household’ and ‘Woman’s’ questionnaires of the NFHS-5 survey was used to elicit information pertaining to cervical cancer screening, demographic, socio-economic factors; and health related factors.

Missing value analysis and data pertaining to present study

As per the guidelines of the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India (MoHFW), women aged ≥ 30 years are recommended to undergo regular cervical cancer screening [17]. Thus, following this eligibility criterion and after adjusting missing variables by complete case analysis (row wise complete deletion) and outliers by blocked adaptive computationally efficient outlier nominators (the ‘bacon’ command in Stata), the final sample size came out to be 3,48,882. It begins by employing a distance metric such as the Mahalanobis distance to choose an initial group of observations devoid of outliers. This step guarantees that there are no outliers in the chosen selection. If an observation is not deemed an outlier using the same distance metric, the program then repeatedly adds it from the original dataset, one at a time. An outlier is identified when an observation’s distance exceeds a predetermined threshold (Fig. 1).

Outcome variable

The eligible women as mentioned in the NFHS-5 Survey, were asked- “ever undergone a screening test for cervical cancer?” The answers were recorded in dichotomous format- “no, yes.” Therefore, the outcome variable is dichotomous in nature and was recorded as self-reported cervical cancer screening.

Explanatory variables

Explanatory variables were categorised in three categories; ‘demographic and socio-economic factors’; ‘health related factors’ and ‘behaviour factors’. Within ‘demographic and socio-economic factors’, we incorporated age-group (30–34/35–39/40–44/45–49 years), religion (Hindu/Muslim/Christian/Others), Caste (schedules caste/scheduled tribe/OBC/other backward caste and others), wealth quintile (poorest/poorer/middle/richer/richest), education status (illiterate/primary/secondary/higher secondary), head of household’s gender (male/female), marital status (married/others), age of first intercourse (< 18/18–29/≥30 years), living children (< = 2/>2), health insurance (no/yes) and region (north/central/east/northeast/west/south). Within ‘health related factors’, we incorporated- Body Mass Index or BMI (Underweight (< 18.5)/Normal (18.5–22.9)/Overweight (23.0-24.9)/Obesity (≥ 25.0)), diabetes status (no/yes/don’t know), hypertension status (no/yes/don’t know). Within ‘behavioural factors’, we incorporated, frequency of fruit consumption (never daily/weekly/occasionally), frequency of fried food consumption (never/daily/weekly/occasionally), smoking or tobacco usage (no/yes), alcohol consumption (no/yes) and media exposure i.e. radio or television or phone (no/yes) [16].

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using STATA software version 17 [18]. The bivariate analysis was conducted to describe in detail the coverage of cervical cancer prevalence with respect to various demographic, socio-economic, health related and behavioural factors. Appropriate survey weights were used. Distribution of coverage of cervical cancer across wealth index was documented as per residence, region, state and union territory using dot plot, radar chart and spatial maps using Microsoft Excel version 2021.

Socioeconomic inequality

The socioeconomic inequalities were disaggregated across wealth index using concentration index [19]. As per the standard guidelines [5, 6], the area between the concentration curve and the line of equality was computed by first plotting the cumulative proportion of the eligible population ranked by wealth index against the cumulative proportion of cervical cancer screening coverage. A concentration index of zero reflects ‘no socioeconomic inequality’, whereas a negative value depicts that prevalence is distributed more among the poorer participants and in contrast a positive value depicts that cervical cancer screening coverage was distributed more among the richer participants. Higher value of concentration index shows greater inequality (both in negative and positive directions). The distribution of disparities or inequalities in cervical cancer screening coverage were measured by difference between richest and poorest (richest-poorest); and ratio (richest/poorest). These was stratified into urban and rural areas with the help of spatial maps. We have categorised the states and union territories (UTs) as low/L (0-33rd percentile), medium/M (34-67th percentile) and high/H (68-100th percentile) as per cervical cancer screening: coverage and difference (poorest-richest); which was further cross tabulated.

Ethics

The NFHS-5 received ethical approval from the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai (2019–21). It was also reviewed by the ICF International Review Board (IRB), which approved it ethically. After receiving complete information about the goal and methodology of the survey, the respondents signed to confirm their agreement. Interviews were conducted only after receiving each participant’s informed consent. Being an anonymous dataset it is publicly available on the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Programme website that cannot be used to identify survey respondents- (https://dhsprogram.com/Countries/CountryMain.cfm?ctry_id=57&c=India).

Results

In the entire nation, 1.96% of women in the reproductive age group had undergone cervical cancer screening. 20.84% of participants were the richest overall, while 18.01% were the poorest. The richest people in urban areas (45.35%) were greater than the poorest (2.68%). The richest participants (8.53%) were less common than the poorest (25.72%) in rural areas, on the other hand. The coverage of cervical cancer screening rose as the wealth quintile increased: in rural areas (0.97 to 2.36%), in urban areas (1.23 to 2.49%), and overall (0.99 to 2.45%) (Table 1).

Discussion

There are relatively few nationally representative studies that look at the distribution of coverage of cervical cancer screening among Indian women within reproductive age-group across wealth index, stratified into residence, region, states and union territories, despite the fact that cancer is becoming more and more common in India. There are more women in this age group who are in the Government of India’s recommended age bracket. Additionally, they make up the majority of the population’s economically productive age group. Knowing the current state of cervical cancer screening, it is pertinent to determine the socioeconomic inequalities across wealth index and the significantly contributing factors that will eventually aid in evidence-based planning, especially considering the early start of NCDs in India and guidelines requiring cancer screening to be provided at public health centres.

The nationwide coverage of cervical cancer screening among the women aged 30 years and above was 1.96% (95% CI: 1.92–1.99). The coverage was higher among the participants aged 15 years and above (overall: 1.9% [21]. This might be because of the higher health awareness of the participants showing higher effectiveness of population based screening (PBS) under NP-NCD [17].

Low coverage of cervical cancer screening was documented in Eastern and Northeastern states. Both West Bengal and Assam (0.20%) had the lowest overall coverage. These areas confront difficulties with awareness, accessibility, and infrastructure for healthcare. Remote and rural locations might not have access to screening resources or qualified staff. Certain states in the Northeast and East have greater rates of poverty [22]. Preventive care is frequently inaccessible to low-income communities. Screening may be discouraged by cultural norms and false beliefs about cervical cancer. Campaigns for awareness must address these obstacles. Coverage is impacted by uneven screening program implementation throughout states. Improving access in low coverage areas requires focused initiatives [23].

In view of socio-economic disparity, the coverage of cervical cancer screening was tilted towards the rich (concentration index: 0.011) This inequality was highest in the north (0.009) and north-east region (0.009)Screening was concentrated towards rich in Mizoram (coverage: 6.96%, concentration index: 0.104)This might be due to higher health literacy, awareness, accessibility and affordability of healthcare infrastructure among the rich. Financial obstacles prevent women with lower income from receiving healthcare. They might be hindered from being screened by copayments, lack of insurance, and travel expenses [24].

As per the results of the decomposition analysis of socio-economic inequalities of cervical cancer, the positively contributing factors were non-exposure to media (49.21%), regional (21.37%), residential (14.17%) and educational variations (9.17%), number of living children (7.15%) and BMI (6.15%). Those with exposure to media and higher levels of education typically possess more health literacy and understanding of preventative actions. They are more willing to seek out screenings because they see their value. Access to healthcare services and cost are positively correlated with higher education [25].

The following factors can have a major influence on the adoption of cervical cancer screening in different regions and residential areas; (a) Access to Healthcare Services, (b) Socioeconomic Factors [11], (c) Cultural and Social Norms [24], (d) Healthcare Policies and Programs, (e) Transportation & Distance [26] and (f) Education and Awareness [27].

The number of living children can have an impact on the uptake of cervical cancer screening for many reasons: (a) Greater Number of Living Children and Time Restrictions, (b) Healthcare Prioritisation [28], (c) Economic Factors: It may be more difficult for larger families to access healthcare services, including screenings, due to financial restrictions. Higher BMI can positively contribute to increased socio-economic inequalities in the screening coverage due to multi-fold factors like; (a) Embarrassment due to Higher BMI, (b) Perceived Health Risks: People may believe that having a higher BMI increases the chance of developing certain illnesses, which makes them prioritise treating such problems over getting screened for them. Targeted actions are needed to remove these obstacles, such as flexible scheduling of appointments, childcare during visits, and making sure that medical facilities are accepting and judgment-free [29, 30].

Negatively contributing factors were variation in caste (−10.99%), regular consumption of fruits (−7.25%). This might be due to the fact that there might be lesser coverage of screening in the marginalised, lower castes irrespective of their financial status, probably due to regional, cultural belief and practice [31]. In marginalised castes, lower educational attainment, awareness, limited access to healthcare facility might result in a decrease in knowledge of the significance of preventive healthcare and cervical cancer screening [32]. The higher health consciousness in regular fruit consuming participants might lead to regular health monitoring with higher screening and reduced inequalities in cervical cancer screening coverage [30, 31].

The concentration index for caste (0.140) favours the concentration of screening in the higher socio-economic class and the elasticity coefficient of caste is negative (−0.269), which indicates that wherever the caste plays a dominant role in screenings, there is lesser coverage of screening in the marginalised, lower castes irrespective of their financial status, probably due to regional, cultural belief and practice. Further, the concentration index for food consumption is negative (−0.167), indicative of the concentration of screening in the lower socio-economic class- which indicates that with higher health consciousness in regular fruit consuming participants among lower socio-economic class might lead to regular health monitoring with higher screening and reduced inequalities in cervical cancer screening coverage. Additionally, the reason for non-exposure to media contributing nearly 50% to inequality is more of a statistical one, as this is based on a complex interplay of multiple factors (variables) thrown together in the decomposition analysis of over 3 lakh data values used in the present study.

The concentration of screening among affluent populations underscores the need for targeted interventions in primary healthcare (PHC) reforms, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMCIs). Recent PHC innovations in India, such as the expansion of Health and Wellness Centers (HWCs) under Ayushman Bharat, aim to bridge these gaps by improving accessibility and affordability of preventive healthcare [33, 34]. However, factors like education, residential variance, and media exposure continue to shape disparities, necessitating a more inclusive approach that integrates community-based awareness programs and equitable healthcare delivery [35, 36]. Strengthening PHC infrastructure, increase female healthcare providers, program availability and addressing regional disparities can enhance screening coverage and reduce inequalities in cancer prevention [37, 38].

Strengths and limitations

The study is one of its kinds which to a large extent fills the under researched areas of distribution of coverage of cervical cancer screening among Indian women within reproductive age-group across wealth index, stratified into residence, region, states and union territories. It further uses the rigorous statistical approach of decomposition analysis to explore the different demographic, socioeconomic and health related drivers of socioeconomic inequalities in the screening coverage [39]. Further, the present study being a nationally representative study with substantially large number participants provide an advantage of better generalizability of the results [40, 41]. The study despite of its uniqueness also faces some limitations. Being a cross-sectional study design, while only associations can be studies, the causality of demographic, socioeconomic and health related drivers of socioeconomic inequalities cannot be established. Due to self-reporting nature of the questionnaire used in the NFHS-5, the recall bias and social desirability biases may occur [42, 43]. We were not able to estimate the contribution of cultural variables in this manuscript due to lack of original dataset. The cervical screening rollouts may differ sub-nationally and the screening adoption rates could have been better in specific Indian states due to the presence of state-specific screening programmes, the impacts of which were too substantial for this study to measure [17]. More such longitudinal decomposition analysis should be undertaken to analyse and comment upon the increasing/decreasing trend in the overall concentration index [44–48].

Conclusion

Increasing cervical cancer screening among Indian women of reproductive age is essential for its early detection and prevention. Even with the presence of national level operational guidelines and screening services being offered at public health institutions, India has a low overall screening adoption percentage. The socioeconomic disparities at different levels can be effectively addressed by early and regular screening and treatment, especially for the poor. The cervical cancer screening coverage can be enhanced through community outreach efforts by partnering with local health organizations, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and community leaders to conduct awareness campaigns about cervical cancer and the importance of regular screening. Concentrated efforts to educate about the Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) vaccine and its role in sizably reducing the risk of cervical cancer, particularly for younger women before they become sexually active can go a long way in reducing the cancer prevalence. Usage of sensible media exposure, telemedicine and setting up of mobile health camps in rural and underserved areas can make the screenings more accessible to women who might not be able to visit a healthcare facility. Further, the formation of the peer support groups can be promoted wherein the women who have undergone screening can share their positive experiences with others to reduce stigma, fear, anxiety and increase the acceptance of cervical cancer screening.
