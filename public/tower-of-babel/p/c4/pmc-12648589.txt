Impact of L-citrulline on meat production and quality: A study on its effects on meat attributes and nutritional composition
Authors: Md Ismile Hossain Bhuiyan, Md Jisan Ahmed, Mustakim Mamun, Khairul Islam, Ritu Chalise, Prajwal Bhandari, Kazi Estieque Alam, Israt Jahan Kaderi, Md Jahangir Alam, Delower Hossain, Mahabbat Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmino acids are fundamental in sustaining physiological homeostasis during stress conditions. l-citrulline (l-Cit), a non-essential amino acid (NEAA) and metabolic precursor of arginine, has been reported to exert beneficial effects on the health and performance of broiler chickens.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with varying inclusion levels of l-Cit on growth performance, meat quality, plasma biochemical indices, and thermoregulatory responses in broiler chickens.MethodsA total of 120 one-day-old chickens of the Lohman meat (Indian River) strain were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments, and each treatment included 30 birds in 3 replicates each. A 7-day feeding trial was conducted, beginning at 21 days of age. The control group (T0) was fed only a basal diet, while l-Cit was administered through the drinking water at concentrations of 0.5 % (T1), 0.75 % (T2), and 1.0 % (T3) to the respective treatment groups, in addition to the basal diet.ResultsDietary inclusion of l-Cit significantly affected broiler performance and physiology. The T2 group demonstrated the highest body weight gain and lowest feed conversion ratio (FCR) compared with T0, T1, and T3 (p < 0.05). Organ development was increased in T2, with significant increases in bursa, heart, and liver indices, alongside reduced abdominal fat (p < 0.05). Meat quality improved through reduced drip loss and elevated muscle pH, particularly in T2. Plasma biochemical analysis showed lower cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL in all l-Cit groups, with optimal in T2 (p < 0.05), while HDL and uric acid remained unaffected. Moreover, rectal temperature decreased significantly across l-Cit groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionOverall, this study suggests that a diet supplemented with 0.75 % l-Cit (T2), which could be a promising amino acid without affecting meat quality or consumer preferences, may improve growth performance, meat quality, the lipid profile (blood plasma), and thermoregulation in broilers.

Introduction

The poultry industry has emerged as one of the most thriving and dynamic sectors globally, playing a vital role in meeting the rising demand for economic and safe animal protein in the form of meat and eggs (Gerber et al., 2007). Owing to its relatively greater growth rate (5 %) than that of beef (2 %) and pork (3 %) in the livestock sector of global meat production, it has emerged as the fastest-growing sector (Taha, 2003). Like underdeveloped nations, Bangladesh has a long history of growing chickens by scavenging or backyard farming methods. Approximately 89 % of rural families raise chickens, with an average of 6.2 chickens per household (Begum et al., 2011).

Since poultry meat accounts for a significant portion of Bangladesh's total meat production (37 %), it is one of the primary sources of daily protein and nutritional supply, which makes poultry subsectors a vital means of reducing malnutrition, enhancing agricultural growth, and improving people's diet (Begum et al., 2011; Raihan and Mahmud, 2008). Currently, broiler chickens exhibit a rapid growth rate, increased muscle mass, and a high feed conversion ratio (FCR) (Zahoor et al., 2017). Additionally, these birds have a high metabolic rate, poor heat tolerance, and low heat dissipation capabilities, and are extremely sensitive to temperature fluctuations in their surroundings (Uyanga et al., 2022). In poultry production, stress can be triggered by multiple factors, including environmental conditions, nutritional imbalances, suboptimal management practices, pathogenic infections, and various disease states (Surai and Fisinin, 2016).

Heat stress (HS) is a significant environmental stressor affecting poultry production and has detrimental impacts on the growth, health, production, and welfare of birds (Saeed et al., 2019). It disrupts nutrient metabolism, which leads to significant changes in the concentrations of blood components such as glucose, insulin, protein, total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (Wang et al., 2021). In addition, it also alters amino acid (AA) metabolism in poultry by regulating the concentration of free amino acids (Ito et al., 2015). However, AAs are vital nutrients involved in essential physiological functions such as protein synthesis, tissue repair, the immune response, and metabolic regulation (Cai et al., 2020). They serve as precursors for various molecules and development, improve metabolic health and homeostasis, and support growth via protein synthesis (Uyanga et al., 2022; Wu, 2013).

Among these different AAs, l-citrulline (l-Cit) is a non-essential amino acid (NEAA) and a potent precursor for arginine synthesis, which is essential in poultry nutrition for growth and development (Bahri et al., 2013; Curis et al., 2005). l-Cit is an important intermediate of the urea cycle and has been identified as a biomarker of HS in poultry, as prolonged thermal exposure reduces its plasma concentration in the brain and liver. Dietary supplementation with l-Cit has been shown to increase thermotolerance in young chicks by mitigating the adverse effects of HS (Chowdhury, 2019). Moreover, it can also reduce oxidative stress in the body by activating antioxidant enzymes (Van De Poll et al., 2007). l-Cit is involved in organ function and cellular metabolism in the majority of mammals (Curis et al., 2005). l-Cit reportedly increases peripheral nitric oxide (NO) levels in both chickens and mammals by promoting the synthesis of l-arginine, thereby improving vasodilation, blood flow, and overall metabolic and immune functions (Uyanga et al., 2022). Studies have shown that heat exposure in chicks leads to a reduction in blood citrulline (Cit) levels (Chowdhury et al., 2014), and oral supplementation with l-Cit has been found to lower rectal temperature (RT) in these chicks without significantly altering plasma nitric oxide (NO) concentrations (Chowdhury et al., 2017).

Citrulline, also called carbamylornithine, is a nonprotein α-amino acid that is synthesized mainly by intestinal cells under the action of ornithine carbamoyltransferase on glutamine. It is transported in the blood to the kidneys, where it generates arginine under the catalytic action of arginine succinate synthetase and arginine succinate cleavage enzymes (Moinard and Cynober, 2007). In the body, citrulline (Cit) is metabolized primarily by arginine and NO, leading to the production of NO and polyamines as its final products by NO synthase, respectively, which can trigger the activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway (Tsuboi et al., 2018). NO is well known as a regulator of body temperature (De Luca et al., 1995; Osaka, 2010). l-Cit has multiple physiological benefits, including improving immunity (Cai et al., 2020), decreasing the proportion of senescent cells (Tsuboi et al., 2018), lowering blood glucose and lipids (Danboyi et al., 2020), mitigating muscle wasting, and improving exercise performance (Eshreif et al., 2020). However, owing to the absence of the enzyme carbamoyl phosphate synthetase, which is essential for synthesizing citrulline from ornithine, chickens are unable to produce sufficient amounts of endogenous citrulline (Tamir and Ratner, 1963)

However, less attention has been given to Cit supplementation, which could be beneficial for chickens under HS, as l-Cit supplementation can efficiently augment the arginine supply in poultry (Uyanga et al., 2022). l-Cit can be produced at a low cost and has a price advantage over arginine. (Ma et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the effects of the oral administration of l-Cit on overall AA metabolism in chickens are not known. Additionally, it is not known whether orally administered Cit can bypass the blood‒brain barrier and enter the brain (Chowdhury et al., 2022). Moreover, it is still not known whether l-Cit-dependent hypothermia can provide thermotolerance in any species (Chowdhury et al., 2014). l-Cit has rarely been studied as a supplement in poultry nutrition. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the effects of dietary l-Cit supplementation on growth performance and production traits in broilers; assess meat quality parameters such as pH, drip loss, glucose, LDL, HDL, TC, and triglyceride concentrations; and identify the optimal inclusion level of l-Cit in broiler diets.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All experimental procedures involving broiler chickens were conducted in accordance with the animal welfare management protocol approved by the Animal Ethics Review Board, Faculty of Biological Sciences, University of Chittagong (Ref: AERB/FBS/UC/3A, 2025). Ethical approval was granted by the Animal Ethics Review Board, ensuring adherence to established standards for the humane treatment and care of animals throughout the study.

Birds and housing

A total of 120 one-day-old Indian River broiler chicks of mixed sex, with an average body weight of 41.4 g, were procured from a commercial hatchery (Kazi Hatchery, Gazipur, Dhaka). Chicks were weighed and randomly assigned to 12 floor pens (each measuring 0.8 × 0.9 m²), with 10 chicks per pen. Each pen was bedded with rice husk and equipped with a heat lamp, a drinker, and a feeder, allowing the chicks free access to both water and feed throughout the study. The temperature and lighting conditions in the room were maintained according to the breeder's guidelines, with average temperatures between 30 and 32°C and a relative humidity of 75–80 %.

L-citrulline supplementation through drinking water

l-Cit was administered through drinking water to evaluate its effects on broiler growth performance, blood biochemical profiles, meat quality, and thermoregulation. The study design has four experimental groups, including a control group (T0) and three treatment groups (T1, T2, and T3). The broilers in the T0 group were not supplied with l-Cit throughout the experimental period, whereas those in the T1, T2, and T3 groups were provided with 0.5 %, 0.75 %, and 1.0 % l-Cit, respectively, from day 21 to day 28. Water was supplied via durable plastic tanks with secure lids for each group.

Experimental diets

Starter feed (provided from day 1 to day 14) and grower feed (from day 15 to day 28) were formulated according to the nutritional requirements for Indian River broilers. Growth performance

Growth performance was evaluated in two phases: before l-Cit supplementation (days 1–20) and during the supplementation period (days 21–28). Daily health monitoring was implemented, and deceased birds were promptly removed. Feed intake (FI) was monitored daily, with residual feed quantified weekly to determine precise consumption (excluding deceased individuals). Individual body weights (BWs) were recorded, and FI was measured for each experimental unit. These data were used to calculate the FI and FCR. FI and FCR were determined via the following formulas:FI(g/bird)=FIinareplicationNumberofbirdsinareplicationFCR=Feedintake(g)Weightgain(g)

Blood glucose and uric acid level measurement

At 28 days of age, blood glucose and uric acid (UA) levels were measured from three replicates (3 broilers/replicates; n = 9) from each group. The feed was withheld for 6 hours before sampling, with ad libitum access to water. Approximately 3 mL of blood was collected from the brachial (wing) vein via a sterile needle and transferred into sodium fluoride–oxalate–coated tubes to prevent glycolysis. Both glucose and UA concentrations were determined via the same point-of-care meter [Tyson Bio TB200, Tyson Bioresearch, Inc., Taiwan], which employs different specialized test strips for each analyte according to the manufacturer's protocols. For glucose measurement, a 1 µL drop of whole blood was applied to a glucose test strip, and the reading was recorded within approximately 5 seconds. For UA measurement, a separate UA-specific strip was used, and a 1 µL drop of whole blood was applied; the UA concentration was recorded within 20–30 minutes. During the entire procedure, the broilers were gently restrained to minimize stress and ensure animal welfare.

Blood lipid profile measurements

Blood lipid profiles, including triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and total cholesterol (TC), were determined from the same blood samples used for glucose and uric acid analyses. The measurements were performed using an automated biochemical analyzer (AU480 Chemical Analyzer, Beckman Coulter Inc., USA).

Slaughter performance

After blood sampling, the three replicates (3 broilers/replicates; n = 9) from each group (the same broilers for uric acid and glucose level analysis) were humanely slaughtered by cervical dislocation, followed by exsanguination, in compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals in research. Carcasses were immediately defeathered and eviscerated, and the breast and thigh muscles, along with internal organs (liver, heart, spleen, and bursa), were collected for further assessment of meat quality parameters and organ indices.

Meat & internal organ quality assessment

At 28 days of age, meat quality was assessed via three replicates (3 broilers/replicates; n = 9) from each group, which were the same birds previously used for blood analysis. The pH of the breast muscle was measured three times per sample, 15 minutes post-slaughter, using a pH meter [FiveGo F2, Mettler Toledo Instruments, China] to assess the initial rate of post-mortem acidification. The internal organs, including the bursa, liver, heart, and spleen, as well as the abdominal fat, were carefully removed and weighed, and their organ indices were calculated via the following formula:OrganIndex(%)=OrganWeight(g)BodyWeight(g)×100

Drip loss of the breast and thigh muscles was measured 24 hours after slaughter, following the procedure outlined by Zhang et al. (2014). The calculation for drip loss was performed via the following formula:DripLoss=W1−W2W1×100

Here, W1 = initial weight, and W2 = weight after 24 hrs. at 4°C storage.

The moisture (%), dry matter (%), crude protein (CP%), crude fiber (CF%), crude fat or ether extract (EE%), total ash (%), and protein solubility (PS%) in breast meat were determined following the procedures described by Helrich (1990). Crude protein, total ash, crude fiber, crude fat, and nitrogen-free extract (NFE) were expressed on a dry matter basis.

Measurement of body temperature

A digital thermometer (BPL DT 03 Flexible Tip Digital Thermometer; BPL Medical Technologies Pvt. Ltd., India) with ±0.1°C accuracy was used to record the rectal temperature of the broiler. Temperature readings were obtained three times at 0-, 60-, and 120-minutes following l-citrulline supplementation at the specified dose. Measurements were recorded from nine birds per treatment group, with three replicates per group.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via a randomized complete block design (RCBD). Differences among treatment means were evaluated for statistical significance via analysis of variance (ANOVA) implemented in R (v4.5.0). Additionally, regression (linear, cubic, and quadratic) models were run to study dose‒dependent responses. When significant differences were detected, a least significant difference (LSD) test at the 5 % significance level was performed via the ‘agricolae’ package (de Mendiburu and de Mendiburu, 2019). Moreover, graphs were generated via the ‘ggplot2’ package (Wickham et al., 2016). All values are presented as the means ± SEs.

Discussion

Supplementing diets with specific AAs, such as l-Cit, can help mitigate heat stress, potentially alleviating its adverse effects on bird behavior and performance, thereby improving feed intake and nutrient utilization. In this study, the addition of l-Cit to the diet of Indian River broilers improved growth, resulting in higher live weights and lower FCR values. It also helped reduce HS, as evidenced by a steady decrease in abdominal fat as the dose increased. The improvement in production performance resulting from l-Cit supplementation may be linked to its direct role in the urea cycle. We also observed that dietary addition of 0.75 % (T2) or 1.0 % (T3) l-Cit significantly reduced triglyceride levels. Reducing blood triglyceride levels in broiler chickens can provide several physiological and production-related benefits, ultimately increasing overall health and performance. Elevated triglyceride concentrations are associated with increased abdominal fat deposition, which can negatively impact carcass quality and feed efficiency.

In the intense summer heat, Indian River broilers suffer from heat stress; their growth is stunted, and feed intake becomes inconsistent. All chickens were raised under identical dietary and environmental conditions, and the current investigation revealed that, after up to 21 days, BW gain, FI, and the FCR did not differ among the groups. This outcome aligns with previous studies where similar feed and rearing conditions yielded no significant differences in early-stage broiler performance (Gulizia and Downs, 2021; Netto et al., 2019). This could be attributed to the fact that young broiler chicks, particularly during the first three weeks of life, are affected mainly by their baseline nutrition and management. In the absence of external interventions, consistency in their diet and environment promotes uniform physiological development (Gous, 2018). The FI, BW gain, and FCR significantly differed among the broiler groups receiving various dietary levels of l-Cit for up to 28 days, with the 0.75 % (T2) supplementation group exhibiting the most efficient feed utilization, as reflected by the lowest FCR. These findings align with previous studies, where at 42 days, the FCR was lower in the l-Cit-treated group than in the T0 (Ma et al., 2025). This improvement in FCR is due to l-Cit serving as a precursor for arginine and enhancing NO production, which promotes better nutrient absorption, gut health, and protein synthesis (Fathima et al., 2023).

The current investigation revealed that l-Cit had a significant effect on the different organ weights of broilers. The weights of the liver, heart, bursa, and spleen increased as l-Cit supplementation increased, peaking in the 0.75 % (T2) group but then beginning to decrease in the 1.0 % l-Cit group. L‑Citrulline enhances immune organ development and reduces oxidative stress through improved antioxidant enzyme activity, facilitating healthier organ growth (Uyanga et al., 2021). An enlarged bursa of Fabricius in treated birds typically signals enhanced B‑cell development, indicating stronger antibody responses after vaccination or infection, which suggests healthier organs and a more effective adaptive immune system (Cazaban et al., 2015). Despite the observed changes in other organ weights, we detected slight alterations in the organ indices, such as those of the bursa and spleen. Consistent with the current findings, Uyanga et al. (2021) reported that l-Cit supplementation affects the heart and liver. In contrast, its effect on the spleen was either negligible or progressively diminished. Another important finding is that l-Cit supplementation significantly reduced the abdominal fat content in broilers, particularly in the 0.75 % (T2) group. This finding was also reported by Fouad et al. (2013), who reported that l-arginine, which is produced from l-Cit, significantly reduced the abdominal fat content. This reduction in fat deposition is associated with improved feed efficiency, increased dressing percentage, and enhanced overall health and metabolic efficiency in broilers (Liu et al., 2019). These findings suggest that l-Cit supplementation can contribute to the production of leaner carcasses and better production performance in poultry farming.

Meat quality depends on multifactorial attributes influenced by genetic, nutritional, and environmental conditions, with nutritional strategies playing a pivotal role in optimizing postmortem muscle traits. Among various dietary strategies, AAs such as l-Cit have gained attention for their ability to modulate muscle metabolism, increase oxidative stability, and promote protein turnover through the NO and urea cycles. In this context, Table 5 summarizes the impact of different dietary l-Cit inclusion levels on key meat quality traits in broiler chickens, including pH, drip loss, moisture, dry matter, crude protein, crude fat, protein solubility, and NFEs. Our study revealed that the pH increased as the level of l-Cit increased, reaching the highest level in the 0.75 % l-Cit (T2) group. However, a study by Ma et al. (2025) revealed no significant difference in pH with l-Cit supplementation. This discrepancy may stem from the more extended rearing period in Ma et al. (2025) (up to 65 days) than in our 28‑day trial, along with differing environmental conditions, which could influence the physiological responses of the birds to L‑citrulline supplementation. Our study revealed decreased drip loss in both thigh and breast meat across all l-Cit-treated groups, particularly in the 0.75 % group (T2), indicating increased meat tenderness and juiciness (Sun et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with an earlier study where drip loss was found to be lower in groups treated with 1 % and 1.5 % l-Cit (Ma et al., 2025). This could be attributed to L‑citrulline increasing systemic arginine concentrations, which enhances NO production, leading to improved blood circulation and antioxidant capacity in muscle tissue, factors that are recognized to decrease drip loss and maintain meat juiciness (Fathima et al., 2024). In terms of moisture, our study revealed no significant differences in moisture content among the treatment groups. These results align with those of a previous study by Ma et al. (2025). In our study, significant differences were observed in the fat content of the breast meat across all the treatment groups, with the 0.5 % l-Cit (T1) group exhibiting the highest crude fat content. This finding contrasts with those of Uyanga, Zhao, et al. (2022) and Ma et al. (2025), who reported a reduction in muscle fat content concomitant with improved protein deposition under HS in broilers supplemented with 1 % l-Cit (T3). This may be due to varying levels of l-Cit affecting the pathways involved in lipid metabolism.

Plasma biochemical markers reveal how broilers respond to changes in their diet, showing effects on protein use, fat metabolism, and overall health. In our study, triglyceride levels were lower in the 0.75 % (T2) and 1 % l-Cit (T3) groups than in T0, whereas the 0.5 % l-Cit (T1) group presented higher triglyceride levels than did the T0. This may be because higher l-Cit regulates enzymes such as NO synthase, stimulating lipolysis and improving triglyceride clearance, resulting in lower plasma triglyceride levels (Uyanga, Zhao, et al., 2022). Another study by Ma et al. (2025) reported higher triglyceride levels in the l-Cit-treated group than in T0, which contradicts our findings. These differences likely stem from variations in environmental conditions and rearing duration. Ma et al. (2025) extended broiler growth to 65 days, whereas our study concluded at 28 days. In our study, the l-Cit-treated group presented lower LDL levels than did T0. Elevated LDL cholesterol is associated with an increased risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases (Roy, 2014). Therefore, maintaining lower LDL levels is generally considered beneficial for health. The observed reduction in LDL in the l-Cit-treated group, especially the 0.75 % l-Cit (T2) group, aligns with this principle, indicating that this supplementation level may positively influence lipid profiles in broilers. In terms of HDL cholesterol levels, the 0.75 % l-Cit (T2) group presented the highest values, followed by the 1 % (T3), 0.5 % (T1), and control (T0) groups. These results align with previous findings (Fouad et al., 2013), in which the 0.5 % l-arginine-treated (T1) group presented a higher HDL content than did the control. HDL helps remove excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and carries it to the liver for excretion, thus reducing the risk of heart disease and stroke (Diaz and Bielczyk-Maczynska, 2025). The glucose level was greater in the T2 and T3 than in the other groups. This result supports the findings of Uyanga et al. (2021), who reported that the l-Cit group presented increased glucose levels under both heat-stressed and thermoneutral conditions. However, these results contradict the findings of Ma et al. (2025). This difference may be related to the extended rearing period (63 days) used in their study, which contrasts with the shorter grow-out durations used in our research. In our study, TC levels were elevated in T0 compared with those in the l-Cit groups, whereas T2 group presented a lower cholesterol level. This study is supported by a previous study in which higher total cholesterol levels were detected in the l-Cit- and l-arginine-treated groups (Fouad et al., 2013; Uyanga et al., 2021). The cholesterol-reducing effect observed at T2 suggests that this level may best support lipid metabolism, possibly by increasing NO-related cholesterol removal by the liver or limiting its production. In our study, the levels of UA in the different treatment groups varied; the level of UA was greater in the l-Cit-treated group. These findings were supported by those of Dao et al. (2021), who reported that laying hens fed an l-Cit–supplemented diet presented higher serum UA concentrations than did those fed a standard protein diet, an effect not observed in our broiler study. Increased arginine availability enhances NOS activity, and since birds eliminate excess nitrogen, chiefly UA, this metabolic shift likely leads to increased urate production (Castro and Kim, 2020).

Monitoring RT provides a reliable measure of thermoregulatory adaptation in broilers, especially under HS. Dietary l-Cit has emerged as a promising thermoregulatory agent, potentially mediating its effects through NO–driven vasodilation and modulation of central heat shock responses. In our study, at baseline (0 minute), all treatment groups had slightly elevated RTs relative to the control. However, after 60 and 120 minutes of exposure, significant differences emerged. At 60 minutes the 0.75 % (T2) and 0.5 % (T1) l-Cit groups presented a significant decrease in RT, with the 0.75 % (T2) group having the lowest values. This lower temperature persisted at 120 minutes; with both groups maintaining a significantly reduced RT compared with the T0 and T3 groups. These results align with previous findings that L‑Cit, a novel hypothermic AA, can significantly lower body temperature under both thermoneutral and heat-stressed conditions (Chowdhury et al., 2015, 2017; Tran et al., 2019; Uyanga et al., 2021). This is due to l-Cit boosting total NO synthase activity in the hypothalamus, which helps widen blood vessels and improve blood flow, allowing more body heat to escape and thereby reducing the core temperature (Uyanga et al., 2021).

Although this study included a control group and provided valuable insights into how varying dietary levels of l-Cit influence broiler performance, meat quality, organ development, plasma markers, and thermoregulation, several limitations should be considered. First, testing only three fixed supplementation levels may have overlooked a better dose. The research was also confined to a single broiler strain and a standard production timeline, which could limit how broadly the results apply to other genetic lines or farming practices. While RT measurements were taken, the lack of a controlled HS scenario means that the study falls short of fully exploring the potential of l-Cit in mitigating heat-related challenges commonly faced in commercial settings. Furthermore, organ weight assessments have not been supported by histological or molecular analyses, leaving questions about the biological mechanisms involved unanswered. Meat quality evaluations focused mainly on nutritional aspects but did not assess critical sensory traits such as color, tenderness, flavor, or oxidative stability, which are all crucial for consumer acceptance. The long-term health impacts and cost-effectiveness of supplementation were also not addressed. Finally, although a total of 120 birds were included, the relatively small number per group might have limited the ability to detect subtle but significant differences. T0 strengthen future investigations, it would be beneficial to explore a broader range of doses, including diverse broiler genotypes, incorporate stress models, conduct deeper physiological examinations, and analyze economic feasibility to increase the practical relevance and robustness of the findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the effects of different dietary inclusion levels of l-Cit in broiler chickens from 21 days of age until the slaughter age of 28 days. All treatment groups were fed the same basal diet, with l-Cit supplemented at 0 %, 0.5 %, 0.75 %, and 1.0 % in groups T0, T1, T2, and T3, respectively. This study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with l-Cit in broiler chickens offers significant benefits in terms of growth performance, carcass quality, physiological development, and overall health. It significantly improved growth rates and feed conversion efficiency, likely due to increased nutrient and energy utilization. Additionally, it led to a reduction in abdominal fat, resulting in leaner carcasses that better align with consumer preferences for healthier poultry products. Physiologically, it resulted in increased development of liver and heart tissues, indicating improved metabolic and cardiovascular function. It also demonstrated better thermoregulation, which may help mitigate HS and support consistent growth under challenging environmental conditions. Blood biochemical analyses revealed a more favorable lipid profile, characterized by reduced levels of triglycerides, LDL, and TC, along with an increase in HDL. These changes not only contribute to improved bird health but also have the potential to enhance the nutritional quality of meat. Meat quality parameters, including higher protein content, acceptable moisture levels, and superior protein solubility, were also enhanced, contributing to improved texture, tenderness, and water-holding capacity. Among all the treatments, the group receiving 0.75 % l-Cit (T2) was the most effective and balanced in enhancing broiler production performance, physiological resilience, and meat quality.
