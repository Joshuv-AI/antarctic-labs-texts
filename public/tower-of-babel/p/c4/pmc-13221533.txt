Evaluation of artificial intelligence-based cephalometric analysis compared to the manual method
Authors: Alfonso Alvarado-Lorenzo, Laura Criado-Pérez, Daniele Garcovich, Sabina Romero, Giuseppe Scuzzo, Giacomo Scuzzo, Adrián Curto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe application of artificial intelligence (AI) to cephalometry has aroused growing interest due to its potential to optimize clinical workflows and improve efficiency. This study aimed to compare the differences and reproducibility of cephalometric measurements obtained using three methods: manual, WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™.Methodology30 patients were analysed, evaluating 9 measurements generated by the three methods at two different timepoints (T1 and T2).ResultsWebCeph™ presented significant differences as compared to the manual method in 7 of the 9 measurements (SNA, SNB, ANB, U1–APg, L1–APg, GoGn–SN, U1–L1), while AngelAligner™ displayed significant differences with the manual method in 4 measurements (SNA, U1–APg, L1–APg and U1–L1). Significant differences were found between the two AI-based systems in 7 of the 9 measurements. Reproducibility was high at all angles with the manual method, except for U1–L1, NLA, and LFH. The ICC was 1 for all measurements for both AI-based systems, indicating optimal reproducibility. When grouping by malocclusions, however, WebCeph™ presented greater differences with the manual method than AngelAligner™.ConclusionAI-based methods offered greater reproducibility than the manual method, but still require human supervision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-026-00628-z.

Background

Cephalometric tracing plays a fundamental role in orthodontic diagnosis, planning and treatment [1, 2]. Cephalometry enables craniofacial structures to be assessed, and therefore the origin of the malocclusion to be identified and the craniofacial growth stage, as well as treatment-related changes, determined [1, 3]. To this end, it is essential that cephalometric points and planes be accurately located in order to measure the position and relationships between craniofacial and dental structures [1].

This can be achieved either through the manual technique, in which anatomical landmarks are identified on radiographic images and manually traced onto acetate paper, or through the use of more recently developed digital cephalometric analysis software [4–7]. Although the manual method is the most widely used, studies show that it requires a longer time to perform than computed assisted or AI-based cephalometric analyses [4, 8], and that it is subject to intraoperative errors [9, 10], being influenced by the clinical experience of the operator and the quality of the X-ray, among other factors [11].

Some of the latest software incorporates algorithm-based artificial intelligence (AI), a tool that is widely used in technology today [6, 8, 12–17] which offers the possibility of automating and improving the accuracy of this process, since it facilitates the acquisition and of images and speeds up measurement [8, 15, 18–21]. As a result, a wide variety of AI-based methods have emerged, and previous studies [6, 12, 21, 22] show that these analyses may be comparable to those performed manually. WebCeph™ is an online platform that enables AI-based cephalometric tracing, among other functionalities. Similary, AngelAligner™ has developed a system which integrates an Intelligent Cephalometry System (ICS) for this purpose. Both systems can be used free of charge, and perform cephalometric analysis quickly, thereby saving considerable time.

Despite its growing relevance, AI remains a complex tool, and current evidence is insufficient to support its use as a standardized standalone solution in clinical practice [8, 21, 23, 24]. Its implementation should therefore be approached with caution [8, 17, 25, 26]. Given the wide range of software solutions currently available [8, 12–14, 18, 19, 21, 27, 28], conducting an exhaustive analysis of all existing systems would be challenging. Nevertheless, continued research may contribute to their further development and optimization, as well as to the identification of the most appropriate methods for their safe and reliable clinical application. With this in mind, the aim of this study was to determine whether different cephalometric analysis methods (manual, WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™) yield different results, and to compare their reproducibility. The null hypothesis was that there would be no differences in the cephalometric measurements generated by the automated AI systems and the manual method.

Methods

Study design

Cephalometric tracing was performed by means of three different methods: manual, WebCeph™ and AngelAligner™, at two different times, 20 days apart (T1 and T2). Both sets of measurements were made on the same radiographic image, thereby minimizing radiation exposure to the patient. A total of 22 points, 7 angles and 2 distances were measured, as detailed in Table 1. Landmarks and measurements were selected based on established cephalometric analyses (e.g., Steiner and Ricketts) and commonly used clinical parameters. The sagittal skeletal malocclusion classification of patients was determined according to the ANB angle: Class III (ANB<0º), Class I (0º< ANB<4º) and Class II (ANB>4º) [29].

Data was collected at the Dental Clinic of the University of Salamanca from lateral cranial teleradiographs taken before orthodontic treatment, as part of the usual orthodontic planning protocol. To ensure the same resolution and quality, all images were acquired by the same technician operating a digital imaging unit (Planmeca ProMax®, Helsinki, Finland). While they were taken, each patient was standing, biting down on a guide bite with the Frankfort plane parallel to the ground (guided by lines of light projected on the patient’s skin) and the tongue resting on the palate, to avoid the appearance of artifacts, following the usual clinical procedure.

Manual tracing on printed radiographs, as an established reference method, enabled comparison with AI-based methods without software-related bias. The AI-based methods investigated were selected because they were easily accessible, free of charge, and had been used in previous studies, facilitating comparisons across studies.

Sample

The study population comprised 30 patients, 50% of whom were male and 50% female, with a mean age of 28.53 years. The distribution by sagittal skeletal malocclusion was as follows: Class I (33.3%), Class II (33.3%), and Class III (33.3%). The sample size required for this study was estimated using G*Power v.3.1.9.6: setting the standard parameters for this type of study, the power analysis yielded 0.92 for correlations and 0.99 for ANOVAs; this indicated that the sample size was adequate, and also comparable to that in previous research [9, 18, 19, 28, 30].

Inclusion criteria were: (1) complete permanent dentition (excluding third molars); (2) cephalograms taken in a standing position with the Frankfort plane parallel to the floor; (3) cephalograms obtained using the same equipment (Planmeca ProMax®, Helsinki, Finland); (4) cephalograms including all relevant structures; and (5) cephalograms acquired with a calibration ruler (allowing magnification to be determined).

Exclusion criteria included: (1) artifacts that could interfere with landmark localization; (2) positional errors indicated by the cephalostat; (3) unerupted or (4) impacted teeth; (5) hypodontia; (6) prosthetic restorations on incisors or molars; (7) presence of intraoral devices; (8) prior orthodontic treatment or orthognathic surgery; (9) history of facial trauma or bone fracture; (10) cleft lip or palate; (11) diagnosis of systemic disease; (12) craniofacial syndromes; (13) presence of cysts or other bone disorders; and (14) poor-quality cephalograms.

Manual method The images were standardized in terms of size, brightness and contrast before printing them at 1:1 scale on photographic paper, adjusting them for correct calibration using the 30 mm ruler shown on the cephalograms. Tracings were made on an A4 size acetate sheet by a single operator—an orthodontist with 12 years of experience—using permanent markers (black for soft tissues and anatomical points, and red for planes and angles), millimetric ruler, angle protractor and set squares.

The reference points on hard and soft tissues were then manually marked on the acetate sheet following a pre-established order. For bilateral structures, a midpoint was determined in order to establish a single reference point.

To evaluate intraoperator differences, measurements (in mm) were made at two timepoints, T1 and T2, with an interval of 20 days, following a protocol similar to that used in other studies [5, 16]. To prevent clinician fatigue and any errors deriving from it, and therefore to reduce intraoperator bias, a maximum of 6 tracings were performed per day. To obviate interindividual variability, only one operator performed all tracings and measurements.

WebCeph™ method

To carry out measurements using this software, an account was created on the WebCeph™ website [31]. As in the manual method, tracings were performed at T1 and T2, 20 days apart. At each timepoint, each image was imported individually and stored in its original uncompressed format (JPEG), as compression could affect landmark detection. Images were calibrated using the 30 mm ruler shown on the cephalograms. Measurements were obtained automatically at the touch of a button, without corrections to the points, and the results were saved as generated by the software.

AngelAligner™ method

The tracing was carried out according to the same protocol as with WebCeph™, ensuring correct calibration beforehand.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the basic principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the provisions of Spanish Organic Law 3/2018, of 5 December, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Salamanca (code: 1074/2023). Participants received an information sheet detailing the nature and objectives of the study, and all provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate differences across methods (manual, AngelAligner™, WebCeph™) and timepoints (T1, T2) and their interaction. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between methods were performed using ANOVA, while differences between T1 and T2 within each method were assessed using paired t-tests. Normality of residuals was evaluated by visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Sphericity was evaluated with Mauchly’s test; when violated, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni procedure to control family-wise error due to multiple testing. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (η²p).

Repeatability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) between T1 and T2 for each method. Agreement between manual and AI-based methods was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, reporting mean bias and 95% limits of agreement.

The sample was subsequently divided into three groups according to sagittal skeletal malocclusion (Class I, II and III), and paired t-tests were used to compare all measurements across the three methods and the two timepoints.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Greater variation in standard deviation was observed among all measurements obtained at T1 and T2 using the manual method. The largest deviations were recorded for the U1–L1, NLA and LFH angles measured with the manual method.

Statistically significant differences between all methods were detected for the SNA angle (p < 0.05). Likewise, the differences between the manual method and WebCeph™ were highly significant (p < 0.01). Interestingly, highly significant differences were observed between the two AI-based methods (p < 0.01).

For the SNB angle, highly significant differences (p < 0.01) were found between the manual and WebCeph™ methods, as well as between the AI methods.

Regarding the ANB angle, highly significant differences (p < 0.01) were observed between the manual method and WebCeph™. A significant difference was also found between the AI methods (p < 0.05).

The U1–APg measurement also displayed highly significant differences (p < 0.01) between all methods. Similarly, highly significant differences were observed between all methods for L1–APg.

The GoGn–SN angle showed highly significant differences (p < 0.01) between the manual and WebCeph™ methods. Likewise, highly significant differences were found between the two AI methods.

Significant differences were also identified for the U1–L1 angle obtained using all methods, with highly significant differences between both the manual method and AngelAligner™, and between the manual method and WebCeph™ (p < 0.01).

Finally, the NLA presented highly significant differences between the two AI methods (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences between the methods evaluated were found for the LFH angle.

Repeatability analysis

To assess the repeatability of each method, ICCs were calculated for each measurement obtained using each method at timepoints T1 and T2. The results indicated high and statistically significant correlations between timepoints for all measurements, except for U1–L1, NLA and LFH angles obtained via the manual method. Discussion

While manual cephalometric tracing is still considered the reference method [13, 32–34], recently introduced artificial intelligence (AI)-based systems have shown promising results in terms of efficiency and automation [17, 25]. However, given their algorithmic complexity and the minimal human intervention involved, it is critical to assess differences with the manual method and validate their reproducibility before widespread adoption in clinical practice.

While some studies have found no statistically significant differences between manual and AI-based methods [27, 30], most of the literature reviewed agrees that automatic measurements still require human supervision and, in many cases, manual correction to achieve acceptable levels of clinical accuracy [7, 17, 18, 21, 29, 35–38]. The findings of the present study support this view, as statistically significant differences between methods were observed in most of the measurements evaluated, leading to the the null hypothesis being refuted.

As far as the individual measurements are concerned, no significant differences in NLA were detected between the manual method and either AI-based method, but there were differences between both AI systems. This variation could be related to the difficulty for digital software to correctly identify soft tissues [14], particularly in structures such as the lips and nasal profile [16, 39]. That being said, Bao et al., 2023 [21] reported greater accuracy in soft tissue vs. dental measurements, and other studies found no significant differences in lip position [14, 18, 19, 29].

In the case of the GoGn–SN angle, differences with manual measurements were found for WebCeph™, but not for AngelAligner™, which is consistent with other studies demonstrating that results vary depending on the software used [7, 12, 14, 18, 21, 29].

On the other hand, there were no significant differences found in SNB and ANB angles between the manual method and AngelAligner™, a finding that coincides with those of other authors [12, 18, 19, 28]. However, discrepancies in these angles were observed with WebCeph™, in agreement with other research [14, 37]. Other authors found differences only for SNB or ANB, but not for both [21, 28, 29, 40]. All this reinforces the need to take the particularities of each automated system into account.

Regarding the SNA angle, significant differences were found between all methods, a finding that has been reported by multiple studies [21, 28, 29, 37, 40], in contrast to what has been observed by other authors [12, 14, 18, 19] for this measurement.

Dental measurements (U1–APg, L1–APg and U1–L1) also showed significant differences among all the methods evaluated, in line with the literature, which reports variability in dental measurements between manual and AI-based methods [5, 7, 18, 19, 21, 37, 40]. Several authors have emphasized the challenges faced by algorithms in accurately identifying dental landmarks, particularly in the presence of overlapping anatomical structures [5, 19]. This issue is especially relevant for the lower incisor, which is often positioned beneath the upper incisor due to factors such as overbite, protrusion, crowding, or malocclusion [21]. These conditions can compromise the determination of dental measurements. In contrast, other authors have reported no significant differences in these variables [29].

The measurements obtained using AngelAligner™ were more closely aligned with those derived from the manual method than those obtained with WebCeph™, which exhibited discrepancies in seven out of the nine parameters analysed.

Overall, the Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated good agreement between methods, with a small bias and clinically acceptable limits of agreement, supporting the accuracy of the digital measurements.

When the sample was divided according to malocclusion type, the present study found that WebCeph™ presented greater discrepancies with respect to the manual tracing method than AngelAligner™. In this context, Çoban et al. [29] reported that the measurements used in cephalometric analysis may vary when patients are grouped according to skeletal malocclusion class. Additionally, Yu et al. indicated that the accuracy of AI-based automatic classification of skeletal malocclusions was high, although the specific analyses used to determine the skeletal class were not detailed [41].

Regarding reproducibility, although some authors [29] have found good correlations for most measurements obtained at different timepoints via both manual and AI-based methods, the present study found that the manual approach demonstrated lower consistency between T1 and T2, with greater variability in measurements such as U1–L1, NLA and LFH— angles that are particularly challenging to assess manually. The manual method also showed greater variations in standard deviation between T1 and T2, further supporting this lower consistency. These findings may be attributed to the difficulty in locating dental reference points [5, 19, 21] and midpoints [13], as well as to errors in identifying bilateral landmarks [6, 15, 16, 19, 21]. In addition, the accuracy of manual measurements may be affected by intra-operator variability [9, 10, 42], which in turn can be influenced by factors such as fatigue and professional experience.

In contrast, the correlation between datasets generated via AI-based systems was consistently high, reaching a value of 1. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as it reflects the deterministic nature of the algorithm when applied to identical input images rather than reproducibility in a clinical context. In the present study, the same radiographs were re-analyzed at T1 and T2, and therefore no biological or operator-related variability was introduced. Consequently, the observed perfect agreement represents algorithmic consistency under standardized conditions, whereby the same anatomical landmarks are consistently identified on the same image. While this level of consistency is difficult to achieve manually due to the small size of certain structures and the reliance on visual tracing [6], it should not be directly interpreted as superior clinical reproducibility.

AI-based cephalometric tracing therefore represents a tool with considerable clinical potential, as it offers time savings, greater procedural standardization, a reduced workload for clinicians, and the possibility of improved diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning [5, 8, 14, 15, 17, 21, 24, 26, 27, 32]. Nevertheless, its clinical application still warrants caution owing to the limitations that remain. Although, in the present study, some measurements showed mean differences of less than 2º or 2 mm, compared with the manual method (SNB, ANB and L1-APg), which may be considered clinically acceptable [14, 33], other variables (U1–L1, NLA and LFH for both AI methods; or SNA, U1–APg and GoGn–SN for WebCeph) exhibited larger discrepancies. Depending on the clinical context, these differences may be relevant, as they can influence processes such as the evaluation of treatment outcomes [43] and surgical simulation or planning [44, 45]. This may currently limit the possibility of completely replacing manual tracing with AI-based systems.

Although direct comparison among studies remains challenging due to the wide variety of cephalometric methods used and the lack of standardization in the measurements assessed, most reports agree that cephalometric results should be interpreted with caution in clinical practice, taking into account the magnitude of potential error. Manual verification of software-generated outcomes is therefore strongly recommended [7, 13, 14, 17, 18, 21, 23, 36–38]. Moreover, the development of more robust AI models based on large and heterogeneous datasets, together with the establishment of standardized methodologies, is essential to ensure their validation and reliable clinical implementation [23, 24, 38].

Finally, although the sample size in this study was adequate and comparable to that of previous research [9, 18, 19, 28, 30], the division according to skeletal class reduced the number of subjects in each subgroup, thereby limiting the generalizability of these findings. Accordingly, subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and potentially underpowered. Future research should therefore include larger and more ethnically diverse samples, as well as evaluate multiple software platforms. This approach would facilitate the exploration of algorithmic differences, the identification of potential sources of error, and the development of automated correction strategies to improve the clinical accuracy of AI-based tools. Furthermore, for AI systems to be safely integrated into clinical practice, it is essential to consider that image quality directly affects the accuracy of cephalometric tracing and, consequently, diagnostic outcomes. Additional studies are required to assess how variations in radiographic quality influence AI performance in real-world clinical settings.

Conclusions

AI-based methods showed perfect repeatability on identical digital inputs, whereas the repeatability of the manual method depended on the specific measurement analysed. Compared with WebCeph™, AngelAligner™ yielded a higher number of measurements that were comparable to those obtained with the manual method. Nevertheless, manual verification of tracings remains necessary to achieve results equivalent to those obtained by an experienced professional.
