A novel multifunctional starch debranching enzyme from Myxococcus sp. V11
Authors: Siting Feng, Peipei Peng, Jinsong Ouyang, Yuxuan Wang, Jinming You, Fei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMDBE, a novel multifunctional isoamylase derived from Myxococcus sp. V11, was heterologously expressed in the Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that MDBE is a multifunctional starch debranching enzyme belonging to the glycoside hydrolase GH13_11 family. Functional identification revealed that MDBE exhibits bifunctional hydrolytic activity, enabling it to hydrolyze both α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds, thereby allowing the degradation of both amylose and amylopectin. Moreover, MDBE can transfer α-1,4-glucan oligosaccharides between molecules through a newly discovered 4-α-D-glucanotransferase activity, resulting in the production of serial oligosaccharides. Under conditions devoid of any added ions, the specific enzyme activity of MDBE was determined to be 250.5 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 and 209. μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 when using corn starch and amylose as substrates, respectively. Application of the Michaelis–Menten equation allowed the calculation of the Vmax, Km, and kcat of MDBE for amylose, yielding values of 3171.8 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1, 17.7 mM, and 18.9 min⁻1, respectively. The hydrolysis rates of MDBE towards corn starch, amylose, and amylopectin were 50%, 48%, and 44%, respectively. In combined enzyme hydrolysis of amylopectin, the yields of G1 and G2 increased by 1.1-fold and 1.8-fold, respectively, compared to the application of maltogenic amylase (BMAL) alone. Our results suggest that MDBE has potential for future applications in starch hydrolysis and bioconversion, pending further optimization of its catalytic efficiency and stability under industrial conditions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13961-4.Keywords: Multifunctional starch debranching enzyme, Myxococcus, Enzymatic properties, Starch hydrolysis, Transglycosylation

Introduction

Starch is a high-molecular-weight carbohydrate composed of glucose units covalently linked by glycosidic bonds, and it is one of the most abundant carbohydrates on Earth (Xia et al. 2021). Starch is primarily classified into two components: amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is a polymer of linear polymers formed by α−1,4-glycosidic bonds, while amylopectin is characterized by linear α−1,4-glycosidic bonded glucan chains with α−1,6-glycosidic branched linkages (Ray 2011). During starch processing, various enzymes utilized for starch modification can efficiently hydrolyze α−1,4-glycosidic bonds in starch molecules; however, their capacity to hydrolyze α−1,6-glycosidic bonds is relatively low, resulting in decreased starch conversion rates and diminished product quality (Hii et al. 2012). Starch debranching enzymes (SDBEs) are a class of hydrolytic enzymes that specifically target α−1,6-glycosidic bonds in starch. Isoamylase (EC 3.2.1.68), one of the four main types of SDBEs, belongs to the glycoside hydrolase GH13 family and exhibits high hydrolytic activity toward amylopectin and glycogen. In the starch processing industry, isoamylase, when used in conjunction with other enzymes such as α-amylase, can significantly improve the hydrolysis efficiency and overall conversion rate of starch (Fang et al. 2005; Li et al. 2017; Tian et al. 2022).

The unique biochemical characteristics and high activity of starch debranching enzymes have garnered significant attention for their potential applications in processes such as saccharification and liquefaction in starch processing (Hashim 2020). When isoamylase acts synergistically with β-amylases at the same substrate concentration, it significantly increases the hydrolysis rate of α−1,6-glycosidic bonds, achieving a liquefaction conversion rate exceeding 80% and producing saccharification liquor with maltose content surpassing 90%. This synergistic effect reduces the amount of starch enzymes required, shortens the production cycle, and improves raw material utilization (Duan et al. 2013). The cooperative catalytic action between starch debranching enzymes and other starch-active hydrolytic enzymes can markedly enhance raw material utilization and production efficiency in critical stages of starch processing, such as saccharification and modification (Xia et al. 2021).

To date, industrial applications of starch debranching enzymes have predominantly utilized pullulanases, with isoamylases being less widely exploited in commercial processes.Reported microbial sources of isoamylase include Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (Ara et al. 1993), Flavobacterium odoratum (Abe et al. 1999), Dyella sp. (Silano et al. 2020), Escherichia coli (Dauvillée et al. 2005) and Pseudomonas amyloderamosa (Amemura et al. 1988). However, traditional isoamylases often exhibit disadvantages such as poor thermal stability, insufficient acid tolerance, and low enzyme activity (Dong et al. 2021). Currently, the commercially available isoamylase is derived exclusively from Pseudomonas amyloderamosa, and in practical applications, it is utilized in combination with other starch enzymes to facilitate starch hydrolysis (Li et al. 2013). From an industrial perspective, there is a pressing need to further explore new isoamylases to provide resources for developing isoamylases with high catalytic efficiency and broad adaptability to current process conditions.

In preliminary work, our laboratory isolated a strain of Myxococcus sp. V11 (Zhao et al. 2021). In addition, our group previously characterized a multifunctional isoamylase, MIsA, from the same Myxococcus sp. V11 strain (Feng et al. 2024). MIsA exhibits both isoamylase and 4-α-glucanotransferase activities, with optimal activity at 50 °C and pH 6.0. However, MIsA shows relatively low activity on amylose (47.8% relative to amylopectin) and produces significant amounts of glucose from starch hydrolysis. The identification of MDBE—encoded by a distinct gene (mdbe, GenBank ID: MCK8499202.1) within the same genome—provides an opportunity to compare two evolutionarily related yet functionally distinct debranching enzymes from a single microbial source, potentially revealing adaptive strategies for carbohydrate utilization in Myxococcus. The amino acid sequence exhibited 58% similarity to previously reported isoamylases, indicating that it represents a novel starch debranching enzyme. While classical isoamylases (EC 3.2.1.68) are strictly defined as debranching enzymes specific for α−1,6-glucosidic linkages with no activity on pullulan, MDBE exhibits broader substrate specificity, including hydrolytic activity toward α−1,4-glucosidic bonds in amylose and 4-α-glucanotransferase activity. Therefore, we describe MDBE as a multifunctional starch debranching enzyme of the isoamylase type, rather than a classical isoamylase. MDBE and isoamylases are classified into subfamily 11 of GH13 (GH13_11) in the CAZy datebase. While the three-dimensional structures of several GH13_11 isoamylases have been determined—including the isoamylase from P. amyloderamosa (Katsuya et al. 1998) and the debranching enzyme ISA1 from C. reinhardtii (Sim et al. 2014)—the biochemical characterization of novel microbial isoamylases with unique catalytic properties remains limited. In this study, MDBE was heterologously expressed and characterized as a novel multifunctional starch debranching enzyme with both α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bond hydrolytic activities, as well as transferase activity. When utilized in combination with other starch-active enzymes, MDBE enhances the hydrolysis rate of starch.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

The multifunctional starch debranching enzyme recombinant plasmid was constructed in Escherichia coli DH5α (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Gene cloning was performed using a one-step cloning kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), with the pET-29a (+) plasmid serving as the expression vector and E. coli BL21 (DE3) (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) as the host for multifunctional starch debranching enzyme expression.

Construction of the expression vector pET29a-mdbe

Using total DNA from Myxococcus sp. V11 as the template, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed with primers MDBE-F: 5′-GGTTCCATGGCTGATATCGGATCCAGCAGGGAGGTCTGGCCG-3′ and MDBE-R: 5′-GTGCTCGAGTGCGGCCGCAAGCTTGCGCTCCACCTGCCGGAA-3′. The PCR product was sent for sequencing to Tsingke Corporation (Hunan, China). After double digestion with Bam HⅠ and Hin dIII, the amplified fragment was inserted into the Bam HⅠ-Hin dIII sites of the pET-29a (+) vector, resulting in the plasmid pET-29a (+)-mdbe, which was subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3).

Expression, purification, and zymogram analysis of MDBE

The recombinant plasmid pET-29a (+) -mdbe was introduced into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells through heat-shock transformation (42 °C). The recombinant cells were first cultivated at 37 °C to the desired cell density (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.6–0.8), after which expression of MDBE was induced by adding IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) (final concentration of 0.2 mM), and the cells were cultivated in Luria–Bertani medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 mg/L) to maintain the pET-29a (+) plasmid which incubated at 16 °C for 24 h to allow protein expression.

Following induction, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C (6,000 rpm for 15 min), and resuspended in 50 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Protein extraction was performed using an ultrasonic cell disruption method. The recombinant MDBE was then purified using Ni–NTA agarose resin (Smart-Lifesciences, Changzhou, China; catalog no. SA005100). The column was washed with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) containing 10 mM imidazole to remove non-specifically bound proteins, and MDBE was eluted with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) containing 100 mM and 200 mM imidazole in a stepwise manner. The eluted protein was subjected to overnight dialysis at 4 °C to remove imidazole. The expression of MDBE was analyzed by 10% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis (Singh et al. 1991), and the protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (Bradford 1976).

The protein samples were analyzed by Native-PAGE (10% acrylamide gel with 0.2% amylopectin) at 4 °C (Li et al. 2015). After electrophoresis, the gel was divided into two parts: one part was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Laemmli 1970), and the other was incubated in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) buffer at 35 °C for 10 min. After incubation, the gel strip was soaked in dilute iodine solution for 10 min to visualize enzyme activity.

Enzyme assay

The activity of MDBE was determined by quantitatively measuring the reducing sugars produced during starch hydrolysis (Li et al. 2017). The enzyme and 0.5% (w/v) corn starch were added to 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0), and the reaction was subjected to incubation at 40 °C for 10 min. Reducing sugars released were quantified using the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (McKee 2017). Enzyme activity is defined as the amount of enzyme required to catalyze the generation of 1 μmol of glucose equivalent per minute at 40 °C in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0), and is expressed as μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 protein.

Enzymatic characteristics of MDBE

To investigate the pH dependence of MDBE activity, various buffers were employed: citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.0–6.0); and Tris–HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0–9.0), with amylopectin serving as the substrate, and reactions were carried out at a constant temperature of 40 °C. To test the pH stability of MDBE, the enzyme solution was subjected to incubation at pH values ranging from 5.0 to 9.0 at 4 °C for 24 h, and enzyme activity was measured under standard conditions. The effect of temperature on MDBE activity was evaluated by incubating the enzyme with starch solution at temperatures between 30 to 50 °C for 10 min. Additionally, the thermal stability of MDBE was assessed by incubating the enzyme solution at different temperatures for various time intervals, with residual enzyme activity measured under standard conditions and unheated enzyme serving as the control (100% activity).

The influence of metal ions on MDBE activity was examined by incubating MDBE with various metal salts and amylopectin at 40 °C for 10 min. The effects of organic solvents were investigated by incubating the enzyme in 10 mM EDTA, 5% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropanol, methanol, ethanol, acetone, 10% (v/v) acetonitrile, DTT (1 mM and 10 mM), and varying concentrations of surfactants (20 mg·mL⁻1 Triton X-100 and 2 mg·mL⁻1 SDS), with enzyme activity in the absence of any additives considered 100%.

Kinetic parameters of MDBE

Amylose (catalog no. A8160; Solarbio, Beijing, China), provided as a fine powder, was used as substrate. The amylose solution (0.2%–1.8%, w/v) was prepared by dissolution in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) with heating at 80–90 °C for 15 min and vigorous vortexing. The reaction mixture (500 µL) contained purified MDBE (6.8 μg/mL, final concentration) and varying concentrations of amylose (0.2%–1.8%, w/v) in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0). The reaction was incubated at 40 °C for 10 min, during which the release of reducing sugars was confirmed to be linear with time, ensuring initial rate conditions. The Michaelis–Menten parameters (Vmax, Km, and kcat) (Cho and Lim 2018) were calculated using Origin 64 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) by nonlinear regression fitting. The kcat value was derived from Vmax and the total enzyme concentration in the reaction mixture.

Substrate specificity of MDBE

To assess the hydrolytic capability of MDBE on different carbohydrates, purified MDBE (1.84 μg/mL, final concentration) was combined with 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.0) and 5 mg·mL⁻1 of tapioca starch, β-cyclodextrin, γ-cyclodextrin, potato starch, soluble starch, amylose, corn starch, pullulan, and amylopectin. The mixture (500 µL) was incubated at 40 °C for 10 min, and enzyme activity was measured for each substrate.

TLC analysis of hydrolyzed products of different substrates by MDBE

A 0.2% (w/v) solution of maltotetraose, maltopentaose, amylopectin, amylose, pullulan, α-cyclodextrin, β-cyclodextrin, and γ-cyclodextrin was prepared in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0). MDBE was combined with these substrates, and the reaction was carried out at 40 °C for 2 h. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC; Merck silica gel 60 plate) was used to analyze the hydrolysis products of MDBE on different substrates.

TLC analysis utilized standard maltodextrin samples (G1–G6). The mobile phase consisted of n-butanol, methanol, and water (4:2:1, v/v/v), and coloration was developed using concentrated sulfuric acid and methanol (1:9) (Hmidet et al. 2008).

Determination of the glycosidic bond ratio and degree of polymerization of MDBE hydrolysis products

To determine the glycosidic bond ratio in amylopectin after MDBE treatment, MDBE was added to a 5% (w/v) amylopectin solution in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0), and the reaction was conducted at 40 °C for 0, 0.5, 1, and 3 h. The reaction was concluded by boiling for 5 min, after which the samples were freeze-dried. Approximately 5 mg of purified starch was weighed and placed in a 1.5-mL tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of DMSO-d₆. The mixture was heated overnight at 80 °C. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant, which was subsequently transferred to an NMR tube for analysis. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz NMR spectrometer at 25 °C, with chemical shifts referenced to the residual DMSO signal (δ 2.50 ppm). The anomeric proton signals of α−1,4-glycosidic bonds (δ ~ 5.12 ppm) and α−1,6-glycosidic bonds (δ ~ 4.78 ppm) were integrated to determine their relative proportions. Spectra were processed using MestReNova (Mestrelab Research, Santiago de Compostela, Spain) (Schmitz et al. 2009; Hong et al. 2015).

To assess the degree of polymerization (DP) of amylopectin after MDBE treatment, MDBE was added to a 1% (w/v) amylopectin solution in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0), and the reaction was performed at 40 °C for 0 and 0.5 h. After the reaction, the mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min to terminate the reaction, then was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The residue was freeze-dried. The measurement method was consistent with that used for MIsA derived from Myxococcus sp. V11 (Feng et al. 2024).

Morphological observation of starch particles after MDBE treatment

A 10% (w/v) corn starch solution was prepared in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0), and treated with MDBE for various durations (0, 1, 6 h). The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was freeze-dried and ground, and then observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM: S3700N, Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan)). Specifically, the freeze-dried and ground samples were mounted on conductive double-sided tape, sputter-coated with a 30 nm gold layer in a vacuum, and observed under the SEM.

Degree of debranching of MDBE to amylopectin

MDBE was mixed with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) buffer and 0.2% (w/v) amylopectin and treated at 40 °C for different time intervals (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 6 h) (Wang et al. 2023). The reaction was terminated by boiling for 10 min, and the reducing sugar concentration was measured using the DNS method. The degree of debranching was determined using the equation [(Rs – Ramylopectin)/(Rd – Ramylopectin)] × 100, where Rs represents the reducing sugar content of the sample at the specific debranching time, Rd represents the reducing sugar content of the amylopectin after 6 h of debranching, and Ramylopectin represents the reducing sugar content of the original amylopectin (Li et al. 2017).

The degree of hydrolysis of different starches by MDBE

Hydrolysis assays were performed separately for each substrate. The reaction mixture (10 mL) contained purified MDBE (6.8 μg/mL) and 2.5% (w/v) corn starch, amylopectin, or amylose in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0). Each substrate was individually dissolved in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.0) by heating at 80–90 °C for 10 min with vigorous vortexing prior to addition of the enzyme. The reaction was incubated at 40 °C for 2–6 h. Following the reaction, it was terminated by boiling for 5 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and the insoluble material was freeze-dried and weighed.

Dual enzyme coupling to enhance amylopectin hydrolysis rate

Previously cloned maltase, named BMAL, from Bacillus sp. B110 (Liu et al. 2023), was utilized to investigate the impact of the combined MDBE and BMAL treatment on amylopectin hydrolysis rate. The enzyme mixture, in a mass ratio of MDBE to BMAL of 3:2, was mixed with a buffer of 50 mM Na2HPO4-NaH2PO4 (pH 6.0), followed by the addition of a 0.2% (w/v) amylopectin solution. The reaction was conducted at 40 °C for 7 h (Siddiqui et al. 2022). After the reaction, it was terminated by boiling for 10 min, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove impurities. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed using a Cosmosil Sugar-D column (Nacalai Tesuque, Kyoto, Japan) with a refractive index detector (RID) at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and water (65:35, v/v), with a flow rate of 1 mL·min−1.

Results

Identification of a multifunctional starch debranching enzyme MDBE from Myxococcus sp. V11

The MDBE encoding gene was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR), resulting in a 2130 bp fragment that encodes 709 amino acids. The amino acid sequence of MDBE was submitted to NCBI, and similar sequences displaying high similarity from the UniProt and Protein Data Bank databases were obtained for alignment. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW implemented in MEGA 5(Tamura et al 2011). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method with the p-distance model, and bootstrap values were calculated from 1000 replicates. (Figure S1). MDBE exhibits 50.5% sequence identity with the glucan debranching enzyme TreX from Saccharolobus solfataricus (Fang et al. 2005), and 54.12% sequence identity with the glucan debranching enzyme GlgX from Mycobacterium bovis AF2122/97 (Malone et al. 2017). While the enzymatic characteristics of GlgX remain experimentally uncharacterized, its inclusion in this phylogenetic analysis is based solely on sequence similarity and genomic annotation as a putative debranching enzyme. The clustering of these three proteins in the same branch suggests a shared evolutionary origin within the GH13_11 subfamily; however, functional similarity to MDBE and TreX can only be inferred for GlgX and requires experimental validation. MDBE shares 39.56% sequence identity with the isoamylase ISA1 from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Sim et al. 2014), and 35.03% sequence identity with the isoamylase from Pseudomonas amyloderamosa (Katsuya et al. 1998). The sequence identity with other limit dextrin α−1,6-maltotetraose hydrolases from various sources ranges from 40 to 50%.

Multiple sequence alignment of MDBE with characterized GH13_11 enzymes revealed four highly conserved motifs characteristic of α-amylases: 274DVVYNH279, 346GFRFDLA352, 387EPWD390, and 454FVTAHD459(Figure S2). The three-dimensional structure of MDBE was predicted using AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al. 2021) and retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Varadi et al. 2022). The predicted structure was superimposed with the crystal structure of TreX (PDB ID: 2VNC) (Woo et al. 2008) using PyMOL to calculate the RMSD value. The structure of MDBE closely resembles the monomer structure of TreX (RMSD = 0.814) and comprises three domains: catalytic domain (Domain A) featuring an (β/α)8 barrel structure—a hallmark of the GH13 family, carbohydrate-binding module CBM48 (Domain N) with a typical immunoglobulin-like β-sandwich structure, and Domain C with a sandwich-like structure formed by eight anti-parallel β-strands (Fig. 1a). The predicted active site residues, Asp350, Glu387, and Asp459, align with those in TreX and are situated within a cleft formed by Domain A (Fig. 1b) (MacGregor et al. 2001; Woo et al. 2008).

The effects of temperature and pH on MDBE are presented below, using amylopectin as the substrate to analyze the catalytic properties of MDBE. The optimal reaction temperature for MDBE is 40 °C (Figure S3a). MDBE is thermosensitive, exhibiting a relative enzyme activity of 14.57% after being incubated at 45 °C for 12 h. When incubated at temperatures below 40 °C for 12 h, its relative enzyme activity remained above 75% (Figure S3b), indicating that MDBE functions as a mesophilic enzyme. The optimal pH for MDBE is 7.0 (Figure S3c), and it maintains stability when stored in a pH 6.0 buffer for 24 h, with a residual enzyme activity of 53.83% at pH 7.0; however, enzyme activity is completely lost at pH 9.0 (Figure S3d).

Using amylopectin as the substrate, different concentrations of metal ions and organic solvents were introduced into the reaction system, with the absence of metal ions and organic solvents serving as the control group. The results indicate that at a metal ion concentration of 1 mM, Li⁺, Mg2⁺, Ca2⁺, K⁺, and Cr3⁺ ions activate MDBE, with Cr3⁺ exhibiting the most substantial activation, showing over 70% enhancement in activity. In contrast, Fe2⁺ had no significant effect on MDBE activity, while other ions generally exhibited inhibitory effects (Table S1).

Additionally, EDTA activated MDBE, increasing its relative enzyme activity by more than 20%. However, DTT, Triton X-100, and DMSO did not significantly affect MDBE activity, while other organic reagents demonstrated inhibitory effects (Table S1).

The kinetic parameters of MDBE were determined using varying concentrations of amylose as the substrate under conditions of 40 °C and pH 7.0 (50 mM Tris–HCl). The Michaelis–Menten equation was applied to calculate Vmax, Km, and kcat, yielding the following values: Km = 17. 7 mM, Vmax = 3171.77 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1, and kcat = 18.99 min⁻1 (Figure S4).

MDBE possesses transglycosylation and hydrolytic activity

The substrates included corn starch, β-cyclodextrin, γ-cyclodextrin, potato starch, amylose, pullulan, amylopectin, tapioca starch, and soluble starch. As indicated in Table 1, corn starch was determined to be the optimal substrate for MDBE, exhibiting an enzyme activity of 250.55 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1. It was observed that MDBE did not hydrolyze pullulan or β-cyclodextrin (Table 1).

To gain a deeper understanding of the hydrolysis mechanism of MDBE, thin layer chromatography (TLC) was employed to analyze the enzyme's activity on various substrates, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The results of the G4 and G6 control experiments, which contained only the substrate, are shown in Figure S5. The results indicate that MDBE does not hydrolyze pullulan to yield maltotriose, suggesting that it is not a pullulanase. Conversely, MDBE hydrolyzes several carbohydrates, including maltotetraose, maltotriose, amylopectin, and amylose, generating maltodextrins (G2-G6) and linear oligosaccharides, but does not produce monosaccharides. These findings imply that MDBE facilitates the intermolecular transfer of glucan by forming α−1,4-glycosidic bonds between α-D-glucan molecules while hydrolyzing α−1,6-glycosidic bonds. Consequently, MDBE exhibits disproportionation activity toward maltodextrins, a characteristic feature of 4-α-glucanotransferase (EC 2.4.1.25) (Tachibana et al. 2000; Takaha et al. 1996; Terada et al. 1999). MDBE does not act on α-cyclodextrin or β-cyclodextrin, but it does act on γ-cyclodextrin, resulting in the release of maltotriose and maltotetraose, as shown in Fig. 3. The hydrolysis of γ-CD, which possesses a side chain of two glucose residues, suggests that MDBE can accommodate minimal glucan units of at least two glucose residues in its active site. However, the exact size of the transferred glucan unit remains to be determined, as the TLC profiles do not resolve the precise length of transferred oligosaccharides. These results confirm that MDBE possesses activities for both α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bonds and glycosyltransferase activity. Thus, MDBE is classified as a transglycosylating amylase.

MDBE modification alters the starch structure

The distribution of glycosidic bond was analyzed using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 4). The chemical shifts of the proton signals corresponding to the terminal isomeric protons of α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bonds were observed at 5.12 ppm and 4.78 ppm, respectively, within the expected range. Quantitative analysis revealed that the α−1,6 glycosidic bonds constituted 6.53% of the total glycosidic bonds in the control group. After MDBE treatment, the intensity of the H-1 (1–6) proton peak diminished, and the content of α−1,6 glycosidic bonds were reduced to 3.65%, 2.87%, and 2.46% after 0.5, 1, and 3 h, respectively. These results indicate that MDBE specifically hydrolyzes α−1,6 glycosidic bonds, reducing the branching degree of amylopectin and confirming its activity as a starch debranching enzyme.

Determination of Glycosidic Bond Ratios. In the control group, α−1,6 glycosidic bonds accounted for 6.53% of the total glycosidic bonds. After MDBE treatment for 0.5 h, 1 h, and 3 h, the α−1,6 glycosidic bonds were reduced to 3.65%, 2.87%, and 2.46%, respectively

To investigate the chain length distribution within the starch, the reaction products following 30 min of MDBE treatment were analyzed using HPAEC (Fig. 5). The results revealed significant alterations in the side chain lengths of amylopectin post-MDBE modification, with a substantial presence of side chains in the DP6-40 range. Although MDBE exhibits both debranching and α−1,4 glycosidic bond hydrolyzing activities, the specific mechanism responsible for the formation of longer chains remains to be elucidated. Therefore, while these preliminary findings are promising, the potential effect of MDBE on enhancing amylopectin hydrolysis requires further mechanistic investigation before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

Chain length distribution (CLD) of amylopectin. after treatment with MDBE for 0 and 30 min

In contrast to untreated corn starch, the microstructure of corn starch following MDBE treatment exhibited significant changes. The native corn starch displayed a smooth surface and intact structure (Fig. 6a). Following 1 h of MDBE treatment (Fig. 6b) and 6 h of treatment (Fig. 6c), notable starch cracking was observed, characterized by surface fissures and the development of irregular block-like fragments. As the duration of enzymatic treatment with MDBE increased, both the number of surface cracks and the presence of starch fragments intensified.

Discussion

The multifunctional starch debranching enzyme MDBE derived from Myxococcus sp. V11 catalyzes the hydrolysis of α−1,6 glycosidic bonds in amylopectin, releasing high-purity linear starch and linear oligosaccharides (Kubo et al. 2010). In this study, MDBE was expressed and purified using Escherichia coli as the host organism. Multiple sequence alignment of conserved regions revealed that MDBE contains a carbohydrate-binding module and four conserved motifs, classifying it within the glycoside hydrolase GH13_11 family. MDBE exhibits optimal activity at 40 °C within a 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer at pH 7.0. In the absence of metal ions, MDBE displayed specific activities of 250.5 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 for corn starch and 209.4 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 for amylose. Kinetic parameters for amylose were determined using the Michaelis–Menten equation, yielding values of 17.7 mM for Km, 3171.77 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1 for Vmax, and 18.99 min⁻1 for kcat. The substrate preference of MDBE—corn starch (250.5 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1) > amylose (209.4 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1) > amylopectin (151.4 μmol·min⁻1·mg⁻1)—reflects its multifunctional catalytic repertoire rather than classical isoamylase behavior. MDBE's ability to hydrolyze α−1,4 linkages in amylose (Table 1) contributes directly to reducing sugar release, enhancing apparent activity on linear substrates. This activity is absent in classical isoamylases.While MDBE debranches amylopectin (Fig. 4), its action is constrained by (i) steric hindrance from dense branching, limiting access to interior α−1,6 linkages; and (ii) the generation of short-chain products (DP6-40, Fig. 5) that may remain bound to the active site or re-associate, reducing turnover rate. The transglycosylation activity further redistributes oligosaccharides rather than releasing reducing sugars, resulting in lower measured hydrolytic activity on amylopectin compared to starch or amylose. Corn starch contains both amylose and amylopectin, providing a heterogeneous substrate landscape where MDBE's dual hydrolytic activities (α−1,4 and α−1,6) and transglycosylation can operate synergistically. Debranching of amylopectin exposes new α−1,4 chain terminals for hydrolysis, while amylose serves as both substrate and acceptor for glycan transfer, collectively enhancing overall catalytic output. These findings support classification of MDBE as a multifunctional debranching enzyme rather than a classical isoamylase. The prominent α−1,4 hydrolytic and transglycosylation activities fundamentally alter its substrate specificity profile compared to EC 3.2.1.68 enzymes, underscoring the need for revised biochemical classification of GH13_11 members with expanded catalytic functions.

In the presence of 1 mM Ca2⁺, K⁺, and Li⁺ ions, MDBE activity increased by more than 1.4 times, similar to the behavior of IsoM from Corallococcus sp. EGB, which is also activated by Ca2⁺ and K⁺ (Li et al. 2017), as well as MIsA from Myxococcus sp. V11, which is activated by Li⁺ (Feng et al. 2024). The hydrolysis pattern of MDBE, assessed by 1H-NMR spectroscopy, revealed its specifically for cleaving α−1,6 glycosidic bonds within the polymer, consistent with the action of MIsA from Myxococcus sp. V11 (Feng et al. 2024). Micromorphological observation of starch particles and High-Performance Anion-Exchange Chromatography (HPAEC) analysis further verified MDBE’s effective debranching activity.

MDBE from Myxococcus sp. V11 is a multifunctional debranching enzyme that does not act on pullulan. It effectively hydrolyzes γ-cyclodextrin but does not hydrolyze α- or β-cyclodextrin, indicating that its minimum active unit for α−1,4 glycosidic bonds involve at least two glucose residues. The 4-α-glucanotransferase (4-α-GTase) catalyzes a disproportionation reaction, transferring glucan chains from the non-reducing end of one glucan molecule to the non-reducing end of another (Takaha et al. 1993; Takaha & Smith 1999). Notably, MDBE's hydrolysis of various sugars, including maltotetraose, maltohexose, amylopectin, and amylose, produces not only maltodextrins (G2-G6) but also linear oligosaccharides, suggesting that the active site can accommodate glucan chains of at least two glucose residues, though the minimum transfer unit requires further investigation with defined substrates (e.g., G7 or G8) and mass spectrometry analysis. These findings indicate that MDBE possesses both isoamylase and transglycosylase activities.

The genus Myxococcus is known for its complex life cycle and predatory behavior, which involves extensive degradation of macromolecules including carbohydrates. Genomic analyses suggest that multiple Myxococcus species harbor several genes within the GH13_11 subfamily, to which isoamylase-type debranching enzymes belong (CAZy database; http://www.cazy.org/). The presence of MDBE-like enzymes in these genomes implies that such multifunctional debranching enzymes may play significant roles in carbohydrate metabolism, potentially contributing to starch/glycogen utilization during the complex life cycle of myxobacteria. The multifunctional nature of MDBE—combining debranching, α−1,4-hydrolytic, and transglycosylation activities—may reflect an evolutionary adaptation to diverse carbohydrate substrates encountered in soil environments.

Comparative analysis of MDBE and MIsA from Myxococcus sp. V11. Both MDBE and MIsA belong to the GH13_11 subfamily and exhibit multifunctional debranching activities; however, several key differences distinguish these two enzymes. First, MDBE demonstrates higher activity on amylose than on amylopectin (Table 2), whereas MIsA preferentially hydrolyzes amylopectin. This inversion of substrate preference suggests distinct active site architectures: MDBE may possess a more open cleft accommodating linear glucan chains, while MIsA's active site may be optimized for branched substrates. Second, MDBE does not produce glucose from starch or maltodextrins, in contrast to MIsA which generates glucose as a major product. This difference indicates divergent cleavage patterns—MDBE acts via exo-type mechanisms releasing maltodextrins (G2-G6), while MIsA exhibits endo-type activity producing monosaccharides. Third, MDBE's optimal temperature (40 °C) is 10 °C lower than MIsA (50 °C), suggesting adaptation to different physiological conditions or cellular compartments. Phylogenetically, MDBE clusters with TreX (54.12% identity), while MIsA shows closer relationship to other Myxococcus GH13_11 enzymes (Feng et al. 2024), implying distinct evolutionary origins despite shared genomic context. These differences underscore that GH13_11 enzymes in Myxococcus have undergone functional diversification, with MDBE and MIsA representing specialized variants for distinct carbohydrate metabolic roles.

MDBE hydrolyzes maltodextrins, yielding a series of maltose oligosaccharides, consistent with the behavior of TreX from Sulfolobus solfataricus ATCC 35092 (Park et al. 2007) and MIsA from Myxococcus sp. V11 (Feng et al. 2024), both exhibiting isoamylase and 4-α-glucanotransferase activities. However, unlike TreX, MDBE demonstrates hydrolytic activity on both amylose and amylopectin, indicating dual activity for α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bonds. In contrast to MIsA, MDBE exhibits higher activity on amylose than on amylopectin and does not yield glucose from the hydrolysis of starch or maltodextrins. The minimum unit for MDBE’s activity involves at least two glucose residues in the side chain, consistent with the findings for GT-apu from Geobacillus thermoleovorans NP33 (Nisha and Satyanarayana 2013). GlgU from Lactobacillus crispatus (Zhang et al. 2022) possesses both α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bond activities but does not exhibit 4-α-glucanotransferase activity (Table 2).

Due to its multifunctionality, MDBE plays a crucial role in starch hydrolysis. After a 6 h of incubation, the hydrolysis rates of corn starch, amylose, and amylopectin were 50%, 48%, and 44%, respectively, corresponding to its substrate specificity. The differential hydrolysis rates among corn starch (50%), amylose (48%), and amylopectin (44%) reflect the structural complexity of these substrates. Corn starch, containing both amylose and amylopectin, exhibited the highest hydrolysis rate, likely due to the synergistic action of MDBE on both α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bonds. The lower hydrolysis rate of amylopectin (44%) may be attributed to its highly branched structure, which limits accessibility of MDBE to interior α−1,6 linkages despite its debranching activity. The lower hydrolysis rates observed for MDBE (44–50%) compared to some reported debranching enzymes (> 80% liquefaction conversion; (Duan et al. 2013)) may reflect its mesophilic nature and lower optimal temperature (40 °C), suggesting that thermal stability engineering could enhance its industrial applicability. When used in combination with maltase from Bacillus sp. B110 (BMAL), MDBE enhanced the hydrolysis rate of amylopectin, yielding glucose (G1) and maltose (G2) levels that were 1.1 and 1.84 times higher, respectively, compared to BMAL alone. The synergistic effect of MDBE and BMAL on amylopectin hydrolysis can be attributed to multiple mechanisms. First, MDBE specifically hydrolyzes α−1,6-glycosidic bonds, releasing linear glucan chains and increasing the number of non-reducing ends available for BMAL attack. This debranching activity is supported by our 1H NMR data showing a reduction in α−1,6-glycosidic bonds from 6.53% to 2.46% after 3 h of MDBE treatment (Fig. 4). Second, the HPAEC analysis revealed that MDBE treatment generated substantial side chains in the DP6-40 range (Fig. 5), which serve as optimal substrates for maltogenic amylases such as BMAL. Third, SEM observation indicated that MDBE treatment caused surface cracking and fragmentation of starch granules (Fig. 6), thereby enhancing substrate accessibility for BMAL.It is noteworthy that the 1.8-fold increase in maltose (G2) was considerably more pronounced than the 1.1-fold increase in glucose (G1). This discrepancy can be explained by the distinct catalytic specificities of the two enzymes: MDBE does not produce glucose from starch or maltodextrin hydrolysis, and its primary contribution is to restructure the substrate to facilitate BMAL-mediated maltose release. The modest increase in G1 likely reflects the enhanced overall hydrolytic efficiency of BMAL on the debranched substrate, rather than a direct glucose-producing activity of MDBE. Consequently, the cooperative action of MDBE and BMAL predominantly enhances maltose production by expanding the available α−1,4-glycosidic bond terminals for exo-acting maltogenic amylase activity.

In conclusion, MDBE is a multifunctional multifunctional starch debranching enzyme possessing both α−1,4 and α−1,6 glycosidic bond hydrolytic and transglycosylase activities, distinguishing it from other isoamylases in the GH13 family. The functional properties of MDBE suggest potential to improve starch hydrolysis while limiting dextrin formation during starch processing, which could enhance the utilization and conversion rate of starch feedstocks if successfully optimized for industrial conditions.
