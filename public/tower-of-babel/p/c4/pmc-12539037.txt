Integrative genome-wide association and haplotype-based analyses reveal genetic structure and local adaptation in Korean landrace soybeans
Authors: Eun-Gyeong Kim, Myoung-Jae Shin, Xiaohan Wang, Yu-Mi Choi, Gi-An Lee, Eunae Yoo, Jae-Eun Lee, Sookyeong Lee, Kebede Taye Desta, Me-Sun Kim, Hyeonseok Oh, Jungyoon Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoybean (Glycine max L.) is a major crop valued for both food and industrial applications. The genetic diversity preserved within landrace accessions serves as a critical resource for improving agronomic traits and enhancing adaptation to climatic variability. Korean landrace soybeans, shaped by long-term cultivation across diverse local environments, provide an ideal population for dissecting genetic variation underlying key traits and regional adaptation.ResultsA genome-wide association study (GWAS) was performed on 1,693 Korean landrace soybean accessions genotyped with 67,222 SNPs from the 180 K Axiom® Soya SNP array. Population structure and genetic diversity were assessed using model-based stratification (K = 15), principal component analysis (PCA), linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis (r² = 0.2 at 309 kb), and FST-based differentiation. GWAS using the MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK models identified 38 significant SNPs associated with flowering date (FD), maturity date (MD), number of seeds per pod (NOSPP), and 100-seed weight (SW), including pleiotropic loci. Candidate genes within LD blocks were annotated and subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, revealing biological processes such as signaling, transcriptional regulation, sterol metabolism, and cell division. Haplotype analysis supported phenotypic differentiation among allelic groups. For SNPs located outside of LD blocks, variation in allele frequencies and trait values across regions indicated evidence of local adaptation.ConclusionsThese findings provide new insights into the genetic architecture of agronomic traits in Korean landrace soybeans. The identified loci, functionally annotated candidate genes, and region-specific alleles constitute a valuable genomic resource for breeding programs aiming to develop climate-resilient and regionally adapted soybean cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07479-6.Keywords: GWAS, Haplotype, Landrace soybean, Local adaptation, SNP

Background

Soybean (Glycine max L.) is a globally important crop that serves as a primary source of protein and oil for both human nutrition and livestock feed industries [1, 2]. In recent years, the rising demand for plant-based protein and health-promoting phytochemicals has intensified interest in exploring the genetic diversity of soybean and accelerating trait improvement [3, 4]. Consequently, extensive efforts are underway to identify valuable genetic resources and dissect agronomically important traits at the molecular level [5].

Modern elite cultivars, however, have undergone intensive selection and recurrent crossing, which has led to a substantial reduction in genetic diversity [6, 7]. As a result, their adaptability to rapidly changing environmental conditions, emerging pests and diseases, and climate-induced stressors has become increasingly constrained [8]. In this context, landrace accessions—characterized by high genetic diversity and long-standing regional adaptation—have reemerged as crucial resources for future-oriented breeding [9] Particularly in East Asia, a wide array of soybean types has been cultivated and consumed over centuries under diverse agroecological conditions [10]. Korean landrace soybeans, preserved through generations of traditional farming practices [11], represent a rich genetic repository that holds insights into natural variation and provides a foundation for breeding programs tailored to regional preferences and environments [12].

Flowering date (FD) and maturity date (MD) are pivotal for soybean adaptation, yield stability, and double-cropping feasibility, with major loci such as E1–E4, GmFT2a, and GmFT5a regulating photoperiod sensitivity, circadian rhythm, and hormonal pathways [13–15]. The number of seeds per pod (NOSPP) contributes directly to yield potential, with GWAS identifying genes like GmKIX8-1 and GmCYP78A72 involved in floral development and assimilate allocation [16, 17]. Seed weight (SW), often expressed as 100-seed weight, is a key yield and quality trait influenced by loci such as GmCYP78A10 and GmPDH1, which affect seed growth, carbohydrate metabolism, and cytoskeleton organization [18, 19]. Although these genetic determinants have been widely studied in diverse germplasm, little is known about their distribution and functional relevance in Korean landrace soybeans, which may harbor unique allelic variants shaped by long-term regional adaptation.

Given the genetic and agronomic significance of these traits, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) offer a powerful approach to dissect their underlying genetic architecture [13]. GWAS enables the detection of genotype–phenotype associations across large panels using high-density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers [20]. These approaches are instrumental for constructing high-resolution genetic maps, identifying trait-associated genes, and developing marker-assisted selection (MAS) pipelines [21]. More recently, GWAS methodologies have expanded beyond single-marker association to incorporate comprehensive frameworks that integrate population structure analysis, linkage disequilibrium (LD)-based candidate gene identification, haplotype-level functional dissection, gene ontology (GO) enrichment, and geographic analyses of allele frequency and phenotypic variation [22]. Such holistic approaches are essential to fully characterize the genetic architecture of complex traits and assess local adaptation across diverse environments [23].

In this study, a comprehensive GWAS was conducted using 1,693 Korean landrace soybean accessions genotyped with 67,222 high-quality SNPs. To ensure both statistical robustness and biological relevance, we combined multi-model GWAS with post-GWAS haplotype-based phenotypic validation, functional gene annotation, and allelic effect analyses. Population structure, LD decay, and FST metrics were assessed to capture genetic diversity, and candidate loci were prioritized based on both statistical significance and functional phenotypic effects. This integrative framework not only elucidates the genetic architecture of important agronomic and seed quality traits but also identifies actionable genetic markers and candidate genes, thereby providing a valuable genomic resource for soybean breeding and aligning to advance crop genetics and improvement.

Results

Population structure, PCA, and genetic differentiation

Population structure analysis was conducted using high-density SNP genotype data from 1,693 Korean landrace soybean accessions (Fig. 1). To determine the optimal number of ancestral populations (K), cross-entropy values were calculated for K ranging from 1 to 20 (Fig. 1A). Although the cross-entropy continuously decreased with increasing K, excessively high K values may compromise interpretability and lead to model overfitting. Therefore, the ancestry proportions of individual accessions were further examined across the K = 15–20 range to identify the most appropriate K value (Fig. S1). At K = 15, more than 250 individuals exhibited nearly pure ancestry (ancestry proportion close to 1.0), and the overall ancestry distribution across accessions was relatively balanced. Hence, K = 15 was selected as the optimal value.

The structure analysis at K = 15 (Fig. 1B) revealed that while some individuals had mixed ancestry components from multiple ancestral populations, others showed a strong genetic affiliation with a single population, indicating the presence of both admixed and genetically distinct groups.

Genome-wide LD decay analysis was also performed to evaluate the extent of LD among SNP markers (Fig. 1C). The red line represents the overall LD trend (r²) across the genome, the green vertical line indicates the inflection point where LD begins to decline rapidly, and the blue horizontal line marks the threshold r² = 0.1. The point at which r² dropped below 0.2 occurred at approximately 309 kb, suggesting that within this distance, SNPs are likely to be inherited together. Therefore, the 309 kb window was adopted as the threshold for candidate gene identification in the GWAS.

PCA was conducted to assess genetic relationships among accessions (Fig. 1D). The three-dimensional PCA plot effectively distinguished genetic variation among populations. Notably, populations such as Pop4 and Pop11 were clearly separated from the others, indicating distinct genetic backgrounds.

To quantitatively assess genetic differentiation, pairwise FST values were calculated based on Wright’s fixation index among the 15 identified populations (Pop1–Pop15) (Fig. 1E, Table S1). Higher FST values reflect greater genetic divergence and reduced gene flow between populations. The analysis revealed substantial differentiation between Pop4 (n = 298) and Pop11 (n = 470) (FST = 0.42), followed by other highly differentiated pairs such as Pop1 (n = 73)–Pop11 (0.38), Pop4–Pop6 (n = 30) (0.35), Pop5 (n = 124)–Pop11 (0.41), and Pop8 (n = 99)–Pop11 (0.49). In contrast, population pairs such as Pop2 (n = 94)–Pop14 (n = 45) (0.06), Pop7 (n = 47)–Pop13 (n = 35) (0.08), and Pop10 (n = 23)–Pop2 (0.09) exhibited low FST values, suggesting high gene flow and little differentiation between them.

Phenotypic variation and trait relationships

Population-level means (± SE) for four traits—FD, MD, NOSPP, and SW—are summarized in Fig. 2A. One-way ANOVA detected strong population effects for all traits (p < 0.001), and post-hoc Duncan’s multiple range tests (DMRT, α = 0.05) resolved several non-overlapping groups, as indicated by the letters above the bars. For FD, pop10 exhibited the latest flowering and formed the top DMRT group, whereas pop3 showed the earliest flowering. For MD, pop12 displayed the latest maturity while pop14 matured earliest. For NOSPP, pop6 had the highest seed number per pod, whereas pop12 had the lowest. For SW, pop7 ranked highest and pop6 the lowest.

Component estimates are presented in Fig. 2B. Across all accessions (N = 1,693; 15 populations), the mean ± SD and CV were as follows: FD, 45.343 ± 7.943 days (CV = 17.517%); MD, 77.742 ± 10.609 days (CV = 13.646%); NOSPP, 2.170 ± 0.328 seeds per pod (CV = 15.130%); and SW, 23.248 ± 9.269 g per 100 seeds (CV = 39.869%). Variance components were estimated with a one-way random-effects model (population as a random intercept; REML for estimation), and the significance of the between-population (σg²) component was evaluated by Maximum Likelihood(ML)-based likelihood-ratio tests (df = 1). The resulting pseudo-heritability estimates were H² = 0.143 for FD (σg² = 9.628; σe² = 57.845; 95% CI, 0.079–0.172; LRT χ² = 106.82; p < 2.2 × 10−16), 0.070 for MD (7.973; 106.600; 0.035–0.094; 53.819; p = 2.20 × 10−13), 0.091 for NOSPP (0.010; 0.102; 0.041–0.114; 54.079; p = 1.93 × 10−13), and 0.615 for SW (64.088; 40.106; 0.574–0.646; 918.62; p < 2.2 × 10−16).

Pairwise associations among FD, MD, NOSPP, and SW were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation with pairwise-complete observations and summarized as a correlation matrix with accompanying histograms and scatterplots (Fig. 2C). Overall, effect sizes were weak to modest (|r| ≤ 0.25). Specifically, MD–NOSPP showed a negative association (r = − 0.25), MD–SW a positive association (r = 0.21), and NOSPP–SW a negative association (r = − 0.18), consistent with a mild seed-number–seed-size trade-off. Correlations involving FD were small in magnitude (FD–MD r = 0.08; FD–NOSPP r = 0.14; FD–SW r = − 0.13).

LD block and haplotype analysis

LD analysis was conducted for 31 of the 38 GWAS-significant loci, retained because they exhibited significant phenotypic differences among haplotypes by DMRT (p < 0.05). Haplotypes were defined from local LD blocks within ± 30 kb, considering only marker sets with pairwise D′ ≥ 0.8 (Haploview). Many of these loci formed well-resolved LD blocks with neighboring markers, enabling haplotype-based assessment of trait variation (Fig. 4). Corresponding haplotype sequences are listed in Table S2.

For example, the FD-associated marker AX-90317042 formed four haplotypes, with accessions carrying Hap3 showing the earliest flowering dates (Fig. 4A). In contrast, AX-90392707 did not show significant phenotypic differences among haplotypes (Fig. 4B), while accessions carrying Hap1 of AX-90406392 flowered significantly earlier than those with Hap2 (Fig. 4C).

For MD, AX-90435384 formed an LD block in which accessions with Hap4 exhibited earlier maturity compared to those with Hap5 (Fig. 4D).

Regarding NOSPP, four LD blocks were identified, and most showed clear phenotypic differentiation among haplotypes. In the case of AX-90334048, five haplotypes were detected, with Hap5 associated with the highest NOSPP and Hap4 with the lowest (Fig. 4E). Similarly, accessions with Hap3 of AX-90525597 (Fig. 4F), Hap2 of AX-90343598 (Fig. 4G), and Hap2 or Hap3 of AX-90467095 (Fig. 4H) exhibited higher NOSPP values.

For SW, AX-90450605 did not show statistically significant differences among haplotypes (Fig. 4I). However, in the other three markers, distinct phenotypic patterns were observed. Accessions carrying Hap2 of AX-90358463 had significantly higher SW compared to other haplotypes (Fig. 4J), while Hap5 of AX-90474666 corresponded to the heaviest seeds and Hap3 to the lightest (Fig. 4K). Lastly, for AX-90486788, accessions with Hap1 or Hap3 produced heavier seeds than those with Hap2 or Hap4 (Fig. 4L).

Geographical distribution of alleles and regional variation in phenotypic traits

Among the significant markers identified through GWAS, 19 SNPs were not part of any LD block but were considered biologically important based on functional annotations. Markers associated with GO terms linked to ≥ 3 candidate genes were prioritized. Fisher’s exact test and chi-square test were used to assess regional differences in allele frequencies, and SNPs with statistically significant p-values (< 0.05) were selected. In particular, markers located near candidate genes mapped to biologically meaningful GO terms were further analyzed for their associations with agronomic traits.

For FD, AX-90453905 was associated with oxidation–reduction process (GO:0055114) and regulation of transcription (GO:0006355) and included three alleles: AA, TT, and AT. In all regions except Jeju, accessions with the TT allele flowered significantly earlier than those with the AA allele (Fig. 5A). Another FD-associated marker, AX-90513027, displayed TT and CC alleles. Accessions carrying the CC allele from Gangwon and Chungbuk regions flowered significantly earlier than accessions from other regions (Fig. 5B).

For MD, two significant markers—AX-90517062 and AX-90420428—were selected based on their mapping to protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468) and regulation of transcription (GO:0006355). AX-90517062 exhibited a higher frequency of the CC allele across most regions. In Gangwon, Chungnam, Jeonbuk, and Jeonnam, accessions carrying CC matured significantly earlier than those with TT, while no significant allele effects were observed in other regions (Fig. 5C). AX-90420428 exhibited TT and GG alleles, and across all regions, accessions with the TT allele matured significantly earlier than those with GG (Fig. 5D).

For NOSPP, AX-90305656 consisted of three allelic groups: CC, TT, and TC. While the TT allele was most frequent in most regions, accessions with the CC allele collected from Gangwon, Gyeonggi, Chungbuk, Chungnam, and Gyeongbuk showed significantly higher NOSPP values (Fig. 5E).

Regarding SW, AX-90341888 was functionally associated with proteolysis (GO:0006508) and had three alleles: AA, GG, and AG (Fig. 5F). Accessions carrying the AG allele from Gangwon and Gyeonggi had significantly lower seed weights than those from other regions, indicating possible regional adaptation effects.

Discussion

Population structure and genetic differentiation among Korean landrace soybeans

The population structure analysis revealed that K = 15 provided the most balanced distribution of ancestry proportions, suggesting that Korean landrace soybeans have undergone extensive diversification through long-term adaptation to local environments. Notably, while some accessions showed strong affiliation with a single ancestral population, others displayed admixed ancestry profiles, reflecting both historical gene flow and the coexistence of genetically distinct subpopulations [24]. These findings were supported by FST estimates, where populations such as Pop4, Pop8, and Pop11 exhibited substantial genetic differentiation, implying that they may have followed relatively independent evolutionary trajectories [25, 26]. This level of intra-species divergence suggests that natural selection or localized breeding pressures have contributed to the accumulation of population-specific genetic variants, reinforcing the uniqueness and breeding value of Korean landraces.

LD decay and discovery of significant and pleiotropic SNPs

The genome-wide LD decay pattern, with r² declining below 0.2 [27] at approximately 309 kb, reflects the extent of genetic linkage among markers in this panel. This value served as a critical threshold for defining candidate gene regions surrounding significant SNPs in the subsequent association analyses. GWAS analysis identified a total of 38 significant SNPs associated with the agronomic traits FD, MD, NOSPP, and SW. Notably, several of these markers exhibited pleiotropic effects, being associated with multiple traits simultaneously. For example, AX-90439043 on chromosome 1 (21,603,721 bp) and AX-90488622 on chromosome 4 (44,504,063 bp) were significantly associated with both FD and NOSPP. Similarly, AX-90458560 on chromosome 20 (7,751,957 bp) was linked to both FD and MD, while AX-90305656 on chromosome 3 (34,014,457 bp) showed significant associations with both NOSPP and SW. These findings suggest that individual loci or genomic regions may influence multiple physiological pathways, underscoring their potential as strategic targets for simultaneous trait improvement in breeding programs [28]. Among the identified SNPs, AX-90346359 on chromosome 18 (59,588,602 bp) explained 42.36% of the phenotypic variation in SW, indicating the presence of a major-effect gene or regulatory element controlling this trait. Such markers with high explanatory power are particularly valuable for marker-assisted selection, as they likely reflect key nodes in the genetic network governing trait expression [29].

Trait-specific GO term enrichment and candidate gene functions

Subsequent GO enrichment analysis revealed biologically meaningful, trait-specific pathways. For FD and MD, enriched GO terms included membrane-associated components, protein phosphorylation, and ATP binding, indicating that the regulation of flowering and maturation is tightly linked to signal transduction and transcriptional control, including photoreception and endogenous signaling cascades [30, 31]. In contrast, genes associated with NOSPP and SW were enriched for sterol metabolism, cytokinesis, proteolysis, and microtubule organization, highlighting roles for cell-cycle regulation, energy metabolism, structural development, and protein turnover in determining yield-related traits [32]. Notably, Glyma.14G018300, a candidate identified for SW, carries GO annotations related to microtubule cytoskeleton organization and cytokinesis, offering physiological insight into mechanisms that may underlie seed filling and final seed weight [33].

To contextualize the novelty of genes proximal to the lead SNPs, a literature-based comparison was performed (Table 2). Of the 71 candidates, 23 were previously reported in soybean, with annotations spanning the focal agronomic traits—FD, MD, NOSPP, and SW—as well as stress-adaptive processes (e.g., disease resistance and abiotic-stress tolerance) (Table S3). This pattern suggests that many candidates occupy regulatory nodes with pleiotropic effects and could serve as multi-purpose markers supporting selection across multiple breeding targets rather than single-trait applications. By contrast, the remaining 46 candidates lack trait-specific functional evidence in soybean and are therefore considered novel with respect to the traits examined here. These loci merit targeted follow-up to resolve causality and prioritize deployable markers.

Haplotype-based trait associations and validation of GWAS results

While candidate genes were identified within a ± 309 kb window based on the genome-wide LD decay threshold, local LD block visualization was performed using a narrower ± 30 kb interval around each lead SNP. This fine-scale window was chosen to capture the immediate region of strongest linkage, improve marker density balance, and remain consistent with previous soybean GWAS studies that employed ± 20–50 kb ranges for haplotype mapping in high-LD regions [29]. Haplotype analysis of markers within LD blocks was then conducted to assess the effects of allelic combinations on phenotypic variation.

For most traits, specific haplotypes showed significant associations with superior phenotypic values, thereby reinforcing the biological relevance of the GWAS results. For instance, in loci associated with FD (e.g., AX-90317042 and AX-90406392), MD (AX-90435384), NOSPP (AX-90334048, AX-90525597, AX-90343598, and AX-90467095), and SW (AX-90358463, AX-90474666, and AX-90486788), certain haplotypes were significantly associated with earlier flowering or maturity, higher numbers of seeds per pod, or increased seed weight. These findings provide indirect yet compelling evidence that the associated genomic regions exert functional influence on trait expression [34].

Conversely, for some markers, no significant phenotypic differences were detected among haplotypes, indicating that not all statistically significant SNPs necessarily correspond to functionally relevant associations. Thus, integrating haplotype–phenotype analyses is an effective approach for filtering false-positive signals and prioritizing functionally meaningful candidate genes for downstream validation [35, 36].

Regional allele frequency analysis and signals of local adaptation

In the case of independent markers that did not form LD blocks, regional allele frequency data were combined with phenotypic traits to assess potential links to local adaptation. Markers such as AX-90453905, AX-90513027, AX-90517062, AX-90420428, AX-90305656, and AX-90341888 were located near genes enriched in GO terms and exhibited significant allele frequency and trait differences across geographic regions. For example, AX-90453905, associated with FD, showed that the TT allele was linked to earlier flowering in most regions. AX-90517062, associated with MD, showed that the CC allele was significantly linked to earlier maturity in Jeonbuk and Chungnam. Similarly, for NOSPP, the CC allele of AX-90305656 was significantly associated with higher pod number in Gangwon and Chungnam, while the AG allele of AX-90341888 was linked to lower seed weight in accessions from Gangwon and Gyeonggi.

Integration of genetic and functional insights for precision breeding

These findings suggest that regional allele-specific trait performance reflects genetic adaptations shaped by long-term selection under local environmental conditions. Such insights provide a practical foundation for designing regionally targeted breeding strategies. In conclusion, this study integrated multiple approaches—including GWAS, LD analysis, GO term-based functional annotation, haplotype evaluation, and regional allele frequency analysis—using Korean landrace soybean accessions [37]. Through this comprehensive framework, we identified trait-associated genes and gene clusters, evaluated genetic diversity, and clarified the link between functional significance and local adaptation. These findings provide valuable insights for future functional genomics research and offer a foundational resource for MAS, regionalized germplasm utilization, and precision molecular breeding using Korean landrace soybeans.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive dissection of the genetic architecture underlying four major agronomic traits in Korean landrace soybeans by integrating GWAS, population structure, LD decay, GO-based functional annotation, haplotype–phenotype validation, and regional allele frequency analyses. The identification of pleiotropic and high-effect SNPs, along with functionally relevant candidate genes and region-specific allele-trait associations, highlights the untapped breeding potential of these landraces. These findings lay the groundwork for future genomic-assisted breeding strategies, enabling precision improvement of soybean traits tailored to local environments and contributing to the sustainable utilization of diverse soybean germplasm.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and agronomic trait evaluation

To prevent genetic erosion and conserve the genetic diversity of Korean soybean landraces, the National Agrobiodiversity Center (NAC) of the Rural Development Administration (Jeonju, South Korea) has undertaken large-scale collection, introduction, and propagation of soybean germplasm. In this study, 1,726 soybean accessions maintained by NAC were evaluated for four key agronomic traits: FD, MD, NOSPP, and SW. FD was recorded as the number of days from sowing to the appearance of the first flower, and MD as the number of days from sowing until approximately 90% of pods exhibited color changes indicating physiological maturity. NOSPP was determined by counting all pods on each plant, and SW was calculated by weighing 100 mature seeds that were dried at 30 °C for 72 h.

Field trials were conducted at the NAC experimental fields (35°50′54.31″ N, 127°7′43.61″ E), characterized by loamy, well-drained soils and a temperate climate with an average annual temperature of 13.9℃ and annual precipitation of approximately 1299.3 mm. During the growing seasons (May–October) of each evaluation year, mean daily temperatures ranged from 17.4℃ to 29.1℃, and total rainfall was 1008.2 mm. Accessions were sown in late May in rows 120 cm apart, with 20 cm spacing between plants. A drip irrigation system provided supplemental water as needed. Fertilization, weed control, and pest/disease management followed the Standard for Research Investigation in Agricultural Science and Technology [38], including manual weeding, application of pre-emergence herbicides, and RDA-recommended pest/disease control measures.

SNP genotyping

Young leaf tissues were collected from each accession during early growth stages for genomic DNA extraction. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer. High-density genotyping of all 1,726 Korean landrace soybean accessions was conducted using the 180 K Axiom® Soya SNP array platform. SNP quality control was performed using TASSEL 5.0 software [39]. First, markers with a call rate below 95% were excluded. Accessions with more than 5% missing data were also removed. Additionally, SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 5% were filtered out to improve the accuracy and robustness of downstream analyses. After quality filtering, a final dataset comprising 1,693 accessions and 67,222 high-quality SNPs was retained for subsequent analyses.

Population structure analysis

To investigate the population structure among soybean accessions, the raw genomic data in Variant Call Format (VCF) were first converted into the STRUCTURE-compatible.str format using VCFtools. The genetic structure of the accessions and the optimal number of ancestral populations (K) were inferred using the sparse non-negative matrix factorization (sNMF) algorithm implemented in the LEA package within the R environment. Model fit across K values was assessed based on cross-entropy, and the elbow point—where the reduction in cross-entropy began to plateau—was selected as the optimal K. Population structure results were visualized as stacked bar plots according to the final K value. In addition, to capture broader patterns of genetic variation, PCA was performed using the adegenet package in R. A three-dimensional PCA based on the genotype matrix was used to evaluate genetic relationships and distances among accessions in a more spatially intuitive manner. To further explore genetic relationships and connectivity among populations, a chord diagram was generated using the circlize package in R. This visualization highlighted shared allele distributions and the extent of genetic exchange or interaction across inferred populations.

GWAS and linkage disequilibrium

To uncover marker–trait associations across the 1,693 Korean landrace soybean accessions, GWAS was conducted using the GAPIT (Genome Association and Prediction Integrated Tool) package in R. Three complementary models were employed: the multi-locus mixed model (MLMM), the Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU), and the Bayesian-information and Linkage-disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway (BLINK). These models improve statistical power and minimize false positives by accounting for both population structure and kinship. Significance thresholds for marker–trait associations were set at –log10(P) ≥ 3.0 as the minimum cutoff, based on the balance between detection power and error rate [40]. SNPs exceeding this threshold were considered significant, and actual –log10(P) values for these loci may be much higher, indicating stronger statistical evidence.

Following GWAS, local LD structure was examined for each of the 38 significant SNPs. For every locus, SNPs within ± 30 kb were extracted, and pairwise LD statistics (D′) were calculated using TASSEL 5.0. Haplotypes were defined when adjacent SNPs exhibited strong LD (D′ ≥ 0.8; Haploview). Accessions were grouped by haplotype, and phenotypic means were compared among groups using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p < 0.05. Loci were retained for LD/haplotype analysis only if at least one trait showed a significant haplotype effect (distinct DMRT letter groupings), resulting in 31 loci being analyzed. LD decay was further characterized by plotting r² against physical distance between SNP pairs. The LD decay threshold was defined as the point at which r2 first dropped below 0.2, which was used to delimit candidate gene search windows. Genome-wide average pairwise r² dropped below 0.2 at approximately 309 kb, and a ± 309 kb window was applied around each retained SNP. For reference, r2 = 0.1 was considered the point of complete LD decay.

GO enrichment and allele frequency

To functionally characterize candidate genes located near significant SNPs associated with each agronomic trait, gene ontology (GO) mapping was performed. Genomic positions of all candidate genes were annotated using the Glycine max genome reference file (glyma.Wm82.gnm2.ann1.RVB6.gene_models_main.gff3) obtained from the SoyBase database. GO terms were assigned to each gene based on this annotation, and enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R. This analysis enabled the identification of trait-specific GO terms that were significantly enriched among the candidate genes. For significant SNPs that formed LD blocks within or near candidate genes, haplotype structures were analyzed using Haploview software. Phenotypic variation among accessions carrying different haplotype groups was then evaluated to assess the functional relevance of the haplotype combinations. In contrast, for independent SNPs that did not form LD blocks and showed low LD with nearby markers, regional allele frequency analysis was conducted. The geographical distribution of major alleles was visualized using geographical and ggplot2 packages in R to investigate spatial patterns of allelic variation and potential local adaptation.

Statistical analysis

All agronomic trait data were collected from 10 randomly selected plants per accession at the same developmental stage. Trait values were averaged for each accession and presented as means with corresponding standard deviations. Various software tools were employed for genomic and phenotypic data analysis. TASSEL 5.0 and VCFtools were used for VCF data preprocessing and LD analysis; PLINK and Haploview were used for LD block construction and haplotype analysis; and R software (version 4.2.2; RStudio, Boston, MA, USA) was used for GWAS, GO enrichment analysis, data visualization, and phenotypic variation analysis. For statistical comparisons, significance testing was performed as needed depending on the trait and grouping factor. These included analysis of variance (ANOVA), Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT), and chi-square tests, which were applied to evaluate differences in trait distributions among haplotype or allele groups.
