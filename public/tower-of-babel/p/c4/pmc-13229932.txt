Traffic jams: music and traffic noise interact to influence the vividness, sentiment, and spatiotemporal properties of directed mental imagery
Authors: Ceren Ayyildiz, Jon B Prince, Joanna Delalande, Steffen A Herff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusic and traffic noise are pervasive elements of modern everyday auditory environments. However, their combined effects on higher-order cognitive processes like mental imagery remain unclear. In a directed mental imagery task, 107 participants continued imaginary journeys towards a landmark, responded to prompts about each journey, and provided written descriptions. Each trial had either silence, traffic noise, music, or combined music and noise. Bayesian Mixed-Effects models revealed that compared to silence, music enhanced imagery vividness and emotional sentiment; traffic noise enhanced vividness but not sentiment; and noise combined with music diminished music’s effect on emotional sentiment. The auditory context further shaped the physical properties of the imagined content: both music and traffic noise increased the imagined distances travelled, whereas only music extended the imagined time travelled. Furthermore, elevated traffic-related themes occurred in both conditions involving traffic noise. Overall, both music and noise can modulate aspects of mental imagery, influencing its vividness, emotional tone, spatiotemporal properties, and thematic content in complex ways. We discuss the practical implications of these findings for imagery-based therapies.Subject terms: Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Perhaps because music and traffic noise constantly surround us, there is substantial research on their processing and perception. However, there is considerably less research on how such auditory stimulation can influence goal-directed mental imagery (i.e., internal perception in the absence of corresponding physical stimuli; Kosslyn et al., 2001). Understanding the role of auditory processing and perception in mental imagery is important because of the vast application of mental imagery in both voluntary (recreational or athletic) and clinical contexts (Janjigian, 2024; Nanay, 2023; Pearson et al., 2015; Pearson, 2019). For instance, while an individual may rely on mental imagery to plan a journey, the presence of background music or the constant hum of traffic could influence the imagery vividness and emotional sentiment. Accordingly, this study explored how background music and noise contribute in affecting mental imagery, in terms of vividness, the emotional sentiment of imagery content, and imagined time/distance travelled.

Mental imagery differs from “true” perception (Dijkstra and Fleming, 2023), and is a central component of everyday cognitive capacities such as memory (Milton et al., 2021), future planning (Bulley and Irish, 2018), language comprehension (Dils and Boroditsky, 2010), decision-making (Skard et al., 2021), and self-regulation (Loft and Cameron, 2013). Mental imagery is also strongly linked to emotion, in fact, it can be more effective for emotion regulation than verbal processing (Holmes and Mathews, 2005; Holmes et al., 2006; Holmes et al., 2009). Manipulating certain characteristics of imagery, such as vividness (generally self-reported clarity, richness, and intensity of the imagined content), emotional sentiment, and spatiotemporal aspects, is crucial as they can determine the outcome of applications such as in therapy (Brown et al., 2023; Mota et al., 2015; Taruffi et al., 2023), and performance enhancement (Callow et al., 2006; Castellar and Duarte-Mendes, 2019; Castellar et al., 2025). Here, we distinguish between undirected and directed mental imagery, where the former is akin to mind-wandering (Christoff et al., 2016), whereas the latter refers to goal-directed or intentional mental imagery. Directed imagery is usually intended to achieve a specific goal, and therefore is guided by the individual themselves, a therapist, a recording, and/or a coach (Holmes et al., 2007). Because directed imagery involves intentional control over the content and vividness of mental imagery, it enables controlled and replicable investigation of imagery processes by specifying what participants imagine, when they imagine it, and the auditory environment in which imagery occurs.

Music is an effective tool for modulating imagery characteristics (Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2022; Taruffi 2021). Compared to silence, music enhances imagery vividness by an average of 2.7 times, and increases positive emotional sentiment (Herff et al. 2021; Herff et al., 2022). Indeed, music can even change the vividness of autobiographical memories (Belfi et al., 2016); such memories are frequently associated with vivid mental imagery in which an individual travels back to the sounds, touch, or sight of that past event (Jakubowski, 2022). Music can also alter the content and nature of mental imagery, likely through evoking complex and nuanced emotions and moods (Juslin and Västfjäll, 2008; Herff et al., 2025; Taruffi and Küssner, 2019; Taruffi et al., 2023). Music-evoked emotions activate visual and motor brain areas, suggesting that music triggers sensory responses in the absence of appropriate external cues—the very definition of mental imagery (Trost et al., 2012). There are suggestions of more complex, bidirectional relationship in which low-level affect (simple, immediate emotional responses triggered by music) induces visual imagery, which subsequently shapes higher-level emotions that are socially and cognitively distinctive from basic emotions such as happiness and sadness (Taruffi and Küssner, 2019).

Interestingly, recent research shows that musical genre, along with factors including familiarity, enjoyment, and emotional expression, shapes the kinds of thoughts listeners report during music listening (van der Walle et al., 2025). Even more fine-grained, small-scale features of music can have robust effects on imagery: micro-variations in musical performance (small deviations in timing and timbre that improve perceived naturalness) amplify the vividness of imagined content compared with mechanically produced drumming (Ayyildiz et al., 2025a). Such findings suggest that both large-scale (e.g., genre-related) and small-scale (e.g., micro-structural) musical features shape not only what people imagine but also how their imagined experiences unfold in time and space. Whereas mental imagery preserves spatiotemporal relationships (Decety and Jeannerod, 1995), adding music dilates imagined space and time (Droit-Volet et al., 2010; Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2022; Schäfer et al., 2013).

Although music uniquely enhances mental imagery characteristics, the effects of other auditory stimuli, particularly noise, remain unknown; research has primarily explored their effects on productivity and attention (Furnham and Strbac, 2002; Kou et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2021; for music-related effects on cognitive performance, see Cheah et al., 2022; Kiss and Linnell, 2024). Broadly, noise represents an unwanted distraction that is both loud (typically >85 dBA; Zhou et al., 2024) and conveys a sense of randomness (Hainge 2013; Keizer, 2010). However, the boundary between music and noise is blurred—what is considered structured and meaningful to one listener may be perceived as chaotic and intrusive by another (Brattico et al., 2017; Mencke et al., 2019; Reybrouck et al., 2019). Contemporary compositions, as well as indigenous musical traditions, intentionally incorporate atonality, dissonance, or overlapping sounds that may be perceived as either music or noise depending on cultural and personal contexts (Reybrouck et al., 2019). Recognising the distinction between music and noise is important in understanding how different auditory environments affect mental imagery.

Here, we focus on environmental noise, which is omnipresent in homes and workplaces and has notable detrimental effects on health (WHO, 2022). In particular, we chose road traffic as it is the most common source of environmental noise and may be particularly disruptive (Muzet, 2007) because it has both high sound level variability (ranging from soft engine hums to loud roads) and unpredictable sound bursts (e.g., car horns and tire squealing). Such noise can disrupt performance on lower-order cognitive tasks involving processing and memory, including text and verbal recall, reading speed, reading comprehension, mental arithmetic, and Stroop tests (Hygge et al., 2003; Schlittmeier et al., 2015; Sörqvist, 2010). Beyond basic cognitive functions, road traffic may also affect higher-order cognition (potentially mediated by annoyance), as demonstrated by associations between noise exposure and increased depression (Eze et al., 2020) and anxiety (Dzhambov and Lercher, 2019). Acoustic noise (n.b., not traffic noise) from a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) machine disrupted imagery vividness on a directed mental imagery task (Mazard et al., 2002). However, adding crowd noise to music recordings did not affect non-directed mental imagery (Davidson and Keene, 2019). A possible reason for this discrepancy may lie in differences in cognitive demands (directed imagery requiring more focus), noise type and characteristics (natural variability in crowd noise versus monotony in fMRI noise), and the surrounding context (Smith, 1989).

Noise can also have beneficial effects on cognitive performance through stochastic resonance (Di Ponzio et al., 2024), a phenomenon where moderate noise ( < 85 dBA) enhances signal processing and improves cognitive functions. For example, background auditory white noise can enhance working memory (Awada et al., 2022) and attention (Nigg et al., 2024), especially in inattentive children (Chen et al., 2022) and neurodivergent adults (Nigg et al., 2024) (e.g., individuals with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder). A review (Zhou et al., 2024) of 43 studies (1980–2023) also found minimal evidence that moderate broadband noise (55–85 dBA) negatively affects cognitive performance, with observed effects primarily influenced by specific conditions such as repetitive exposure, cognitive load, and individual differences (e.g., sex). Considering that prior research predominantly explored the negative impacts of environmental noise and some aspects of moderate broadband noise on human cognition (Thompson et al., 2022), these findings highlight the need to further explore the nuanced effects of more ecologically valid noise (traffic noise), where the main auditory stimulus remains clear and understandable, yet the background noise is still present and perceptible.

Understanding the precise effects of both noise and music influencing imagery characteristics, especially when combined, is essential for clarifying how different auditory environments shape mental experiences and perception. The present study comprises an experimental test of how music, traffic noise, and their combination affect the perceived vividness and sentiment of participants’ directed imagery. Adapting the directed mental imagery paradigm (Herff et al., 2021), participants heard either music, traffic noise, both (with traffic noise 15 dB softer than music), or silence while watching a short (15 s) video clip (see Fig. 1) and subsequently closing their eyes. They then reported imagery vividness, the imagined time and distance travelled, and wrote a description of the contents of their imagery episode.

The hypotheses are that (1) compared to silence, imagery during music listening will be more vivid, have more positive emotional sentiment, and greater imagined time and distance. These expectations are based on prior work showing that music enhances the vividness, emotional sentiment, and spatiotemporal aspects of imagined content (Ayyildiz et al., 2025a; Herff et al., 2021, Herff et al., 2022, Herff et al., 2025). Additionally, (2) compared to silence, the effects of traffic noise will decrease imagery vividness, have more negative emotional sentiment, yet have greater imagined time and distance (due to the implied presence of motion). Further, (3) we predict that compared to music alone, the mixed music and traffic noise will decrease evoked imagery vividness and positive emotional sentiment because traffic noise is generally negatively valenced (more annoying; Eze et al., 2020; Reybrouck et al., 2019), but imagined time and distance will be comparable, being shaped more by perceptual and motor continuity rather than emotional valence (e.g., Decety and Jeannerod, 1995; Droit-Volet et al., 2010). Beyond these primary measures, we also investigated how auditory conditions influenced the relationship between imagined time and distance travelled. As an exploratory measure, we derived a psychological index of imagined speed (the ratio of imagined distance to imagined time) to capture potential differences in how quickly participants mentally traversed the imagined journey under each auditory condition.

Methods

Participants

Data were collected from 122 undergraduate students at Murdoch University, Perth, Australia. Of these, 15 were excluded for incomplete data (N = 12) or failing to engage with the task (N = 3), leaving a final sample of 107. The final sample included 84 females and 23 males aged 17–50 (M = 22.89, SD = 6.47). Participants were reimbursed for their time with either course credit (N = 101) or $20 payment (N = 6). All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and provided informed consent. The Murdoch University Human Research Ethics Committee approved this experiment (2023/050).

Music and noise stimuli

The auditory stimuli comprised four samples of traffic noise and three musical pieces. The musical stimuli were a subset from a prior study that reported high vividness ratings for these excerpts (Herff et al., 2021). Specifically, the musical stimuli included Ravel’s orchestral rendition of Claude Debussy’s ‘Tarantelle Styrienne’, conducted by L. Slatkin, Orchestre National de Lyon, 2016; ‘My Favourite Things’ performed by The John Coltrane Quartet, 1961; and Bach’s ‘O Haupt voll Blut und Wunden’, conducted by J. E. Gardiner, Monteverdi Choir, English Baroque Soloists, 1989. Participants heard only the first 105 s of each musical piece. To minimise habituation and piece-specific biases, different musical excerpts were used rather than repeating a single piece. This ensures that effects reflected more general responses to music rather than idiosyncratic features of a specific composition. These clips had previously been loudness-normalised to the common value of −23 LUFS (±5 × 10−7), i.e., 70–80 dB, as per European Broadcasting Union R-128 (EBU, 2011).

Traffic noise was selected here as a pervasive environmental noise pollutant likely to be heard in the background of a therapy session (Muzet, 2007; Schlittmeier et al., 2015). The specific noise clip used was the low-diversity city noise stimulus from Stobbe et al. (2022). In that study, these traffic noise soundscapes had no effects on lower-order cognition (accuracy and speed on a digit-span recall task and a continuous recognition n-back task) but did have effects on self-reported depressiveness, suggesting an impact on higher-order cognitive processes. The stimulus features car horns, combustion engines, and a constant, subtle traffic flow, creating high noise signal variability and frequent deviant sounds (engine revving, vehicle horns, brakes, etc.), which have been found to contribute to noise disruptiveness (Hughes et al., 2007; Jones et al., 1992; Vachon et al., 2017). The 6-min clip was split into four separate clips of 105 s. This meant that, although participants heard the same disruptive noise type throughout all noise conditions in the experiment, they did not hear the exact same exemplar more than once. Each 105-s clip was loudness normalised with the pyloudnorm (Steinmetz and Reiss, 2021) Python library and had a brief (5-ms) fade-out applied to avoid clipping.

In the combined noise-music conditions, the noise was set to be 15 dB softer than the music condition. The music was louder than the noise to mimic the effects of traffic noise as background. Pilot work revealed that a 15 dB signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of music relative to background noise avoided potential auditory masking of the music stimuli while still being disruptive. Note that other noise types disrupt lower-order cognitive tasks with SNRs between −5 and +15 dB (Kjellberg et al., 2008; Murphy et al., 2000). The combined noise-music clips were also loudness normalised to the same LUFS.

Procedure

Participants completed the experiment individually, either online on their own device or in-person at the Murdoch University Music Cognition Lab. As the experiment was a within-subject manipulation, this hybrid approach did not affect the design validity.

Participants completed the experiment via the web-based platform Pavlovia. Participants viewed the visual inducer previously used in Herff et al. (2021), a 15-s video of the opening sequence of the video game ‘Journey’ (with written permission of Jenova Chen, CEO of ThatGameCompany, https://thatgamecompany.com, see Fig. 1). The video features a figure ascending a small hill (Fig. 1a). At the top of the hill, a vague landmark (an illuminated mountain) appears in the far distance (Fig. 1b). This visual inducer offered a clear start and direction for the guided mental imagery task. After viewing the inducer, participants were instructed to close their eyes and imagine the figure continuing to walk towards the landmark. A gong sound was played signalling the start of the mental imagery task. After 90 s, a second gong sound instructed participants to open their eyes and stop the mental imagery task. In each trial, from the beginning of the video until the end of the imagined period, participants heard either the disruptive noise condition, one of the music conditions, one of the mixed music and noise conditions, or silence. After each imagined period, participants completed six self-report questions adapted from Herff et al. (2021) to quantify vividness, imagined distance, and imagined time. They estimated both the real and imagined time intervals (in seconds, minutes, or longer units), and both the real and imagined distances in metres or kilometres). Participants then rated how vivid their imagery was on a scale from 0 (not very clear) to 100 (very clear), and described their imagery in detail in a free-text box. To avoid introducing bias, no formal definition of ‘imagination’ was provided—participants were left to interpret the instructions freely.

As the task involved a visual inducer accompanied by auditory stimuli, most responses reflected primarily visual imagery, though many also included non-visual elements such as kinaesthetic components.

Participants repeated the above process for each condition for a total of eight trials. Each participant heard, in a random order, one silent trial, one randomly assigned noise sample, three musical pieces, and the same three musical pieces with added noise. In the combined music and noise conditions, assignment of the four noise samples to the three musical clips was also randomised to ensure effects were not specific to a particular noise/music combination. This randomisation ensured that participants did not repeatedly encounter conditions in a fixed order, thereby minimising potential interference based on anchoring, or at least equally distributing such effects across condition and increasing the robustness of the effects observed. The final stimuli and the spectrograms and energy spectra for each possible condition can be accessed on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/pmvb9; Fig. S1). After the last mental imagery trial, participants completed two self-report scales as detailed below. The experimental session from start to finish took around one hour to complete, depending on the detail of participants’ descriptions.

Measures

To determine participants’ musical background, we administered the Goldsmith-Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI) (Müllensiefen et al., 2014), a well-validated, widely used measure of musical training developed using a large sample of English-speaking adult participants from the general population. Only the musical training subscale (seven items, M score = 3, SD = 1.30) was used in analysis. There were no hypotheses for musical training effects, instead this subscale was included to control for potential effects from formal training as have been identified in prior studies (Presicce and Bailes, 2019).

The short form of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) (Lovibond and Lovibond, 1995) was also administered to investigate the effects of mood symptoms on mental imagery across multiple experiments (Herff et al., 2022). The DASS-21 has demonstrated good reliability and validity in assessing depression, anxiety, and stress in non-clinical populations, based on large samples representative of the general adult population (Henry and Crawford, 2005). The 21-item version of the measure also has several advantages over the full-length version, being shorter with a cleaner factor structure and smaller inter-factor correlations (Antony et al., 1998). The DASS results were not included in this analysis as they are part of a larger study and will be reported elsewhere.

Imagined sentiment was assessed by applying the National Language Tool Kit (NLTK) (Bird et al., 2009) and the Valence Aware Dictionary for Sentiment Reasoning (VADER) model (Hutto and Gilbert, 2014) to detailed descriptions of the mental imagery provided in the free-format responses. The VADER works by mapping lexical features to emotional valence and intensity on a continuum ranging from negative to positive emotionality. Sentiment scores were given a numerical value, with higher values indicating more positive sentiment. Descriptions were also content-analysed for the inclusion of traffic-related terminology by the second first co-author, who was blinded to the respective experimental conditions whilst performing the annotation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical approach closely follows the approach used in prior work deploying the same paradigm (Ayyildiz et al., 2025a; Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2022; Herff et al., 2025). We used Bayesian Mixed-Effects models to predict our variables of interest (Vividness; Sentiment; Imagined Time; Imagined Distance; Imagined Speed; and whether the descriptions of imagined content contained mentions of traffic) while controlling for cross-random effects of participants and trial number (Baayen et al., 2008). Trial number was included as a covariate in all models to further account for residual order or repetition effects. We also included musical expertise as a predictor; however, we did not observe strong evidence that it influenced the models’ predictions. We implemented all models in R using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017). Similar to prior work in auditory perception (Breaden Madden et al., 2023; Cecchetti et al., 2023; Dobrowohl et al., 2019; Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2023; Milne et al., 2023; Smit et al., 2020; Smit et al., 2022), all continuous variables were standardised (M = 0, SD = 1), and all models were provided with a weakly informative prior (a t-distribution with a mean of 0, a standard deviation of 1, and 3 degrees of freedom) (Gelman et al., 2008). Because participants varied dramatically in their reported imagined time and distance travelled, we natural log-scaled participants’ responses, before performing participant-wise normalisation (M = 0, SD = 1). Imagined speed was computed as imagined distance divided by imagined time (m/s), log-transformed, and normalised participant-wise to obtain relative imagined speed values.

We ran all models with 1000 warm-ups and 3000 iterations on four chains. All models converged (all R-hats = 1.00). To evaluate the evidence, we performed hypothesis tests and report model coefficients (β) relevant to the specific hypotheses, the estimated error of these coefficients (EEβ), as well as the evidence ratio in favour of a given hypothesis (Oddsβ). For convenience, we denote effects that can be considered ‘significant’ under an alpha level of 5% with * (i.e., evidence ratio ≥ 19) (Milne and Herff, 2020).

Results

Vividness

We observed strong evidence that, compared to the Silence condition, participants reported more vivid imagery in all other conditions: Music (β = 0.82, EEβ = 0.08, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*), Traffic (β = 0.77, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*), and Music+Traffic (β = 0.78, EEβ = 0.08, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*). Vividness was comparable between the Music, Traffic, and Music+Traffic conditions (all Odds < 3). Figure 2a, b depicts these results.

There is strong evidence that the Music (β = 0.59, EEβ = 0.11, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*), and Music+Traffic conditions (β = 0.26, EEβ = 0.11, Odds (β > 0) = 105.67*) led to more positive sentiment in the imagined content compared to the Silence condition. Compared to the Silence condition, we observed strong evidence that the imagined time travelled was greater in both the Music (β = 0.43, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*) and the Music+Traffic conditions (β = 0.27, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) = 319.00*) but not in the Traffic alone condition (β = −0.10, EEβ = 0.12, Odds (β < 0) = 3.63). Distance

There is strong evidence that compared to Silence, all other conditions showed greater imagined distance travelled: Music (β = 0.64, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*), Traffic (β = 0.29, EEβ = 0.13, Odds (β > 0) = 86.91*), and Music+Traffic (β = 0.62, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*). Both the Music (β = 0.35, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*) as well as the Music+Traffic conditions (β = 0.33, EEβ = 0.10, Odds (β > 0) = 7999*) led to greater imagined distances travelled compared to the Traffic alone condition, but were comparable with each other (β = 0.02, EEβ = 0.07, Odds (β > 0) = 1.5). Figure 3c, d depicts these findings.

Speed

We observed strong evidence that Silence had lower imagined speed than both the Traffic (β = 0.42, EEβ = 0.13, Odds (β > 0) = 3999*) and Music+Traffic (β = 0.31, EEβ = 0.11, Odds (β > 0) = 499*) conditions. However, there is no compelling evidence that Silence and Music differed (Odds = 2.32; Fig. 4a). Music had slower imagined speed than both Traffic and Music+Traffic, which were comparable with each other (Odds = 0.17). The imagined time-distance space (Fig. 4b) mirrored this pattern, with Traffic and Music+Traffic conditions showing regions of greater imagined distance than Silence for comparable or shorter durations, suggesting faster imagined speed.

Imagined traffic content

We observed clear support that the imagined content was influenced by the auditory condition. However, as this analysis was not pre-registered and was conducted post-hoc, these findings should be considered exploratory. The probability of imagining traffic-related content was comparable between the Silence and the Music condition (β = 0.07, EEβ = 0.40, Odds (β > 0) = 1.25). However, both the Music+Traffic and, in particular, the Traffic alone conditions showed higher probabilities of imagining traffic-related content when compared to Silence (Traffic: β = 3.67, EEβ = 0.41, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*; Music+Traffic: β = 2.55, EEβ = 0.37, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*), as well as compared to the Music alone conditions (Traffic: β = 3.60, EEβ = 0.30, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*; Music+Traffic: β = 2.48, EEβ = 0.24, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*). In addition, we observed strong evidence that participants in the Traffic alone condition imagined more traffic-related content than participants in the Music+Traffic condition (β = 1.12, EEβ = 0.19, Odds (β > 0) > 9999*). Discussion

In the present study, we investigated whether music, traffic noise, and their combination affect the imagery characteristics of vividness, emotional sentiment, and imagined time/distance travelled in a directed mental imagery task. Compared to silence, music enhanced imagery vividness, positive sentiment, imagined time, and imagined distance. In addition, traffic noise had a similar effect on vividness as music but had a lesser impact on imagined time, distance, and emotional sentiment. Adding traffic noise to music reduced effects on sentiment (compared to the music-alone condition) but did not affect imagined vividness, imagined time or distance. Additionally, traffic noise led to faster imagined speed compared to silence, and it was also reflected in participants’ imagined content, demonstrating its influence on mental imagery. In the following, we discuss the results in detail, as well as their theoretical and applied implications.

Vividness

Compared to silence, all other conditions had comparable effects on imagined vividness. Thus, multiple forms of auditory input can increase directed imagery vividness. Interestingly, noise on its own enhanced vividness to a similar degree to music—why? Juslin and Västfjäll (2008) propose that the mechanisms through which music evokes emotions—including mental imagery and episodic memory—may be present in non-musical stimuli. Indeed, an exploration of different sensory cues used to retrieve autobiographical memories suggests that non-musical auditory information (including environmental sounds) is a dominant mechanism for probing vivid memories (Willander et al., 2015). Environmental sounds can also evoke more memories than music, although with less positive valence (Jakubowski and Eerola, 2022). Our findings align with the idea that music is not unique in its ability to evoke vivid mental images and that the acoustic features of a variety of non-musical sounds can induce rich imagery—including moderate levels of environmental background noise, such as traffic sound.

Despite the effects of both music-alone and noise-alone conditions, their combination did not have an additive effect; instead, all non-silence conditions showed similar levels of vividness. Perhaps participants were able to ignore the traffic noise in the combined condition, given that we set the noise level to be 15 dB quieter than music and considered a moderate level of noise. These results may illustrate a phenomenon similar to the ‘cocktail party’ effect, in which listeners suppress speech from competing sources to better concentrate on a target speech stimulus through auditory stream segregation (Bronkhorst, 2015; Cherry, 1953). However, this explanation cannot explain why vividness in the noise condition was equal to all conditions except silence. Furthermore, vehicle- and traffic-related words featured in many of the imagery descriptions in the trials with traffic noise, so traffic noise contributed to the imagined content yet did not decrease imagery vividness.

Instead of tuning out unexpected or competing stimuli, it is possible to ‘tune them in’ to incorporate them into the signal of interest. Testing listening comprehension in the presence of an attention-demanding stimulus, Russo and Pichora-Fuller (2008) found that older adults focused their attention on the speech foreground and attempted to tune out the music background, whereas younger adults attended to both. In this study, 91% of the participants were younger adults (<30 years old), perhaps enabling them to tune in and incorporate the traffic noise into their imagined episodes, particularly because they were unconstrained in their imagination content and relied on perceptual mechanisms to tune out the noise. Younger listeners may naturally filter out noise through perceptual mechanisms, while older adults often rely on cognitive resources such as memory and attentional control (Pichora-Fuller et al., 1995). This difference may help explain why younger participants in our study integrated noise into their mental imagery, though the extent of any disruption remains unclear. It also seems unlikely that noise was the sole cause of the effects on imagery vividness in the combined trials, since music was significantly louder than the background noise and itself is a powerful enhancer of imagery vividness. Perhaps a lower (music) signal to (traffic) noise ratio could reveal different effects of noise. Alternatively, perhaps the mechanisms involved are more dichotomous in that mental imagery might draw from any form of concurrent auditory signal to enrich the vividness and overall quality of mental imagery (provided the input is sufficiently rich and not overly disruptive). Unfortunately, the current study cannot disentangle the relative contributions of noise and music on vividness in the combined condition.

Another possible explanation is that music enhanced the memory of the imagery rather than the imagery itself. Music is known to enhance recall, increase resistance to interference from other information, and make internally generated experiences feel easier to access (Cecchetti et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2018; Herff et al., 2019; Schaal et al., 2021). However, because participants described their imagery immediately after each 90-second trial, it seems unlikely that the observed effects were driven solely by differences in memory retrieval. Moreover, the content analysis showed that participants in the auditory conditions had rich, coherent content that clearly reflected the corresponding stimuli (e.g., theme-related content) and did not exhibit recall difficulties, further suggesting that the manipulations influenced imagery generation itself rather than post-hoc memory. This reasoning applies particularly strongly to auditory conditions, although differences involving silence could also reflect subtle introspective factors such as mind-wandering (Kiss and Linnell, 2024).

Emotional sentiment

Unlike vividness, the effects of music and traffic noise on sentiment mix together. Although participants’ imagery was similarly vivid in the music trials regardless of whether they contained traffic noise, their associated sentiment was more negative in the presence of noise. This finding supports the notion that participants were unable to tune out the traffic noise and instead allowed it to shape their imagery content (and thus associated sentiment). Noise alone, however, resulted in sentiment comparable to silence. Because environmental sounds can generate emotional responses (Ma and Thompson, 2015), noise could have decreased emotional sentiment relative to silence. However, even though silence is a useful control condition, it nevertheless is not truly ‘neutral’ or emotionless as the complete absence of auditory input can invoke negative sentiment (Wilson et al., 2014). Regardless, traffic noise evokes imagined content that is similar in sentiment to that evoked by silence but is not necessarily the same content.

Additionally, effects of auditory stimuli (musical and non-musical) on imagery characteristics tend to be stimulus-specific. Prior work found that different pieces evoked different degrees of sentiment of imagined content (Herff et al., 2021; Koelsch et al., 2019; Taruffi et al., 2017). For example, a relaxing piece evoked more positive sentiment than silence, despite no difference between silence and an arousing piece (Herff et al. 2022). Accordingly, the effects of a non-musical signal likely depend on specific and as-yet unclear acoustic and associative features. Tempo is also a likely modulator, as only the faster rendition of a particular piece induced positive sentiment (Herff et al., 2021). Valence, familiarity, and contrast of auditory stimuli (primarily music) also affect imagery characteristics (Dahl et al., 2022; Day et al., 2020; Margulis, 2017; Sheldon et al., 2020; Sheldon and Donahue, 2017; Taruffi et al., 2017; van der Walle et al., 2025). Thus, a noise stimulus with different acoustic features might shape emotional sentiment more dramatically.

Imagined time, distance, and speed

We extend the existing findings on imagined time and distance in directed mental imagery (Ayyildiz et al., 2025a; Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2022) by showing that despite its potential cognitive demand, traffic noise had a comparable influence as silence on imagined time travelled. Accordingly, traffic noise, with the present 15 dB, did not decrease or increase the time-dilating effects in a mental imagery context. Imagined distance, however, shows that the music-only and traffic-only noise conditions increased the imagined distance travelled compared to silence, with music having a more pronounced effect than traffic noise. Similar to imagined time, the combined condition yielded equivalent effects as music alone. The combined music-traffic condition produced a similar increase in imagined distance as the music condition, though slightly smaller in magnitude.

One explanation for traffic noise affecting imagined distance but not time is the familiarity with vehicles and their tendency to travel at higher speeds (more distance over the same time) than other means of transport, such as walking (shown as part of the visual inducer in our experimental task). The increased mentions of traffic-related words in conditions involving traffic noise mean that participants incorporated vehicles into their imagined journeys. Although traffic noise does not distort time like music, a faster vehicle would travel greater distances than other transportation modes within the same given time. Yet, participants imagined greater distances travelled in the music-only compared to the traffic-only condition (see Fig. 3c). Therefore, either (a) the additional imagined travel time in the music condition compared to the traffic condition is sufficient to outpace the vehicle in the traffic condition, or (b) in the music condition, participants also utilise alternative modes of transportation (perhaps by flying, mountain biking, etc.), (c) a combination of both, or (d) the relationship between imagined spatiotemporal characteristics and auditory conditions is nonlinear and may not follow a predictable pattern.

Fortunately, the combined music-traffic condition can help disentangle these possible explanations as it yields similar distances travelled compared to the music-only condition. Perhaps music overrides the effect of traffic (explaining the equivalence between music and combined conditions), which then discards the distance boost of using cars (over silence), in favour of whichever transport modes participants use in the music-only condition, as well as the advantage of additional travel time. Yet this is unlikely because in the combined condition, traffic-related themes remain strong in the imagined content (Fig. 5). Alternatively, the participants might use music’s stretching of imagined time to travel extra distance with a vehicle? This is certainly possible given the high prevalence of traffic-related themes in the combined condition. However, this prevalence is still meaningfully lower in the combined condition compared to the traffic-only condition, suggesting that there is less vehicle usage in the music-only compared to the combined condition. If the music-only condition outdistanced the traffic-only condition solely due to the increased travel time (possible explanation (a) above), then the combined condition should show greater distances travelled than the music condition, as it combines increased travel time (Fig. 3a, b) with faster modes of transportation. Yet they are comparable, which suggests that the average speed of these alternative modes of transportation is roughly equivalent to that of the vehicles imagined in the traffic condition. In summary, the present pattern of results suggest that explanation (c) is so far the most compelling, specifically that participants use a combination of alternative modes of transportation and greater travel times in the music condition to outpace the silence and traffic condition. In addition, the mean travel speed of these modes is roughly equivalent to that of the vehicles in the traffic condition, and when combining music with traffic noise, vehicles replace some of those alternative modes of transportation.

Correspondingly, the analysis of imagined speed showed a different pattern compared to imagined time and distance alone. Both the traffic and mixed music-traffic conditions led to greater imagined speed than silence, whereas music, this time, did not compellingly differ. Importantly, imagined speed here is not meant in a physical sense, but as a psychological index of how much spatial change was mentally replayed within a given imagined time. In everyday life, time can feel elastic: it may seem to drag during uneventful moments but to ‘fly’ when we are engaged or moved by enjoyable experiences such as music (Sackett et al., 2010). Spatial experience, by contrast, generally depends on how we mentally navigate environments and imagine movement within them (Bocchi et al. 2017; Decety and Jeannerod, 1995). From this perspective, and as shown by the results here, music seems to stretch the subjective flow of imagined time and distance, while traffic noise compresses it, creating shorter yet more compact mental journeys. This pattern reveals the broader phenomenon of temporal compression, where it refers to remembered events unfolding more quickly than their real or represented duration (Leroy et al., 2024), a process that appears to extend to mental imagery.

Practical Implications

Meaningful change in imagery characteristics mediated by music and traffic noise or their interaction has practical implications for real-world applications such as therapy, where background noise is present. For instance, if the goal is to enhance vividness in an imagery context, adding music remains effective in the presence of background noise. However, in therapeutic settings, the impact of noise should be carefully considered, as its presence may influence the overall experience depending on therapy’s goals. Given recent WHO (2022) reports highlighting the ubiquity of traffic noise in urban centres—where over half of the EU population is exposed to road noise levels exceeding WHO recommendations—these findings have nuanced implications for therapeutic practices in noisy environments. Although music and traffic noise can enhance imagined vividness, this might not be desirable for all therapies, as some may aim to reduce vividness rather than increase it (e.g., in Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing), whereas other therapies may aim to increase imagined vividness (e.g., Imagery Exposure). The ecological validity of this study, particularly the choice and level of traffic noise, reflects real-world auditory environments where imagery-based interventions might occur. This suggests that background noise does not necessarily undermine their effectiveness. However, therapists should consider whether the interplay of music and noise supports or hinders desired outcomes.

The other side of the coin is that clients are likely to incorporate any auditory stimulation into their imagery. Practitioners should be aware of how traffic noise may shape aspects of mental imagery, particularly emotional sentiment and content. Although noise does not eliminate music’s enhancing effects on positive sentiment, it does significantly reduce them. Thus, traffic sounds may reduce the efficacy of music-aided imagery therapy by suppressing emotional shifts that are key to treatment success (Brown et al., 2023). Further, traffic noise alone may shape emotional sentiment in a slightly negative way—albeit no more so than completely silent therapeutic conditions. Depending on how participants engage with the background stimulus (tuning out or in), practitioners should consider how background noise might shape the content of patients’ mental imagery. Even background sounds do not reduce the vividness of mental imagery; clients are likely to incorporate environmental sounds into imaginings, which could have unforeseen or unwanted effects on the therapy.

Limitations and future directions

Although mental imagery is a complex cognitive phenomenon with multiple dimensions, this study represents a necessarily reductive approach in using a few simple measurements. Because we cannot necessarily generalise beyond the samples of traffic noise and music used, future work should consider a more detailed analysis of imagined content to explore how acoustic properties influence imagined time and distance. Another potential limitation concerns the congruency between auditory context and imagined content. It is possible that, despite having lower emotional sentiment compared to music and silence, vividness in the traffic condition benefited from contextual congruence with the imagery task (e.g., movement or travel). Future studies could systematically manipulate congruency between auditory environments and imagined scenarios to test how contextual fit moderates the effects of music and noise on different characteristics of mental imagery.

More broadly, there is evidence that the disruptive effects of noise depend on noise type, task type, and broader context (Smith, 1989; Szalma and Hancock, 2011), so the cautious interpretation is that this study presents an example of how background traffic noise samples can affect directed mental imagery. Future research should explore how varying the SNR influences various facets of mental imagery, such as vividness. Investigating these dynamics across diverse noise conditions and auditory stimuli could help clarify the mechanisms underlying these effects, enabling more targeted applications for therapy, education, and workspace design.

Although we used directed imagery in the present study, the paradigm retains a significant degree of participant spontaneity. Participants were free to imagine anything from the point of the figure arriving on top of the hill in Fig. 1, and imagery descriptions accordingly varied widely both within and between participants. This lack of constraint likely allowed participants to incorporate the traffic noise stimuli into their mental imagery, potentially making it less disruptive. It remains unclear how the present results would differ in a paradigm prompting participants to imagine a specific scenario or recall a particular memory. Replicating the present experiment with imagery tasks designed to more closely resemble those featured in imagery-based interventions may produce evidence with greater clinical applicability. Future research could also explicitly investigate the multimodal nature of directed imagery elicited by combined visual and auditory cues to determine whether effects differ across various modalities, including kinaesthetic, taste, and others.

A significant majority of participants completed the experiment online on their own device, where there was no control over audio quality or external environmental noise. We cannot even be certain that participants adhered to the instructions of using headphones. The participants attending the lab experienced a more controlled environment, so this factor would be less influential for them, but there were not enough of these participants to make a systematic comparison across formats. Although the within-subjects manipulation mitigates this limitation to some extent, future studies could provide valuable contributions by exerting greater control over extraneous environmental noise.

Furthermore, as all participants were enrolled and based at a major metropolitan university, and were therefore likely accustomed to an urban environment, future studies could examine whether familiarity with specific soundscapes, such as traffic noise versus rural ambient noise, and differences in daily auditory exposure (e.g., drivers versus non-drivers; rural versus urban community participants) could influence the effects of auditory context on mental imagery. Finally, because the sample primarily comprised younger adults, future research should examine whether these effects generalise to older populations, as ageing may influence sensory processing (Humes et al., 2013) and component imagery processes such as generation and maintenance (Kemps and Newson, 2005), with recent qualitative work suggesting that music-evoked mental imagery may hold a meaningful role in older adulthood (Ayyildiz et al., 2025b).

Conclusion

We show how the presence of background noise interacts with music’s known effects on mental imagery (Ayyildiz et al., 2025a; Day et al., 2020; Herff et al., 2021; Herff et al., 2022; Koelsch et al., 2019; Küssner and Eerola, 2019; Vuoskoski and Eerola, 2015). These results suggest that auditory environments, including those with moderate background noise, can meaningfully enhance or alter the mental imagery characteristics of vividness, emotional sentiment, imagined distance, imagined time, imagined speed, and content. Such insights underscore the importance of understanding how music and traffic noise contribute to cognitive and emotional experiences in everyday and therapeutic contexts.
