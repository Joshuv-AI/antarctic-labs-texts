A rare culprit: Acute pancreatitis associated with anisakiasis
Authors: Fumiue Harada, Morihito Takita, Hiroaki Saito, Masahiro Kami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute pancreatitis is an uncommon but clinically significant manifestation of food allergy. We describe the case of a 72-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension who developed acute abdominal pain three hours after consuming raw flounder. On admission, laboratory testing demonstrated leukocytosis, markedly elevated serum amylase (807 U/L) and urinary amylase (12,428 U/L). Imaging studies revealed diffuse pancreatic edema and surrounding fat stranding consistent with acute pancreatitis. Importantly, the patient had no history of gallstones, alcohol consumption, or structural biliary abnormalities. A remarkable elevation in Anisakis-specific IgE (76 Ua/mL; reference <0.34) and total IgE (572 IU/mL; reference <170) strongly implicated an allergic mechanism. He was treated conservatively with fasting and intravenous fluids, and his symptoms gradually improved. Following strict avoidance of raw fish, his IgE levels showed a marked decline and there was no recurrence of pancreatitis for over a year. Interestingly, his long-standing chronic urticaria also improved markedly with dietary modification. This case highlights the importance of considering Anisakis-induced allergy in the differential diagnosis of idiopathic acute pancreatitis, particularly in countries with high raw fish consumption. Serological testing for Anisakis-specific IgE can provide a valuable diagnostic clue, and dietary counseling may prevent recurrence. Greater awareness of this rare but important etiology is warranted among clinicians worldwide. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis has diverse etiologies, including gallstones, alcohol, genetic predisposition, infections, and food allergy. caused by a variety of adverse stimuli, including gallstones, alcohol, and trypsinogen gene mutations. It is also known to be induced and caused by food allergies [1]. In this study, we experienced acute pancreatitis, which was probably due to an allergy caused by Anisakis infection.

Anisakis, a parasitic nematode found in raw fish, can cause a variety of allergic reactions in humans. Common symptoms include urticaria, abdominal pain, and anaphylaxis [2]. In recent years, infections caused by the parasite anisakiasis have been increasing worldwide. More than 90 % of Anisakis infections reported in Japan are gastric anisakiasis, but there are also reports of ectopic anisakiasis and anisakiasis allergy [3].

Diagnosis of Anisakis infection is made by detailed interview, ultrasonography and serological tests to confirm elevated IgE antibody titres, but definitive diagnosis requires detection of the worms by upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. However, in some cases emergency endoscopy is difficult to perform and anisakiasis infection is likely to be underdiagnosed [4]. IgE antibody quantitative immunoassay has proven to be a powerful tool in allergic disease management. In this study, we experienced a case of pancreatitis caused by Anisakis infection in which IgE was useful for diagnosis.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 72-year-old male with a medical history of diabetes and hypertension who developed severe abdominal pain three hours after consuming raw flounder given to him by an acquaintance. Upon emergency transport, his vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.8°C, heart rate 76 beats per minute, and SpO2 95 %. Upon examination, tenderness was noted in the epigastric area without rebound tenderness or guarding. Laboratory findings revealed an elevated white blood cell count of 14.5 × 10⁹/L, suggesting a systemic inflammatory response. Serum amylase was markedly elevated at 807 U/L (reference range: 44–132 U/L). Urinary amylase was also significantly increased, measuring 12,428 U/L (reference range: 57–813 U/L), indicating pronounced pancreatic enzyme excretion. Imaging revealed diffuse pancreatic edema and surrounding fat stranding, suggestive of inflammation. Imaging and serological evaluations led to a diagnosis of acute pancreatitis. Notably, the patient had no prior episodes of acute abdominal pain. He was started on fasting intravenous fluids and conservative treatment for pancreatitis, resulting in symptomatic improvement over several days. The patient had no history of gallstones or alcohol consumption. Magnetic resonance imaging did not reveal any abnormalities such as biliary junction anomalies. A significant finding was a marked increase in specific IgE antibodies to Anisakis (76 Ua/mL, with a reference maximum of 0.34) and an elevation in non-specific IgE (572, with a reference maximum of 170). The patient's condition improved steadily, and he continued to attend regular follow-up visits after discharge. Upon advising him to avoid raw fish consumption, his IgE antibody levels showed a significant decline over time, and he experienced no recurrences of pancreatitis for over a year. Additionally, there was an improvement in chronic urticaria that he had suffered from for many years. Upper endoscopy could not be performed because the vomiting reflex was very strong.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare but clinically important association between Anisakis-induced allergy and acute pancreatitis. The patient presented with acute pancreatitis in the absence of common etiological factors such as alcohol consumption, cholelithiasis, or structural abnormalities. Notably, the onset of symptoms occurred shortly after ingestion of raw flounder, and a markedly elevated levels of Anisakis-specific IgE level strongly implicated an allergic mechanism as the underlying etiology. Infectious etiologies, such as mumps, viral hepatitis, and scrub typhus [5], as well as autoimmune pancreatitis, were considered but excluded based on clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and absence of relevant risk factors in this patient.

Although the patient had a long-standing history of chronic urticaria, which may have contributed to a persistently elevated baseline IgE levels, it is unlikely that this alone accounted for the markedly elevated IgE level of 572 IU/mL observed at the onset of pancreatitis. Several studies have investigated serum levels in patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), indicating that while elevated IgE is observed in approximately 30–50 % of cases, levels exceeding 500 IU/mL are uncommon. For instance, Maurer et al. reported that most CSU patients had IgE levels within the normal range, and that elevated IgE did not consistently correlate with disease activity [6]. Similarly, Kessel et al. (2005) found higher IgE levels in patients with more severe or treatment-resistant CSU; however, the majority of these cases exhibited only modest increases, typically around 300 IU/mL [7]. Given that the IgE level in this case exceeded 570 IU/mL—nearly triple the commonly reported elevated levels in CSU—it is highly suggestive of an acute immunologic trigger, rather than merely reflecting the patient’s baseline allergic state.

Furthermore, the time course of the anti-Anisakis IgE antibody showed a steep decline by day 37, followed by a plateau phase, mirroring the clinical trajectory of the pancreatitis. This pattern reinforces the hypothesis that Anisakis exposure was responsible for the abrupt immunologic response and the onset of pancreatitis.

Traditionally, pancreatitis associated with anisakiasis has been attributed to mechanical obstruction caused by larvae invading the gastrointestinal tract [8]. However, in this case, the absence of endoscopic abnormalities and the resolution of both symptoms and IgE levels following dietary elimination of raw fish suggest a non-mechanical, immunologically mediated mechanism. Notably, the patient's chronic urticaria, previously refractory to treatment, also resolved with the elimination of raw fish, further reinforcing the diagnosis of Anisakis-induced allergy.

The severe pain and functional impairment associated with acute pancreatitis acted as a powerful catalyst for sustained behavioral modification. The patient adopted a diet restricted to thoroughly cooked fish, resulting in complete symptom resolution and a sustained reduction in Anisakis-specific IgE titers. This outcome is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that dietary modifications can alleviate chronic urticaria symptoms in individuals with food allergies [9], [10], [11].

Given the increasing global popularity of raw fish consumption—particularly sushi and sashimi—clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for Anisakis allergy as an underrecognized etiology of acute abdominal symptoms, even in regions where raw fish consumption is less frequent or traditionally less reported. While allergic reactions to Anisakis are often immediate, they may also manifest in delayed or atypical forms, including anaphylaxis and, as illustrated in this case, acute pancreatitis. Notably, allergic responses can occur even with cooked seafood, due to the presence of thermostable Anisakis allergens [12].

Preventive strategies—including avoidance of wild-caught raw fish and preference for properly frozen or farmed seafood—may reduce the risk in sensitized individuals. Serological testing for Anisakis-specific IgE should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained acute abdomen, especially in coastal or high-consumption populations. As global food distribution and refrigeration technologies advance, raw seafood will become increasingly accessible. Greater awareness of Anisakis-related allergic conditions is essential, and further case reports are needed to elucidate their full clinical spectrum and inform management strategies.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that Anisakis-induced allergy can serve as an underlying etiology of acute pancreatitis. The markedly elevated levels of Anisakis-specific IgE, along with clinical improvement following dietary modification, strongly support an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity mechanism. In regions where raw fish consumption is prevalent, Anisakis allergy should be considered in the differential diagnosis of idiopathic pancreatitis.
