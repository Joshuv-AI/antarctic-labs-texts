Craniofacial and dental arch morphological changes in pediatric obstructive sleep apnea syndrome following rapid maxillary expansion
Authors: Yu-Hao Liu, Yu-Shu Huang, Yi-Jing Hwang, Cheng-Hui Lin, Shan-Shan Lee, Peng Hsu, Chung-Hsing Li, Li-Chuan Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeRapid maxillary expansion (RME) is a non-invasive treatment option for children with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS). Yet, there’s limited research on the effects of RME on the craniofacial, dental arch morphology and polysomnographic measures in pediatric OSAS.Materials and methodsMedical records of patients aged 4–14 years with apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) > 1 event/hour and available cephalometric radiographs were reviewed. The Hyrax appliance was used for RME. Lateral and posterior-anterior cephalometric radiography, dental cast analysis and polysomnography (PSG) were performed before and after a six-month treatment. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.ResultsDue to data availability, 40 lateral cephalometric records, 33 posterior-anterior cephalometric images, 24 dental casts, and 40 polysomnographic records were analyzed. Significant increases were found in nasopharyngeal distances (PNS-AD1, PNS-AD2, and PNS-NPhp) and the minimal velopharyngeal distance (MinRPA). The length of the jugal process and the intermaxillary suture increased significantly. The intermaxillary suture opening in the frontal plane displayed an inverted V-shaped pattern. An inverse association was observed between the maxillary arch width and the arch length. RME also induced spontaneous dentoalveolar adaptations in the mandibular arch. The reductions in PSG measures were not statistically significant. A marked nominal decrease in AHI was seen in the moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) group compared with the mild group.ConclusionRME treatment modifies craniofacial structure and dental arch morphology in children with OSAS. However, these anatomical improvements do not translate into statistically significant reductions in AHI. A more pronounced nominal decrease in AHI occurs in patients with moderate-to-severe OSA, compared with those with mild OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is a respiratory disorder characterized by upper airway narrowing that compromises ventilation during sleep.1 In pediatric patients, untreated OSAS may lead to severe complications, including growth impairment,2 neurocognitive deficits,3 and cardiovascular sequelae.4,5 Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA),6 where the severity of OSA is quantified by the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI).7

The etiology of OSAS includes anatomic airway narrowing and abnormal upper airway neuromotor tone (which varies by age), with adenotonsillar hypertrophy being the most common cause in children8 for which adenotonsillectomy remains the first-line treatment. However, according to Friedman et al.,9 adenotonsillectomy resolved OSA in only 59.8 % of children with mouth breathing, while residual OSA persisted in the remaining 40.2 % of children postoperatively.

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) is a non-invasive treatment option for pediatric OSA. It increases upper airway volume, reduces nasal resistance, and modifies tongue posture.10 Piełunowicz et al.11 found that orthodontic treatments (including RME) may improve sleep quality, mitigate symptoms of sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), and enhance sleep parameters. However, a clear correlation between orthodontic interventions and AHI change has not yet been fully established. Besides, while studies on RME for pediatric OSA have advanced the understanding of upper airway volume, cross-section area, and bony support, there remains a need for comprehensive investigations into the full range of dentofacial characteristics.12–14

Therefore, our study aimed to investigate the changes in craniofacial and dental arch morphology and PSG readings in children with OSA post RME treatment. We hypothesized that (1) RME induces changes in craniofacial structure and dental arch morphology in children with OSAS, and (2) RME treatment reduces AHI in pediatric OSAS, irrespective of disease severity.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Human Investigation Committee at the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (201601892A3, August 8 2017; 201702098A3, February 7 2018). The privacy rights of patients were observed, and written informed consent was obtained prior to the start of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 4 and 14 years; (2) diagnosis of OSA, defined as AHI greater than 1 event per hour according to the guidelines of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM); (3) transverse deficiency of the maxillary dental arch; and (4) absence of severe adenotonsillar hypertrophy.

The exclusion criteria were epilepsy, head trauma, severe developmental delays or intellectual disabilities, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), schizophrenia, or severe depression. Additionally, patients with severe adenotonsillar hypertrophy were excluded―for which adenotonsillectomy remains the first-line treatment, so the current study could focus on patients with recurring or residual OSAS postoperatively. Cases where post-RME PSG data were missing and where patients were unable to cooperate during the PSG measurement or for oral device fabrication were also excluded.

Polysomnography (PSG)

Overnight PSG was conducted at the Sleep Center of the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital at baseline and at a minimum of six months following the RME treatment.

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) device

The Hyrax expander, a fixed screw-activated maxillary expansion device, was used as the treatment. The selection of anchorage teeth was based on the stage of dentition to optimize the force transfer to the maxillary sutures for effective skeletal expansion. For patients with primary dentition, bilateral second primary molars were used as anchorage. For those with mixed dentition, the appliance was secured to bilateral second primary molars and first permanent molars. For patients with permanent dentition, bilateral premolars and first permanent molars served as anchors.

A uniform activation protocol was applied across all age groups and dentition stages. Patients and their caregivers were instructed to activate the device twice daily for an expansion of approximately 0.5 mm per day over a three-week period. Compliance was monitored via caregiver reports and verified by clinical measurement of the screw expansion during follow-up visits. All participants included in the final analysis completed the full activation phase. The expander was left in for six months to allow for consolidation and retention. During the retention period, fixed orthodontic appliances were applied in certain cases for minor dental leveling and alignment, which would not confound RME-induced skeletal and airway shifts materially.

Cephalometric radiography

Digital lateral cephalometric radiographs were obtained at baseline and at least six months post rapid palatal expansion. All images were captured with the patient’s head in a natural position and their teeth in centric occlusion. A total of 24 cephalometric measurements were recorded, including 9 angular and 15 linear parameters. Descriptions of landmarks, variables, and their definitions are presented in Fig. 1. The images were analyzed by the lead author of this report using a digital image software package (GE Medical Systems Inc. Released 2006. Centricity Enterprise, Version 3.0., USA: GE Healthcare Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Digital posterior-anterior cephalometric radiographs were obtained at baseline and three weeks following the treatment. All images were captured with the patient’s head in a natural position and their teeth in centric occlusion. Five linear measurements were taken. Descriptions of landmarks, variables, and their definitions are presented in Fig. 2. The images were analyzed by the lead author of this report using the same digital image software package.

Dental cast

Upper and lower dental impressions were collected at baseline and at least six months after the treatment. The following parameters were analyzed: intercanine width, intermolar width, upper arch length, lower arch length, and palatal height. The definitions of these parameters are detailed in the note field of Fig. 3.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Means and standard deviations were computed for each variable in each group. Pre- and post-treatment comparisons for polysomnographic readings, lateral and posterior-anterior cephalometric measures, and dental casts were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05.

Results

After consecutive screening process, a total of 48 patients met the initial inclusion criteria. Eight patients were later excluded due to incomplete post-treatment PSG data. A total of 40 patients (girl: boy = 14 : 26; mean age in years: 9.07 ± 2.36) were included in the final analysis (Table 1). Thirty cases (75 %) were classified as mild OSA, nine as moderate, and one as severe. There were no statistically significant differences in age, gender, body weight, and body mass index (BMI) in baseline between the two groups. Due to data availability, 40 lateral cephalometric records, 33 posterior-anterior cephalometric images, 24 dental casts, and 40 polysomnographic records were analyzed. The pre- and post-treatment comparisons are presented as follows.

Lateral cephalometric measures

The posterior facial height (S-Go), anterior facial height (N–Me), anterior cranial base (S–N), and upper airway width (PNS-NPhp, MinRPA, PNS-AD1, and PNS-AD2) became significantly larger (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Posterior-anterior cephalometric measures

The distance between the jugal process of the maxilla and the intermaxillary suture became significantly longer (P < 0.05) (Table 3). RME facilitated bilateral maxillary expansion, yielding an average increase in maxillary cross-sectional width (JR to JL) of 3.75 mm. Additionally, the average intermaxillary suture opening in the frontal plane was more pronounced inferiorly (CR-CL = 5.05 mm) than superiorly (AR–AL = 3.47 mm), displaying an inverted Vshaped configuration.

Dental cast measurements

Post treatment, the upper and lower intercanine and intermolar widths were significantly wider (P < 0.05), and the upper arch length, significantly shorter (P < 0.05) (Table 4). The primary and most direct expansion occurred in the maxillary arch (treated area), with mean increases of 4.92 mm at the canines and 5.52 mm at the molars. Some expansion was also observed in the lower arch (untreated area), with mean increases of 1.12 mm at the canines and 1.61 mm at the molars, due to secondary uprighting or decompensation of the mandibular dentition. A decrease of 0.90 mm was seen in the maxillary arch length.

Polysomnography data analysis

No PSG measures reached statistical significance post RME, including AHI (aggregate), AHI during rapid eye movement (REM), hypopnea index (HI), and respiratory index (RDI) (Table 5). Within each subgroup, no uniform treatment effects were observed. In patients with mild OSA, AHI decreased in 13 patients (43 %), remained unchanged in two patients (7 %), and increased in 15 patients (50 %). In patients with moderate-to-severe OSA, AHI decreased in six patients (60 %) and increased in four patients (40 %). Despite the lack of uniform effects, a directional trend emerged where patients with moderate-to-severe OSA showed a more pronounced nominal AHI reduction than their peers in the mild OSA group (Table 6 and Fig. 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate lateral and posterior-anterior cephalometric radiography, dental cast analysis, and polysomnography in one study to assess the effects of RME in children with OSA.

The lateral cephalometric measures in our study showed significant increases in the nasopharyngeal distance (PNSAD1, PNS-AD2, and PNS-NPhp) and the minimal velopharyngeal distance (MinRPA) following RME treatment. This is consistent with the significant increase in nasopharyngeal width post RME treatment observed by Galeotti, A. et al.16 in 37 children (aged 4–10 years) with OSA. These findings suggest that RME plays a significant role in expanding the sagittal dimensions of the upper airway. In our study, significant increases were also found in the posterior facial height (S-Go), anterior facial height (N–Me), and anterior cranial base (S–N). These changes are most likely attributable to growth-related factors.

Our posterior-anterior cephalometric analysis revealed a significant increase in both the length of the jugal process and the intermaxillary suture post RME. The maxillary cross-sectional width (JR to JL) expanded by an average of 3.75 mm, which echoes the average increase of 5.91 mm in the maxillary cross-sectional width (JR to JL) reported by P. Pirelli et al. in their study of 60 children between 6 and 13 years of age.17 In addition, we observed an inverted Vshaped opening of the intermaxillary suture in the frontal plane―with greater inferior than superior vertical expansion― in all patients. In comparison, Kinzinger et al.18 found a V-shaped pattern in children aged ≥12 years and a more parallel expansion in children ≥10 years, which suggests an age-dependent effect of RME on palatal expansion. A number of factors may have contributed to these divergent results. First, posteroanterior cephalometry―a two-dimensional (2D) projection―used in our study may obscure the superior-inferior geometry of suture opening, compared with the volumetric precision of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) used by Kinzinger et al. Second, differences in mid-palatal suture interdigitation complexity and bioelasticity between the Asian population in our study and the Caucasian population in the study of Kinzinger et al. may result in distinct biomechanical responses to expansion forces, which could be further modulated by the baseline transverse deficiency in both studies. Third, the wider age range of 4–14 years in our study versus 7.3–13.8 years in the study of Kinzinger et al. may have affected the suture maturation status. Lastly, unlike the general orthodontic cohort in the study of Kinzinger et al., the participants in our study were all diagnosed with OSA which might exhibit unique resistance patterns during expansion.

The inverse association between maxillary arch width and arch length found in our study― yielding a more rounded arch configuration―mirrors the digital measurements of three-dimensional (3D) models reported by Bogdanov et al. in a study of 33 RME-treated patients.19 The RME-elicited spontaneous dentoalveolar adaptations in the mandibular dental arch in our study is also consistent with the digital analysis of 3D models among 21 RME-treated patients by Cossellu et al.20 who attributed the mandibular arch adaptations to shifts in the balance between tongue and buccinator muscle forces.

The lack of uniform treatment effects in both subgroups in our study echoes the findings by Quo et al. (2017) who studied 45 children aged 3–14 years. They reported that AHI decreased in three patients (25 %) and increased in nine patients (75 %) with mild OSA and that AHI decreased in 27 patients (82 %) and increased in six patients (18 %) with moderate-to-severe OSA.21 The findings of both studies, thus, do not support the second prong of our hypothesis of post-RME AHI reduction in pediatric OSAS, independent of severity. Furthermore, while post-RME morphological corrections are significant in our study (confirming the first prong of our hypothesis), they did not translate to significant AHI decreases (at both aggregate and subgroup levels). This is evidenced in the absence of statistical significance of various PSG measures and the lack of uniform treatment effects within groups. A future study recruiting a larger sample will be needed to more definitively illustrate how craniofacial and dental morphological characteristics and AHI changes may correlate with treatment outcome to inform individualized RME treatment planning.

The wide range of age in the inclusion criteria encompasses the primary and mixed dentition stages―the optimal therapeutic window for maxillary expansion prior to mid-palatal suture maturation. The self-controlled study design eliminated potential confounding effects from this wide age range, with each patient serving as their own control. Any RME-induced morphological changes can still be distinguished from the natural transverse growth, given the short treatment duration of six months and an RME of 0.5 mm/day which significantly outpaced the physiological transverse growth.

The current study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and also heavily skewed towards mild OSA (mild: moderate-to-severe OSA = 30 : 10), which may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance of post-RME PSG changes. Second, the self-controlled design of the study may have introduced confounding factors which interfered with the interpretation of the treatment outcome. A fully controlled study will be needed to further delineate the treatment outcome of RME in pediatric OSAS in relation to the parameters investigated in our study. Third, the expander remained in place during the follow-up sleep study and may have artificially elevated the postoperative AHI. Fourth, two-dimensional lateral cephalometric analysis provides a static, awake, upright image that cannot capture airway dynamics during sleep. Furthermore, since airway dimensions vary throughout the respiratory cycle, dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may offer a superior visualization of functional airway collapse in OSA, particularly the lateral pharyngeal wall collapse seen in more severe OSA cases.22

RME treatment modifies craniofacial structure and dental arch morphology in children with OSAS. However, these anatomical improvements do not translate into statistically significant reductions in AHI. A more pronounced nominal decrease in AHI occurs in patients with moderate-to-severe OSA, compared with those with mild OSA. This suggests a potential trend of OSA severity-dependent RME effects on sleep parameters. Future investigations may incorporate assessment methods or diagnostic tools in addition to PSG to more comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic outcome of expansion intervention. Studies can also be conducted over a longer period of time to assess the long-term effects.
