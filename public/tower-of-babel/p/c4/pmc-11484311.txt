Clinical application and efficacy analysis of robot-assisted laparoscopic tumor enucleation combined with tumor aspiration in the treatment of renal hilar angiomyolipoma
Authors: Zhenyu Xu, Haixiang Qin, Mei Yu, Qing Zhang, Xiaogong Li, Hongqian Guo, Guangxiang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor renal hilar angiomyolipoma, general surgical resection is often quite challenging. The aim of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of an innovative robot-assisted laparoscopic strategy that combines tumor enucleation with tumor aspiration in the treatment of renal hilar angiomyolipoma.MethodsThe clinical data of 38 patients with renal hilar angiomyolipoma who went through robotic tumor enucleation combined with tumor aspiration in the Department of Urology, the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School, from December 2019 to December 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The basic characteristics, the perioperative variables, intraoperative procedures, and postoperative complications were all recorded. Patients were followed up to evaluate the angiomyolipoma recurrence and the renal function by urologic CT or ultrasound and renal function tests.ResultsAll surgeries were successfully completed without conversion to radical nephrectomy or open surgery. One patient developed urinary extravasation 10 days after surgery, and was readmitted to the hospital for transurethral ureteral stenting considering the injury of the renal collecting system. The median operative time was 181.5 (123.8-206.3) min, the warm ischemia time was 20.0 (17.3–24.0) min, the blood loss was 125 (100.0-262.5) ml, and no patients received blood transfusion during and after surgery. The median hospitalization time was 7.0 (6.0–9.0) days, and the duration of indwelling drainage tube was 2.0 (2.0–3.0) days. The serum creatinine (Scr) on the first day, 3 months, and 6 months after surgery were 55.5 (50.8–62.8) µmol/L, 55.5 (48.0–62.0) µmol/L and 54.0 (51.8–63.5) µmol/L, respectively, and there was no significant difference compared with the preoperative level 56.0 (47.8–60.3) µmol/L. All patients were followed up after surgery, and the urinary CT scan or renal color doppler ultrasound, and renal function were reexamined. The median follow-up time of all patients was 19.0 (14.75-33.0) months, and no local recurrence of angiomyolipoma was seen in all patients.ConclusionsThe surgical strategy of robot-assisted laparoscopic tumor enucleation combined with tumor aspiration is safe and effective in the treatment of renal hilar angiomyolipoma.Keywords: Renal angiomyolipoma, Renal hilum, Robot-assisted laparoscopy, Enucleation, Aspiration

Background

Renal angiomyolipoma (RAML) is one of the most common benign solid renal tumors, accounting for about 0.3-3.0% of renal tumors [1, 2]. RAML exceeding 4 cm in diameter is typically regarded as a crucial indication for clinical intervention, such as arterial embolization and surgical management [3, 4], both of which are the first-line management options. Arterial embolization is relatively safer, however, it has a higher recurrence rate and is more suitable for emergency situations, such as tumor rupture and bleeding [5, 6].

For RAML located in non-adjacent to renal hilum, the current relatively mature surgical methods mainly include partial nephrectomy and RAML enucleation, and a few units have attempted to carry out RAML aspiration [7–9]. For these cases, surgical difficulty is acceptable. However, for the RAML located in the renal hilum, especially when tumor extends inward and is embedded in the renal sinus, it is often difficult to expose the surgical field, and the tumor is closely related to the peripheral blood vessels and renal pelvis collecting system, and the tumor shape is often irregular, so it is easy to bleed and impairment the collecting system during resection [10, 11]. Besides, there are fewer normal tissues around it that can be sutured to stop bleeding after tumor resection, so the surgical difficulty of both the traditional partial nephrectomy or tumor enucleation of the hilar RAML is relatively hard [12]. For the treatment of hilar RAML, our unit has innovatively carried out robot-assisted laparoscopic tumor enucleation combined with tumor aspiration via transperitoneal approach for the first time, and is now reviewed and summarized to explore the safety and efficacy of this operation procedure.

Methods

Research subjects

The clinical data of a total of 38 patients with RAML adjacent to the renal hilum were retrospectively collected from December 2019 to December 2022 in our center. Patients whose preoperative diagnosis were indeterminate or ambiguous, and who were suspected to be epithelioid angiomyolipoma by preoperative imaging (contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and three-dimensional reconstruction imaging) are excluded absolutely in this study. All patients were evaluated by R.E.N.A.L score [13, 14] according to the CT images, then all patients underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic RAML enucleation combined with tumor aspiration. There were 6 males and 32 females, with the average of age (49.1 ± 12.7) years, body mass index (24.2 ± 2.9) kg/ m², with the preoperative median of Scr 56.0 (47.8–60.3) µmol/L, hemoglobin (Hb) 133.5 (117.5–138.0) g/L. Preoperative CT image showed that the median maximum diameter of the tumor was 7.1 (6.6-9.0) cm, and the tumor score was: (8 points: 14cases; 9 points: 18 cases; 10 points: 5 cases; 11 points: 1 case), with 25 cases on the left side and 13 cases on the right side (Table 1 for details). All patients underwent urinary CT or renal color Doppler ultrasound, and renal function tests at 3 and 6 months after surgery, and then were followed-up annually. Surgical method

All patients underwent surgical procedures using the da Vinci Xi system, and the transperitoneal approach were adopted [15]. Figure 2 shows the key steps of the surgical technique. The pneumoperitoneum pressure was originally set at 12mmHg, with the flow rate set at 20 L/min.

Key steps of the surgical technique in a patient with a hilar RAML (left renal posterior lip) A Separation and suspension of the renal artery. B Exposure of the renal tumor. C Tumor enucleation at the crown of the tumor. D Rapid and sharp dissection at the base of the tumor. E Destroying and aspirating the basal residual tumors rapidly by an aspirator. F Hemostasis of the tumor base by bipolar electrocoagulation. G Continuous overlock suture using barbed threads and Hem-o-loks. H Hemostatic agents were applied on the wound. I Reconstruction of perirenal fat sacs

Exposure of the kidney and tumor

By dissecting cautiously, the anterior renal fascia was incised near the renal hilum to expose the renal vein and artery. Then a vascular sling was brought across the back of the renal artery to suspend it for later use (Fig. 2A). The perirenal fat sac was incised in the middle of the kidney, and the medial, ventral, lateral, dorsal, and upper and lower poles of the kidney were fully exposed, and the RAML could be clearly seen adjacent to the renal hilum. (Fig. 2B). Further dissection was performed to expose the boundary between the tumor (golden or reddish due to the main components of fat and micro-vessels) and the surrounding normal renal parenchyma (ruddy). The ureter was exposed in front of the psoas muscle at the medially lower pole of the kidney, and was gradually freed upward to the renal pelvis to avoid accidental injury during the surgery. If the tumor is located at the posterior lip of the renal hilum, the kidney needs to be mobilized more thoroughly so that the kidney can be turned medially to better expose the tumor.

Tumor enucleation and aspiration

The surgeon pulled the vascular sling to expose the renal artery, and the assistant inserted a bulldog clamp through the 12 mm auxiliary port to block the renal artery. Cut open the renal cortex 2–3 mm outside the boundary of RAML with the robotic monopolar scissors, then expand the gap along the incision using bipolar forceps bluntly, to grope for and confirm the pseudocapsule of the tumor, then separate the tumor from the renal cortex bluntly along the pseudocapsule (Fig. 2C). When cord structures were encountered, electrocoagulation was required before separation. The separation was gradually carried out until to the renal medulla or to the tumor base. There was generally no obvious capsular-like space here, and the tumor base was rich with vasa vasorum and closely adjacent to thicker artery branches and pelvic collecting systems. Therefore, the tumor could be quickly and sharply dissociated at the level of the cortico-medullary junction or at the tumor base (Fig. 2D), and the residual basal part of the tumor could be curetted and aspirated by the assistant using a suction device (Fig. 2E) until normal renal tissue showed (Fig. 2F). Be sure to keep the curettage and suction quick but gentle, to avoid damaging the collection system during tumor aspiration. We hold the opinion that maintaining an appropriate suction pressure (40 ∼ 53 kPa, 300-400mmHg) is very crucial, which is neither too low to achieve complete tumor aspiration, nor too high to risk causing additional injuries. During tumor aspiration, the intra-abdominal pressure will drop dramatically. At this time, we usually raise the pressure of the pneumoperitoneum to 14–16 mmHg, and the flow rate to 30 L/min. By the way, an intelligent insufflation system such as AirSeal system which employs trocars without valves or membranes, responding immediately to slight variations in intra-abdominal pressure, will help control and stabilize the pneumoperitoneum pressure with smoke evacuation and continuous suctioning in dynamic situation, especially during the tumor aspiration phase [16, 17].

Hemostasis and suture

Hemostasis was achieved using a combination of cautery, hemostatic agents, and suturing [15] (Fig. 2G-H). Firstly, the bipolar forceps was used for electrocoagulation on the wound to decrease bleeding, which was usually effective for haemorrhage from small veins and capillaries. The monopolar scissors on the right hand were exchanged for the needle drivers, then an appropriate suturing strategy shall be employed according to the actual situation. If there were enough normal renal tissues available for suturing around, we used pre-prepared 2 − 0 Vicryl sutures (the end of the 2 − 0 Vicryl was clipped with a 10 mm Hem-o-lok and then knotted and fixed) to suture the wound intermittently, then clipped another two or three 10 mm Hem-o-lok continuously at the suture of needle end, and then gradually tighten the suture to close the gap of renal parenchyma. However, if the wound located at the anterior or posterior lip of the renal hilum, it generally cannot be sutured appositionally, then the barbed threads supplemented with Hem-o-lok can be considered for continuous lock-stitch suture. Loosen the blocking bulldogs, and reduce the pneumoperitoneum pressure to 8 mmHg, if there was no obvious bleeding on the wound surface, then suture the perinephric fat sac with 3 − 0 barbed suture (Fig. 2I).

Evaluation indicators

Record the operation time, warm ischemia time (WIT), estimated intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative and postoperative blood transfusion volume, Hb and renal function indicators on the first day after surgery, indwelling time of drainage tube, gastrointestinal ventilation time, discharge time. All patients returned to the hospital 3 months and half a year after the surgery for urinary CT or renal color Doppler ultrasound, as well as renal function tests, to assess the tumor recurrence and renal function.

Statistical methods

SPSS Version 23.0 software was applied for the statistical analysis. Enumeration data were expressed in number of cases and percentage [n (%)]. The continuous data with the homogeneous distribution and homoscedasticity were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (±s), and paired t-test was used for comparison between the two groups. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as median (interquartile range) [M (IQR)], and Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparison between groups. p < 0.05 indicates that the difference is statistically significant.

Results

The surgeries were successfully completed in all patients, and no case was converted to nephrectomy or open surgery. The median operation time was 181.5 (123.8-206.3) min, the WIT was 20.0 (17.3–24.0) min, the blood loss was 125 (100.0-262.5) ml, and no patients received blood transfusion during and after surgery. The median hospitalization time was 7.0 (6.0–9.0) days, and the median indwelling time of abdominal drainage was 2.0 (2.0–3.0) days, as shown in Table 2. On the first day after surgery, Hb (130.7 (117.5-137.25) g/L), Scr (55.5 (50.8–62.8) µmol/L), and eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) (118.9 (104.9-132.4) ml/min/1.73m2) were re-examined, and there were no significant differences compared with those before surgery, as detailed in Table 3. One patient developed urinary extravasation 10 days after surgery, complaining of abdominal pain with low-grade fever. Physical examination showed mild tenderness in the affected abdomen, then re-examination of the CTU showed leakage of contrast agent. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with the injury of renal pelvis collecting system (Clavien-Dindo grade IIIb). He was readmitted to the hospital for transurethral double J ureteral stent implantation, which was successfully completed. After one week, the urinary CT scan showed that the extravasated urine was significantly reduced, and the ureteral stent was removed two months later. All patients came to the hospital for short-term follow-up at 3 months and 6 months after surgery, then for annual review. The current follow-up date is September 28, 2023, and the median follow-up time is 19.0 (14.75-33.0) months, and no local recurrence of hilar RAML was seen in all patients. The Scr at 3 months and 6 months after surgery were 55.5 (48.0–62.0) µmol/L and 54.0 (51.8–63.5) µmol/L, respectively, and there was no significant difference compared with the preoperative level (56.0 (47.8–60.3) µmol/L).

Discussion

Traditionally, sporadic RAML with the diameter ≥ 4 cm, internal bleeding and rupture, serious infection, or acute progressive renal impairment, requires clinical treatment [18]. Treatment options for RAML mainly include surgical resection (nephron-sparing surgery and radical nephrectomy), selective artery embolization, thermal ablation, and even kidney transplantation. Selective artery embolization appeared to reduce the volume of RAML with minimal invasiveness, but required secondary treatment in 30% of the cases [19]. Nephron-sparing surgery was considered as the most effective treatment for preventing RAML recurrence and the need for secondary procedures [8, 20, 21].

During the previous surgical enucleation of RAML, we found that the tumors compress the renal parenchyma at the interface to form a “pseudocapsule”, and this layer of loose mesh formed by the compressed renal parenchyma and fibrous tissue is very obvious in the renal cortical area, so this part of the tumor is more suitable for detachment by enucleation. However, this “pseudocapsule” may be absent in the renal medulla level, especially at the base of the RAML, so the boundary between the tumor and the normal renal tissue is not clear, thus it is difficult to accurately find the anatomical plane or the resection boundary, whether in partial resection or tumor enucleation. On the other hand, the hilar RAML, especially those with a deep embedded base in the renal sinus, are very difficult to expose the anatomical plane and the surgical field. The space to operate is extremely narrow, so the laparoscopic instrument is limited to give full play to superiority. In addition, the tumor base is very close to the sinus blood vessels and the collecting system, so the traditional partial nephrectomy or tumor enucleation is very easy to impairment the blood vessels and cause serious bleeding, or injure the collecting system to cause postoperative urinary extravasation. In extreme cases, nephron-sparing surgery was even compelled to transformed to radical nephrectomy because of serious hemorrhage or peripheral viscera injury, which brings irreversible impairment to the patient’s renal function and quality of life [8]. In view of the fact that the vast majority of RAML are benign, we suggest that the basal part of the tumor does not necessarily need to maintain the integrity during removal, but can be treated by curetting and aspirating with a suction tube, so as to reduce intraoperative bleeding, shorten the WIT, and reduce the risk of transforming to radical nephrectomy [7].

Therefore, for hilar RAML, our center has innovatively carried out a robot-assisted laparoscopic surgical strategy that combines tumor enucleation with tumor aspiration. Dong K et al. reported a partially similar technique (curettage, suction, packing, and binding technique), for off-clamp retroperitoneoscopic evacuation of sporadic renal angiomyolipoma [9]. Compared with their retroperitoneal approach and conventional laparoscopy, our study was the first one that employed tumor enucleation combined with aspiration technique in the hilar RAML via transperitoneal approach and by robot-assisted laparoscopy. The data in our study showed that the tumor enucleation combined with tumor aspiration was safe and feasible for the treatment of hilar RAML, and the key perioperative parameters such as WIT, blood loss, Scr, eGFR, and Hb on the first postoperative day, and the rate of postoperative complications are acceptable. The median operation time in this group was 181.5 (123.8-206.3) min, which included the time of establishing pneumoperitoneum, placing the da Vinci ports, and assembling the robotic operating platform, which usually takes half an hour, more or less. On the other hand, it also indicated that it’s more difficult to spare the nephron of hilar RAML than that in other parts of the kidney. After a period of time (the minimum follow-up time was 10 months), the reexamination of renal function was ideal, and there was no recurrence found on CT or ultrasound imaging.

During the postoperative follow-up, one patient developed iatrogenic collecting system injury and was readmitted to the hospital for transurethral ureteral stent implantation. The characteristics of this case were that the maximum diameter of the tumor was about 6.7 cm, and the R.E.N.A.L. score was 9 A, and the main body of the tumor was located in the renal sinus, which is closely attached to the collecting system. So even tumor aspiration was applied carefully at the tumor base, the impairment to the collecting system cannot be completely avoided. Therefore, for hilar RAML, in order to reduce the risk of urinary extravasation, we recommend: (1) Take full preoperative evaluation of tumor location and size, and perform ureteral stent implantation in advance if necessary, so that even if there is a small injury of collecting system after surgery, urine can be fully drained through the ureteral stent, which can relieve the patient’s discomfort and facilitate local healing. (2) For cases of which the renal pelvis collecting system was injured suspiciously in surgery, local suture repair should be performed decidedly with 4 − 0 Vicryl suture or barbed suture. However, for cases where the exposure of the surgical field at the tumor base was not ideal, local suture repair may not be accurate or effective, so it is recommended to pay close attention to the patient’s postoperative symptoms and signs, and reexamine the creatinine value of the drainage fluid after surgery, and recheck CTU if necessary, so as to improve the early identification of postoperative urinary extravasation and perform ureteral stent implantation as soon as possible.

There are differences in the propensity of laparoscopic approaches of RAML between surgeons. Some urologists suggest to employ the transabdominal approach, which has a better surgical field and a larger operating space. But the disadvantage is that it is easily affected by the intestine, and it is more difficult to expose the renal artery. Other surgeons prefer the retroperitoneal approach, of which the surgical field is slightly narrow, but the renal artery is relatively easy to dissect [8, 22, 23]. Therefore, it is necessary to individually select the surgical approach with the greatest benefit based on the tumor’s morphology and location. For hilum RAML, we recommend the transperitoneal approach. The tumor characteristics in this study showed that the median maximum diameter of the tumor is 7.1 (6.6-9.0) cm, and the R.E.N.A.L. score with class A (ventral) was accounting for 65.8% (25/38). Thus, it can be seen that hilum RAML are often large and located on the ventral side, so the transperitoneal approach benefits the surgeons in exposing the surgical field and anatomical plane, and the working space is larger, reducing the difficulty of surgery. As we all know, the WIT is the key factor affecting the renal function. Kural et al. reported that the median WIT in robotic-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) was 27.3 (13–40) min; Williams et al. reported that the mean WIT in RAPN was (18.5 ± 7.6) min. In addition, some studies have pointed out that the kidney WIT should be controlled within 30 min, otherwise it may cause irreversible renal ischemic injury [24, 25]. In this study, the median intraoperative WIT was 20.0 (17.3–24.0) min, and there was no significant impairment in Scr and eGFR in postoperative reexamination.

Conclusions

Our center innovatively proposes that the surgical strategy combining robot-assisted laparoscopic tumor enucleation with tumor aspiration for hilar RAML is safe and reliable, which can accelerate tumor resection without damage to renal function.
