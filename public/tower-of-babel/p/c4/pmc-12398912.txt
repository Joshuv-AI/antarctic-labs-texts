An intelligent closed-loop CES for depression treatment using brain signals and machine learning
Authors: Fahimeh Mosayebinejad, Hamid Mirhosseini, Sara Jambarsang, Fahimeh Saeed, Reza Daneshmand, Morteza Mojaver, Setareh Zargar, Asma Khosravi Najafabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This method presents a double-blind, randomized, parallel-group clinical trial aimed at personalizing cranial electrotherapy stimulation (CES) according to individual brain signals, assessing the effect of Intelligent Closed-Loop Cranial Electrotherapy Stimulation (IC-CES1) on Major Depressive Disorder (MDD).Objective: To evaluate the effectiveness of IC-CES in treating MDD.Design: 120 participants were randomly assigned to either the IC-CES group or the CES group.Assessment: Depression, anxiety, and sleep quality were measured at multiple time points using standard questionnaires.

Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) involves a consistently low mood, recurrent thoughts about death, and a range of physical and mental symptoms. Individuals diagnosed with MDD often experience a lower quality of life, which may be due to the disorder itself, co-occurring medical conditions, social challenges, and reduced ability to function in daily life [1]. Therefore, it is important to treat depression.

Non-pharmacological treatments for depression are often preferred because of their lower risk of side effects and lower likelihood of treatment discontinuation [2]. Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) represents a method for modulating brain activity [3]. Cranial Electrotherapy Stimulation (CES), a non-invasive neurostimulation technique, is an intervention for alleviating symptoms of anxiety, depression, and insomnia in individuals with anxiety disorders [4]. CES modulates neural activity by delivering pulsed electrical currents through surface electrodes, which can influence neuroplasticity, coordinate cortical networks, and ultimately affect cognitive and behavioural functions [5].

However, several criticisms have been raised regarding the use of CES. For instance, Brunyé et al. [6] highlighted limitations in the existing literature, including heterogeneity in stimulation parameters, lack of blinding, and potential methodological biases. These inconsistencies have hampered the ability to draw reliable conclusions regarding the efficacy and mechanisms of CES.

Signals from electroencephalography (EEG) have the potential to enhance insights into the pathophysiological processes involved in Major Depressive Disorder [7]. By integrating quantitative EEG (QEEG) data into CES protocols, clinicians can tailor stimulation parameters to each patient’s unique neural profile. This personalized strategy may lead to more targeted interventions, enhanced therapeutic outcomes, and greater efficiency in terms of time and cost. Neuroimaging studies have already demonstrated that CES modulates cortical activity and functional connectivity [8], supporting the need for individualized optimization of stimulation parameters. Therefore, personalized and data-driven protocols, such as those informed by QEEG, may offer a promising pathway to standardize and optimize CES application, improving both reliability and replicability of outcomes across studies and populations.

In this context, emerging evidence suggests that tailoring stimulation delivery based on real-time brain states may improve outcomes. For instance, Sellers et al. [9] introduced a closed-loop neurostimulation protocol using chronically implanted devices that administer stimulation specifically during high-symptom states. This personalized and responsive approach not only avoids habituation effects associated with continuous stimulation but also optimizes therapeutic efficiency by minimizing total stimulation time.

Regarding the effects of CES, individual differences remain a major challenge. Certain patients do not respond to conventional treatments. Therefore, developing interventions that effectively address this issue would be a notable advancement in mental health treatment. This study proposes an innovative approach that combines machine learning with brain signal analysis to enhance treatment specificity for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), with the expectation that this method will significantly improve treatment options and overall therapeutic outcomes.

Method details

This was a double-blind, randomized, parallel-group trial conducted at two psychiatric clinics in Tehran, Iran. Ethical approval was granted by the Social Welfare and Rehabilitation University Ethics Committee (IR.USWR.REC.1402.097), and the study was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20230112057118N1).

One hundred ten adults (aged 18–65) diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) via DSM-5 criteria and a structured clinical interview were enrolled. Eligible participants had a BDI-II score >14, no recent use of antidepressants or anxiolytics, and could attend all five treatment sessions; those with contraindications to cranial stimulation, other psychiatric or neurological disorders, major physical illnesses, or emergent high-risk suicidal ideation were excluded. They were randomly assigned (1:1) to either IC-CES or conventional CES using SPSS-generated random numbers, with allocation concealed in sealed envelopes and all outcome assessments performed by a blinded researcher.

Sample size (N = 55 per group) was calculated in G*Power (v3.1.9.4) for 80 % power at α=0.05 (effect size f = 0.25). Group codes remained offsite until data lock to ensure unbiased allocation.

The IC-CES system (“Good Vibes Solutions,” PCT/IB2022/058,302) was developed in-house and is covered under the Patent Cooperation Treaty.

This system is based on a patented invention titled "EEG-guided treatment based on cranial electrotherapy stimulation". IC-CES begins with QEEG acquisition, processed via Independent Component Analysis (ICA) using Fast ICA (run in EEGLAB v14) and Current Source Density (CSD) filtering, which was a Spherical spline CSD transform (ε = 1e-5) applied to sharpen spatial resolution. Multi-dimensional feature vectors are extracted using statistical and frequency-domain methods such as Z-score, entropy, skewness, kurtosis, and Lomb-Scraggle periodogram. Then, the preprocessed EEG feature vectors—each a 400-dimensional representation comprising 10 regional averages of 40 statistical and spectral measures—were first standardized to ensure comparability across subjects. We split the database of feature vectors into distinct training, validation, and test subsets (70 %/15 %/15 %), considering the group, sex, and age factors to prevent data leakage and to rigorously evaluate model generalizability. We fit a StandardScaler on the control group of the train set and then applied the learned means and variances to both the validation and test sets. A K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN) machine learning algorithm (KneighborsClassifier, metric=Euclidean) is used to optimize stimulation parameters by matching each patient’s pre-treatment EEG features with a normative EEG database. Hyperparameter tuning was carried out via GridSearchCV over a two-dimensional grid—n_neighbors ∈ {3, 5, 7, 9} and weights ∈ {‘uniform’, ‘distance’}—using five-fold cross-validation. The scoring metric was negative mean squared error between the predicted and actual stimulation parameters.

Beyond K-Nearest Neighbors, we also evaluated other models to ensure that K-NN offered the best trade-off between accuracy, interpretability, and real-time performance. Within scikit-learn (v0.24.2), we compared RandomForestRegressor (n_estimators ∈ {50,100,200})), and GradientBoostingRegressor (learning_rate ∈ {0.01,0.1}, max_depth ∈ {3,5}). We also tested XGBoost (v1.6.0) via the xgboost Python API, tuning n_estimators, eta, and max_depth with the same 5-fold CV protocol (scoring=neg MSE). Each model was wrapped in the identical Pipeline (StandardScaler + estimator). Although Random Forest and XGBoost slightly outperformed K-NN in cross-validated MSE (by ∼2–3 %), their inference times (25–40 ms per patient) exceeded our clinical threshold for real-time adjustment. K-NN (with BallTree acceleration) delivered comparable accuracy (within 5 % of the best MSE) while guaranteeing sub-millisecond neighbor queries, and its simplicity facilitated on-the-fly updates of the normative database without retraining a full ensemble.

To accelerate neighbor queries in this high-dimensional space, we set algorithm='ball_tree', enabling sub-millisecond lookups per patient after a single precomputation of neighbor indices and distances. Parameters such as waveform, frequency, and amplitude are determined by majority voting from the most similar cases. Treatment response is evaluated by comparing the Euclidean distance between the pre- and post-treatment EEG features and healthy averages.

Depression, anxiety, and sleep quality were assessed using validated Persian versions of the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II, 21 items, Cronbach’s α=0.87, test–retest r = 0.74.), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI, 21 items, validity r = 0.83, reliability r = 0.72–0.92.), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI, α=0.75–0.83.) at four time points: before treatment, mid-treatment (after session 3), two weeks post-treatment, and one month follow-up. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test (α=0.05), inspecting QQ‐plots, and confirming skewness and kurtosis values fell within ±1. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to evaluate the main effects of time, group, and their interaction on BDI, BAI, and PSQI scores, including within-subject contrasts for trend analysis and comparison of estimated means between and within groups across time. We tested the sphericity assumption via Mauchly’s test; whenever Mauchly’s test was significant, we applied the Greenhouse–Geisser correction to adjust degrees of freedom.

An Intention-To-Treat (ITT) analysis was also performed alongside Per-Protocol (PP) analysis to address potential bias due to missing data. Missing values from 10 participants (8.3 %) were imputed using linear regression, and repeated measures analyses were re-run under ITT to assess robustness of findings.

Method validation

Preliminary findings from our pilot study suggest a promising trend in reducing depression and anxiety symptoms with the IC-CES device. However, no significant differences in sleep quality were observed between the IC-CES group and the CES group.

This methodological report is part of a co-submission. The main research article, entitled "An Intelligent Closed-Loop CES for the Treatment of Depression Using Brain Signals and Machine Learning: A double-blind, randomized clinical trial", which presents the results and discussion, is currently under review in the Journal of Affective Disorders.

Limitations

Since we anticipated that long-term access to patients might not be feasible, the long-term effects of treatment beyond one month would also require investigation.

Additionally, patients with somatoform depressive symptoms were not included in this study, and future research involving this population is warranted given the potential clinical relevance.

The lack of a sham stimulation group represents another limitation of this study. Because participants were drawn from a clinical population actively pursuing treatment, there was limited willingness to participate in sham conditions, which were likely viewed as lacking therapeutic benefit.
