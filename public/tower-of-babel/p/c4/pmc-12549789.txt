Label-free photoacoustic imaging of glassfrog development
Authors: Qiangzhou Rong, Carlos Taboada, Van Tu Nguyen, Rui Yao, Jesse Delia, Yushun Zeng, Xiaoyi Zhu, Qifa Zhou, Junjie Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A primary focus of contemporary biology is to understand how internal molecules influence natural development. Many amphibians serve as highly effective model organisms for this research due to their rapid growth rates and transparent tissues, which facilitate high-resolution imaging. In our research, we utilized two complementary photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) configurations: hyperspectral PAM (HS-PAM) and ultrafast functional PAM (UFF-PAM). HS-PAM enabled us to achieve cellular-level resolution in vitro, while UFF-PAM allowed us to capture hemodynamic changes of adult specimens in vivo. We monitored the morphological changes in glassfrogs from neurulation to the tadpole stage by detecting a variety of intrinsic contrasts, including DNA/RNA, yolk proteins, lipids, hemoglobin, and melanin. The PAM images provided detailed depictions of anatomical development. To further explore the versatility of these systems, we also imaged tissue structures within the skeletal muscle, liver, and fat tissue of other treefrog species. Additionally, we monitored blood flow dynamics in two species of glassfrogs under both awake and under anesthesia. Overall, our findings demonstrate that PAM is a powerful and versatile method, that can be coupled with different species of amphibians to inform applications in developmental biology.

Introduction

Over the years, amphibians have made significant contributions to our understanding of essential biological processes. Sharing fundamental traits with other vertebrates, they offer unique insights that are invaluable across various fields of research, ranging from basic studies in comparative anatomy and evolution to translational applications. Amphibians have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of muscle function [1], [2], nerve impulses [3], [4], [5], endocrinology [6], [7], [8], [9], and organogenesis and embryology [10], [11], [12], [13], [14].

The approximately 8810 living species of amphibians are grouped into three orders—Gymnophiona (caecilians), Caudata (salamanders), and Anura (frogs and toads). The latter is the largest, including more than 7761 species currently recognized (AmphibiaWeb 2024). The most common reproductive mode in anurans is a fully biphasic cycle characterized by aquatic eggs that develop into exotrophic aquatic tadpoles that, in turn, metamorphose into four-legged frogs. In this case, part of the developmental energy is obtained from the vitellogenic yolk and part by feeding in water [15]. However, besides these biphasic reproductive modes, anurans exhibit some of the most diverse parental strategies in vertebrates, such as semiterrestriality (terrestrial eggs, aquatic larva), terrestriality (terrestrial eggs and larva), direct development (terrestrial eggs, no larva) and live-bearing (no eggs, no larva) [16]. These diversity in reproductive modes makes anurans an unparalleled system for the investigation of the evolution of embryogenesis, organogenesis, and parental care to name but a few.

Among their many advantages, frog embryos stand out due to their large size and abundant availability, making them particularly amenable to experimental manipulations such as microsurgery, lineage tracing, and genetic modification [17]. These features, combined with their conserved developmental pathways, position amphibians as an ideal system for gaining insights into vertebrate biology and embryology, and for addressing questions with profound evolutionary and biomedical implications. Their versatility and accessibility continue to make them indispensable in scientific discovery, from investigating fundamental evolutionary questions [18], [19], [20], [21], exploring natural products with translational applications [22], [23], [24], to unraveling the molecular and physiological mechanisms underlying health and disease [17], [25]. Notably, while most research in anuran embryology, genetics, and biomedicine has focused on traditional model organisms such as the African clawed frog, the great diversity of species, with their numerous novel adaptations [26], [27], [28], biochemical innovations [22], [29], [30], [31], and unique physiological traits [13], [32], [33], [34], offers unparalleled opportunities to pose new questions and opens numerous research avenues.

Traditional experiments using light-field microscopy have illuminated the early stages of frog development [35], aided by the large size of the embryos and their external development, which make them particularly convenient for laboratory studies. To investigate fundamental processes within developing embryos, most approaches have relied on histological examinations. These methods address the challenge of limited light penetration in specimens by analyzing thin slices that are typically fixed, stained, or immunolabeled [35], [36], [37], [38]. Histology has been essential in describing and elucidating innumerable traits, from detailed anatomical and embryological studies [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44] to investigating various aspects of amphibian natural history. The examples of using this approach are vast and include studies on the prevalence and distribution of natural fluorophores and pigments [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], photoreceptors [50], the distribution of different metabolites in skin glands [51], [52], [53], and the cornification of appendages [39], [54], [55], to name a few.

Despite the wide range of imaging tools, investigating the distribution of biological molecules at the whole-embryo level in intact specimens, both in vivo and in vitro, presents significant challenges. This is particularly true for species with high concentrations of pigmented molecules, which limit the applicability of most optical approaches [29], [44], [56]. This limitation becomes more pronounced as embryos and tadpoles accumulate lipids, secrete fibrous proteins into the extracellular matrix, and deposit pigments, all of which attenuate light propagation and hinder the imaging of structures deep within organs or tissues. These factors make it difficult to investigate specimens during mid-to-late developmental phases [57].

In contrast to staining-dependent optical methods, photoacoustic microscopy (PAM) is a label-free approach that can simultaneously depict both structural and molecular information based on the specific absorbance of chromophores [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66]. This technique has been successfully implemented in the study of model and non-model organisms [58], and it suggests a future trend towards functional, label-free methods for identifying chromophores, easing sample preparation, and minimizing toxicity from dyes [67], [68]. Other label-free PA approaches have been implemented for the in vivo investigation of hemodynamics in frogs, particularly of glassfrogs of the species Hyalinobatrachium fleischmanni, known to possess extraordinarily transparent tissues that help with their camouflage [69]. Notably, glassfrogs typically lack melanin and reflective elements in their ventral skin [31], [70] and muscles, providing a transparency window to image the cardiovascular system as well as other anatomical features and physiological processes in vivo. As amphibian hemoglobin in red blood cells (RBCs) shares the same spectral characteristics as the respiratory pigments of mammals and other vertebrates [31], [71], PAM has proven exceptionally useful for investigating glassfrog hemodynamics and uncovering the hidden physiological mechanisms behind their transparency. Taboada et al. described how glassfrogs can actively modify RBC distribution in circulation to conceal them from view while they sleep, thereby reducing light absorption and allowing the animals to blend into the vegetation. This study also recognized PAM as an unmatched modality for noninvasive imaging of glassfrog hemodynamics. Ultrafast functional PAM (UFF-PAM), can precisely capture real-time dynamic changes in RBC circulation while simultaneously enabling the quantification of hemoglobin oxygen saturation (sO2). However, while UFF-PAM has enabled the recording of rapid phenomena, imaging hemodynamic patterns in awake, moving animals remains notoriously challenging. In this context, the use of UFF-PAM to image glassfrogs provides an unparalleled opportunity to investigate hemodynamic changes beneath the skin and in freely moving vertebrates.

In this work, two advanced PAM systems, HS-PAM and UFF-PAM, were employed to investigate the embryonic growth and cellular architecture, of developing glassfrogs of the species Teratohyla pulverata (See Fig. 1). By harnessing natural optical contrasts, we imaged the morphological progression of embryogenesis in live specimens at distinct developmental stages. Furthermore, we examined the vascular hemodynamics of adult glassfrogs, both in anesthetized and actively moving specimens. Results

HS-PAM and UFF-PAM of glassfrogs with endogenous contrast

The HS-PAM setup features a tunable wide-spectrum laser and a reflective acoustic-optic combiner with a broadband lens and a compact ultrasound transducer. The laser is integrated through the ROL-based combiner (see Fig. 1a). Detailed system configuration can be found in our prior publication [58]. Animals were positioned beneath the combiner, enabling selection of wavelengths from UV to NIR for sample illumination. Due to its operation in reflection mode, the system accommodates organs of different sizes.

The UFF-PAM setup features a 12-facet diamond-ground metal polygon driven by an underwater high-speed DC motor, aligning laser and ultrasound beams for simultaneous steering (see Fig. 1b). It fires two laser pulses at 532 nm and 558 nm in rapid succession. This system's setup is elaborated in a separate study [71]. The differing photoacoustic signals from oxy-hemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxy-hemoglobin (Hb) are due to their unique optical absorption profiles, which are utilized to calculate blood perfusion and oxygen saturation (sO2). The system achieves a volumetric imaging frame rate of 3 Hz across a 9 mm2 field of view.

Given the distinctive transparency of multiple amphibian embryos, they have been comprehensively utilized in research to understand vertebrate anatomical development [72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78]. In this study, we conducted longitudinal imaging of embryos of the glassfrog T. pulverata from the neurulation phase through to the tadpole stage (Fig. 1c), by employing HS-PAM with various light wavelengths tailored at specific chromophores.

Nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) can be imaged by PAM using ultraviolet (UV) light ∼266 nm, which can help to identify nucleus morphological features [79]. Additionally, we used HS-PAM at 430 nm to target common contrast agents in the frog pigmentary system, such as yellow nitrogen-rich pteridines [80], [81] and carotenoids [82], as well as the Soret band of porphyrins and hemoglobin [83], [84], [85]. Additionally, we used 532 nm to target the Q band of hemoglobin [85] or myoglobin [29] and 670 nm to detect the blue/green pigments present in many glassfrogs’ eggs and embryos, seemingly due to the high concentrations of a biliverdin-binding serpin [31], with maximum absorbance at 673 nm (biliverdin Q band) [86]. Last, we obtained images using 1210 nm, which has been exploited to visualize lipid abundant plaques, taking advantage of the contrast from the second overtone vibration of C-H bonds in lipids [87], [88], [89], [90].

Glassfrogs at the early stages

Our investigation of the glassfrog development using PA commenced during the neurula stage, at which point the neural plate's contours emerged. The eggs and developing embryos of T. pulverata are green/blue during their development (Fig. 1c), possibly due to the expression of a biliverdin-binding protein with absorption maxima at 673 nm. Furthermore, amphibians are known to deposit carotenoids in the oocytes [91], which confer yellow colors that can be tracked using 430 nm. By analyzing the embryo at 430 and 670 nm (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. s4), we could image the neural folds on its dorsal surface adjacent to the neural groove, as shown in Fig. 2a. During subsequent stages the tailbud embryo's body encircled the yolk mass (Fig. 2b) and early somites became discernible along the tail region. The ensuing stages were characterized by the lengthening of the body and the formation of the tailbud. During the tadpole phase, the outer jelly layer surrounding the embryo opened, and the tail, originally broad, gradually extended and sharpened—a transformation particularly evident in lateral views where somites become more obvious (Fig. 2c). As shown in Fig. 2c, we could see the developing vitelline vessels with high contrast at 430 nm. Deeper in the yolk, through the forming vessels, the blue proteins deeper in the developing embryo can be observed at 670 nm (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Furthermore, at this stage, multiple dots composed of dark pigment –likely melanin in melanophore cells (MP) were formed on the dorsal area of the body (Fig. 2d). In the advanced tadpole phase, the tadpole became more pigmented in its dorsal surface, but its ventral surface appeared transparent due to less pigmented skin and muscle, enabling the imaging of detailed morphological and vascular structures specifically captured at 430 nm. Observations at 532 nm uncovered similar morphological characteristics, along with putative MP, which can also be revealed at 670 nm and at 1210 nm as seen in Fig. 2f. Adult glassfrogs: muscles, liver, and fat

We further evaluated the capabilities of HS-PAM by inspecting multiple tissues from adult frogs of the species Sphaenorhynchus lacteus and Dendropsophus ebraccatus, in toto. For this purpose, we imaged three different tissues or organs: striated muscles, liver, and fat bodies, which are visceral fat deposits in anurans.

The skeletal or striated muscle, which is also recognized as voluntary or striated muscle, is the most thoroughly studied among the three types of muscles [1], [92], [93], [94], [95]. Frogs have been used in pioneering studies on muscle contractile properties and energetics [2], [96]. In our research, we targeted a section of the rectus abdominis, the flat muscle that extends along the front of the abdomen and is involved in the flexion of the trunk. We excised a small section of rectus abdominis, containing both muscle fibers and tendinous insertions. After preservation, it appeared semi-translucent in natural light (Fig. 3a). The skeletal muscle consists of fascicles, which are collective structures of muscle fibers. These fibers are multinucleated cells containing myofibrils—elongated cylindrical structures predominantly made of two kinds of contractile proteins, actin and myosin filaments [95], [96], [97]. The formation of myofibrils is the result of the merging of multiple progenitor cells [98], [99], leading to their extensive length and the multinucleate characteristic of separate fibers—meaning multiple nuclei within a single fiber—as depicted in Fig. 3b-c. Typically, these nuclei are situated along the outer edges of the fiber [100]. Myofibers contain myoglobin [101], a hemoprotein that plays an important role in oxygen storage and transport in skeletal muscle. Due to its binding to a heme group that coordinates an iron ion in a similar way to hemoglobin [102], myoglobin absorption spectrum resembles that of hemoglobin, with a strong absorption peak, the Soret absorption band at 405–430 nm [103], thus allowing the imaging of individual myofibers using 430 nm (Fig. 3c). Additionally illustrated in Fig. 3b are the elements of the fascia, an elastic fibrous sheath that surrounds, supports, and separates muscle fibers and myofibers. In vertebrates, the liver is critical for various bodily functions, including the metabolism and storage of nutrients [105], [106]. We prepared a frog liver sample by dissecting the apical portion of the left lobe (approximately 4 mm×4 mm x 4 mm) and captured images of a section of 1.5 mm by 1.5 mm using HS-PAM (Fig. 3d). We investigated the detailed micro-anatomy of UV-absorbing pigments at 266 nm (Fig. 3e). The image shows that the hepatocytes form clusters and cord-like structures (as shown in Fig. 3e). At 430 nm and 532 nm, we noticed a considerable presence of melanin-rich aggregates of cells known as melanomacrophage centers (MMCs) [107], [108], situated close to or within the sinusoids (Fig. 3f and g). MMCs are aggregates of highly pigmented phagocytes in the livers, spleen and kidneys of many vertebrates that play a role in the humoral adaptive immune response, and in other physiological functions such as phagocytosis of exhausted cells, particularly red blood cells [107]. In amphibians these MMC can increase their concentrations during hibernation [109], under hypoxia [110], under certain illumination conditions [111], and at different times of the breeding cycle [112] among others. Due to the weak absorption of light by the central veins that are depleted of RBCs after fixation, its cross-section was not visible in the images.

Visceral fat, also referred to as abdominal fat or organ fat, is found within the abdominal cavity in vertebrates and it is interspersed amongst organs such as the stomach, liver, intestines, and kidneys [113], [114]. In frogs, there is a wide variability in the distribution of fat tissue, but it is often confined to structures known as fat bodies (Fig. 1d), which are the largest body lipid deposits [86], [87], [88], [89]. Abdominal fat bodies are conspicuous structures attached to the gonadal mesentery at the anterior pole of the kidneys and gonads and hang loosely in the abdominal cavity. They can differ considerably in size among specimens throughout the year and breeding seasons, often undergoing reversible extreme changes in lipid content [115]. Fat bodies contain adipocytes that contain lipid droplets in the cytoplasm and resemble those of white adipose tissue (WAT) [116]. Depending on the diet of the animals, adipose tissue can accumulate various amounts of carotenoids, which often confer the tissue a distinct yellowish and even orange color depending on the concentration and identity of the carotenoids. This has been described in amphibians [117] and other animals [118]. These elements exhibit distinctive absorption signatures across a wide light spectrum. In this work, a section of the abdominal fat bodies comprising various lobules was carefully dissected from adult hatchet-face frogs (Fig. 3h) and subsequently mounted for HS-PAM at 266 nm, 430 nm, and 1210 nm (as shown in Fig. 3i-k). At 266 nm, optical absorbers, likely lipid droplets and glyocogen deposits in adypocytes, as well as cellular nuclei, appeared uniformly distributed throughout the specimen. At 430 nm, the distribution of absorbers is compatible with the presence of lipophilic carotenoids such as xanthophyll [117] with a maximum absorption peak at the 400–500 nm range. Observations made at 1210 nm revealed considerable amounts of lipid content uniformly spread across the fat body.

Skin and toes

Frogs and toads have four-fingered hands and, with few exceptions, five-toed legs [119]. With their toe pads, tree frogs, for instance, can adhere to a variety of materials, from smooth glass to rough wood, in both dry and wet environments. Several studies have investigated the surface morphology and explained the adhesive mechanism of their toe pads [120], [121]. In this work, we mainly focus on the histological contrast of the toes of the hourglass treefrog, Dendropsophus ebraccatus (Fig. 4a, for the whole feet see Supplementary). We targeted the epidermis, dermis, skeletal muscles, pigments, and the disc-shaped structures of the toe tips using four different wavelengths, as shown in Fig. 4b–e. The skin of the toes of Dendropsophus frogs is yellow/orange [122], likely due to the presence of carotenoid pigments. By imaging the third toe (toe III) using three different wavelengths in the blue-green region of the spectrum, we revealed a decrease in signal from 430 to 532 nm, which is consistent with the absorption peak of carotenoids [123]. Additionally, distinct discrete structures absorb evenly throughout the spectrum, likely explained by the presence of melanophore cells in the skin. Cardiovascular functions

The investigation of frogs using PA has been successful for the imaging of unique cardiovascular features, in particular in glassfrogs of the species Hyalinobatrachium fleischmanni. The use of UFF-PAM has uncovered a strategy used by this glassfrogs to maintain transparency by hiding their RBCs from view by pooling most of them inside their livers while they sleep. Using UFF-PAM, we investigated the versatility of the system to image hemodynamic changes as glassfrogs recover from anesthesia and begin to freely move in the experimental tray (Fig. 5). We quantified the relative concentrations of oxyhemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) from the PA signals generated by a pair of laser pulses at 532 nm and 558 nm. The temporal dynamics of RBCs in the glassfrog's torso were detailed by continuously tracking the sO2 signal (Supplementary movie 1). Fig. 5a shows the original position and posture of the frog under anesthesia. The sO2 map of the vasculature of the head and upper torso were captured (Fig. 5b). The pericardium and many visceral peritonea in the glassfrog H. fleischmanni contain highly reflective guanine crystals, known to attenuate over 80 % of incident visible light [30], making the detection of RBC signal from these organs particularly challenging, especially from actively moving structures such as the heart. However, with our system, we were able to successfully image the heart through the reflective pericardium (Fig. 5b–c). To image the hemodynamic changes as the frog recovered from anesthesia, we targeted small areas of the frog’s belly and thorax to account for the FOV of the system and optimized the mounting method to allow the frog to move within a confined space within the imaging window while remaining on the focal plane. In our experiments, the frog was initially anesthetized (Fig. 5b) and then gradually woke up. Later, the frog moved, exposing its middle torso, where the abdominal vein that runs along the middle line of the ventral body wall became visible (Fig. 5c). Throughout this transition, we were able to successfully record diastole-systole cycles of the heart (Supplementary movie 1). Additionally, the speed of the system allowed us to capture the sequential cycle of expansion and contraction of the lungs (Supplementary movie 1), revealing the resumption of active breathing. Fig. 5(e) display the sO2 map of the left leg and foot of the active frog. Fig. 5f provides insight into the vasculature of the plantar surface of the foot, enabling the identification of the digital artery-vein pairs that supply the toes. Discussion

Our investigation primarily focused on the anatomical and functional attributes of glassfrogs transitioning embryos through tadpoles. Utilizing HS-PAM, we could capture sequences of organ formation and scrutinize inherent biological contrasts, from the neurula stage to early-stage free larvae. In the transition from embryo to tadpoles we could describe distinct morphological and vascular traits including the formation of vitelline veins, and the high concentration and resorption of the biliverdin-binding proteins from the yolk that confer the characteristic green hue to the oocytes, eggs and embryos of the glassfrog

In this work, we have engineered a hyperspectral photoacoustic microscopy (HS-PAM) system that affords us the opportunity to monitor the embryonic development of glassfrogs with cellular precision. This innovative system enables the creation of a tool designed to reveal developmental events from the earliest stages of the frog embryo through to the tadpole phase by extracting and visualizing the formation of various anatomical systems. It also holds promises for the investigation of specific tissue signals such as those present in skeletal muscles, the liver, and adipose tissue at multiple stages of growth. Additionally, an in-house ultra-fast functional photoacoustic microscopy (UFF-PAM) system was employed to assess the hemodynamics of these amphibians under anesthesia and in active state after recovery. This system's rapid scanning abilities are key for observing RBC circulation changes with remarkable accuracy and resolution, including precise oxygenation measurements. The wavelengths we selected effectively differentiated these features. These methods provide a suitable technique for the visualization of tissue development in frogs without the need for labeling.Investigations into large, excised tissues have confirmed expected wavelength-dependent contrast within the tissue. UV is routinely used to identify cell nuclei [79], with incidental imaging of lipids in adipose tissues. However, due to the prevalence of UV-absorbing molecules in frog tissues, signal crossover from alternative molecules is expected, diminishing the distinctions between nuclei and lipids, as well as from other pigments normally used for the creation of coloration. Visible light excitation at 430 nm accentuated structures containing high concentrations of carotenoids, pterins, and porphyrins, the latter including heme-bearing respiratory pigments like hemoglobin, and myoglobin in muscle fibers [83], [84]. At 532 nm we targeted the Q-bands of heme in hemoglobin and myoglobin [31], [71], [85], [103]. With 670 nm we targeted the blue proteins of glassfrogs and followed their fate during embryogenesis, from a homogeneous distribution within the egg to a more a localized distribution in the yolk sac. Lipid contrast in adipose tissue was successfully achieved using near-infrared (NIR) wavelengths at 1210 nm, uncovering a wealth of lipid molecules. These insights enrich PAM's applicability for histology-like investigations.

Concluding our study, we harnessed UFF-PAM to image hemodynamic changes in glassfrogs on both macro and micro scales while exploring hemoglobin-mediated oxygen transport. The high frame rate of UFF-PAM allowed us to detect changes in oxygen levels. Observations, such as lowered hemoglobin oxygenation in anesthetized glassfrog lungs, are consistent with prior studies [31], suggesting that the glassfrogs might have a depressed oxidative metabolism while under anesthesia or that the oxygen take-up through the lungs is limited. Conversely, awake glassfrogs demonstrated more robust blood oxygenation, probably facilitated by lung ventilation. Interestingly, we could capture the beating heart of the glassfrogs, both the single ventricle and arterial cone, despite having a highly reflecting pericardium [31], [127]. The use of UFF-PAM successfully expanded the method's applicability to other frog species, demonstrating both the versatility of the instrument and the suitability of additional glassfrog species for in vivo hemodynamic investigation.

In conclusion, PAM serves as an invaluable instrument for collecting histological contrast data across different molecules and mapping out tissue structures. This study lays the foundation for further exploration, providing the baseline for developmental profiles and methodologies for researching frog embryos, their histology, and associated hemodynamics.

Materials and methods

In imaging, we used two distinct PAM systems to explore endogenous contrasts: (1) a hyperspectral PAM (HS-PAM) [58] to map the early developmental stages of glassfrogs, and various tissue types from additional treefrogs, achieving cellular-level resolution in vitro; (2) an ultrafast functional PAM (UFF-PAM) [71] to capture hemodynamics of two species of glassfrogs in vivo.

HS-PAM system. The system consists of a wavelength-tunable laser with wavelength range 210–2300 nm. The broadband laser wavelength was divided into four sub-bands. All wavelengths were finally combined into a reflective objective lens (ROL) with zero chromatic aberration over a broad spectral range. A needle transducer (NT) with a center frequency of 35 MHz and bandwidth ∼43 MHz was used to detect the photoacoustic signals. Mechanical scanning of the sample was provided by the linear stepper motor stages. (2) UFF-PAM system. We used a pulsed Nd: YAG laser as the source for optical excitation, with a wavelength of 532 nm and a pulse repetition rate of up to 800 KHz. An optical fiber-based Raman shifter was developed to excite a longer wavelength of 558 nm. The remaining 532 nm light and the emitted 558 nm light are combined as excitation laser source. A focused ring-shaped ultrasonic transducer was used as the detector. A lab-made hexagonal mirror scanner was used to steer the light and acoustic wave. The detail information of these two systems can be found in our previous publications in [58], [71].

PAM data processing

We used a bandpass filter on all acquired acoustic signals at each scanning location, to first improve the signal-to-noise ratio. We then applied a Hilbert transform to the filter signal and extract the signal envelop at each optical excitation wavelength. For further data processing, we used either the maximum amplitude projection images (MAP) along the depth direction or the volumetric images.

Quantification of blood oxygenation: We computed a map of oxygen saturation of hemoglobin (sO2) using PA signals induced at 532 nm and 558 nm for UFF-PAM using our multi-spectral method (see [71]. The acquired PA signals at different wavelengths are essentially a linear mixture of the signals from the oxy-hemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxy-hemoglobin (Hb), weighted by their relative concentrations. By solving the linear equations with their concentrations as unknowns, we can estimate the relative concentration of HbO2 and calculate sO2.

Preparation of animal models

All animal procedures were approved by the Duke University Medical Center Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol numbers: A174–21–08, A009–20–01) and were conducted in accordance with the United States Public Health Service’s Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Frogs

The following frog species were used for the current research: powdered glassfrogs (Teratohyla pulverata), Fleischmann's glass frog (Hyalinobatrachium fleischmanni), the hatchet-faced treefrog (Sphaenorhynchus lacteus), and the clown treefrog (Dendropsophus ebraccatus). These species were maintained in bioactive vivaria (12-hour light/dark cycles, temperature 22–25°C, relative humidity >70 %) with plants, small invertebrates, and microbiota. The animals were fed a mixed diet of crickets and fruit flies every 2–4 days.

Eggs/embryos/tadpoles

Egg clutches of T. pulverata were carefully disengaged from the upper side of Philodendron leaves in the frog enclosures and immediately transferred into Petri dishes previously filled with a 2–3 mm layer of water.

Tissues

We harvested tissues from euthanized adult specimens of D. ebraccatus and S. lacteus. Liver, fat bodies, and muscles were excised and fixed in 10 % formalin pH 7, and transferred to PBS before imaging.

Imaging

For the imaging of embryos, the egg jelly was manually removed before placing them on the imaging stage. The imaging stage consisted of a Petri dish covered in agar with a small notch to hold the specimen and minimize rotation and translation during the imaging session. The same stage was used for the study of excised tissues.

For imaging hemodynamics in both anesthetized and freely moving frogs, adult specimens of T. pulverata and H. fleischmanni were placed in Petri dishes equipped with a lid containing an optically and acoustically transparent window made of food-grade PVC film. A water tank was placed between the Petri dish and the ultrasound transducer (UT) to couple PA signals from the frog to the UT.

For all the experiments, distilled water was treated with the reverse-osmosis water conditioner R/O Rx (Josh Frogs, Josh’s Frogs, Owosso, MI).

Anesthesia

Adult frogs and tadpoles were anesthetized by immersion in 0.35 g/L tricaine methanesulfonate solution (MS-222; Sigma-Aldrich).
