Multimodal deep learning for bone tumor diagnosis with clinical imaging, pathology, and blood biomarkers
Authors: Hang Sang, Tao Lin, Lincong Luo, Mingrui Liu, Jiaying Li, Xiang Luo, Jianlin Shen, Shizhen Zhong, Lin Xu, Wenhua Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate classification of bone tumors as benign, malignant, or intermediate is crucial for patient treatment decisions. Misclassification may result in overtreatment of benign cases or delayed intervention for aggressive tumors, significantly impacting patient prognosis. However, current methods rely heavily on single-modality imaging analysis, making it difficult to handle variable lesion locations and complex cancer types. To address these limitations, we propose a novel multimodal deep learning framework that integrates clinical images, pathological slices, and blood biomarkers for automated bone tumor detection and three-class classification. The framework operates in two stages: first, a YOLOv5-based detection model localizes tumor regions on clinical images. Next, a classification model utilizes ResNet to extract deep features from both the clinical images and pathological slices, while abnormal blood biomarkers are transformed into descriptive text by a large language model and subsequently encoded into semantic features using BioBERT. Finally, features from all three modalities are integrated via a fusion module to capture complementary information and enable accurate tumor classification. The evaluation was performed using two distinct datasets: a clinical imaging dataset for bone tumor detection, and a separate multi-modal cohort comprising clinical imaging, pathology, and blood biomarkers for tumor classification. The detection model demonstrated strong localization capabilities, achieving a test mAP@0.5 of 0.7925. For the classification task, ablation studies validated the complementary contribution of each modality. Notably, our multimodal fusion approach outperformed unimodal baselines, attaining a macro-average precision of 0.9056, F1-score of 0.8736, and AUC of 0.9759 in tumor classification—outperforming existing models.

Introduction

Bone tumors represent a broad and heterogeneous group of diseases. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, bone tumors are mainly divided into three categories: benign, intermediate, and malignant types [1], [2]. It is particularly important to emphasize that malignant bone tumors, if not diagnosed and treated promptly, may result in poor prognosis for patients, such as limb amputation or distant metastasis, and are associated with a high risk of morbidity and mortality [3], [4]. The 10-year survival of localized osteosarcoma is almost 70%, yet rapidly declines to 20%–30% in patients with metastases [5]. Therefore, early detection, accurate classification, and timely intervention are crucial for improving patient outcomes and overall survival rates. Nevertheless, clinical diagnosis remains a significant challenge due to the rarity of bone tumors [6], their biological diversity, and the overlapping clinical presentations between benign and malignant lesions [7]. Imaging examinations, such as X-ray, MRI, and CT, serve as the cornerstone for initial assessment and differential diagnosis of bone tumors [8]. Nevertheless, clinicians often encounter significant challenges in attaining both diagnostic accuracy and timeliness, given that current diagnostic approaches are frequently resource-intensive, economically burdensome, time-constrained, and subject to interobserver variability based on the physician’s level of training and clinical experience [9], [10].

Pathological assessment via biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis [11], providing crucial insights into tumor grade and cellular structure. Likewise, blood biomarkers like alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) offer valuable information on tumor activity and therapy response, with elevated ESR indicating tumor burden and progression in multiple myeloma [12]. However, relying on any single modality may miss key features, so a comprehensive diagnosis should integrate imaging, pathology, and biomarkers for a more reliable conclusion.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning and deep learning, has rapidly advanced in medical image analysis and computer-aided diagnosis. These algorithms open new opportunities in bone tumor diagnostics. AI-assisted bone tumor diagnosis typically involves detection and classification. Early methods used traditional machine learning models. For instance, Hong et al. [13] developed a random forest model [14] to distinguish bone islands from osteoblastic metastases on CT scans, achieving diagnostic performance comparable to junior radiologists. However, these models relied on handcrafted features, which missed complex spatial information in medical images. With the rise of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [15], deep learning models have become dominant in tumor classification tasks. For example, Do et al. [16] used a CNN to classify knee bone regions as normal or malignant, and He et al. [17] proposed a CNN for automatic classification of bone tumors into benign, intermediate, or malignant categories. Chen et al. [18] improved CNNs by integrating VGG-16 and Vision Transformer models, boosting classification accuracy on CT images. Li et al. [19] used a YOLO-based method to detect and classify bone tumors end-to-end. However, most studies focus solely on clinical images, limiting their ability to capture the multidimensional characteristics of tumors. Recent studies have incorporated richer data sources to enhance classification generalizability. Liu et al. [20] fused MRI and patient age data for spinal tumor diagnosis, and another study by Liu et al. [21] combined X-ray images with clinical metadata, surpassing unimodal methods for three-class classification. However, these approaches often reduce clinical parameters to simple numbers, ignoring their biological and pathophysiological relevance.

In summary, AI-assisted bone tumor classification faces three key challenges: (1) Lack of automatic tumor detection or restrictive coupling of detection and classification; (2) Overreliance on unimodal data, limiting diagnostic accuracy for complex tumors; (3) Insufficient interpretability, hindering model understanding and clinical adoption.

Recent advancements in language models (LMs) have demonstrated strong semantic understanding of textual data, opening new possibilities for enhancing multimodal information comprehension. Inspired by this, we propose a novel multimodal bone tumor classification framework to tackle these challenges and assist clinical decision-making, particularly in primary healthcare settings. Our key contributions are as follows:

•We propose a two-stage detection-classification architecture that first localizes bone lesions and then performs classification, enabling flexible and automated improvement of both tasks.•We comprehensively integrate three types of data modalities — clinical imaging, pathological slides, and blood biomarkers — transcending the limitations of unimodal approaches and promoting synergistic learning with improved adaptation for complex cases.•We introduce large language models to transform blood biomarkers into semantically enriched text, extracting auxiliary biomedical knowledge via pretrained language encoders to improve the classification process.

Materials and methods

This chapter overviews the materials and methods used in our study. We collected a cohort of bone tumor cases from Hospital and constructed a multimodal dataset including clinical images, pathological slices, and blood biomarkers. The diversity of this dataset provides a solid foundation for in-depth analysis and the application of advanced computational techniques to bone tumor research.

Our framework operates in two stages. First, deep learning–based detection identifies tumor regions in clinical images. Second, multi-source features are extracted from imaging, pathology, and biomarkers, and fused via a multimodal integration network to classify tumors as benign, intermediate, or malignant. This two-stage approach significantly improves both sensitivity and classification accuracy, supporting early and individualized diagnosis.

Stage 1: Bone tumor detection using clinical imaging

Imaging preprocessing and data enhancement

Clinical images (X-ray, CT, MRI) were standardized to RGB, normalized to reduce intensity variation, and transformed to enhance bone–soft tissue contrast. Augmentations such as flipping and rotation improved generalization. Channel boosting added edge and texture cues for subtle lesion detection.

As shown in Fig. 1 Stage 1, we use YOLOv5 [22], [23], a widely used object detection model, to identify bone tumors in X-ray, CT, or MRI scans. These tumors often appear as small, irregular, and low-contrast areas, making their detection challenging. The second stage outlines the classification process leveraging multimodal data, including clinical imaging, pathological slice, and blood biomarkers. Features from clinical imaging and pathological slice are extracted via convolutional operations and ResNet, while features from blood biomarkers are obtained through preprocessing and BioBERT. These diverse features are then integrated by a Multi-source integrator and subsequently classified into benign, intermediate, or malignant categories.

The model’s backbone extracts key features from the images, with a Focus layer that enhances image details to better detect small tumors. YOLOv5 also employs advanced techniques to distinguish tumors from surrounding bone structures and benign conditions [24]. To improve detection across different tumor sizes, the model combines features at multiple scales. This enables it to detect both small and large tumors, even when they overlap with other anatomical structures. The model then predicts tumor locations and sizes with high accuracy. Finally, a post-processing step removes redundant predictions, ensuring that only the most confident tumor candidates remain.

Stage 2: Bone tumor classification using multimodal data

As illustrated in Fig. 1 Stage 2, our classification model integrates three types of clinical information: tumor images from X-ray, CT, or MRI; pathological tissue slides; and blood biomarker results. Each data type provides complementary insights into the tumor’s characteristics. To make full use of this multimodal information, we extract meaningful features from each source and combine them to support accurate classification into benign, intermediate, or malignant categories.

The architecture of ResNet

To analyze medical images from radiology or pathology, we use ResNet-50 [25], a deep learning model that automatically extracts meaningful visual features. It starts by detecting simple patterns like edges and textures, then progressively learns more complex characteristics such as tumor morphology and tissue structure. Each image is ultimately converted into a compact feature vector that captures key diagnostic information for classification, as illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2The framework preprocesses blood biomarker data (a clear legend as shown in Table 1), extracts abnormal biomarkers, and generates descriptions with a language model. The architecture of biomarkers stream

Blood biomarkers — such as elevated alkaline phosphatase or abnormal erythrocyte sedimentation rate — can indicate tumor activity or systemic effects. However, these values are often interpreted in context, requiring clinical knowledge to assess their significance.

To incorporate this information effectively, we first identify which biomarkers are outside normal ranges. Then, instead of using raw numbers directly, we convert these abnormal results into a short natural language description. For example: “The test result for Glucose is 7.71, with a reference range of 3.9–6.1, and the test indicator shows high; The test result for Uric Acid is 440, with a reference range of 208–428, and the test indicator shows high; The test result for Bicarbonate is 18.9, with a reference range of 23–29, and the test indicator shows low”.

We generate such descriptions using a large language model, ensuring that only clinically relevant changes are highlighted in a way that preserves their biological meaning. This text is then processed by BioBERT [26], a language model specifically trained on biomedical literature to capture semantic relationships between medical terms—for instance, associating “elevated ALP” with “increased bone turnover”. By converting these clinical narratives into numerical representations, the model gains the ability to interpret not only the raw biomarker values but also their clinical context and potential pathological implications.

Enhancing classification with multi-modal fusion

Imaging, pathology, and blood biomarkers offer complementary tumor insights. Our model fuses their features into a unified representation, enabling joint interpretation of diverse evidence—such as subtle imaging changes, abnormal cells, and systemic markers. The fusion module learns adaptive weights to improve classification into benign, intermediate, or malignant categories, enhancing accuracy and robustness over single-modality approaches.

Experimental results

Datasets

For the bone tumor detection task, 1,115 clinical images were collected, with each tumor lesion carefully annotated by experienced specialists using bounding boxes. Data augmentation methods — including random flips, angle rotations, and channel boosting — were used to improve generalization, resulting in a final dataset of 2,179 images. The dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio, ensuring balanced case distribution.

For the classification task, each patient cases included multiple clinical images, pathological slices, and blood biomarkers. By pairing images and slices with their respective biomarkers, we constructed 577 data groups from 38 patient cases. After screening and according to the fifth edition of the WHO classification, all 577 cases were labeled as benign, malignant, or intermediate tumors, with all lesions pathologically confirmed, as shown in Table 2. System performance and clinical deployment

To assess clinical applicability, we evaluated inference time, scalability, workflow integration, and data privacy. All experiments were conducted on a workstation with dual Intel Xeon CPUs and four NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs.

Inference time: The average end-to-end inference time per patient is 0.68 s, broken down as: tumor detection (0.01 s), clinical image feature extraction (0.02 s), pathological slice processing (0.04 s), biomarker encoding via LLM and BioBERT (0.6 s), and fusion (0.01 s).

System scalability: The modular design supports flexible deployment. In resource-limited settings, the system can run on a single GPU with minor latency increase. Lightweight variants enable edge deployment, and batch processing improves throughput.

Integration into clinical workflows: The framework can be integrated into hospital PACS, LIS, and HIS. Upon data availability, results are automatically generated and delivered to clinicians via a structured report interface, supporting real-time diagnosis and multidisciplinary decision-making.

Data Privacy: All patient identifiers were removed from images, pathology slides, and blood biomarkers. DICOM metadata were stripped and identifiable regions blurred. Blood biomarker inputs retain only abnormal values and textual descriptions. Models are deployed locally within the hospital intranet; no data leave the secure environment, ensuring compliance with privacy regulations.

Experiment details of tumor detection

We initialized model weights using YOLOv5-s pre-trained parameters to leverage robust feature extraction for transfer learning. To improve generalization, we applied Mosaic and MixUp data augmentations, each with a probability of 0.5, expanding the diversity of clinical imaging samples during training. The model was trained using the standard YOLO multi-anchor loss, covering both bounding box regression and classification. Performance was evaluated every 10 epochs by computing mean Average Precision (mAP) on a validation set.

Training was divided into two phases: a 30-epoch backbone freezing stage (training only detection head with a batch size of 8), followed by a 1000-epoch unfreezing stage (training all parameters, batch size 8). This approach reduced memory usage and stabilized early training. Optimization used stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with an initial learning rate of 1×10−2, momentum 0.937, and weight decay 5×10−4, with cosine annealing scheduling the learning rate down to 1×10−4. Batch size and learning rate were automatically adjusted at phase transitions for stability.

Experiment details of bone tumor classification

All images were normalized and resized according to preprocessing protocols. Model training used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1×10−4 and standard cross-entropy loss for 3-class classification, with updates performed on batches of 4 samples. Text features were tokenized using the BioBERT tokenizer to better handle clinical language.

To objectively assess bone tumor detection performance, we employed several standard metrics, including mean Average Precision (mAP), Intersection over Union (IoU), Precision, Recall, and F1-score. mAP evaluates the overall detection accuracy at different IoU thresholds by measuring the area under the precision–recall curve. Precision reflects the proportion of correctly identified tumor regions among all positive predictions, while Recall denotes the proportion of actual tumors correctly detected. The F1-score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced view of the model’s sensitivity and specificity. These metrics together offer a comprehensive evaluation of both localization and classification capability in clinical bone tumor detection.

Fig. 3(b) shows the mean Average Precision at IoU threshold 0.5 (mAP@0.5) on the validation set across epochs. The mAP@0.5 improved rapidly in the first 100 epochs, then gradually stabilized as training continued. The best mAP@0.5 of 0.6996 was reached at epoch 550, evidencing efficient learning and good generalization capability of the model.

When combining two modalities, the model’s performance increases notably. These results demonstrate that multimodal fusion significantly enhances bone tumor classification accuracy by leveraging the complementary information from imaging and biomarkers.

Discussion

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technology has brought new developments to the field of medical imaging diagnosis. Core algorithms including image segmentation, lesion detection, disease classification, and image enhancement have been successfully applied in medical imaging [27], [28], [29]. These technological progressions have created novel opportunities for building computer-aided medical imaging diagnostic systems.

Numerous studies have shown that combining medical imaging with deep learning to construct relevant auxiliary diagnostic models can assist radiologists in making diagnoses. For example, Gu et al. [30] used deep learning models to detect intracranial aneurysms, and the findings revealed that this deep learning model is capable of accurately detecting intracranial aneurysms, enhancing clinicians’ sensitivity and reducing reading time. Cheng et al. [31] employed deep convolutional neural networks to detect and localize hip fractures on pelvis X-rays, with results indicating that the algorithm achieved an accuracy of 91% for identifying hip fractures, a sensitivity of 98%, a false negative rate of 2%, and an AUC of 0.98. The visualization algorithm demonstrated an accuracy of 95.9% for lesion identification. Therefore, deep convolutional neural networks not only detected hip fractures on pelvis X-rays with a low false negative rate but also exhibited high accuracy in localizing fracture lesions. Furthermore, existing studies have reported that deep learning-based AI models can improve the accuracy of diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction in bone tumor identification, classification, segmentation, and visual interpretation [32].

Our study demonstrates that a carefully designed multimodal fusion strategy — integrating clinical imaging, pathology, and blood biomarkers — can substantially surpass standard unimodal deep learning models in the challenging context of bone tumor detection and classification. Ablation studies further highlight the indispensable contribution of each modality, as omitting any input source leads to significant declines in precision or overall discriminative performance. The adoption of a two-stage detection-classification pipeline not only ensures more robust lesion localization but also enables more accurate and reliable tumor grading by harnessing the complementary advantages of each data type.

Compared to classical models that depend solely on imaging or handcrafted features, our approach addresses both the lack of generalizability and interpretability often encountered in clinical AI applications. The integration of large language models to convert biochemical test results into semantically meaningful text representations is particularly powerful, enabling cross-domain knowledge transfer and enhancing the model’s ability to handle complex, heterogeneous data.

Despite promising results, several limitations remain. The dataset, though diverse, is still relatively small by deep learning standards and specific to a single institution. Multicenter validation and augmentation with larger cohorts are imperative for stronger clinical generalizability. Additionally, while our interpretability is improved compared to black-box models, further work is needed to explain the internal decision process, especially in borderline or misclassified cases. Finally, adaptation to other bone tumor sites and integration into real-world workflow require continued multidisciplinary collaboration and system usability studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this work presents a comprehensive, interpretable, and accurate AI-assisted bone tumor diagnosis framework that robustly combines imaging, pathology, and biochemical laboratory information via state-of-the-art deep learning and language processing models. Our approach realizes substantial performance improvements over unimodal and classical methods, setting a new benchmark for automated bone tumor detection and grading. Future research will expand data sources, refine fusion strategies, and further bridge the gap towards clinical deployment for intelligent, individualized musculoskeletal tumor care.
