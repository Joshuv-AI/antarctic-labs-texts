Area Deprivation Index as an Additive Marker of Postdischarge Care Vulnerability in Pediatric Acute Hematogenous Osteomyelitis
Authors: Connor C Park, Keith D Brendes, Nicholas B Williams, Jens Verhey, Cameron Morris, Jessica D Burns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) in children requires prolonged antibiotic therapy and structured follow-up. However, successful treatment depends not only on medical management but also on consistent adherence to antibiotics and outpatient care. Socioeconomic disadvantage may influence a family's ability to complete treatment and attend follow-up appointments. The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) is a validated measure of neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage and may help identify disparities in treatment continuity. This study sought to determine whether higher ADI scores were associated with (1) documented antibiotic adherence, (2) increased loss to follow-up, and (3) differences in clinical severity or treatment burden among pediatric patients with AHO.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on pediatric patients (≤18 years) with AHO treated at Phoenix Children's Hospital from January 2018 through January 2025. Clinical, demographic, and socioeconomic data, including state and national ADI scores based on patient postal codes, were extracted from the electronic medical record. Outcomes included antibiotic completion, follow-up adherence, length of stay, surgical interventions, and complications. Associations were evaluated using ADI quartiles, continuous analyses, and multivariable logistic regression adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and access-related factors.ResultsHigher ADI levels demonstrated significant unadjusted gradients in antibiotic completion and loss to follow-up. Documented antibiotic completion decreased from 90.9% in the least-deprived quartile to 57.9% in the most-deprived, and loss to follow-up increased from 5.9% to 39.5% across the same gradient. In multivariable models adjusting for race/ethnicity, insurance status, geographic access, and clinical factors, these associations were attenuated and were not statistically significant, although effect estimates remained directionally consistent. ADI was not associated with inpatient disease severity, operative burden, readmission, or documented long-term complications.ConclusionsNeighborhood deprivation is associated with meaningful unadjusted disparities in postdischarge care continuity among children with AHO. After adjustment, these relationships appear to reflect overlapping structural factors rather than an independent effect. Introduction

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) remains a significant cause of morbidity in the pediatric population, often necessitating prolonged hospitalization, surgical intervention, and extended courses of antibiotic therapy [1,2]. Children are particularly susceptible due to the unique vascular anatomy of the growing metaphysis, where sluggish blood flow and nutrient-rich capillary networks facilitate bacterial seeding [3]. Infection most commonly involves the long bones of the lower extremity, including the tibia and femur, as well as the pelvis, though upper-extremity involvement also occurs [[1], [2], [3]]. Clinically, patients typically present with fever, localized pain, and limited use of the affected limb, prompting urgent evaluation and management.

Accurate pathogen identification is central to effective treatment. Blood cultures should be obtained prior to antibiotic administration whenever feasible, and definitive diagnosis may require cultures from bone, tissue, or abscess collections obtained during surgical intervention [4]. Staphylococcus aureus remains the most frequently identified organism, with an increasing prevalence of methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA) associated with more severe disease and higher rates of complications [3,5,6]. In response, many institutions have implemented standardized clinical care pathways to optimize diagnostic evaluation, streamline antimicrobial management, and reduce variability in care. These protocols have been associated with improved adherence to best practices, such as higher rates of preantibiotic blood culture collection, and reductions in hospital length of stay and readmission rates [7,8].

In addition to these advances, optimal outcomes in AHO extend beyond inpatient management. Successful treatment requires strict adherence to prolonged antibiotic regimens and reliable outpatient follow-up to monitor clinical response and detect complications. Children typically require several weeks of antimicrobial therapy, and premature discontinuation of antibiotics or failure to attend follow-up visits may result in persistent infection, recurrent osteomyelitis, growth disturbance, pathologic fracture, chronic osteomyelitis, or the need for additional surgical intervention [[1], [2], [3],5]. Continued outpatient follow-up also allows clinicians to monitor treatment response, identify medication-related adverse effects, and recognize complications that may not become apparent until weeks or months after the initial infection. Socioeconomic factors may substantially influence a family's ability to complete therapy and engage in necessary longitudinal care. Prior research has demonstrated that race, insurance status, and broader social determinants of health can affect healthcare access and continuity in pediatric populations [[9], [10], [11]]. The Area Deprivation Index (ADI), a validated measure of neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage, has emerged as a valuable tool for quantifying community-level risk and identifying disparities in pediatric outcomes [12]. However, despite growing recognition of the impact of neighborhood disadvantage on pediatric health, little is known about whether ADI is associated with antibiotic completion, outpatient follow-up, or disease burden in children treated for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis.

Understanding whether socioeconomic deprivation contributes to differences in treatment adherence, follow-up completion, or complication rates in pediatric AHO is critical for improving equity in care delivery. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between ADI and key clinical outcomes in children with AHO. We hypothesized that greater neighborhood deprivation would be associated with lower rates of documented antibiotic completion and outpatient follow-up, as well as greater disease burden, including longer hospital stays, increased readmissions, more surgical procedures, and higher rates of long-term complications. By identifying these associations, this study seeks to highlight systemic gaps in care and inform targeted interventions, such as enhanced discharge planning, coordinated outpatient support, or telehealth-based follow-up, to improve outcomes among vulnerable pediatric populations.

Materials and methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Phoenix Children's Hospital, a tertiary pediatric referral center, and included patients treated between January 2018 and January 2025. Patients were identified through institutional databases using diagnostic codes corresponding to acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO). Medical records were reviewed to collect clinical, laboratory, imaging, and treatment data. Inclusion required confirmation of AHO based on clinical presentation in conjunction with imaging findings and/or positive culture results. Patients with concomitant septic arthritis and multifocal AHO were included if they otherwise met the inclusion criteria. Cases were not excluded based on the presence of adjacent joint infection or involvement of multiple anatomic sites. Patients older than 18 years, those with incomplete medical records, those with nonhematogenous osteomyelitis, or cases in which care was initiated at an outside facility were excluded.

Data collection

Demographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type (private vs public), and distance from the hospital. Race/ethnicity was documented in our electronic medical records as a single combined category. Socioeconomic status was assessed using both state and national Area Deprivation Index (ADI) scores derived from patient postal codes. ADI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and stratified into quartiles.

Clinical variables included infection site, microbiologic data, hospital length of stay, number of surgical procedures, use of incision and drainage, central venous access placement, antibiotic bead placement, duration of fever, and total prescribed antibiotic duration. Readmissions and long-term complications were also recorded.

All data were collected from the electronic medical record, including clinical notes, laboratory results, imaging reports, operative reports, and discharge summaries. Diagnostic and treatment practices followed institutional standards. During the study period, patients were managed according to institutional pediatric AHO clinical pathways, which included standardized recommendations for diagnostic evaluation, antimicrobial therapy, and outpatient follow-up.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were antibiotic completion and loss to follow-up. Prescribed antibiotic duration was confirmed using discharge orders. Patients were routinely discharged with prescriptions covering the planned outpatient antibiotic course; scheduled follow-up visits were intended to assess clinical response and treatment completion rather than provide additional antibiotic prescriptions. Antibiotic completion was defined as documentation in outpatient clinic notes confirming completion of therapy, with a visit occurring on or after the final prescribed treatment date; absence of such documentation was categorized as an unknown completion status. Loss to follow-up was defined as failure to attend any outpatient visit after hospital discharge. Secondary variables describing healthcare utilization and clinical course included hospital length of stay, readmission, number of surgical procedures, and long-term complications. Long-term complications were defined as persistent or recurrent infection, growth disturbance, pathologic fracture, chronic osteomyelitis, or additional unplanned procedures identified during follow-up. Only patients with at least 30 days of potential follow-up after discharge were included in long-term complication analyses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages.

Associations between ADI and outcomes (antibiotic completion, loss to follow-up, and clinical severity measures) were evaluated by comparing ADI quartiles and continuous ADI scores. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test or analysis of variance for normally distributed variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal–Wallis tests for nonparametric variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

To account for potential confounding, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between ADI and the primary outcomes of antibiotic completion and loss to follow-up. Separate models were developed for state and national ADI analyzed as continuous variables. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and variables that differed across ADI, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance status, distance from the hospital, and hospital length of stay. Race/ethnicity was included as a categorical variable using dummy coding. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were conducted using RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA).

The cohort was 56.0% White and 29.1% Hispanic; 53.7% had private insurance. The femur (32.9%) and tibia (15.7%) were the most common infection sites. Among patients with confirmed cultures (n = 96), methicillin-susceptible S aureus (MSSA) was the most common pathogen (58.6%), followed by methicillin-resistant S aureus (MRSA) (20.8%).

Primary outcomes

Higher neighborhood deprivation was significantly associated with lower rates of documented antibiotic completion and greater loss to follow-up.

Patients in the highest state ADI quartile had substantially lower documented completion rates than those in the lowest quartile (57.9% vs 90.9%, P = .02, Table 3). A similar gradient was observed using national ADI (50.0% vs 88.6%, P = .017, Table 3). When analyzed continuously, patients without documented completion had significantly higher median state and national ADI scores (both P = .007, Table 4). Completion did not differ significantly by age, insurance type, or race/ethnicity, although Native American patients demonstrated the lowest completion rate; this did not reach statistical significance.

Higher ADI was not independently associated with antibiotic completion after adjustment. Increasing state ADI was associated with lower odds of completion (aOR, 0.91, 95% CI, 0.75-1.09, P = .294), with similar findings for National ADI (aOR, 0.99, 95% CI, 0.97-1.01, P = .230).

For loss to follow-up, higher ADI remained directionally associated with increased risk after adjustment. State ADI demonstrated higher odds of loss to follow-up (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 0.96-1.45, P = .108), and national ADI showed a similar pattern (aOR 1.02, 95% CI 1.00-1.05, P = .067). Although effect estimates were consistent with unadjusted gradients, these associations did not meet our thresholds for statistical significance.

Secondary variables

Neighborhood deprivation was not associated with indicators of disease severity or treatment burden. Hospital length of stay did not differ significantly across ADI quartiles, nor were there differences in readmission rates or long-term complications. Operative management, including incision and drainage, peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) placement, and the total number of procedures, was comparable across ADI strata.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of children treated for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO), neighborhood-level socioeconomic deprivation demonstrated a clear unadjusted gradient in postdischarge care continuity. Children residing in more deprived neighborhoods had lower documented antibiotic completion and substantially higher rates of loss to follow-up. However, multivariable modeling adjusting for race/ethnicity, insurance status, geographic distance, and length of stay attenuated these associations, suggesting that ADI reflects overlapping determinants of care rather than acting as an independent causal driver of outcomes.

AHO remains a clinically significant pediatric infection requiring coordinated medical and surgical care over extended periods. Successful treatment depends not only on standardized inpatient management but also on consistent outpatient follow-up and completion of prolonged antibiotic regimens [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Consistent with prior studies, infections in this cohort predominantly involved the pediatric femur and tibia, with Staphylococcus aureus identified as the most common causative organism [[13], [14], [15]]. In this cohort, 69.4% of patients required operative intervention, which falls within the widely reported range of 26% to 74%, underscoring the variability in surgical rates across institutions [16]. Additionally, 27.6% of patients in this cohort were discharged with a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) for continued antibiotic therapy, a proportion that has likewise shown considerable variability in the literature [17,18].

The ADI is a validated composite measure of neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage increasingly used in pediatric research to capture multidimensional vulnerability [[19], [20], [21]]. By quantifying socioeconomic context, the ADI helps highlight patients who may face barriers to care that are not evident from clinical measures alone. While social determinants of health have been shown to influence access and adherence across pediatric conditions, their role in AHO outcomes has remained underexplored. Our findings demonstrate a gradient in postdischarge treatment adherence and follow-up based on patients’ ADI.

Children residing in the most deprived neighborhoods were significantly less likely to have documented completion of their antibiotic regimens. It is important to note that attenuation of this relationship after logistic regression suggests that factors encompassed by ADI, including insurance coverage, transportation burden, and broader structural inequities, likely play an important role in linking neighborhood deprivation to antibiotic adherence. Accordingly, ADI should be interpreted as a marker of vulnerability rather than an isolated predictor. This aligns with prior research demonstrating that lower neighborhood-level socioeconomic status is associated with reduced medication adherence [22]. The consequences of incomplete antibiotic therapy are well established. Inadequate treatment increases the risk of persistent infection, recurrent osteomyelitis, additional procedures, prolonged antimicrobial therapy, and long-term skeletal complications [[1], [2], [3],5]. Although incomplete antibiotic documentation in our cohort was not significantly associated with higher readmission or complication rates, prior studies have linked noncompletion and short treatment courses to lower treatment success and a potentially increased risk of antimicrobial resistance [23,24]. Survey-based literature suggests that antibiotic noncompliance is strongly influenced by limited health literacy, lower educational attainment, reduced family income, and inadequate understanding of the treatment importance [25,26]. These factors are closely tied to neighborhood-level deprivation, supporting the premise that ADI may serve as a meaningful proxy for barriers to sustained outpatient treatment.

Loss to follow-up demonstrated the most pronounced unadjusted gradient across deprivation levels. Although adjusted models attenuated statistical significance, effect estimates were directionally consistent. Children in the highest ADI quartiles were more likely to fail to return for outpatient evaluation after discharge. This contrasts with prior orthopaedic literature that has not identified significant differences in outpatient engagement when investigating neighborhood deprivation. For example, Cinquegrani et al. reported no differences in outpatient appointment frequency following supracondylar humerus fracture management across ADI strata [27], and Mahindroo et al. similarly did not detect variation in outcome patterns among children treated for musculoskeletal infections [28]. Our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating that in pediatric AHO, a condition requiring longitudinal reassessment, socioeconomic deprivation as defined by ADI is strongly associated with failure to engage in continued outpatient care. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly examine rates of loss to follow-up in children with AHO.

Importantly, ADI was not associated with measures of initial disease severity, surgical intervention, or readmission risk. These findings are consistent with Mahindroo et al., who similarly reported no association between ADI and inpatient severity metrics in pediatric musculoskeletal infections [28]. Other studies regarding separate pediatric diseases, however, have identified associations between higher ADI and increased readmission or adverse outcomes [27,29,30]. In our cohort, the absence of significant differences in long-term complications must be interpreted cautiously. Because children from higher ADI neighborhoods were disproportionately lost to follow-up, complications in this group may be underdetected. Loss to follow-up therefore represents both an adverse outcome and a potential source of outcome misclassification.

Beyond clinical endpoints, our data highlight structural gradients across ADI quartiles. Patients in the highest deprivation groups were more likely to be publicly insured, from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds, and to reside substantially farther from the tertiary referral center. Travel distance increased significantly with deprivation, suggesting that geographic and transportation barriers may compound socioeconomic disadvantage. These findings echo broader healthcare concepts, such as “medication deserts,” in which low-socioeconomic-status neighborhoods face reduced access to pharmacy services and healthcare infrastructure [31]. ADI therefore appears to help describe overlapping domains of vulnerability that include access, resource limitation, and structural inequity.

Taken together, these findings support the use of ADI as a practical risk-stratification tool for identifying children at heightened risk of postdischarge care disruption. Because disparities in this cohort emerged after hospitalization rather than during inpatient treatment, interventions focused on the transition to outpatient care may offer the greatest opportunity for impact. Targeted strategies may include enhanced caregiver education regarding antibiotic completion, proactive scheduling and reminder systems, transportation assistance, pharmacy coordination, and care navigation for families residing in high-ADI neighborhoods. It is important to recognize that ADI should complement, not replace, clinical judgment when identifying patients who may benefit from intensified outpatient support. At our institution, this insight has informed a new initiative aimed at systematically capturing patient- and community-level factors that can hinder successful postdischarge follow-up.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential documentation and selection bias, particularly regarding antibiotic completion and follow-up occurring outside the health system. The single-center setting may limit generalizability. Additionally, some patients may have continued follow-up outside our health system, particularly those residing farther from our tertiary referral center, resulting in incomplete capture of outpatient visits, antibiotic completion, or late complications. Furthermore, although long-term complications were assessed, variable follow-up duration may have limited detection of delayed sequelae such as growth disturbance, avascular necrosis, chronic osteomyelitis, or pathologic fracture. Subgroup analyses by race and ethnicity were underpowered due to small sample sizes. Additionally, neighborhood-level ADI may not fully reflect individual household circumstances. Multivariable modeling demonstrated attenuation of statistical significance, which may reflect both overlapping structural factors and limited statistical power. Nevertheless, consistent directional trends across analytic approaches and sensitivity analyses support the clinical relevance of neighborhood deprivation in shaping post-discharge care continuity.

Conclusion

In this cohort of pediatric patients with AHO, neighborhood deprivation demonstrated clear unadjusted gradients in documented antibiotic completion and loss to follow-up, highlighting disparities in postdischarge care continuity. After adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and access-related factors, these associations were attenuated but remained directionally consistent, suggesting that ADI reflects overlapping structural vulnerability rather than serving as an independent predictor of outcomes. These findings support interpreting ADI as an additive contextual tool that can help identify patients at increased risk for disruption in longitudinal care. When integrated with clinical judgment, ADI may guide targeted interventions such as patient education, care coordination, telehealth outreach, and transportation support. Future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate intervention strategies informed by neighborhood-level risk and to further clarify the pathways linking socioeconomic context to treatment continuity in pediatric musculoskeletal infections.
