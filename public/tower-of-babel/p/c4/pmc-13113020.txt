Operating room performance metrics of anesthesia and surgery duration: a descriptive analysis of emergency surgical cases
Authors: Haiyan Yu, Yujie Yuan, Ying Zhang, Xiaolong Zuo, Ronggui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient operating room (OR) time management is critical for healthcare quality, yet the precise relationships among key OR metrics remain underexplored. This study analyzed data from 3,423 patients treated in a Medical Center’s operating rooms between 2020 and 2022. Key time efficiency metrics of operating rooms included the surgery-to-anesthesia duration ratio and the operation preparation-to-surgery duration ratio. Analysis revealed a weak correlation (r = 0.10) between two performance metrics: the discrepancy between surgery and anesthesia duration, and the discrepancy between surgical preparation and actual surgery time. Significant differences were observed across anesthesia duration, surgery duration, and preparation duration when comparing cesarean sections to the overall sample. For cesarean sections specifically, the surgery-to-anesthesia duration ratio was significantly lower under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia (0.60) than under epidural anesthesia alone (0.65). Additionally, when comparing surgical levels II and III for cesarean sections by anesthesia method, significant differences emerged in both anesthesia and surgery durations. Operating room management should leverage these insights to optimize anesthesia protocols and surgical workflows, potentially reducing costly delays and improving patient throughput without compromising care quality.

Background

The operating room (OR) is a pivotal center in hospital care [1], facilitating surgeries under anesthesia. Estimates suggest that the cost of operating room utilization per minute exceeds 36 US dollars [2], with surgical care accounting for nearly one-third of all healthcare spending in the United States [3]. These figures underscore the imperative for strategic planning to maximize OR efficiency. Efficient OR time management profoundly impacts patient outcomes, healthcare expenses, and the quality of care [4]. Hospital administrators frequently aim to minimize OR downtime and optimize its use [5]. Effective OR management, especially in assessing its influence on length of stay and surgical duration, is vital for improving medical service quality [6, 7]. However, the precise correlation between these variables, particularly concerning length of stay and surgical duration metrics, remains unclear.

Existing literature consistently highlights the dynamic interaction among anesthesia management, surgical duration [8], and hospital stay length in ORs [9]. Streamlining anesthesia techniques and perioperative processes shows potential for improving efficiency, shortening surgical procedures, and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes. Several performance metrics have been established to evaluate OR efficiency and effectiveness. These metrics encompass metrics such as the number of procedures per OR per month, the total utilization time per OR per month, workload, and others. These indicators assist hospitals in pinpointing areas for enhancement and in formulating strategies to optimize OR management and patient care [10]. However, few studies examined the OR performance with anesthesia and surgery duration. Further research and quality enhancement endeavors are essential to understand these correlations and implement effective strategies for optimizing perioperative care [11]. Thus, this study utilizes data from an Emergency Medical Center’s OR in a metropolitan area, employing statistical analysis to explore the interconnectedness of surgical preparation duration, anesthesia duration, and surgery duration. It is important to note that while time-based efficiency is one component of OR performance, it does not encompass the full scope of ‘quality,’ which includes patient outcomes, safety, appropriateness, and patient-centered care. The metrics proposed in this study are best understood as process measures that correlate with OR time manangement.

Results section presents the findings concerning anesthesia and surgical time in ORs, with a focus on cesarean sections. This includes analyses of OR time management, anesthesia techniques, and surgical procedures. The Discussion and Conclusions section concludes with final remarks and discussion points.

Literature review

The literature review aims to succinctly summarize OR efficiency, factors impacting surgical and anesthesia duration, and the connection between length of stay and anesthesia/surgical duration.

OR efficiency is influenced by diverse factors, including anesthesia and surgical time, patient attributes, and hospital management practices [12]. Understanding the causes of delayed case starts is crucial for addressing OR inefficiencies [13]. A team-based approach has been suggested to enhance orthopedic OR efficiency [3]. Dexter et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis revealed a correlation between surgery duration and length of stay, emphasizing the need to optimize anesthesia management to minimize surgical time and improve OR efficiency [14]. Prolonged anesthesia and surgery duration can increase patient risk [15], extend hospital stays, and raise healthcare costs. In laparoscopic gastric bypass, OR inefficiencies were also noted in terms of time variability, indicating the need for further quality control measures to enhance OR efficiency. Prolonged anesthesia and surgery duration can increase patient risk, extend hospital stays, and raise healthcare costs. In laparoscopic gastric bypass procedures, OR inefficiencies have been evaluated in terms of time variability [16]. However, enhancing OR efficiency remains imperative from a quality control perspective.

Examining factors affecting surgical duration is crucial for optimizing medical resource allocation and surgical practices. These factors include anesthesia type and surgical procedure complexity. For instance, research measured the impact of anesthesia techniques on surgical duration, finding that patients receiving regional anesthesia had shorter surgery duration compared to those under general anesthesia [17]. Anesthesia choice significantly influences procedure efficiency [18]. Surgical scheduling often involves parallel processing, such as prepping patients in a separate area and readying the OR for the next patient, which can enhance surgical induction and overall quality of care in ORs.

Multiple studies have explored the correlation between anesthesia, surgical duration, and patient outcomes. Effective anesthesia management significantly enhances surgical efficiency and reduces patient hospital stays. Research indicates that anesthesia choice, particularly regional techniques, can impact surgery duration, with patients under regional anesthesia experiencing shorter procedures compared to those under general anesthesia [19]. Various modifiable factors, including preoperative optimization, intraoperative anesthesia management, and provider familiarity, contribute to surgical duration and hospital stay variations [17]. Prolonged anesthesia durations, specifically exceeding 3.5 h, correlate with increased postoperative venous thromboembolism risk [20]. Investigations into anesthesia settings and surgery durations reveal time allocations, such as 3 min for intravenous injection initiation, 7 min for pre-oxygenation, 15 min for intubation, and 30 min for anesthesia induction [19]. On average, it takes approximately 49.81 min from entering the OR to starting the operation, with a 50% improvement over 20 years, partly due to equipment preparation for new surgical techniques. However, there is a lack of metrics for quality control regarding the anesthesia-surgical duration relationship in ORs.

The quality of medical services in the OR is often evaluated based on surgical preparation duration, surgery duration, and anesthesia duration. A quality improvement initiative aimed at optimizing anesthesia and surgical workflows was conducted to reduce inefficiencies in the OR [21]. Through process redesign and staff training, they successfully decreased surgery duration and patient length of stay for elective procedures [22]. This study emphasizes the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and continuous improvement efforts in improving OR performance and patient outcomes. While the ratio of post-anesthesia care unit stay time to surgery duration can help assess resource needs [23], it alone is inadequate for measuring OR service quality.

Patient outcomes and hospital efficiency are influenced by both length of stay and duration of surgery [23]. Research indicates that longer surgeries and anesthesia durations correlate with extended hospital stays and increased healthcare expenses [20, 24]. In the Friuli Venezia Giulia region, a meta-analysis revealed that patients undergoing cesarean sections stayed an average of 5.2 days, with anesthesia duration significantly affecting length of stay [25]. Analyzing previous studies, the efficacy of transversus abdominis plane block in Caesarean delivery was assessed, showing reduced morphine consumption and pain scores in the first postoperative day [26]. However, the relationship between length of stay and duration of surgery lacks comprehensive understanding, particularly concerning the quality control of ORs.

Methods

Data source

The data utilized in this study are sourced from a dataset comprising 3,423 patients treated in the ORs of the Chongqing Emergency Medical Center (EMC). This data was collected from October 1, 2020, to September 30, 2022. The EMC fulfills the public health rescue obligations of the Chongqing Municipal and serves as the first aid for the 120 command and dispatch system.

All surgeries within the dataset were classified as emergency procedures. The average age of the patients was 51.49 years. The patient cohort included 1,989 records (58.1%) for male patients and 1,434 records (41.9%) for female patients. Within the surgical grades, there were 1,644 and 961 cases classified as grades III and IV (see Fig. 1), respectively. In terms of incision grades, levels 1 and 2 represented the majority, with 1,215 and 1,374 cases, respectively.

The duration of surgical preparation, anesthesia duration, and surgery duration (as depicted in Fig. 2) collectively provide a temporal perspective on the time management of medical services within ORs. Surgery duration signifies the total time elapsed from the initiation to the completion of a surgical procedure. It encompasses all the stages involved in the surgical process, which can vary based on the complexity of the surgery, the patient’s condition, and the proficiency and efficiency of the surgical team. Surgery duration is a critical metric for assessing the efficiency of surgical care and can significantly impact resource allocation, patient recovery, and overall healthcare costs.

Diagram of surgery-related time nodes for medical services in operating rooms. PD: surgical preparation duration; SD: surgery duration; AD: anesthesia duration

For a given operation, variations in duration occur due to the various methods of anesthetic intervention utilized. Surgical preparation duration refers to the time invested in preparing for a surgical procedure before its begins. This preparation involves activities such as setting up the OR, conducting patient examinations and preparations, conducting team briefings, ensuring the readiness of equipment and instruments, and other tasks that precede the start of the operation. Surgical preparation is a critical component of patient care, as it establishes the foundation for a smooth and efficient surgical process. It is essential that this preparation is comprehensive and executed effectively to minimize delays and to ensure the safety and well-being of the patient.

Anesthesia duration refers to the period during which a patient remains under the influence of anesthesia, beginning with the initiation of anesthetic administration and ending upon the return of consciousness. Anesthesia stop time objectively marks the conclusion of the anesthesia provider’s direct intraoperative responsibility and is clinically indicated by observable events, such as (1) changes in physiological monitor signals, (2) the patient’s response to simple commands or purposeful movement, or (3) the cessation of anesthetic agent delivery. In practice, however, this timestamp is often recorded imprecisely, typically within a five-minute window of the actual event. Anesthesia duration is a pivotal aspect of surgical procedures and can be influenced by multiple factors, including the nature and complexity of the surgery, the thoroughness of surgical preparation, the patient’s health status and age, the specific anesthetic protocols employed, and the anesthesiologist’s proficiency and experience. Anesthesiologists meticulously monitor this duration to safeguard patient well-being and ensure comfort throughout the surgical intervention.

To quantify and compare the time efficiency of medical services within ORs, this study further introduces specific indicators for statistical analysis.

Research methods

In the high-stakes environment of the operating room (OR), efficient resource utilization is critical for both patient outcomes and institutional productivity. While surgical success is paramount, the management of time within the OR has a direct impact on costs, patient flow, and overall healthcare delivery. However, evaluating the time efficiency of OR processes remains a challenge, as traditional metrics often fail to capture the nuanced relationship between the distinct phases of a patient’s perioperative journey. This study addresses this gap by quantifying specific dimensions of OR time efficiency through complementary pairs of metrics (see Table 1) that differentiate between surgical activity, overall anesthetic care, and preparatory work.

Definitions of operating room time-based performance metrics

Let AD denote anesthesia duration (here it also refers to the total length of the patient’s OR stay) and SD denote surgery duration (the length of the surgical procedure). Given the clinical workflow—where anesthesia induction precedes the first incision and emergence follows the final closure—it is a logical constraint that anesthesia duration encompasses the entirety of the surgery duration, resulting in the inequality AD > SD, and consequently AD-SD > 0.

The first metric, the ratio of surgery duration to anesthesia duration, is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{R}}_{\mathrm{sa}}\mathrm{=}\frac{\mathrm{AD}}{\mathrm{SD}}$$\end{document}. This ratio represents the efficiency of OR time utilization by indicating the proportion of total anesthesia time that is actually dedicated to the surgical procedure itself. Because AD > SD, the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{R}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{a}$$\end{document} falls strictly between 0 and 1. A higher ratio, approaching 1, suggests that non-surgical time—such as induction, positioning, cleaning, and emergence—is minimal relative to the surgery time, implying higher process efficiency.

The second metric, the difference between surgery duration and anesthesia duration, is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Tams}\mathrm{=}\mathrm{AD}\mathrm{-}\mathrm{SD}\mathrm{.}$$\end{document} This difference quantifies the absolute amount of non-surgical time the patient spends in the OR environment. It represents the aggregate duration of activities that support but are distinct from the surgery itself, including anesthesia induction and patient preparation, surgical prepping and draping, and awakening and reversal. A smaller difference indicates a faster turnover between the surgical and anesthetic phases for that specific patient, reflecting tighter coordination of perioperative processes. Given that the duration of surgery is typically shorter than the duration of anesthesia, we have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}$$\end{document}>0.

Let PD denote surgical preparation duration—the time required for prepping and draping the patient prior to surgery. In the context of Chinese operating rooms, this duration encompasses all activities from the completion of anesthesia induction to surgical incision. For elective procedures, this includes standard technical setup: patient positioning, surgical site disinfection, sterile draping, and final safety verification. For emergency procedures, this duration reflects expedited protocols using standardized emergency checklists and, where applicable, pre-assembled surgical kits designed to minimize preparation time while maintaining essential safety requirements. In the present case study, the dataset includes both elective and emergency procedures.

The third metric, the ratio of surgical preparation duration to surgery time, is defined as Rps\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{=}\frac{\mathrm{PD}}{\mathrm{SD}}$$\end{document}. This ratio expresses the magnitude of preparation time relative to the time spent on the surgical procedure itself. It provides insight into the proportionality of pre-surgical activities; a lower ratio suggests that preparation is efficiently integrated relative to the overall surgical effort.

The fourth metric, the difference between surgical preparation duration and surgery duration, is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{T}}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{=}\mathrm{SD}\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PD}$$\end{document}. Unlike the ratio, which offers a relative comparison, this metric quantifies the absolute difference between the two durations. A positive value indicates that preparation time exceeds surgery time, while a negative value suggests the reverse, offering a direct measure of the temporal balance between these two critical phases of the operation.

The subsequent section implements these operating room performance metrics using data from the operating room of the Emergency Medical Center.

Results

Anesthesia and surgical time in the ORs

This study employs SPSS 24 for statistical analysis and quantifies the measurements of time efficiency in the ORs. Statistical analysis was conducted on data from 3,423 patients in the operating rooms of EMC (see Fig. 3). Following comprehensive data quality checks, the final sample size was reduced to N = 3,419. Four records were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete timestamp data, which were essential for computing duration metrics. The average surgery duration was 123.35 min, with approximately 80% of the data falling within the range of 0–180 min, predominantly between 60 and 120 min. The surgical preparation duration had the most data points, accounting for about 40% of the total; the average anesthesia duration was 191.05 min, with about 80% of the data within the range of 60–240 min, with the 120-180-minute range having the most data; and the average surgical preparation duration was 50.76 min, with about 80% of the data in the interval of 20–80 min, with the most data in the interval of 20–40 min.

Specifically, the mean of Tams for level III surgeries is 50.42, which is higher than 46.71 for level II surgeries. The standard deviation for level III surgeries is 17.44, which is also higher than 15.33 for level II surgeries.

The ratio of surgical preparation duration to surgery duration for cesarean sections with level III surgeries is smaller than that with level II surgeries in terms of mean (0.64 < 0.66), while the standard deviation is slightly larger (0.32 > 0.26).

Furthermore, both the mean and standard deviation of the difference metrics for cesarean sections with level III surgeries are greater than those with level II surgeries. Specifically, the mean of Tpms is 34.60 for level III surgeries, which is higher than 26.87 for level II surgeries. The standard deviation for level III surgeries is 32.83, which is lower than 23.42 for level II surgeries.

Discussion and conclusions

To investigate operating room (OR) time management and its impact on healthcare quality, this descriptive study quantitatively analyzed data from 3,423 patients in an emergency medical center. Focusing on surgical preparation, anesthesia, and surgery durations, we proposed new efficiency metrics: the ratios of surgery-to-anesthesia duration and surgical preparation-to-surgery duration. Our analysis reveals that surgical preparation duration significantly impacts overall OR service efficiency. Cesarean sections exhibited significant disparities compared to the total sample across all measured intervals, including anesthesia duration, surgery duration, surgical preparation duration, and the difference between preparation and surgery time. For cesarean sections specifically, the surgery-to-anesthesia duration ratio was notably lower for combined spinal-epidural anesthesia (0.60) than for epidural anesthesia alone (0.65). Furthermore, when comparing surgical levels II and III for cesarean sections by anesthesia method, significant differences were found in anesthesia duration, surgery duration, and the gap between surgical preparation and surgery time. Operating room management should leverage these insights to optimize anesthesia protocols and surgical workflows, potentially reducing costly delays and improving patient throughput without compromising care quality.

While this single-center study demonstrates the feasibility of computing time-based operating room performance metrics and reveals patterns worthy of further investigation, its primary limitation is the reliance on data from a single emergency medical center. Consequently, the findings should be considered preliminary and specific to the emergency context studied, with substantial limitations to their generalizability. These findings generate specific hypotheses regarding the relationships among preparation, anesthesia, and surgery durations that warrant confirmatory investigation in broader settings. Validation in elective settings, multi-center cohorts, and diverse healthcare systems is essential before these metrics can be recommended for widespread use in OR management. Further research is warranted to investigate how specific factors, such as patient attributes and hospital management practices, influence anesthesia and surgical durations. To that end, we will conduct multivariable linear regression analyses to identify factors independently associated with our primary outcome metrics. By more deeply investigating the impact mechanisms of these intervention factors on OR time efficiency, future research can provide a broader understanding of how to optimize surgical care.
