Beyond opioid dosage: temporal dynamics of intravenous patient-controlled analgesia use captured by electronic device logs—a post hoc analysis of three randomized controlled trials
Authors: Sooah Cho, Soo-Hyuk Yoon, Ho-Jin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) is widely used for postoperative pain management. However, its temporal usage pattern remains poorly characterized.MethodsThis secondary analysis included 332 patients from three randomized controlled trials investigating postoperative analgesia following robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, and minimally invasive distal gastrectomy (MIDG). Each trial compared two analgesic strategies, yielding six groups. Only the conventional MIDG group received IV-PCA alone without multimodal analgesia. Hourly fentanyl-based IV-PCA consumption and bolus attempts were obtained from electronic device logs. The primary outcome was the first time when 75% of patients reached an hourly IV-PCA consumption of ≤ 20 µg fentanyl/hr, predefined as the low-utilization threshold. The secondary outcomes included the first time when 75% of patients reached a bolus attempt rate ≤ 1/hr, temporal trajectories of PCA use, delivery-to-demand ratio, and correlations between pain scores and cumulative opioid consumption.ResultsThe low-utilization threshold was reached within 6–8 hours by 75% of patients in all groups, except for the conventional MIDG group. Similarly, bolus attempts declined to ≤ 1/hr after 7–9 hours postoperatively, again excluding the opioid-only group. Delivery-to-demand ratio was the lowest immediately after surgery and increased over time, showing higher values with multimodal analgesia. Pain intensity correlated significantly, but only weakly, with PCA consumption (r = 0.16–0.24, P < 0.05 for all comparisons).ConclusionsFentanyl-based IV-PCA use declined rapidly, indicating that its benefit is largely confined to the early postoperative phase and that its duration can be shortened when multimodal analgesia is well established.

INTRODUCTION

Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) is widely used for postoperative pain management because it allows patients to self-administer analgesics on demand; this enables individualized titration tailored to the momentary pain requirement and an improvement in patient satisfaction [1]. However, IV-PCA is generally opioid-based and excessive use often leads to higher cumulative opioid exposure. This increases the risks of adverse effects such as sedation, nausea, vomiting, and respiratory depression [1,2]. With the growing awareness of opioid-related adverse effects in surgical patients [3], minimizing unnecessary opioid exposure has become a key priority in contemporary perioperative care. Consequently, many clinical trials have focused on reducing IV-PCA use, frequently adopting postoperative opioid consumption as the primary endpoint in evaluating opioid-sparing strategies [1,4].

Achieving adequate analgesia while minimizing opioid consumption requires a detailed understanding of IV-PCA usage patterns. Such insights can help balance effective pain relief with reduced opioid exposure, and support the optimization of patient-tailored analgesic regimens. However, evidence in this area remains limited. Prior studies have examined only total IV-PCA consumption rather than characterizing temporal usage patterns [5,6], while others have focused on intraoperative determinants such as surgical approach and anesthetic technique [7]. Consequently, the temporal dynamics of IV-PCA use remain underexplored.

This study aimed to analyze the hour-by-hour usage patterns of fentanyl-based IV-PCA using detailed device-log data from three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted at a single institution. In all three trials, an electronic PCA pump capable of exporting time-stamped records of the bolus attempts and delivered doses was used. By characterizing the temporal usage patterns, the authors’ findings offer valuable insights into the clinical application of IV-PCA, facilitating the delivery of adequate analgesia while minimizing opioid consumption and its associated adverse effects, thereby promoting enhanced postoperative recovery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design and population

This study was a secondary analysis of data from three RCTs [8–10] conducted at Seoul National University Hospital, a tertiary academic medical center in South Korea. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB no. H-2510-046-1681) and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

The study population included patients in the original analyses of the three RCTs. In these trials, postoperative analgesia was administered using an electronic PCA device (Accumate® 1200; Woo Young Medical), which enables the extraction of detailed PCA usage data in Excel format (Supplementary Material 1). Patients without available PCA device logs were excluded from analysis.

2. Studies included in the analysis

This analysis includes three RCTs that provided detailed IV-PCA usage data from patients undergoing robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy (RALP; ClinicalTrials.gov no.: NCT05685342) [8], video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS; ClinicalTrials.gov no.: NCT05366777) [9], and minimally invasive distal gastrectomy (MIDG; ClinicalTrials.gov no.: NCT05649319) [10]. These three studies were among the authors’ recent RCTs in which detailed PCA usage data were captured directly from an electronic PCA device. The first trial enrolled 152 patients undergoing RALP and investigated the timing of an intravenous fixed-dose combination of acetaminophen (1,000 mg) and ibuprofen (300 mg) administered either before incision (preemptive group) or at the end of surgery (preventive group). The second trial included 90 patients who underwent unilateral pulmonary resection via VATS and compared the efficacy of an intravenous fixed-dose combination of acetaminophen (1,000 mg) and ibuprofen (300 mg) administered every 6 hours from the start of surgery through the first 24 hours postoperatively (intervention group) with a placebo (control group). The third trial involved 92 patients who underwent MIDG and compared an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol (ERAS group) with conventional perioperative care (conventional group). Among the six groups from the three RCTs, only the conventional MIDG group received standard postoperative analgesia exclusively with IV-PCA without multimodal analgesia. Fig. 1 summarizes the details of perioperative analgesic management in each trial.

3. Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia regimen

The same IV-PCA regimen was used in all three trials. At the start of fascial closure, all patients received a 50 μg intravenous fentanyl loading dose, after which they were connected to an electronic IV-PCA device (Accumate® 1200) containing fentanyl 20 μg/mL in a total volume of 100 mL. The device was programmed to deliver a 20 μg bolus with a 10-minute lockout interval and no background infusion. Bolus attempts, delivered doses, and cumulative consumption were automatically recorded. IV-PCA was maintained for up to 48 hours postoperatively in the RALP and VATS trials, and for up to 72 hours in the MIDG trial.

4. Study outcomes and data collection

The primary outcome was the first time point when 75% of patients reached an hourly IV-PCA consumption of ≤ 20 µg fentanyl/hr (= 1 mL/hr), predefined as the low-utilization threshold. This threshold was determined based on pharmacokinetic considerations and clinical workflow relevance. Assuming an adult systemic clearance of 30–60 L/hr for fentanyl, a constant infusion rate of 20 µg/hr yields an estimated steady-state plasma concentration of approximately 0.33–0.67 ng/mL, calculated as Css = R/CL (steady-state plasma concentration = rate of administration/clearance). This concentration lies near or below the reported minimum effective analgesic concentration for postoperative pain (approximately 0.6–1.0 ng/mL) [11,12], indicating that continued IV-PCA use at or below this level is unlikely to provide additional clinically meaningful analgesia. Furthermore, an infusion rate of 20 µg fentanyl/hr (= 1 mL/hr) corresponds to the background infusion rate commonly used in clinical practice, underscoring its clinical relevance as a reference threshold. Secondary outcomes included: (1) the first time point when 75% of patients’ hourly number of bolus attempts first decreased to ≤ 1; (2) the percentile-based temporal trajectories (25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) of hourly IV-PCA consumption and bolus attempts within 48 hours postoperatively; and (3) IV-PCA efficiency, defined as the ratio of administered to attempted doses (delivery-to-demand ratio), which represents the adequacy of the PCA system and its effectiveness in meeting patient analgesic demands, as previously described [13]. The delivery-to-demand ratios approaching 1 indicated that most activation attempts resulted in successful drug delivery. Conversely, lower ratios denoted reduced IV-PCA efficiency as a larger proportion of attempts failed to achieve drug delivery, typically because of lockout-restricted intervals or inadequate pain control. Associations were also examined between (1) pain intensity at rest and during coughing at 24 hours postoperatively and cumulative IV-PCA consumption up to 24 hours, and (2) pain intensity at rest and during coughing at 48 hours postoperatively and cumulative IV-PCA consumption up to 48 hours.

The data were obtained by extracting internal records stored in the Accumate® 1200 device. After discontinuation of IV-PCA, the devices were collected, and data automatically recorded by the device were extracted using AccuLinker software (version 1.1; Woo Young Medical), a dedicated program designed for the Accumate® 1200 device [14]. These records included the exact time of each patient-initiated demand, whether the bolus was delivered, cumulative number of attempts, and cumulative dose, all documented with precise dates and timestamps (hours, minutes, and seconds). The device also automatically stored data at 1-hour intervals and provided the elapsed time from the initiation of PCA. Based on the elapsed time, the hourly consumption and number of bolus attempts were calculated.

In addition to detailed IV-PCA usage data, the following variables collected in the original trials were included in this analysis: sex, age, body weight, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification, preoperative Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score, surgery time, intraoperative remifentanil dose, and pain intensity at rest and during coughing at 24 hours and 48 hours postoperatively, as assessed using the numeric rating scale. Preoperative psychological distress was evaluated using items 14 (“feeling worried or anxious”) and 15 (“feeling sad or depressed”) of the QoR-15 questionnaire. Each item was scored on an 11-point numeric scale (0–10), where 0 indicated “always” during the past 24 hours and 10 indicated “not at all.”

5. Statistical analysis

Percentile-based analyses were performed to characterize postoperative temporal trajectories of hourly IV-PCA fentanyl consumption in each cohort, using the 25th, 50th (median), and 75th percentiles. For each percentile curve, the first postoperative time point when the hourly consumption reached ≤ 20 µg fentanyl (= 1 mL/hr), predefined as the low-utilization threshold, was identified and indicated on the graphs. Cumulative IV-PCA consumption was overlaid on the same graphs as hourly dose trajectories. For PCA activation attempts, percentile-based trajectories were similarly analyzed, and the first postoperative time point when the hourly number of bolus attempts reached ≤ 1 per hour was identified and annotated in separate graphs. Additionally, during the revision process, the authors have added corresponding graphs stratified by the presence or absence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within the first 24 hours postoperatively.

To address the limitations of the primary outcome, which did not capture individual patient–level time-to-event information, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve analyses were also performed for event achievement across the surgical procedures and treatment groups as part of the revision. A 3-hour low-utilization period was defined as the earliest time point at which the hourly fentanyl dose remained ≤ 20 μg for at least 3 consecutive hours, and likewise, a 6-hour low-utilization period was the earliest time point at which it remained ≤ 20 μg for at least 6 consecutive hours, according to a prespecified operational definition. If fewer than 3 hours remained before the 48-hour mark, the event was determined solely based on whether the criterion was met during the remaining time. Group comparisons within each surgical category were performed using log-rank tests.

To calculate the PCA delivery efficiency, time-stamped data on successful bolus deliveries and total activation attempts were aggregated at 1-hour intervals for each patient. The mean hourly PCA delivery efficiency was defined as the ratio of successful deliveries to total attempts (%). The mean values across patients were computed at each postoperative hour, excluding time points with zero attempts. Overall temporal trends were visualized using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing with a smoothing span of 0.25, and subgroup analyses were conducted according to surgical procedure and analgesic strategy.

Finally, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between pain intensity at rest and during coughing at 24 hours and cumulative IV-PCA consumption up to 24 hours, as well as between pain intensity at rest and during coughing at 48 hours and cumulative IV-PCA consumption up to 48 hours. These analyses were repeated in two subgroups: (1) patients without PONV during the first 24 hours postoperatively and (2) patients reporting no preoperative anxiety or depression (scores of 10 on items 14 and 15 of the preoperative QoR-15 questionnaire).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Among the 334 patients enrolled in the three RCTs, two from the MIDG study were excluded as the IV-PCA device logs were unavailable. Consequently, 332 patients were included in the final analysis, with 152, 90, and 90 patients in the RALP, VATS, and MIDG cohorts, respectively. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the overall cohort and each trial.

1. Primary outcome

Fig. 2 illustrates the percentile-based temporal trajectories of hourly IV-PCA utilization during the first 48 postoperative hours across the three RCTs. In the RALP trial, the first time point when 75% of patients had an hourly IV-PCA consumption of ≤ 20 µg fentanyl/hr was 6 hours postoperatively in both the preemptive and preventive groups. In the VATS trial, the corresponding time points were 7 hours in the control group and 6 hours in the intervention group. Notably, in the MIDG trial, marked differences were observed according to the analgesic strategy, with 75% of patients in the ERAS group reaching the threshold by 8 hours, compared with 27 hours in the conventional group. Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 illustrate the temporal patterns of IV-PCA use during the first 24 hours postoperatively in patients without PONV (n = 258) and those with PONV (n = 74), respectively.

Fig. 3 shows the KM analyses for the achievement of predefined events across the surgical procedures and treatment groups. No significant differences were observed between the RALP and VATS groups. However, in the MIDG, the ERAS group reached the targets significantly earlier than the conventional group (3-hour low-utilization period: log-rank P = 0.010; 6-hour low-utilization period: log-rank P = 0.004).

2. Secondary outcomes

The hourly PCA activation attempts showed a temporal pattern similar to that of PCA consumption across all surgical cohorts (Fig. 4). In the RALP trial, 75% of patients first reached a frequency of ≤ 1 bolus attempt per hour at 8.0 hours postoperatively in the preemptive group and at 9.0 hours in the preventive group. In the VATS trial, the corresponding time points were 7.0 hours in the intervention group and 9.0 hours in the control group. In the MIDG trial, the ERAS group showed an early reduction, with 75% of patients reaching ≤ 1 attempt per hour at 8.0 hours, whereas the conventional group showed a markedly delayed decline, reaching this threshold only at 29.0 hours.

Fig. 5 illustrates the temporal trend of the mean hourly PCA delivery efficiency, defined as the ratio of successful deliveries to total activation attempts, in the overall cohort and each surgical subgroup. The delivery efficiency was initially low but gradually increased over time, and distinct patterns of PCA use were identified according to the surgical type and multimodal analgesic strategy.

Fig. 6 shows the association between postoperative pain intensity and cumulative IV-PCA consumption. Across the entire cohort, both pain at rest and during coughing demonstrated weak but statistically significant positive correlations with cumulative PCA use at 24 and 48 hours postoperatively (r = 0.16–0.24, all P < 0.05). Supplementary Fig. 3 shows similar results among patients without PONV during the first 24 hours postoperatively with comparable correlation magnitudes. Supplementary Fig. 4 demonstrates consistent findings among patients without preoperative anxiety or depression, defined as scores of 10 on QoR-15 items 14 and 15.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed detailed IV-PCA dosing histories extracted from electronic PCA devices to characterize postoperative PCA use patterns across different surgical procedures. Unlike previous studies that assessed the cumulative PCA consumption over predefined time intervals, this analysis utilized detailed time-stamped records to provide a novel and more granular understanding of IV-PCA use dynamics. The hourly PCA consumption and bolus attempts declined rapidly after surgery in all groups. The delivery-to-demand ratio, indicating PCA efficiency, was the lowest immediately after surgery and gradually increased over time, with higher efficiency observed in the multimodal analgesia groups (ERAS and intervention) than in the control groups. Postoperative pain intensity showed only a weak yet statistically significant correlation with PCA use, suggesting that factors beyond pain intensity influence PCA use behavior.

The rapid decline in the IV-PCA demand during the early postoperative period supports the notion that background infusion is unnecessary beyond this period in routine clinical practice. Although the background infusion is typically set at approximately 1 mL/hr, percentile-based analysis showed that 75% of patients reached an hourly PCA consumption at or below this level within approximately 8 postoperative hours across all groups, except for the conventional MIDG group, in which multimodal analgesia was not applied. This finding indicates that continued infusion beyond this point constitutes unnecessary opioid administration. In opioid-naïve adults, routine background infusion is not recommended as it provides no additional benefit for postoperative analgesia, sleep, or recovery and increases the risk of respiratory complications [15]. While most previous studies have used morphine-based IV-PCA, recent studies have demonstrated that fentanyl-based IV-PCA with background infusion provides no clinical advantage and may even worsen outcomes [13,16,17]. Recent data from Korea indicate an emerging shift away from routine background infusions consistent with these findings. A 2016 nationwide survey across 65 Korean hospitals reported that 95.5% of all evaluated IV-PCA regimens included background infusion [18]. Similarly, a 2018 survey found that 99.5% of surgical ward nurses believed that PCA devices always delivered a continuous background dose [19]. However, a 2022 survey of a different cohort of Korean ward nurses found that only 33.3% agreed with this statement [20]. Although both surveys were conducted among Korean ward nurses, the respondent groups differed, making a direct comparison difficult. Nevertheless, this period coincided with the broader introduction of PCA devices without background infusion in Korea and the observed changes in responses may at least partially reflect this evolving practice. Furthermore, a recent study identified background infusion as a significant risk factor for early discontinuation [21]. Using detailed PCA dosing histories, this study provides direct evidence that maintaining a 1 mL/hr background infusion beyond 8 hours after surgery can lead to redundant opioid administration.

The present findings suggest that the need for IV-PCA decreases during the early postoperative period rather than remaining constant throughout recovery, supporting reconsideration of whether routine IV-PCA continuation beyond the early postoperative period is universally required. The pharmacokinetic estimates showed that the infusion rate approximated or fell below the minimum effective analgesic concentration for postoperative pain, suggesting that continued IV-PCA use beyond this point may provide limited additional analgesic benefits. This rapid decline in opioid requirement likely reflects the combined effects of minimally invasive surgery and multimodal analgesia, both of which reduce the postoperative nociceptive input. Notably, the temporal patterns varied across surgical types and analgesic regimens. The multimodal groups demonstrated consistently earlier transitions to low PCA utilization, whereas the conventional MIDG group, in which structured multimodal analgesia was not implemented, exhibited a markedly delayed transition and prolonged reliance on IV-PCA. These differences underscore the effectiveness of multimodal analgesia in reducing the postoperative opioid requirements. By reducing the overall analgesic demand, multimodal strategies can shorten the duration of IV-PCA for most patients, thereby decreasing avoidable opioid exposure and facilitating the earlier removal of the IV line. However, in settings where multimodal analgesia is not fully implemented or when non-opioid analgesics cannot be used because of contraindications or adverse effects [22], a longer duration of IV-PCA may still be required to ensure adequate analgesia. Given that the required duration of IV-PCA may vary depending on the perioperative context, clinicians should tailor its use to individual clinical situations rather than applying a uniform timeframe to minimize unnecessary opioid exposure.

The analysis of the PCA device logs showed that the delivery-to-demand ratio was initially low during the immediate postoperative period. This early inefficiency is most likely attributable to frequent unsuccessful bolus attempts during the lockout interval, as patients experiencing severe pain repeatedly press the PCA button while analgesic delivery is temporarily disabled. This “lockout-limited” phase represents a transient mismatch between analgesic demand and delivery. As the pain subsided and patients became familiar with the PCA system, redundant attempts decreased, resulting in improved delivery efficiency and more stable analgesic behavior. The delivery efficiency also varied according to the surgical type and analgesic strategy; in both the MIDG and VATS cohorts, the multimodal analgesia groups (ERAS or intervention group) demonstrated higher efficiency than the conventional or control groups. Although the direct evidence linking delivery efficiency to patient satisfaction remains limited, prior research has reported that nearly half of the patients were uncertain whether pressing the PCA button actually delivered the medication, and one-fifth believed that this uncertainty worsened their pain [23]. Another study proposed the delivery-to-demand ratio as an important indicator of PCA system adequacy [13]. Collectively, these findings suggest that multimodal analgesia not only reduces pain intensity, but also enhances the functional efficiency of IV-PCA, potentially improving patients’ confidence and satisfaction with postoperative pain management.

This study identified a weak correlation between pain intensity and opioid consumption, both of which are commonly used outcome measures in postoperative pain research. Similarly, previous studies have shown that opioid consumption correlates only weakly with reported pain intensity [24]. This observation suggests that IV-PCA use does not directly reflect a patient’s true analgesic requirement but instead represents the combined effects of multiple interacting factors [24]. Despite comparable pain experiences, the inter-individual variability in opioid responsiveness arising from both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic heterogeneity can lead to substantial variations in consumption [25]. Psychological factors such as anxiety, emotional distress, and pain catastrophizing may further drive analgesic use independent of nociceptive input. Conversely, opioid-related adverse effects, particularly PONV, can exert a counteracting influence as patients often consciously limit the use of IV-PCA to prevent or alleviate these distressing symptoms, even at the expense of suboptimal pain control [16,26]. This avoidance behavior effectively imposes a functional ceiling on opioid consumption, governed more by side effect tolerance than by analgesic need. However, in the present study, the authors did not observe a significant difference in the use of IV-PCA between patients with and without PONV. This may be because the influence of PONV on PCA behavior was not adequately captured in their study. The intermittent nature of the authors’ PONV data collection precluded clarification of the temporal sequence: whether PONV occurrence suppressed subsequent PCA use or whether higher PCA use contributed to the development of PONV.

Furthermore, even after excluding patients who experienced PONV or exhibited preoperative anxiety or depression, the correlation between postoperative pain intensity and PCA consumption remained weak. This persistence suggests that additional unmeasured determinants such as differences in pain-coping strategies, individual expectations, cognitive appraisal of pain, and behavioral tendencies toward medication use may play a significant role [27]. Overall, opioid consumption via IV-PCA is influenced by multifaceted pharmacological and psychological factors and, similar to pain perception, exhibits substantial inter-individual variability.

This study had some limitations. First, as a single-center analysis, the observed patterns of PCA use may have been influenced by institution-specific surgical and perioperative practices. Moreover, all trials included in this analysis were conducted in a controlled environment. Given the relatively low real-world uptake of multimodal analgesia [28,29], the clinically required duration of IV-PCA may be longer in routine practice than that observed in the present study. Second, considerable heterogeneity existed across the three RCTs with respect to surgical procedures, background analgesic regimens, and overall clinical context. This heterogeneity limits the direct comparability of PCA use patterns across procedures and may affect the generalizability of the findings. Nonetheless, this variation also provides a broader context, as detailed PCA usage data were analyzed across diverse surgical populations treated with multimodal analgesia, most commonly involving widely adopted modalities, such as acetaminophen, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and regional blocks. Although this diversity does not fully resolve the limitations of generalizability, it may partially mitigate them by situating the findings in various clinical contexts. Third, these findings may not be directly generalizable to PCA regimens with opioids other than fentanyl, different lockout intervals, or drug concentrations. Fourth, the effects of rescue analgesics could not be fully evaluated. In a previous study, only the presence or absence of rescue analgesics was recorded without detailed timing information, thereby preventing integration into the time-series analysis. Nevertheless, rescue analgesic use may have influenced PCA utilization behavior. Fifth, the low utilization thresholds that the authors employed have not been examined in prior studies, and their validity as an indicator of clinically meaningful PCA use remains to be established. Therefore, additional studies are required to refine this definition and evaluate its applicability in diverse clinical settings. Sixth, “most patients” were defined as using the 75th percentile threshold; consequently, the remaining 25% of patients were not explicitly represented in this definition. This decision was made because the relatively small sample size in each group rendered higher percentiles more susceptible to the influence of a small number of extreme values. Accordingly, caution is warranted when interpreting the term “most” in the context of these findings. Finally, the postoperative pain intensity was measured at discrete time points rather than continuously, meaning that only snapshot values were captured at each assessment. Consequently, finer temporal fluctuations in pain that may influence PCA use could not be fully considered. Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful exploratory insights. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to analyze early postoperative opioid use using detailed PCA log data. Future investigations incorporating larger cohorts and diverse clinical settings with similarly detailed PCA log records are essential to fully elucidate postoperative opioid consumption patterns.

In conclusion, a detailed analysis of fentanyl-based IV-PCA records showed substantial variation in PCA utilization across postoperative time courses and surgical types, with most patients transitioning to low utilization during the early recovery phase following minimally invasive surgery with multimodal analgesia. These findings suggest that the need for IV-PCA may be confined primarily to the early postoperative period. However, in settings where multimodal strategies are not fully established, a longer PCA duration may still be required to maintain adequate analgesia. Taken together, the present results underscore the importance of tailoring PCA use to individual patients and procedural contexts while simultaneously striving to reduce unnecessary opioid exposure through the broader adoption of effective multimodal analgesic strategies.
