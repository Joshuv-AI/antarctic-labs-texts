Ectopic cervical thymus in a child: A rare neck mass case report
Authors: Wail Alqatta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and ImportanceEctopic cervical thymus is a rare congenital anomaly caused by aberrant thymic migration. It commonly presents in the pediatric population as a neck mass and is often misdiagnosed due to its nonspecific clinical features.Case presentationThis report describes a 12-year-old female presenting with a progressively enlarging, painful mass in the left lower cervical region over four months. Clinical examination revealed a firm, mildly tender mass inferior to the left thyroid lobe and medial to the carotid sheath. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and thyroid function tests, were normal. Neck US showed a hypoechoic lesion with echogenic foci, with starry sky appearance, consistent with thymic tissue. Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck and chest confirmed a well-circumscribed, homogeneously enhancing 3 × 1 cm mass along the thymopharyngeal tract, medial to the carotid sheath, with the anterior mediastinal thymus present and normal. The patient underwent surgical excision via a transcervical approach, preserving the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands. Histopathology confirmed ectopic thymic tissue with corticomedullary differentiation and Hassall's corpuscles. The postoperative course was uneventful. At 16 months postoperatively, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of recurrence on clinical examination or imaging.Clinical discussionEctopic cervical thymus should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric neck masses, particularly those adjacent to the thyroid gland. High-resolution imaging is critical for accurate diagnosis and operative planning. While conservative management may be appropriate in asymptomatic cases, surgical excision is warranted for symptomatic, enlarging, or diagnostically uncertain lesions. Complete resection typically results in excellent outcomes with minimal morbidity.ConclusionAlthough rare, ectopic cervical thymus should be recognized as a potential cause of pediatric neck masses. Surgical excision offers definitive treatment with favorable long-term outcomes and low recurrence risk.

Introduction

The thymus is a primary lymphoid organ essential for T-cell maturation. It originates from the third pharyngeal pouch and descends into the anterior mediastinum, where it enlarges during childhood and gradually involutes at puberty [1,2]. Aberrations in this developmental pathway may result in ectopic cervical thymus (ECT) or other related anomalies [[3], [4], [5]].

The pathophysiology of ECT involves abnormal descent, tissue sequestration, and failure of involution [6]. Thymic tissue along the route from the mandible to the mediastinum may be located anteriorly, deep to the middle third of the sternocleidomastoid, adjacent to the carotid sheath, and occasionally in the retropharyngeal space [7]. Approximately 50 % of cervical thymic masses maintain a connection to the mediastinal thymus, either directly or via a vestigial cord [8].

Cervical thymic lesions can be solid, partially cystic, or predominantly cystic, with cystic forms representing 76–92 % of cases [9]. Cervical thymic cysts are usually soft, 1–15 cm in size, unilocular or multilocular, and most commonly located on the left (60–70 %), followed by the right (20–30 %), and midline or pharyngeal (5–7 %) [10]. They typically present as painless neck swelling, although 10 % of patients may experience stridor, dyspnea, or dysphagia due to tracheal or esophageal compression [11].

While myasthenia gravis occurs in nearly 47 % of patients with mediastinal thymomas [12], ECT shares similar gross and microscopic features but rarely presents with paraneoplastic syndromes. ECT is generally benign, though very rare cases of metastasis or recurrence have been reported [13].

ECT closely resembles normal thymus, allowing diagnosis to be suggested on imaging. Ultrasound typically demonstrates a characteristic “starry sky” pattern, with bright echogenic foci within a hypoechoic background [14]. CT and MRI further assist in detecting additional ectopic thymic tissue, confirming a normal mediastinal thymus, and delineating surrounding neck structures, particularly vascular anatomy. On MRI, thymic tissue is usually homogeneous, isointense on T1-weighted images, and hyperintense on T2-weighted images relative to muscle [14,15]. Surgery remains the preferred approach for both diagnosis and treatment of most ectopic cervical thymic lesions [16]. Recent case series have emphasized the importance of high-resolution imaging in diagnosis and confirmed surgical excision as the standard management in symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain cases [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]].

Several pediatric cases of ectopic cervical thymus have been reported, with patient ages ranging from 1 to 8 years and involving both sexes. Lesions were located on either side of the neck and commonly presented as painless neck or submandibular masses. Ultrasound was employed in all cases, often supplemented by CT or MRI, and surgical excision was performed in every instance. Case presentation

A 12-year-old female presented with a painful left-sided neck mass persisting for four months. The mass was gradually enlarging, with no associated systemic symptoms such as fever, dysphagia, hoarseness, weight loss, or respiratory difficulty. Her growth parameters were appropriate for age, and she had reached all expected developmental milestones. She had no history of recurrent infections, immunodeficiency, or symptoms suggestive of myasthenia gravis.

On clinical examination, a clearly visible and palpable left cervical mass was noted. The mass was firm, non-pulsatile, and mildly tender on palpation, located in the lower left anterior cervical region. There were no overlying skin changes, and no other cervical masses were palpable. No signs of systemic illness or lymphadenopathy were observed.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. Thyroid function tests were also normal.

Differential diagnoses included thyroglossal duct cyst, branchial cleft cyst, lymphadenopathy, and ectopic thyroid. Imaging characteristics and location medial to the carotid sheath favored ectopic thymus. Neck US revealed a well-defined, hypoechoic soft tissue mass measuring approximately 3 × 1 cm, located inferior to the left thyroid lobe and medial to the left carotid sheath, along the thymopharyngeal tract. The lesion exhibited echogenicity and internal texture similar to normal thymic tissue, raising suspicion for an ectopic cervical thymus. No cervical lymphadenopathy was identified.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck and chest revealed a well-circumscribed mass medial to the left carotid sheath and inferior to the thyroid lobe, measuring approximately 3 × 1 cm, with imaging characteristics similar to the anterior mediastinal thymus. Given the imaging findings and the progressive and painful nature of the lesion, the decision was made to proceed with surgical excision. Under general anesthesia, a transverse cervical incision was made. Intraoperatively, the left thyroid lobe was dissected medially from the carotid sheath. The ectopic mass was identified in a paraoesophageal position, medial and adherent to the left carotid sheath, and clearly distinct from the thyroid gland, which was grossly normal (Fig. 5). The mass was completely excised while preserving the left thyroid lobe and adjacent critical structures including the recurrent laryngeal nerve, and the left superior and inferior parathyroid glands (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). The postoperative course was uneventful. Enteral feeding was initiated on the same day of surgery. The patient had a normal voice, indicating intact recurrent laryngeal nerve function. Serum calcium levels were within normal limits, confirming preservation of parathyroid function. The drain was removed after 24 h, and the patient was discharged in stable condition on postoperative day one.

Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of ectopic thymic tissue, revealing a lobulated lesion with thin fibrous septa, composed of lymphoid cells with distinct cortical and medullary areas containing scattered Hassall's corpuscles. No evidence of malignancy or dysplasia was identified in any of the examined sections (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Histopathology of the excised mass showing lobulated thymic tissue with thin fibrous septa, distinct cortical and medullary areas, and scattered Hassall's corpuscles. The patient's parents expressed satisfaction with the timely diagnosis, surgical management, and favorable outcome. They reported reassurance following the uneventful recovery and absence of recurrence during follow-up. At approximately 16 months postoperatively, the patient remained asymptomatic with no clinical or functional concerns. Follow-up neck US demonstrated no evidence of residual or recurrent ectopic thymic tissue.

Discussion

The thymus originates from the ventral wings of the third, and occasionally the fourth, pharyngeal pouches during the sixth week of embryogenesis. By the eighth week, the thymic primordia migrate medially and caudally toward the thyroid gland. After midline fusion, the paired primordia descend into the superior mediastinum, posterior to the sternum [21]. Aberrations in this migration can result in various forms of ectopic thymic tissue, including accessory cervical thymus, cervical thymic cyst, undescended cervical thymus, persistent thymopharyngeal duct, persistent thymic cord, and cervical extension of the mediastinal thymus [3,4].

The human thymus undergoes rapid postnatal growth, reaching its maximal size around puberty. Subsequently, it undergoes gradual involution, characterized by progressive reduction in both volume and cellularity as functional parenchyma is replaced by adipose tissue. This involution process is closely linked to the rise in sex steroid levels during puberty [23]. In infants and children, however, the thymus remains critical for T-cell maturation and immune regulation, playing a pivotal role in the prevention of autoimmune disease [24].

Ectopic thymus is a rare entity that may be found in various locations, including the pleura, lung, neck, and other thoracic sites. When present in the neck, it is referred to as ectopic cervical thymus (ECT) [25]. The typical presentation is a firm, asymptomatic neck mass, although lesion size may fluctuate and occasionally enlarge in response to minor trauma, vaccination, or infection. ECT is uncommon in children younger than two years and is most frequently diagnosed between the ages of 2 and 13 years [21]. Given its variable clinical features and overlapping imaging characteristics, ECT should be carefully considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric neck masses.

The differential diagnosis of pediatric neck masses is broad and includes benign tumors such as dermoid and epidermoid cysts, malignant tumors including lymphoproliferative disorders and sarcomas, thyroglossal duct cysts, branchial cleft cysts, lymphovascular malformations, and laryngocele [26]. Ultrasound (US) is the first-line imaging modality for cervical ectopic thymus, typically demonstrating echogenic flecks within a hypoechoic background, the characteristic “starry sky” appearance. MRI and CT are often employed for further evaluation to delineate anatomical relationships and assess for connection with the mediastinal thymus. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation [27], with the presence of Hassall's corpuscles, as seen in this case, being pathognomonic. Additional histologic features may include inflammatory infiltration, cholesterol granulomas, and lymphoid follicles [28].

Differentiating ectopic cervical thymus (ECT) from other pediatric neck masses remains challenging due to overlapping clinical and radiologic features. Thyroid nodules or ectopic thyroid tissue, for example, may mimic thymic location and echogenicity; however, they can usually be distinguished by characteristic vascular patterns and elastography findings [29]. Branchial cleft cysts are another important consideration. These lesions are typically superficial, anechoic on ultrasound, low attenuation on CT, and demonstrate low T1 and high T2 signal intensity on MRI, without any mediastinal extension. In contrast, cervical thymic cysts are located more deeply, often fusiform in shape and abutting the carotid sheath. On imaging, they appear hypoechoic or heterogeneous on ultrasound, hypodense on CT, and low-to-intermediate T1 with high T2 signal on MRI [30].

Solid ECT usually presents as a well-defined, hypoechoic nodule with multiple punctate echogenic foci, the classic “starry sky” appearance, typically located posterior to the inferior thyroid pole. These lesions show minimal Doppler vascularity and should be compared with the echotexture of the mediastinal thymus to aid diagnosis. Cervical lymph nodes, by contrast, are usually oval or reniform with a hypoechoic cortex, central echogenic hilum, absent internal echogenic flecks, and demonstrate more prominent vascularity [31].

Another key differential is paraganglioma, which appears as a well-circumscribed mass at the carotid bifurcation, causing splaying of the internal and external carotid arteries. On ultrasound it is hypoechoic and markedly hypervascular; on CT it enhances homogeneously; and on MRI it shows the characteristic “salt-and-pepper” appearance, with angiography confirming its hypervascularity [32]. Recognition of these distinguishing features underscores the value of high-resolution US, CT, and MRI in guiding accurate preoperative diagnosis.

Although conservative management of ECT is possible, there is a potential risk of malignant transformation, so thymectomy is generally recommended for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes [33]. In pediatric patients, however, removal of thymic tissue may impair long-term cell-mediated immunity, potentially leading to immunodeficiency. Therefore, confirming the presence of a normally developed mediastinal thymus before excision is essential to minimize postoperative complications [34].

Surgery is indicated for large, symptomatic, or diagnostically uncertain lesions, whereas conservative management may be appropriate for asymptomatic children, particularly if the ectopic thymus represents the sole functional thymic tissue. Ishida et al. reported that up to 50 % of ECT patients lack a mediastinal thymus, highlighting the importance of thorough preoperative assessment [9]. The largest systematic review to date by Chang et al. [27], analyzing 151 pediatric cases, confirmed that preoperative imaging to verify a normal mediastinal thymus is essential to avoid postoperative immunodeficiency. Similarly, recent case reports [20,21] highlight that transcervical excision is both safe and effective, with no recurrence observed at follow-up.

This case highlights the importance of considering ectopic cervical thymus in the differential diagnosis of pediatric lateral neck masses. Accurate preoperative evaluation with high-resolution ultrasound and CT or MRI, including confirmation of a normal mediastinal thymus, is essential to ensure safe excision and preserve immune function. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology, demonstrating lobulated architecture, corticomedullary differentiation, and Hassall's corpuscles. Symptomatic, enlarging, or diagnostically uncertain lesions are best managed with careful surgical excision while preserving adjacent structures such as the thyroid, recurrent laryngeal nerve, and parathyroid glands, resulting in excellent outcomes with minimal risk of recurrence.

Conclusion

Ectopic cervical thymus is a rare but important cause of pediatric neck masses. Preoperative imaging confirming a normal mediastinal thymus, combined with histopathological confirmation, guides safe surgical excision of symptomatic or enlarging lesions. This approach preserves immune function, avoids unnecessary thyroidectomy or misdiagnosis as a malignant neck mass, and achieves excellent long-term outcomes, as demonstrated by our patient's 16-month follow-up with no recurrence or functional deficits.
