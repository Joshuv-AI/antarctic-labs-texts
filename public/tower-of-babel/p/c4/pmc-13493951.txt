DNA-programmed galvanic Ostwald ripening in nanoparticle assemblies
Authors: Xin Luo, Jun Zhu, Mirali Seyed Shariatdoust, Daniel Saliba, Felix J Rizzuto, Reuven Gordon, R Bruce Lennox, Hanadi F Sleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOstwald ripening of nanoparticles, where larger particles grow at the expense of smaller ones, is typically a thermodynamically driven process that is difficult to control. Here, we report contact-dependent, localized galvanic Ostwald ripening, a mechanism that can harness Ostwald ripening of nanoparticles as a programmable tool for nanofabrication. Using DNA origami to precisely control interparticle distances, we show that silver grows homogeneously on gold seeds until nanoparticle electrical contact triggers asymmetric ripening, driven by size-dependent electrochemical potentials. By programming when and where nanoparticles touch, we direct ripening pathways to create designer heterogeneous nanoparticle structures with enhanced plasmonic properties. In this work, we elucidate the origin of heterogeneous silver growth in nanoparticle clusters and establish design principles for engineering asymmetric nanostructures with predetermined morphologies, advancing both fundamental colloidal nanoscience and plasmonic device fabrication.Subject terms: Organizing materials with DNA, Self-assembly, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Ostwald ripening is a thermodynamically driven process where larger particles grow at the expense of smaller ones, thereby changing the size distribution1,2. During liquid-phase synthesis of nanoparticles (NPs), this mechanism plays an important role in the size and morphology evolution through particle dissolution and redeposition3. Ripening has also been reported to occur electrochemically when NPs are immobilized on a conductive surface4–7 or with a redox mediator/catalyst in solution8,9. Ostwald ripening of nanoparticles is also one of the major mechanisms that causes coarsening of metal NP catalysts, leading to their deactivation10–13. While maintaining the performance of NPs often requires the suppression of Ostwald ripening to retain their size and morphology10–13, programming the ripening process may provide a pathway to selectively alter the size and shape of specific NPs in an assembly, resulting in the formation of customized asymmetric NP architectures.

The favorable optical, plasmonic and electronic properties of metallic nanoparticles depend on their size, morphology and spatial arrangement14–16. DNA origami has emerged as a powerful material to organize and manipulate metal NPs with sub-nanometer precision17–22. With DNA as chaperones, user-defined metal NP structures of high complexity and dynamic character have been assembled for various applications in nanophotonics23–25, biosensors26,27 and single-molecule studies28,29. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are predominantly employed to assemble on DNA structures due to their high chemical stability and ease of DNA functionalization23,30–32, but their plasmonic enhancement properties are not as strong as their silver counterparts33–35. As direct silver-DNA conjugation often suffers from surface silver oxidation36,37, silver enhancement of DNA-AuNP assemblies has become a prevalent method to acquire improved plasmonic performance38–41. However, random heterogeneous silver growth often occurs during this process and affects NP uniformity and function, which has been periodically observed in the literature but rarely discussed40–43. It is important to understand this heterogeneity to construct efficient plasmonic structures.

Here, we report a contact-dependent, localized galvanic Ostwald ripening (CLGOR) pathway, which not only elucidates the above-mentioned heterogeneity, but also provides a distinct strategy to program Ostwald ripening of nanoparticles and fabricate asymmetric gold-silver core-shell nanoparticle (Au@AgNP) arrays on a DNA-origami template. At the nanoscale, the prominent surface energy endows metal NPs with size-dependent electrochemical potentials. Templated NPs that are forced into electrical contact can create a same-metal galvanic couple, where the smaller NP with lower potential erodes and redeposits onto the adjacent larger NP in contact (Fig. 1). By tuning Ag+-NP affinity, we can prescribe the initial Au@AgNP sizes and precisely regulate their ripening pathway on the DNA origami. While Ostwald ripening is often viewed as an undesirable process that undermines the uniformity and performance of NPs during storage and application10–13, we demonstrate that our mechanism of CLGOR can lead to controlled dissolution and growth of specific NPs at the same time, enabling the construction of morphology-controlled, heterogeneous metal nanostructures in a one-pot synthesis. We further demonstrate by S/TEM-correlated Raman microscopy that simple heterogeneous Au@AgNP metal nanostructures are promising surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) substrates with a single-structure enhancement factor up to 107. While heterogeneous NP structures can be synthesized through other methods44,45, our work reveals a contact-dependent Ostwald ripening mechanism that transforms a stochastic process into a programmable tool for nanofabrication, offering both fundamental insights into colloidal nanoscience and design principles for engineering asymmetric plasmonic structures.

Results

Distance-dependent asymmetric silver growth

In the absence of a DNA-origami template, the reduction of silver nitrate with ascorbic acid coats DNA-functionalized AuNP seeds (Supplementary Notes 1, 2) with a layer of silver metal, forming homogeneous, spherical gold-silver core-shell NPs (Au@AgNPs) in a halide-free HEPES buffer solution. When the same reaction was undertaken on AuNP seeds hybridized into a linear trimer along a DNA-origami template (Supplementary Note 3), however, we observed two distinct NP size regimes: some AuNP seeds acquired a thick silver shell, while others had barely any silver deposited on their surfaces (Supplementary Figs. 1–3, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Note 4). The amount of silver deposited onto the AuNP seeds was not related to their relative position on the DNA origami (Supplementary Fig. 4), resulting in a mixture of trimer morphologies with large and small Au@AgNPs (Supplementary Figs. 5–6). Similar inhomogeneous silver growth on closely templated NPs has been frequently observed in literature but not explained40–43.

We discovered that this asymmetric NP growth was dependent on interparticle distances on the DNA-origami template. We systematically positioned two AuNP seeds on the DNA origami with controlled interparticle distances of 16, 32 or 48 nm (Fig. 2a–c). Increasing amounts of AgNO3 solution were then added to grow Ag shells on the Au seeds, followed by stabilization with a low-molecular-weight ligand (4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, 32, 35, 38, 41, 44, 47, 50, 53-hexadecaoxa-28, 29-dithiahexapentacontanedioic acid, OEG) and washing with water (see Methods). The products were then imaged by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). In all cases, we observed the formation of asymmetric heterodimers (Fig. 2, magenta circles) past a critical amount of Ag growth. We observed that the farther apart the AuNP seeds were, the greater the quantity of Ag+ was required to form the heterodimers (Figs. 2a, 2.5 µL; 2b, 5 µL; 2c, 20 µL). Excess Ag+ resulted in merging of the heterodimers (Fig. 2, gray circles).

A subtle balance between Ag+ added and AuNP seed distance is thus required to form the heterodimers in high yield (Fig. 2d–f, see Methods for statistical analysis details). For example, in the presence of 2.5 µL 1.5 mM Ag+, the seeds separated by 32 nm and 48 nm yielded homodimers (cyan circles) of two Au@AgNPs with similar sizes (Fig. 2b, c, 2.5 µL, respectively), while the seeds 16 nm apart (Fig. 2a, 2.5 µL) predominantly resulted in heterodimers (magenta circles). As more Ag+ was used to grow on the 16 nm dimer, the percentage of the heterodimer decreased slightly due to the merging of the Au@AgNP silver shells into a fused dimer (gray circles) (Fig. 2d). With 5 µL Ag+ growth, the seeds 32 nm apart (Fig. 2b, 5 µL) started to produce heterodimers (40%, Fig. 2e), while the seeds 48 nm apart (Fig. 2c, 5 µL) still yielded mostly homodimers (93%, Fig. 2f). Only when excess Ag+ (20 µL) was applied did the 48 nm dimer start to produce a notable population of heterodimers (22%, Fig. 2c, 20 µL and 2f). Notably, the size of the smaller Au@AgNP in a heterodimer grown with 5 or 20 µL Ag+ was smaller than that of the Au@AgNPs in a homodimer grown with 2.5 µL Ag+ (Fig. 2b). This indicated that one of the Au@AgNPs in the homodimer was digested during a later stage of silver growth. Results from growing silver on the DNA-origami-templated AuNP dimers with 1.5 and 30 µL Ag+ follow the same trend (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Kinetics of heterogeneous silver growth

We hypothesized that, past a critical moment of NP proximity in a dimer, as the silver shells grew and the surface-to-surface distance decreased, one Au@AgNP continued to grow as its adjacent Au@AgNP decreased in size. Thus, we investigated the progression of this process through sequential quenching experiments (Fig. 3). The deposition of silver onto the AuNP dimers was quenched by adding an excess of OEG ligand to aliquots extracted from the silver growth at different time points. As demonstrated in Fig. 3a, b, homogeneous deposition was observed at the beginning of silver growth. As the Au@AgNPs grew past a certain size (after 25 min), the percentage of heterodimers (with a small portion of fused dimers) increased rapidly. The percentage of heterodimers continued to increase over around one hour, suggesting that homodimers were transforming into heterodimers once a certain NP size (and thus NP proximity) was reached. This is consistent with our observations in Fig. 2, where origami-templated dimers with larger interparticle distances required more Ag+ to generate heterodimers.

UV-vis spectroscopy of the 80 min aliquot before OEG treatment further confirmed that particles were in proximity during this process (Supplementary Fig. 12). Prior to OEG addition, prominent longitudinal plasmonic resonance peaks at longer wavelengths indicated nanometer-scale proximity or a conductive junction46–48 between Au@AgNPs during the growth (Supplementary Fig. 12c). After OEG treatment, which was necessary to prevent aggregation during NP washing for characterization, these coupled absorption peaks disappeared, confirming particle separation. This also explains why particles appear separated in TEM/STEM images: the OEG passivation layer induces electrostatic repulsion that increases interparticle distances after the passivation. Control experiments with Au@AgNP monomer growth showed no such longitudinal coupling peaks, consistent with isolated particle growth (Supplementary Fig. 12d).

We further measured the size of Au@AgNPs in both dimer structures at each time point using computer-aided image analysis and calculated their silver shell volumes (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Note 5). The total silver shell volumes remain comparable between homodimers and heterodimers throughout the reaction (Fig. 3c), indicating that Ag shell digestion and redeposition is highly localized within each dimer structure. Critically, the decreasing percentage of homodimers over time (Fig. 3b) demonstrates their conversion to heterodimers as silver growth progresses. During this transformation, one Au@AgNP actively shrinks: the smaller particles in heterodimers at later time points are actually smaller than the Au@AgNPs in homodimers from earlier stages, confirming NP dissolution rather than merely slower growth (Fig. 3d). These data demonstrate that our spatially fixed Au@AgNPs grow homogeneously until their silver shells come into proximity, after which silver is preferentially transferred from the smaller particle to the larger one.

A contact-dependent galvanic Ostwald ripening mechanism

These observations point towards a distance-dependent, localized ripening process distinct from conventional Ostwald ripening. Conventional Ostwald ripening proceeds through bulk solution and is typically slow under our conditions (low temperature, no agitation)2,49, whereas our mechanism resembles a galvanic process, where electrical contact enables electron and mass transfer between coupled materials of different electrochemical potentials50. It has been reported that the oxidation potential of metal NPs decreases with their size51,52. We confirmed this trend for our core-shell Au@AgNPs, by measuring their oxidation potential as a function of size, using linear sweep voltammetry (Supplementary Fig. 14, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Note 6). While DNA-bound Au@AgNPs grow homogeneously at the beginning of the silver deposition (Fig. 1b), the subtle differences in their size and surface conditions result in divergence in their electrochemical potentials during growth. Surface DNA and origami become embedded within the growing silver shell during deposition, maintaining structural integrity even under denaturing conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15). This embedding preserves the designed architecture while permitting electrical contact between particles. As the silver shells of templated Au@AgNPs grow into nanoscale proximity, thermal fluctuation of the assembly in solution drives transient, reversible electrical contact between the particles (Fig. 1d). At each moment of contact, a local electron flow can run from the slightly smaller particle (anode, lower potential) to the slightly larger one (cathode, higher potential) through the contact junction. The anodic Au@AgNP consequently loses electrons to the cathodic Au@AgNP, with oxidized Ag⁺ ions redepositing onto the larger Au@AgNP (Fig. 1d, inset). Critically, the two particles remain distinct entities throughout this process and do not undergo permanent fusion. This unidirectional silver transfer continues until either the electrical contact is disrupted (e.g., by adding a strong ligand) or the electrochemical potentials equalize (when the smaller particle’s silver shell becomes thin enough to partially expose surface ligands or the gold core).

This phenomenon is analogous to the macroscopic galvanic corrosion process, where the more active metal with lower electrode potential in a galvanic couple corrodes preferentially when two dissimilar metals are in contact. Here at the nanoscale, the preferential erosion can occur between particles of the same material, due to their different electrochemical potentials caused by the prominent size-dependent surface energy of NPs. This mechanism is also in agreement with the well-established mixed potential theory7. Similar to galvanic corrosion, we observed that electrical contact between NPs was critical to this process. We introduced a DNA-functionalized iron oxide nanoparticle (IONP) as an insulator between two AuNP seeds spaced 32 nm apart on the DNA-origami template (Fig. 4a). During growth, silver was observed to deposit only on the AuNP seeds but not on the IONP (Fig. 4b, c). Even when excess Ag+ was used (15 µL), homodimers were almost exclusively formed (Fig. 4d). The addition of a spacer between growing NPs prevents their contact and inhibits the proposed galvanic silver transfer pathway. Moreover, the results also eliminate through-DNA electron transport as a mechanism for this reaction. Similarly, OEG-passivated homodimers remained unchanged over two months without converting to heterodimers, as the OEG layer prevents electrical contact between particles (Supplementary Fig. 15).

We thus define this silver transfer process as CLGOR. The CLGOR model is distinct from conventional Ostwald ripening1,2 and NP coalescence53,54 because the electrical contact between NPs is preconditional and NP fusion is not necessary. Our process is also distinct from electrochemical ripening, which requires a redox catalyst or conductive surface to facilitate ripening4–9, as the DNA-origami template precisely positions the NPs to enable direct electrical contact between them, without relying on a secondary conduit. This electrical contact, which could occur through direct atomic contact46,47, quantum tunneling55,56, or molecular junctions57,58 at nanometer scales, enables galvanic electron transfer between particles. Critically, we observe active dissolution of smaller Au@AgNPs rather than merely differential growth rates, distinguishing CLGOR from diffusion-limited growth phenomena59–61 (Supplementary Note 7). Unbound from the DNA origami, the CLGOR pathway is suppressed due to the use of surfactants and low local NP concentrations, which prevent NPs from coming into contact.

To probe whether contact-driven silver redistribution can also occur outside the DNA-templated context, we performed bulk centrifugation experiments that decouple the ripening from the silver-growth process (Supplementary Fig. 17). Silver shells were first grown homogeneously on AuNP monomer seeds following the silver-growth protocol described. Without any additional Ag⁺ present, these Au@AgNPs were then subjected to high-speed centrifugation (21,000 × g), forcing the particles into direct contact in a dense pellet. After redispersion, UV-vis spectroscopy revealed a blue-shift from 415 to 404 nm with concurrent peak broadening, and STEM imaging confirmed a broader bimodal size distribution containing both smaller and larger particles compared to the control sample. This bimodal redistribution upon forced contact, without any additional Ag⁺, is consistent with the contact-dependent silver transfer we characterize in detail on the DNA origami platform, indicating that this contact-driven behavior is not limited to the DNA-templated system.

Programming ripening pathways using DNA

We further took advantage of the CLGOR pathway to fabricate heterogeneous metal structures with customizable locations of large and small Au@AgNPs. By ensuring that some particles were larger at the time of NP contact, we could transfer silver directionally from selected AuNP seeds to adjacent ones, generating alternating NP sizes in high yield (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 18). To control the size of Au@AgNPs on the DNA origami, we functionalized specific AuNP seeds with poly(cytosine) strands (polyC). Ag+ binds to cytosine with a higher affinity than the other nucleobases62–64, and we hypothesized that its inclusion in our system would promote the formation of larger NPs at distinct locations, as compared to a random-DNA-sequence-functionalized AuNP seed, which would produce smaller core-shell NPs (Supplementary Note 8).

AuNP trimer structures (≈0.032 pmol) are grown with 1.5 µL (e), 2 µL (d, f), or 5 µL (g, h, i) Ag+. HAADF-STEM images of corresponding trimer structures and their relative frequency (%) are shown in colored boxes (homotrimers: cyan; SLS trimers: red; LSL trimers: green). Scale bars are 50 nm. See Supplementary Fig. 18 for Au@AgNP structure distributions and Supplementary Figs. 19–21 for additional images.

Three AuNP seeds were spaced 32 nm apart on the DNA origami. The seeds were functionalized with either strand A (red) or strand B (purple), which are each different mixed DNA sequences, and some were additionally functionalized with polyC. After incubating with Ag+, mixed DNA sequences A and B bound Ag+ to a similar extent (Fig. 5a), while AuNP seeds with polyC sequences bound substantially more Ag+ (Fig. 5b, c). Upon the addition of the reductant, AuNP seeds functionalized with only mixed sequences grew similarly in size (Fig. 5d), leading to uncontrollable silver transfer directions when their silver shells grew to the point of making contact (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 19). In contrast, the AuNP seeds with more Ag+ bound to their DNA strands (i.e., AuNPs with polyC) grew thicker silver shells than the other seeds before NP contact (Fig. 5e, f). When the shells grew into contact, the pre-designed size arrangement of the Au@AgNPs dictated the direction of the CLGOR pathway, transferring silver from the smaller NP to the larger one (Fig. 5h, i, and Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). The heterogeneous structure (small-large-small, SLS, or large-small-large, LSL) is purely directed by the initial concentration of local Ag+ attracted by the DNA strands. The smaller Au@AgNPs are consumed until only a very thin layer of silver shell coats the AuNP seed, yielding uniform structures of either a SLS trimer (Fig. 5h) or an LSL trimer (Fig. 5i). The deterministic outcomes of these polyC-directed experiments further confirm that the direction of silver transfer at the moment of contact is dictated by the relative sizes of the Au@AgNPs, providing direct evidence for the proposed CLGOR mechanism. By combining Ag+ affinity modulation with DNA-origami-controlled interparticle distances, this approach could enable both temporal and spatial programming of CLGOR events to create more complex nanoparticle arrays with defined morphologies and plasmonic properties.

Programmable asymmetric metallic nanostructures are promising candidates for SERS substrates65. We investigated the SERS performance of the Au@AgNP homodimer structures and LSL-heterotrimer structures using 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA) as a Raman reporter, through an S/TEM-correlated single-particle Raman method (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Note 9). While the Raman signals from Au@AgNP monomers were undetectable, homodimer structures (Fig. 6a) and LSL-heterotrimer structures (Fig. 6b) yielded distinct characteristic Raman peaks of 4-MBA (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating a strong Raman enhancement from Au@AgNP cluster hotspots. Experimental SERS enhancement factors (EFs) were calculated to be 3 × 106 for the Au@AgNP homodimer and 1 × 107 for the LSL Au@AgNP heterotrimer (Supplementary Note 10 and Supplementary Fig. 24). To understand the origin of these enhancements, we performed finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations of the electric-field intensity enhancement in both structures (Fig. 6c). The simulations revealed field intensity enhancements of 1.37 × 103, 2.49 × 103, and 2.45 × 103 at the primary hotspots of the homodimer, triangular LSL-heterotrimer and linear LSL-heterotrimer, respectively, corresponding to calculated EFs of 2.49 × 106, 8.49 × 106, and 8.72 × 106 at the 1074 cm−1 Stokes band (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 25). Interestingly, the addition of a small particle to convert a homodimer into a triangular LSL-heterotrimer further enhanced the electric field at the same hotspot position between the two large particles. This enhancement suggests that our heterogeneous constructs enable some cascading field amplification similar to self-similar nanolenses65, where fields enhanced by larger particles are further amplified by the smaller particle. While other methods have reported SERS substrates that can achieve comparable or higher EFs66, our CLGOR approach could offer alternative pathways towards single-molecule SERS substrates as DNA-templated assembly enables precise single-analyte positioning at hotspots29,67, and the dynamic gap engineering through controlled silver growth eliminates the trade-off between gap accessibility and field enhancement68. With further optimization of CLGOR pathways, more complex asymmetric structures such as multiplicative cascade nanolenses could be achieved.

Discussion

The CLGOR mechanism elucidates the phenomenon of random heterogeneous silver growth on templated seeds that has been frequently observed but rarely explained in the literature40–43. When growing silver on DNA-origami-templated AuNP seeds, silver initially deposits homogeneously until the growing shells make dynamic electrical contact. At this critical point, subtle differences in size-dependent electrochemical potentials initiate electron flow from the smaller particle to the larger one through the contact junction, driving dissolution of silver from the smaller NP and its redeposition onto the larger NP. The observation of this phenomenon is enabled by our DNA-origami system: unlike conventional colloidal approaches where surfactants are needed to prevent aggregation but also prevent interparticle contact, DNA origami maintains precise NP positioning while allowing unpassivated, freshly-deposited silver surfaces to reach programmable proximities, creating ideal conditions for studying distance-dependent phenomena that would be challenging to investigate in traditional colloidal systems.

While CLGOR shares the same fundamental thermodynamic driving force as conventional Ostwald ripening processes, i.e., minimizing system surface energy, the mechanisms and pathways are fundamentally different. Conventional Ostwald ripening proceeds through bulk solution diffusion of dissolved species, while CLGOR requires direct particle contact and operates through highly localized mass transfer at the contact junction. Unlike electrochemical ripening that requires external redox catalysts or conductive surfaces, our DNA-origami template precisely positions particles to enable direct electrical contact without relying on secondary conduits. The precise nature of the electrical contact that enables galvanic electron transfer between particles requires further investigation, though it likely involves nanoscale phenomena such as atomic46,47 or molecular57,58 junctions and quantum tunneling55,56 at the particle-particle interface. The CLGOR mechanism is also not limited to silver chemistry; we further demonstrate that contact-driven ripening can be induced in a Au-on-Au system under appropriate redox conditions (Supplementary Figs. 26–28 and Supplementary Note 11), supporting CLGOR as a general consequence of size-dependent electrochemical potentials between metal NPs forced into electrical contact.

Our work demonstrates how understanding fundamental phenomena can lead to useful nanomaterial fabrication methods. By leveraging the CLGOR mechanism with DNA nanotechnology, we harness Ostwald ripening of NPs, which is typically considered a stochastic bulk process, and transform it into a tool for deterministic size modulation in NP assemblies. By controlling when particles touch and their relative sizes at contact, we can direct ripening pathways to create nanostructures with predetermined morphologies. The Au@AgNP structures fabricated in this work demonstrate promising SERS performance, which can be further optimized and engineered towards dynamic single-molecule SERS systems with in-situ hotspot tunability through metal growth. By programming more complex CLGOR pathways with potential templates like reconfigurable DNA origami structures69, cascading networks and other sophisticated asymmetric structures can be synthesized for advanced applications such as autonomous computing materials70 and evolving plasmonic devices.

Methods

Materials

Agarose (electrophoresis grade) and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris, >99.5%) were purchased from Bioshop Canada; glacial acetic acid (ACS reagent grade), sodium chloride (99%), sodium hydroxide (>97.0%) and ammonium hydroxide (concentrated, ACS reagent grade) from ACP Chemicals; gold(III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥99.9% trace metals basis), PEG (n = 7) acid disulfide (4,7,10,13,16,19,22,25,32,35,38,41,44,47,50,53-hexadecaoxa-28,29-dithiahexapentacontanedioic acid; OEG; CAS 873013-93-5, ≥95%), HEPES ( ≥ 99.5%), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP360, average molecular weight ≈ 360,000 g mol−1), magnesium nitrate (99%), silver nitrate (≥99.0%), trisodium citrate (≥99.0%), L-ascorbic acid (≥99%), 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA, 99%) and bis(p-sulfonatophenyl)phenylphosphine dihydrate dipotassium salt (BSPP, 97%) from Sigma-Aldrich; and Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units from Fisher Scientific. Azide-functionalized iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs, 10 nm) were from Ocean NanoTech; carbon-coated and London Finder TEM grids (400 mesh) from Electron Microscopy Sciences; and ruby mica (V1/V2, grade 2) from S&J Trading. For DNA synthesis, universal controlled-pore-glass (CPG) columns (1000 Å, 1 μmol) and deoxyribonucleoside phosphoramidites (BioAutomation), and 5′-DBCO-TEG phosphoramidite (Glen Research) were used. M13mp18 plasmid DNA (Guild BioSciences) and DNA staple strands (Bioneer and Integrated DNA Technologies) were used for DNA origami assembly. All reagents were used as received and Milli-Q water was used for all aqueous solutions. Buffers were: 1× TBE (90 mM Tris, 90 mM boric acid, 1.1 mM EDTA, pH ≈ 8.3); 1× TAMg (40 mM Tris, 12.5 mM Mg(NO3)2, pH 7.8 ± 0.1); 10× HEPES (1 M HEPES, pH 7.4 ± 0.1); 10× HEPES-Mg (1 M HEPES, 125 mM Mg(NO3)2, pH 7.4 ± 0.1); 1× HEPES-Mg, diluted from 10× HEPES-Mg; and 1× HEPES-Na, diluted from 10× HEPES with 0.1 M NaCl.

Instrumentation

Solid-phase DNA synthesis was performed on a BioAutomation MerMade MM6 synthesizer. DNA concentrations were measured on a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Annealing and heated incubations used Bio-Rad T100 and ProFlex 3 × 32-well thermal cyclers. Agarose gel electrophoresis used a Thermo Scientific Owl EasyCast B1 system, and gels were imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP. AuNP assemblies were quantified on a BioTek Synergy HT plate reader. Centrifugation used an Eppendorf 5702 R (nanoparticle workup) and a Sorvall Legend Micro 21 (Amicon filtration). AFM images were acquired on a Bruker Multimode 8 with a Nanoscope V controller. Bright-field TEM, HAADF-STEM imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping used a Thermo Scientific Talos F200X G2 S/TEM. Raman spectra were collected on a Bruker Senterra II microscope with an Olympus MPlan 50×/0.75 NA objective.

DNA synthesis

Oligonucleotides were synthesized by standard automated solid-phase synthesis on a 1 μmol scale using universal CPG supports (1000 Å). Phosphoramidites were activated with 0.25 M 5-(ethylthio)-1H-tetrazole in acetonitrile and coupled to the growing chains; failed couplings were capped with THF/lutidine/acetic anhydride followed by 16% 1-methylimidazole in THF; the phosphite was oxidized with 0.02 M I2 in THF/pyridine/H2O; and the 5′-dimethoxytrityl (DMT) group was removed with 3% dichloroacetic acid in dichloromethane to monitor coupling efficiency. The DMT-protected dithiol phosphoramidite (DTPA) and the 5′-DBCO-TEG phosphoramidite were coupled off-column: each was diluted to 0.1 M in acetonitrile in a glovebox and coupled manually to the supported sequence with an equimolar amount of ethylthiotetrazole (0.1 M in acetonitrile) for 10 min, after which the column was returned to the synthesizer for oxidation, capping and deblocking. After synthesis, strands were deprotected in 28% ammonium hydroxide for 16–18 h at 65 °C, dried under reduced pressure at 60 °C and resuspended in Milli-Q water. DTPA-functionalized strands were used without gel purification, as only full-length strands conjugate to AuNPs through the 5′-DTPA disulfide. DNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Gold nanoparticle seed synthesis

Citrate-stabilized 12 nm AuNP seeds were prepared by citrate reduction. Briefly, 1 mL of 10% (w/v) HAuCl4·3H2O was added to 300 mL Milli-Q water and brought to a boil with vigorous stirring under reflux; 12 mL of 0.1 M trisodium citrate was injected rapidly, turning the solution dark red within seconds, and boiling was continued for 10 min before cooling to room temperature. BSPP (0.2 g) was then added and stirred overnight to improve stability. The AuNPs were precipitated by adding NaCl, collected by centrifugation (1818 × g, 10 min) and redispersed in 0.25 mM BSPP. Monodispersity was improved by electrophoresis on a 3% agarose gel (120 V, 1–3 h), excising the front half of the band and recovering it by gel electroelution. Purified AuNPs were quantified on a plate reader using ε450 = 9.59 × 107 M−1 cm−1.

DNA functionalization of AuNP seeds and IONPs

AuNP seeds were functionalized by heating purified 12 nm AuNPs (20–50 pmol) with 200 equiv. of DTPA-modified strand A or B in 1× HEPES-Na (50–100 μL) to 50 °C for 1 h and cooling to 20 °C over 30 min in a thermal cycler. OEG (40,000 equiv.) was then added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature to passivate the remaining surface. The conjugates were washed five to six times with fresh 1× HEPES-Na in a 100 kDa Amicon Ultra filter (0.5 mL) to remove excess strands and OEG, and requantified by absorbance at 450 nm. For controlled CLGOR experiments, an additional 200 equiv. of poly14C strands were included during the incubation. IONPs were functionalized analogously by heating azide-functionalized IONPs (20 pmol) with 400 equiv. of DBCO-B strands in water containing 1 M NaNO3 (100 μL) at 50 °C for 1 h, cooling to 20 °C over 30 min, and washing six times as above.

DNA origami design and assembly

The single-layer rectangular DNA-origami template (Supplementary Fig. 1) was folded from the M13mp18 scaffold. Selected staples were replaced by 3′-extended versions presenting A′ or B′ capture sequences to position A- or B-functionalized AuNP seeds and IONPs on the back face of the tile, with binding-site distances estimated as 0.34 nm per base pair. Origami was folded in one pot by heating 3 nM M13mp18 scaffold with 45 nM of each staple in 1× HEPES-Mg to 95 °C for 5 min and annealing to 20 °C at −1 °C min−1. Excess staples were removed by five rounds of 100 kDa Amicon filtration (first round 3968 × g, 4 °C, 5 min; subsequent rounds 2348 × g, 4 °C, 6 min, adding 400 μL 1× HEPES-Mg each time). The origami concentration was determined from the absorbance at 260 nm using A260 = εbc (b = 1 cm), with ε = 6700 × ds + 10,000 × ss M−1 cm−1, where ds and ss are the numbers of double- and single-stranded bases. A complete list of the DNA origami sequences is included in the Supplementary Data.

AuNP seed-origami assembly

To assemble AuNP seeds or IONPs onto the DNA-origami template, 5 nM DNA origami was slowly added to a concentrated AuNP-B or IONP-B solution and mixed well with a pipette. After 5 min incubation at room temperature, concentrated AuNP-A was added subsequently to the mixture and mixed with a pipette. The mixture was then slowly annealed overnight (ca. 16 h) from 44 to 10 °C (incubated at 44 °C for 30 min, then cooled down to 10 °C with a rate of 11 min °C−1). In general, 5 equiv. NPs (regarding to the binding site) were used to increase the assembly yield. The excess AuNP seeds and IONPs were removed by an agarose gel-based purification (see Supplementary Fig. 2).

Purification of AuNP/IONP–origami assemblies

Assembled AuNP/IONP–origami structures were separated from excess nanoparticles by agarose gel electrophoresis. A 4% agarose gel in 1× TAMg was cast and overlaid with a 1% agarose layer in 1× TAMg. The crude assembly, mixed with 1/4 volume of glycerol (7:1 v/v in H2O), was run at 80 V for 1.5–2 h at 4 °C. A well was then carved in the 1% layer just ahead of the red AuNP–origami band; the running buffer was refreshed with 1× TAMg, the well was filled with 1× HEPES-Mg containing 2% (w/v) PVP360, and the band was electro-eluted into the well at 60 V for 15–20 min and collected with a pipette. The recovered assembly was quantified by absorbance at 450 nm and used directly for silver growth, with PVP360 also serving as the surfactant.

Silver growth on origami-templated seeds

16 μL 10× HEPES-Mg buffer, 8 μL 10× HEPES buffer and 100 μL H2O were mixed with 1–20 μL (depending on the target Au@AgNP size) 1.5 mM AgNO3/H2O solution in an Eppendorf tube. ≈0.026 pmol (molar quantity of AuNP seeds) purified AuNP-origami assembly in 1× HEPES-Mg buffer containing 2% w/v PVP360 (average molecular weight ≈360,000 g mol−1) (30–50 μL) was then added and mixed with a pipette. The Eppendorf tube was chilled in an ice bath for 5–10 min with a separate tube containing 2 μL 50 mM L-ascorbic acid and 18 μL 1× HEPES-Mg buffer. After the two solutions were chilled, L-ascorbic acid was quickly added to the AuNP-origami containing tube and mixed well with a pipette. For experiments of controlled CLGOR pathways (i.e., with poly14C-functionalized AuNP seeds) the two solutions were chilled in the ice bath for 1 h instead before mixing. The tube was generally kept on ice overnight (ca. 16 h) for the reaction to complete, but we observed no additional silver growth after 2–3 h. After the reaction, 1 μL 0.1 M OEG was added to the tube and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to stabilize the NPs before washing the sample 1–3 times with MilliQ water by centrifugation (15,871 × g). The OEG treatment causes separation of Au@AgNPs that were in contact during silver growth, as confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 11g).

Sequential OEG-quenching kinetic experiment

For the kinetic study, silver was grown on 32 nm-apart AuNP dimers with 20 μL Ag+ using 1.5× the volume of all reaction components (≈300 μL total). After adding the reductant, 50 μL aliquots were withdrawn at 20, 25, 30, 40, 55 and 80 min and quenched into 2 μL of 0.1 M OEG, then washed three times with Milli-Q water by centrifugation before STEM imaging. Silver growth on AuNP seeds without the origami template used identical conditions, with ≈0.026 pmol AuNPs dispersed directly in 1× HEPES-Mg containing 2% (w/v) PVP360.

AFM and TEM/STEM sample preparation

For AFM, 5 μL of sample (5 nM origami) was deposited on freshly cleaved mica for 30 s, washed three times with 50 μL Milli-Q water, dried under a stream of compressed air for 30 s, and held under vacuum for at least 2 h before imaging in ScanAsyst mode (ScanAsyst-Air silicon tip: radius 2 nm, k = 0.4 N m−1, f0 = 70 kHz; Bruker). For TEM/STEM, 5 μL of sample in buffer was deposited on a carbon-coated grid and blotted with filter paper after 1 min; washed crude products dispersed in water were instead applied as three small droplets and air-dried. Grids were held under vacuum for at least 16 h before imaging.

Au@AgNP oxidation potential measurement

A custom electrochemical cell consisting of an ITO/Glass working electrode, a Pt wire counter electrode, and a Ag/AgCl reference electrode (with saturated KCl) was used for linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements. Indium-tin-oxide (ITO)-coated glass (ITO/Glass) (Elmwood Electronics Inc., Toronto, ON, R = 10−15 Ω in−2) was first cleaned in acetone, ethanol and 2-propanol by sonication, and dried under argon stream. 100 µL ( ≈ 0.026 pmol) Au@AgNPs of various sizes (prepared under the same silver growth condition discussed above) were drop-casted onto the ITO/Glass working electrode respectively, confined to a round surface area with a 1 cm diameter. The working electrode was then dried in vacuum oven at room temperature overnight before cell assembly. 0.1 M H2SO4 was used as the electrolyte solution. All LSV spectra were collected in the range of 0.1–0.9 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1 under argon protection.

S/TEM-correlated SERS measurements

After the silver growth of origami-templated AuNP structures, 50 μL 1 mM 4-MBA was added to the Au@AgNP sample instead of OEG. The sample was then washed with MilliQ water before being drop-casted onto a London Finder Grid and dried under vacuum. Raman mapping was first performed after the sample preparation. An area of 20 μm × 20 μm on the sample-loaded grid was selected as the target area consisting of 625 evenly distributed sampling points. The mapping was carried out with a depolarized 633 nm laser (1 μm spot size) working at a power of 0.02 mW, with 3 co-additions (averaged over 3 measurements) and 3 s integration time for each spectrum collection. The characteristic Raman peak of 4-MBA at 1074 cm−1 was integrated (1050–1095 cm−1) to generate a heat map of the Raman signals over the target area. The same area was then characterized under TEM and STEM. A series of low-magnification TEM images were first captured, rotated, translated and overlayed on top of the Raman map to match the Au@AgNP clusters (on the TEM image) to intense Raman signals (on the Raman map). Finally, high-resolution STEM images of individual Au@AgNP structures were captured and correlated to the Raman spectra extracted from the Raman map.

SERS enhancement factor estimation

Enhancement factors (EFs) were estimated by comparing the SERS intensity of 4-MBA with its normal Raman intensity in solution, normalized by the number of probed molecules: EF = (ISERS/NSERS)/(Isolution/Nsolution). ISERS and Isolution are the 4-MBA intensities at 1074 cm−1 from SERS mapping and from a 96 mM 4-MBA solution, respectively, both acquired at 0.02 mW with 3 co-additions and 3 s integration. NSERS was estimated from a saturation coverage of ≈3 molecules nm−2 of 4-MBA on silver (θ = 0.25), accounting for the fact that more than 96% of the enhancement arises from less than 5% of the surface area (the interparticle gaps). Nsolution was obtained from the 4-MBA concentration and the confocal probe volume, modeled as a cylinder with diameter equal to the laser spot size (2r = 1 μm) and height h = 5.8 μm, estimated from the numerical aperture (NA = 0.75) and the refractive index of water (n = 1.33). This gave EFs of ≈3 × 106 for the homodimer and ≈1 × 107 for the LSL heterotrimer.

FDTD electromagnetic-field simulations

Electric-field intensity enhancement of the homodimer and LSL-heterotrimer core-shell structures were studied using the FDTD method. The modeled structures consisted of 12 nm diameter gold core spheres that reached 40 nm total diameter after silver shell growth, with 1 nm separation gaps between adjacent nanoparticles in all assemblies. A 6 nm thick carbon layer with amorphous optical properties was placed beneath the structures, extending to the calculation volume boundaries to simulate the substrate. The calculation volume dimensions were 130 nm × 100 nm × 60 nm, bounded by perfectly matched layers (PML). A total-field scattered-field (TFSF) depolarized electromagnetic source spanning 600–700 nm wavelengths was applied with dimensions of 110 nm × 55 nm × 50 nm. An override mesh volume with the same dimensions as the TFSF source employed a uniform 0.3 nm mesh size for high spatial resolution. The dielectric constants for gold and silver were obtained from the Johnson and Christy database. Electric-field intensity enhancement was recorded using z-normal and y-normal field monitors, both with dimensions of 110 nm × 55 nm, to capture field enhancement relative to the incident electromagnetic wave. Due to the structural symmetries, symmetrical boundary conditions were applied along the x and y axes to reduce computational requirements. The theoretical SERS enhancement factors were calculated from the maximum electric field intensity enhancement values using the relationship EF = Ee2×Es2, where Ee2 and Es2 represent the field-intensity enhancement at excitation wavelength (633 nm) and Stokes wavelength (679 nm), respectively.

CLGOR in the Au-on-Au system

AuNP seeds were assembled on DNA origami at a 16 nm interparticle distance using the assembly protocol above and immobilized on freshly cleaved mica: 5 μL of 5 nM assembly was diluted with 15 μL 1× HEPES-Mg, deposited on mica for 10 min, washed five times with 100 μL 1× HEPES-Mg, and incubated with 50 μL of PVP360-containing 1× HEPES-Mg for 5 min. A growth mixture of 450 μL 1× HEPES-Mg and 15 μL 20% (w/v) PVP360 was prepared; 50 μL was added to the immobilized assembly, followed by 1 μL of 10 mM LiBr (200 μM final Br⁻) and 1–2 μL of 0.12% HAuCl4. After 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking (gold deposition under low-Br⁻ conditions), 5 μL of 1 M LiBr was added to raise the Br⁻ concentration to 100 mM and the sample was incubated for a further 1 h to allow CLGOR to proceed. Finally, 1 μL of 0.1 M OEG was added for 30 min to passivate the structures before washing and AFM imaging. For the low-Br⁻ control, the 1 M LiBr addition was omitted and the sample was held at 200 μM Br⁻ throughout.
