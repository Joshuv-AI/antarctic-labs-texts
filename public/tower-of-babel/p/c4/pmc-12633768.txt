Predictive Utility of Genetic Risk for Myopic Maculopathy Presence and Progression in a Chinese High Myopia Cohort
Authors: Zhixi Li, Ziyi Zhao, Kai Zhang, Feng Jiang, Liqiong Xie, Mingguang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the predictive utility of genetic risk for predicting the presence and longitudinal progression of myopic maculopathy among Chinese pediatric and adult high myopia cohorts.MethodsHighly myopic participants (n = 623, spherical equivalence [SE] <–6.00 diopters [D]) were recruited from 2011 to 2012 and followed up with 2-year intervals. Multivariate logistic regression models and time-to-event analyses using Cox proportional hazards models were adopted to estimate the presence and progression of myopic maculopathy in cross-sectional analyses and follow-up visits. Conventional factors included age, gender, education level, SE, or axial length. Genetic risk factors included polygenic risk scores (PRSs) derived from genome-wide significant single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with myopia.ResultsThe participants had a mean age of 21.02 ± 11.85 years and an average SE of –9.76 ± 3.25 D. For the prediction of the presence of myopia maculopathy, the PRS had an odds ratio of 2.62 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.43–4.85; P = 0.002) and resulted in an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.589. However, adding PRS to the conventional models did not significantly improve prediction (P = 0.155). Likewise, over a follow-up of 4.94 ± 2.75 years, participants in the uppermost PRS quartile demonstrated a 2.33-fold (95% CI, 1.17-4.84; P = 0.018) elevated risk of myopic maculopathy progression compared to the lowest-risk group. The PRS yielded an AUC of 0.578 for forecasting the progression of myopic maculopathy. Integrating the PRS with conventional models did not enhance the prediction accuracy significantly (P = 0.575).ConclusionsThe PRS alone fails to predict myopic maculopathy among Chinese highly myopic pediatric and adult populations, and the enhancement in prediction performance is quite limited when it is added to conventional predictors.

In recent decades, a remarkable epidemiological shift has emerged as a critical public health challenge, characterized by accelerated refractive error progression toward high myopia.1–6 Progressive myopic severity demonstrates a dose-dependent association with myopic maculopathy, a vision-threatening ocular complication of high myopia, recognized as a principal contributor to global blindness that places substantial socioeconomic burdens on health care systems.2,7

Emerging evidence underscores the substantial impact of genetic susceptibility on myopia and high myopia, where polymorphisms in key loci have been shown to substantially elevate susceptibility to this sight-threatening complication.8–10 Genome-wide association studies (GWASs), when aggregated via meta-analytic approaches, have pinpointed numerous genetic loci that show significant associations with both myopia and refractive error. Myopic maculopathy, recognized as an extreme phenotypic manifestation of myopia and high myopia, demonstrates significant genetic underpinnings. Current genetic studies on this condition or pathologic myopia predominantly rely on diagnostic criteria exclusively defined by refractive error and/or axial length (AL) parameters. Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) have emerged as a critical tool for disease genetic risk assessment, providing a systematic approach to evaluate the aggregated effects of multiple genomic polymorphisms linked to multifactorial disorders. Recent studies from Taiwan, Singapore, and other regions have tried to use the PRS to stratify prospective susceptibility to myopia, high myopia, and myopic maculopathy.7,10–13 However, the predictive utility of these genetic risks for myopic maculopathy in Chinese populations remains underexplored.

Hence, this study seeks to evaluate the performance of the PRS to predict both the presence and progression of myopic maculopathy among Chinese pediatric and adult high myopia populations. To systematically evaluate predictive capacity, we incorporated baseline assessments from initial ophthalmic evaluations and prospective data with follow-up intervals.

Methods

Study Population

This was a prospective longitudinal cohort study conducted at the Zhongshan University Ophthalmology Center between 2011 and 2012 and followed up at 2-year intervals. Our analysis examined data spanning enrollment through 2021. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The human tissue experiments complied with the guidelines of the ARVO Best Practices for Using Human Eye Tissue in Research (November 2021). Institutional review board/ethics committee approval was obtained, and the study protocol was previously described in publications.14,15 The cohort comprised individuals meeting criteria for bilateral high myopia (spherical equivalence [SE] ≤−6.00 diopters [D]) with baseline ages ranging from 7 to 70 years.

Eligibility for baseline assessment in this current study required completion of standardized assessments encompassing AL measurement, cycloplegic refraction, retinal imaging, and GWAS genotyping. Exclusion criteria comprised prior intraocular procedures, targeted myopia interventions, nonmyopic ocular comorbidities, or vision-impairing systemic conditions. Participants missing all scheduled follow-up evaluations were excluded from the longitudinal component.

Examinations

Ocular examinations were conducted under a standardized protocol to ensure consistency and accuracy of measurements. AL was measured prior to cycloplegia via the Lenstar LS-900 optical biometer (Haag-Streit AG, Gartenstadtstrasse, Köniz, Switzerland) and the IOLMaster device and the IOLMaster biometer (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Oberkochen, Germany). All AL assessments were carried out in a dimly lit environment to reduce the influence of ambient light on measurement precision.7,16

Following complete mydriasis, fundus photographs were obtained using a Canon CX-1 fundus camera (Canon, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Ophthalmologists evaluated the presence and severity of myopic maculopathy using the META-PM Study Group classification criteria, which includes five categories: normal fundus (C0), fundus tessellation (C1), diffuse chorioretinal atrophy (C2), patchy chorioretinal atrophy (C3), and macular atrophy (C4). All graders were masked from participants’ PRSs and other genetic information. The intergrader agreement was assessed as previously reported.15 Moreover, pathological features, including lacquer cracks, Fuchs’ spots, and choroidal neovascularization, were recorded as a plus lesion. Myopic maculopathy was defined as diffuse atrophy (C2), more advanced macular changes, or any plus lesions.7,16 Myopic maculopathy progression was defined through the presence of ≥1 predefined criteria: the initial appearance of plus lesions not noted at baseline, advancement to higher-severity categories, a conspicuous enlargement of preexisting atrophic zones, or an increase in the number of previously identified plus lesions. To determine such changes, graders conducted side-by-side comparisons of follow-up versus baseline fundus images, with full awareness of the original grading results.17

PRS

The blood sample collection and genotyping process protocol were described previously.18–20 At baseline, peripheral venous blood specimens were obtained from enrolled participants for DNA isolation. Genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiling was performed utilizing the Axiom Precision Medicine Research Array platform via the Affymetrix GeneTitan System (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Subsequent genotype imputation was conducted using IMPUTE2 software (version 2.3.2), referencing the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, version 5 release panel. A rigorous quality control protocol was applied throughout the genotyping and imputation processes to ensure data reliability.

The PRS was calculated by integrating the aggregated effect estimates (β-coefficients) scaled by the count of risk alleles for each participant within this GWAS data set.8 Briefly, for each individual, the number of alleles (0, 1, or 2) was multiplied by the corresponding SNP–myopia effect size, and the total was calculated. The β-coefficients for SNP–myopia associations were derived from prior genome-wide association studies.8 SNPs absent in the Chinese cohort data set were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis was restricted to data from participants’ right eyes, as all participants had bilateral high myopia, and ocular parameters were highly correlated between eyes. The SE was calculated through incorporating the spherical power with half of the cylindrical power.16 Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables, including age, gender, and educational level. For continuous variables such as SE and AL, differences across groups were assessed using analysis of variance. Multiple models were constructed to estimate the relationship between the PRS and both the presence and progression of myopic maculopathy. Model 1 incorporated age, gender, education level, and SE. Model 2 comprised age, gender, education, and AL. Model 3 incorporated only the PRS. Model 4 combined the PRS with the predictors in model 1. Model 5 combined the PRS with the predictors in model 2. Model 6 included PRS quintiles together with the predictors in model 1. Model 7 included the PRS quintiles together with the predictors in model 2.

In the cross-sectional analyses, logistic regression models were utilized to examine the correlation between risk factors and the presence of myopic maculopathy. For the longitudinal analyses, Cox proportional hazards models were performed to quantify the impact of these factors on disease progression.

Exploratory age-stratified analyses were performed by dividing participants into three age bands (7–18, 19–40, and >40 years) to evaluate whether PRS predictive performance in both the presence and longitudinal progression of myopic maculopathy varied across age groups.

Model predictive accuracy was quantified through area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with goodness-of-fit metrics and comparative performance via Akaike's information criterion (AIC). To determine the incremental predictive value of adding the PRS to traditional risk models, we computed the net reclassification improvement (NRI) indices. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of Participants for the Presence of Myopic Maculopathy in the Cross-sectional Analysis

Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and clinical features of the 623 highly myopic participants included in this current study. The mean age was 21.02 ± 11.85 years, with 356 (57.1%) within the 7- to 18-year age range, followed by 215 (34.5%) between 19 and 40 years and 52 (8.4%) older than 40 years. Within the baseline characteristics, 117 individuals (18.8%) were diagnosed with myopic maculopathy. Females comprised 53.3% of the cohort compared to 46.7% males. Moreover, no statistically significant gender disparity was observed between participants with and without myopic maculopathy (P = 0.094). Significant age-related disparities were detected among participants stratified by the presence or absence of myopic maculopathy (P < 0.001). Similarly, the distribution of educational attainment differed significantly between these two groups (P = 0.018).

Predictive Value of the PRS for the Presence of Myopic Maculopathy

Figure 2 demonstrates the predictive performance of different models. Odds ratios (ORs) of PRS in models 3 to 7 indicate that the PRS is an independent predictor of risk. Model 1, incorporating age, gender, education level, and SE, achieved an AUC of 0.861 with an AIC of 419.384. Model 2, substituting AL for SE, demonstrated a similar performance (AUC = 0.841, AIC = 425.189). In contrast, model 3, which relied solely on the PRS, demonstrated significantly poorer predictive performance (AUC = 0.589, AIC = 596.097), with an OR of 2.62 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.43–4.85; P = 0.002).

(B) AUCs of different models for myopic maculopathy prediction. (C) AIC values of the different models for myopic maculopathy prediction. Model 1 included age, sex, education level, and SE. Model 2 included age, sex, education level, and AL. Model 3 included PRS as a continuous variable. Model 4 included the risk factors from model 1 with PRS as a continuous variable. Model 5 included the risk factors from model 2 with PRS as a continuous variable. Model 6 included the risk factors from model 1 with PRS as a categorical variable. Model 7 included the risk factors from model 2 with PRS as a categorical variable.

When the PRS was added to model 1 and model 2, forming models 4 and 5, only minimal improvements were observed. Specifically, the AUC increased modestly from 0.861 to 0.868 for model 4 and from 0.841 to 0.848 for model 5 (P = 0.155 and P = 0.076, respectively), with corresponding slight reductions in AIC (Fig. 2C, from 419.384 to 416.388 for model 4 and from 425.189 to 422.776 for model 5). Furthermore, the NRI values for the addition of PRS were minimal (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that the incorporation of PRS did not yield a statistically significant improvement in predictive performance. The calibration plots of all models are presented in Supplementary Figure S3. Hosmer–Lemeshow tests showed adequate calibration for models 1 to 6 (all P > 0.05), whereas model 7 exhibited a statistically significant lack of fit (P < 0.05). Specifically, the poor calibration of model 7 was primarily attributed to an overrepresentation of observed events in the lowest predicted-risk decile. This discrepancy suggests that model 7 underestimated the risk for individuals in this group, leading to a higher number of observed events compared to the predicted values (χ² contribution = 12.40, 62.5% of total χ²).

Age-stratified analyses were conducted using three predefined subgroups: 7 to 18 years (n = 356), 19 to 40 years (n = 215), and >40 years (n = 52). In the 7- to 18-year subgroup, model 3 produced an AUC of 0.571. Adding the PRS to model 1 and model 2 changed the AUC from 0.770 to 0.714 (P = 0.164) and from 0.783 to 0.736 (P = 0.193), respectively. In the 19- to 40-year subgroup, model 3 produced an AUC of 0.623. Adding the PRS to model 1 and model 2 changed the AUC from 0.894 to 0.877 (P = 0.495) and from 0.900 to 0.878 (P = 0.463), respectively. In the >40-year subgroup, model 3 produced an AUC of 0.571. Adding the PRS to model 1 and model 2 changed the AUC from 0.878 to 0.918 (P = 0.206) and from 0.874 to 0.908 (P = 0.290), respectively.

Clinical Attributes of Participants With Myopic Maculopathy Progression in the Longitudinal Analysis

Table 2 presents the clinical attributes of individuals with high myopia who have myopic maculopathy progression. This analysis, consisting of 560 participants, took part in a subsequent follow-up of 4.94 ± 2.75 years. Among them, 117 (20.89%) individuals demonstrated progression of myopic maculopathy. Significant differences in age distribution were observed between participants with and without progression (P < 0.001). Notably, while 58.8% of the total cohort were aged 7 to 18 years, only 47.0% of those with progression fell within this age range compared to 61.9% in the nonprogression group. In contrast, participants aged over 40 years constituted 18.0% of the progression group versus only 5.2% of nonprogressors. Gender distribution did not differ significantly between the progression group and the nonprogression group (P = 0.194), nor did educational attainment (P = 0.368). SE and AL also varied significantly between these groups (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, the distribution of PRS quintiles suggested a potential genetic influence on disease development, as a higher proportion of participants in the progression group were observed with higher PRS groups (P < 0.001). Compared to the lowest PRS group, the uppermost group had a 2.33-fold risk (95% CI, 1.17–4.84; P = 0.018) of myopic maculopathy progression.

Predictive Value of PRS for Myopic Maculopathy Progression

Figure 3 depicts the predictive efficacy of different Cox proportional hazards models in forecasting myopic maculopathy progression. Cox model 1, integrating age, sex, educational attainment, and SE, demonstrated an AUC of 0.758 with an AIC of 1136.169. Comparative analysis revealed that Cox model 2, which replaced SE with AL measurements, exhibited moderately reduced predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.735, AIC = 1128.835). Notably, Cox model 3, exclusively incorporating PRS parameters, exhibited markedly diminished predictive capacity (AUC = 0.578, AIC = 1237.947), although it produced a statistically significant hazard ratio (HR) of 1.87 (95% CI, 1.08–3.25; P = 0.025), confirming that the PRS retains independent prognostic value for myopic maculopathy progression.

When the PRS was added to Cox models 1 and 2 to form Cox models 4 and 5, only minimal improvements were observed. Specifically, Cox model 4 exhibited a slight increase in AUC to 0.762 and a marginal change in AIC (1137.855). Cox model 5 maintained an AUC of 0.737 with an AIC of 1130.683. Furthermore, the NRI values of the addition of the PRS were minimal (Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, the HRs for the PRS in these models were not statistically significant (all P > 0.05). These findings indicate that although the PRS independently correlates with myopic maculopathy progression, ‌this association may be attenuated through covariate adjustment‌ (all P > 0.05). Notably, including the PRS in existing prediction frameworks does not significantly improve model accuracy for forecasting myopic maculopathy progression (all P > 0.05). Age-stratified analyses using three predefined bands (7–18, 19–40, and >40 years) yielded similar results: PRSs were nonsignificant within each stratum, and adding the PRS did not significantly improve AUC (all P > 0.05).

We further used Kaplan–Meier analysis of the cumulative hazard for myopic maculopathy progression within a 10-year follow-up period. The curves for the highest 20% and the lowest 20% intersected and the 95% CI for the two groups overlapped considerably (Fig. 3D), indicating that the differences in progression risk by PRS were not statistically significant over the examined time frame. Hosmer–Lemeshow tests showed no significant difference between predicted and observed values across all Cox models (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this current study is the first to comprehensively assess the PRS's ability to predict both the presence and the progression of myopic maculopathy in Chinese pediatric and adult populations with high myopia. We found that higher PRSs were significantly associated with an increased risk of the presence and progression of myopic maculopathy. However, when used alone, the PRS achieved AUCs of 0.589 for distinguishing existing myopic maculopathy and 0.578 for predicting its progression. In addition, when the PRS was added to models that included age, gender, education, SE, or AL, it yielded only a modest improvement in overall predictive performance. The findings provide a pragmatic assessment of the current clinical utility of the PRS in a non-European high myopia population and set empirical boundaries for what genome-wide common-variant scores can realistically contribute to screening strategies today. These considerations motivate us to evaluate the limited incremental discrimination of the current PRS beyond established clinical predictors to improve genetic prediction. However, it is important to note that the PRSs used in our research were derived from European-ancestry GWAS, and we did not incorporate behavioral risk factors into our analysis. These factors may have possible implications for the interpretation of our results.

In Chinese pediatric and adult populations, the discriminative ability of the PRS for categorizing myopic maculopathy risk in high myopia was limited in clinical applicability. In fact, age, sex, educational attainment, and key refractive parameters emerged as the most influential determinants. Notably, an investigation demonstrated that the PRS exhibited no significant association with myopic maculopathy risk across European, African, South Asian, and East Asian populations after adjusting for SE, which was concordant with our observations in this Chinese high myopia cohort.21 Conversely, a Singaporean multiethnic study reported that the PRS achieved an AUC of 0.66, with the combined model integrating PRS, demographic factors (age and sex), educational attainment, and genetic ancestry, attaining an AUC of 0.79.22 However, critical methodological limitations in the latter study, including failure to adjust for the principal confounding variable (SE) and restricted statistical power due to limited sample size (n = 361 adult high myopia cases), likely account for these discrepant outcomes relative to our findings.

Our study corroborates these findings by demonstrating that although the PRS is an independent predictor of myopic maculopathy, its overall contribution to risk prediction is limited when added to conventional clinical parameters. This suggests that while genetic risk assessment may have a role in identifying individuals at increased risk, its current predictive accuracy is insufficient to replace or substantially augment traditional clinical screening methods, such as cycloplegic autorefraction.

The limited utility of incorporating the PRS into predictive models can be attributed to multiple factors: (1) Unresolved genetic etiology of myopic maculopathy: the 161 loci incorporated in the current analysis were selected based on findings derived from an extensive GWAS meta-analysis of refractive error rather than myopia maculopathy due to the lack of relatively large-scale GWAS data for the latter.8 While few studies have reported associations between genetic variants and myopic maculopathy when compared with emmetropic controls, no associations were found when compared to high myopia without myopic maculopathy,23,24 likely due to limited statistical power from small sample sizes and phenotypic complexity. Current PRS-based investigations largely rely on interchangeable definitions of high myopia and myopic maculopathy, primarily defined by refractive error or SE.22,23 Our findings underscore the necessity for dedicated investigations to determine whether genetic distinctions exist between myopic maculopathy and myopia or whether both conditions share common genetic regulators. (2) Ancestry-related genetic heterogeneity: our cohort comprised Chinese highly myopic individuals aged 7 to 70 years, whereas the GWAS-derived SNPs predominantly originated from European populations. This ancestry mismatch likely reduces PRS performance in our Chinese cohort. Empirical differences in genetic architecture between populations, including allele frequency differences, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and heterogeneous effect sizes, can attenuate transferred effect estimates and lower variance explained. It indicated that common genetic variants account for 21.4% of refractive error heritability in European ancestry populations versus 5.3% in Asian ancestry populations, likely due to biases in cross-ancestry heterogeneity of effect sizes.8 This ancestry-specific genetic architecture likely diminishes the predictive utility of SNP data in non-European cohorts. To improve predictive performance for East Asian individuals, future PRS development should incorporate large-scale East Asian GWAS or trans-ancestry meta-analyses and consider ancestry-specific weighting or multiancestry PRS approaches. (3) The saturation of traditional model performance: our traditional model incorporating demographic, clinical, and ocular parameters (e.g., age, sex, education, SE, AL) achieved strong predictive accuracy (with an AUC of 0.735–0.861), indicating that a significant portion of the discriminative information is already captured by routine clinical and ocular measures, resulting in limited scope for the PRS to offer meaningful incremental discrimination within this cohort.

The strengths of our study were as follows: first, enrollment of a sizable Chinese cohort with high myopia spanning pediatric and adult populations; second, an integrative analytical framework combining baseline ophthalmic phenotyping with prospective progression monitoring with long-term follow-up; and last, utilizing extremely large GWAS data sets to compute PRS, followed by assessing its performance in a fully independent validation data set.

Nonetheless, this study had certain limitations. Traditional factors incorporated in the analysis did not encompass variables such as near work or outdoor activities, which may have somewhat diminished the predictive utility of conventional risk factors. At the same time, the PRSs used in this current research were derived from GWASs predominantly conducted in European populations, which may influence their applicability in other ethnic groups. In addition, we only included participants of Chinese ancestry in this study. Therefore, our results cannot be generalized to non-Chinese populations. Moreover, the definition of myopic maculopathy in the current study was based on the META-PM classification, which primarily relied on definitions derived from fundus photography and disregarded changes in optical coherence tomography (OCT) structures. Incorporating related OCT structural changes may enhance predictive accuracy. Moreover, genetic risk scores should be interpreted as complementary rather than as standalone tools. When combined with functional assessments of retinal and visual pathway integrity, PRSs may improve early disease detection.25,26

In conclusion, the higher PRS was associated with an increased risk of myopic maculopathy presence and progression but showed limited predictive performance in Chinese pediatric and adult high myopia cohorts, and its addition to conventional clinical predictors yielded only minimal improvement. By contrast, routinely collected demographic and refractive measures already provide substantial prognostic information, supporting their central role in cost-effective risk stratification for clinical surveillance and management. Importantly, although the current PRS provides only modest incremental value, studies incorporating ancestry-matched GWAS and variants specifically associated with myopic maculopathy rather than refractive error are needed to better understand the genetic role in myopic maculopathy and the integration with environmental exposures, for optimizing individualized surveillance strategies in the future.
