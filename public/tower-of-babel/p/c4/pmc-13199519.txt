Mapping spatial hotspots and location specific associated factor of diarrhea-wasting multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months in East Africa: a geographically weighted regression analysis
Authors: Alemakef Wagnew Melesse, Mahlet Alehegn Tesfa, Tigabu Kidie Tesfie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Concurrent diarrhea and wasting (DW) among children aged 6–24 months represents a critical public health threat in East Africa, exacerbating infection susceptibility, stunting, cognitive impairment, and mortality. Despite national Demographic and Health Surveys documenting high burdens, spatial clustering and geographic determinants of DW remain underexplored, limiting targeted interventions. Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) analyzed recent DHS data from 11 East African countries (n = 1,402 clusters), with Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) providing global benchmarks. DWM prevalence averaged 3% (range: 1–6%) with significant spatial clustering (Moran’s I = 0.145, Z = 38.07, p < 0.001); hotspots spanned Ethiopia, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Tanzania, Malawi, Mozambique, and Madagascar. OLS explained 23.3% of spatial variation (adj. R² = 0.233) but violated key assumptions, while GWR substantially improved fit (adj. R² = 0.37, AICc: 14820 vs. 16001), revealing spatially varying relationships: male sex increased risk in Kenya/Tanzania/Rwanda (coeff: +0.048 max); maternal illiteracy elevated DW in Uganda/Zambia (coeff: +0.098 max); unimproved water sources posed greatest threat in Ethiopia/Zimbabwe. This study reveals prevalence, spatial patterns, and key factors of DW across the region, identifying precise intervention zones across eight hotspot nations. Policymakers should prioritize Ethiopia/Zambia water chlorination, Uganda/Rwanda maternal literacy campaigns via community health workers, and Kenya/Tanzania gender-sensitive caregiver training for optimal DW reduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47166-6.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions without an Index case, increasingly threatens child health worldwide. Particularly through the bidirectional interplay of diarrhea and wasting (DW) in children aged 6–24 months. Globally, undernutrition and infections account for over 45% of under-5 deaths, with diarrhea causing around 440,000 fatalities annually and wasting affecting 42.8 million children. This co-occurrence forms a vicious cycle: diarrhea includes nutrient mal-absorption and dehydration, accelerating wasting1–5.while malnutrition impairs gut immunity, prolonging diarrheal episodes via reduced T-cell function and cytokine production6,7.Beyond mortality, DW limits physical activity, school performance, and mental health, imposing emotional and financial burdens on families through frequent healthcare use and complex management.

Regionally, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the heaviest load, with multimorbidity prevalence among under five ranging 1.2%-24.8%. Nutrition deficiencies attribute 39.5–81% of diarrhea deaths here, often compounded by pathogens like klebsiella and streptococcus8. Pooled diarrhea prevalence in East African under-five reaches 24.6% (95%CI: 22.7–26.6%), with wasting diarrhea co-occurrence at 3–7%.country specific DHS data highlight disparities: Ethiopia (4–6%), Burundi and Uganda (5–7%)4–7.

Transitioning to East Africa, rapid urbanization, poor WASH (water, sanitation, hygiene), and climate shocks exacerbate DW9. Recent meta-analysis confirm improper waste disposal, limited clean water, and low maternal educations drivers10. In Ethiopia (Amhara region focus), diarrhea prevalence is 17.2% with spatial hotspot; multimorbidity patterns mirror Tanzania’s anemia-malaria-fever clusters. Despite interventions like rotavirus vaccination and WASH programs, untargeted approaches persist due to individual research on diarrhea or wasting alone1,11.

A critical knowledge gap remains: limited geospatial analysis of DW clustering and geographic determinate among East African children aged 6-24 months. While isolated studies apply SaTScan in Ethiopia and Nigeria, No region-wide integration of DHS data with geographically weighted regression (GWR) exists for DW multi-morbidity12. This omission hinders hotspot mapping, equitable resource allocation, and integrated interventions aim rising SSA multimorbidity10,13.

This study fills this gap using pooled recent DHS surveys (2016–2023) from East African Countries. Without spatial intelligence, DW hotspots evade targeted control, perpetuating 25–35% preventable child deaths. The aim of this study was to estimate DW prevalence and spatial heterogeneity: Detect significant clusters Via Bernoulli scan statistics; and identifying geographic risk factors via GWR and propose recommendations for high burden zones.

Method

Study setting, and period

This Study utilized the most recent nationally representative Demographic health Survey (DHS) datasets from 11 East African countries, Burundi(2016-17),Ethiopia(2015),Kenya(2022), Madagascar (2021), Malawi (2015-16), Mozambique (2022-23), Rwanda (2019-20), Tanzania (2022), Uganda (2016), Zambia (2018), and Zimbabwe (2015). These surveys span 2015–2024 and cover Eastern Africa’s diverse ecological zones—from Horn of Africa highlands to Great Lakes regions—with populations totaling ~ 487 million across 6.67 million km². The region faces heterogeneous climates (highland temperate vs. lowland humid) exacerbated by climate change, including rising temperatures (projected 2.5–4 °C by 2060) and variable rainfall patterns that amplify diarrheal disease transmission14–18.

Children record(KR) files from 11 countries yielded 78,962 weighted children aged 6–24 months eligible for diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity(DW) analysis(Fig. 1) .Three countries were exclude due to un available datasets. Sampling procedure and sample size determination

DHS employs two-stage stratified cluster sampling: primary sampling unites (PSUs, typically enumeration areas) selected via probability proportional to size(PPS), followed by systematic household sampling within clusters(25–30 households per cluster ) Weights(v005) adjustfor unequal selection profanities, stratification(v022), and PSU clustering(v021) to ensure national representativeness19. A total weighted sample size of 78,962 children aged 6–24 months was included in the current study (Table 1).

DW Multimorbidity was the outcome variable for this study. It was recoded as.

“1” if the child have co-occurrence (presence) of both diarrhea and wasting diseases, “0” if the child does have not have diarrhea and wasting co-occurrence at the same time.

Maternal education, child age, child sex, birth weight, timely breastfeeding, maternal age, marital status, wealth index, place of residence, ANC visit, timing of first ANC check, birth order, parity, media exposure, preceding birth interval, history of terminated pregnancy, and distance to get to a health facility were considered determinants of Diarrhea wasting multimorbidity(Table 2).

Operational Definition

Media exposure was generated by combining 3 variables from the survey: listening radio, watching television and reading newspaper. The response was categorized as yes for those who answered that they had access to either one of the three and no for those who had no exposure to all three of them20.

The birth weights were categorized into 3 groups. If the child birth weight was less than 2500 g, it was categorized as small birth weight; if the birth weight was between 2500 g and 4500 g, it was categorized as average (normal), and if the birth weight was > 4500 g, it was grouped under large birth weight21.

The timely initiation of breastfeeding is defined as the initiation of breastfeeding in newborns within the first hour of birth. Children who breastfeed within the first hour were categorized as yes to timely initiation of breastfeeding, and the rest were categorized as no to timely initiation of breastfeeding22,23.

Data management and analysis

After receiving the data, STATA version 17 was used for data extraction, cleaning, and appending the datasets. The data were weighted by using a sampling weight (v005), primary sampling unit (v021) and strata (v022) before any analysis to restore the representativeness of the survey and take into account the sampling design to obtain reliable statistical estimates. Missing values were handled through complete case analysis (4.2% overall missing excluded), with sensitivity analyses confirming result robustness; list wise deletion was preferred over imputation due to DHS data quality protocols and minimal bias risk for key covariates (< 10% missing per variable.

Spatial analysis

ArcGISpro, ArcGIS version10.7 software and SaTScan version 10.1 software were used to identify the geographic variations in DW among children aged 6–24 months in 11 EA countries. The Africa base map was downloaded from the Africa open shape file24. The coordinate points in shape file format that were received from the DHS program upon request were used for each country. The weighted proportion of DW multimorbidity was computed via Microsoft Excel. After the coordinate files were merged into single data, cluster values with zero latitude and longitude values were removed. The data were then exported to STATA and merged with the weighted proportion of DW multimorbidity.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation(Global Moran’s I) statistics was conducted to assess whether the DW multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months wasdispersed, clustered or randomly distributed in East African countries. Moran’s I values close to -1 indicates DW multimorbidity distribution was dispersed, close to + 1 indicates clustered, and if Moran ‘I value zero indicates randomly distributed. The null hypothesis of the test states that the DW pattern among children aged 6–24 months is randomly distributed across EA, and a Moran’s I (P value < 0.05) indicates spatial autocorrelation and rejection of the null hypothesis25.

Spatial Hotspot analysis

Getis‒Ord Gi* statistics had been calculated to measure how Spatial autocorrelations differs through the study locations by computed Gi* statistics for each area. Z-score was calculated to ensure the statistical significance of clustering and P-value calculated the significance p-value < 0.05 at 95% CI if Z-score is between-19.96 and + 1.96, the p-value must greater than 0.05 and vice versa. If p-value is less than − 1.96 it is declared as a cold spot and if greater than + 1.96 it is declared as hotspot26,27.

Empirical Bayesian Kriging spatial interpolation

The spatial interpolation technique is used to predict DW multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months for unsampled areas in the country based on sampled EAs. For the prediction of unsampled EAs, we used deterministic and geostatistical empirical Bayesian Kriging Spatial interpolation techniques. Empirical Bayesian Kriging relaxes the assumption of Gaussian distribution of the observed semivariogram in the input data, which rarely holds true practice. Empirical Bayesian Kriging interpolation works by generating a new simulated semivariogram at each location from the estimated semivariogram from the input data. The weight for the new simulated semivariogram is calculated by Bayes rule28.

Spatial scan (SaTScan) statistical analysis

We employed Bernoulli-based model Spatial scan Statistics to identify the geographical location of statistically significant clusters with a high proportion of DW multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months using Kuldorff’s SaTScan version 10.1 software. The scanning window that moves across the study area, in which children aged 6–24 months with DW multimorbidity were taken as a case and those with no DW multimorbidity as controls to fit Bernoulli model. The default maximum Spatial cluster size < 50% of the population was used as an upper limit, allowing both small and large clusters to be detected, and ignored clusters that contained more than the maximum limit with the circular shape of the window. Most likely clusters were identified using p-values and likelihood ratio tests based on 999 Monte Carlo replications29.

Spatial regression models

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression and Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) statistical analyses were employed to explore the spatial relationship between diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months and the explanatory variables influencing this outcome. The outcome variable for the spatial regression analysis was the percentage of diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months at the enumeration area (EA) level in East Africa. A neighborhood or bandwidth represents the distance band or number of neighbors used for each regression equation; it serves as the most critical parameter in spatial regression, controlling the degree of smoothing in the model. The complexity of the spatial regression model depends not only on the number of variables but also on the bandwidth. Three bandwidth methods are available: AICc, cross-validation (CV), and fixed bandwidth parameter. For this study, we used an adaptive kernel, with the bandwidth determined by minimizing the AICc value30–32.

Ordinary least squares regression

Spatial regression modeling was conducted to identify predictors of the spatial heterogeneity of diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity among children aged 6–24 months in East Africa. OLS is a global statistical model that tests and explains relationships between dependent and independent variables. It employs a single equation to estimate these relationships and assumes stationarity, or a consistent relationship, across the study area. OLS served as a diagnostic tool for selecting appropriate predictors to inform the GWR model30.

OLS automatically checks multicollinearity among independent variables using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). VIF values greater than 7.5 in the OLS model indicate multicollinearity, prompting a leave-one-out approach based on VIF values. Additionally, autocorrelation statistics detected spatial autocorrelation or clustering in residuals, which violates OLS assumptions. Residual spatial independence was assessed using the global Moran’s I coefficient to determine the need for GWR1,30,31,33.

Geographically weighted regression

GWR is a local spatial statistical technique that assumes non-stationarity, or heterogeneity, in relationships between the dependent variable (diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity) and explanatory variables across EAs. GWR is applied when the Koenker (BP) statistic is significant (p < 0.05), indicating that relationships vary by location. In GWR, coefficients for explanatory variables differ across the study area. Mapping these GWR coefficients provides insights for targeted interventions. In this study, we compared OLS and GWR models using corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and adjusted R-squared; the model with the lowest AICc and highest adjusted R-squared was deemed best-fitting31.

Spatial regression analysis of Diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity among children aged 6 to 24 months in East Africa

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression analyzed determinates of diarrhea and wasting (DW) multimorbidity, explains 23.3% of its variation (adjusted R2 = 0.233). Model fitness was assessed through the six OLS assumptions. The joint F and Wald Statistics were significant (p < 0.001), confirming overall model significance and that at least one predictor accounted for spatial variability in DW multimorbidity.

No multicollinearity was evident (VIF < 7.5). However, residuals violated normality (Jarque-Bera test: p < 0.001) and showed spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.018, p < 0.001, Z = 4.86), biasing OLS predictions. Heteroscedasticity or spatial nonstationarity was also detected (Koenker Statistic: p < 0.001), indicating inconsistent predictor DW multimorbidity relationships across the study area.

These violations support using Geographically Weighted regression (GWR), a local model that allows spatial variation in relations. In OLS, Significant DW multimorbidity predictors among children aged6-24 months include male sex, maternal illiteracy or higher education, maternal unemployment, unimproved toilet facilities, unimproved water source, and delayed breastfeeding initiation(S Table 2).

Geographically weighted regression (GWR)

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) used the same independent variables as the OLS model. Compared to OLS, GWR performance improved markedly, with adjusted R2 rising from 0.233 to 0.37, an additional 13.7% of spatial variation in diarrhea and wasting (DW) multimorbidity explained by GWR.

Model fit further improved, as evidenced by a substantial AIC reduction (OLS AIC = 16001.78 to GWR AIC = 14820.39; ΔAIC > 3) (S Table 3). Residuals also shifted from spatial clustering in OLS to random distribution in GWR (Fig. 7A, B).

Discussion

Diarrhea and wasting multimorbidity (DWM) among children aged 6–24 months exhibited significant spatial clustering across 11 East African countries (Moran’s I = 0.145, Z = 38.07, p < 0.001), with identified hotspots in Ethiopia, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Tanzania, Malawi, Mozambique, and Madagascar. The Ordinary Least Squares regression model explained only 23.3% of the variation in DWM and also violated the assumptions of normality, spatial independence, and homoscedasticity. However, the application of Geographically Weighted Regression substantially improved fit metrics (adjusted R2 = 0.37; AIC: 14820.39 vs. 16001.78). This model revealed spatially varying relationships for male sex, extremes of maternal education, employment status, unimproved water/latrine access as well as delayed breastfeeding initiation among other factors34,35.

Spatial variations in DWM are indicative of the disparities in sociocultural, economic, and health service domains across East Africa. In Kenya, Zambia, Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi, and Madagascar, a positive association was established with the male sex variable, which may be attributed to either gender-specific caregiving practices or biological vulnerabilities36–38. Maternal illiteracy has shown a positive correlation with DWM in Uganda, Rwanda, Tanzania, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Kenya; this reflects an inability to access health information effectively39,40. Conversely, higher levels of maternal education have been related to increase DWM in Ethiopia. This finding may suggest urban-rural differentials concerning knowledge or trade-offs concerning childcare due to employment41–43.

Unimproved water sources increased the risk of DWM primarily in Ethiopia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe through the pathway of microbial contamination44–47. The unimproved latrines had the strongest positive effects in Ethiopia. Maternal unemployment protected against DWM in Rwanda, Burundi, and Tanzania due to more caregiving time but increased risk in Kenya and Tanzania due to economic constraints48–50. Late initiation of breastfeeding increased DWM in uganda, Rwanda, Zambia, Malawi, and Madagascar due to deprivation of colostrum and vulnerability to infection22,51.

These spatially non-stationary associations enhance the findings from separate studies on diarrheal diseases and wasting conducted in East Africa, corroborating the geospatial analyses carried out in Ethiopia and northwest Ethiopia. Contrary to global analyses that assume homogenous effects across regions, the GWR model uncovers context-specific patterns that align with regional studies on child undernutrition20,33,52. The positive male vulnerability noted in this study is consistent with reports from Ethiopia and Nigeria, yet it diverges from the prevailing trend of female disadvantages observed in other regions53,54. The paradoxical effect of maternal education on child health outcomes is in line with findings from Nigeria and other low- and middle-income countries, while the risks associated with water and sanitation infrastructure substantiate pathways of contamination55–59. The results regarding employment and breastfeeding practices corroborate hypotheses concerning trade-offs in caregiving responsibilities as well as the benefits derived from colostrum60–64.

Public health implications of the DWM spatial hotspots that coincide with countries exhibiting high under-5 mortality rates, such as Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Uganda, necessitate prioritized and specific interventions65,66. Sanitation improvements should be prioritized in the water hotspots of Ethiopia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, while maternal education campaigns are urgently needed in the illiteracy clusters of Uganda and Rwanda47,67–69. Gender-specific healthcare provision in areas where male are particularly vulnerable, like certain regions in Kenya and Tanzania, as well as breastfeeding promotion initiatives targeted at Uganda and Malawi, have the potential for substantial returns on investment70,71. This spatial targeting through GWR is considerably more effective than any uniform strategy and will be critical in directing monitoring efforts based on DHS data and the allocation of result-based financing. Coordination at the multilateral level within WHO/UNICEF frameworks is essential to address cross-border hotspots.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study leverages large, nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) datasets from 11 East African countries, enhancing regional generalizability. Spatial and SaTScan cluster analysis identified statistically significant DW multimorbidity hot spots. Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) accounted for spatial dependency and autocorrelation, yielding more reliable, location-specific estimates than global models.

Limitations, Key variables including vaccination status (e.g., measles) and economic factors (e.g., health insurance) were unavailable across all countries, potentially confounding DWM associations. DHS data recency varied (2016–2024), introducing temporal inconsistencies. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Enumeration area coordinates were randomly displaced (≤ 2 km urban, ≤ 5 km rural), introducing minor spatial uncertainty.

Conclusion and recommendation

Geographically Weighted Regression revealed spatially varying diarrhea-wasting multimorbidity patterns among East African children aged 6–24 months, identifying actionable hotspots and location-specific risk factors including male sex, maternal education extremes, unemployment, delayed breastfeeding, and poor WASH access. Unlike global models, GWR enables precise spatial targeting that explains 37% of variation versus OLS’s 23.3%.

Policymakers should prioritize Ethiopia/Zambia water chlorination campaigns, Uganda/Rwanda maternal literacy programs via community health workers, and Kenya/Tanzania gender-sensitive caregiver cooperatives. East African governments must coordinate cross-border WASH-nutrition interventions and integrate GWR mapping into national DHS monitoring for sustained DWM reduction.
