Exploring the relationship between annual soil loss and formation rate in different land use scenarios using support vector machine (SVM) learning models in Tigray Highlands
Authors: Mulata Haftu, Eskinder Gidey, Paidamwoyo Mhangara, Oagile Dikinya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extensive soil degradation in the Tigray Highlands, Ethiopia, threatens agricultural sustainability. This study quantifies the critical imbalance between soil loss and formation in the severely affected Kola Embahasti Watershed. An integrated modeling approach was employed to assess the watershed’s soil budget. Soil erosion was estimated using the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE), with the land use/land cover (LULC) C-factor classified using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) model trained on field and satellite data (Sentinel-2B). Soil formation rates were calculated using the Arrhenius equation to model weathering processes. Statistical analysis was used to determine the relationship between erosion and formation rates. The results reveal a severe soil degradation, with a mean annual soil loss of 61.29 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, far exceeding the mean soil formation rate of 2.45 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1. This yields a net annual soil loss of 58.84 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, indicating an unsustainable rate of degradation. A strong negative relationship was found between soil loss and formation, heavily influenced by land cover. Bare land exhibited the highest erosion (94 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1) and lowest formation (0.98 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1), while dense forest demonstrated the lowest erosion (5.14 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1) and highest formation (4.9 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1). The RUSLE model, physiochemical soil formation model, and SVM algorithm are integrated for innovative conservation action, recommending targeted interventions e.g., afforestation and sustainable land management.

Introduction

Human activities and resource demands are significantly degrading global land and water resources, resulting in a notable decline in soil productivity and, in certain instances, complete loss1,2. Soil erosion represents a significant aspect of land degradation, characterized by the removal of the land surface through agents such as water and wind3,4. Water erosion poses a significant issue by removing fertile topsoil, resulting in considerable environmental and economic challenges. It is estimated that wind and water erosion remove 75 billion metric tons of soil annually on a global scale, predominantly from agricultural lands5,6. Approximately 80% of global agricultural land is subject to moderate to severe erosion, which jeopardizes food production capabilities and intensifies food shortages for an increasing population7,8.

In Ethiopia, land degradation, characterized by soil erosion and declining fertility, presents a significant challenge to agricultural productivity and economic development9,10. The nation’s population has increased threefold in the past fifty years, resulting in significant deforestation for agricultural expansion and the overgrazing of existing resources11. This trend has led to one of the highest soil erosion rates globally, with cultivated fields experiencing an estimated average loss of 42 tons per hectare annually12. The Tigray Highlands exemplify a significant paradox, being noted for both severe land degradation and substantial conservation initiatives aimed at addressing this issue13,14. Despite these commendable initiatives, soil erosion has persisted in watersheds such as Kola Embahasti in Endamehoni Wereda, leading to a decline in agricultural productivity. Evaluating the risks associated with soil erosion is essential for effective forecasting and prevention strategies. For decades, researchers have predominantly utilized empirical models like the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE) to estimate average soil erosion rates, considering variables such as rainfall, soil type, and topography15.

Since the late 1980s, the integration of remote sensing (RS) and geographic information systems (GIS) has enhanced the application of these models, facilitating cost-effective assessments across extensive regions16,17. These empirical models frequently depend on generalized parameters. Recent advancements in machine learning, particularly with models such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), have demonstrated their efficacy as robust alternatives. These models can solve complex issues and increase the accuracy of site-specific estimations. Despite advancements in modeling, a substantial knowledge gap remains in nearly all studies of soil erosion. Research has predominantly concentrated on quantifying the rate of soil loss, regardless of whether traditional or machine learning methodologies are employed. This study uses SVM machine learning to model soil loss and formation rates, focusing on the rate of soil formation, to predict sustainable land use, addressing the gap in erosion mapping and soil loss prediction. Additionally, a process-based physiochemical model, the Arrhenius equation, is employed to estimate soil formation. This integrated framework enables the calculation of the net soil balance, indicating the deficit or surplus of soil. This metric offers a precise assessment of land degradation and sustainability that has been previously neglected in this region. This study aimed to (i) estimate the mean annual soil loss and formation rate for each land use type; (ii) determine the net annual soil loss at both watershed and land use levels; (iii) explore the relationship between soil loss and formation rates; and (iv) propose targeted conservation strategies based on the findings. The study’s findings offer a thorough understanding of the net soil balance, serving as an essential resource for policymakers and land managers to implement effective conservation strategies, protect soil resources, and promote sustainable land management in the watershed and comparable degraded environments.

Materials and methods

Study area

The research was carried out in the Kola Embahasti Watershed, in Endamehoni Wereda, Southern Zone of the Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia. The watershed is located about 120 km south of Mekelle, next to the primary Mekelle-Maichew highway. The area is defined by coordinates from 39°30′30" to 39°35′30" East and 12°46′0" to 12°51′30" North, encompassing a total of 4,795.37 hectares (47.95 km2). The topography features a notable altitudinal gradient, with elevations between 1908 and 3450 m above sea level (m.a.s.l.), situating the watershed within various agro-climatic zones (Fig. 1). The watershed is predominantly underlain by Tertiary volcanic formations, particularly the mafic Ashangi Basalts. The rocks formed in the Oligocene epoch typically exhibit a porphyritic texture characterized by phenocrysts of plagioclase and pyroxene, which have impacted the region’s soil and mineral composition18.The local climate exhibits a broad altitudinal range, encompassing the Weyna Dega (temperate) and Wurch (cold, Afro-alpine) agro-climatic zones19. The region exhibits a unimodal rainfall pattern, characterized by mean annual precipitation ranging from 1071 to 1100 mm, predominantly occurring during the summer months of June to September. Mean annual temperatures range from approximately 16–20°C in lower elevations to below 10°C in higher regions, where frosts frequently occur (Fig. 2).Fig. 1Location map of Kola Embahasti Watershed.Fig. 2Average monthly rainfall and temperature for the Kola Embahasti Watershed (2011–2020).

Human activity primarily influences the land use and land cover (LULC) within the watershed. The dominant land use and land cover category is cultivated land, encompassing 2,108.53 ha (43.97%) of the total area, suggesting that subsistence agriculture serves as the principal means of livelihood. Data acquisition

This study utilized a combination of satellite remote sensing data, gridded climate datasets, global soil databases, and parameters derived from peer-reviewed literature. A variety of datasets were utilized in this study. Satellite imagery was obtained from the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/), specifically a high-resolution Sentinel-2B image with a 10-m spatial resolution, which was used to classify land use/land cover (LULC) in the watershed. Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data was sourced from the ALOS PALSAR mission, featuring a 12.5-m spatial resolution, and downloaded via the NASA Alaska Satellite Facility (ASF) data portal (https://search.asf.alaska.edu/). Soil data were acquired from the FAO/UNESCO Digital Soil Map of the World (Version 3.6), supplemented by field measurements of soil depth across various land use types. Precipitation and temperature data consisted of a 10-year (2011–2020) monthly gridded time series from the Climate Research Unit (CRU TS v4.07), accessed through the official archive (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/hrg/). Additionally, field data were collected using a handheld GPS device to gather ground truth points for validating the LULC classification.

Data processing and analysis

This section provides details of the analytical procedures used to estimate soil loss using the RUSLE model, the rate of soil formation from its constituent parts, and finally, quantify the net soil balance across the watershed. All spatial analyses were conducted using ArcGIS 10.7.1. The spatial distribution of potential mean annual soil loss was estimated using the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE) within a GIS framework. The RUSLE model15,20 is a globally recognized empirical model for predicting sheet and rill erosion (Eq. 1). GIS layers were created for each of the five RUSLE factors and then multiplied together on a cell-by-cell basis to produce a final map of potential soil loss (A) in tons per hectare per year (t ha⁻1 yr⁻1).

Cover-management (C-factor)

The C-factor was derived from the 2024 Land Use/Land Cover (LULC) map, which was generated using Sentinel-2B imagery (10 m resolution, acquired in January 2024) obtained from the Copernicus Open Access Hub. The imagery underwent atmospheric and radiometric corrections to reduce noise and enhance spectral quality. A supervised Support Vector Machine (SVM) classification algorithm was implemented in ArcGIS 10.7 to categorize land cover types based on the spectral signatures of training samples collected from field observations and high-resolution Google Earth imagery. SVM was selected for its high accuracy in distinguishing spectrally similar classes and its effectiveness in handling high-dimensional datasets. The classification identified six major LULC classes: bare land, cultivated land, shrubland, open forest, dense forest, and built-up areas. Validation was performed using independent ground-truth points, and an error matrix was generated to assess classification accuracy. The map achieved an overall accuracy of 95% and a Kappa coefficient of 0.91, indicating strong agreement between the classified and reference data. Net soil balance and relationship analysis

To quantify the net soil balance and link it to land management, a final spatio-statistical analysis was performed. First, a net soil balance raster was computed for the entire study area by subtracting the soil formation rate (SF) raster from the RUSLE-derived annual soil loss (A) raster (Eq. 10).11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Net Soil Balance }} = {\text{ A }} - {\text{ SF}}$$\end{document}where A represents the annual soil loss rate (t ha−1 yr−1) derived from the RUSLE model, and SF denotes the annual soil formation rate (t ha−1 yr−1) estimated from integrated weathering processes.

This output spatially delineates areas of soil deficit (positive values) from areas of stability or surplus (negative values). The recalculated values are based on updated area coverage for each land use class, with pixel counts adjusted accordingly (1 pixel = 0.01 ha). Total soil loss or formation per zone was derived by multiplying the mean rate with the respective area. These computations produced a clear representation of the average soil balance across different land uses. Finally, to investigate the relationship between the two processes, a scatter plot and linear regression model were generated in R software, correlating the estimated soil loss rates with the calculated soil formation rates. Results and discussion

Estimation of mean annual soil loss and formation rates by land use type

Figures 5, 6, 7, 8 below depicts the spatial distribution of important erosion-controlling elements that have an effect on soil deterioration throughout the watershed under consideration. The patterns that have been provided offer unmistakable proof of the environmental factors that are the primary contributors to soil erosion. A range of 594 to 610 MJ mm ha⁻1 h⁻1 yr⁻1 is shown in Fig. 5a, which suggests that the rainfall–runoff erosivity (R-factor) is present. Strong rainfall energy, capable of separating soil particles and causing overland flow, reflects these high numbers. Both Gashaw et al.42 and Hurni21 have observed similar magnitudes in Ethiopian highland contexts, which are characterized by seasonal rainfall regimes that enhance erosion concerns. While rainfall intensity is recognized as the principal erosive force29, the erosivity algorithm applied in this study is based on total annual precipitation and does not explicitly account for rainfall intensity. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as potential erosion driven by rainfall amount. This is especially true in highland agro-ecological zones. It can be seen in Fig. 5b that soil erodibility, also known as the K-factor, varies everywhere within the watershed, with values ranging from 0 to 0.153. The eastern and northeastern sectors exhibit the highest values, indicating their greater susceptibility to separation and movement compared to other regions. This spatial pattern is associated with changes in the soil qualities. In contrast to the sandier BE1 unit, which has a lower value of 0.095, the Eutric Cambisol (BE) unit displayed a K-value that was within the moderate range of 0.153. The lower erodibility of BE1 can be linked to its high infiltration capacity, which restricts runoff and, as a result, hinders soil detachment15 Morgan, 2005). Correlation between soil erosion and soil formation rate

The correlation between annual soil loss and soil formation rates across various land use/land cover (LULC) categories indicates a significant association between degradation and natural regeneration. Land units with minimal vegetative cover, such as Bare Land and Cultivated Land, demonstrate high erosion rates of 94.00 and 35.50 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, respectively, alongside low formation rates of 0.98 and 1.47 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1. The values indicate substantial net soil losses of 93.02 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1 for bare land and 34.03 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1 for cultivated land, highlighting the unsustainable nature of land management practices in these regions. In contrast, Dense Forest exhibited the lowest soil loss at 5.14 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1 and the highest soil formation rate at 4.90 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, resulting in a nearly neutral net balance of 0.24 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1. This suggests that in stable, well-vegetated environments, natural soil regeneration can almost offset erosional losses, aligning with earlier research that emphasizes the protective function of forest cover (Vanacker et al.,2007). Figure 7 statistically confirms this pattern, presenting a scatter plot that illustrates the relationship between mean annual soil loss (x-axis) and formation rates (y-axis) categorized by land use and land cover (LULC) type. The result demonstrates a significant negative correlation (r = –0.78, p = 0.12), suggesting that increased soil loss is associated with reduced formation rates. This inverse relationship indicates a feedback loop in which landscapes most susceptible to erosion are simultaneously those least able to recover naturally, thereby exacerbating degradation over time. This dynamic indicates a wider ecological issue. Erosion represents not only the loss of topsoil but also a disruption of essential ecological processes, such as organic matter cycling, microbial activity, and pedogenesis, as highlighted by (Lal, 2001)and Pimentel & Burgess(2013). This study supports these conclusions, indicating that vegetation loss, particularly in vulnerable ecosystems, diminishes the soil’s ability to regenerate, thus hastening processes of land degradation and potential desertification (Nyssen et al.,2004 ;Assennato et al., 2020). The observed imbalance aligns with global research indicating that soil formation rates typically do not exceed 1.0 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1 under natural conditions, whereas human-induced erosion can exceed 40 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1 (Montgomery, 2007).The mean annual soil loss in the study watershed is 61.29 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, significantly surpassing the mean formation rate of 2.45 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, indicating a critical soil sustainability deficit. This gap necessitates immediate landscape-level conservation and land management measures that combine erosion control with strategies to improve soil formation capacity. The “Dense Forest” and “Open Forest” land use patterns form a “zone of equilibrium,” with high soil formation and low erosion rates. The “Shrub/Bush Land” and “Cultivated Land” zones are unstable, with moderate to high erosion and deteriorating formation capacity. The “Bare Land” zone demonstrates ecological breakdown. Drivers of soil loss in the kola Embahasti watershed, Tigray highlands

The highest mean annual soil loss rates were observed in Bare Land (94.00 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1) and Cultivated Land (35.50 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1), together totaling over 147,000 tons of soil loss annually due to sparse or no vegetative cover and land use practices that exacerbate surface disturbance. Additionally, the upland areas characterized by long and steep slopes predominantly exhibit LS-factor values, reaching a maximum of 33.4. These areas are frequently farmed without sufficient soil and water conservation measures, which markedly enhance the erosive capacity of overland flow because significant areas of agricultural land do not implement effective support practices. The extensive removal of natural vegetation for agricultural purposes has diminished the land’s tolerance capacity, resulting in increased soil exposure to raindrop impacts and surface runoff. This finding is consistent with the findings of Nyssen et al. (2004) and Walsh et al. (2005), who emphasized that the removal of forest and shrubland in the Ethiopian highlands significantly contributes to accelerated erosion. In the study area, rainfall exacerbates erosion, varying from 594 to 610 MJ mm ha⁻1 h⁻1 yr⁻1, signifying a substantial erosive force during the rainy season. The intense seasonal rain coincides with peak land use activities, such as plowing and sowing, which further amplify surface disturbance and runoff. The vulnerability is particularly evident in regions with fragile soil types such as EutricCambisols, which, despite exhibiting moderate K-values, are susceptible to detachment when vegetative cover is removed. Conventional agricultural methods, including downslope plowing, continuous cropping without fallow periods, and minimal organic input, exacerbating soil degradation and diminishing infiltration capacity. This undermines soil resilience, as evidenced by the significant negative correlation (r = -0.78) between soil loss and formation rates. Degraded areas, particularly bare and cultivated lands, experience rapid soil loss and slow regeneration, rendering them critical zones of unsustainable land use. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors, including insecure land tenure, labor shortages, and limited awareness of sustainable practices, impede the broad implementation of conservation measures21, Tenge & Hella, 2005).

Conclusion

This study provides informative quantitative evidence of a significant soil sustainability imbalance in the Kola Embahasti watershed, where soil erosion rates frequently surpass natural soil formation. This research accomplished a spatially thorough evaluation of soil degradation dynamics by merging the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE) with Support Vector Machine (SVM)-based land use/land cover (LULC) categorization and the Arrhenius equation for estimating soil formation. The results indicated a watershed-wide average net soil loss of 58.84 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1—an unsustainably elevated depletion rate significantly beyond globally recognized tolerable thresholds. Land use and land cover were recognized as the primary factors contributing to this mismatch. Regions with dense forest cover demonstrated near-equilibrium conditions, with a marginal net soil loss of 0.24 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, highlighting the significant importance of forest ecosystems in mitigating erosion via canopy interception, litter cover, and low surface disturbance. Conversely, bare lands and agricultural zones were identified as the most susceptible to erosion, suffering significant net soil losses of up to 93.02 t ha⁻1 yr⁻1, attributable to inadequate vegetative cover, frequent plowing, and steep topography. The findings validate the efficacy of comprehensive soil modeling in pinpointing deterioration hotspots and guiding targeted actions. This study offers spatially explicit, practical insights into sustainable land management. The lack of ground-truth validation for soil formation estimations constrains the work, potentially impacting the absolute accuracy of formation rates. The report advocates for immediate conservation initiatives in regions experiencing significant erosion, especially in bare and heavily cultivated areas. Advancing sustainable farming methods, augmenting vegetation cover, and introducing adaptive monitoring systems will be critical measures for restoring soil equilibrium. This study underscores the imperative of transitioning from reactive to proactive, evidence-based watershed management to guarantee the long-term viability of soil resources in the Kola Embahasti region.
