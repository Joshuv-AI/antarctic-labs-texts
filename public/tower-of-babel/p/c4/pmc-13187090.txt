Intracellular and dual-site inhibition of a bitter taste GPCR
Authors: Nitsan Dallal, Gil Daniel Paz, Noga Nir-Marom, Yael Keselman, Shir Eyal, Evgenii Ziaikin, Alon Rainish, Sam RJ Hoare, Lior Peri, Einav Malach, Masha Y Niv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) are G protein-coupled receptors expressed in both gustatory and extraoral tissues and activated by a broad range of compounds. TAS2R14 is among the most promiscuous members of this family, responding to many structurally diverse ligands. Cryo-electron microscopy structures of TAS2R14 have revealed agonists binding in an intracellular pocket, raising the question of the main sites of interaction for known TAS2R14 antagonists. To address this, we examined the effects of mutations at residues located in the extracellular and intracellular regions on receptor inhibition by three antagonist compounds: LF1, LF22 and probenecid. Mutations in the extracellular region reduced the inhibitory effect of LF22, whereas all three compounds showed reduced inhibition in the intracellular mutants. Computational co-folding of these ligands with TAS2R14 supported these observations, indicating that LF22 interacts with both top and bottom binding sites, whereas LF1 and probenecid engage predominantly the intracellular site adjacent to the G protein interface. Interestingly, LF1 is more potent for TAS2R16 than its known inhibitor probenecid. Introduction

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) represent the largest family of cell-surface receptors in humans, with nearly 800 members involved in transducing extracellular signals into cellular responses [1]. They regulate numerous physiological processes and are targeted by over one-third of all FDA-approved drugs, of which more than half act as antagonists [1]. Within this family, bitter taste receptors (TAS2Rs) form a small but distinctive subfamily of 26 functional receptors in humans [2–6]. Human TAS2Rs detect over two thousand structurally diverse natural and synthetic bitter molecules and play a crucial role in taste perception [7]. In addition, these receptors are implicated in extra-oral functions such as immune defense, airway smooth muscle relaxation, and cancer biology [8–10].

Among TAS2Rs, TAS2R14 is the most promiscuous member, activated by structurally diverse ligands, including flavonoids, alkaloids and peptides [7, 11]. TAS2R14 is expressed in taste buds and in multiple extra-oral tissues, such as the respiratory tract, placenta, thyroid, and pancreas, where it has been implicated in both protective and pathological processes [12–14]. Consistent with this dual role, high TAS2R14 expression has been linked to improved survival in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, whereas in other cancers it correlates with poorer prognosis [12, 15]. This broad expression pattern and ligand diversity highlights TAS2R14 as a receptor of major physiological and therapeutic relevance [4].

Contrary to the wealth of agonists identified [16–20], only a few TAS2R14 antagonists have been described to date, i.e [20, 21]. For example, our virtual screening discovery efforts yielded a small set of TAS2R14 antagonists, which were later validated as chemical probes and shown to inhibit TAS2R14 activity in several cancer cell lines [13, 20]. Specifically, the compounds LF1 and LF22 (Fig. 1) were found based on their predicted fit to the canonical extracellular binding pocket of the three-dimensional model of the TAS2R14 receptor, traditionally assumed to accommodate both agonists and antagonists [11, 20, 22]. After the discovery of these antagonists, several cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) structures of TAS2R14 were solved, providing unprecedented insight into its architecture [23–26]. The experimental structures unexpectedly revealed an additional, intracellular, agonist binding pocket. In other GPCR families, intracellular binding pockets have been detected in a few cases and usually associated with antagonist binding [27–32]. This raised the possibility that TAS2Rs might also be inhibited through an intracellular site. However, no experimental TAS2R14 structure has been solved with an antagonist, leaving open the question of where antagonists interact with this receptor. Furthermore, studies leading to the discovery of TAS2R14 antagonists were conducted prior to the intracellular pocket elucidation and did not evaluate inhibitory mechanisms relative to the two spatially distinct binding sites [20, 21].

Agonists and antagonists used in this research

In this work, we explored whether TAS2R14 antagonists LF1 and LF22 act through the extracellular pocket (for which they were originally designed), the intracellular pocket, or both. We also included probenecid, known to inhibit TAS2R16 [33], and recently shown to also inhibit TAS2R14 [34], and tested all three compounds on TAS2R16 to provide a comparative reference. Using functional assays of representative mutants, we examined antagonists’ dependence on residues lining each pocket. This approach allowed us to distinguish antagonists that act mainly through the intracellular site from those requiring contributions from the extracellular binding regions. By placing antagonist behavior in the context of the recently resolved dual-pocket architecture and integrating computational modeling with functional data, our study provides a mechanistic framework for interpreting TAS2R14 inhibition.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Experiments were performed using HEK293T cells. Cells were cultured in plates containing 10% Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine amino acid, and 0.2% penicillin streptomycin. Every 3–4 days, when plates were at 80% confluence, the medium was removed, and cells were transferred into a fresh growth medium. Cells were kept in the incubator, at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

IP-One assay

For functional expression of the human bitter taste receptor TAS2R14 or TAS2R16, HEK293T cells were grown at about 80% confluence and transiently transfected with 2 µg of a plasmid (pcDNA3.1) encoding N-terminally modified TAS2R14 or TAS2R16 (N-terminal addition of a cleavable HA-signal peptide, followed by a FLAG-tag and the first 45 amino acids of the rat somatostatin receptor 3) and 1 µg of a plasmid encoding the chimeric Gαqi5 protein, as described in previous work [20]. Transfections were performed using Mirus TransIT-293 (Mirus Bio) at a 1:3 DNA to reagent ratio.

24 h after transfection, the transfected cells were suspended in 10% DMEM and seeded onto a 384-well culture micro plate (Greiner) at a density of 5 × 105 (cells/ml). The plate was then kept in an incubator for 24 h to obtain cell adherence. The next day, cell culture medium supernatant was removed from the plates and stimulation buffer (Stim B) from the IP-One kit, containing 50 mM LiCl to prevent IP1 degradation, was added to each well. Cells were then treated by the addition of the tested compounds. Plates were incubated for 150 min at 37 °C, to allow IP1 accumulation inside the cells [20, 35]. For investigation of TAS2R14 or TAS2R16 inhibition, the cells were first pre-incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with potential inhibitor, followed by addition of either 1 µM FFA or 10 mM salicin and continuing incubation for further 150 min. For Schild analysis experiments, cells were pre-incubated with a range of concentrations of antagonist for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by application of a range of concentrations of agonist and further incubation of 150 min (see details in Supplementary data).

Accumulation of the second messenger was stopped by adding detection reagents (IP1-d2 conjugate and Anti-IP1 cryptate TB conjugate) dissolved in kit lysis buffer (HTRF IP-One Gq Detection Kit, Revvity). FRET-ratios were calculated as the ratio of emission intensity of the FRET acceptor (665/10 nm) divided by the FRET donor intensity (620/10 nm) (ClarioStar, BMG). Raw FRET-ratios were normalized to buffer conditions (0%) and the maximum effect of FFA or salicin (100%), and the obtained responses were analyzed using the equation for sigmoid concentration-response curves (four-parameter) implemented in GraphPad Prism for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA) to derive the maximum effect (Emax, relative to salicin) and the ligand potency (EC50 or IC50). For antagonist properties, data were normalized as a percentage of the effect of 1 µM FFA or 10 mM salicin in the absence of antagonist.

Compounds

Reagents and compounds were obtained from the following suppliers: Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA), Sigma (USA; flufenamic acid, salicin, probenecid), Gibco (USA; DMEM, FBS), Revvity (France; IP-One HTRF kit), BLDpharm (China), Enamine (Ukraine; LF1 (Cat. No. Z85879385) and LF22 (Cat. No. Z3906872218)), and Mirus Bio (USA; TransIT-293 reagent). All compounds were dissolved in DMSO. Serial dilutions of compounds were prepared in the stimulation buffer of the kit (containing 50 mM LiCl to prevent IP1 degradation) at the desired working concentration on the day of the experiment (Cisbio IP-ONE Gq KIT).

Co-folding of protein-ligand complexes

Boltz-2 was used to predict the structure of the protein-ligand complex using the default MMseqs2 server to generate multiple sequence alignment (MSA), with a diffusion sample number of 20 [36, 37]. The input data consisted of ligand SMILES taken, where possible, from PubChem [38] or from the original publications in which LF1 and LF22 were discovered [20]. The TAS2R sequences were taken from BitterDB [7].

Results

Recent cryo-EM structures of TAS2R14, solved independently by different groups, consistently revealed two binding sites within the transmembrane core: an extracellular (top, canonical) pocket and an intracellular (bottom) pocket at the receptor-G protein interface [23–26]. In PDB 8RQL, two copies of flufenamic acid (FFA) simultaneously occupy both binding sites, an uncommon scenario featuring two copies of the same agonist in separate pockets [24] (Fig. 2a).

To determine where known antagonists interact with the receptor, we evaluated how mutations at key residues influence FFA-induced activation. We focused on positions previously highlighted by structural and mutagenesis studies as important for either the top or the bottom pockets. In the top pocket, we examined S65A2.60 and S265A7.38 (position annotation follows the Ballesteros-Weinstein scheme for GPCRs [39]). Residue S652.60 forms polar interactions with the amine linker of FFA, and substitutions at this position influence agonist recognition [24]. S2657.38 corresponds to a position where naturally occurring variants have been identified and have been reported to affect agonist potency and efficacy [11, 24, 40]. In the bottom pocket, we tested S194A5.54 and H276F7.49. Both residues directly interact with FFA in the Cryo-EM structure of TAS2R14 [24], and previous work has shown that both mutations impair receptor activation [24]. Position 7.49 is a highly conserved histidine present in almost all human TAS2Rs [24, 41]. We measured FFA-induced responses in HEK293T cells expressing either wild-type (WT) or mutant receptors (Fig. 2d-e).

WT exhibited an EC₅₀ of 0.37 ± 0.06 µM, in agreement with previous studies [24, 25]. In the top binding site, both S65A2.60 (EC₅₀ = 0.70 ± 0.08 µM) and S265A7.38 (EC₅₀ = 0.33 ± 0.05 µM) showed EC₅₀ values similar to WT and both mutants reached approximately 100% of the WT Emax (Fig. 2d). In the bottom binding site, S194A5.54 displayed a marked decrease in potency (EC₅₀ = 2.80 ± 1.23 µM) together with a reduced maximal activation to about 60% of WT Emax. H276F7.49 also showed a rightward shift (EC₅₀ = 0.90 ± 0.09 µM) and an even stronger reduction in maximal activation to approximately 40% (Fig. 2e). Together, these results indicate that mutations in the bottom pocket have a clear functional impact on FFA activation, whereas the effects of the top-pocket mutations are minimal. Importantly, all the mutants still respond to FFA in a dose-dependent manner, allowing the next step - testing the effects of antagonists.

LF1, LF22 and probenecid.

We next asked how antagonists interact with TAS2R14. LF1 and LF22 were originally found through predicted compatibility with the canonical binding site of TAS2R14, prior to the availability of the TAS2R14 cryo-EM structures and before the identification of a second intracellular pocket [20]. In addition to LF1 and LF22, we tested probenecid, a small-molecule drug previously characterized as a TAS2R16 antagonist, suggested to act via the TAS2R16 intracellular site [33, 43] and recently shown to inhibit TAS2R14 [34].

In WT, LF1 inhibited TAS2R14 with an IC₅₀ of 18.0 ± 0.50 µM. In the top site mutants, S65A2.60 and S265A7.38 displayed IC₅₀ values of 3.0 ± 8.10 µM and 11.6 ± 1.70 µM respectively (Fig. 3a). In the bottom site mutants, S194A5.54 showed an IC₅₀ of 2.90 ± 1.40 µM, while H276F7.49 completely abolished LF1 inhibition (Fig. 3b). Taken together, top pocket mutations had minimal influence on LF1 inhibition, while bottom pocket mutations, particularly H276F7.49, strongly disrupted LF1 activity.

LF22 inhibited FFA-induced responses in WT TAS2R14 with an IC₅₀ of 31.30 ± 10.30 µM. In the top-site mutants, S65A2.60 displayed an IC₅₀ of 4.70 ± 3.20 µM, while S265A7.38 showed no measurable inhibition (Fig. 3c). In the bottom site mutants, S194A5.54 abolished LF22 inhibition, whereas H276F⁷·⁴⁹ increased potency with an IC₅₀ of 0.12 ± 0.06 µM (Fig. 3d). These results show that LF22 inhibition is disrupted by mutations in both pockets, specifically by the top pocket mutant S265A7.38 and the bottom pocket mutant S194A5.54.

Probenecid completely abolished the WT response to FFA, with an IC₅₀ of 0.60 ± 0.70 mM. In the top site mutants S65A2.60 and S265A7.38, probenecid retained its full inhibitory capacity, showing IC₅₀ values of 2.30 ± 1.30 mM and 1.0 ± 0.20 mM, respectively (Fig. 3e). In contrast, mutations in the bottom binding pocket eliminated inhibition: both S194A5.54 and H276F7.49 failed to respond to probenecid and showed no reduction in FFA-induced activity (Fig. 3f).

To visualize possible interaction modes of antagonists with TAS2R14, we used Boltz-2, a structure-based deep-learning framework that integrates protein flexibility and ligand sampling to predict binding poses [36]. A key feature of Boltz-2 is its ability to model ligand binding without predefining a binding pocket, enabling unbiased exploration of possible interaction sites across the receptor surface. Boltz-2 takes the receptor sequence as input and jointly samples receptor conformations and protein ligand interactions [36]. For each antagonist, five independent simulations were performed, and the resulting poses were clustered to identify the most probable binding modes.

Across the five predicted poses, LF1 was consistently placed in the bottom pocket (Fig. 3g). LF22 produced a mixed pattern, with two poses positioned in the bottom pocket and three located in the top pocket (Fig. 3h). Probenecid localized to the intracellular site in all predicted complexes (Fig. 3i). Two-dimensional interaction maps illustrating the predicted contacts between each antagonist and TAS2R14 are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

To further characterize the inhibitory mechanism of LF1 and LF22, we performed Schild analysis [44] to evaluate whether inhibition is consistent with simple competitive antagonism or reflects a more complex behavior.

For both LF1 and LF22, increasing antagonist concentrations caused rightward shifts of the agonist dose response curves, accompanied by a reduction in maximal response. The calculated A2 values differed between antagonists and depended on the agonist used (Supplementary Table 1). In addition, Emax values decreased progressively with increasing antagonist concentration for both compounds (Supplementary Table 2), indicating that inhibition was not purely competitive.

Antagonists cross-reactivity.

Probenecid is one of the few established TAS2R antagonists [33]. Mutations in the intracellular region reduced probenecid efficacy without affecting agonist activation, and probenecid lowered the maximal response to salicin without shifting its EC₅₀, indicating that it decreases receptor efficacy rather than competing with the agonist at its binding site. The recent Cryo-EM structure of TAS2R16, solved with salicin bound in the extracellular binding site [45], is compatible with this mechanism. Our results indicate that probenecid inhibits TAS2R14 through dependence on the intracellular site, and we next asked whether TAS2R14 antagonists also inhibit TAS2R16.

To examine TAS2R16 activation, we first measured the response to salicin in both WT and the N96T3.43 mutant, which lies within the intracellular region of the receptor and has been examined in previous studies [43]. In WT, salicin induced a clear dose-dependent activation with EC₅₀ of 13.00 ± 4.30 mM. The N96T3.43 mutant showed a markedly stronger response, reaching 372% of the WT maximal activation, consistent with increased receptor sensitivity. This was also accompanied by a lower EC₅₀ of 5.52 ± 0.62 mM (Fig. 4a).

Data represent mean ± SEM of 3–6 replicates

We next examined inhibition by probenecid. In WT, probenecid completely blocked salicin-induced activation, with an IC₅₀ of 0.77 ± 0.33 mM. In N96T3.43, inhibition was substantially weaker, and the IC₅₀ shifted to 2.18 ± 1.01 mM (Fig. 4b).

LF1 also inhibited TAS2R16 in both WT and N96T3.43, reducing salicin-induced responses with IC₅₀ values of 0.032 ± 0.021 mM and 0.42 ± 0.11 mM, respectively (Fig. 4c). This is similar to probenecid effect in terms of compatibility with intracellular inhibition, but the IC50 of LF1 is an order of magnitude lower than of the established TAS2R16 inhibitor probenecid. Predicted interaction maps of LF1 and probenecid with TAS2R16, generated using Boltz-2, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. In contrast, LF22 did not inhibit salicin-induced activation of TAS2R16 (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

Our study was motivated by our recent Cryo-EM finding of an intracellular binding site in TAS2R14 [24]. Although 22 structures of TAS2R receptors have been solved to date, none have been determined in complex with antagonists, and previous antagonist studies were conducted without reference to the dual-pocket structural framework. As a result, the structural basis for bitter taste receptor-antagonist interactions remains largely unknown. Here, by combining mutagenesis with functional assays targeting residues in both the extracellular and intracellular binding pockets, we mapped antagonist behavior onto the two structurally defined regions of the receptor. This approach enabled a distinction between predominantly intracellular and dual-site antagonists, opening new avenues for antagonist development.

Our results show that LF1 and probenecid depend primarily on the intracellular pocket for inhibition. Both antagonists inhibited WT TAS2R14, and this inhibition persisted in the upper-site mutants S65A2.60 and S265A7.38, indicating that the extracellular pocket is not essential for their activity. In contrast, mutations in the lower pocket produced ligand-specific effects. The S194A5.54 mutation abolished inhibition by probenecid yet did not impair LF1 inhibition, which remained comparable to or slightly stronger than in the wild type receptor. In contrast, H276F7.49 eliminated inhibition by both antagonists, demonstrating that this intracellular position is essential for their action. LF22 displayed a different pattern. It inhibited WT TAS2R14, yet its activity was lost in both S265A7.38 (upper-site) and S194A5.54 (lower-site) mutants, suggesting that LF22 relies on interactions in both pockets.

The Schild analysis supports LF1 as a stronger antagonist than LF22 and indicates that LF1 and LF22 do not act as simple competitive antagonists. The reduction in maximal response observed at higher antagonist concentrations is consistent with inhibition that involves intracellular interactions rather than exclusive competition at a single extracellular site. These functional features align with the mutagenesis data, which show a dominant role for the intracellular pocket in LF1 inhibition and combined pocket contributions for LF22. Together, the Schild analysis strengthens the model in which antagonist binding to the intracellular region modulates receptor signaling in a manner that cannot be explained by classical competitive antagonism.

Boltz-2 co-folding simulations provided an independent computational framework that is consistent with the functional and mutagenesis data. Because Boltz-2 performs unbiased ligand placement without pre-defining a binding pocket, it allows assessment of preferred binding regions across multiple potential pockets. Across five predictions per ligand, LF1 and probenecid were repeatedly positioned in the intracellular pocket of TAS2R14, while LF22 yielded poses in both top and bottom binding site. Together, these results reveal distinct antagonist classes, with LF1 and probenecid acting mainly through the intracellular pocket while LF22 depends on both pockets.

Because probenecid inhibited both TAS2R14 and TAS2R16, we asked whether this cross-receptor inhibition extends to additional antagonists. We examined probenecid, LF1 and LF22 effects on TAS2R16 activation by salicin. Probenecid and LF1 inhibited TAS2R16, and both showed reduced activity in the intracellular N96T3.43 mutant, mirroring their dependence on intracellular interactions in TAS2R14. LF22, which was the only one out of the tested antagonists that was sensitive to top site mutations in TAS2R14, did not inhibit TAS2R16.

Together, our findings show that the more potent LF1 and the weaker probenecid inhibit both TAS2R14 and TAS2R16. For LF1, the IC₅₀ values were 18.0 ± 0.50 µM for TAS2R14 and 32 ± 21 µM for TAS2R16, indicating comparable inhibitory potency for the two receptors, with a slight preference for TAS2R14. Probenecid inhibited TAS2R14 with an IC₅₀ of 0.60 ± 0.70 mM, and TAS2R16 with 0.77 ± 0.33 mM.

The identification of intracellularly acting antagonists may also carry physiological implications. In this context, endogenous intracellular molecules could potentially modulate TAS2R14 activity through binding to this site. Because the intracellular pocket is located adjacent to the G protein interface, ligand engagement at this site could influence receptor-G protein coupling efficiency rather than solely competing with extracellular agonists. Such modulation may affect signalling strength or duration. Given the expression of TAS2R14 in multiple extraoral tissues, intracellular inhibition could shape local cellular responses. Interestingly, TAS2R14 has been reported to exhibit agonist-dependent internalization [46], suggesting that intracellular interactions may contribute to its regulation and trafficking.

More broadly, our work highlights that TAS2Rs can host antagonists at intracellular sites. Antagonists may rely mainly on the bottom pocket or require combined top and bottom interactions. This may have implications for designing compounds with either wide or receptor-specific activity [5, 47, 48]. Targeting the intracellular pocket represents an alternative strategy that may suppress signaling downstream of ligand binding. The differential cross-reactivity observed here, where dual site targeted LF22 inhibited TAS2R14 but not TAS2R16, while the intracellularly targeted LF1 and probenecid inhibited both, suggests that pocket preference may be related to selectivity. Although limited to the receptors examined in this study, these findings provide practical considerations for future development of bitter blockers or TAS2R modulators with defined selectivity profiles.
