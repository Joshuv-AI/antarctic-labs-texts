Family Caregivers' Experiences With Follow‐Up Healthcare for Frontotemporal Dementia: A Narrative Inquiry
Authors: Hege Rasmussen Eid, Kristin Berre Ørjasæter, Ingunn Bosnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionFrontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a neurodegenerative disease that often causes young‐onset dementia and affects patients' behaviour and personality. Although FTD significantly burdens patients' family caregivers, their experiences with follow‐up health care services remain poorly understood.AimIn our study, we explored how family caregivers of patients with FTD have experienced follow‐up health care for FTD, particularly their involvement in, influence over and support received during the pre‐ and post‐diagnostic stages.MethodsWe conducted a narrative inquiry to explore family caregivers' experiences with follow‐up health care services from their own perspectives.ResultsFamily caregivers reported that barriers to effective, individualised follow‐up health care include patients' impaired insight due to FTD symptoms, inadequate competence and coordination between levels of care, and their own limited opportunities to participate and be recognised for their knowledge and familiarity with the patients.ConclusionFollow‐up health care for FTD, beginning at diagnosis, should involve both health care professionals and family caregivers to ensure comprehensive support and address FTD's unique challenges. Our findings highlight the need for health care professionals to balance the patient's right to make decisions and the inclusion of family caregivers in the care process as crucial providers of person‐centred care.

Introduction

Worldwide, dementia is a leading cause of disability and mortality among older adults, one that imposes an extraordinarily high cost on societies and a massive burden on patients, family caregivers and health care systems [1]. Most people who suffer from dementia are cared for at home by informal caregivers, including family members, friends and/or neighbours [2]. Among them, family caregivers are cornerstones of the support provided to people with dementia across all stages of the disease [3]. Globally, the recommended approach to care for people with dementia is integrated, person‐centred care [4]. Because person‐centred care is achieved in light of interpersonal knowledge of the individual being cared for, family caregivers' knowledge of the people with dementia is an important resource for health care professionals [5]. As dementia progresses, several health care professionals may become involved in the care, and their continued collaboration with family caregivers is important to achieve integrated, person‐centred care [6, 7].

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a neurodegenerative disease that predominantly affects people 45–65 years old [8] but may affect people who are younger [9] or older [10] as well. FTD causes the progressive atrophy of the brain's frontal and/or temporal lobes and is characterised by neuropsychiatric and neuropsychological symptoms [11, 12]. Atrophy in the frontal lobe causes changes in behaviour, personality and emotional regulation (i.e., behavioural‐variant FTD), where early symptoms often include behavioural disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive or ritualistic behaviours and changes in eating habits. These symptoms are frequently accompanied by executive dysfunction such as impairments in planning, problem‐solving, mental flexibility and abstract reasoning [13]. Atrophy of the temporal lobes causes language‐variant FTD, primary progressive aphasia with the agrammatic variant (i.e., avPPA) or semantic variant (i.e., svPPA) where the main symptoms are language deficits [12]. Although deficits in insight manifest early in people with FTD [14, 15], memory is mostly spared until the disease's later stages [16]. At present, no pharmacological treatment exists for FTD [17, 18].

FTD can also overlap with motor neuron diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Approximately 15% of ALS patients develop FTD, typically with symptoms such as executive dysfunction, reduced verbal fluency, apathy and diminished empathy. The overlap between ALS and FTD reflects shared genetic and pathological mechanisms [19].

Most people with FTD, as with other types of dementia, are cared for at home by family caregivers, including family members, partners or spouses, before being placed in institutional care [20]. Being a family caregiver for someone with FTD is particularly burdensome, often due to the disease's young onset, early changes in patients' behaviour and personality and delays in diagnosis [21, 22]. In many cases, people with FTD also resist assessment [20, 23, 24], and their symptoms may be misinterpreted to indicate psychiatric disorders [20, 25]. Their family caregivers may thus witness changes in them years before the correct diagnosis is made [20, 26]. Even once the condition is correctly diagnosed, family caregivers of patients with FTD have reported less satisfaction with the follow‐up health care provided before receiving institutional support (e.g., in‐home help and home nursing services) than caregivers of patients with Alzheimer's disease [27]. They have also reported a lack of appropriate support services and health care services' failure to provide practical advice about changes in the patients' behaviour and personality [23]. The resulting stress and burden often lead to the patients' placement in nursing homes earlier than patients with other types of dementia [27]. At that point, caring for patients with FTD is no less demanding for health care professionals, as the patients may show challenging, unpredictable behaviour and declining abilities in communication, both of which affect the quality of the caregiver–patient relationship [28].

The primary intervention for FTD is to provide support to patients and their families [29, 30]. It is essential that family caregivers receive adequate support from health services to effectively care for their loved ones at home [20]. Despite this necessity, there is a notable lack of research on family caregivers' experiences of follow‐up health care for people with FTD living at home [31, 32]. This is also the case in Norway.

This study was conducted in Norway, where national guidelines for dementia care emphasise structured follow‐up for patients and caregivers [33]. However, FTD presents challenges that may not be fully addressed by general dementia care protocols. It is therefore relevant to explore how family caregivers experience follow‐up care within this framework. Therefore, the aim of our study was to explore the experiences of family caregivers of patients with FTD, particularly their involvement in, influence over and support received during the pre‐ and post‐diagnostic stages. To that end, our study was guided by the following research question: How do family caregivers of patients with FTD experience follow‐up care provided by health care services across the stages of the disease?

Method

Design

To explore FTD caregivers' experiences with follow‐up from health care services, we employed a qualitative narrative design. Following a reflexive approach [34], we interpreted the data with reference to our combined clinical and academic backgrounds and perspectives.

Context

Norway's health care system comprises primary and secondary levels of health care, and patients with dementia can receive follow‐up health care from both levels of care. In primary health care, patients and their family caregivers receive follow‐up health care including assessments, respite stays, long‐term care services in care institutions and home‐based services (e.g., in‐home help or home nursing care). Secondary health care, by contrast, includes hospital services such as outpatient assessments and follow‐up health care for patients less than 65 years old or when a comprehensive assessment is needed for another reason [33]. When dementia is suspected, the general practitioner (GP) initiates a dementia assessment and, based on the results, prescribes a basic assessment in primary health care or a comprehensive assessment in secondary health care via referral. In this article, we focus on family caregivers' experiences with follow‐up health care from both the primary (i.e., respite stay, home nursing care and nursing homes) and secondary levels (i.e., comprehensive assessments of dementia).

In Norway, although national guidelines regarding follow‐up health care for dementia do not include specific guidelines for FTD [33], several guidelines and laws regulate cooperation between levels of health care. On the one hand, secondary health services are tasked with providing guidance for primary care, which entails advising on follow‐up health care, developing individual plans for patients and providing ambulatory services [35]. On the other hand, primary health services are tasked with providing justifiable, secure health care [36]. Despite their distinct tasks, the Norwegian Coordination Reform also stresses the importance of and need for cooperation between the two levels [37].

The Norwegian Patient and User Rights Act strongly upholds patients' rights to self‐determination and making decisions about their treatment [38]. Because people with dementia thus have the right to autonomy, health care professionals need to facilitate their ability to consent to medical care [33]. To that end, the general rule is that individuals retain their decision‐making capacity even when health care professionals (or family caregivers) doubt their ability to make decisions. However, as dementia progresses, it impairs the individual's ability to plan, make decisions and give consent, and at some point, they may be assessed as no longer having a sufficient capacity to understand information, recognise their situation, make choices or comprehend the consequences of their decisions. In such cases, the GP has to assess and document in writing that the individual lacks decision‐making capacity, as stipulated in Chapter 4, Section 4‐3, of the Patient and User Rights Act [38].

Meanwhile, according to Chapter 3 of the Patient and User Rights Act, if the patient consents or if circumstances warrant it, then the patient's next of kin should be informed about their health condition and the care provided [38]. Added to that, the Directorate of Health's guidelines state that the relatives of patients with dementia should receive tailored information and follow‐up, both of which healthcare professionals are responsible for delivering. Furthermore, if the patient lacks decision‐making capacity, then the municipality and/or specialist health services have to ensure that the next of kin are offered information and opportunities to participate before decisions about health care measures are made [33].

Participants

The sample for our narrative inquiry consisted of nine family caregivers of people with FTD, all recruited in 2016 for a qualitative study on family caregivers' experiences with patients showing early, pre‐diagnostic symptoms of FTD. All participants, consisting of husbands, wives, daughters and a brother, were more than 18 years old (Table 1).

Recruitment

The participants were recruited in collaboration with medical staff at psychogeriatric and neurological units in two hospitals in Norway. The staff received written information about the study and were asked to inform potential participants (i.e., family caregivers of individuals with FTD) about the study and gauge their interest in participating. Inclusion required a close relationship with a person diagnosed with FTD. The first author mailed each potential caregiver an invitation to participate, along with a consent form and contacted them by phone when their consent form was received.

Data Collection

The first author conducted in‐depth, face‐to‐face, semi‐structured interviews with each participant. The approach involved posing a mix of predefined questions while allowing the flexibility to explore topics more deeply as they emerged during the interviews [39]: ‘What kind of support or follow‐up health care did you and your loved one receive from the health care service?’; ‘Was the health care service's support helpful?’; and ‘What did the experience mean to you?’ The first author, in aiming for a conversational interview, adjusted the order of questions to ensure a smooth dialogue and optimise the use of time. Each interview lasted 60–120 min and was conducted at a time and place of the participant's choosing. Eight interviews were performed at the participant's home without the family member with FTD present, whereas the other interview was performed in a meeting room at a hotel in the participant's hometown. All interviews were audio‐recorded using digital equipment and transcribed verbatim by the first author.

Analysis

To analyse the data, we employed Riessman's narrative inquiry approach [40], which is particularly useful for understanding how individuals construct and convey their experiences in stories [41]. When focusing on the family caregivers' stories, we expected narrative inquiry to provide a valuable lens for exploring the depth, context and nuances of their experiences, particularly regarding their loved one's diagnosis with FTD and the follow‐up health care received, from initial concerns to the diagnostic process to subsequent support. Because narrative inquiry is time‐consuming and painstaking, it is most suitable for studies with small samples [40].

To become familiar with the data and gain insight into all nine participants' stories, the first and third authors read the transcripts multiple times. Afterward, workshops were held with all three authors to first collectively review the data, discuss overall impressions and identify key issues in the participants' stories. During those workshops, we all discussed similarities and differences among the participants' stories, with a particular focus on the challenges that they faced before, during and after their loved ones were diagnosed with FTD. Our discussion also addressed how smoothly aspects of the process went, the support and assistance received by participants and their experiences with health care professionals regarding mutual collaboration, what information about the diagnosis was provided and the diagnosis's consequences.

Per the results of our preliminary analysis, participants' roles as family caregivers were demanding and required them to be assertive in order for their knowledge and perspectives to be recognised. At times, they felt sidelined by both the patient and health care professionals, which caused frustration and worry that their loved ones were not receiving adequate care. In view of those findings, we decided that the first and third authors would draft two scenario narratives as an ideographical long narrative based on all participants' stories, which was expected to highlight the tension in and heterogeneity of their experiences as narrated by them. With reference to the interview transcripts, we revised the two scenario narratives multiple times to ensure that they closely reflected the participants' stories.

Methodological Considerations

According to Riessman, researchers are not neutral or objective but actively shape the narratives of their participants [40, 41], and that dynamic was also apparent in our study. As authors, our combined clinical backgrounds and experience in mental health services at both primary and secondary levels, along with our academic backgrounds, influenced certain aspects of the study. For one, the first and third authors' clinical experience in a geriatric psychiatric department shaped their interest in FTD and their analysis of the narratives. For another, their extensive clinical experience highlighted the importance of family caregivers as vital resources in the early stages of FTD who could furnish crucial information for early diagnosis. We also recognised our preconceived notions about the importance of early access to health care professionals' expertise and assessment and the deficiencies in the support system for FTD care. Meanwhile, the second author, an expert in narrative inquiry, brought an outsider's perspective by having no prior experience with FTD despite a clinical and academic background in mental health. Rigour was ensured by the combined expertise on our research team, which consisted of a neuropsychologist, a psychiatric nurse and a clinical social worker and family therapist.

The data collected for the study date back to 2016. Despite being several years ago and prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic and its impact on health care services, we believe that the data remain relevant today. According to a recent review, follow‐up health care for FTD should follow a multidisciplinary approach in order to achieve person‐centred care plans [30]. National guidelines in Norway specify that person‐centred care is the recommended intervention for people with dementia and that family caregivers are important contributors in such care [33]. Among other possible limitations, because the data derived from the Norwegian context, the study's relevance for an international audience is debatable. Although FTD's global prevalence is challenging to determine due to underdiagnosis [16, 42], it is considered to be the second‐most common type of dementia among younger adults [43]. A recent systematic review of studies on the support‐related needs and experiences of people with FTD, including in Western countries, concluded that support for family caregivers of people with FTD needs to be increased due to challenges in such caregiving [20]. That result suggests that our study is relevant to countries with similar health care services, including Western ones.

Ethical Considerations

Initial ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK No. 2015/847). In 2019, we sought and received approval to reuse the data, which required requesting additional written consent from participants to use their data. Participation was voluntary, and participants were told that they could withdraw at any time for any or no reason. All participants' written consent to participate and have their interviews recorded was obtained as well.

Findings

The two following scenario narratives highlight the differences and similarities in the experiences of family caregivers of patients with FTD in the follow‐up health care provided by health care services.

Anna's Story: She Was Able to Hide All the Challenges in Her Everyday Life

I persuaded her [Mom] to visit our GP. Mom insisted that there was nothing wrong with her, but I told the GP about all the changes. The GP referred Mom to a neurologist, which was a relief. Mom underwent an extensive examination, and my father and I were fortunate to talk to the neurologist alone afterward. We got the opportunity to verify whether the things that Mom had said during interviews were true or not, which allowed us to talk about all the changes and how we felt about them. It felt as if our experiences were considered; it was a relief that they were included in the assessments. The neurologist told us about the diagnosis and explained the disease to us and what was happening in Mom's brain. It was a relief to learn about the reasons for Mom's changes; at the same time, it was also frightening and sad. We were told that no medical treatment existed for the disease, that Mom was going to get gradually worse, and that she would eventually die from the disease. At that moment, I was overwhelmed; a thousand thoughts popped into my head. What would the future bring? Mom didn't seem affected at all. She didn't seem to understand, and that was devastating. After some months, Dad was exhausted from trying to care for her. I tried to step in daily to help him. The situation affected our daily lives, for instance, regarding sleep and coping with our careers. A daily follow‐up health care from the home nursing care service was arranged. Dad and I were looking forward to including the health care staff in our challenges in everyday life and to getting some assistance. However, we were totally sidelined! Mom didn't want us to be involved, and she made plans with the health care staff only when she was home by herself. She also often made sure to leave the house when the health care staff were expected to arrive. That way, she was able to hide her challenges in everyday life. That was frustrating and exhausting for us because we constantly had to try to get in touch with the health care staff to tell them how things really were. Now, two years later, Mom lives in a nursing home. She receives good care, and the health care staff does a good job of including Dad and me. But it still feels sad; she's a relatively young woman, but she lives in a small room in a residence with people much older than her. It makes us feel guilty in a way.

Analysis

Anna's story, as the first scenario narrative, delves into the experience of a daughter coping with contradictory wishes between her and her mother. It also delves into the experience of being a close family member of a person whose behaviour and personality have changed. It reveals that family caregivers often harbour concerns about potential illness when observing and worrying about possible signs of illness. However, it may take time before the affected person agrees to seek consultation with a GP and undergo further assessment [24, 44]. It also shows that the health care professionals' approach to family caregivers is important even at the early stages of FTD. This understanding aligns with another study, which suggests that FTD family caregivers need support throughout the caregiving process [24]. Anna valued how the GP and neurologist took her concerns seriously and included her in the work supporting the assessments. She felt that both professionals were genuinely interested in her perspective and that her experiences about her mother's changes in behaviour were important. She felt seen, heard and treated as an important resource in the assessments. On that topic, research has shown that health care staff should speak with family caregivers alone during the assessment period in order to gather information about their loved one's history of subtle behavioural changes [45].

Anna experienced relief, sadness and fear upon learning that her mother had FTD. It is common for family caregivers to feel relief and grief simultaneously upon learning about a loved one's grave diagnosis [24, 44]. However, it is also frustrating to sense that the loved one does not seem to take the diagnosis to heart or, as in Anna's case, does not seem to understand it or their prognosis. The difference in recognition made it impossible for Anna to share the process of fear and grief with her mother, to be a support for her and to make plans for the future in agreement with her.

Having had positive experiences collaborating with the GP and neurologist, Anna was not prepared for the health care from home nursing care not to recognise her ability to contribute to her mother's follow‐up health care. Her perspectives and concerns were not taken into consideration because her mother retained the competence to consent. From Anna's perspective, that condition negatively impacted her mother's follow‐up health care, for she perceived that her mother lacked insight and could not realistically meet her daily needs without assistance. In that phase, Anna felt helpless and was unable to maintain a role as a supporter. This aligns with other studies showing that the adult children of patients with dementia do not feel that they are consulted by health care staff about the process of their loved ones' illness or the planning of services and support [46]. Also, other studies confirm that health care professionals find it challenging to balance patients' autonomy with family caregivers' involvement in patient–family interventions [47]. If home nursing staff experience that a patient is reluctant, distancing or unpleasant, then they could find it difficult to establish a relationship, and their desire to care could wane. That outcome may lead to avoidance in visiting the patient because the nurses may feel insecure and powerless [48].

Anna was frustrated and disappointed by her mother's reluctance to cooperate with her and the home nursing care staff. Her mother's resistance severely impacted Anna's life, including her primary needs (e.g., sleep) and her personal and professional life. One explanation for the mother's reluctance to involve Anna in the follow‐up health care could be a lack of insight. Alternatively, the mother may have become more stressed about the diagnosis and about accepting assistance than Anna was aware of. A recent review has shown that even if persons with dementia are sometimes assumed to be unable to grieve because of cognitive decline, they may grieve for the person whom they used to be and for how other people see them [49]. Moreover, the mother could have had discouraging experiences with visits from the home nursing care staff. People with dementia may feel insecure, lonely and alienated during visits from the home nursing care staff if they do not feel seen or included. If a holistic approach is lacking in meetings with the home nursing care staff, then the patient may feel disempowered, lonely and aloof from a human community [50].

Ultimately, Anna's mother was admitted to a nursing home. Although the decision was a relief for Anna, it also meant that the disease had indeed progressed irrevocably. In the process, Anna's inclusion in her mother's care by the health care staff gave her a sense of security. At the same time, she felt torn between being relieved and feeling guilty about placing her mother in a nursing home. That experience echoes the findings in another study, where family caregivers describe their struggles with having a bad conscience when their loved ones are placed in nursing homes [24].

Greta's Story: It Was Difficult to Know Whom to Rely on

I persuaded my husband to get additional medical examinations for his problems. Something just didn't feel right. I repeatedly asked his GP to refer him to a neurologist. We finally got an appointment with a neurologist who, after several examinations, told us that my husband had developed FTD. For me, it felt right for him to get a diagnosis, but I became afraid of the future when I realised the seriousness of the situation. After the diagnosis, several actors were included in follow‐up health care: the GP, the neurologist, the memory clinic, and health care staff in primary health care, including occupational therapist services and the local dementia team. It was difficult to know whom to address. My husband was given a case manager who was supposed to coordinate everything and keep us oriented, but I was rarely involved in the decisions that were made. I felt powerless and wanted a new case manager; however, I was told that my husband would have to agree to that change because he still had the competence to consent. But my husband wasn't interested in receiving any assistance from anyone but me, and it was difficult to make him give consent to anything. It was also challenging for me to express the need for assistance when my husband wouldn't accept that the need existed. It all came to nothing. I felt alone in my situation. My husband started respite stays in a nursing home seven days a month. I looked forward to getting some rest and leaving the care responsibilities to someone else. However, he didn't like it there. He called me constantly on the phone and yelled at me and wanted me to pick him up. He even ran away from the respite stay several times. It was exhausting. In time, he had to be admitted to a nursing home permanently, but the health care staff there lacked expertise with FTD. He was aggressive to the staff, and they complained about it to me. They told me that he was wearing them out. A lot of what was said was hurtful. I also felt that they didn't care about him and didn't look after him properly. He lost a lot of weight, and many dangerous situations occurred. He ran away from that nursing home several times, too. One day, he left the nursing home with hardly any clothes on when it was −10°C outside. I was really scared that he would get injured, get sick, or even die because they didn't care for him or look after him. I became frustrated and disappointed and had many conflicts with the nursing home.

Analysis

Greta's story, as the second scenario narrative, illustrates the exhausting struggle of family caregivers to be recognised and respected. It also illuminates how insufficient knowledge about FTD among health care staff affects access to assessments, follow‐up health care and the care of people with dementia in general.

When Greta sensed that something was wrong with her husband, she sought out a referral to a neurologist. She did not trust the GP's examination or diagnosis because the changes in her husband were evident to her. Research has shown that family caregivers discover changes in the behaviour and personality of their loved ones earlier than health care professionals and often have to convince the GP or visit the GP repeatedly before being taken seriously, that is, referred to a specialist [24, 44]. Greta also faced the challenge of her husband's lack of concern for his own health, which, in requiring her to contend with her loved one, only complicated her situation as a family caregiver.

Greta's narrative also shows a lack of trust in follow‐up health care provided by primary health care services. Having several caregivers on the health care staff in follow‐up health care challenged Greta's collaboration with them because establishing a relationship with any of them proved challenging. In her overall experience, the health care staff did not recognise or treat her as a resource, as has been observed in other studies as well [24, 47]. Instead, Greta described being sidelined because her husband still had the competence to consent. At that point, it became difficult for her to contact the health care professionals in order to provide information about the homecare situation and her husband's status, as well as to leave messages, report her concerns and ask for assistance. Her predicament mirrors findings from another study, in which family caregivers, as reported by patients, tend to exaggerate the patients' conditions and report that the patients are downplaying their conditions [47]. Thus, Greta was left alone, helpless and disarmed.

Even when her husband began respite stays at the nursing home, Greta did not feel that the health care professionals had sufficient competence in treating patients with FTD. Before his respite stay, she felt guilty for wanting to relax and recover as a family caregiver. However, after a while, along with guilt, she experienced intense uncertainty and worry about her husband's situation, and the idea that the respite stays were supposed to give her time to recuperate vanished. Instead, they became a source of potential danger for him, a situation that persisted when her husband moved into a nursing home permanently. As a family caregiver, she described the burden of the nursing home's inability to provide personalised care for her husband and was left worrying that the lack of competence and proper follow‐up and care would result in injury or worse, even though he should be safe in a primary health care setting. On that count, research has also shown that assessments to achieve safe environments are particularly important for patients with FTD [51].

Discussion

Our findings show that effective, personalised care for individuals with FTD is hindered by several factors: lack of insight into the affected individuals, challenges with competence‐based consent, limited specialised knowledge among health care professionals, and the insufficient involvement of family caregivers. In the following, we discuss challenges in collaboration across different levels of care and between key stakeholders, including patients, family caregivers and healthcare professionals.

An important cornerstone of collaboration is agreement with and participation from the patient. However, this can be particularly challenging in the cases of FTD. A lack of insight among people diagnosed with FTD surfaces in the narratives, starting in the pre‐diagnostic phase. Lack of insight is a core diagnostic feature of FTD that manifests early in the course of the disease [14, 15]. Patients with FTD also often show an impaired theory of mind [52], described as the ability to understand other people's beliefs, motivations and goals, which can be a challenging condition for family caregivers [21, 22, 53]. The patient's lack of insight, combined with the competence to consent and laws such as duty of confidentiality, has created difficulties in accessing follow‐up health care, which can be provided only with the patient's consent unless a statutory authority or other valid legal basis is given for health care to be provided without consent. The patient may be deprived of consent based on impaired competence, either in whole or in part, due to psychological disturbances, dementia with cognitive decline and/or intellectual disability [38]. However, in cases of FTD, the patient's memory is often spared until the later stages of the disease [16], making autonomy difficult to determine [54]. If the people affected are considered to have the competence to consent, then they have the right to reject the health care provided and to prevent family caregivers from being involved in their care. Meanwhile, health care staff are bound by the duty of confidentiality [55]. This creates a barrier for collaboration between family caregivers and health care professionals.

Altogether, the various allowances and restrictions create a complex situation for family caregivers as well as health care professionals and result in a lack of space for family caregivers to give health care staff insights into the total picture of their loved ones' conditions and need for assistance. The situation also results in a lack of space for all parties to assess and discuss patients' competence to consent. However, other studies have shown that family meetings within families with dementia give family caregivers opportunities to handle the situation with their loved ones and to collaborate with health care professionals. Arguably, if a person with dementia can no longer contribute meaningfully to the family meetings, then the meetings may be held without the patient. However, making that decision presents a difficult ethical dilemma [56].

Our study revealed insufficient knowledge sharing among all stakeholders involved in the patient's care. Firstly, the family caregivers experienced a lack of knowledge about FTD in health care professionals and an unwillingness to consider their knowledge as close family members. The narratives illustrate this experience by describing situations perceived as unsafe within institutional care. The family caregivers did not experience the care as personalised, tailored, or adequately ensuring the patients' safety. The lack of knowledge in health care professionals may be explained by the fact that FTD is a rare, poorly understood disease, even among specialised professionals [27], and its symptoms are often unfamiliar to staff in primary health care [9]. Our results underscore how a family caregiver's knowledge of the patient's cognitive and physical abilities as well as behaviour is important and should be included in assessments and in planning care [57].

Our findings also indicate a lack of knowledge exchange between secondary health care and the primary health care services. A recent scoping review has stressed that achieving safe, person‐centred care for patients with FTD in nursing homes or home‐based care requires particular competence among health care staff regarding how to collaborate with specialists and family caregivers [58]. This is also underlined by another study which emphasises the crucial role of interdisciplinary collaboration in providing optimal care for individuals with FTD. This review highlights the importance of a coordinated approach, in which specialised professionals jointly plan regular follow‐up consultations every 3–6 months. The multidisciplinary effort enables comprehensive evaluations of caregiver burden and facilitates discussions around complex issues such as financial planning, medical treatment, assessments, end‐of‐life decisions and competence for consent [59]. Proper care for people with FTD depends not only on clinical expertise, but also on close cooperation across professional boundaries and specialised support for family caregivers [23, 24]. Providing information for the entire family can benefit attempts to cope with the patient's behaviour and interactions with primary health care services, which could also postpone premature institutionalisation [27].

Because FTD can overlap with motor neuron diseases such as ALS [60], it is of interest to look into the Norwegian guidelines regarding follow‐up health care for ALS. Such follow‐up health care has concrete guidelines when it comes to collaboration between the primary and secondary levels of health care, the latter of which is required to provide a coordinator for the patient and all parties involved in follow‐up. Therein, systematic collaboration between the coordinator and the patient's GP and family caregivers is mentioned in particular. Primary health care is also required to offer a coordinator and a cross‐sectional team that includes both the patient and the family caregiver [61].

Clear guidelines for the follow‐up care of patients with FTD may enhance the quality of support for both patients and their families. However, it is crucial to maintain a reflective and adaptive approach, given the complexity and individual variability associated with FTD. The condition presents unique challenges that may not be adequately addressed through rigid protocols alone. A recent scoping review by Österholm et al. [6] highlights the importance of complementing guidelines with flexibility and responsiveness from all stakeholders involved in collaborative care, to ensure truly person‐centred practices. Moreover, Tookey et al. [20] emphasise the role of communication and relationship‐building with both patients and family caregivers who often face a distinct form of loss as essential components in tailoring follow‐up health care to the lived experience of FTD.

Conclusion

Our study has illuminated the challenges associated with follow‐up health care for FTD. Among the barriers faced by family caregivers, a significant concern is their limited inclusion by and access to health care staff. When individuals affected by FTD decline the involvement of family caregivers while still demonstrating the competence to consent, health care staff need to preserve the patient's autonomy while upholding their own duty to confidentiality. The consequences of inadequate involvement from family caregivers are twofold: increased burden on caregivers and potential risks to person‐centred, individualised care. FTD differs from other forms of dementia due to features that require adapting guidelines for follow‐up health care.

Implications

In Norway's case, we propose that follow‐up health care should be initiated when FTD is diagnosed, in a meeting involving representatives of both secondary and primary health care, the family caregiver(s) and the patient. To ensure a person and family centred approach, it is essential to bring together the family caregivers deep understanding of the individual living with FTD, with the specialist's expertise on the disease, its symptoms and progression, along with the primary care services insight into local support and available resources. We recommend having such collaboration coordinated by a case manager, initiated immediately after diagnosis and continued throughout the disease's progression. Arguably, follow‐up health care for FTD should be organised and described in the national guidelines similarly to follow‐up for ALS. Also, the family caregivers should be offered regular follow‐up from the primary health care service concerning their experiences and challenges in providing care for the person with FTD. This may contribute to ensuring person‐centred and safe follow‐up care throughout the course of the disease.
