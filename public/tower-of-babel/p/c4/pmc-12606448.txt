Serum Neurofilament Light Is Associated With Future Disease Activity in Clinically Isolated Syndrome and Early Multiple Sclerosis Only After Non‐Optic Neuritis Onset
Authors: Philipp Klyscz, Klemens Ruprecht, Lina Carlotta Anderhalten, Tanja Schmitz‐Hübsch, Jens Kuhle, Tatiana Usnich, Judith Bellmann‐Strobl, Friedemann Paul, Patrick Schindler, Susanna Asseyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWe compared the prognostic value of serum neurofilament light chain (sNFL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP) for clinical and radiologic disease activity among patients with clinically isolated syndrome or early multiple sclerosis (MS) with optic neuritis (pwON) and non‐optic neuritis (pwNON) as the first manifestation.MethodsPatients within 7 months (pwON and pwNON) from disease onset and patients with MS with a disease duration longer than 12 months (pwMS) as controls were included. sNFL and sGFAP were analyzed at baseline and at follow‐ups using SIMOA technology. Linear mixed models and Cox regression analyses were applied.ResultsWe included 165 samples of 86 patients (18 pwON, 46 pwNON, 21 pwMS). Median follow‐up time was 80 months. Mean sNFL z scores were higher in pwNON (1.06) than pwON (0.53) and pwMS (0.94). In pwNON, but not pwON, higher sNFL z scores were associated with an elevated risk for a subsequent attack (pwNON: HR 1.63 [95% CI 1.12 to 2.27], p = 0.005; pwON: HR 0.80 [95% CI 0.51–2.27], p = 0.318) and new MRI T2 lesions (pwNON: HR 1.66 [95% CI 1.31 to 2.11], p < 0.001; pwON: HR 1.16 [95% CI 0.82 to 1.63], p = 0.404). sGFAP z scores were associated with a lower risk of a subsequent attack in pwON (HR 0.34 [95% CI 0.12 to 0.98], p = 0.047).ConclusionsNFL but not sGFAP predicted future clinical and MRI disease activity only in pwNON, potentially suggesting that the prognostic value of sNFL may depend on the type of the first manifestation.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is the most common demyelinating disease of the central nervous system [1]. In 25% of patients with MS, optic neuritis (ON) is the first clinical manifestation [2]. Other first clinical manifestations of MS include sensory, motor, and cerebellar symptoms [3, 4]. Patients with a first onset of clinical symptoms that were attributed to a demyelinating episode but who do not fulfill the current criteria for diagnosis of MS are diagnosed with a clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) [5]. Conversion rates of CIS to MS are dependent on several factors. The presence of inflammatory lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) specific oligoclonal bands (OCB) are the strongest predictors of conversion from CIS to MS [6, 7]. While the presence of infratentorial [8] and spinal cord lesion [9] increases the risk of early conversion to MS, former studies hinted towards a lower risk of a second relapse in patients with ON as a first onset [10, 11]. However, recent studies, which adjusted for the presence of inflammatory lesions in MRI, did not confirm these results [7]. As early initiation of immunosuppressive treatment delays subsequent attacks and disability accrual [12], biomarkers identifying patients that have a higher risk of future disease activity are an important clinical need.

In recent years, neurofilament light (NFL) has been extensively studied as a prognostic marker in CIS and MS. Serum NFL (sNFL) levels are associated with disease activity (i.e., clinical relapses [13] and MRI lesion formation [14]), disability worsening [15] and failure to immunosuppressive treatments [16]. Further, reference values of sNFL with regard to age and body mass index (BMI) have been established (z scores) [17]. Another emerging biomarker is serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP), which has been shown to be associated with greater T2 lesion volume on MRI and disability progression in people with MS [18, 19].

The increase of sNFL during an acute attack is dependent on the extent of tissue damage and the anatomical lesion location [20]. Spinal cord and infratentorial lesions may lead to a more pronounced rise of sNFL levels [21]. In patients with ON, sNFL concentrations showed no relevant dynamic changes, potentially due to a lower extent of tissue inflammation during ON compared to attacks in other locations of the central nervous system (CNS) or limited sample sizes [22]. For sGFAP, higher levels have been shown in patients with acute aquaporin 4‐IgG associated ON [23]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no systematic analysis of sNFL or sGFAP has been performed in patients with CIS or early MS with or without ON as a first manifestation.

Thus, we conducted a retrospective, longitudinal analysis (1) to compare sNFL and sGFAP levels between patients with CIS or early MS and ON (pwON) and non‐ON (pwNON) as first manifestation, as well as patients with relapsing–remitting MS with a disease duration of more than 12 months (pwMS) as the comparator group, and (2) to evaluate the capacity of sNFL and sGFAP in pwON and pwNON for the prediction of a subsequent attack or new inflammatory lesion in MRI.

Methods

Study Cohort

Data of study participants of two prospective cohort studies (CIS‐cohort, BERLimmun) at Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin were included between 2011 and 2024. Details regarding these trials can be found elsewhere [24]. Briefly, patients with CIS or MS diagnosed according to the 2017 McDonald diagnostic criteria [5] with only one prior attack and disease onset within 12 months were eligible to participate in the CIS‐cohort. In BERLimmun, any patient with a diagnosis of CIS, MS, or other demyelinating disorders of the CNS could be included. For the current analysis, patients with CIS or early MS with a first study visit within 7 months from disease onset and grouped according to the phenotype of first disease manifestation being ON were included (pwON and pwNON). Additionally, we included a random set of cross‐sectional data of pwMS with a disease duration of more than 12 months as a control group. Exclusion criteria for the current analysis were diagnosis of progressive MS or a diagnosis other than MS.

Follow‐ups were performed 6 months after study inclusion and thereafter annually. Clinical data comprising demographic information, disease duration, medical history, information regarding previous and recent attacks, and prescription of disease‐modifying therapies were assessed at study entry and all follow‐ups. Furthermore, all patients received a neurological examination according to the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) and MRI of the brain at baseline and all follow‐ups.

Conventional MRI data were acquired at baseline and follow‐up using a 3 Tesla Siemens scanner (MAGNETOM Trio Tim and MAGNETOM Prisma; Erlangen, Germany), including an isotropic T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) and 3D fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence. T2‐hyperintense brain lesions were automatically segmented using the Lesion Segmentation Toolbox for SPM12, based on coregistered MPRAGE and FLAIR images. The resulting lesion maps were subsequently manually corrected using ITK‐SNAP (www.itksnap.org) to ensure anatomical accuracy. MRI with contrast agent administration was only performed at baseline in pwON and pwNON.

Laboratory Measurements

Sera of included pwON (mean 132 days from previous attack) and pwNON (mean 139 days from previous attack) at baseline and, if available, at 6 and 18‐month follow‐up and of pwMS (mean 1797 days from previous attack) were analyzed. sNFL/sGFAP levels were determined using single molecule assay (SIMOA) neurology 2‐Plex B (N2PB, Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) at the University Hospital Basel, Switzerland. Samples were shipped frozen on dry ice. The specific laboratory procedures for measuring sNFL and sGFAP are described elsewhere [18, 25]. sNFL and sGFAP values were transformed to z scores to adjust for age, BMI and sex for sGFAP [17, 26]. Z scores represent continuous measures in reference to a healthy control group (expressed in standard deviations [SD]). Therefore, a sNFL/sGFAP z score of 0 represents the 50th percentile of a reference group of healthy controls and a z score of 1 represents the deviation of 1 SD from the mean of the reference group with adjustment for age and BMI (i.e., 84th percentile) [17].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0). Categorical variables are described in absolute numbers and relative percentages; nonnormally distributed continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), and normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Disease group differences of sNFL and sGFAP were evaluated using linear mixed models to account for multiple measurements of individual patients. sNfL and sGFAP z scores were included separately as dependent variables and adjusted for MRI T2 cerebral lesion count and the number of days between prior attack onset and date of sampling (“lme4” package).

Further, generalized linear mixed models were employed to assess the association between MRI lesions (T2 lesion number, T2 lesion volume, number of gadolinium‐enhancing lesions) and sNFL/sGFAP levels. For group differences, estimated marginal means (EMM, “emmeans” package) and for association analyses between continuous and noncategorical variables β and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported (“stats” package).

The value of sNFL/sGFAP z scores for predicting future relapses or new MRI T2 lesions in pwON and pwNON was evaluated using Cox proportional hazard regression with time‐varying covariates (sNFL/sGFAP) adjusted for sex and clustering for multiple intraindividual measurements of each patient (“survival” package). Time intervals between different sNFL/sGFAP measurements for each patient were calculated.

Additionally, in 52 patients who had more than one sNFL/sGFAP measurement, the temporal evolution of sNFL/sGFAP was evaluated using linear mixed models. Hereafter, the predictive value of changes of consecutive sNFL/sGFAP levels between baseline and second measurement adjusted for baseline sNFL/sGFAP were analyzed using regular Cox regression analysis. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported. Adjusted survival curves based on the Cox regression models with time‐varying predictor variables (sNFL z scores) were plotted (“ggsurvfit” and “survminer” packages) using dichotomized sNFL z scores with an arbitrary threshold of 1.5 (i.e., 1.5 SD higher than mean). No dichotomization of sGFAP z‐scores in pwON and pwNON was performed due to the small number of patients with a sGFAP z score above 1.5. Lastly, we examined the influence of a combined analysis of sNFL/sGFAP on the risk of subsequent attack and new MRI T2 lesions. The detailed description and the results of the analysis can be found in the supplement (Tables S1 and S2). p values < 0.05 were defined as statistically significant. No correction for multiple testing due to the exploratory and retrospective nature was performed. All interpretations were based on effect sizes and need to be treated cautiously.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 166 serum samples of 87 patients (47 pwNON, 18 pwON, 22 pwMS) were available. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Mean age across the entire cohort was 34 years (SD 8 years) and two‐thirds of patients were female (64%). No relevant differences between pwNON and pwON were seen regarding disease duration, MRI T2 lesion count, and MRI T2 lesion volume. As expected, pwMS had a higher number of previous attacks, longer disease duration, and a higher MRI T2 lesion count than pwON and pwNON (Table 1).

Group Differences of sNFL/sGFAP Between pwON, pwNON, and pwMS

sNFL z scores were numerically higher in pwNON compared to pwON (pwNON vs. pwON: 1.05 vs. 0.53, p = 0.056; Figure 1A). By comparison, sNFL z scores of pwMS were closer to pwNON (pwNON vs. pwMS: 1.05 vs. 0.94, p = 0.753) than to pwON (pwON vs. pwMS: 0.53 vs. 0.94, p = 0.317). A subgroup analysis including only samples from the first study visit yielded a similar difference in sNFL z score between pwNON and pwON (1.33 vs. 0.76, p = 0.069).

sGFAP z scores did not differ relevantly between pwON and pwNON (0.12 vs. −0.05, p = 0.493, Figure 1B). Further, pwMS had numerically higher sGFAP z scores than pwNON (pwMS vs. pwNON: 0.47 vs. −0.05, p = 0.111) and pwON (pwMS vs. pwON: 0.47 vs. 0.12, p = 0.331). Group differences of sGFAP z scores persisted when analyzing only baseline samples (data not shown).

In pwON, sNFL but not sGFAP z scores were significantly associated with the EDSS visual functional score at baseline (sNFL: β = 0.60 [95% CI 0.15 to 1.06], p = 0.012; sGFAP: β = 0.57 [95% CI −0.04 to 1.18], p = 0.063). Additionally, baseline sNFL and sGFAP were predictive for long‐term visual function at 6‐month follow‐up (sNFL: β = 1.01 [95% CI 0.37 to 1.65], p = 0.005; sGFAP: β = 0.92 [95% CI 0.06 to 1.77], p = 0.038). The results of the analysis between sNFL/sGFAP z scores and the other EDSS subscores are summarized in Table S3.

Association Between sNFL/sGFAP and MRI Parameters

sNFL z scores were not associated with MRI T2 lesion counts in pwON (β = 0.01 [95% CI −0.10 to 0.12], p = 0.901) and in pwNON (β = −0.02 [95% CI −0.07 to 0.04], p = 0.534). However, in pwMS sNFL z scores were clearly associated with MRI T2 lesion counts (β = 0.26 [95% CI 0.19 to 0.32], p < 0.001). Furthermore, no association between sGFAP z score and MRI T2 lesion counts was shown in pwON (β = −0.03 [95% CI −0.16 to 0.06], p = 0.648). In pwNON, sGFAP z scores were numerically associated with MRI T2 lesion number (β = 0.08 [95% CI −0.00 to 0.16], p = 0.055). sGFAP z scores were also associated with MRI T2 lesion counts in pwMS (β = 0.63 [95% CI 0.54 to 0.72], p < 0.001).

No clear association was seen between sNFL or sGFAP z scores and MRI T2 lesion volume for any disease group (Figure S1).

Gadolinium was only administered at baseline in pwON and pwNON. Here, sNFL z scores were associated with the number of gadolinium‐enhancing cerebral lesions in pwNON (β = 1.54 [95% CI 0.82 to 2.48], p < 0.001) but not in pwON (β = −0.26 [95% CI −1.13 to 0.41], p = 0.448). On the other hand, lower sGFAP z scores were potentially associated with higher numbers of gadolinium‐enhancing lesions in pwON (β = −0.76 [95% CI −1.68 to 0.06], p = 0.081) but not in pwNON (β = −0.01 [95% CI −0.92 to 0.85], p = 0.982).

Temporal Evolution of sNFL/sGFAP in pwON and pwNON

Fifty‐two patients (14 pwON, 38 pwNON) had two sNFL/sGFAP measurements and 27 patients had three sNFL/sGFAP (10 pwON, 17 pwNON) measurements available for analysis. The mean decline of sNFL z scores in pwON was 0.11 per month between baseline and 6‐month follow‐up and 0.02 per month between 6‐month and 18‐month follow‐up. In pwNON, the mean decline of sNFL z scores was 0.10 per month between baseline and 6‐month follow‐up and 0.01 between 6‐month and 18‐month follow‐up. sNFL decrease was associated with increasing time since disease manifestation in pwON (β = −0.03 [95% CI −0.06 to −0.00], p = 0.039) and pwNON (β = −0.03 [95% CI −0.05 to −0.01], p = 0.018).

For sGFAP z scores, the mean decline in pwON was 0.05 per month between baseline and 6‐month follow‐up while sGFAP levels remained stable between 6‐month and 18‐month follow‐up. In pwNON, sGFAP z scores declined 0.03 per month between baseline and 6‐month follow‐up and showed an increase of 0.02 per month between 6‐month and 18‐month follow‐up. No relevant association between sGFAP z scores and time since first disease manifestation in pwON (β = −0.02 [95% CI −0.03 to 0.02], p = 0.465) or pwNON (β = −0.00 [95% CI −0.02 to −0.02], p = 0.632) was present.

Prediction of Future Disease Activity Through sNFL/sGFAP

Median follow‐up time in pwON was 69 months (IQR 35 months) and in pwNON 82 months (IQR 73 months). sNFL z scores were positively associated with an increased risk of a subsequent attack in pwNON (HR 1.63 [95% CI 1.12 to 2.27], p = 0.005) but not in pwON (HR 0.80 [95% CI 0.51–2.27], p = 0.318). The interaction term (i.e., difference between slopes) between sNFL z scores and patient group (pwON/pwNON) on the risk of a new attack was significantly different (HR 2.02 [95% CI 1.14 to 3.58], p = 0.015).

Additionally, in pwNON but not pwON, sNFL z scores were associated with a higher risk for new MRI T2 lesions (pwNON: HR 1.66 [95% CI 1.31 to 2.11], p < 0.001; pwON: HR 1.16 [95% CI 0.82 to 1.63], p = 0.404; interaction term: HR 1.48 [95% CI 1.00 to 2.17], p = 0.048). Dichotomization of sNFL z‐scores into low (< 1.5, 93rd percentile) and high (≥ 1.5) yielded similar results (Figure 2).

sGFAP z scores were associated with a lower risk of a subsequent attack in pwON (HR 0.34 [95% CI 0.12 to 0.98], p = 0.047) but not in pwNON (HR 1.20 [95% CI 0.74 to 1.92], p = 0.461). Furthermore, no association was observed for neither pwON nor pwNON between sGFAP z scores and risk of new MRI T2 lesions (pwON: HR 1.25 [95% CI 0.77 to 2.04], p = 0.360; pwNON: HR 0.93 [95% CI 0.69 to 1.26], p = 0.646), respectively.

There was no clear association of sNFL and sGFAP between the difference of baseline and the second measurement of sNFL/sGFAP z scores and the risk for a subsequent attack (Table S4).

Discussion

This retrospective, longitudinal analysis of patients with CIS or early MS stratified by ON or non‐ON as a first disease manifestation and established MS as a control group showed that (1) pwNON had numerically higher levels of sNFL than pwON, (2) higher sNFL z scores predicted a shorter time to a subsequent attack and new MRI T2 lesions in pwNON but not pwON, and (3) sGFAP z scores were potentially associated with less future disease activity in pwON.

NFL is a neuron‐specific cytoskeletal protein that is released into the CSF after neuronal damage and can be detected at low concentrations in the peripheral blood [20]. In MS, sNFL levels may be elevated compared to healthy controls even in stable disease intervals and increase during an attack with a slow decline in the following months before reaching a preattack level after approximately 6 months [13]. Hence, sNFL concentrations are dependent on the extent of neuroaxonal damage shown by the association to the number of MRI T2 lesions and gadolinium enhancing lesions [16, 27]. Up to now, the temporal evolution of sNFL concentrations was not studied in relapses only affecting the optic nerves [22]. Indeed, CSF NFL levels were described as higher in attacks involving supratentorial, infratentorial, or spinal lesions compared to the optic nerve [28]. In line with these previous results, pwON in our cohort had numerically lower sNFL z scores than pwNON independent of cerebral MRI T2 lesion counts. In patients with MS or CIS, ON is typically short‐segmented and of medium severity compared to antibody‐mediated demyelinating diseases [29]. Therefore, a potential explanation for the lower sNFL levels in ON might be the lower volume of inflamed central nervous tissue.

Previous studies consistently showed that higher sNFL levels predict relapse associated disability worsening, new MRI T2 lesion and subsequent clinical relapses in patients with CIS, early MS, and established MS [17, 30, 31]. However, data regarding the prognostic value of sNFL in pwON remained sparse. Higher CSF NFL levels have been linked to poor visual outcome after acute ON and to progression to MS after isolated ON [32]. We extend these findings by showing that sNFL levels may also predict long‐term visual outcome in pwON. Furthermore, sNFL levels in patients with acute ON were associated with visual evoked potential latency delays but not with longitudinally thinning of the peripapillary nerve fiber layers or combined ganglion and inner plexiform layer measured by optical coherence tomography [22]. The absence of associations between sNFL z scores and future attacks as well as new MRI T2 lesions in pwON support these results. The exact mechanism between sNFL and future disease activity remains poorly understood. In early MS disease stages, sNFL may reflect more focal inflammation driven activity while in later stages it might indicate inflammation‐independent neurodegeneration [33, 34]. Also, sNFL levels are associated with the extent of focal inflammation during an attack [16]. Hence, a potential explanation for the absence of the association between sNFL and future disease activity in pwON is that the elevation in sNFL, which is driven by the acutely symptomatic lesion, is attenuated due to the smaller volume of inflamed tissue during ON.

In contrast to sNFL, no clear difference of sGFAP z scores between pwON and pwNON was shown. GFAP is a filament in cytoskeletons of astrocytes, released upon astrocyte damage or reactive gliosis [35]. While sNFL levels are strongly affected by acute focal inflammation [19], sGFAP is more clearly associated with relapse‐independent disease progression [18]. Contrarily, we showed that in pwON sGFAP is potentially associated with a lower risk of a subsequent attack. The pathophysiological background of this association remains to be elucidated. However, as prognostic biomarkers in pwON are still lacking, sGFAP is currently under evaluation in a large prospective trial [36].

The strengths of the current trial are the prospective inclusion of patients with and without ON as first manifestations, a long follow‐up period, continuous examination of patients by specialized physicians in neuroimmunological diseases, standardized assessments of sNFL/sGFAP measurements and brain MRIs, and a homogenous cohort of study participants.

Nevertheless, several limitations need to be discussed. Most importantly, this is a retrospective analysis of a relatively small number of patients. Still, the data of the patients were derived from a well‐described prospective cohort. Additionally, we did not have access to spinal MRI data for the current study. While pwON and pwNON only had mild symptoms expressed in low EDSS subscores, we cannot exclude the possibility of asymptomatic spinal cord lesions influencing sNFL/sGFAP levels. Finally, while the focus of this work was to evaluate sNFL/sGFAP as prognostic biomarkers in pwON and pwNON, a control group of healthy controls would have enhanced the validity of our findings.

In conclusion, in this study of patients with early MS or CIS, sNFL was associated with an elevated risk for a subsequent attack and new MRI T2 lesion in pwNON but not pwON. Further research is needed to determine the benefit of blood‐based biomarkers in pwON for disease prognostication.
