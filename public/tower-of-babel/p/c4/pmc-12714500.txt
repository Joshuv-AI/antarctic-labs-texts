Coronary Alignment With a Balloon-Expandable Valve in a Coronary Artery Anomaly

ABSTRACT

Commissural alignment during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has proven to be a highly valuable technique for optimizing the function of bioprosthetic valves and facilitating future coronary reaccess.1 However, eccentric positioning of the coronary ostia within the sinus of Valsalva, or the presence of an anomalous origin of the coronary arteries (AOCA), may result in coronary overlap even after optimal commissural alignment.2, 3, 4 When anatomical characteristics are not conducive to commissural alignment, coronary alignment has emerged as a new technique that may help reduce coronary overlap.2, 3, 4Take-Home Messages•Coronary alignment is a feasible technique with BEVs to improve coronary reaccess in the presence of anomalous coronary artery origins.•Commissural or coronary alignment can be achieved in a BEV through crimping the valve with the aid of cardiac CT planning.

Coronary alignment is preferred over commissural alignment because it improves coronary reaccess in the event that future interventions are required.2,3 Furthermore, in the case of a future TAVR-in-TAVR procedure, the risk of coronary occlusion may be increased. Therefore, coronary alignment helps to optimize the orientation of the valve frame and neocommissures relative to the coronary ostia, making leaflet modification more feasible and potentially reducing technical challenges during redo TAVR procedures.5

Although previous studies have been conducted on commissural and coronary alignment, they have been described mainly for self-expanding valves.1, 2, 3, 4 Both techniques have not been well established for balloon-expandable valves (BEVs), particularly with Edwards valve technology. We present a case involving the planning and successful outcome of coronary alignment using an Edwards Sapien Ultra valve in the presence of AOCA.

Case Description

A 79-year-old woman with hypertension and diabetes presented with syncope and angina. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe aortic stenosis. Because of her high surgical risk, TAVR was considered. Preprocedural coronary angiography revealed an anomalous origin of the right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 1, Video 1). Cardiac computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an annular area of 539 mm2, with the RCA originating from the left coronary sinus, specifically at the commissure between the left and right coronary sinuses (Figure 1). The left coronary artery height was 12 mm, and the RCA height was 13 mm. Sinus widths were as follows: right coronary sinus 31 mm, left coronary sinus 33.1 mm, and noncoronary sinus 35 mm. The sinotubular junction measured 29 × 28 mm, with a height of 23 mm. Left leaflet length was 12.2 mm. Heavy calcification was observed in the valvular plane and a bicuspid valve with type 1 morphology, featuring a raphe between the right and left coronary sinuses. During the procedure, aiming for a neocommissure at 1 o’clock according to the positioning on cardiac CT, we positioned the commissure at 7 o'clock during crimping (Figure 3). This strategic orientation ensured that upon deployment, the neocommissure was correctly aligned at 1 o'clock, thereby avoiding overlap with the anomalous RCA. The delivery system was introduced at the 12 o'clock position. We ensured that no rotation of the system occurred and maintained this orientation upon reaching the annular plane. The device was advanced without complications, thanks to the absence of aortic tortuosity. An aortogram was performed in the 3-cusp view with a pigtail catheter positioned in the noncoronary sinus (Figure 4). After the procedure, we successfully accessed both the right and left coronary arteries using a 5-F Tiger catheter, without any complications (Figure 4, Video 2). Transthoracic echocardiography performed immediately postprocedure showed no paravalvular leak and a mean gradient of 5 mm Hg.

Three months postprocedure, a follow-up cardiac CT was performed to assess coronary alignment and coronary overlap. Discussion

Coronary alignment is still not well established in BEV, especially in patients with AOCA. Although previous studies have been conducted on commissural and coronary alignment in BEV,6,7 this is to our knowledge the first reported case of coronary alignment with a BEV in the presence of AOCA with a successful outcome.

Precise orientation of the neocommissures, guided by preprocedural cardiac CT imaging, is essential to achieve the desired outcome, even in patients with AOCA.2, 3, 4 In such cases, CT imaging provides critical anatomical information to direct the positioning of the neocommissures.

Tips and tricks

Unlike self-expanding valves, where coronary alignment is achieved by rotating the delivery system (either before advancing it or at the annular plane) according to the anatomical requirements, coronary alignment adjustments in BEV are made during the crimping process, not with the delivery system. In BEV, a technique known as “neocommissural inversion” can be employed.5 In our case, if a neocommissure is intended to be positioned at 1 o'clock on CT imaging, during crimping, it should be placed at 7 o'clock, effectively rotating it 180° to account for the anticipated rotation during the advancement of the release system (Figure 3). This inversion ensures that upon deployment, the neocommissure aligns correctly with the intended position. It should be emphasized that the neocommissure placed either at the top or bottom of the crimper is the one that determines the positioning strategy of the neocommissure at the valvular level on cardiac CT. This is because the lateral neocommissures only move slightly from anterior to posterior or vice versa, whereas the central neocommissure is the most important and shows the greatest movement when advancing the delivery system.

It is important to mention that alignment success in BEV may vary depending on aortic arch/root anatomy and crimping fidelity. Anatomies such as tortuous aortas or dilated or horizontal aortas can cause changes in the orientation of the delivery system. Additionally, in cases of bicuspid valves with heavy calcification, crossing the annular plane can be challenging, requiring extensive manipulation of the delivery system, which may disrupt the positioning of the neocommissures.8

Key points of the case

In certain anatomical scenarios such as this one, coronary alignment should be preferred over commissural alignment, beyond considerations of coronary reaccess. With the increased use of TAVR in younger patients, the eventual need for TAVR-in-TAVR and the associated risk of coronary occlusion are heightened.9 Therefore, proper placement of the first valve is essential to facilitate potential future procedures. Correct orientation of the coronary ostia and neocommissures must be ensured to avoid coronary overlap. If one of the commissural posts lands in front of a coronary ostium, reaccess to that coronary artery can be very difficult. Moreover, in the event of a future TAVR-in-TAVR, coronary overlap would prevent the use of techniques such as leaflet laceration to avoid coronary obstruction.5

In our case, had we performed commissural alignment, one of the neocommissures would have landed in front of the RCA (Figure 2). By performing coronary alignment instead, we were able to free both ostia and preserve the possibility of future leaflet modification in the event of a redo TAVR.

Although coronary alignment is a feasible technique for ensuring coronary reaccess and enabling future reintervention, there is currently no scientific evidence regarding valve function or the presence of hypoattenuated leaflet thickening associated with this technique. Therefore, follow-up is necessary in patients undergoing coronary alignment to assess valve functionality over time.

This case underscores the importance of meticulous preprocedural planning and the application of advanced imaging techniques to achieve optimal coronary alignment in patients with complex coronary anatomy undergoing TAVR.

Conclusions

Coronary or commissural alignment, as with self-expanding valves, can also be performed with BEVs such as the Edwards valve, even in patients with complex anatomies such as AOCA. Proper CT planning and valve positioning during crimping are essential to achieve the desired outcome. Although this is a case with a successful result, randomized studies with the Edwards valve are needed to validate these findings.
