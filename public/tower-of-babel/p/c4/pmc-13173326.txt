Evaluation of a Pharmacist‐Led Intervention Focused on the Deprescribing of Cardiovascular and Antidiabetic Medication in Older Patients With Polypharmacy: Patients' and Pharmacists' Experiences
Authors: Jamila Abou, Petra J. M. Elders, Rob van Marum, Jacqueline Hugtenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn the Netherlands, community pharmacists (CPs) are required to conduct annual clinical medication reviews (CMRs) for older patients with polypharmacy. In practice, these reviews are insufficiently implemented, and opportunities to deprescribe inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medications are often missed. The LeMON study focused on deprescribing, enhancing CPs' awareness and ability to reduce unnecessary medication through close collaboration with general practitioners (GPs).AimThe aim of the study was to evaluate the experiences of patients and CPs who participated in the LeMON study.MethodsPatient experiences and satisfaction were assessed using the Patient‐Reported Experience Measure (PREM) and the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication (TSQM). Semistructured interviews with CPs were analysed using the extended normalization process theory (eNPT), covering potential, capability, capacity and contribution.ResultsOf 140 patients, 62 (44%) completed the questionnaires. Patients reported high satisfaction, felt their medication concerns were addressed andappreciated CP recommendations. TSQM scores were moderate to high across effectiveness, side effects, convenience andoverall satisfaction. CPs valued the online training and acknowledged the importance of deprescribing. Challenges included addressing patient concerns in the absence of symptoms and deprescribing medications initially prescribed by specialists, which could reduce CPs' confidence and patient trust.ConclusionPatient and CP experiences highlight the need for specific knowledge and training, shared clinical data andclose CP–GP collaboration to implement deprescribing‐focused CMRs routinely. Addressing patient concerns and deprescribing specialist‐initiated medications requires effective communication skills and structured local or regional protocols for multidisciplinary collaboration between primary and secondary care.

Introduction and Background

Polypharmacy, usually defined as the concurrent use of five or more medicines, mostly to prevent and/or treat a chronic disease, is common in older people with multimorbidity [1]. Once prescribed, in the absence of adequate follow‐up, the harms of the medication may outweigh its benefits and certain medicines become inappropriate. In older patients potential inappropriateness is mostly the result of age‐related changes in pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics andincreased frailty [2, 3, 4]. Hyperpolypharmacy (≥ 10 medicines) [5], and the prescribing of (potentially) inappropriate medication, further increases the risk of a wide variety of drug‐related problems [1, 6, 7, 8], including adverse events, cognitive and functional impairment, drug–drug interactions, falls and medication nonadherence [6, 7, 8].

Deprescribing, that is, the discontinuation or deintensification of medication use under the supervision of a healthcare provider (HCP) on the basis of shared decision‐making, is an important means to address inappropriate polypharmacy [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Deprescribing can be particularly beneficial for older patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and/or a cardiovascular disease, especially hypertension, because overtreatment with antidiabetic and antihypertensive medication, respectively, significantly increases these patient's susceptibility to hypoglycaemia and hypotension, which often results in hospital admission [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. However, a multitude of barriers to the deprescribing this medication has been documented [14, 21, 22, 23, 24].

In the Netherlands, over the past 15 years considerable effort has been made to address these barriers. Important steps were the introduction of the Multidisciplinary Guideline ‘Polypharmacy in older patients’ (MDRP) in 2012 and making the CMR for vulnerable older patients with (hyper)polypharmacy a mandatory and renumerated pharmacotherapeutic service. At least once a year, a patient‐centred CMR has to be performed by CPs in collaboration with the patient's GP. Moreover, in 2020, the MDRP was supplemented with specific guidance on the deprescribing of inappropriate medication [14, 21, 22, 23, 24].

Although CPs and GPs now have the resources to fully implement the CMR, this still has been done only in part, mainly due to the time required to conduct a CMR and an often hesitant collaboration with GPs. Moreover, despite the increasing number of eligible patients, HCPs do not seem to prioritize the deprescribing of inappropriately used cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication [22, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. Among other things, this has been attributed to the impact of barriers such as difficulty in determining the benefits and harms of preventive cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication in individual patients, a lack of confidence, competence and adequate communication skills, and organizational issues, which prevent CPs from fully integrating deprescribing into the CMR process [22, 30, 34].

In the Less Medicines in Older people in the Netherlands (LeMON) study (Box 1), an online training on performing a more deprescribing‐focused CMR and strict use of GP clinical data led CPs to reduce the use of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication in home‐dwelling older patients with polypharmacy, compared to regularly performed CMRs (Box 1) [31, 35]. However, because the study was underpowered as the result of the COVID‐19 pandemic, this effect was not statistically significant [31].

The present study reports on the experiences of patients and CPs who participated in the LeMON study. The results may increase CMR effectiveness and shed more light on the feasibility to deprescribe inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication in older patients with hyperpolypharmacy.

Patients participating in the study were 70 years or older with polypharmacy and a systolic blood pressure below 140 mmHg and using antihypertensive medication and/or an HbA1c level below 54 mmol/mol and using antidiabetic medication were included.

Materials and Methods

The LeMON study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [36].

Study Design

A mixed‐methods approach was used to evaluate patients' and CPs' experiences with the CMRs focused on the deprescribing of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication. The entire LeMON study was conducted between November 2020 and December 2021 in Dutch community pharmacies [31, 35].

In the LeMON study, there were two follow‐up time points: within 4 weeks (T1) and 3 months (T2) after the CMR. At T2, CPs of the intervention group were invited to participate in semistructured interviews to provide their perspectives on and experiences with the deprescribing‐focused CMR and the preceding online training as part of a process evaluation. At T2, all patients who participated in the study were asked to complete the Patient‐Reported Experience Measure (PREM) and the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for Medication (TSQM) [31, 35].

Ethics Statement

As part of the LeMON study protocol (Netherlands Trial Register Registration No. NL8082), this supplementary study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the VU University Medical Center in Amsterdam (2019.326). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Setting

The LeMON study was conducted in Dutch community pharmacies, where pharmacists play an active role in clinical care, including medication management and patient‐centred services. They have access to complete medication records through their Pharmacy Information and Administration Systems, enabling personalized care and interventions such as the CMR. CPs collaborate with GPs and nurse practitioners, especially in managing older patients with polypharmacy, following national guidelines like the MDRP [37]. Eighteen pharmacies of the BENU pharmacy chain (8 intervention and 10 control) participated in the LeMON study. BENU is the largest pharmacy chain in the Netherlands, which includes about 15% of the nearly 2000 community pharmacies in the Netherlands, and its pharmacists generally follow the same procedures in patient care. BENU provided a longlist of eligible CPs working in medium‐sized and large BENU pharmacies across the country. An important aspect of this study was the targeted selection of patients for whom deprescribing based on clinical and/or laboratory values was considered feasible. Eligible patients were identified and selected using pharmacy and general practice data extracted from the respective electronic information systems.

Deprescribing Within the Framework of a CMR

Deprescribing was implemented within the framework of the CMR, a medication evaluation method routinely used by Dutch CPs. In a CMR, which should include a patient interview, the pharmacotherapeutic treatment of a patient is comprehensively evaluated. Performing a CMR is a stepwise process applying specific tools to optimize treatment (e.g., recommending the use of alternative medication better suited to a patients' needs), identify the inappropriate use of medication and undertreatment and make treatment adjustments such as a dose reduction or the discontinuation of medication [37, 38]. Discussing the deprescribing of medication with the patient can be prompted by several factors, such as the total quantity of medication taken, nonadherence, the use of inappropriate medication, side effect and symptoms, frailty, cognitive function or changed treatment goals [37]. All CPs participating in the LeMON study had, at some point prior to the study, completed a basic training provided by the pharmacy chain on using the MDRP and performing a CMR.

Patient Questionnaires

At T2, all LeMON study participants were asked to complete the PREM and TSQM questionnaires. Because of the COVID‐19 pandemic, patients were sent the questionnaires by email, or they were contacted by phone after their consultation with the CP.

The patient satisfaction questionnaire was designed based on the validated PREM version chronic health. The questionnaire included an assessment of the overall satisfaction with the CP's service. It included nine questions on satisfaction with and the perceived usefulness of the treatment and/or advice received, trust in the CP, and communication by the CP, as well as five questions on the preferred content of the patient consultation. Answers were recorded using a 5‐point Likert scale. Two self‐composed open‐ended questions were included to obtain feedback on positive aspects and areas for improvement.

The TSQM questionnaire evaluates the satisfaction of patients with their medication or treatment across four distinct domains. Reliability of the TSQM questionnaire is good with a Cronbach's α coefficient > 0.80. The TSQM version 2 includes 11 items, which are categorized into the following four domains: effectiveness, side effects, convenience and global satisfaction. The domains are specifically designed to capture different facets of a patient's treatment experience. The scores for each domain are determined by summing the responses to the items within each domain, and the total score is then converted into a value ranging from 0 to 100 according to the manual [39, 40, 41].

Semistructured Interviews With CPs

Semistructured interviews were conducted with CPs of the intervention group, to explore their experiences with and perspectives on both the online training and the performing of a deprescribing‐focused CMR (LeMON). An interview guide (Appendix 1) was developed based on the extended normalization process theory (eNPT) [42]. The eNPT includes four constructs. The first three consist of the social‐cognitive resources that are available to HCPs, that is, potential, possibilities that are offered by the LeMON intervention, that is, capability, and social‐structural resources available to HCPs, that is, capacity. Together these three constructs impact the final construct, which indicates what HCPs do to implement deprescribing, that is, contribution. Interviews were held digitally via Zoom Video Communications, recorded and transcribed verbatim by researchers (D.H. and J.A.). After each interview, the researchers evaluated how the conversation had proceeded, whether the topic‐guide was adequate, and whether the discussion had sufficiently covered the process of the intervention. To conclude the interview, CPs were asked whether they had anything to add that had not been covered by the questions but might be relevant for the intervention and its future implementation.

Data Collection and Analysis

Data were collected in a database, Castor EDC system [43]. Patients could choose to receive the questionnaires through a web link provided by email or to answer the questions from the questionnaire in a telephone call with a researcher (D.H. and J.A.).

Qualitative data were analysed using the framework of the eNPT with MAXQDA software. In addition to examining the overall implementation process, we also explored the positive factors and barriers to implementing deprescribing in the process of a CMR. Two researchers (JA, GE) independently conducted the analysis, comparing their coding and categorization to ensure consistency and rigour. In cases where the researchers encountered uncertainties or unclear interpretations during the analysis, they consulted with two supervisors, J.H. and P.E., who have extensive experience in qualitative analysis and with the eNPT framework.

Quantitative data analysis included descriptive statistics and inferential analyses to assess patient satisfaction and experiences with deprescribing strategies. The statistical analyses of the PREM and TSQM questionnaires were performed using SPSS Statistics Version 26 (Armonk, NY, USA). To compare scores between groups, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Internal consistency of the TSQM subscales was assessed using Cronbach's α. Values ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable, indicating adequate reliability.

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ‐32) list was used to check the reporting of important aspects of the research team, study design, analysis andfindings (supplement).

Material Resources

The online training on the deprescribing of cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication was considered valuable for implementing deprescribing in the CMR. The CPs also valued the material resources available for the implementation of deprescribing at CMRs. Having access to data and information to operationalize deprescribing (GP patient record data and MDRP guidelines on deprescribing) was considered important by all CPs. Discussion

Although the MDRP provides guidance on performing CP‐led CMRs in older patients with polypharmacy, together with pharmacotherapeutic criteria and several tools [37], for years CMR implementation by CPs and GPs in daily practice has been difficult, especially with regard to the deprescribing of inappropriate medication. In the LeMON study it was assessed whether an online training for CPs on the deprescribing of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication and strict use of GP data when performing a CMR in older patients with polypharmacy reduced the use of this medication. For CPs who had participated in the training, the number of patients in whom cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication was deprescribed was greater than for CPs conducting regular CMRs, but the difference was not statistically significant [31]. Nevertheless, both CPs and patients were well satisfied with the greater focus of the CMR on the deprescribing of the abovementioned medication. CPs were also positive about the compact online training. With regard to implementing the deprescribing‐focused CMR in daily practice, CPs indicated that performing this CMR required more time than a regular CMR. Efforts to deprescribe were also often hampered by the absence of side effects and the perceived benefit of their current medication, which made patients reluctant to accept recommended medication adjustments and limited GP commitment. In addition, CPs had the impression that GPs were reluctant to deprescribe medication that had initially been prescribed by medical specialists.

Patient Perspectives

When deprescribing is recommended, it is crucial to take the patient's perspective into account [14, 29, 37]. Patients of the intervention group were generally well satisfied with the information and advice resulting from the CMR. They also indicated that their concerns and questions about their medication and its use were heard and adequately discussed. However, although still being substantial, with regard to trust in the CP, the score of the intervention group was significantly lower than that of the control group. Strong relationships built on trust and transparency are important for delivering patient‐centred care and addressing polypharmacy [9, 14, 22, 23, 24, 30, 34, 44, 45]. While overall communication between HCPs and patients was not rated poorly, some CPs indicated that limited collaboration with GPs and occasional inconsistencies between CMR recommendations and their implementation might have created confusion for patients. This may have influenced how patients perceived the role and trustworthiness of the CP [23, 24]. Indeed, discussing deprescribing can cause anxiety or make them doubt whether they should trust the CP, or the GP or specialist [9, 14, 21, 46].

For patients the advice to discontinue using certain medication can be unexpected especially when patients have no complaints about their use, feel to derive significant benefit from taking their medication and/or if clinical parameters are within the recommended target ranges. In this respect, it has been observed that in older T2DM patients, the deprescribing of antidiabetic medication was more difficult if they had not experienced a recent hypoglycaemic event or cardiovascular health problem or vice versa [32, 45] or that the antidiabetic medication had been prescribed by a specialist [14, 22, 26]. In addition, for both patients and GPs, a history of cardiovascular disease was a barrier to deprescribing cardiovascular medication [33]. However, if follow‐up clinical data were shared and if they had the option to restart, patients expressed a willingness to have their medication eventually deprescribed [46].

Whatever reasons may be involved, the doubts and eventual objections to deprescribing expressed by patients show that training in specific communication skills for CPs is needed and that a collaborative approach with enhanced GP involvement and effective communication is essential for successful deprescribing [14, 22, 23, 24, 26, 32, 34]. Wherever applicable, especially the need to lower doses or discontinue medication should be emphasized or the arguments for lowering or discontinuation clearly presented and discussed within the explicit context of shared decision making, such as the patients' future health status in relation to the risks and/or potential perceived disadvantages of using certain medicines at older age and the broader limit values for older patients that might make the use of certain medicines redundant [22, 33, 34, 45, 46, 47]. Several other aspects of health and treatment that patients might find important to discuss with the CP at a CMR were considered of similar importance by patients of both groups. However, possibly as the result of discussing the deprescribing of medication and thus meeting their wishes of treatment adjustment, patients in the intervention group expressed a lower interest in adjusting their medication.

CPs' Perspectives

CPs mostly experienced a shared commitment towards deprescribing with the GPs with whom they had collaborated in performing the CMRs. They indicated that as such the deprescribing of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication would be feasible in the context of a regular CMR, notably if GPs, in line with MDRP recommendations, routinely share clinical parameter data with CPs and provide feedback on recommendations [27, 34, 37]. However, some had difficulty recommending deprescribing in the absence of side effects and patient complaints, which, according to the CPs, were also recognized by some GPs. Several studies also indicate that deprescribing is easier in patients who have experienced side effects or other health problems [14, 22, 24, 26, 32, 34, 45]. On the other hand, both patients and HCPs sometimes entertained concerns about the consequences of deprescribing, especially if clinical parameters were within target range and there were no disease symptoms or side effects.

Deprescribing was also experienced as more difficult in the case that medical specialists had prescribed medication largely because CPs experienced that GPs were hesitant to cooperate to deprescribing if they were not the first prescriber [14, 22, 23, 25, 26, 34, 48]. GPs also reported reluctance to discontinue medication prescribed by other HCPs, particularly when the initial rationale for prescribing was unclear [14, 24]. These persistent barriers might be overcome by introducing a protocol for multidisciplinary collaboration and agreements between first‐ and second‐line HCPs involved in the prescribing of cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication, such as cardiologists, internists and diabetologists [14, 22, 25, 27]. Moreover, the involvement of elderly care specialists and/or geriatricians would strongly support CPs in the decision‐making process [12, 34, 48].

Although the MDRP contains extensive guidance on deprescribing (MDRP 2020) [14, 25, 37] and CPs participating in the study had received basic training to perform the mandatory CMRs as provided by the pharmacy chain, CPs of the intervention group indicated that the online training had made them much more alert to identify and deprescribe inappropriate medication. Therefore, they would recommend the training to their colleagues. However, full implementation of the MDRP in daily practice including the objective of focusing on the deprescribing of inappropriate medication would require more CMR time [14, 22, 27, 34]. This remark touches on the current problems with the performing of regular, mandatory CMRs. CPs can only meet this requirement by applying patient selection criteria that are much stricter than those listed in the MDRP. In view of the ever‐increasing number of older patients with hyperpolypharmacy—as in the LeMON study (32)—who meet these stricter criteria, it is unrealistic to assume that without specific measures more deprescribing‐focused CMRs will be performed in daily practice. In addition to the growing number of older patients, implementation is also impeded by the ever‐increasing workload in the pharmacies as caused by persistent staff shortages and medication supply problems [25, 34, 49]. Furthermore, general practices are also struggling with high workloads due to the interrelated problem of increasing numbers of very elderly patients with their specific problems and GP and staff shortages. However, as indicated by the evaluation, the online training could contribute to a greater focus on the deprescribing of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication at regular CMRs. The same may apply to other often inappropriately used medicines such as proton pump inhibitors, benzodiazepines and anticholinergics [14, 18, 26, 47]. Furthermore, the results of the LeMON study show that the quality of CP‐led CMRs can be improved by a better coordination of activities and further intensification of collaboration between CPs and GPs, for example, by routinely sharing clinical parameter data [14, 25, 34, 48, 50].

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess the feasibility of deprescribing cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication within the context of a CMR using CP and GP data. The use of qualitative methods, the eNPT and validated patient questionnaires provided valuable insights into the various barriers and facilitators that impede or promote the implementation of this complex intervention in daily practice. However, some limitations should be addressed. The small sample size may have affected the robustness of the results. However, by collecting quantitative data from patients using a telephone call, the response rate was very high, contributing to the reliability of the results.

Interviews with CPs in the control group would likely have highlighted potential differences and areas of concern more clearly. Furthermore, patients' experiences were not evaluated by means of a qualitative interview. As a result, reasons underlying the somewhat lower satisfaction of patients with CP recommendations regarding the use of their medication and the deprescribing of some medication observed in the intervention group could not be explored. Although pharmacies were selected that were assumed to operate in a similar manner because they were part of the same pharmacy chain, there may have been individual differences in the way participating CPs conducted their CMRs, which may have influenced the outcomes.

Conclusions

The experiences of patients and CPs participating in the LeMON study show that the CMR, led by a CP and in collaboration with the GP, as outlined in the MDRP, can be a useful tool to support the deprescribing of inappropriate cardiovascular and antidiabetic medication in older patients with polypharmacy. The evaluation emphasizes the need for specific knowledge and training, as well as the sharing of relevant clinical data and close collaboration between CPs and GPs, to routinely perform a deprescribing‐focused CMR in daily practice. Managing various patient concerns and difficulties with the deprescribing of medication initially prescribed by specialists are issues that can be dealt with by adequate communication skills and local or regional protocols for multidisciplinary collaboration and agreements between first‐ and second‐line HCPs, respectively.
