Targeting HAS2 to enhance anti-tumor immunity in pancreatic cancer via PD-L1 regulation
Authors: Chuifang Kong, Yanchun Fang, Lili Shen, Zhihua Shui, Mengjie Lv, Xinyu Zhang, Hanmeng Xu, Xunxia Bao, Pengwei Cao, Wenjing Ding, Tengfei Yu, Shengming Ruan, Juan Ran, Jiangming Chen, Juling Wang, Dongsheng Hou, Fubao Liu, Yanping Wang, Qiong Li, Daoxiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic cancer is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Recent advances in immunotherapy have shown potential for improving outcomes; however, therapeutic resistance remains a challenge. We demonstrate that HAS2 is significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancer, and is associated with poor prognosis, reduced infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, and shorter survival. Mechanistically, HAS2 stabilizes PD-L1, a key immune checkpoint molecule, by modulating K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination, enhancing PD-L1 stability and suppressing immune responses. Our study further reveals that HAS2 activates the AKT signaling pathway to downregulate March4 expression, a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase that negatively regulates PD-L1 stability, thereby promoting PD-L1 stabilization. In vivo, HAS2 knockout in KPC mice reduces PD-L1 levels, increases March4 expression, enhances T cell infiltration, and delays cancer progression. Reduced collagen deposition in HAS2⁺/⁻ tumors further underscores its role in tumor microenvironment remodeling. Targeting the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 axis through HAS2 inhibition, anti-PD-L1 therapy, or March4 overexpression suppresses tumor growth and improves survival.Subject terms: Pancreatic cancer, Targeted therapies

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a five-year survival rate below 10%1,2. Its aggressiveness is driven by both intrinsic tumor cell properties and a uniquely desmoplastic tumor microenvironment (TME)3. This dense and fibrotic TME, characterized by abundant extracellular matrix (ECM) components, serves as a physical barrier to immune cell infiltration and therapy delivery while creating a pro-tumorigenic niche that promotes tumor growth, invasion, and immune evasion4,5.

Among the ECM components, hyaluronan (HA), a glycosaminoglycan, plays a pivotal role in shaping the pancreatic TME6. Elevated HA levels are a hallmark of pancreatic cancer, contributing to increased tissue stiffness, elevated interstitial pressure, and limited immune cell infiltration7–9. HA synthesis is catalyzed by the hyaluronan synthase (HAS) family, comprising HAS1, HAS2, and HAS310,11. Of these isoforms, HAS2 is particularly critical due to its strong enzymatic activity and upregulation in various cancers, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)12. HAS2 has been implicated in driving tumor progression and therapeutic resistance by promoting ECM remodeling and immune evasion13. However, the molecular mechanisms by which HAS2 regulates immune escape remain poorly understood.

Immune evasion is a defining characteristic of pancreatic cancer14. The expression of immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), enables tumor cells to suppress T cell-mediated immune responses, facilitating immune escape15–17. Emerging evidence suggests that HAS2 may regulate PD-L1 expression and contribute to immune suppression within the TME13, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Moreover, the interplay between HAS2 and key regulatory molecules, such as E3 ubiquitin ligases, in controlling PD-L1 stability has not been fully elucidated.

In this study, we investigate the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 regulatory axis in pancreatic cancer. We hypothesize that HAS2 promotes tumor progression and immune evasion by stabilizing PD-L1 protein levels through modulation of K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination. We further demonstrate that HAS2 activates the AKT signaling pathway, which leads to downregulation of March4, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that negatively regulates PD-L1 stability, thereby facilitating PD-L1 stabilization. Additionally, we explore the role of March4 in counteracting HAS2-driven PD-L1 stabilization and its potential to restore anti-tumor immunity. This study provides critical insights into the molecular regulation of the pancreatic cancer tumor microenvironment and highlights the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 axis as a promising therapeutic target.

Result

HAS2 expression is associated with poor prognosis and immune infiltration

The HAS family consists of three hyaluronan synthase isoforms (HAS1, HAS2, HAS3) responsible for synthesizing hyaluronan, a major component of the extracellular matrix11. Among these, HAS2 is considered the most critical due to its high enzymatic activity and significant roles in physiological and pathological processes18. To evaluate the association between HAS2 expression and pancreatic cancer prognosis, we analyzed its expression in normal and tumor tissues using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Bioinformatic analysis revealed that HAS2 is significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancers (Fig. 1A). This elevated expression was further validated in paired human PDAC tissues through immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1B). Similarly, pancreatic cancer cell lines (PANC-1, CFPAC-1, CAPAN-1, and MIAPACA-2) exhibited higher HAS2 expression levels compared to the normal cell line HPDE (Fig. 1C).

Analysis of TCGA data confirmed that high HAS2 expression is negatively correlated with overall survival (Fig. 1D). To assess the role of HAS2 in tumor immunity, bioinformatic analysis across multiple cancer types revealed a positive correlation between HAS2 expression and markers of T cell exhaustion, including CXCL13, HAVCR2, LAG3, LAYN, PDCD1, and TIGIT, in pancreatic cancer (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that HAS2 plays a pivotal role in modulating immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. To validate these observations, we generated cell lines with ectopic HAS2 expression. Overexpression of HAS2 resulted in increased HABP2 levels, higher tumor weight, and shorter survival times (Fig. 1F, G). Moreover, HAS2 overexpression significantly decreased CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 1H). In contrast, pharmacological inhibition of HAS2 with 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU) reduced tumor weight, extended survival, and enhanced CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (Fig. 1I–K).

In summary, HAS2 is strongly associated with poor prognosis and immune infiltration in pancreatic cancer, highlighting its role as a key regulator of tumor progression and immune evasion.

HAS2 upregulates PD-L1 expression in pancreatic cancer

As PD-L1 is a critical mediator of tumor immune evasion17, we investigated the regulatory effects of HAS2 on PD-L1 expression. Bioinformatic analyses across multiple cancers identified a strong positive correlation between HAS2 expression and PD-L1 levels specifically in pancreatic cancer, whereas HAS1 and HAS3 showed no such association (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of human clinical samples confirmed this positive correlation between HAS2 and PD-L1 expression (Fig. 2B), which was further validated across various pancreatic cancer cell lines (Fig. 2C, D).

To determine whether HAS2 directly regulates PD-L1 expression, pancreatic cancer cell lines were treated with 4-MU, an inhibitor of HA synthesis. Immunoblotting analysis revealed a marked reduction in PD-L1 protein levels upon HAS2 inhibition (Fig. 2E). Similarly, treatment with HYAL, an enzyme that degrades HA in the tumor microenvironment, also suppressed PD-L1 expression (Fig. 2F). Conversely, cell lines with HAS2 overexpression exhibited an enlarged pericellular matrix and increased PD-L1 levels, as confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 2G, H). Overexpression of HAS2 also elevated AKT phosphorylation, a known downstream indicator of HA–CD44 signaling activation, confirming that HAS2 overexpression was functionally effective (Fig. 2H). Genetic knockout of endogenous HAS2 using shRNA selectively reduced PD-L1 protein levels in Capan-1 and Miapaca-2 cells (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HAS2 regulates PD-L1 expression, potentially through modulation of the tumor microenvironment and associated signaling pathways.

HAS2 regulates the stability of the PD-L1 protein by modulating K6- and K63-mediated ubiquitination

To examine the impact of HAS2 on PD-L1 protein stability, we used cycloheximide (CHX) to assess changes in PD-L1 half-life. The half-life of PD-L1 was significantly prolonged in cells overexpressing HAS2, indicating that HAS2 enhances PD-L1 protein stabilization (Fig. 3A, B). To determine whether this stabilization involves the proteasome or lysosome pathway, HAS2-knockout cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 or the lysosome inhibitor CQ. Proteasome inhibition restored PD-L1 expression in HAS2-knockout cells, while lysosome inhibition had no effect, suggesting that PD-L1 stability is regulated through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (Fig. 3C).

To investigate the role of HAS2 in PD-L1 ubiquitination, we analyzed ubiquitinated PD-L1 levels following HAS2 overexpression. Notably, HAS2 overexpression significantly increased total PD-L1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3E), an effect enhanced in the presence of MG132 (Fig. 3F), confirming the involvement of the proteasome in PD-L1 regulation. Ubiquitination involves lysine residues, which influence protein stability, localization, and function19. To identify the specific effects of HAS2 on PD-L1 ubiquitination, we employed a panel of ubiquitin mutants in which one lysine remains intact while all other lysines are mutated to arginine (K6-only, K11-only, K27-only, K29-only, K33-only, K48-only, or K63-only). This approach allows assessment of which linkage types contribute to HAS2-mediated ubiquitination. Using this system, our data revealed that the K63-only, which increases ubiquitination associated with increased protein stability20, exhibited significantly higher PD-L1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3G–J). Conversely, the K6-only, which affects ubiquitination linked to decreased protein stability21, also showed a marked reduction in PD-L1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3D).

These findings demonstrate that PD-L1 stability is concurrently regulated by K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination, with HAS2 playing a critical role in modulating these modifications.

March4 is a key E3 ligase involved in the regulation of PD-L1 protein levels by HAS2

The above results suggest that HAS2 regulates PD-L1 protein levels by influencing ubiquitination. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, RNA sequencing was performed on capan-1 cells with ectopic HAS2 expression, identifying 34 differentially expressed genes (fold change > 2) induced by HAS2 overexpression (Fig. 4A). Among these, the E3 ubiquitin ligase March4 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 4A). Immunoblotting assays confirmed that March4 protein levels decreased with HAS2 overexpression and increased with HAS2 knockout (Fig. 4B, C). Pharmacological inhibition of HAS2 using 4-MU upregulated March4 expression, whereas treatment with HA suppressed March4 expression, indicating that HAS2 negatively regulates March4 expression (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether March4 regulates PD-L1 protein levels, we performed IHC staining of human clinical samples, revealing an inverse correlation between March4 and PD-L1 expression (Fig. 4E). Consistently, analysis of TCGA data showed that high March4 expression was associated with improved overall survival (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, March4 expression positively correlated with the number of immune effector cells. (Fig. 4G). Immunoblotting analysis demonstrated that March4 knockout increased PD-L1 protein levels, whereas March4 overexpression reduced PD-L1 expression (Fig. 4H, I).

Previous studies have reported that HAS2 and HA signaling primarily activate the ERK, AKT, and NF-κB pathways22,23. Consistent with these reports, we observed that genetic deletion of HAS2 markedly reduced the phosphorylation levels of ERK, AKT, and p65, indicating decreased pathway activity (Fig. 4J). In contrast, direct HA treatment of Capan-1 cells enhanced the activation of all three signaling pathways (Fig. 4J). To determine which downstream pathway mediates HAS2-dependent regulation of March4, Capan-1 cells were treated with specific inhibitors targeting ERK (PD98059), PI3K/AKT (LY294002), or NF-κB (BAY 11-7082). Notably, inhibition of the AKT pathway significantly increased March4 protein levels, accompanied by a reduction in PD-L1 expression. In contrast, inhibition of the ERK or NF-κB pathways had no significant effect on March4 expression (Fig. 4K). These findings indicate that HAS2 suppresses March4 expression predominantly through activation of the AKT signaling pathway.

In vivo, tumors derived from March4-overexpressing cells exhibited reduced tumor weights and longer survival times compared to controls (Fig. 4L, M). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that March4 overexpression reduced CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in tumors (Fig. 4N). Collectively, these findings identify March4 as a key E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates PD-L1 protein levels and plays a critical role in modulating tumor immunity.

March4 regulates the stability of the PD-L1 protein by modulating K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination

Based on the preceding findings, March4 was hypothesized to regulate PD-L1 stability. To investigate this, cells were treated with CHX to determine the half-life of PD-L1. Ectopic March4 expression significantly reduced the half-life of PD-L1 (Fig. 5A), indicating its role in destabilizing PD-L1. Additionally, treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 restored PD-L1 expression in March4-overexpressing Capan-1 and Pan02 cells (Fig. 5B), whereas the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) had no effect (Fig. 5B), confirming that March4-mediated PD-L1 degradation occurs via the proteasome pathway.

To verify the interaction between March4 and PD-L1, co-overexpression experiments were conducted in 293 T cells. Immunoprecipitation of exogenous March4 successfully pulled down PD-L1, as confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 5C), and reciprocal immunoprecipitation using an anti-PD-L1 antibody corroborated this interaction (Fig. 5D). Further analysis revealed that while HAS2 overexpression increased total PD-L1 ubiquitination, March4 overexpression reduced these levels (Fig. 5E, F).

To elucidate the mechanism of March4-mediated ubiquitination of PD-L1, Flag-PD-L1 was co-expressed with March4 and site-specific ubiquitin mutants (HA-tagged, lysine-to-arginine substitutions). Pull-down assays with anti-HA antibodies demonstrated that March4 significantly decreased K63-linked polyubiquitination of PD-L1(Fig. 5H–K), which is associated with protein stability. Notably, March4 also increased K6-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 5G), which promotes protein degradation. These findings suggest that March4 simultaneously modulates stabilizing and destabilizing ubiquitination processes on PD-L1.

To further investigate the interaction between March4 and PD-L1, truncation constructs of PD-L1 were generated and analyzed by immunoprecipitation (Fig. 5L). The results showed that March4 preferentially interacts with the extracellular domain of PD-L1 (Fig. 5M). Collectively, these findings highlight March4 as a key regulator of PD-L1 stability through modulation of K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination.

Targeting the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 regulatory axis exhibits anti-tumor effects in vivo

The HAS2-March4 axis regulates PD-L1 expression in pancreatic cancer tissues, contributing to tumor progression and immune evasion. To test the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis, anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy was combined with March4 overexpression in a murine syngeneic tumor model. Overexpression of March4 significantly suppressed tumor progression, and anti-PD-L1 monotherapy further enhanced this effect in mice with ectopic March4 expression (Fig. 6A).

To explore the immune-mediated effects, the OT1 mouse system with OVA-expressing Pan02 cells was used to enhance immune responses (Fig. 6B). Consistent with prior findings, HAS2 overexpression in Pan-OVA cells promoted tumor growth, while March4 overexpression suppressed tumor progression in OT1 mice (Fig. 6C). Moreover, HAS2 overexpression significantly inhibited T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro, whereas March4 overexpression enhanced it (Fig. 6D).

To further assess the role of HAS2, HAS2+/− conditional knockout KPC mice were generated (Fig. 6E). Immunoblotting and IHC analyses showed that conditional HAS2 knockout decreased HABP2 and PD-L1 levels while increasing March4 protein levels (Fig. 6F, G). Sirius Red staining revealed reduced collagen deposition in HAS2+/− tumors (Fig. 6H), while H&E staining indicated delayed pancreatic cancer progression (Fig. 6H). IHC staining demonstrated increased infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in HAS2 knockout tumors (Fig. 6I).

In summary, the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 regulatory axis plays a critical role in tumor progression and immune evasion (Fig. 6J). Targeting this axis, through strategies such as HAS2 inhibition, March4 modulation, or anti-PD-L1 therapy, exhibits significant anti-tumor effects in vivo, presenting a promising therapeutic approach for pancreatic cancer.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most aggressive and lethal malignancies, characterized by a highly complex and desmoplastic TME24. Unlike other cancers, the TME in pancreatic cancer is dominated by a dense ECM, extensive fibrosis, and limited immune infiltration. Among various ECM components, HA, a glycosaminoglycan, plays a central role in structuring the pancreatic cancer TME6. HA accumulates in the ECM of pancreatic tumors, contributing to increased tissue stiffness, elevated interstitial pressure, and impaired blood perfusion25,26. These mechanical changes not only support tumor growth and invasion but also hinder immune cell infiltration and reduce therapeutic efficacy. The synthesis of HA is catalyzed by the HAS family, including HAS1, HAS2, and HAS3. Among these, HAS2 is the most critical isoform due to its high enzymatic activity and ability to produce high-molecular-weight HA. HAS2 expression is significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancer and correlates with poor prognosis and reduced survival. Emerging evidence suggests that HAS2 plays a pivotal role in regulating immune responses within the tumor microenvironment13. High HA levels driven by HAS2 create an immunosuppressive barrier that restricts T cell infiltration and activation. HAS2 has also been implicated in modulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, further promoting immune evasion13,27. However, the precise role of HAS2 in tumor immunity remains incompletely understood. This study elucidates the role of the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 axis in PD-L1 regulation and its broader implications for pancreatic cancer progression and immune modulation.

The upregulation of HAS2 in pancreatic cancer tissues and its correlation with poor prognosis underscore its importance in tumor biology. Elevated HAS2 expression was associated with increased tumor weight, reduced immune cell infiltration (CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells), and shorter survival times, as confirmed by bioinformatic analysis of TCGA data and in vivo studies. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of HAS2 using 4-MU effectively suppressed tumor growth, decreased PD-L1 expression, and increased immune cell infiltration, emphasizing the critical role of HAS2 in shaping the tumor microenvironment and immune landscape.

PD-L1 is a well-established immune checkpoint molecule that facilitates immune evasion by interacting with PD-1 on T cells15,17. Our findings demonstrate a strong positive correlation between HAS2 and PD-L1 expression in pancreatic cancer, as supported by bioinformatic analysis, clinical IHC data, and in vitro experiments. Mechanistically, HAS2 overexpression stabilized PD-L1 protein by modulating K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination, as revealed through ubiquitin mutant assays and protein degradation studies. Specifically, K63-linked polyubiquitination enhanced PD-L1 stability, whereas K6-linked ubiquitination destabilized the protein. Notably, the simultaneous regulation of both K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination suggests that HAS2 does not merely switch PD-L1 expression on or off but instead fine-tunes PD-L1 protein homeostasis. K63-linked ubiquitination is generally associated with protein stabilization and signaling functions28, while K6-linked ubiquitination has been implicated in protein quality control and degradation-associated processes29. By promoting K63-linked ubiquitination while suppressing K6-linked ubiquitination, HAS2 creates a dual regulatory mechanism that cooperatively enhances PD-L1 stability, thereby sustaining immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment. Such dual ubiquitin coding has been reported in other immune regulatory pathways, where multiple ubiquitin linkages on the same substrate enable dynamic and context-dependent control of protein fate.

This study also identified March4 as a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase that directly interacts with PD-L1 and regulates its stability. Interestingly, HAS2 negatively regulated March4 expression, establishing an inverse relationship between March4 and PD-L1 levels. Overexpression of March4 shortened the half-life of PD-L1 and suppressed its stability by modulating K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination. In contrast to HAS2-mediated stabilization, March4 promoted K6-linked ubiquitination while reducing K63-linked ubiquitination of PD-L1, thereby shifting the ubiquitin balance toward protein destabilization and degradation. In vivo, March4 overexpression reduced tumor progression and improved survival, likely through its ability to diminish PD-L1 levels and restore T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. The opposing effects of HAS2 and March4 on K6- and K63-linked ubiquitination highlight a tightly controlled ubiquitin-based regulatory circuit governing PD-L1 protein turnover. Similar dual ubiquitination mechanisms have been described for TRIM family E3 ligases, such as TRIM21, which coordinate multiple ubiquitin linkages on the same substrate to balance protein stability and immune signaling30. These parallels suggest that the HAS2–March4–PD-L1 axis employs a conserved ubiquitin-coding strategy to dynamically regulate immune checkpoint stability in response to tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental cues. Mechanistically, our study further demonstrates that HAS2 suppresses March4 expression through activation of the AKT signaling pathway. HAS2 overexpression led to enhanced AKT phosphorylation, whereas pharmacological inhibition of AKT restored March4 expression.

The HAS2-March4-PD-L1 axis emerged as a critical pathway regulating immune evasion and tumor progression. Conditional knockout of HAS2 in KPC mice not only reduced PD-L1 expression but also increased March4 protein levels, leading to enhanced CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and delayed pancreatic cancer progression. Sirius Red and HE staining further revealed reduced collagen deposition and improved tissue architecture in HAS2+/− mice, underscoring the role of HAS2 in ECM remodeling and its contribution to the physical and immunological barriers within the TME. Additionally, combinatorial anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy with March4 overexpression significantly suppressed tumor progression in vivo, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis.

In conclusion, this study establishes the HAS2-March4-PD-L1 axis as a critical regulator of pancreatic cancer progression and immune evasion. By modulating PD-L1 stability and immune cell infiltration, this pathway profoundly influences tumorigenesis. Targeting this axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance anti-tumor immunity and improve clinical outcomes for pancreatic cancer patients.

Method

Cell culture and reagents

The cell lines HPDE (RRID:CVCL_4376) PANC-1 (RRID:CVCL_0480), CFPAC-1 (RRID:CVCL_1119), CAPAN-1(RRID:CVCL_0237), CAPAN-2 (RRID:CVCL_0026), MIAPACA-2 (RRID:CVCL_0428), and PAN02 (RRID:CVCL_D627) were obtained from the BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China). All cell lines were obtained from the sources stated above and were routinely tested to confirm they were free of mycoplasma contamination. Cell line identity was authenticated by STR profiling performed by the supplier. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Primary antibodies against HAS2, GAPDH, PD-L1, Tubulin, AKT, Flag, HA, and p-AKT were sourced from Proteintech (Rosemont, USA), while antibodies against HABP2 and March4 were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). CD4 and CD8 antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. The luciferase reporter assay system was purchased from Biosharp (Beijing, China), and 4-Methylumbelliferone was obtained from MCE (Beijing, China).

Pancreatic cancer clinical samples

Pancreatic cancer clinical samples were collected from Anhui Medical University. The study protocol and all procedures related to human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee/Institutional Review Board of Anhui Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians. All relevant ethical regulations for research involving human participants were followed.

Real-time PCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer’s instructions, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the high-capacity cDNA synthesis kit. Quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green mix on a StetOnePlus real-time PCR system to determine cycle threshold values for target and internal reference cDNA levels. The specific primer sequences were as follows: Human HAS2: forward (5′-GTG TTA TAC ATG TCG AGT TTA CTT CC-3′) and reverse (5′-GTC ATA TTG TTG TCC CTT CTT CCG C-3′). Human GAPDH: forward (5′-CAT CAC TGC CAC CCA GAA GAC TG-3′) and reverse (5′-ATG CCA GTG AGC TTC CCG TTC AG-3′). Human March4: forward (5′-TGT GAT GGC TCG GTC AAG TGC A-3′) and reverse (5′-CAA TGA CCG TCA GAG AGA TGG C-3′).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from treated and untreated cells using RIPA buffer supplemented with 10% protease inhibitors and quantified using the BCA assay. Proteins were separated on freshly prepared 4%–10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes at 250 mA for 2 h at 4 °C. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following extensive washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent substrates were used for signal detection, and results were visualized using an imaging system.

Drug treatment

For chemical treatment, Miapaca-2, Pan02, Capan-1, CFPAC-1, PANC-1, and SW1990 cells were seeded into 12-well plates 24 h prior to stimulation. Cells were treated with 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU) at concentrations of 20 μM or 40 μM for the indicated durations. For hyaluronidase (HYAL) treatment, the same panel of cell lines was stimulated with 2.5, 5, or 10 μg/ml HYAL.To inhibit protein synthesis, Capan-1 and Pan02 cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX, 200 μg/ml) for 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, or 8 h. Lysosomal inhibition was achieved by treating cells with chloroquine (CQ) at 60 μg/ml or 100 μg/ml, whereas proteasome inhibition was induced using MG132 at 50 μg/ml or 100 μg/ml. Following drug treatment, cells were subjected to immunoblotting analyses as described in the corresponding figure legends.

Lentiviral knockout, knockout, and overexpression of HAS2 and March4

Specific sgRNA sequences targeting HAS2 (HAS2-KO1: GGTGAAAAGGCTAACCTACCC, HAS2-KO3: GCTTGATAGGCAGCGATGCAA) were inserted into the pLentiCRISPR v2 lentiviral vector (Open Biosystems) to generate HAS2 knockout constructs. For overexpression studies, the coding sequences of HAS2 and March4 were cloned into the pHR-SIN lentiviral expression vector. To generate March4 knockout cells, sgRNA sequences targeting March4 (March4-KO1: ACTTGACCGAGCCATCACAG, March4-KO3: CTTCTGTAAGGAGAAGACCG, March4-KO4: GGCAGGATGGTATAAGCACAG) were designed and inserted into the pLentiCRISPR v2 vector. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting the respective expression plasmids with psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging plasmids into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Viral supernatants were collected 72 h after transfection, filtered, and used to infect target cells in the presence of 6 μg/mL polybrene. Infected cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) to establish stable knockout, knockout, or overexpression cell lines. The efficiency of gene modulation was verified by Western blotting.

IHC staining

Paraffin-embedded human or mouse pancreatic tumor samples were sectioned into 6-µm slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was conducted using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker at 120 °C for 10 min, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H₂O₂ for 15 min. Tissue sections were then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The sections were subsequently treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and visualized using an HRP substrate kit. Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on fresh-frozen tissue samples or cultured cells. Samples or cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by staining with DAPI and secondary antibodies diluted in 0.1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Particle exclusion assay

The pericellular matrix of the indicated cells was visualized using a particle exclusion assay. Cells were seeded in 35-mm culture dishes and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was carefully aspirated and replaced with a suspension of formalin-fixed sheep red blood cells (107 cells/mL) in PBS containing 0.1% BSA. The suspension was allowed to settle onto the cell monolayer for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then imaged using a phase-contrast microscope. The cell-associated pericellular matrix was identified as a clear particle-excluded zone surrounding individual cells, where erythrocytes were excluded from direct contact with the cell surface. For quantitative analysis, the area of the particle-excluded zone can be measured using ImageJ software and normalized to the corresponding cell area to provide a relative measure of pericellular matrix thickness.

Genetic mouse models

All mice were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions in the Anhui Medical University Laboratory for Animal Care barrier facility. Experimental procedures were approved by the Anhui Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines. KPC (p48-Cre; LSL-KrasG12D; Trp53flox/+) mice were generated by breeding C57BL/6 LSL-KrasG12D and Trp53flox to p48-Cre mice that had been backcrossed onto the C57BL/6 background. Congenic marker analysis was performed to confirm the C57BL/6 background identity of the KPC colony founder mice. To generate conditional HAS2-deficient KPC mice, HAS2flox/flox mice were introduced into the KPC breeding scheme by standard crossing. Briefly, HAS2flox/flox mice (backcrossed to C57BL/6) were first crossed to LSL-KrasG12D; Trp53flox mice to produce progeny carrying HAS2flox/flox with the conditional Kras/Trp53 alleles. These offspring were then crossed with p48-Cre mice to obtain p48-Cre; LSL-KrasG12D; Trp53flox/+; HAS2 +/flox offspring (hereafter referred to as HAS2+/flox KPC). Genotypes for Cre, LSL-KrasG12D, Trp53flox, and the HAS2 allele were determined by PCR on genomic DNA from tail biopsies using allele-specific primers. Transgenic OT-I mice were obtained from the National Resource Center for Mutant Mice (Nanjing, China).

Mice syngeneic model

All mice were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions at Anhui Medical University. Experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University. C57BL/6 mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) were used for syngeneic tumor experiments unless otherwise stated. For subcutaneous tumor studies, 1 × 10⁵ Pan02 cells were injected into the flanks of 6- to 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to the control or treatment groups. In the 4-MU treatment group, mice received 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU, 200 mg/kg) by oral gavage every other day, starting when the tumor reached approximately 0.5 cm in diameter. Tumor size was measured with calipers every 2–3 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = (W² × L)/2, where V represents tumor volume, W is tumor width, and L is tumor length. Mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume exceeded 1200 mm³. For survival studies, humane endpoints were defined as a tumor volume of ≤2000 mm³ or body weight loss of ≥20%. Mice were monitored three times per week for tumor volume and body weight throughout the experiment. The maximal tumor burden permitted by the ethics committee was a tumor volume of ≤2000 mm³ or body weight loss of ≥20%, and this limit was not exceeded in any of the experiments. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism as indicated in the figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. n represents biologically independent experiments, as specified in each figure legend. Experiments were repeated independently with similar results, including those shown as representative images. Where applicable, quantified data were normalized to the control group.
