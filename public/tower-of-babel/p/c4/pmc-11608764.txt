Analysis of Failed Posterior Fossa Decompression and an Effective Revision Surgery in Patients with Basilar Invagination and Atlantoaxial Dislocation
Authors: Maoyang Qi, Yueqi Du, Boyan Zhang, Zong Xin, Can Zhang, Zhenlei Liu, Jian Guan, Zuowei Wang, Fengzeng Jian, Wanru Duan, Zan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to analyze failed posterior fossa decompression (PFD) in patients with basilar invagination and atlantoaxial dislocation (BI‐AAD). Revision surgery in these patients is challenging and has been rarely reported. In addition, the anatomical variations of the vertebral artery increase the risk of revision surgery. Here, we introduce the implementation of a new type of one‐stage posterior revision surgery, whose difficulties and effects are summarized.MethodsA total of 21 patients with BI‐AAD who underwent PFD were retrospectively analyzed in our center from November 2017 to April 2021. The revision surgery in all patients was performed through the posterior approach. The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score and the Short Term 12 (SF‐12) score were employed to evaluate the clinical symptoms and health status. The distance from the tip of the odontoid to Chamberlain's line (DCL), the atlantodental interval (ADI), the clivus‐canal angle (CCA), the diameter of the subarachnoid space (DSS), and the craniovertebral junction triangular area (CTA) were assessed radiographically. The pre‐ and postoperative results were compared by paired t test.ResultsThe data of 21 consecutive patients were reviewed, with an average follow‐up period of 28 ± 14 months. Postoperative imaging showed effectively reduced compression of BI‐AAD. No implant failure or neurovascular injury occurred. Eleven patients had vertebral artery abnormalities, but none had vertebral artery injury. All patients had evidence of bone fusion on the CT scan images within a 12‐month follow‐up period. The JOA and SF‐12 scores were significantly improved 1 year postoperatively (p < 0.001).ConclusionPosterior surgery using the technique of interarticular distraction, fusion with cage grafting, and fixation is a safe and effective revision surgery to treat patients with basilar invagination and atlantoaxial dislocation who failed PFD, which will result in good outcome.Keywords: Atlantoaxial Dislocation, Basilar Invagination, Posterior Fossa Decompression, Reduction and Internal Fixation, Revision Surgery

INTRODUCTION

Basilar invagination (BI) is a kind of bony malformation at the craniovertebral junction (CVJ), which is often associated with Klippel–Feil syndrome, C1 assimilation, clivus malformation, and odontoid deformity.1, 2 Goel divided the condition into two types: type A and B. 3 BI Type A is characterized by obvious atlantoaxial instability. A posterior reduction and internal fixation technique should be used to reduce the atlantoaxial dislocation and relieve compression of the neural structures. Surgical treatment of the posterior reduction and internal fixation is the most widely applied treatment for the reduction of atlantoaxial dislocation and the compression of the neural structures. BI Type B is characterized by stable atlantoaxial joints, usually combined with a short clivus and platybasia,3, 4 which should be treated with posterior fossa decompression (PFD). Basilar invagination and atlantoaxial dislocation (BI‐AAD) is commonly associated with Chiari's malformation and syringomyelia.5, 6 Lack of knowledge about the underlying pathomechanism of BI‐AAD and Chiari malformation may lead to misdiagnoses and inadequate surgical treatment, such as PFD without reduction or fixation. Aggravation of the atlantoaxial dislocation in the BI‐AAD usually occurs after PFD. Therefore, some patients with BI‐AAD that have undergone PFD need revision surgery. Anatomical challenges, such as occipital scale bony structural defect and scar formation after the initial operation, hinder the effectiveness of revision surgery.7, 8 Few revision surgery strategies have been reported for patients with BI‐AAD after PFD, including anterior approach by Yin, 9 anterior combined with posterior approach by Perrini and Tan, and one‐stage posterior approach by Goel and Du.1, 10, 11, 12 However, there are many disadvantages to the anterior approach, such as a narrow visual field and operating space, high postoperative infection rate, and acute bleeding. Additionally, for the single posterior revision surgery, either autologous bone or spacer grafting could not achieve long‐term stability and a high rate of bone fusion, which might lead to failed reduction and fracture of the internal fixation system.

In this study, we included 21 consecutive patients. We released the anterior tension band through interarticular distraction by the posterior approach, grafted a cage filled with autologous bone, and performed interarticular fixation. In the present work, we summarize the pathomechanism, treatment principles, and challenges for BI‐AAD after PFD and present a novel direct posterior revision technique with several advantages and favorable effects.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion Criteria

In this retrospective study, we enrolled 21 patients with BI‐AAD (mean age, 39 ± 8 years) who had undergone PFD from November 2017 to April 2021 at the China International Neuroscience Institute (China‐INI). All patients underwent posterior approach revision surgery with postoperative follow‐up of more than 1 year. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) radiographic findings of BI‐AAD and (2) symptoms aggravated after PFD. All revision surgeries were performed by a senior specialist. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, with the assigned ethics number LYS [2022]175. The hospital ethics committee approved this study, and informed consent was obtained. The exclusion criteria implemented were as follows: (1) rheumatoid arthritis, (2) infection, (3) craniocervical junction trauma, (4) osteoporosis, (5) odontosomes, and (6) bony fusion between the atlantoaxial joints.

Baseline Clinical and Imaging Data

Baseline data of all patients were collected after obtaining consent, including gender, age, symptoms, and the interval between the two operations. The clinical symptoms and quality of life of the patients were evaluated preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively by the JOA score and SF‐12 score. The data of the preoperative and postoperative CT scans were used for performing measurements, including that from the tip of the odontoid to the Chamberlain's line (DCL, normal <5 mm), of the atlantodental interval (ADI, normal 2.3 ± 2.6 mm), and the clivus‐canal angle (CCA, normal 157.92° ± 8.99°). The diameter of the subarachnoid space (DSS) and the craniovertebral junction triangular area (CTA) were measured on the MRI scan images. Fusion was defined as the visible bony bridge on the sagittal and coronal CT scans. All patients underwent X‐ray, CT, and MRI examinations 3 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. All radiologic indicators were measured by two observers simultaneously to ensure accuracy.

Surgical Procedure

Preparation and Exposure

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position with the headstock fixed. We used 20% of the body weight for cervical traction so that the head and neck were in a neutral and slightly extended position. A straight incision was made along the scar of the previous operation at the back of the neck, exposing the occipital scales and the C2 lamina. Then, the scar tissue on the surface of the dura mater is removed along the normal dura mater, thoroughly exposing the dura mater of the defect area. The occipital bone is separated downward to expose the posterior edge of the occipital condyle. If the vertebral artery is obstructed, the vertebral artery along with the surrounding scar tissue is pushed downward from the posterior edge of the occipital condyle to expose it. Subperiosteal separation was performed along the isthmus of the C2 pedicle, and the posterior margin of the lateral mass joint was explored.

Interarticular Distraction Technique

Interarticular distractors (WeGo Medical System; WeGo Company, Shandong, China) were inserted into the C1‐2 facet by the posterior approach and were then vertically rotated to open the facet. The ligaments in the front of the atlantoaxial joint were released; the radiological result was judged using an O‐arm‐based navigation system.

Cage Grafting with Cancellous Bone

The cancellous bone from the ilium was filled into A cage (WeGo Company, Shandong, China) with appropriate dimensions was grafted the cage (6–9 mm high, 9 mm wide, and 18 mm long), which was placed on both sides of the atlantoaxial joint space. Intraoperative O‐arm scanning was used to judge the proper position.

Fusion and Fixation

Occipital plate fixation is preferred in patients with C1 assimilation if sufficient occipital scales have been preserved in the previous operation. If the occipital scales have been extensively resected, appropriate screws are implanted into the C1 lateral mass/occipital condyle and C2 pedicles. An abnormal vertebral artery is sometimes encountered during operation, and thus, in the surgery we performed, we pushed the vertebral artery down along the periosteum with a nerve dissector to expose the bone for screw placement. When the C2 pedicle was too slender, the fixation system of extended C3 pedicle screws or C2 cross‐laminar screws was selected. The titanium rod was cut and prebent to the appropriate length and was then implanted into the C2 pedicle and the occipital condyle screw tail. The titanium rod tip was next pressurized forward to reduce the axial horizontal dislocation by the cantilever technique. An intraoperative O‐arm was utilized to judge the effect of the reduction of the dislocation.

Postoperative Management

Cefuroxime (2 g, ivgtt) was prophylactically given 30 min before and within 24 h after the operation (2 g, q12h, ivgtt). If the patients developed a fever with a distinct elevation of infectious indexes, a higher level antibiotic including sulbactam sodium could be used. Early postoperative rehabilitation was suggested, but small amounts of weight‐bearing activities protected by cervical bracing were allowed during the first postoperative 3 months.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the preoperative and postoperative ADI, DCL, DSS, CCA, CTA, and JOA scores, and the SF‐12 scores. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Independent sample t tests were employed for pre‐ and postoperative comparisons. A p value <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Clinical Data

The baseline data are presented in Table 1. A total number of 21 patients (5 [23.8%] men and 16 [76.2%] women, 39 ± 8 years old) with BI‐AAD who had undergone PFD presented at our institution for revision surgery. The average height and weight are 167.5 ± 6.6 cm and 55.6 ± 10.8 kg, respectively. The time interval between the previous operation and the current operation was 5.0 ± 4.4 years (range 0.7–20.0 years). One week after the initial operation, five patients had aggravated symptoms, three patients had significant improvement, and 13 patients did not perceive any improvement. At the time of revision surgery, the patients had the following typical symptoms: occipital/neck pain (7/21, 33.3%), paresthesia (14/21, 66.7%), extremities weakness (13/21, 61.9%), dysphagia/hoarseness (2/21, 9.5%), and ataxia (8/21, 38.1%). Among the 21 patients with BI‐AAD, 17 had C1 assimilation, 9 had Klippel–Feil syndrome, and 10 had Chiari malformation. Abnormal vertebral artery occurred in 11 patients (11/21, 52.4%).

Surgical data

The average operative time was 178.8 min (range, 116–273 min), and the mean surgical operative bleeding volume was 128.6 mL (range, 30–500 mL). No implant failure or neurovascular injury occurred. In cases 2, 5, and 20, a titanium plate was fixed at the posterior occipital scale because of the sufficient remaining osseous tissue. In the other cases, C1–C2 pedicle screw fixation was applied. Due to the small C2 pedicles in cases 5, 13, and 19, the pedicle screws were extended to the C3 segments to increase stability. In addition, we chose C2 lamina screw fixation in case 2, in which the C2 pedicles were extremely small.

In the 1‐year follow‐up period, the JOA score increased from 12.6 ± 1.9 preoperatively to 14.4 ± 2.0 postoperatively (Table 2). The physical activity score (PCS‐12) increased from 37.6 ± 8.6 before surgery to 48.8 ± 5.4 1 year after surgery. However, the improvement of the mental score (MCS‐12) was not obvious, and it slightly increased from 52.7 ± 6.5 before the operation to 57.5 ± 4.2 1 year after the operation. Two patients had short‐term postoperative complications (mild pneumonia) and recovered with targeted antibiotics. One patient had new limb numbness, and four patients had occipital and neck pain. The patients received comprehensive assessments, which facilitated the implementation of targeted treatment including physiotherapy and pain management. Furthermore, all patients were regularly followed up, and their symptoms were diligently monitored to ensure timely recovery and appropriate management, all of which were relieved within 6 months postoperatively.

Radiology Data

The imaging measurements demonstrated high interobserver reliability, with an ICC value of 0.992 (p < 0.001). The mean follow‐up period was 28 months (range, 12–59 months). A CT scan 1 week postoperatively showed significant improvements in the ADI of BI‐AAD (4.1 ± 0.7 mm vs. 2.8 ± 1.2 mm, p = 0.001). The CCA was also significantly improved compared with its preoperative value (131.4 ± 11.3° vs. 139.2 ± 10.2°, p < 0.001). The distance of the odontoid tip to Chamberlain's line was 9.2 ± 3.0 mm preoperatively, whereas it was 4.3 ± 2.6 mm postoperatively (p < 0.001). The postoperative MRI scan showed that the DSS on the ventral side of medulla oblongata increased from the preoperative 1.2 ± 0.5 mm to 2.4 ± 0.9 mm (p < 0.001). CTA decreased significantly from 212.9 ± 56.5 to 162.4 ± 39.3 mm2 (p < 0.001). All 21 patients with BI‐AAD had CM or syringomyelia complications. Sixteen patients had syringomyelia but improved during the 1‐year follow‐up period. At 6 months postoperatively, solid bone fusion occurred in 19 patients (19/21, 90.4%), whereas in two patients (2/21, 9.6%) with severe occipital dysplasia, solid bone fusion was achieved at 12 months postoperatively (Table 3; Figures 1 and 2).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the surgical results of patients with BI‐AAD after PFD, explored the difficulties in the revision surgery, and proposed the surgical strategy and the associated risks and considerations. Direct posterior revision technique, using interarticular distraction, fusion with cage grafting, and fixation, effectively treats patients with BI‐AAD after PFD, achieving satisfactory reduction rates, bony fusion rates, and symptom relief rates.

Pathomechanism, Treatment Principles, and Challenges for BI‐AAD after PFD

BI is a rare and complex developmental deformity at the craniocervical junction region, which often presents as neck discomfort, dyskinesia, paresthesia, and ataxia. Aggravated symptoms are caused mainly by the mechanical instability of the atlantoaxial joint. 3 PFD is sometimes performed due to misdiagnosis as Chiari malformation or syringomyelia. The symptoms will improve mildly initially, but simple PFD can damage the cephalic and cervical splint muscles, which leads to increased atlantoaxial instability and triggers an increase in symptoms. Therefore, certain patients need revision surgery after PFD. Maintaining the stability of the CVJ region and relieving the ventral compression of the medulla oblongata is essential to successful operation outcomes. The rate of symptom improvement in the 21 patients with BI‐AAD was 3/21 (14.3%) after the first operation and 15/21 (71.4%) after the last operation.

Revision surgery after PFD with BI‐AAD is challenging. Surgical scarring and adhesions increase the risk of injury to the dura and vertebral artery during surgery.10, 13, 14 After the decompression of the occipital bone (and the posterior arch of the atlas), the internal fixation screws cannot be installed on the occipital bone. The previous repositioning by cantilever operation of the internal fixation system will be difficult to achieve, and the traditional posterior site for bone grafting is lost. Notably, Salunke and Sahoo found that the occlusion of an abnormal vertebral artery such as a high‐riding vertebral artery accounted for 20%, which considerably increased the difficulty of dealing with the atlantoaxial joint. 15 In C1 lateral mass placement, a vertebral artery may pass high through the occipital condyle, and the lower margin of the lateral occipital scale may also be too low, which could occlude the screw placement path and increase the risk of injury to the hypoglossal canal and vertebral artery. Therefore, preoperative CTA is a critical examination that can reduce the risk of neural and vascular injury. 16

Revision Surgery for Failed BI‐AAD after PFD

Few cases of such revision surgery have been previously reported. For example, Perrini et al. proposed that odontoidectomy in revision surgery was to be performed through the anterior approach combined with the posterior approach, which achieved good clinical outcomes in three of five cases. 17 In addition, Tan et al. reported a similar technology, after the application of which infection occurred in one of 16 cases. 12 Thus, this approach can increase surgical trauma and the infection rate. In another study, Yang et al. used a simple anterior transoral atlantoaxial reduction plate system, which achieved an effective reduction in 30 patients with BI‐AAD after PFD. 9 However, the anterior approach has many disadvantages, such as a narrow visual field and operating space, a high postoperative infection rate, and acute bleeding, among other serious complications. 14 In contrast, Goel et al. proposed a simple posterior revision surgery and reported the implantation of autologous bone grafting/spacer by interarticular distraction in three cases, achieving good fusion results. 11 This technique achieves a reduction in atlantoaxial dislocation by joint distraction, followed by interarticular bone fusion. It simultaneously addresses the challenges of reduction and bone fusion after PFD. The use of occipital condyle screws resolves the difficulty of the inability to place an internal fixation system following the occipital bone defect.

Advantages and Effects of Direct Posterior Revision Technique

Posterior approach surgery has become the mainstream surgical method for such cases.6, 18, 19, 20 Chen improved Goel's technique by designing dedicated tools for posterior approach atlantoaxial joint release and a specialized interarticular fusion device. This technique effectively treats various types of BI‐AAD, achieving satisfactory reduction rates and bony fusion rates.7, 8, 21, 22 By performing joint distraction through the posterior approach, the tension band and interlocking of the atlantoaxial joint can be fully released, allowing for direct reduction in BI‐AAD. Simultaneously, the implantation of a cage filled with autogenous cancellous bone between the joints can effectively disperse the stress borne from the internal fixation system, maintaining reduction stability and promoting the formation of strong bony fusion in the atlantoaxial joint.

This technique also offers advantages in cases of BI‐AAD following revision PFD surgery. By performing joint distraction through the posterior approach to release the lateral interlocking and anterior tension band, the additional anterior release is avoided, which improves the direct posterior approach reduction rate and reduces the trauma and the incidence of complications. Additionally, cage grafting between the atlantoaxial joints promotes the formation of precise bony fusion, which addresses the issue of bone graft bed loss resulting from occipital bone defect and the inability to perform posterior lateral fusion in the occipitocervical region. The appropriate position of the interarticular cage is critical to the success of the entire surgery. It not only has a key role in resetting the BI‐AAD, but the autogenous bone in the cage can also form a strong and stable bony structure conforming to Wolff's law after fusion, which effectively reduces the stress of the internal fixation system. In our group of patients, a satisfactory reduction in BI‐AAD was achieved. All data revealed a significant relief of spinal cord ventral compression, a noticeable reduction in spinal cord cavity size, and satisfactory recovery of neurological function. Compared with the cases reported by Du et al., the main difference and advantage of our technique is the use of an interarticular autogenous bone cage instead of simple autogenous bone grafting, achieving better stability and a higher rate of bone fusion. 10

Most patients with BI‐AAD also have C1 assimilation, who are usually treated by traditional internal fixation using midline occipital bone screws and C2 pedicle screws. In our group, the occipital bone was resected during the previous surgery, so it is impossible to implant midline occipital bone screws. Therefore, internal fixation after craniovertebral reduction is also a challenge in such cases. We chose occipital condyle screws as a substitute for midline occipital bone screws. Patients with BI‐AAD often have complex variations of the vertebral artery, which often runs on the surface of the atlantoaxial joint. There is a risk of causing fatal posterior circulation ischemia following damage to the vertebral artery during posterior approach joint distraction and implantation of occipital condyle screws, especially when the contralateral vertebral artery is thin or absent. It is essential to evaluate both vertebral arteries by CTA preoperatively for preventing vertebral artery injury during surgery. When the vertebral artery runs posterior to the atlantoaxial joint, we perform subperiosteal dissection along the occipital bone, pushing the vertebral artery downward to expose the posterior margin of the occipital condyle. A 2‐mm drill and depth‐limiting drill are used to create a hole in the occipital condyle for avoiding the hypoglossal canal. Then, the occipital condyle screw is implanted. In our group, there were no cases of vertebral artery or hypoglossal nerve injury. One year postoperatively, all patients underwent follow‐up examinations, which revealed stable internal fixation systems without displacement or breakage. Strong bony fusion was observed in all interarticular spaces, confirming the reliable efficacy of the joint fusion technique.

Limitations

This study has potential limitations associated with a small sample size. The incidence of BI‐AAD combined with syringomyelia is low, and it is sometimes misdiagnosed by less experienced physicians. We still collected only a small number of cases in our National Neurospine Centre like the reported case size. However, statistical findings show that our procedure is highly effective (p value is much less than 0.05). We will take this into account in our future work like expanding the sample size or employing additional statistical methods.

Conclusion

Posterior surgery using the technique of interarticular distraction, fusion with cage grafting, and fixation is a safe and effective revision surgery to treat patients with BI‐AAD who failed PFD. It avoids the complications of the many disadvantages of anterior surgery, which is worthy of clinical promotion. Preoperative assessment of surgical feasibility and vertebral artery status could ensure surgical safety and efficacy.
