Germline and somatic mutational variants of Tunisian high grade serous ovarian cancer identified by next-generation sequencing
Authors: Nihel Ammous-Boukhris, Rania Abdelmaksoud-Dammak, Wala Ben Kridis, Dorra Ben-Ayed-Guerfali, Arwa Shtaiwi Abed, Souhir Guidara, Slim Charfi, Ameni Feki, Tahia Sellami-Boudawara, Hassen Kamoun, Afef Khanfir, Jamel Daoud, Raja Mokdad-Gargouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) is one of the leading causes of death from gynecological cancers in the world, primarily due to late stage diagnosis. Genetic mutations in genes involved in key cellular functions such as BRCA1, and BRCA2 significantly contribute to ovarian tumorigenesis. This study investigates the genetic landscape of Tunisian patients with familial or sporadic high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) to guide therapeutic strategies and genetic counseling.MethodsWe conducted a genetic study by targeted Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) on 54 Tunisian HGSOC patients. A cancer panel including 31 cancer-associated genes was used to analyze DNA extracted from both paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues and blood samples.ResultsGermline and somatic pathogenic variants (PVs) were detected in 20.3% and 27.77% of patients respectively. Five patients carried both germline and somatic BRCA1 PVs. Somatic PVs were identified in Homologous Recombination-related genes including ATM, RAD50, and BRIP1. Interestingly, four recurrent BRCA1 PVs were observed, one of them is novel. Germline BRCA1/2 PVs were significantly more frequent in patients under 50 years of age and were associated with improved overall survival. Additionally, 19 variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were detected.ConclusionThis study provides the first comprehensive genetic profiling of Tunisian HGSOC patients, highlighting the prevalence of pathogenic mutations in key cancer-related genes. These findings emphasize the importance of genetic screening in the management and treatment of OC particularly in populations with unique genetic backgrounds.

Background

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a significant global health concern, ranking as the second leading cause of death among gynecological cancers in women. In Tunisia, the incidence of OC is estimated at 3.6 per 100,000 women and accounts for 1.6% of all cancer-related deaths in women [1–3]. OC is a heterogeneous disease with varying causes and molecular profiles. It is generally classified into two main types: Type I includes low-grade serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, and transitional cell carcinomas, and Type II consists of high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSOC), undifferentiated carcinoma, and carcinosarcoma [4]. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with 51% at stage III and 29% at stage IV, leading to a poor prognosis [5]. The high mortality rate and resistance to therapy in epithelial OC have driven extensive research into its pathophysiology [5].

It is well documented that genetic factors play a crucial role in the etiology of OC. Germline mutations in key genes such as BRCA1, and BRCA2, which are core components of the homologous recombination pathway, are significantly associated with increased risk of OC development [6]. The presence of these mutations has significant clinical implications, including the potential for targeted therapy based on PARP inhibitors in BRCA-mutated patients, and the need for intensive surveillance and preventive strategies in high-risk individuals [7–9]. In epithelial ovarian cancer, germline BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations have been identified in approximately 20% of patients and are associated with improved platinum responsiveness and prolonged survival [7, 9]. Furthermore, somatic BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations have been identified in approximately 10% of cases [10, 11].

With the emergence of new sequencing technologies, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), additional risk genes for OC have been identified. These genes encode proteins involved in homologous recombination (HR) (e.g., PALB2, BRIP1, RAD51C, RAD51D) and mismatch repair (e.g. MSH2, MSH6) [12, 13]. Population-specific differences in the prevalence and spectrum of these variants have been reported. For example, the Ovarian Cancer Association Consortium has sequenced over 3,000 unselected OC patients and 3,000 healthy controls to identify potential risk genes [12]. Significant differences in the distribution, spectrum, and frequency of genetic variants across different regions and populations were reported. In Chinese OC patients, deleterious germline and somatic mutations were identified in 29.0 and 67.7% of patients, respectively [11]. Furthermore, the study of Miwa et al., conducted on a multicenter retrospective cohort study on Japanese OC patients, reported that BRCA1/BRCA2 germline PVs were found in 10.3% of patients [14]. The PREDICT study in the Gulf region found somatic BRCA PVs in 17% of patients [15].

However, data on the prevalence and spectrum of pathogenic variants in Tunisian HGSOC patients remains limited, as most studies focused on Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancers [16–18] leaving a gap in the understanding of ovarian cancer genetics in the Tunisian population. This gap in knowledge is concerning because it hinders the development of effective genetic screening programs and the implementation of personalized treatment strategies tailored to this population.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive genetic profiling of 54 HGSOC Tunisian patients using targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) on both FFPE tumor tissues, and blood samples to ensure a thorough analysis of both somatic and germline mutations.

Our study is particularly significant as it represents the first genetic analysis of Tunisian HGSOC patients. By identifying the mutation spectrum within this cohort, we aim to contribute to the global understanding of ovarian cancer genetics while providing valuable insights to improve clinical management in Tunisia. Moreover, the discovery of founder mutations will facilitate genetic counseling, risk assessment, and the development of a cost-effective screening strategy for Tunisian patients.

Methods

Study cohort

Fifty-four patients with histologically confirmed HGSOC were recruited from the Medical Oncology Department of Habib Bourguiba Hospital of Sfax-Tunisia, between August 2020 and September 2024. Ethical approval for this investigation was obtained by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Sfax, Tunisia, and all patients completed and signed a written informed consent. Genetic counseling was performed in the presence of the geneticists to acquire the clinical personal and familial history of patients. Blood samples and tumor tissues (FFPE) were collected for DNA extraction and targeted-NGS analysis.

DNA extraction and targeted NGS library preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples and FFPE tissues using the QIAamp DNA Blood mini kit, and QIAamp DNA FFPE tissue Kit in accordance with the protocol described by Qiagen.

DNA quantification of was performed using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Libraries for targeted sequencing were prepared using the OncoRisk panel (Celemics, Seoul, Korea), covering 31 cancer-associated genes. The detection of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and insertions/deletions (INDELs)), were generated using this panel that includes BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, BARD1, BRIP1, RAD51C, RAD51D, RAD50, NBN, MRE11A, ATM, CHEK2, TP53, PTEN, APC, BLM, BMPR1A, CDH1, CDK4, CDKN2A, EPCAM, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, PMS2, PRSS1, SLX4, SMAD4, STK11, and VHL. Sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq Instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using 2 × 151 bp paired-end reads on a V3 flow cell (Illumina).

Variant calling and classification

Raw sequencing reads were analyzed using the cloud-based tool by illumina: basespace. Variant calling was carried out on a panel of 31 genes that were selected based on their clinical actionability. During variant calling, modifications identified by the software were refined based on criteria including a coverage of more than 100× and a variant allele frequency (VAF) of at least 5%.

Germline variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines into five categories: benign, likely benign, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic, and pathogenic. Annotation was performed using ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), Varsome (https://varsome.com), and the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS, https://www.hgvs.org) nomenclature. Somatic variants were interpreted using OncoKB, COSMIC https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic), and ClinVar. OncoKB Level of Evidence scores were used to assess clinical relevance.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0. Associations between clinicopathological features and the presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (P/LPVs) were tested using the χ² test. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared with the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

NGS analysis was performed on 54 HGSOC patients. The mean age at diagnosis was 52.44 years (range 34 to 76). Advanced FIGO stage (III/IV) was observed in 70.37% (38/54) of patients. A positive family history of cancer (breast, ovarian, or prostate) was reported in 44.4% (24/54), familial cases were defined according to NCCN guidelines. Seven patients (13%) died during follow-up, with an overall survival time ranging from 16 to 84 months (median 43 months), (Table 1).

Overall mutation rate and correlation with clinicopathological features

Pathogenic variants in BRCA genes were identified in 24.1% of cases (13/54), including 9 in BRCA1 and 4 in BRCA2. Additional PVs were detected in 8 patients (14.81%) in other cancer-related genes including PALB2 (1), PTEN (1), ATM (2), PMS2 (1), BARD1(1), and RAD50(1). Most of PV’ carriers (19, 50%) are TP53 positive, consistently with HGSOC molecular features (Fig. 1).

Significant associations were observed between the mutation status and age at diagnosis. Germline PVs in BRCA1 gene were significantly more frequent in patients under 50 years old (7/25, 29.1%), (p = 0.019, Table 1). Patients with a family history of breast cancer were also more likely to carry germline BRCA1 PVs (p = 0.077; Table 1).

The period of follow-up varied between 10 and 84 months (median 31 months), and 7 patients (12.97%) died with their malignancy. Survival analysis was conducted to compare the overall survival rates between patients carrying or not germline BRCA1/2 PVs. Using the Kaplan–Meier–Log Rank Test, patients with BRCA PVs have significantly longer overall survival time (pLog rank = 0.001, Fig. 2).

Germline pathogenic variants in HGSOC patients

Germline PVs were identified in 11/54 patients (20.37%) mainly in BRCA1 gene (9, 81.8%) while only 2 patients (18%) carried BRCA2 PVs. The most common types of mutation were frameshift indels (4), stop-gain (1) and missense variants (2) (Table 2). Interestingly, we identified 4 recurrent PVs in BRCA1 namely the c.5093_5096 delCTAA, c.5030_5033 delCTAA, c.5309G > T, and c.2338 C > T, each one was found in 2 unrelated patients (Fig. 3; Table 2). Among BRCA1 PVs, one was not reported in public databases: the c.5093_5096 del, (Table 2). This novel recurrent variant was identified in 2 unrelated young patients (aged 39 and 40 years), both of whom reported a strong family history of breast and ovarian cancer. The first patient had a mother and two sisters diagnosed with breast cancer, while the second patient had a mother and a maternal aunt diagnosed with ovarian cancer. PVs in BRCA2 were located in exon 11 (c.3248delA), and in exon 19 (c.8486 A > G), both variants have been previously reported (Figs. 3 and 4b; Table 2). VAF values for all variants are provided (Table 2).

Somatic pathogenic variants in HGSOC patients

Somatic BRCA1/BRCA2 PVs were identified in 7/54 patients (12.96%), and additional somatic PVs (8, 14.81%) were found in ATM (2), PMS2 (1), PALB2 (1), RAD50 (1), PTEN (1), BRIP1 (1) and BARD1(1) genes (Fig. 4c; Table 2). Among patients carrying only somatic PVs, (56%) carried somatic P/LPVs in the TP53 gene primarily missense variants located in exons 4 to 8 and all variants have been already reported in public databases (Table 2).

Furthermore, five patients carried both germline and somatic BRCA1 PVs.VAF values for all somatic variants are provided (Table 2).

Variants of uncertain significance (VUS)

Nineteen VUS were identified in genes such as BRCA1 (5), BRCA2 (2), SLX4 (2), APC (2), CDK4 (1), CHEK2 (1), BARD1 (1), BRIP1 (1), RAD50 (1), RAD51C (1), NBN (1), and PMS2 (1) (Fig. 4d; Table 3). The CHEK2 (c.1117 A > G; p. Lys373Glu), and RAD51C (c.634 C > T; p.Arg212Cys) variants were found in 2 unrelated patients, and 4 patients carried VUSs in 2 different genes (Fig. 4d; Table 3).

Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the mutational spectrum in HGSOC patients, and to identify major contributing genes in this malignancy in a cohort of patients selected from the south region of Tunisia. Germline PVs were found in 20.37% of patients (9 in BRCA1 and 2 in BRCA2) whereas 7 patients carried somatic PVs in BRCA1/2 genes. In OC, the rate of PVs in BRCA1/BRCA2 genes varies widely and was reported to be around 13–20% depending on the histological subtype and the population [19, 20]. In HGSOC, the most prevalent histological subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer, BRCA PVs have been reported in approximately 25% of cases [5, 8, 21].

Several studies have reported that patients with germline PVs in the BRCA1 gene have a family history of cancer [14, 22–25]. For example, Siraj et al. showed that BRCA1 PVs in Middle Eastern populations were significantly associated with positive family history, and highlighted the importance of genetic counseling, and setting-up cost-effective screening programs [26].

In our cohort, 24 out of 54 patients had a family history of cancer, and among them 9 carried germline BRCA PVs, specifically 7 in BRCA1, and 2 in BRCA2. Interestingly, 4/5 BRCA1 PVs (c.5093_5096 delCTAA, c.5030_5033 delCTAA, c.5309G > T, and c.2338 C > T) were found in two unrelated patients, and are thus likely to be recurrent in the south region of Tunisia. Furthermore, BRCA1 PV (c.5093_5096 del) is novel and has not been previously reported in public databases. Only five patients carried both germline and somatic BRCA1 PV. Additionally, only two patients carried somatic PVs in BRCA2 gene.

In Tunisia, most of the molecular investigations have focused on familial breast/ovarian cancers and only a few have looked specifically on ovarian cancer [16–18]. For example, the study of Jandoubi et al., including 19 °C patients from the North region of Tunisia reported that 5 cases (26.3%) carried BRCA PV: 3 in BRCA1gene (c.915T > A, c.1612 C > T, and c.3049G > T) and 1 (c.249delG) in BRCA2 gene [27]. It is important to mention that no common BRCA PV were identified between our study and that of Jandoubi et al., which is in favor of a high genetic heterogeneity of cancer-predisposing genes in Tunisian patients.

In addition to PVs, 19 patients carried VUS, which were mainly missense variants, and 52.6% of them were found in both somatic and germline samples. While these variants are not currently actionable, some may have functional consequences. Their recurrence in patients with strong family histories or accompanying somatic hits could support reclassification in the future.

The association of BRCA PVs and age at diagnosis remains controversial, with some studies finding a significant association while others didn’t find significant differences [8, 28, 29]. In our study, we found that BRCA PVs were more frequent in patients under 50 years old, suggesting early-onset disease may be more genetically driven in our population.

It is well established that the search for deleterious variants in the BRCA1/BRCA2 genes in the tumor tissues of OC patients impairs the repair of DNA damage by HR mechanisms and could therefore be sensitive to PARP inhibition (PARPi) [30]. The frequency of somatic P/LPVs varied from 6 to 41% according to the population or geographical region as reported by previous studies [11, 31–33]. In our study, we found that 12.96% of patients carried somatic PVs in BRCA1/BRCA2, genes, and 8 patients carried somatic PVs in other genes playing a pivotal role in Homologous Repair (HR) such as ATM, BRIP1, PMS2, PALB2, BARD1, and RAD50.

It is well documented that TP53 mutations were found in more than 80% of HGSOC cases [34–37]. In our cohort, among patients carrying P/LPVs, most are positive for TP53 P/LPVs which are located in exons 4 to 8. All identified variants are shared by at least 2 patients and have been previously reported in public database. Functionally, TP53 mutations were divided into missense mutations that result in gain of oncogenic function, and frameshift indels, or nonsense, resulting in loss of tumor-suppressing activity [38]. In this study, we found mainly missense gain of function mutations such as the His179Arg, Arg248Gln, and Arg249Ser.

Although, there are currently no approved targeted therapies for TP53-mutated tumors, it has been reported that TP53 may have an effect on the response to PARPi treatment, and therefore a functional TP53 may be necessary for PARPi efficacy [39].

This study is the first focusing on HGSOC patients in Tunisia, however it has few limitations.

The sample size is small, and large structural variants (such as BRCA1/2 rearrangements) were not described, which could lead to underestimation of germline mutation prevalence. This is relevant since 10% of PVs in some populations are large rearrangements. Moreover, wider testing methods such as whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) could capture the full mutational spectrum as we only analyzed a 31-gene panel, and we may have missed mutations in other potentially relevant genes.

Finally, the clinical interpretation of VUS remains challenging. In fact, patients with strong family histories but no detected PVs harbored VUS. Although we did not observe somatic alterations in the same genes as these VUS, their presence in patients with clinical suspicion of hereditary cancer highlights the need for careful follow-up and potential reclassification in future studies.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive genetic characterization of Tunisian HGSOC patients, with the analysis of both germline and somatic PVs using a cancer panel. Interestingly, BRCA1 was the most frequently mutated germline gene, while TP53 was the most frequent somatic mutated gene. Four germline BRCA1 PVs (c.5093_5096delCTAA, c.5030_5033delCTAA, c.5309G > T, and c.2338 C > T) seem to be recurrent in HGSOC in southern Tunisian patients.

These findings are of great value to cancer patients in Tunisia, as they provide crucial insights into the genetic landscape of HGSOC, which can help in improving genetic counseling, early detection strategies, and the implementation of targeted PARPi therapies. These results suggest that there is a great necessity for patient’s genetic screening and personalized treatment in Tunisian OC patients to ameliorate their outcome.
