Toxicity and efficacy of antibody–drug conjugates in advanced solid tumors: a retrospective single-center analysis of clinical trials☆
Authors: R. Woodford, H. Almarzouq, N. Beygozlu, A. Cammarota, K. Joshi, R. Grochot, A. Williams, C. Swanton, E. Fontana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) combine targeted monoclonal antibodies with cytotoxic payloads and are an emerging modality in systemic cancer therapy. Thirteen ADCs are Food and Drug Administration approved, with many more in development. However, design and use remain challenging, with issues including on/off-target toxicity, resistance from prior exposure to payload classes, and optimal target/payload selection.Patients and methodsThis pooled analysis included patients treated on 19 clinical trials (9 phase I, 10 phase II/III) of 14 novel ADCs at Sarah Cannon Research Institute, London (2012-2025). Patients who received one or more doses of the study drug were analyzed. Descriptive statistics and Cox regression were used to evaluate demographics, tumor characteristics, toxicity and outcomes, overall and in subgroups.ResultsA total of 163 patients [median age 61 years (range 31–82 years); 46.8% male] were included. Most had breast (n = 53, 32.5%) or gynecological cancers (n = 36, 22.1%), with a median of 4 prior treatments (range 1-20). Payloads included alkylating agents (n = 3), microtubule inhibitors (n = 8), and topoisomerase inhibitors (n = 3). Four ADCs targeted an oncogene (HER2); others targeted tumor-associated antigens (TAAs).Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred in 84% (grade 3-4 in 29%). Rates of any-grade colitis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), neuropathy, ocular toxicity, and hepatotoxicity were 2.4%, 6.0%, 25.1%, 18.0% and 22.8%, respectively. Most toxicities emerged within 6 weeks, except colitis (median 18.2 weeks), ILD (13.1 weeks), neuropathy (11.6 weeks) and thrombocytopenia (23.2 weeks). No significant difference in grade ≥3 AEs was seen across payload classes (P = 0.50), although HER2 targeting was associated with higher rates (P = 0.04). Overall objective response rate was 16%, higher with topoisomerase payloads (43%), HER2 targeting (49% versus 6% for TAAs), in breast cancer (39%), and at recommended phase II dosing (25%).ConclusionsADCs show activity across tumor types, with greater efficacy when targeting oncogenes. Toxicities are frequent and often early. Anticipating timing of toxicities is key to effective clinical management.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) consist of a monoclonal antibody joined via a linker to a payload, most frequently a cytotoxic molecule.1 Targeted delivery of the payload occurs through antigen recognition and cancer cell internalization, theoretically allowing this therapeutic class to overcome issues of systemic toxicity due to poor specificity and a narrow therapeutic index.2 This class of agents has been characterized by marked success in certain tumor types3 with 13 agents already approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and many more under investigation.

Despite these successes, challenges of design persist, notably regarding selection of target antigens and mechanisms of payload delivery. Most commonly, ADCs are built upon the architecture of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies with a linker that allows for release of a drug. This drug, called the ‘payload’, can be a cytotoxic molecule, an immune-stimulating agent or a radionuclide, among others, and is released upon cell internalization in the setting of a noncleavable linker, or the presence of low pH or proteinases of the tumor microenvironment for a cleavable linker.4,5 Payloads used in ADCs mostly fall into several classes of cytotoxic agents, chosen for their ability to trigger cellular apoptosis at subnanomolar levels.6 These include microtubule inhibitors such as auristatins and maytansinoids, DNA-damaging agents such as alkylants (calicheamicins, pyrrolobenzodiazepines) and topoisomerase inhibitors (camptothecins).

Targets for ADCs are generally one of two classes: oncogenes or tissue-based antigens, the latter of which can be divided into tumor-associated antigens and tumor-specific antigens based on distribution within the body.7 One type of toxicity is therefore driven by target antigen selection, with ‘on-target off-tumor’ toxicity designating effect from the ADC upon normal tissues expressing the matching antigen.8 Contrastingly, ‘off-target off-tumor’ toxicity is due to premature uncoupling of the antibody and payload in the systemic circulation and reflects the toxicity profile of the payload.9

Each component of the ADC can influence efficacy and toxicity, and are critical considerations in ADC design and clinical development.10 However, the timeframes for toxicity development can vary, sometimes manifesting after the classical dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) period, and the impacts of target and payload selection, treatment exposure and patient demographics on efficacy remain unclear. We therefore undertook a retrospective review of patients treated with investigational ADCs at the Sarah Cannon Research Institute United Kingdom (SCRI UK) drug development unit, to assess outcomes and adverse effects from treatment and the timeframes at which these occurred.

Methods

We reviewed electronic medical records (EMRs) for patients treated with investigational ADCs at SCRI UK between January 2012 and January 2025. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they received at least one dose of ADC following trial enrolment. Patients were excluded from assessment if they withdrew for any reason before administration of the investigational therapeutic.

Patient and tumor characteristics, previous treatments and details of investigational therapy were collected independently by three investigators (RW, NB, HA), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus. Treatment-emergent adverse effects (TEAEs), as recorded into the EMR by site investigators at the time of patient assessment, were collected inclusive of dates of onset and off-set, severity and management.

Patient outcomes were extracted to estimate efficacy of therapy using progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), objective and best overall response rate (ORR/BOR), disease control rate (DCR) and duration of response (DOR). PFS was defined from the start of treatment to progression or death. OS was defined as the time from the start of first trial treatment to death. ORR was assessed based on best response to treatment according to assessments by site investigators according to RECIST version 1.1, and duration of response from the time of response to recorded progressive disease or death based on RECIST estimates. If patients were lost to follow-up, they were censored at the time of their last recorded follow-up visit.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroups of interest for ADC efficacy included class of payload based on cytotoxic action on tumor cells, previous exposure to the same chemotherapy as the payload class, target antigen as divided by tissue-based antigens and oncogenes, tumor type and dose level. Several patients received treatment within studies in dose escalation, and it was therefore of interest to compare activity and safety objectives at recommended phase II dosing (RP2D) versus non-RP2D dosing. For each of these groups, outcomes were assessed separately for OS, PFS, ORR, DCR and DOR.

For toxicity, subgroups of interest included patients dosed at RP2D as opposed to non-RP2D dosing, as well as payload class, targeting of a tumor-associated antigen versus an oncogene and body mass index (BMI) categories of <25 and ≥25 due to differences in dosing schedules towards adjusted and total body weight. Adverse events (AEs) of special interest (AESI) for these comparisons were ILD, colitis, ocular toxicity, hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Patients enrolled on to more than one trial were excluded from overall survival analysis at second enrolment but included for tumor response outcomes and PFS.

Kaplan–Meier modelling was used to estimate survival outcomes, with Cox regression used to provide an estimate of the hazard ratio (HR). Log-rank tests were used to assess the association between variables with cut-points taken as <0.05 as per convention.11 Best overall response and duration of response were depicted through waterfall plot and swimmer plot analyses.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize TEAEs for all and grade 3-4 occurrences, as well as time of onset of these AEs. This was translated into a graph of cumulative incidence and logistic regression, adjusting for key patient and ADC characteristics. The Pearson χ2 test was also used to assess associations with toxicity.

All analyses were undertaken using StataSE 15.2 (Statacorp LLC, College Station, TX) and R v4.1.2 (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Of the 94 trials prospectively registered to the SCRI UK EMR system between December 2012 and January 2025, 19 involved ADCs, from which 163 patients were eligible for inclusion into our analysis (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). These trials comprised 9 phase I studies and 10 phase II-III studies assessing 14 distinct ADCs.

Four patients received treatment with a second investigational ADC after progression on an earlier study and were analyzed for response according to the statistical plan. Four ADCs targeted the oncogene HER2, with the remainder targeting a variety of tissue antigens.

Efficacy

Median follow-up was 5.8 months [range 0.2-226.8 months] and 161 cases were included in the response assessment. ORR was 17% [95% confidence interval (CI) 11.4-23.5], comprised of 25 partial responses (16%) and 2 complete responses (1%) (Figure 1A). These responses were disproportionately seen in breast cancer, with 2 (100%) complete and 19 (76%) partial responses. The DCR was 71% (95% CI 63.8%-78.3%). Median OS of the total population (N = 163) was 10.7 months (95% CI 7.8-16.1 months) and median PFS of all trial encounters (N = 167) was 5.7 months (95% CI 3.5-8.9 months) (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). For the four patients treated with a subsequent ADC, median PFS was 2.8 months (95% CI 1.3 months-not available) for the first ADC and 3.3 months (95% CI 2.1 months-not available) for the second, with an ORR of 0% and 25%, respectively.

Efficacy by payload

Response rates were higher for patients receiving a topoisomerase-I inhibitor payload with an ORR of 43% compared with 3% and 16% for those receiving an alkylating agent or microtubule inhibitor payload, respectively. Median DOR was 27.7 months (range 8.1-50.2 months) for topoisomerase-I inhibitors, 22.1 months (range 1.6-109.2 months) for microtubule inhibitors and not estimable for alkylating agents. Similarly, survival was markedly improved for agents with a topoisomerase payload, with a median PFS not reached (95% CI 8.8 months-not reached) as opposed to those with alkylating agents or microtubule inhibitors [4.2 months (95% CI 3.1-6.8 months) and 5.0 months (95% CI 2.5-20.3 months) respectively]. The same trend was observed for overall survival [21.5 months (95% CI 6.2 months-not reached) versus 11.9 months (95% CI 5.8-16.2 months) and 9.7 months (95% CI 6.7-16.1 months)].

Efficacy by prior treatment

All patients received ADC in the second, or later lines of treatment. Only a minority (9.8%) had received one prior line of treatment before ADC (Table 1). Previous exposure to chemotherapy agents of the same class as the ADC payload was only evaluable for alkylating agents and microtubule inhibitors and noted inferior outcomes, with an ORR of 0% and 2% (DOR not estimable), median PFS of 3.0 months (95% CI 1.1 months-not available) and 2.8 months (95% CI 1.6-4.1 months), and a median OS of 9.1 months (95% CI 3.6 months-not available) and 6.2 months (95% CI 4.5-11.7 months), respectively, compared with those who had received no prior exposure (P < 0.01) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573).

Efficacy by RP2D

Dosing at RP2D was also superior to lower doses used for escalation, at an ORR of 25%, DOR of 27.4 months (range 2.2-109.2 months), PFS of 9.6 months (95% CI 5.6-16.5 months) and OS 16.1 months (95% CI 7.2-28.0 months) compared with an ORR of 11%, DOR of 11.2 months (range 1.6-25.9 months), PFS of 3.5 months (95% CI 2.3-8.8 months) and OS of 7.8 months (95% CI 3.2-11.3 months, P = 0.02).

Efficacy by target

Compared with those targeting tumor tissue antigens, HER2-oncogene-targeted ADCs demonstrated superior response rates, PFS and OS (P < 0.01). ORR was 49% for oncogene-targeted agents versus 6% for tissue antigen targets and median DOR 28.4 months (range 7.5-109.2 months) versus 12.4 months (range 1.6-27.4 months). Median PFS for oncogene-targeted agents was 34.1 months (95% CI 15.2 months-not reached) and median OS was 27.2 months (95% CI 15.7-59.3 months), compared with a median PFS of 7.8 months (95% CI 5.6-10.7 months) and median OS of 3.4 months (95% CI 2.6-5.0 months) for tissue-antigen-targeting ADCs.

PFS curves of the subgroups of interest for efficacy are presented in Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573.

Onset of adverse events

The median time of onset for all TEAEs was 8.8 weeks (range 0-279.6 weeks), with the majority of individual AEs occurring at <6 weeks (71.4%, Table 3). Colitis, neuropathy, cardiac toxicity, ILD and thrombocytopaenia were the exception to this, presenting at a median of 18.2 weeks (range 1.5-138.3 weeks), 11.6 weeks (0-91.8 weeks), 9.6 weeks (7.8-41.7 weeks), 13.1 weeks (4.9-30.0 weeks) and 23.2 weeks (0.3-61.6 weeks), respectively. Adverse events by subgroups

Rate of all-grade TEAEs for BMI <25 and ≥25 was 91% and 80%, respectively, and 24% and 33% for grades 3-4, with no significant difference between either (P = 0.21 and P = 0.59) (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). Rates of all-grade TEAEs for patients dosed at RP2D versus lower doses was 86% and 93% (P = 0.15), and 19% and 36% for grades ≥3 (P = 0.07) (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). Rates for other key subgroups are listed in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573, with the only significant association being higher rates of grade 3-4 TEAEs with agents targeting a TAA compared with an oncogene although this association was not significant after adjusting for other factors (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). This was unchanged when stratifying by BMI (data not shown).

Adverse events of special interest

Adverse events of special interest (AESIs) included colitis, neuropathy, ILD, ocular toxicity and hepatotoxicity and occurred at rates of 2%, 25%, 6%, 18% and 23%, respectively, for any grade, and 2%, 1%, 1%, 0% and 1% for grades 3-4 (Table 3).

Associations between AESIs and clinical characteristics were assessed using univariable analyses, with multivariable modelling not undertaken due to small event numbers. There were significant associations between payload type and both all-grade peripheral neuropathy (P < 0.001) and all-grade ocular toxicity (P < 0.001). Microtubule inhibitor payloads were associated with higher rates of all-grade neuropathy, while alkylating agent payloads were more commonly associated with all-grade ocular toxicity. No significant differences were observed between payload and all-grade colitis (P = 0.49), ILD (P = 0.20) or hepatotoxicity (P = 0.54).

Toxicity patterns also varied according to the ADC target. HER2-targeted ADCs were associated with higher rates of all-grade ocular toxicity and hepatotoxicity (P < 0.01 for both). ADCs targeting TAAs were associated with a significantly higher incidence of all-grade peripheral neuropathy (P = 0.03), reflecting the predominance of microtubule inhibitor payloads in this group. No significant differences were seen for all-grade colitis or ILD by target.

Time to onset of all-grade peripheral neuropathy with agents containing a microtubule inhibitor was not substantially altered by prior exposure, at a median 12.1 weeks (range 0-23.4 weeks) compared with 10.3 weeks (range 1-91.8 weeks) without prior exposure. Numbers were too small to assess for differences in onset of other key toxicities with prior exposure.

When comparing patients dosed at RP2D versus those who were not, differences in all-grade toxicity were observed across all AESIs except colitis (P = 0.16) and ILD (P = 0.43) (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105573). No significant differences in AESI rates were observed between patients with BMI <25 versus ≥25.

Discussion

ADCs demonstrate activity across a range of tumors, although with notable differences in efficacy and toxicity dependent on target and payload characteristics. Despite a predominance of breast cancer and the clinical trial population’s inherent selection biases, we observed a meaningful number of objective responses and a substantial disease control rate. This is noteworthy given the heavily pretreated nature of the cohort. ADCs with topoisomerase inhibitor payloads demonstrated the highest efficacy, likely reflecting both an absence of prior exposure and high DARs of the included agents. In addition, these agents were investigated exclusively in breast and genitourinary cancers for our population, tumor types already associated with some sensitivity to topoisomerase inhibition. In contrast, other ADCs were investigated across a broader range of tumor groups, containing participants who had previously received chemotherapeutics from the same payload class.

While resistance to chemotherapeutics of the same class is well documented, inducible cross-resistance to other drug classes is increasingly recognized, especially where drugs may have overlapping mechanisms of action.12 Tumor resistance may be generated through multiple mechanisms, such as impaired proteolytic degradation, overexpression of drug transporters, alterations in intracellular trafficking and loss of target expression.13,14 Among participants previously exposed to the same class of chemotherapy, we observed a reduction in ORR and survival, suggesting payload class resistance and underscoring the importance of payload diversification.15 Notably, none of the four participants who received sequential ADCs were treated with combinations utilizing payloads from the same class. While assessment of time to progression on prior therapies and intervals between sequential treatments was beyond the scope of our data, both are frequently used to determine likelihood of response to subsequent courses.12

In our analysis, ADCs with microtubule inhibitor or alkylating agent payloads had lower DARs and remained in dose escalation with higher numbers of participants receiving doses under the RP2D, influencing efficacy outcomes. Even so, outcomes remained meaningful even for those treated at doses less than RP2D given the extensively pretreated nature of the population.

Patients who received oncogene-targeted ADCs demonstrated longer survival compared with those treated with agents targeting tissue-associated antigens. This activity reflects both the inhibition of oncogene signaling and efficient internalization of cytotoxic payload upon ADC binding. Consequently, while HER2-targeted ADCs exhibit activity in HER2-low tumors, efficacy does not match that observed in HER2-high tumors due to differences in receptor function. Trastuzumab deruxtecan may represent an exception, demonstrating activity in HER2-low and HER2-negative tumors, attributed to its strong bystander effect from payload diffusion following cleavage.16 This characteristic has resulted in a comparative higher efficacy relative to other HER2-targeted agents.3

The spectrum of toxicities observed in our study aligns with the known profiles of the chemotherapy payloads, constituting off-target off-tumor toxicities associated with premature deconjugation.9 Most toxicities occurred early in the treatment course, with the exception of colitis, ILD and neuropathy. Although neuropathy was more frequent with microtubule inhibitor payloads, the onset was neither substantially earlier nor more severe in participants with prior exposure, likely reflecting trial eligibility criteria requiring resolution of previous toxicities. The delayed onset of ILD, colitis and neuropathy suggests cumulative toxicity from repeat exposure, and a possible critical monitoring period of 10-18 weeks.

We conducted additional subgroup analyses to assess differences in toxicity profiles and time to onset, including assessments based on dosing at RP2D, DAR, payload type and BMI. BMI was assessed due to its potential impact on drug distribution,17 which has led to the use of adjusted ideal body weight and capping strategies for certain ADCs.18 However, neither incidence of all-grade AEs nor AESIs differed significantly between BMI groups. Similarly, although there was a trend towards higher rates of grade 3-4 TEAEs among patients dosed at RP2D compared with those dosed below RP2D, this did not reach statistical significance.

While lower DAR is associated with a reduced amount of payload per internalized antibody and a corresponding impact on efficacy, it often characterizes higher potency payloads, such that toxicity may still be considerable.19 In our analysis, toxicity was more strongly associated with payload class than other ADC features such as DAR and target, consistent with literature demonstrating similarity in toxicity profiles among ADCs sharing the same linker-payload complex.19, 20, 21 Assessment of toxicity differences based on linker chemistry was unfortunately limited, as only one ADC with a noncleavable linker was included.

There are several limitations to our study. This was a retrospective analysis and, as such, can only be hypothesis generating. We lacked access to pharmacokinetic data and antigen expression levels, limiting correlation with activity. Additionally, the nature of a clinical trial population with inclusion and exclusion criteria on the basis of prior toxicity resolution may have resulted in artificially lower toxicity rates, reducing the significance of subgroup differences and applicability to a real-world population. The heterogeneous dosing within the population, with many participants receiving doses below the RP2D, may have resulted in a cumulative underestimation of both toxicity and activity. Although our study included 163 patients treated with a variety of ADCs, rates of AESIs were low, limiting subgroup analysis.

As the ADC portfolio expands, further pooled analyses such as this will become increasingly important to quantify benefit across patient subgroups and inform agent selection and sequencing. Payload diversification and careful antigen selection remain paramount considerations in ADC design, particularly as patients become increasingly pretreated. In parallel, a robust understanding of expected toxicities and their timeframes is critical to identify at-risk patients, highlighting the need for further research into toxicity mitigation strategies.

Conclusions

ADCs demonstrate activity across a range of tumors, although with notable differences dependent on type of antigen target and payload. Characterization of toxicity profiles and patients’ risk factors, as well as identification of timelines of expected toxicities, many of which occur early in the treatment course, is critical to management and mitigation strategies.
