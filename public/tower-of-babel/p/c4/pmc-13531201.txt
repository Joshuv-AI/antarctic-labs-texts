Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillators in Pregnant Individuals
Authors: Nichola Bomani Gonzalez, Jennifer McCormick, Nidhi Mehta, Amy Ahnert, Joanne N. Quiñones-Rivera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence-based guidance for pregnancy management in patients with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) includes careful multidisciplinary antepartum and delivery planning.MethodsCase series describing outcomes of 23 pregnancies among 9 patients with arrhythmias and ICDs.ResultsAmong the 9 patients all 23 pregnancies were carried to term, 13 of which were managed by our cardio-obstetrics program from March 2010 to October 2025. Median delivery gestational age was 39.3 weeks. Primary cesarean delivery rate was 55.5%, with cesareans performed for obstetric indications. Three pregnancies experienced cardiac complications: antepartum ventricular tachycardia, postpartum atrial fibrillation, and postpartum heart failure. No patients experienced inappropriate firings or device complications.ConclusionsMost patients in our case series experienced excellent perinatal and cardiac outcomes, with a maternal cardiac event rate of 13.0%. Our case series highlights the crucial role of coordinated multidisciplinary care, including antiarrhythmic therapy, fetal surveillance, labor and delivery planning, and close follow-up during the postpartum period.

Background

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) have been shown to be effective1 at preventing sudden cardiac death among individuals with structural heart disease and high-risk ventricular arrhythmias. Expanded ICD use means more reproductive age women with high-risk arrhythmias attempt pregnancy. The hemodynamic changes of pregnancy may increase the risks of recurrent arrythmias and heart failure.2Take-Home Messages•Evidence-based guidance for pregnancy management in patients with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) can optimize the perinatal outcomes for patients with high-risk arrhythmias.•Management of pregnant patients with ICDs requires team-based care.•With careful antepartum management, treatment of arrhythmias and/or heart failure, and postpartum follow-up, patients with ICDs can achieve successful pregnancy outcomes.

There is a limited but growing body of literature addressing pregnancy in the setting of ICDs, with consensus guidelines sourced from registries, case reports, and case series.3 This case series aims to contribute to the growing pool of knowledge by describing perinatal outcomes across 23 pregnancies in 9 patients with ICDs managed at a single academic community tertiary center.

Methods

This descriptive case series includes 9 patients managed in a cardio-obstetrics program from March 2010 to October 2025. All 23 pregnancies in these patients are discussed, including those managed outside the program or before ICD implantation, to provide a comprehensive account of each patient's cardiac and obstetric history (Table 1). Multidisciplinary management includes consultation with a cardiologist and perinatologist, device interrogation by an electrophysiologist, diagnostic studies, medication adjustment, and fetal surveillance. For patients in the cardio-obstetrics program, individualized intrapartum and postpartum plans are created before delivery, including timing, location and mode of delivery, anesthesia consultation, and cardiac monitoring.

Case Series

Case 1

The patient was diagnosed with nongenetic long QT syndrome (LQTS) after hospitalization for diabetic ketoacidosis 3 years before conception. She underwent single-chamber ICD placement given a family history of sudden cardiac death. After conceiving, she was maintained on metoprolol and was induced at term. After laboring for >24 hours, she underwent primary cesarean for fetal indications. She presented 7 days postpartum in pulmonary edema with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45% to 50%. She received intravenous furosemide with appropriate diuresis. Cardiac function recovered within 7 months, with LVEF 60%. She was not referred for preconception counseling and conceived again within 1 year.

The second pregnancy was managed by the newly established cardio-obstetrics program. The patient was closely monitored with echocardiograms given the concern for recurrence of pulmonary edema. LVEF remained normal during pregnancy. The patient underwent a term repeat cesarean delivery and planned sterilization. Postpartum course was unremarkable.

Case 2

This patient's first pregnancy occurred 2 years before the diagnosis of LQTS in the setting of recurrent syncope and ventricular tachycardia (VT). A dual-chamber ICD was placed at age 19 at an outside institution. The subsequent 2 pregnancies were managed by her obstetrician and cardiologist. The patient was maintained on metoprolol and delivered vaginally at term, with the third pregnancy complicated by fetal growth restriction. The fourth pregnancy resulted in a first trimester miscarriage. The patient was referred to the cardio-obstetrics program for the fifth and sixth pregnancies and was maintained on metoprolol. The fifth pregnancy was uncomplicated. A year after her fifth pregnancy, her device was replaced with a subcutaneous single-chamber ICD. Her sixth pregnancy, conceived 1 year after the last battery change, was complicated by gestational diabetes and a primary cesarean for fetal indications. The patient was monitored with continuous telemetry during the intrapartum and postpartum periods. Before her sixth pregnancy, genetic testing revealed mutations in the KCNJ2, HCN4, and PKP2 genes.

Case 3

After a maternal cousin experienced cardiac arrest, the patient underwent genetic testing diagnostic for LQTS3 mutation and underwent subsequent single-chamber ICD placement. The ICD has never fired. Eight years later, during her first pregnancy, the patient was prescribed nadolol but discontinued it because of side effects. She delivered at term, with planned intrapartum and postpartum telemetry. The patient’s subsequent pregnancy 5 years later was uncomplicated. Her device reached elective replacement indicator (ERI) early in pregnancy, and the generator was changed at 28 weeks of gestation. She delivered vaginally at term, with planned telemetry as well.

Case 4

The patient was diagnosed with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at birth. Single-chamber ICD was placed at age 18 given polymorphic nonsustained VT, syncope, and family history of sudden cardiac death. Genetic testing was negative for catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT). Atenolol was prescribed but was stopped, as the medication was not effective in suppressing nonsustained VT and as atenolol is generally not recommended during pregnancy given fetal growth concerns. The patient was maintained on metoprolol, and she delivered at term. Two subsequent pregnancies were managed with metoprolol through the cardio-obstetrics program. Ejection fraction was normal during the second pregnancy and was slightly lower (50%) in the third pregnancy. Intrapartum and postpartum telemetry was recommended, with intermittent nonsustained VT and premature ventricular contractions noted after the third delivery. The patient had limited prenatal care during the fourth pregnancy, was not followed in the cardio-obstetrics program, and was not on metoprolol. The pregnancy was uncomplicated, and metoprolol was prescribed after delivery.

Case 5

The patient was diagnosed with LQTS2—KCNH2 mutation—during adolescence. Single-chamber ICD was placed at age 24 after 2 syncopal episodes. She presented to our cardio-obstetrics program for preconception counseling. The first pregnancy was terminated in the second trimester because of lethal fetal anomalies. The second pregnancy was managed on nadolol. After labor induction for fetal growth restriction, she underwent a primary cesarean for fetal indications. Continuous cardiac monitoring was implemented during labor and 24 hours postpartum. The third pregnancy was delivered via repeat-term cesarean, without complications.

Case 6

The patient had a history of births 14 and 11 years before LQTS diagnosis. The third and fourth pregnancies were early spontaneous miscarriages. Two years before her fifth pregnancy, she experienced cardiac arrest in the setting of ventricular fibrillation. After therapeutic hypothermia, single-chamber ICD was placed. Genetic testing for LQTS was negative. The patient presented for consultation to the cardio-obstetrics program for the fifth pregnancy, conceived 2 years after ICD placement. The pregnancy was unplanned, without previous preconception counseling. She continued metoprolol, the device was serially interrogated, and she delivered at term. Telemetry was implemented during and after delivery.

Case 7

Three years before ICD placement, this patient underwent a term cesarean delivery because of labor arrest. Three years later, she experienced ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest, leading to a presumed LQTS diagnosis and dual-chamber ICD placement. She subsequently experienced recurrent polymorphic VT (PVT). Genetic testing for LQTS mutations was negative.

The patient conceived 3 years after ICD placement. At 10 weeks of gestation, she experienced PVT with an effective ICD shock. She received an additional dose of nadolol, and maintenance dose was doubled. The remainder of the pregnancy course was uncomplicated, and the patient underwent a repeat-term cesarean. The postpartum course was uneventful. The patient's third pregnancy resulted in an early miscarriage. Four years later, the patient presented to the maternal fetal medicine team for preconception counseling. CPVT is now considered the patient's primary diagnosis, and referral to cardiogenetics was placed.

Case 8

The patient presented to the cardio-obstetrics program during her first pregnancy, with a history of right ventricular outflow tachycardia and ventricular arrhythmia managed with radiofrequency ablation 16 years prior. Her mother's genetic testing for LMNA mutation was pending at the time of this visit. Holter monitoring showed high-density and complex atrial and ventricular arrhythmias; metoprolol was prescribed. Although the antepartum course was uncomplicated, the patient experienced premature ventricular contractions during labor and atrial fibrillation postpartum. She was managed with intravenous metoprolol, and further adjustments of maintenance metoprolol resolved the arrhythmia. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a normal ejection fraction. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with the LMNA mutation. She underwent implantation of a single-chamber ICD 16 months postpartum.

The patient's second pregnancy resulted in an early miscarriage. The third pregnancy progressed without antepartum or intrapartum complications, and she delivered vaginally at term. Telemetry was implemented during labor and for 24 hours postpartum.

Case 9

The patient presented to our program with a medical history of CPVT due to an inherited pathogenic RYR2 variant (c. 7276C>T, [p.Leu2426Phe]) and 3 cardiac arrests as a child. She underwent dual-chamber ICD placement as a teenager. She was not seen for preconception counseling but was advised by her electrophysiologist that she did not have a contraindication to conceive. During pregnancy, she was maintained on nadolol, and her device was serially interrogated. Multidisciplinary planning included a detailed plan for a maternal code scenario using CPVT as the cardiac arrest etiology. She underwent labor induction at 37 weeks of gestation given fetal growth restriction and was delivered by cesarean because of failed induction. Postpartum course was uncomplicated.

Discussion

In this case series of 23 term pregnancies among 9 patients with ICDs, 13 pregnancies were managed by our cardio-obstetrics program. Median gestational age was 39.3 weeks (IQR: 39.0-39.9 weeks), and median neonatal birthweight was 3,140 g (IQR: 2,780-3,430 g). Primary cesarean rate was 55.5% (5 of 9 patients), with obstetric indications rather than cardiac complications or maternal decompensation. Cardiac diagnoses include LQTS, VT in the setting of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, CPVT, and LMNA-related cardiomyopathy. No patients experienced inappropriate ICD shocks or device complications. Beta-blocker use was common, as recommended by current guidelines.2 Two-thirds of the patients (6 of 9) had preserved cardiac function via echocardiogram and no persistent arrhythmias. Three pregnancies experienced cardiac complications: arrhythmias in 2 patients (1 antepartum, 1 postpartum), and postpartum heart failure in 1 patient, for a maternal cardiac event rate of 13.0%. This rate is similar to the cardiac event rate predicted for patients considered modified World Health Organization 2.0 classification II-III.4, 5, 6 This classification includes patients with 1) low-risk LQTS: no previous events and on full-dose beta-blocker therapy, and 2) low-risk CPVT well controlled by medical therapy.

A systematic review of 133 patients with ICDs demonstrated no inappropriate device fires during the intrapartum period, 1 patient with heart failure, and a cesarean rate comparable to that of the general population.2 Our primary cesarean rate is slightly higher than that in the general population, but all were performed for obstetric indications. Our inclusion of presenting symptoms, clinical characteristics, and management of the patients in our case series with complications may aid in recognition and treatment of patients in similar clinical scenarios.

Intrapartum hemodynamics in patients with ICDs requires careful monitoring, as patients receive continuous intravenous fluids during labor and/or before regional anesthesia to prevent maternal hypotension7 and subsequent fetal heart rate abnormalities. Excessive intravenous fluid administration may prompt volume overload, pulmonary edema, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and possibly death.8 In patients experiencing prolonged labor inductions, volume status must be monitored closely with serial physical examinations, intake and output documentation, and invasive hemodynamic monitoring when indicated.

As the incidence of pregnant patients with ICDs increases, professional societies have developed guidelines to streamline the care of these patients.3,4,9 A 2024 position statement of the United Kingdom Maternal Cardiology Society, British Heart Rhythm Society, and British Maternal and Fetal Medicine Society recommends multidisciplinary care planning in the antepartum and intrapartum settings, continuous fetal monitoring, and maintaining intravenous access, as well as access to antiarrhythmics, external cardioverter-defibrillators, and magnets to deactivate ICDs if needed.3 The multidisciplinary team should be involved in preconception care and antiarrhythmic initiation, particularly beta-blockers. During pregnancy, regularly scheduled prenatal visits are essential to monitor fetal growth when beta-blockers are used. Upon delivery admission, continuous cardiac monitoring for high-risk patients is recommended. Use of guidelines and protocols is supported by evidence,9 and collaboration between cardiology, electrophysiology, maternal-fetal medicine, obstetrics, and anesthesia with attention to individual patient characteristics can improve outcomes for high-risk patients.

As almost half of the pregnancies in this series occurred before the establishment of the cardio-obstetrics program, we cannot determine a direct effect derived strictly because of the program. However, cardio-obstetrics programs can optimize the care of patients with high-risk arrhythmias by providing coordinated multidisciplinary care in a shared mental model, allowing joint discussions with a cardiologist and perinatologist, addressing all aspects of care including medications, delivery planning, and postpartum follow-up. Overall, pregnancy outcomes can be favorable in women with ICDs, and our case series underscores the importance of multidisciplinary care.
