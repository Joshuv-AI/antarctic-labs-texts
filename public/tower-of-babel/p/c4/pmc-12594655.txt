Sensory integration deficits in Parkinson’s disease with freezing of gait: cortical network dynamics and paradoxical dopaminergic modulation
Authors: Sijie Chen, Qiaodan Pan, Qing Zhao, Jingling Jin, Xia Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFreezing of gait (FOG) in Parkinson's disease (PD) is a disabling motor symptom with unclear pathophysiology. Beyond known basal ganglia dysfunction and cognitive impairment, sensory integration deficits are increasingly key, though their cortical network mechanisms during walking remain poorly understood.ObjectiveThis study used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) during sensory-contextual walking to clarify FOG-related cortical network dynamics, aiming to: (1) Characterize FOG-specific cortical responses to sensory challenges; (2) Distinguish PD-specific versus FOG-associated cortical network dysfunction; (3) Explore dopaminergic modulation of these mechanisms.Methods40 PD patients [20 with FOG (PD-FOG), 20 without FOG (PD-nFOG)] and 23 healthy-controls (HC) completed four walking tasks [routine Walking-on-Ground (WG), Walking-on-Foam with proprioceptive challenge (WF), Walking-through-Narrow-gate with visuospatial challenge (WN), and Walking-through-Slope with combined proprioceptive/visuospatial challenges (WS)] under OFF and ON dopaminergic medications. Cortical hemodynamics in the Supplementary Motor Area (SMA), Primary Motor Cortex (M1), Primary Somatosensory Cortex (S1), Somatosensory Association Cortex (SAC), Prefrontal Cortex (PFC), and Frontal Eye Field (FEF), as well as walking performance were recorded. Group and medication-state differences were analyzed using ANOVA models.Results(1) PD-FOG lacked adaptive cortical plasticity (activation/connectivity response) to increasing sensory demands compared to PD-nFOG/HC; (2) PD-FOG exhibited cognitive-sensory/motor hyperconnectivity versus PD-nFOG during WF. PD patients showed widespread sensory-mediated hyperconnectivity and focal sensorimotor hyperactivation during WG/WN versus HC, but FEF hypoactivation during WS; (3) Medication improved gait but suppressed sensorimotor activation and reduced frontoparietal connectivity in PD-FOG; (4) Elevated M1-SMA connectivity predicted OFF-medication FOG, while elevated S1-FEF connectivity predicted ON-medication FOG. Daily FOG severity correlated with distinct OFF-/ON-medication connectivity patterns.ConclusionThis study reveals a medication-dependent, biphasic cortical dysfunction in PD-FOG. The OFF-medication state shows compensatory hyperconnectivity that fails under sensory challenges, indicating deficient plasticity, whereas the ON-medication state exhibits a paradoxical suppression of sensorimotor and integrative networks despite gait improvement. This reconceptualizes FOG as a dynamic network adaptation failure and points to state-specific therapeutic strategies.Registration number ChiCTR2300072744.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Freezing of gait, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), Cortical networks, Dopaminergic medication

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), a common neurodegenerative disease, is mainly characterized by symptoms such as bradykinesia, tremors, muscle rigidity, and balance and walking disorders, which seriously affect the motor function and quality of life of patients [1]. Freezing of Gait (FOG) is the most common walking disorder in the middle and late stages of the PD course. It is manifested as a sudden transient inability to take steps or difficulty in walking during the walking process [2], easily leading to falls and disabilities in patients, seriously affecting their quality of life [2, 3]. Its pathogenesis remains elusive, posing a significant obstacle to the development of precise treatment strategies.

Traditional pathophysiological models attribute FOG to basal ganglia dysfunction and loss of automatic motor control, primarily driven by dopaminergic deficits [4]. However, these models inadequately explain the complex, context-dependent FOG phenotype, prompting a shift towards cortical-subcortical network decoupling mechanisms [5, 6]. Neuroimaging evidence dose demonstrate disrupted connectivity between the basal ganglia and key cortical regions, including the prefrontal cortex, supplementary motor area, and sensorimotor cortex [6, 7]. Given this complexity, several cortical mechanistic hypotheses have been proposed: the interference model (overloaded basal ganglia circuits during concurrent information processing), the cognitive model (impaired conflict resolution and response automaticity), and the decoupling model (disconnection between motor planning and motor output) [7, 8]. Within these hypotheses, cognitive dysfunction, particularly in executive function and attention, represents a key contributing factor. However, therapeutic strategies targeting executive training yield inconsistent improvement on FOG [9], and some patients with severe FOG exhibit preserved cognitive function [7]. The discrepancy highlights the need to explore alternative factors, such as sensory integration deficits, which may operate independently or synergistically with cognitive dysfunction.

Emerging evidence suggests that sensory integration deficits may serve as a pivotal underlying mechanism contributing to the pathophysiology of FOG [10–12]. Clinically, FOG patients exhibit excessive visual reliance but impaired vestibular and proprioception integration for balance control, where FOG severity positively correlates with visual dependency and exaggerated postural instability under sensory perturbations [12–14]. Proprioceptive deficits, characterized by elevated thresholds for detecting joint movements, further underscore disrupted sensory processing pathways in this population [15]. Notably, augmented sensory cueing interventions (e.g., auditory, visual or proprioceptive cues) transiently alleviate freezing, supporting the key role of sensory integration deficits in FOG [16, 17].

Despite their strong behavioral correlates, sensory organization deficits remain understudied in mechanistic models of FOG. Current mechanism research faces significant limitations. Neuroimaging is the predominant method, yet studies using motion-artifact-sensitive modalities like Magnetic Resonance Imaging typically assess static brain structures or activation during stationary tasks [6, 7]. Increasing research employing motion-tolerant modalities such as functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) examines ecological walking scenarios but often focuses on isolated brain regions and primarily cognitive activity [18]. Hence, dynamic network adaptations during real-world sensory-contextual walking remain largely unexplored. This study addressed the existing gap by employing fNIRS to simultaneously monitor multiple cortical networks during a multimodal walking paradigm with diverse sensory challenges (e.g., proprioceptive perturbations, visuospatial constraints). Cortical hemodynamics were recorded across three groups—PD with FOG (PD-FOG), PD without FOG (PD-nFOG), and healthy controls (HC)—to characterize response patterns within cognitive, sensory, and motor networks. The aimed to (1) elucidate cortical network dynamics during sensory-contextual walking in PD-FOG; (2) identify PD-specific and FOG-associated cortical network dysfunction; (3) clarify dopaminergic modulation of these mechanisms.

Methods

Participants

We recruited participants with PD (PD-FOG and PD-nFOG) and age-matched HC from June to September 2023. PD participants’ inclusion criteria comprised: (1) having a definite diagnosis of PD according to the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria [19], (2) being stably treated with medication, (3) ability to walk independently for at least 10 m during the OFF-medication state, (4) willingness to complete experimental tasks during both OFF- and ON-medication states, and (5) agreement to pause deep brain stimulation during experimental tasks for implanted patients. PD participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) having non-PD neurological conditions, (2) having cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination score < 24) [20], or (3) having any other disorder or dysfunction that affected balance (e.g. musculoskeletal, or sensory disorders). PD participants were stratified into PD-FOG and PD-nFOG groups based on item III score from the Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q), with PD-FOG defined as a score ≥ 1 and PD-nFOG as a score = 0 [21–23].

All participants provided written consent [Ethics No. M2023-019, Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital (Shanghai Sunshine Rehabilitation Center)]. This clinical trial has been registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300072744).

Experimental protocol

Demographics and clinical features were systematically documented. General clinical features included cognitive status (assessed by Mini-Mental State Examination), sensory organization ability (evaluated via Balance Evaluation Systems Test's sensory section with conditions like eyes open/closed on ground/foam and eye-closed on incline), fear of falling (measured by Activities-specific Balance Confidence Scale), and the number of falls in the past 6 months for all participants. For PD participants, additional PD-specific features were recorded, including PD duration, medication (used to calculate the Levodopa equivalent daily dose, LEDD), motor function (assessed by the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III, MDS-UPDRS-III), disease severity (classified via the Hoehn & Yahr staging), and FOG severity (evaluated using the FOG-Q). The motor function, disease severity and the subsequent experimental walking tasks were assessed in both OFF- and ON-medication states. Other clinical features were evaluated solely during the ON-medication state for PD participants. Note that for the questionnaires, PD participants were asked to recall daily experiences in both medication states. The OFF-medication state was defined as patients having discontinued drug intake for at least 12 h. Conversely, the ON-medication state was set at 1 h or more after drug administration and through self-report right before testing. For patients with implanted deep-brain stimulation devices, the stimulation was turned off at least 1 h before trial initiation [24–26], and remained off throughout the experiment.

Four sensory-contextual walking tasks were designed by integrating principles from the sensory organization test of the Balance Evaluation Systems Test [27] and previously reported FOG-provoking sensory-related contexts (narrow-space) [2]. They were as follows (Fig. 1):

WG (Walking-on-Ground): Straight walking on a flat ground path, involving routine sensory integration.

WG (Walking-on-Ground): Straight walking on a flat ground path, involving routine sensory integration.

WF (Walking-on-Foam): Straight walking on a foam-covered path, attenuating ankle proprioception.

WF (Walking-on-Foam): Straight walking on a foam-covered path, attenuating ankle proprioception.

WN (Walking-through-Narrow-gate): Straight walking through a 0.5-m-wide gate in the middle of the path, potentially inducing visuospatial disorientation.

WN (Walking-through-Narrow-gate): Straight walking through a 0.5-m-wide gate in the middle of the path, potentially inducing visuospatial disorientation.

WS (Walking-through-Slope): Straight walking through a 0.75-m 10° ascent slope followed by a 0.75-m 10° descent slope along the path, challenging both proprioceptive and visuospatial processing.

WS (Walking-through-Slope): Straight walking through a 0.75-m 10° ascent slope followed by a 0.75-m 10° descent slope along the path, challenging both proprioceptive and visuospatial processing.

Experimental walking tasks in a pseudorandom order

The four walking tasks were performed in a pseudorandom order for three times. Each task consisted of a 5-s preparation period (standing still with forward gaze fixation), followed by walking on a 5-m path for 20 s and a 20-s rest. Prior to the walking tasks, participants stood still for 3 min while maintaining forward gaze fixation to establish a standing baseline.

Walking tasks were video-recorded, and walking performance parameters, including gait speed, frequency of FOG episodes, and FOG duration in each test trial, were extracted from these recordings. Gait speed was calculated by dividing 5 m by the time taken to walk the first 5 m without turning. FOG was defined by hallmark interruptions in walking, such as sudden hesitation, an inability to initiate or continue stepping, or a feeling of the feet being “glued to the floor.” Each episode began with initial hesitation, followed by progressively shorter steps accompanied by trembling or shuffling without forward progression. Termination occurred with a sudden breakaway step that restored effective gait. The frequency of FOG was defined as the number of episodes with identifiable onset and termination within each trial. The duration of FOG was calculated as the total time occupied by all FOG episodes from onset to offset per trial. Two independent researchers identified FOG episodes from video recordings; any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

fNIRS acquisition and processing

During walking and pre-walking standing tasks, cortical hemodynamics were monitored using an NIRSport 8 × 8 portable fNIRS system (NIRx Medical Technology LLC, USA) with a sampling rate of 8.7 Hz. Optodes (light sources and detectors) were positioned according to the international EEG 10–20 system standard, with the Cz point (intersection of the nasion-inion line and the bilateral tragus-to-tragus line) as the anatomical reference. The spatial arrangement of regions of interest is illustrated in Fig. 2, encompassing the following brain areas: Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) (channels 8, 9, 12–14, 16–19, 21), Primary Motor Cortex (M1) (channel 7, 11), Primary Somatosensory Cortex (S1) (channels 5, 6), Somatosensory Association Cortex (SAC) (channels 10, 14), Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) (channel 20), and Frontal Eye Field (FEF) (channels 15, 22–23). To minimize external light interference, participants wore a black, light-shielding cap compatible with the fNIRS system throughout the experiment. E-Prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) is used to design task sequences, deliver prompts for task initiation, and synchronize fNIRS recordings of cortical hemodynamic activity (Fig. 2). The raw data of cortical hemodynamics during experimental walking tasks are shown in Supplementary Information 1.

Schematic diagrams of the source, detector, and channel locations, channel planar map

Optical density data of fNIRS were processed using Homer 2 (v2.0) in MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks, Natick, MA). Signal quality control was implemented through quartile dispersion coefficient screening (exclusion threshold: coefficient < 0.4474), followed by band-pass filtering (0.01–0.2 Hz) to attenuate physiological artifacts (cardiac/respiratory oscillations) and systemic low-frequency drift [23, 24]. Hemodynamic conversion was performed via the modified Beer-Lambert law [25] using wavelength-specific differential pathlength factors (760 nm: 7.25; 850 nm: 6.38) to compute oxyhemoglobin concentration changes (ΔHbO2) [28].

Task-evoked ΔHbO2 of each channel was calculated by subtracting the 5-s preparation baseline average from the 20-s task-period mean for each channel. Regional activation was quantified as the mean ΔHbO2 across all channels within each predefined region of interest. Interregional functional connectivity was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients (R) between regional representative signals. These signals were extracted by applying principal component analysis to the channel-level ΔHbO2 data within each region and retaining the first temporal principal component for subsequent correlation analysis [29, 30].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp.). Data normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests applied to demographic and clinical variables, regional activation (ΔHbO2), and functional connectivity matrices (R), stratified by groups (HC, a combined PD group consisting of PD-FOG and PD-nFOG, PD-FOG, and PD-nFOG) and walking tasks with four sensory-contextual conditions (WG/WF/WN/WS). Since trial repetition showed no significant effect on gait performance or cortical regional activation in each task, data from all three trials were included to analyze. For the analysis of demographic and clinical variables, independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U test were employed to assess between-group differences depending on the normality of data distribution, with specific comparisons between HC with the PD group, as well as between PD subgroups (PD-FOG and PD-nFOG).

To address the study’s specific hypotheses, three primary hierarchical analytical models were employed:

Baseline modelFor walking performance and task-evoked cortical hemodynamic responses in HC and PD participants during the OFF-medication state, two primary comparisons were performed to analyze: (1) between HC and PD, and (2) between the PD-FOG and PD-nFOG. Independent t-tests assessed between-group differences for each comparison in each walking task (WG/WF/WN/WS). Additionally, within each cohort (HC, PD-FOG, PD-nFOG), one-way ANOVAs assessed task-dependent modulation by examining differences in walking performance and cortical responses across the four walking tasks.

Dopaminergic modulation modelTo assess group differences in medication effects on walking performance and cortical responses in PD participants, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA [factors: group (PD-FOG vs. PD-nFOG) and medication state (OFF vs. ON)] was conducted for each walking task (WG/WF/WN/WS), using LEDD as a covariance. Significant interactions underwent simple effects analysis. Within each group (PD-FOG, PD-nFOG) at each medication state (OFF/ON), one-way ANOVAs evaluated task-dependent modulation by examining differences in walking performance and cortical responses across the four tasks.

Statistical significance for all statistical analyses was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Bonferroni adjustment was used for multiple comparisons.

Results

Demographic and clinical features of participants

The study involved 23 HC and 40 PD participants (20 PD-nFOG and 20 PD-FOG). Compared to HC, PD participants had lower balance confidence, poorer sensory organization ability, and higher fall rates (all P < 0.05), despite comparable cognitive level (P > 0.05). Within PD subgroups, PD-FOG showed poorer sensory organization (significant under eyes-closed-on-ground, P < 0.05; near-significant under eyes-opened-on-foam, P = 0.066) and lower balance confidence (P < 0.05) than PD-nFOG. PD-FOG also had higher LEDD, advanced Hoehn and Yahr staging at ON-medication state, and higher FOG scores (all P < 0.05). No significant differences in disease duration, motor function measured by MDS-UPDRS-III, or fall history were observed between PD subgroups (Table 1).

Baseline walking performance and cortical network dynamics during experimental tasks

Walking performanceThe PD group in OFF-medication exhibited significantly slower gait speeds than HC across all walking tasks (P < 0.05). Within PD subgroups, PD-FOG walked significantly slower than PD-nFOG in each task (P < 0.001). All participants showed speed reductions as task difficulty increased, especially in WF and WS compared with WG (P < 0.05). We observed freezing episodes exclusively in 15 out of 20 PD-FOG patients during walking tasks, without significant variation across specific tasks (Table 2).

Within PD subgroups, PD-FOG walked significantly slower than PD-nFOG in each task (P < 0.001). All participants showed speed reductions as task difficulty increased, especially in WF and WS compared with WG (P < 0.05). Cortical regional activationCortical regional activation analysis revealed distinct activation deficits in the PD group relative to the HC. Relative to HC, the PD group exhibited compensatory cortical sensorimotor hyperactivation, including elevated S1 and M1 activation during WG, elevated S1, SAC, M1 activation during WN (P < 0.05), alongside reduced FEF engagement during WS (P < 0.05). No significant activation differences were observed between PD-FOG and PD-nFOG across any walking condition (all P > 0.05). HC showed significant task effects in SMA and FEF (P < 0.05), with post-hoc comparisons confirming elevated activation during WS compared to WG in both regions (P < 0.05). The PD-nFOG group demonstrated only a task effect in S1 (P < 0.05) without significant post-hoc differences. PFC activation remained stable across all tasks and groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Cortical functional connectivityCortical functional connectivity analysis revealed significant alterations in PD group, particularly in PD-FOG. Compared to HC, PD group exhibited widespread hyperconnectivity during walking conditions: increased SAC-PFC connectivity during WG (P < 0.05), elevated S1-M1 connectivity during WF (P < 0.05), and heightened S1-SMA/S1-FEF/S1-PFC/SAC-M1/SAC-FEF/SAC-PFC/PFC-M1 connectivity during WN. Similar hyperconnectivity patterns emerged in S1-PFC/S1-FEF/S1-SMA networks during WS. Critically, direct comparisons between PD subgroups highlighted further impairments specific to PD-FOG. PD-FOG patients showed significantly WF-task hyperconnectivity in S1-FEF/FEF-SMA/FEF-M1/PFC-SMA pathways (P < 0.05). Notably, task-dependent modulation, which was quantified as connectivity changes from WG to complex tasks (WF/WN/WS), revealed different adaptive responses among groups: Significant elevation in PFC-M1 (P < 0.05) and marginal elevation in S1-FEF/S1-PFC/SAC-SMA/SAC-PFC (P < 0.10) were observed exclusively in PD-nFOG, indicating compensatory cognitive- and sensory-motor network adaptation. No such modulation occurred in HC or PD-FOG (Fig. 3, Supplementary Information 2).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the cortical mechanisms of FOG induced by sensory integration deficits, utilizing a multimodal sensory-contextual walking paradigm and fNIRS neuroimaging. We obtained four important findings: (1) PD-FOG lacks adaptive cortical plasticity during complex sensory-contextual tasks with proprioceptive (WF/WS) and visuospatial (WN/WS) challenges relative to a simple task (WG), while PD-nFOG and HC demonstrated cortical adaptation although in different manner; (2) Within the same task, compared to PD-nFOG, PD-FOG exhibited compensatory hyperconnectivity in sensory-cognitive and cognitive-motor networks. Meanwhile, compared to HC, the PD group demonstrated widespread cortical hyperconnectivity during all tasks, along with cortical sensorimotor hyperactivation during WG and WN, but hypoactivation in FEF during WS; (3) paradoxical dopaminergic modulation was observed in PD-FOG, where medication suppresses cortical sensorimotor activation (S1/M1) and disrupts fronto-parietal connectivity while improving gait; (4) Different cortical dynamics during OFF- and ON-medication states have been identified as correlating with FOG occurrence.

Baseline model

Distinct cortical strategies for adapting to increasing sensory challenges during tasks were observed among the HC, PD-nFOG and PD-FOG groups. The HC exhibited efficient adaptation, which was characterized by task-specific increases in the regional activation of the SMA and FEF during tasks involving proprioceptive/visuospatial challenges (e.g., WS), while maintaining stable functional connectivity. This targeted allocation of neural resource aligns with the specialized functions of these regions: the SMA generates integrated motor programs and initiates anticipatory postural control via cortico-reticular projections [7, 31], whereas the FEF facilitates voluntary visuospatial attention and precise saccadic movements through cortico-collicular projections [4, 32, 33].

In PD-nFOG, the lack of regional activation modulation aligns with reports of impaired regional recruitment during complex gait tasks in both PD subgroups [34]. However, we identified a novel compensatory mechanism in PD-nFOG: enhanced PFC-M1 connectivity, with marginal increases in the connectivity of S1-FEF/S1-PFC/SAC-SMA/SAC-PFC. The upregulation of cortical connectivity may compensate for inefficient regional activation, but could also reflect a pre-FOG vulnerability to network overload, as suggested by the network overload model of FOG [35, 36].

However, PD-FOG exhibited no adaptive changes in brain activation or connectivity when faced with increasing challenges. This is in contrast to earlier reports of abnormal hyperconnectivity during simple tasks [34] and reduced SMA-SMC connectivity with increasing difficulty [28], suggesting that higher cognitive-sensorimotor demands may overwhelm even previously dysfunctional compensation strategies or disrupt pre-existing network stability in PD-FOG. Furthermore, the absence of significant variation in FOG episodes across tasks with increasing sensory complexity, challenges the conventional assumption that FOG incidence directly scales with task complexity with high cognitive load or complex movement [37]. This may imply that the mechanism underlying sensory complexity triggering FOG could be different from the cognitive model of FOG. Alternatively, the sensory complexity designed in the experimental tasks could be not challenging enough to elicit increased FOG episodes. Moreover, the preserved modulation of gait speed in PD-FOG across tasks, similar to that in HC and PD-nFOG, further suggests that gait pace and FOG occurrence are dissociable phenomena, possibly regulated by distinct neural pathways.

Within the same tasks, PD-FOG patients walked slower than PD-nFOG patients, and all PD patients walked slower than HC. In-task FOG occurred only in PD-FOG group. Widespread hyperconnectivity was observed in PD compared to HC across tasks, especially within the sensory-mediated networks (e.g. S1-SAC during WG, S1-M1 during WF, S1-SMA/FEF/PFC and SAC-M1/FEF/PFC during WN, S1-PFC/FEF/SMA during WS). These findings align with the global network reorganization models of PD [36, 38] and extends their applicability to more focal sensory-mediated networks, while also demonstrating task-dependent features. Besides, the PD group retain residual focal compensatory regional hyperactivation (e.g., in sensory/motor regions during tasks without proprioceptive challenge (WG/WN). Although PD patients showed compensatory hyperconnectivity and regional hyperactivation, their gait speed remained consistently slower than that of HC across all tasks. This persistent performance gap suggests that such compensatory mechanisms are insufficient to normalize motor function, even under low-demand tasks. As task difficulty increased, particularly during WS, which has high demands on both proprioceptive and visuospatial challenges, the PD group showed evidence of compensatory breakdown, exemplified by FEF hypoactivation relative to HC. Given the role of FEF in visuospatial attention and environmental monitoring [4, 32], the hypoactivation could reflect a collapse of compensation under heightened demands, potentially representing a pre-FOG vulnerability in PD. PD-FOG exhibited compensatory hyperconnectivity in sensory-cognitive and cognitive-motor networks compared to PD-nFOG during walking with proprioceptive challenge (WF), highlighting cognitive networks as the pivot pathological marker of FOG under postural threat. This extends previous reports on compensatory prefrontal overactivation yet impaired performance in complex tasks [39].

Dopaminergic modulation model

Dopaminergic medication enhances gait speed in both PD subgroups and reduces in-task FOG occurrence during most tasks in PD-FOG, consistent with previous studies [40–42]. Paradoxically, it suppresses cortical sensorimotor activation (S1/M1) and disrupts frontoparietal connectivity specifically in PD-FOG. Behavioral gains may arise from dopaminergic restoration within subcortical circuits, while the cortical suppression could represent a reactive shift towards neural efficiency, facilitated by the improved subcortical function. Neural efficiency has been proposed as a concept to explain the phenomena observed in sports experts, who utilize a lower amount of cortical resources while achieving superior behavioral performance compared to novices [43]. However, this cortical downregulation solely in PD-FOG may occur excessively or inappropriately. This phenomenon could be explained by a larger acute motor response to medication in PD-FOG, due to worse motor performance at baseline compared to PD-nFOG. Such medication-induced cortical suppression could lead to later development of treatment complications, including dopamine-resistant symptoms [44]. Alternatively, this cortical suppression may reflect an inverted U-shaped dopaminergic effect on cortical function in PD [45, 46].

Furthermore, dopaminergic medication exerted task-dependent and group-specific effects on frontoparietal networks. The most striking medication-induced differentiation between PD-FOG and PD-nFOG emerged during proprioceptive challenge (WF). Medication enhanced connectivity in S1-FEF and S1-PFC in PD-nFOG, suggesting improved sensory integration, while reducing FEF-SMA connectivity in PD-FOG, indicating a failure to sustain the baseline compensatory networks. This divergence highlights a fundamental disparity in sensory integration capacity under dopamine modulation. Similarly, during routine walking (WG), medication increased FEF-M1 connectivity in PD-nFOG while decreasing M1-SMA connectivity in PD-FOG, indicating group-specific motor network response even to simple tasks. Under visuospatial challenge (WN), medication induced a general suppression of frontoparietal integration, significantly reducing FEF-SMA connectivity in PD-FOG and SAC-M1 connectivity in PD-nFOG, with additional marginal decreases in S1-PFC and SAC-PFC connectivity across both groups. This pattern suggests that visual constraints elicit a more generalized medication-induced suppression of integrative networks, potentially overwhelming FOG-specific compensatory mechanisms. For the most demanding task (WS), medication uniformly reduced S1-FEF connectivity regardless of FOG status, suggesting that high-complexity environments may overwhelm group-specific compensatory mechanisms. This progression of medication effects from clear group separation during sensory-specific challenges to convergent suppression under high cognitive load supports the concept that FOG represents a failure of task-appropriate network modulation rather than a uniform connectivity deficit [47, 48].

Cortical-behavioral correlation

Correlation analyses of in-task FOG identified that elevated SMA-M1 connectivity in OFF-medication independently predicts in-task FOG during overall tasks in the PD-FOG group. Notably, while baseline SMA-M1 connectivity did not differ between PD and HC or PD-FOG and PD-nFOG, it showed a medication-induced reduction in PD-FOG during routine walking. This suggests that in OFF-medication, SMA-M1 connectivity in PD-FOG group, comparable to levels in HC and PD-nFOG, may represent a maximal compensatory cortical effort to inhibit M1 hyperactivation [49]; The failure of this connectivity could trigger FOG by decoupling M1 from motor execution [50, 51]. In ON-medication, only heightened S1-FEF hyperconnectivity predicts in-task FOG. This hyperconnectivity, which was initially observed during complex tasks in PD-FOG patients in OFF-medication, persisted despite medication. Critically, we found that medication induced a further increase in S1-FEF connectivity in PD-nFOG but not in PD-FOG, supporting the view that PD-FOG patients are impaired in maintaining the baseline compensatory strategies. We hypothesize that, in OFF-medication, S1-FEF hyperconnectivity likely compensates for FEF hypoactivity by enhancing sensory input to support FEF-driven attention, meanwhile S1 hyperactivity existed. However, medication-induced suppression of S1 hyperactivity may render the persisting S1-FEF hyperconnectivity insufficient and vulnerable to depletion, thereby contributing to FOG.

Analyses of daily FOG also identified three medication-dependent predictors: elevated M1-PFC connectivity and reduced M1-SAC connectivity in OFF-medication, and elevated S1-PFC connectivity in ON-medication. In OFF-medication, elevated M1-PFC connectivity aligns with cognitive models underlying FOG [7, 8], reduced M1-SAC connectivity indicates impaired sensorimotor integration. In ON-medication, elevated S1-PFC connectivity suggests more sensory-cognitive crosstalk, similar to the S1-FEF hyperconnectivity predicting in-task FOG in ON-medication. Collectively, these predictive models support distinct FOG mechanisms between the OFF- and ON-medication states: FOG in OFF-medication involves excessive cognition-motor crosstalk and deficit sensorimotor integration, whereas FOG in ON-medication involves excessive sensory-cognition crosstalk.

In summary, this study integrates sensory integration deficits, network dysregulation, and dopaminergic modulation, providing pivotal insights into the pathophysiology of FOG in PD. PD-FOG exhibits a distinct medication-dependent biphasic cortical dysfunction: in OFF-medication, they show PD-specific hyperconnectivity within sensory-mediated networks, FOG-specific hyperconnectivity within cognitive-sensory and cognitive-motor networks, and impaired cortical plasticity in response to increasing sensory challenges; Crucially, we demonstrate a paradoxical effect of dopaminergic medication: although it improves gait, it simultaneously induces a maladaptive suppression of sensorimotor activation and disrupts integrative frontoparietal connectivity specifically in PD-FOG. Furthermore, distinct FOG predictors were identified for each medication state, indicating that the cortical pathophysiology of FOG is medication-state-dependent and pointing to state-specific therapeutic targets.

Limitations

This study has several limitations: (1) the exclusion of PD patients with cognitive impairment may limit the generalizability of our findings to the full clinical spectrum of FOG; (2). The limited spatial resolution of fNIRS restricted the analysis to predefined cortical regions, potentially overlooking contributions from deeper or uncovered cortical/subcortical structures. Future studies could combine fNIRS with fMRI to improve spatial resolution while preserving ecological validity; (3) Our sensory-contextual walking paradigms elicited task-specific cortical dynamics, but failed to trigger differences in in-task FOG across tasks, suggesting that future protocols require greater sensory or cognitive challenges to fully identify FOG triggers; (4) Patient classification relied on the FOG-Q (item III). However, when validated against video-rated in-task FOG in this study, the tool exhibited high sensitivity (100%) but modest specificity (80%), potentially due to recall bias or inadequate task provocation [52, 53]; (5) the correlational analysis limited our assessment to pairwise regional connectivity. Thus, machine learning methods in future studies may better capture whole-brain network patterns.

Conclusion

Our findings establish a medication-state-dependent, biphasic model of cortical dysfunction in FOG. The pathophysiology of FOG dynamically shifts from ineffective compensatory hyperconnectivity in the OFF-state to a drug-induced maladaptive suppression of sensorimotor and integrative networks in the ON-state. This paradigm shift, framing FOG as a failure of dynamic network adaptation to sensory challenges, highlights the critical need for state-specific neuromodulatory interventions.

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request. The raw functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) data, walking performance video recordings (with participant identifiers anonymized), and demographic/clinical feature datasets analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to restrictions related to participant privacy and the informed consent provided by the participants, which did not include authorization for public data sharing. Requests for access to de-identified data should be directed to Xia Shen (Email: shxia18003@tongji.edu.cn), and will be reviewed by the research team to ensure compliance with ethical guidelines and data protection regulations (Ethics No. M2023-019, Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital). The study protocol and statistical analysis code are available from the corresponding authors upon request. This clinical trial has been registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300072744), where additional study-related information can be accessed.
