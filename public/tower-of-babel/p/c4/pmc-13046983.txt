Epidemiological Measles Outbreaks Investigation in Nogob Zone, Somali Region, Eastern Ethiopia: Trends and Insights from 2019 To 2024
Authors: Ahmed Bihi Abdi, Seid Muhumed Abdilaahi, Mohammed Adem Maalin, Ahmed Mohammed Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasles remains a major vaccine-preventable cause of childhood morbidity and mortality, particularly in low-coverage settings. Measles remains endemic in Ethiopia, and Somali Regional State (SRS) experiences high incidence rates despite vaccination efforts.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate measles outbreaks and provide critical insights into the epidemiological trends.MethodsA retrospective descriptive study was conducted using surveillance data from Nogob Zone. All reported measles cases (confirmed, epidemiologically linked, and clinically compatible; n = 2,214) were included through inclusion of complete line lists. WHO case definitions were applied. Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, Epi-Info, and ArcGIS 10.8 to generate descriptive statistics, epidemiological curves, attack rates, and spatial maps.ResultsFrom 2019 to 2024, a total of 2,214 measles cases and 58 deaths were reported (case fatality rate: 2.6%), exceeding national rates. Children aged 5–14 years were most affected (42%), followed by those aged 15 years and older (33%). Geographically, Duhun and Garbo woredas had the highest attack rates (18 and 17 per 1,000 population, respectively). The outbreak peaked in 2023, with a decline thereafter.ConclusionsThe study shows that Nogob Zone has faced a substantial measles burden, reporting 2,214 cases and a 2.6% case fatality rate between 2019 and 2024. The notably high attack rates observed in Garbo and Duhun woredas, along with a major outbreak surge in early 2023, point to persistent gaps in vaccination coverage and systemic barriers. Strengthening routine immunization, improving surveillance, and implementing targeted catch-up campaigns are recommended to prevent future outbreaks.Keywords: Measles, Outbreak, Epidemiology, Investigation, Nogob, Somali regional state, Ethiopia

Introduction

Measles, caused by a highly contagious morbillivirus of the Paramyxoviridae family, remains a leading vaccine-preventable cause of childhood mortality worldwide [1, 2]. The virus transmits via respiratory droplets and persists in airborne particles for up to two hours, facilitating rapid outbreaks in under-immunized populations [3]. Before widespread vaccination, measles caused an estimated 2.6 million deaths annually worldwide and led to 15,000–60,000 cases of blindness each year (mostly in children) [4].

Global efforts have achieved significant progress. Between 2000 and 2021, measles vaccination prevented an estimated 56 million deaths [5]. However, measles cases surged to an estimated 10.3 million worldwide in 2023 a 20% increase from 2022 resulting in approximately 107,500 deaths, mostly among unvaccinated or under‑vaccinated children under 5 years [6, 7]. Despite vaccination averting 60.3 million deaths from 2000 to 2023 and WHO regional elimination pledges, no region sustained measles-free status by 2023; COVID-19 dropped first-dose measles-containing vaccine (MCV1) coverage to 81% (lowest since 2008) and second-dose measles-containing vaccine (MCV2) to 74%, with only partial recovery to 83% MCV1 and 74% MCV2 in 2022–2023 amid stalled progress and outbreaks in 57 countries [2].

Ethiopia faces persistent measles endemicity, with annual outbreaks reported nationwide. There were an estimated 149,415 confirmed cases of measles in Ethiopia from 2000 to 2023 [8]. From August 2021 to May 2023, the country reported 16,814 laboratory-confirmed measles cases and 182 deaths, resulting in a case fatality ratio of 1.1%. Notably, the annual number of confirmed measles cases increased significantly from 1,953 in 2021 to 9,291 in 2022, indicating an almost fivefold rise. In 2022, the nationwide measles incidence rate was 82 cases per one million population. The highest reported rates were in the Somali region, which reported 540 cases per one million population. Factors contributing to this surge include low population immunity, concurrent epidemics, conflict, forced displacement, and other humanitarian crises that disrupt routine childhood vaccinations [9, 10].

Between 1 January and 2 April 2023, the Somali Region reported 56 laboratory-confirmed measles cases and 364 epidemiologically linked cases. In 2022, the national measles incidence rate was 82 cases per one million population; however, the Somali Region reported the highest incidence, with 540 cases per one million population [9].

The region represents a clear example of regional vulnerability in the context of public health and infectious disease control. Limited health infrastructure, nomadic population movement, and recurring humanitarian challenges contribute to the amplification of measles outbreaks.

A spatial analysis conducted between 2022 and 2023 demonstrated a marked geographic concentration of measles cases within the Somali Region. The Nogob zone was disproportionately affected, accounting for 37.7% of all reported cases, followed by the Erer zone (17.3%) and the Jarar zone (16.1%).

Children under five years of age were the most affected group, representing 88.7% of all cases. Additionally, more than two-thirds of cases (82.6%) developed complications, highlighting the severe impact of measles outbreaks and the heightened vulnerability of young children in this setting [11].

Vaccination coverage in these areas remains alarmingly low. As reported in the aforementioned spatial analysis in the Somali Region shown that the MCV1 was administered to only 53.8% of the population, while MCV2 coverage reached 80.4%. Overall, the region’s measles vaccination coverage stood at 59.4%, with a measles incidence rate of 0.087 cases per 100 people [11]. This is significantly below the 95% coverage threshold recommended by the WHO to achieve herd immunity and prevent measles transmission [9]. Nationally, Ethiopia struggles with MCV2 pooled coverage at 50.5% (95% CI: 39.0-61.9%), lowest in Somali (21.4%) and Afar regions [12]. These gaps leave populations susceptible, particularly in hard-to-reach pastoralist areas prone to outbreaks.

Several factors contribute to the low vaccination coverage in Ethiopia generally. A qualitative study identified substantial health system barriers, including restricted availability of immunization services, vaccine stockouts, shortages of cold chain technologies, and data inaccuracies due to deliberate falsification or accidental manipulation [13]. These systemic challenges undermine effective vaccination program delivery and compromise immunization data accuracy, complicating efforts to identify and reach unvaccinated children. Despite these efforts, challenges persist, underscoring the need for ongoing epidemiological investigations to inform targeted interventions, therefore, this study investigates epidemiological patterns of measles outbreaks in Nogob Zone, Somali Region, Eastern Ethiopia, from 2019 to 2024. Findings aim to guide targeted interventions, enhancing elimination strategies amid evolving threats.

Methods and Materials

Study Area

The study was conducted in Nogob, a zone in the Somali Region of Ethiopia. The Nogob Zone is bordered on the south by Gode Zone, on the southwest by Afder Zone, on the west and northwest by the Oromia Region, on the north by Fafan Zone, on the east by Jarar Zone, and on the southeast by Korahe Zone. The Erer River flows through this zone. Nogob has 7 woredas: Ayun, Segeg, Garbo, Horushagah, Elweyne, Dihun, and Hararey (Fig. 1).

Nogob Zone Map

Study Design and Period

A retrospective descriptive study was conducted using surveillance data collected from 2019 to 2024.

Population

The source population includes all individuals living in the Nogob zone, while the study population comprises all confirmed measles cases, epidemiologically linked and clinically compatible measles cases identified during the measles outbreak in the zone.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study included completed measles outbreak line lists databases, whereas incomplete line lists were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Determination

The study included all measles outbreak surveillance line lists reported within the study period. As all eligible records were analyzed, no sampling technique or sample size calculation was applied. This complete inclusion of available surveillance data enhanced the validity and reliability of the findings.

Data Collection Tools

Data were collected using a standardized measles outbreak line list tailored to the Ethiopian context, presented in English. The line list included various fields such as serial number, region, zone, woredas, country of origin, reporting HF, outpatient/inpatient, name of patient, sex, age (in years), age (in months), age group, Kebele/PA’s town, sub-kebele, Date for onset of rash, date seen at health facilities, Epi week, No. of valid measles doses (0, 1, 2, 3, 99), specimen taken (yes/no), lab results, complication (yes/no), outcome (alive or dead), fever (y/n), rash (y/n), cough (y/n), conjunctivitis, (red eyes) y/n, coryza(runny nose) y/n, year and along with any additional comments.

Data Quality Control

Data quality was maintained through spot-checking and thorough review of the complete Line lists by the SMA, AMI and MAM, ensuring that all information was both complete and consistent.

Operational Definition

Suspected Measles Case

An illness in a patient with fever and generalized maculopapular (non-vesicular) rash, or in a patient whom a health care worker suspects has measles.

Clinical Measles Case

Any person in whom a clinician suspects measles infection; or Any person with fever and maculopapular rash (i.e., non-vesicular) and.

Cough, or.

Cough, or.

Coryza (i.e., runny nose) or.

Coryza (i.e., runny nose) or.

Conjunctivitis (i.e., red eyes).

Conjunctivitis (i.e., red eyes).

Laboratory-confirmed Measles Case

A suspected case of measles that has been confirmed positive by testing in a proficient laboratory, and vaccine-associated illness has been ruled out.

Epidemiologically Linked Measles Case

A clinical case of measles that has not been confirmed by a laboratory, but was geographically and temporally related, with dates of rash onset occurring 7–21 days apart from a laboratory-confirmed case or another epidemiologically linked measles case.

Data Analysis

Data were cleaned and analyzed using Microsoft Excel for descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means). Attack rates (cases per 1,000 population) were calculated by woredas using population estimates. Epidemiological curves (weekly cases over time) were generated. Spatial distribution was mapped using ArcGIS 10.8 (spot maps of case counts by woredas). Age, sex, vaccination status, laboratory confirmation, and case fatality were summarized with tables.

Results

Overall Case Burden and Mortality

From 2019 to 2024, a total of 2,214 measles cases were reported in the Nogob Zone, with 58 deaths, resulting in a case fatality rate (CFR) of 2.6%. The analysis excluded five incomplete line lists. While sporadic cases were reported throughout 2022, the region experienced a major epidemic wave in the first quarter of 2023. This was followed by a gradual tapering of the outbreak, with significantly lower and more stabilized case counts observed throughout the latter half of 2023 and into January 2024 (Fig. 2).

Trends in Measles Outbreak Epi Curve Analysis

The epidemiological curve shows that the number of reported cases began to rise gradually in late 2022, with a marked surge in cases starting around November 2022. The outbreak reached its peak between January and February 2023, with the highest weekly case counts observed during this period.

Following the peak, there was a noticeable decline in cases starting from March 2023, with sporadic smaller peaks occurring throughout the following months. By mid-2023 and into early 2024, the number of new cases continued to drop, stabilizing at much lower levels with only occasional cases reported (Fig. 2).

A children aged 5–14 years were the most affected, comprising 42% of cases, followed by those aged 15 years and older at 33% while only 5% of cases involved infants aged 0 to 11 months. This distribution underscores the vulnerability of school-aged children (Table 1).

Vaccination Status by Woreda

Vaccination coverage varied across the seven woredas in Nogob Zone from 2019 to 2024. Garbo and Duhun recorded the highest numbers for all antigens, while Hararey and Ayun had the lowest uptake. All woredas showed a consistent decline from Penta 1 to Penta 3 and further reductions through MCV1 and MCV2, with the sharpest drop‑offs observed in Hararey and Horshagah. Elweyne demonstrated the best continuity of vaccination, maintaining relatively small gaps between doses (Table 3).

Case Distribution and Fatality Rates by Sex

Among the reported measles cases, 1,164 (52.5%) were male and 1,053 (47.5%) were female. The case fatality rate (CFR) was 2.6% for males and 2.5% for females, indicating similar mortality outcomes between the two sexes (Table 5).

Case Fatality by Age Group

Out of 2,214 reported measles cases, a total of 58 deaths were documented, resulting in an overall CFR of 2.6%, which exceeds the WHO emergency threshold. The highest fatality occurred among infants (7.3%) and children aged 1–4 years (5.1%), reflecting the critical vulnerability of under-five children due to low immunization coverage and limited access to timely case management (Table 6).

Spatial Analysis of Measles Case Burden

The spot map revealed that there are marked variations in the measles burden among the woredas, with the highest concentration of cases observed in Garbo woreda, which reported 1,249 cases. Dhuxun woreda also exhibited a high caseload, with 400 cases reported over the same period. Intermediate levels of cases were noted in Ayun (211 cases), Horashagah (112 cases), and Sagag (164 cases), while relatively low counts were observed in Hararey (62 cases) and Elweyne (16 cases).

This spatial pattern indicates that the measles outbreak was particularly severe in central and northeastern Zone, especially Garbo and Dhuxun. The varying caseloads across woredas may reflect differences in population density, immunization coverage, or healthcare access (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this outbreak investigation period from 2019 to 2024, a total of 2,214 cases were reported and 58 deaths occurred, resulting in a case fatality rate (CFR) of 2.6%. This substantial burden exceeds the national CFR of 1.1% reported between August 2021 and May 2023 (16,814 cases, 182 deaths) and the updated national CFR of 0.81% through December 2024 (78,106 cases, 633 deaths) [14, 15]. This CFR aligns with findings from other Ethiopian regions facing similar access barriers, such as Artuma Fursi woreda CFR 2.6%, and Tocha District CFR 2.72% [16, 17].

This elevated CFR may reflect limited healthcare access, delays in case detection, and challenges in managing complications, particularly among children under five, who accounted for 88.7% of cases in prior regional studies [11]. The severity of outbreaks in Nogob Zone underscores the need for strengthened health system interventions to mitigate morbidity and mortality. This study reported attack rate (8.53 per 1,000) falls near the median of other Ethiopian outbreaks from the last decade, where rates have ranged between 4 and 12 per 1,000 in the region and nearby regions [18–20]. However, significant local variation was observed: Duhun and Garbo woredas experienced the highest attack rates (18 and 17 per 1,000, respectively). These findings are in line with previous analyses that identified heterogeneous attack rates across regions and woredas depending on local risk factors and immunity gaps [18].

The age distribution revealed 42% of cases in children aged 5–14 years and 33% in individuals ≥ 15 years, diverging markedly from global patterns where 70–80% burden children under 5 years [6]. This atypical shift parallels Somali Region trends (Jarar: 45% in 5–14 years; Dollo Zone: 30% adults) [11, 21] and signals accumulated immunity debt from chronic MCV1 (53.8%) and MCV2 (80.4%) shortfalls below WHO’s 95% herd immunity threshold [11, 22]. Expanding preventive and reactive campaigns to 15 years, as per updated WHO guidelines, becomes essential for catch-up strategies targeting school-age children and adolescents [23].

Geographic analysis revealed significant variation in attack rates, with Duhun (18 per 1,000) and Garbo (17 per 1,000) woredas being the most affected. Garbo woreda, with 1,249 cases, was the epicenter of the outbreak, likely driven by higher population density or lower immunization coverage, as the region’s overall vaccination coverage of 59.4% falls well below the WHO-recommended 95% for herd immunity [13, 14]. These results are consistent with prior spatial analyses identifying Nogob Zone as a measles hotspot, contributing 37.7% of regional cases [11]. The clustering of cases in Garbo and Duhun emphasizes the need for geographically targeted interventions.

The epidemiological curve reveals a sharp increase in measles cases beginning in November 2022, reaching its peak between January and February 2023, and then gradually declining. This decline likely reflects the impact of reactive vaccination campaigns and/or a natural increase in population immunity. Despite the overall reduction, sporadic cases continued into 2024, indicating ongoing transmission risks and highlighting that the outbreak was not fully eliminated. This pattern suggests a large-scale outbreak with a distinct peak, followed by effective control measures or natural decline due to increased immunity, resulting in a significant reduction in new cases over time. The data emphasize the need for timely outbreak response and sustained surveillance to prevent future surges. However, systemic barriers such as vaccine stockouts, cold chain deficiencies, and data inaccuracies identified in previous studies continue to undermine immunization efforts [13]. The low MCV1 coverage (53.8%) and slightly higher MCV2 coverage (80.4%) in Nogob Zone further contribute to susceptibility [11].

Males accounted for 52.5% of reported cases, while females accounted for 47.5%. The case fatality rates were similar between the two sexes, at 2.6% among males and 2.5% among females. The interplay of conflict, displacement, and concurrent epidemics, as noted in previous reports, likely disrupted routine immunization, exacerbating the outbreak [10, 14]. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive strategies to address both health system and contextual challenges.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This study revealed that measles continues to pose a substantial public health challenge in Nogob Zone, Somali Region, Eastern Ethiopia, driven by persistent immunity gaps and health system constraints. The observed age distribution, geographic clustering, and temporal pattern of the outbreak emphasize the critical need to address vaccination coverage shortfalls, particularly among school-aged children and in high-burden woredas.

To prevent future outbreaks, it’s recommended that public health authorities strengthen routine immunization, improve cold chain infrastructure, and improve surveillance. Targeted catch-up campaigns for children and adolescents, community engagement, and integrating measles vaccination into emergency responses are crucial.
