Trends and determinants of early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia: evidence from the Ethiopian demographic and health survey data
Authors: Tsion Mulat Tebeje, Beminate Lemma Seifu, Yordanos Sisay Asgedom, Kusse Urmale Mare, Zufan Alamrie Asmare, Hiwot Altaye Asebe, Abdu Hailu Shibeshi, Afework Alemu Lombebo, Kebede Gemeda Sabo, Bezawit Melak Fente, Bizunesh Fantahun Kase
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly neonatal mortality (ENM) accounts for three-fourths of all neonatal deaths and one-third of overall child mortality. While previous studies have primarily focused on neonatal and under-five mortality, ENM has received comparatively less attention. This study aims to address this gap by analyzing trends and identifying factors associated with ENM in Ethiopia.MethodsData from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) conducted between 2005 and 2016 were analyzed, comprising a total weighted sample of 22,310 participants. To account for the hierarchical structure of the EDHS data, a mixed-effects binary logistic regression model was applied to identify associated factors. In the multivariable analysis, adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to demonstrate the statistical relationship between ENM and explanatory variables.ResultsThe early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia increased from 20.4 per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 17.4–23.9) in 2005 to 21.4 per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 18.3–24.9) in 2011 before declining to 16.7 per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 14.0–19.9) in 2016. The multilevel analysis revealed that the odds of ENM were lower among mothers of female newborns, those who initiated breastfeeding within two days after delivery, mothers who attended antenatal care at least once to three times, and those residing in the Afar, SNNPR, and Gambela regions. In contrast, cesarean section delivery and multiple pregnancy were significantly associated with increased odds of ENM.ConclusionsGiven the slow decline in ENM, sustained efforts are essential to improve access to quality healthcare, strengthen the health system, and address key health determinants to achieve better neonatal outcomes. Implementing evidence-based strategies, including enhancing antenatal care services, promoting early initiation of breastfeeding, and providing specialized care for multiple pregnancies and cesarean deliveries, can play a crucial role in reducing ENM and ensuring healthier outcomes for newborns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06022-8.

Introduction

Globally, as the level of under-five mortality has been declining, 47% of all under-five deaths occur during the neonatal period. The annual reduction in the global pattern of neonatal mortality rate between 1990 and 2019 was 2.5% only, which is slower than the under-five mortality [1, 2]. Neonatal mortality is an indicator of the health and economic status of a nation, and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) planned to reduce it to 12 or less per 1000 live births [3, 4]. To lower the mortality rate of neonates, the Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP) was created in 2014 to achieve a national newborn mortality rate of 10 or less and 12 or fewer per 1000 live births by 2035 and 2030, respectively [5]. A high burden of neonatal mortality is seen in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and South Asian countries compared to the rest of the world, with 79% of the total burden of neonatal deaths. SSA countries have the highest mortality, with 27 neonatal deaths per 1000 live births, comprising 43% of global newborn deaths [6, 7].

Different initiatives have been proposed to reduce neonatal and maternal mortality, such as the ENAP, Ending Preventable Maternal Mortality (EPMM), and Global Financing Facility (GFF), aimed at ensuring well-being and enabling environments. They provide a roadmap of strategic actions to end preventable newborn mortality and stillbirth [8, 9]. However, this problem persists despite these initiatives.

Therefore, there is an urgent need to reduce neonatal mortality rates in Ethiopia. Ethiopia is the 5th country in the world, with the highest number of newborn deaths. Although the progress of neonatal mortality in the country is slower than that in the post-neonatal period, the neonatal mortality rate declined from 49 to 33 per 1000 live birth in 2000 and 2019, respectively. Regional variation in the country slows this progression [10–12]. Currently, the country has planned to reduce neonatal mortality from 33 to 21 per 1000 live births by 2024/25 [13]. According to the national burden of under-five, infant, and neonatal mortality in Ethiopia, there is a highest inter-regional disparity in the neonatal mortality rate, which ranged from 7.6 in Addis Ababa to 27.3 in the Benishangul-Gumuz region, per 1000 live births. Most of the regions showed a slightly lower neonatal mortality than the national which is 26.6 per 1000 live births [14].

Most neonatal deaths occur in the first week of life. Early neonatal mortality is the death of a newborn between zero and seven days after birth [15]. It accounts for 75% of all neonatal deaths and 33% of all child deaths [16, 17]. Previous studies have shown that factors that increase the risk of ENM include: lower age at pregnancy, low birth weight, home delivery, multiple pregnancy, lower level of maternal education, being twin, being male, having low APGAR score at one minute, being a rural resident, and a mother experiencing pregnancy complications, while utilization of focused antenatal care (ANC) and postnatal care within two days of delivery decreased the risk of ENM [17–20]. Most of the conditions that lead to ENM in developing countries can be easily prevented by contraceptive utilization, vaccination during pregnancy, hygienic institutional delivery, training of health workers, early initiation of breastfeeding, and skin-to-skin care [15].

Previous research has largely focused on neonatal and under-five mortality without considering the role of ENM on the components of both mortalities. The limited number of studies conducted on ENM focused on the magnitude and associated factors without considering the temporal distribution of early neonatal mortality [18, 19]. Therefore, this study provides comprehensive evidence by investigating the trends and determinants of ENM in Ethiopia using the EDHS 2005-16. It will provide evidence-based information for policymakers and program planners to conduct targeted interventions by detecting high-risk areas, identifying risk factors, improving health care, and addressing health disparities at national and regional levels.

Methods and materials

Study setting and data source

Secondary data analysis was conducted based on the 2005, 2011, and 2016 EDHS, which is a population-based cross-sectional study. Ethiopia is divided into nine administrative regions (Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Southern Nations Nationalities and People’s Region (SNNPR), Benishangul-Gumuz, Somali, Gambella, and Harari) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa). Each region is sub-divided into zones, each zone into a woredas, each woreda into towns, and each town into kebeles. Approximately 80% of Ethiopian households are found in three 3 regions: Oromia, Amhara, and SNNPR, and only 30.7% of households are in urban areas [21]. All EDHS samples were stratified and selected in two stages. In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs) was selected with a probability proportional to the EA size and independent selection in each sampling stratum. In the second stage, a fixed number of households per cluster were selected with equal probability systematic selection. Details of the sampling procedure can be obtained from each EDHS report [22–24]. The Kids record (KR) dataset is used in this study.

Study population

The source population were reproductive age women who had given live birth within five years prior to each survey in Ethiopia, while those from designated EAs were chosen as the study population. There were 9861, 11,654, and 10,641 live births in 2000, 2011, and 2016, respectively. After taking only the youngest or most recent children and dropping residents who were not dejure, the total live births were 6516 (weighted = 7238) for the 2005 survey, 7573 (weighted = 7675) for the 2011 survey and 7084 (weighted = 7454) for the 2016 survey.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable in this study was early neonatal mortality, defined as deaths of live born children within the 0–6 days after birth [25]. ENM was dichotomized with possible values of no = 0 if the child lived past the 6th day of birth and yes = 1 if the child died within the first six days of birth.

Independent variables

Considering the hierarchical nature of the EDHS data, both individual- and community-level variables were considered. The individual-level factors included religion, maternal education level, sex of the household head, wealth index, sex of the newborn, mode of delivery, working status, maternal age, birth interval, number of ANC follow-ups, postnatal care, place of delivery, marital status, media exposure, number of babies at birth, and birth order. Community-level factors included region and residence (Supplementary Table 1).

Data management and analysis

The data was cleaned, coded and analyzed using Stata V.17.0. To make the data more representative and consider the sampling design, it was weighted by generating a weighting variable using the sampling weight (v005). Descriptive and summary statistics were used to describe the main features and summarize the important characteristics of the dataset.

The ENM trend was analyzed separately in three phases for the periods 2005–2011, 2011–2016 and 2015–2016. This was assessed using descriptive statistics stratified by participant characteristics.

To consider the hierarchical nature of the EDHS data, where participants are nested by cluster and those in the same cluster are more similar, a statistical model that considers between-cluster variations, a multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression, was used. The degree of heterogeneity of ENM between clusters was quantified using intra-class correlation (ICC) and the median odds ratio (MOR). Using EDHS 2016, four models were utilized: null model (without predictors), model one (individual-level model), model two (community-level model) and model three (both individual- and community-level models). The models were compared by deviance (−2 log likelihood), and the best-fit model yielded the lowest deviance value [28]. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bi-variable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. Factors with a p-value < 0.05 in multivariable analysis were significantly associated with ENM and were reported with adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All the variables had VIF values below five, with a mean VIF of 1.76, indicating that multicollinearity was not a significant concern (Supplementary Table 2).

Results

Background characteristics of the participants

The study used a weighted total of 22,310 participants (7238 from 2005, 7675 from 2011 to 7454 from the 2016 EDHS). The majority of respondents (91.5% in 2005, 85% in 2011, and 87.4% in 2016) lived in rural areas. Regarding maternal age, more than 7 in 10 of them were 20–35 years old. Across the three survey periods, the proportion of mothers with no education showed a decreasing trend, from 78.6% in 2005 to 63.5% in 2016, while the proportion of mothers with higher education showed an increasing trend, from 0.54% in 2005 to 2.9% in 2016. The proportion of respondents who had media exposure increased from 2.8% in 2005 to 32.8% in 2016. Of the total participants in all three survey years, the majority (70.3%) were not working (Table 1).

Pregnancy, neonatal and maternal health service-related characteristics of respondents

With regard to the age of the mothers at first birth, the proportion of mothers aged 18–30 at first birth showed a relatively stable trend across the three survey periods (55.8%, 59.7%, and 59.7% in 2005, 2011, and 2016, respectively). The proportion of antenatal care visits four or more times increased consistently from 12.1% in 2005 to 19.1 in 2011 to 31.8% in 2016. Delivery in health institutions and cesarean deliveries increased from 6.4% to 1.2% in 2005 to 31.5% and 2.4% in 2016, respectively. Most mothers (62.7% in 2000, 65.2% in 2005, and 64.6% in 2011) gave birth 30 months after their previous delivery. (Table 1)

The trend of early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia

The early neonatal mortality per 1000 recent live births showed an unstable trend as it increased from 20.4 per 1000 live births (95% CI: 17.4, 23.9) in 2005 to 21.4 per 1000 live births (95%CI: 18.3, 24.9) in 2011 and decreased to 16.7 per 1000 live births (95%CI: 14.0, 19.9) in 2016 (Fig. 1). The highest ENM was observed in the Somali region in 2016 which was 33.8 per 1000 live births. Factors associated with early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia

In the bi-variable multilevel logistic regression model, variables with a p-value less than 0.25 were included in the multivariable regression, where four models were fitted. The ICC value in the null model 0.091 (95%CI: 0.018, 0.35) shows 9.1% of variation in ENM is explained by variation between the clusters. The value of the MOR in the null model was 1.32 which indicates the presence of a variation in ENM between clusters. When early neonates are randomly selected from two different clusters, the one from the high risk cluster of ENM would have 1.32 times higher odds of having early neonatal mortality compared to the one from lower risk of ENM.

The 4th or the final model was the best fitted model since it resulted in the highest value of log-likelihood (−445.3) and lowest value of deviance (890.6). In the multivariable multilevel logistic regression model; sex of the newborn, breastfeeding within two days of delivery, number of ANC, number of babies at birth, delivery by cesarean section and region were significantly associated with early neonatal mortality.

Mothers of female newborns were 46% (AOR = 0.54, 95%CI 0.35 to 0.84) less likely to experience ENM compared to mothers of male newborns. Mothers who breastfed their baby within two days after delivery had a 73% (AOR = 0.27, 95%CI 0.13 to 0.54) lower odds of early neonatal death than their counterparts. Mothers who attended ANC 1–3 times had 47% (AOR = 0.53, 95%CI 0.30 to 0.93) reduced odds of experiencing ENM compared to those who did not have any ANC follow-up. Mothers with multiple pregnancies were 9.6 times (AOR = 9.62, 95%CI 4.7 to 19.7) more likely to experience early neonatal death compared to those with singleton pregnancies. Additionally, mothers who delivered via cesarean section were 3.9 times (AOR = 3.9, 95%CI 1.38 to 11.1) more likely to experience ENM than their counterparts. When compared to women from Somali region, those from Afar, SNNPR and Gambela regions showed a protective effect against ENM, having 75% (AOR = 0.25, 95%CI 0.09 to 0.70), 76% (AOR = 0.24, 95%CI 0.09 to 0.63) and 76% (AOR = 0.24, 95%CI 0.07 to 0.76) lower odds of early neonatal death, respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the trends and determinants of early neonatal mortality in Ethiopia. The trend of ENM decreased from EDHS 2005 to 2016, despite an increase from 2005 to 2011. Between 2005 and 2011, the ENM increased by 1 death per 1,000 live births. However, from 2011 to 2016, the ENM decreased by 4.7 deaths per 1,000 live births. Overall, from 2005 to 2016, the ENM decreased by 3.7 deaths per 1,000 live births, representing an 18.1% reduction over 11 years. Although the decline is slow, the trend is consistent with neonatal mortality. A related study found that the mother’s age, education level, marital status, previous birth interval, pregnancy type, and child’s sex contributed to the observed decline in the ENM in Ethiopia [29]. The country is expected to achieve the SDG of reducing neonatal mortality. Consequently, the health sector transformation plan outlines specific neonatal health targets aimed at meeting the SDG targets [30, 31].

This study showed that the odds of ENM were lower among mothers who had ANC follow-up of one to three times as compared to those with no ANC follow-up. This is consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia [32], Nigeria [17], Afghanistan [33], Indonesia [34], and Bangladesh [35] where receiving antenatal care during pregnancy is a protective factor. Similarly, in the united states of America, women without prenatal care have an increased risk of neonatal mortality [36]. This might be because ANC improves pregnancy outcomes by identification and management of pregnancy complications in which pregnant mothers obtain counseling about nutrition, prenatal screening and testing, safe delivery practices, breastfeeding, and early management of newborn illness. Additionally, those who utilize ANC are more likely to deliver their baby with skilled attendance which will reduce the risk of ENM [37–39]. Hence, birth outcomes and survival of newborns can be improved by antenatal care visits.

The odds of ENM were higher among mothers with multiple pregnancies compared to singleton pregnancies. This is in line with previous studies in Ethiopia [18] and Afghanistan [33]. Multiple pregnancies put newborns at higher risk of death specifically in low-resource settings due to poor management of multiple pregnancies and a higher proportion of preterm births which predispose newborns to a higher risk of infection, hypoglycemia, and hypothermia [40–42]. In addition to this newborns of multiple pregnancies are more likely to have growth restrictions, low APGAR scores, very low birth weights, and complications during pregnancy, at delivery, and after delivery which increases the occurrence of early neonatal death as compared to singleton pregnancies [43–45].

Mothers of female newborns were found to have lower odds of experiencing early neonatal mortality as compared to mothers of male newborns. This is corroborated with studies conducted in Ethiopia [19, 46], Zambia [47], and India [48]. The possible reason could be girls produce stronger innate and humoral immune responses to infections. Additionally, while testosterone suppresses the immune system in boys, estrogen and progesterone improve immune responses in girls [49–51]. The study also showed that the odds of ENM were lower among those from Afar, SNNPR, and Gambela as compared to those from the Somali region. This might be because, as a pastoralist community, Somali region has relatively lower maternal and child healthcare services as a result of their nomadic and scattered lifestyle which makes it difficult to access healthcare [12].

Mothers who breastfed their baby within two days after delivery had a lower chance of death in the early neonatal period than their counterparts. This is consistent with studies from Ghana [52], India [53] and Nepal [54]. This might be because early breastfeeding initiation can reduce mortality resulting from neonatal infections [54]. Moreover, colostrum, which gives important immune protection, is secreted in the first 2–3 days after delivery [55].

Mothers who delivered via cesarean section were significantly associated with higher odds of experiencing ENM than those with spontaneous vaginal birth. This is supported by studies conducted in Iran [56], United States [57], Mexico [58] and SSA [59]. The possible justification might be cesarean delivery is associated with a low APGAR score, higher incidence of respiratory distress syndrome, and greater risk of pulmonary hypertension while in spontaneous vaginal delivery, compression of the newborn’s chest in the birth canal causes surfactant synthesis as a prevention of respiratory diseases [56, 58, 60]. However, some studies contradict this finding and reported neonatal mortality is lower in cesarean deliveries [36, 61]. This is explained by cesarean deliveries are conducted when vaginal delivery is associated with maternal or neonatal risks or complications during pregnancy or labor. The association between cesarean delivery and early neonatal mortality is complex and the discrepancy can be attributable to different criteria for choosing cesarean section. Therefore indications for cesarean delivery need to be taken into consideration when interpreting findings.

As a strength, the study is based on a nationally representative three EDHS datasets which can be generalizable at a national level. The sample size of the survey was also large and had adequate statistical power. To make the data more representative estimates were weighted. To prevent recall bias and missing variables, only recent births or youngest children were taken. The limitation of the study was that the data’s cross-sectional nature makes temporal relation not to be established.

Clinical and public health implications

Our study has important implications for both clinical practice and public health strategies aimed at improving outcomes for newborns. Early neonatal mortality remains a significant global public health concern, requiring targeted interventions to reduce its burden. Based on our findings, enhancing perinatal care such as increasing the number of antenatal care visits and promoting early initiation of exclusive breastfeeding can improve medical interventions and newborn survival. Identifying risk factors and assessing the quality of care during pregnancy and childbirth are essential for developing targeted strategies to reduce neonatal mortality. Regarding public health implications, this study helps inform public health policies by identifying high-risk groups, such as mothers experiencing cesarean section delivery or multiple pregnancy. It emphasizes the need for healthcare resource allocation to maternal and neonatal care in regions with high ENM, such as the Somali region. Additionally, by monitoring trends, identifying gaps, and evaluating the impact of interventions within maternal and neonatal health programs, both strengths and areas for improvement can be identified. Consequently, these findings contribute to achieving the goals of the ENAP and SDG in reducing neonatal mortality in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

This study found that ENM in Ethiopia has declined slowly over an 11 year period. Sex of the newborn, breastfeeding initiation within two days of delivery, ANC visits, geographic region, multiple pregnancy, and cesarean section delivery were significant determinants of experiencing ENM. Given the slow reduction of ENM, sustained efforts are essential to improve access to quality healthcare, strengthen the health system, and address key health determinants to enhance neonatal outcomes. Targeted public health interventions are needed to reduce ENM, particularly among high-risk populations. Policies should prioritize expanding ANC services, promoting early breastfeeding initiation, and providing specialized care for multiple pregnancies and cesarean deliveries to improve neonatal survival and overall health outcomes.
