Sustainable Self-Healing Elastomers: Chitosan-Tuned Ionic Dynamics in Natural Rubber Networks
Authors: Feng Liu, Xuan Zhao, Liu Yang, Xinyu Sun, Lei Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current self-healing elastomers face a fundamental challenge in reconciling robust mechanical properties with efficient autonomous repair due to the incompatibility between permanent covalent networks and reversible dynamic bonds. While chitosan (CTS) has shown potential in rubber modification, its role in establishing stimuli-responsive ionic networks within nonfunctionalized natural rubber (NR) to achieve intrinsic self-healing remains underexplored. Following a systematic screening of CTS molecular weight (MW) and degree of deacetylation (DDA), we engineered sustainable self-healing NR composites using CTS as a cross-linker. CTS was mechanically blended with unmodified NR, followed by thermal activation to form reversible [COO–]–[NH3 +] ionic bonds between CTS amines and native phospholipid-derived pyrophosphate termini on NR chains. Self-healing efficiency, tensile recovery, and cyclic hysteresis were quantitatively evaluated. Critical evidence from FTIR revealed ionic cross-linking via the amide band at 1741 cm–1 with characteristic bathochromic shifts; XRD confirmed disruption of CTS crystallinity; cross-link density analysis (T2 relaxation) revealed chain confinement modulated by CTS DDA and MW; and SEM demonstrated homogeneous CTS dispersion below 10 phr compared to agglomeration above 30 phr. We found that 10 phr CTS enables near-complete self-healing after 20 min of ambient contact, attributed to rapid ionic bond reorganization evidenced by high cyclic residual strain. However, this occurred with and approximately 22% decline in tensile strength versus neat NR. Excess CTS (>10 phr) reduced self-healing efficiency due to restricted chain mobility from ionic clusters and steric hindrance, coupled with a 22% tensile strength decline. Low-MW CTS facilitated superior healing by minimizing entanglement constraints. This study resolves the healing-mechanics trade-off by leveraging native NR components as dynamic anchors, eliminating the need for synthetic functionalization. By interfacing renewable polysaccharides with nonreactive polymers, our work advances circular design principles for next-generation sustainable materials.

Introduction

Self-healing polymeric materialsa crucial category of biomimetic smart materialsdemonstrate potential for applications in advanced engineering fields such as aerospace systems, high-durability devices with extended lifecycle requirements, and maintenance-inaccessible engineering components. − These intelligent substrates exhibit autonomous damage recovery through spontaneous molecular reorganization or triggered repair mechanisms activated by external stimuli, including thermal, mechanical, or photonic activation. , Recent advancements emphasize the development of dynamic covalent bonds , and supramolecular interactions , to synergistically optimize mechanical robustness and self-reparative capacity. Considerable research efforts have focused on two primary strategies: (1) designing novel dynamic networks via topological entanglement engineering and (2) functionalizing existing polymeric matrices through chemical modifications. , Employing the strategic introduction of a primary amine into tung oil-derived networks, Yun Hu et al. engineered dynamically cross-linked PNMETs with topological transformation capability, achieving catalyst-free self-healing and tunable mechanics. Their PNMETs/MWCNT@Fe3O4 composites demonstrated multistimuli responsiveness (light/heat/IR/voltage) and practical electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, where dynamic network rearrangements enabled concurrent shape memory and self-healing. This biobased platform provides a scalable route to multifunctional EMI shielding materials.

A primary challenge in developing self-healing rubbers arises from the inherent conflict between dynamic reversibility and permanent cross-linked networks. , Traditionally, achieving the necessary elastic properties in rubbers requires a robust network composed of permanent covalent sulfur or carbon bonds. However, the irreversible nature of these bonds prevents autonomous repair after network rupture. Conversely, the strategic incorporation of dynamic bondsessential for enabling reparative necessitates a critical trade-off between maintaining sufficient cross-link density to preserve mechanical integrity and avoiding undesirable damping of material properties. − Chitosan (CTS) , a renewable cationic polymerprovides a sustainable pathway toward intrinsic self-healing. Its abundant –NH2 groups enable reversible charge-mediated cross-linking, yet its potential to generate stimuli-responsive networks within unmodified natural rubber (NR) remains underexplored. − Crucially, NR contains native phospholipid-derived pyrophosphate termini that decompose into reactive –COOH groups during processing. − We hypothesize these native functional groups can dynamically interact with CTS amines to form self-healing [COO–]–[NH3+] ionic bonds, eliminating synthetic rubber modification.

Researchers have incorporated chitosan into various polymeric matrices, including natural latex, epoxidized natural rubber (ENR), poly­(lactic acid) (PLA), silicone, styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) among others, to investigate their vulcanization characteristics and mechanical performance. Natural rubber (NR), unlike synthetic rubbers, contains 5–8 wt % nonrubber constituents, including proteins, acetone extract, ash, and moisture. The protein content accounts for approximately 2–3 wt %; prior research − indicates that protein degradation yields ethanolamine, choline, and amino acids, with contain reactive carboxylic acid (−COOH) and amino (−NH2) groups. Significantly, these protein-derived constituents within NR can interact with chitosan to form ionic cross-links via salt formation, concurrently generating abundant hydrogen bonds. This synergistic interplay creates multifunctional reversible cross-linking sites. Consequently, the established dynamic network offers a promising avenue for imparting self-healing characteristics to the material. Based on this principle, this study employs chitosan as a dynamic cross-linking agent within an NR matrix. Varying chitosan loadings are incorporated to systematically tailor the reversible network density, thereby aiming to achieve stimuli-responsive self-healing capabilities.

Experiments

Materials

Natural rubber (SCR-5 grade) was procured from Hainan Sinochem Rubber Co., Ltd. (China). Chitosan (CTS) was supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Five distinct chitosan grades characterized by molecular weight (M W) and degree of deacetylation (DDA) were employed:

CTS: MW = 1.53 kDa, DDA = 95.1%.

CTS-low MW1:MW = 1.53 kDa, DDA = 81.06%.

CTS-low MW2:MW = 1.53 kDa, DDA = 84.39%.

CTS-high MW: MW = 230 kDa (range 150–250 kDa), DDA ≥95%.

CTS-med MW: MW = 165 kDa (range 100–200 kDa), DDA ≥95%.

Preparation

The base formulation consisted of natural rubber (NR, 100 phr) and variable CTS content (0, 10, 20, 30, 40 phr). Five distinct chitosan grades, as previously detailed (Section ), were investigated: CTS, CTS-low MW1, CTS-low MW2, CTS-high MW, and CTS-med MW. Initially, the NR gum was sheared into strips and preblended using a two-roll mill at ambient temperature for 1 min. Subsequently, The designated chitosan type and loading were then incorporated into the NR matrix, followed by three crosswise cuts on each side and five triangular folds to ensure homogeneous dispersion. The obtained compound was sheeted out and aged at room temperature for 12 h.

Thermomechanical processing was carried out in a compression molding machine at 80 °C for 20 min (followed by cold pressing for 2 min) to activate dynamic ionic cross-linking without conventional curatives. All specimens were conditioned for 10 h prior to property characterization.

Characterization and Analysis

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was conducted using a TGAIRVERTEX70 spectrometer (Bruker GmbH, Germany) equipped with an ATR accessory. Spectral acquisition was performed in the range of 4000–500 cm–1 with 32 scans at 4 cm–1 resolution.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

Structural characterization of CTS, NR, and their composites was carried out on a D/max220 diffractometer (Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (60 kV,200Ma). Continuous scanning was employed from 5° to 50° (2θ) at a rate of 5°/min.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology of the vulcanized specimens was sputter-coated with a 5 nm Au/Pd layer (Quorum Q150T ES). Morphological examination was performed using a field-emission SEM (JSM-6700F, JEOL Ltd., Japan) operated at 3 kV with secondary electron detection.

Mechanical Testing

Uniaxial tensile tests were conducted on an AI-7000S universal testing machine (GaoTie Testing Machine Co., China) in accordance with ASTM D412. Specimens (n = 5) were stretched to failure at 500 mm/min.

Cyclic loading was performed on preconditioned specimens through successive strain level applications (50, 100, 150, and 200%) at a crosshead speed of 200 mm/min.

Self-healing performance was evaluated by manually aligning the undamaged interfacial regions of fractured specimens under controlled compressive loading, followed by a 20 min dwell time. Healing efficiency (η), a critical performance metric for self-healing materials, is defined as the ratio of the ultimate tensile strength measured via quasi-static tensile testing after the intervention to the tensile strength of the pristine material before mechanical damage.

NMR Measurement of Cross-Link Density

Cross-link density was characterized using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) relaxometry on an XLDS-15 cross-link density analyzer (NETZSCH, Selb, Germany). Measurements were performed at a magnetic field intensity of −0.35 T and a resonance frequency of 15 MHz. Transverse (T2) and longitudinal (T1) relaxation times were derived via inverse Laplace transform fitting of the NMR decay data. − The transverse relaxation time (T2), being inversely correlated with cross-link density (i.e., shorter T2 values indicate higher cross-link density), served as the primary indicator for comparing network structure among the samples.

Results and Discussion

FTIR Analysis of NR/CTS Composites

To elucidate the interfacial salt-forming interactions between CTS and NR, comparative FTIR analyses of NR, CTS, and NR/CTS composites were systematically conducted, as shown in Figure . The spectrum revealed distinct vibrational signatures in CTS, characterized by absorption bands at 1650 cm–1, attributable to the CO stretching vibration of residual acetyl groups; 1594 cm–1 to the N–H bending vibration of glucosamine units; and 1315 cm–1 to the C–N stretching mode. The fingerprint region exhibits characteristic carbohydrate structural motifs through absorption bands at 1152 cm–1, diagnostic of the C–O–C glycosidic linkage, and 1028 cm–1, representative of C–O stretching vibrations in primary hydroxyl groups. Crucially, the NR/CTS composite spectrum reveals two emergent features at 2363 cm–1 and 1741 cm–1, respectively assignable to the asymmetrical N–H bending vibration of protonated amine groups ([NH3]+) and the carbonyl stretching mode intrinsic to amide linkages. These spectral signatures provide direct evidence for ionic cross-linking ([COO]−-[NH3]+) at the NR/CTS interface. Specifically, the progressive intensification of the 1741 cm–1 band, coupled with a bathochromic shift from 1737 cm–1, establishes quantitative linear correlations between CTS concentration and ionically associated network formation. NMR Relaxation Parameters of NR/CTS Composites

The T2 of NR/CTS composites was systematically investigated by varying CTS MW and DDA. As summarized in the Table , T2 values exhibited a significant dependence on CTS characteristics. Low-MW CTS samples (1.53 kDa) showed the highest T2, with CTS-low MW1 registering 5.09 ms, followed by CTS-low MW2 at 4.89 ms. In contrast, high-MW CTS demonstrated a markedly reduced T2 of 3.62, which confirms that molecular weight elevation intensifies chain mobility restriction. This phenomenon primarily stems from extended polymer chains augmenting physical entanglement density and free energy barriers, thereby accelerating relaxation kinetics. The low-MW series revealed a distinct DDA-dependent effect wherein reduced deacetylation elevated T2 by 3–8%. This inverse correlation between DDA and chain confinement arises because lower DDA content decreases hydrogen-bond density, as acetyl groups disrupt polar interactions between CTS chains and the NR matrix. Consequently, both higher MW and higher DDA enhance polymer chain mobility restriction through synergistic mechanisms, while high-MW CTS dominates through topological constraints, high-DDA CTS amplifies electrostatic interactions.

Effect of CTS Content on Mechanical Properties of NR

The tensile performance of NR exhibits phase-dependent responses to CTS incorporation, fundamentally governed by competing interfacial mechanisms (Table and Figure a). Neat NR achieves a tensile strength of 0.84 MPa, primarily attributable to its intrinsic self-reinforcement via strain-induced crystallization during deformation. − Upon initial CTS addition (10 phr), strength precipitously declines 74% to 0.22 MPa, a phenomenon mechanistically rooted in crystallization disruption. Rigid CTS domains act as physical defects that locally hinder macromolecular alignment, thereby suppressing the formation of load-bearing crystalline regions. With progressive CTS loading beyond 10 phr, however, a critical mechanistic inversion occurs. Beyond 20 phr, CTS transforms from a defect into an active reinforcing network. This reinforcement drives nonlinear strength recovery to 0.40 ± 0.02 MPa at 40 phrrestoring 82% of the initial loss yet retaining a 52% deficit relative to neat NR.

Mechanical Properties of NR/CTS Composites

The tensile properties of NR/CTS composites are markedly influenced by the MW and DDA of the incorporated chitosan, as shown in Table . Incorporation of CTS-high MW significantly enhances tensile strength and modulus development, attributable to synergistic reinforcement from both densely distributed amine-mediated cross-links and robust topological constraints from elongated polymer chains. Conversely, CTS-low MW1 exhibits minimal strength and low moduli with elongation at break, reflecting chain slippage behavior dictated by its dual deficiencies: insufficient chemical cross-linking and negligible physical entanglement. A progressive restoration in properties is observed with increasing DDA at CTS-low MW2 shows intermediate strength and elongation at breakconfirming amino group density governs plasticity-modulus trade-offs. Significantly, CTS-med MW achieves optimal balance, retaining 83% of the strength enhancement seen in CTS-high MW while maintaining elongation, suggesting molecular weights provide critical entanglement networks without inducing excessive embrittlement. These systematic variations establish that chemical cross-linking primarily governs large-strain modulus, while physical entanglementsdominate early stage stiffness.

The cyclic tensile curves in Figure b provide critical insights into the dynamics of ionic cross-linking. NR/CTS composites exhibit substantially higher residual strain (ε = 186%) compared to pristine NR (ε = 66%) after four loading cycles. This behavior results from the dynamic reorganization of reversible ionic cross-links during deformation, where temporary dissociation of ionic bonds under stress allows network restructuring, and their subsequent reformation establishes new topological constraints that hinder molecular chain relaxation. The progressive increase in residual strain from <50% (first cycle) to 186% (fourth cycle) quantitatively demonstrates cumulative energy dissipation through successive bond rupture and reformation events. These unique viscoelastic characteristics directly correlate with enhanced self-healing performance. The energy-dissipative ionic network facilitates localized chain mobility during repair processes, while the high density of reconfigurable cross-links enables efficient restoration of mechanical integrity. This combination of tunable mechanical properties and intrinsic healability opens new application frontiers in advanced functional materials, particularly in medical-grade dielectric composites and high-performance polymeric adhesives, where damage tolerance and extended service life are critical.

Self-Healing Properties of NR/CTS Composites

The evaluation methodology for assessing self-healing capability in polymeric materials demonstrates remarkable operational simplicity. As illustrated in Figure , tensile specimens were bisected through the midsection and immediately brought into contact, followed by under ambient conditions for 20 min to facilitate autonomous repair. Figure a presents the mechanical response of healed NR/CTS composites under tensile loading, revealing exceptional healing efficiency, with specimens enduring elongations exceeding 200 mm without interfacial failure at the repaired region.

Cut samples after self-healing at room temperature for 20 min: (a) NR/CTS composites under stretching, (b) stereomicroscopy photos of neat NR and NR/CTS composites. Photograph courtesy of Liu Yang. Copyright 2025.

Critical examination using stereomicroscopy (Figure b) provides microstructural validation, demonstrating that pristine NR controls retained visible shear-induced discontinuities at the contact interface after healing, whereas NR/CTS composites exhibited negligible interfacial defects. The healed NR/CTS composites consistently withstood elongations exceeding 200 mm (with a tensile specimen gauge length of 25 mm) without failure at the healed interface. This confirms the CTS-enhanced healing dynamics facilitated by ionic bond reorganization mechanisms. ,

Quantitative healing efficiency (HE) was determined through tensile recovery tests, where HE is defined as the ratio of postrepair tensile strength to the original strength (HE = σhealed/σoriginal × 100%). As shown in Figure a, neat NR exhibited a modest HE of 37% due to its intrinsic autoadhesion properties. The incorporation of CTS significantly enhanced interfacial reconstruction, achieving near-complete restoration (98% HE) at an optimal CTS loading of 10 phr. This enhancement primarily results from dynamic ionic cross-links introduced by CTS, where reversible ion–dipole interactions enable rapid bond reconfiguration during the healing process. However, HE progressively decreases at CTS concentrations exceeding 10 phr (Figure a), with reduced healing performance attributed to two synergistic factors: restricted polymer chain mobility caused by excessive ionic cluster formation and steric hindrance effects that substantially impede ionic bond reformation dynamics. While CTS exhibits intrinsic adhesive properties that may facilitate interfacial contact between crack surfaces, , the observed nonmonotonic healing efficiency, rapid 20 min recovery kinetics, and FTIR-confirmed ionic bond formation collectively establish ionic cross-linking as the dominant self-healing mechanism.

The MW of chitosan emerges as the decisive factor governing healing efficiency in NR/CTS composites (Figure b), dominating over the DDA − Low-MW CTS achieves near-complete healing due to its minimal entanglement constraints, enabling rapid chain reptation and ionic bond reassociationDDA variations prove negligible as all exceed the critical amine density threshold for effective ionic reassembly. In contrast, high-MW CTS suffers severe mobility restriction from topological confinement, where extended chains enhance cross-link density but sterically impede bond reorganization kinetics, reducing healing to 70%.

Self-Healing Mechanism

Fresh natural rubber latex contains two distinct protein componentsα-globulin and rubberinalongside phospholipids. These proteins comprise 31 amino acid species. Partial protein removal during processing leaves residual proteinaceous residues entrapped within the crude rubber matrix. These residues induce terminal functionalization of NR macromolecular chains with decomposition products derived from fatty acids and phospholipids, predominantly pyrophosphate ester groups (Figure a).

Schematic illustration of formation of ammonium carboxylates links between NR and CTS: (a) degradation of NR phospholipid terminals generating –COOH groups; (b) ionic bonding between NR carboxylates and CTS ammonium groups.

These thermolabile esters undergo dual reactivation during thermal processing at 80 °C: Trapped trace water (0.8–1.2 wt %) mobilizes to hydrolyze pyrophosphate groups, generating proximal carboxylic acid motifs, with kinetic enhancement from bound-water activation at elevated temperatures. Simultaneously, thermal degradation of protein residues liberates endogenous alkaline componentsparticularly ethanolamine and cholinecreating confined high-pH microenvironments along the polymer chains. The nascent carboxyl groups exhibit strong electrophilicity toward CTS amino moieties. Within the locally alkaline domains, these acids undergo spontaneous deprotonation, enabling dynamic ionic cross-linking via pH-modulated electrostatic interactions (Figure b). This cooperative mechanism underpins autonomous self-healing capabilities of CTS-NR composites.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that chitosan (CTS)-modified natural rubber (NR) composites achieve exceptional self-healing efficiency (98% at 10 phr CTS) through dynamic ionic cross-linking between pyrophosphate ester termini on NR chains and amine groups of CTS. These reversible ([COO]−–[NH3]+) bonds enable rapid interfacial reorganization while maintaining mechanical integrity, as validated by FTIR (amide I band at 1741 cm–1), XRD (attenuation of CTS crystallinity at 20.12°), and SEM (homogeneous CTS dispersion). Critical insights from T2 relaxation reveal that low-MW CTS minimizes entanglement constraints, facilitating superior bond mobility and healing, while high-MW CTS restricts chain reptation. However, excessive CTS loading (>10 phr) introduces performance trade-offs due to restricted chain mobility from ionic cluster formation and steric hindrance, quantitively evidenced by cyclic tensile hysteresis and reduced elongation at break. The mechanism leverages native NR componentsphospholipid-derived pyrophosphate estersas zwitterionic anchors, establishing a biomimetic healing strategy applicable to protein-rich elastomers. While the material exhibits promising sustainability and scalability for medical or soft robotic applications, challenges persist in balancing tensile strength (22% decline at 10 phr CTS) with reversibility. In the future work, we would address hierarchical ionic network engineering and in situ bond kinetics monitoring to optimize lifecycle durability. This CTS-NR paradigm advances eco-friendly self-healing materials by bridging polysaccharide functionality with rubbery matrices, offering foundational insights for interfacing biopolymers with nonreactive polymers.
