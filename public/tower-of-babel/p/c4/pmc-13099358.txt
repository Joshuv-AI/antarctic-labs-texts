Unexplored biosynthetic gene clusters in bacteria isolated from Brazilian stingless bee honey with activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens
Authors: José Fhilipe De Miranda Da Silva, Carlos Filipe Ribeiro de Matos, Larissa Martins Moura, João Pedro da Cunha Tavares, Pâmela Talita de Aguiar e Silva, Patrícia Perina de Oliveira, Giovanna Felette de Paula, Per Sunnerhagen, Marcelo Brocchi, Elizabeth Bilsland

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Unexplored source of microorganisms with antibacterial activity;.•New species of Bacillus sp. with activity against multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae;.•Potential chemical and molecular novelty of antibacterial compounds based on genome mining.Keywords: Biological activity, Multidrug-resistant bacteria, Honey from native Brazilian stingless bees, Bacillus spp

Importance

There has been a steady decline in investment and interest in research on microbial natural products, mainly due to the depletion of traditional sources for new microorganisms and the high rediscovery of known molecules. In the case of bacterial infectious diseases, it has been over three decades since a new class of antibiotics entered the market. Furthermore, the spectrum of activity of existing antibiotics is becoming increasingly limited. It is therefore crucial to explore new sources of antibacterial compounds from underexplored environments, such as honey produced by native Brazilian stingless bees, which harbors microorganisms with activity against multidrug-resistant bacteria, and to harness the hidden antibiotic production potential through genome mining.

Introduction

The metabolism of any living organism can be distributed into two groups: primary metabolism, responsible for the vital functions of the organism, such as the production of carbohydrates, proteins and nucleic acids, and secondary metabolism, which is related to the production of molecules with specific ecological functions (Borges and Amorin, 2020), also referred as natural products (NPs). For millennia, humanity has empirically been using NPs for therapeutic purposes. During the 19th century, with the development of analytical chemistry, several drugs began to be purified from medicinal plants (Filho et al., 2001), resulting in the emergence of the field of chemistry of NPs, which aims to isolate and characterize secondary metabolites with biological activity (Geller-Mcgrath et al., 2023). Initially, the chemistry of natural products focused on metabolites originating from plants, but today only 25 % are extracted from plants and >50 % of all drugs originating from NPs are from bacteria (Kalkreuter et al., 2020; Newman and Cragg, 2020).

Microbial secondary metabolites are excellent sources of drug prototypes or potential drugs due to the almost infinite diversity of their molecular structures (only a small fraction of which has been characterized) and their enhanced targeting capability, a result of co-evolution that favors the selection of unique biochemical characteristics (Filho et al., 2001; Gogineni et al., 2020).

Currently, multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria are a global problem, being considered by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the ten main threats to global health (Brazil, 2021). Furthermore, according to the United Nations Environment Program (UNEP), more deaths across the planet related to antibiotic-resistant bacteria (around 10 million) are predicted for the year 2050 than the number of current deaths from cancer (United Nations Environment Programme, 2023). Thus, the fight against MDR bacteria is urgent, however, the development of novel antimicrobials requires around one to two decades of research and optimization. Furthermore, a decline in the discovery of new antimicrobials has been observed, which was further enhanced by the COVID-19 pandemic, delaying studies involving the search for antibiotics (Kwon and Powderly, 2021). This has resulted in more than three decades without the release of new classes of antibiotics on the market (Chevrette et al., 2019).

In addition, with the constant emergence of new microbial infectious diseases, it is necessary to develop new strategies for the discovery and production of new antimicrobial drugs, with microbial NPs being an excellent source of inspiration for these new compounds (Kalkreuter et al., 2020; Atanasov et al., 2015). To this end, a strategy that has been employed is the study of insect microbiota, which interact in a symbiotic way, contributing to the production of antimicrobial substances, which helps in the defense of these organisms (Chevrette et al., 2019).

The Insecta class represents the most abundant class among animals on the planet, participating in diverse interactions with microorganisms, where they present great potential for new classes of antimicrobials, especially when combined with modern genetic techniques (Xie et al., 2019). It is known that social insects, such as ants and bees, are susceptible to high parasitic pressure, making it necessary not only to defend themselves from pathogens, but also to protect their food sources. Thus, a strategy acquired throughout the evolution of these insects was the symbiotic association with microorganisms capable of producing metabolites with antibacterial and antifungal actions (Menegatti, 2016).

An important discovery of symbiotic association between insects and microorganisms was between Attini ants and actinobacteria, where it was revealed that these ants have their exoskeletons covered by actinobacteria Pseudonocardia sp., which biosynthesize antifungal compounds against Escovopsis sp., a specific pathogen of these ants (Currie et al., 1999). In more recent studies, using the same species of ants, several other NPs were revealed to be produced by other symbiotic actinobacteria, such as antimycins, actinomycins, valinomycin and cyfomycin (Schoenian et al., 2011; Chevrette et al., 2019).

There is little work involving microbiota associated with stingless beeshives, as well as their potential for producing bioactive metabolites, making it an excellent field for discovering new natural products with biological activity (Cusinato, 2020). The primary sources of the microbiota present in honey are: air, water, dust, soil, the pollination environment (nectar, pollen, flowers), the nest and the intestinal microbiota of the bee and other insects that may enter the hive. Hence, as we purchased honey from bee species with different environmental niches, we broadened the diversity in microorganisms we found in each sample. It is imperative to highlight that working with commercial honey, a secondary source of microorganisms could be harvest and post-harvest manipulations of the product (Tsadila, Amoroso, Mossialos, 2023; Christensen et al., 2024; de Paula et al., 2021).

The first report in the literature of a symbiotic association between stingless bees and microorganisms was in 2015, involving Scaptotrigona depilis bees and Zygosaccharomyces sp. fungi, for nutritional purposes, where the latter acts as a source of ergosterol for the larvae of these bees (Menezes et al., 2015). Regarding substances produced by symbionts used by bees as defense agents, eleven compounds, belonging to the anthracycline class, were isolated from the association of Melipona scutellaris with Micromonospora sp. (Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2019). Thus, different species of stingless bees represent new sources for discovering new bioactive metabolites. Several new species of fungi and bacteria that produce antimicrobials were previously found in Brazilian bee hives (Santos et al., 2023; Cusinato, 2020), however, the literature provides little information regarding the metabolites produced by these microorganisms, presenting a high potential for discovering new bioactive compounds. Honey in particular is a little explored source of microorganisms, thus we provide a novel source of robust microorganisms with excellent antimicrobial properties. In the present work we isolated hundreds of bacteria from honey produced by brazilian stingless bees, evaluated their antimicrobial activity against antibiotic resistant bacteria, sequenced the genomes of two selected isolates and predicted a potential antibacterial activity for one putative compound.

Results

Microbiota associated with commercial honeys and biological activity of isolates

We diluted and plated 12 commercial honey samples derived from different species of native Brazilian stingless bees onto 15 different culture media, obtaining isolates from 13 of these media, resulting in 378 isolated microorganisms, with a range of colony morphologies, as illustrated in Fig. 1, Figure S1 and Table S1. When comparing different culture media, morphologically similar colonies could be observed, requiring taxonomic analysis and partial sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene regions to estimate the number of species isolated. It is worth noting that on the HS-Ethanol and MCK media, no microbial growth was observed.

Initially, we performed agar diffusion tests against standard laboratory Escherichia coli DH5α to investigate the formation of inhibition halos by each of the microorganisms isolated from honey samples (378). We selected a standard laboratory strain due to its safety profile, tolerance to antimicrobials and ease to be employed on a large scale.

We thus observed the formation of inhibition halos for 103 isolates, with diameters varying between 8 and 29 mm, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Table S2 presents the results of inhibition halos by culture media.

Plates illustrating inhibition halo tests performed by spotting isolated strains onto lawns of E. coli DH5α. Swabs were used to spread OD600 0.1 culture of E. coli DH5α on the surface of plates, followed by spotting 5 μl of isolated cell suspensions dropped onto paper discs placed on the plates’ surface. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h.

We then selected 100 isolates (44 active against E. coli DH5α and 56 not active against the same strain) to perform the disk diffusion test against five ATCCs (American Type Culture Collection), namely: E. coli strain 1532 (ATCC 35,218, resistant to ampicillin), E. coli strain Seattle 1946 (ATCC 25,922), Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus strain F-182 (ATCC 43,300, resistant to methicillin and oxacillin), Enterococcus faecalis strain NJ-3 (ATCC 51,299, resistant to vancomycin and susceptible to teicoplanin) and Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Enteritidis strain CDC K-1891 (ATCC 13,076). As a result, we obtained that 61 isolates showed action against S. aureus 43,300, 30 against E. coli 35,218, 31 against E. coli 25,922, 25 against E. faecalis 51,299 and 11 against S. enterica 13,076. The results can be seen in Fig. 3 and S2.

We performed an UpSet analysis to visualize the profile of combinations between the antimicrobial activities of our isolates (Fig. 4A). Through this analysis, we calculated a coverage of approximately 53 % for E. coli DH5α, which indicates that it could be used as a pre-screening, but not as a predictive model for activity against ATCC strains. Isolates that demonstrated activity against the screening strain exhibited significantly higher antimicrobial activity values ​​against ATCC strains than non-active isolates. This observed difference was statistically significant (Fig. 4B, Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.001), confirming a strong positive association between successful screening of the laboratory strain and subsequent antimicrobial efficacy against ATCC strains. Finally, when evaluating the frequency of isolates with antimicrobial activity against ATCCs, in the groups with and without activity against E. coli DH5α, we observed a significantly higher percentage of isolates with activity against 4 or 5 ATCCs in the group with activity against the laboratory strain. Isolates without activity against E. coli mostly show activity against none or only 1 ATCC (Fig. 4C).

We screened the activity of 59 isolates against Klebsiella pneumoniae (Fig. 5), using three different strains of the pathogen: K. pneumoniae strain NCTC 9633 (ATCC 13,883), which does not present any natural resistance to antibiotics, K. quasipneumoniae K6 strain (ATCC 700,603), which produces extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL), and K. pneumoniae 1000,523 (ATCC BAA-2146), which is resistant to several classes of antibiotics.

Antibacterial activity against different strains of K. pneumoniae. The results show two promising microorganisms against the most antibiotic-resistant strain, K. pneumoniae 1000,527.

We observed activity of 41 isolates against this strain, thus having 69 % of antibiotic activity against the K. pneumoniae NCTC 9633. Next, we evaluated antimicrobial activity against the K. quasipneumoniae K6 strain, which produces extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL), specifically the SHV-18 enzyme, making it resistant to β-lactam antibiotics, especially third-generation cephalosporins, such as ceftazidime. We identified five isolates with antibiotic activity (8.5 %) against K6, three of which were also active against the NCTC 9633 strain. Finally, we used K. pneumoniae 1000,523, including resistance to β-lactams (cephalosporins, carbapenems, and penicillins), some fluoroquinolones, and some aminoglycosides, among others, making it a more challenging strain. As a result, we had two isolates (3.5 %) that showed antibiotic activity against the strain. One of these isolates, showed activity against the three strains tested, while the second showed activity against NCTC 9633 and 1000,523.

Molecular identification of the microbiota associated with commercial honeys

We then performed PCR, using pools of primers directed to the coding region for 16S rRNA from bacteria or 18S from fungi, for some microorganisms selected based on their antimicrobial activities and unique colony morphology. The selected primers were designed using the mopo16S algorithm, by Sambo et al. (2018). The pools with primer R1.2 and primer R1.1 annealed to the DNA of most of the microorganisms tested, obtaining fragments of around 650 bp (Figure S3). Therefore, we used both primer combinations for large-scale molecular identification of isolates.

After purification of the PCR product, they were sequenced using the Sanger method (Centro Genomics – USP), and sequencing results were compared to public sequences using blastN (NCBI) and EzBioCloud (Table S3). By resolving the 16S rRNA gene region, we identified a species of Streptomyces sp. among the isolates, which had biological activity against S. aureus 43,300.

Microorganisms of the Bacillus genus dominated the bioprospection, which was already expected. The literature points to an abundance of Bacillus isolated in honey and other regions of the hive, especially B. amyloliquefaciens (Sinacori et al., 2014). Pomastowski et al. (2019) also pointed out the predominant presence of B. subtilis and B. cereus in honey samples from different geographic origins. The main justification lies in the ubiquity of the species in the environment and their function in hives and the potential deterioration of honey. Fig. 6 illustrates the colony morphology of the isolates while Figure S4 shows some of the microorganisms identified as Bacillus spp. under an optical microscope.

Colony morphology of selected microorganisms mentioned in this work. A - M14.48; B - M12.43B; C - M8.44;D - M14.50; E - M15.18; F - M12.30; G - M11.9; H - M12.33.

Some genera identified in our screens, such as Heyndrickxia and Niallia, were formerly classified as Bacillus (Gupta et al., 2020), thus, we can observe the predominant presence of species of the genus Bacillus or related species. The biotechnological potential of Bacillus has already been widely studied, with several clinical and agricultural applications. That is confirmed by our data demonstrating the antimicrobial activity of the isolates, especially against antibiotic-resistant strains, which could counteract the decline in the efficiency of antibiotics currently used in the clinic.

Phylogenomic analysis of isolates with activity against K. pneumoniae BAA-2146

We sequenced the genomes of the two isolates (M12–28 and M14–41) that exhibited antimicrobial activity against K. pneumoniae BAA-2146, aiming to perform genome mining for potential new biosynthetic clusters (Table S4). For isolate M12–28, the closest sequenced strains was Shouchella rhizosphaerae SC-N012, with a d4 dDDH (by TYGS, Meier-Kolthoff et al., 2022) of 94.5, confirming that the isolate belonged to the same species. For isolate M14–41, the highest d4 dDDH was 75.9 compared with Bacillus thuringiensis ATCC 10,792, a value slightly above the standard for considering a candidate new species. Nevertheless, there was the indication that the isolate is a potential new species, as it does not belong to any species found in the TYGS database. For S. rhizosphaerae M12–28, there are four distinct biosynthetic classes among the BGCs present, with a cluster related to ectoine synthesis, and one each for T3PKS, NI-siderophore, and lanthipeptide class I. For the isolate Bacillus sp. M14–41, there are clusters related to NRPS, terpenes, betalactone, furan, and NI-siderophore. Thus, we see a wide variety of specialized metabolites potentially produced by the two isolates. Six clusters showed similarity confidence with the antiSMASH database, one medium for S. rhizosphaerae M12–28 and three high and two low for Bacillus sp. M14–41, showing great potential for metabolites not yet biosynthetically characterized.

We then used NPBdetect machine learning (Goyat et al., 2025) to predict the biological activity of BGCs, with the aim of mining promising clusters with antibacterial activity. The NPBdetect is trained to indicate positive activity for clusters with p > 0.5. Thus, the model predicted two clusters with antibacterial activity for S. rhizosphaerae M12–28 and four for Bacillus sp. M14–41 (Fig. 8).

We used the genomes annotated by antiSMASH for an analysis in ARTS 2.0 (Mungan et al., 2020), which predicts antibiotic resistance genes across the entire genome. What makes this particularly interesting is that, with the biosynthetic regions annotated, we can observe resistance genes or mechanisms, such as duplication of essential genes, near or within these clusters, which is a strong indication of self-protection against bacterial activity. For S. rhizosphaerae M12–28, 16 resistance genes were found within the genome, including the efflux pump EmrB, glycine cleavage system H protein, and DNA gyrase. Two essential gene duplications were found near biosynthetic clusters: sortase, duplicated near regions 2 (NRPS) and 4 (lanthipeptide), and ribokinase, also duplicated in region 4.

For the Bacillus sp. M14–41 isolate, 17 resistance genes were predicted, two near biosynthetic clusters. There is a gene encoding an EmrB efflux pump near region 4 (NRPS) and a dihydrolipoyllysine-residue succinyltransferase near region 2, strongly indicating the antimicrobial activity and functionality of both clusters. Furthermore, there are 16 essential genes duplicated near or within biosynthetic clusters, including three duplicated stage V sporulation genes in region 11 (lanthipeptide), an ATP-dependent Clp endopeptidase also in region 11, two duplicated genes in region 5 (amino acid carrier protein and threonine-tRNA ligase), four genes in region 10 (terpene), three genes in region 1 (NRPS-like), and two in region 8 (bacteriocin).

Finally, we used CHAMOIS (Larralde, 2025) machine learning to predict the chemical profile of the metabolites potentially produced by our isolated strains. CHAMOIS generates a chemical hierarchy with chemical class probabilities in the final BGC product; the full results of our clusters can be found in Text S1. To simplify visualization of the chemical potential of our isolates, we plotted an UpSet graph containing all predicted chemical class combinations in the genomes (Figure S6).

BiG-SCAPE cluster reveal the potential new metabolites

We used the BiG-SCAPE 2.0 cluster (Navarro-Muñoz et al., 2020), with a cutoff of 0.3, and compared it with the MIBiG 4.0 repository (Zdouc et al., 2025) to group our BGCs into families (GCFs) and predict the compounds produced by our two isolates (Oyedele, 2025). All BGCs from S. rhizosphaerae M12–28 clustered into singleton families, showing potential to produce new compounds compared to those deposited in the repository. For Bacillus sp. M14–41, 10 clusters formed singleton GCFs, 1 cluster (region 12) grouped with BGC0000502 (cerecidin A1 to A7), and BGC region 4 grouped with BGC0000942 (petrobactin).

The function of petrobactin also appears to be linked to the growth and oxidative stress of microorganisms in the environment, being a protective mechanism already documented in species of the genus Bacillus (Manetsberger et al., 2025; Hagan et al., 2018).

Finally, an analog of the cerecidin family, encoded by BGC 8, was predicted (Fig. 9). Since it is a lanthipeptide, a promising class for biotechnological applications, especially as an antimicrobial (García-Ausencio et al., 2025; Ramírez-Rendón et al., 2023; Dischinger et al., 2014), a more in-depth analysis was performed. First, we analyzed the conservation between the BGC of our isolate and the cluster responsible for the cerecidin family (Fig. 10A). We then compared the predictions of the NPBdetect and CHAMOIS machine learning for the clusters (Fig. 10B, C).

There is a high similarity between region 8 and cluster BGC0000502 (present in B. cereus As 1.1846), which is validated as a cerecidin biosynthetic cluster. Although analysis of BGC0000502 using NPBdetect revealed an absence of antibacterial activity, with a p-value slightly below 0.5, the isolated molecules A1 and A7 showed antibacterial activity against S. aureus MRSA and vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis (Wang et al., 2014).

Regarding the comparison of the chemical profiles within the family, there are also specific differences between region 8 of our isolate and BGC0000502, such as the prediction of a monocarboxylic acid or derivatives for BGC0000502. In fact, as they are post-translationally modified ribosomal peptides, they present both a C-terminal region, directly derived from a monocarboxylic acid, and aspartic acid and glutamic acid residues, which provide free lateral carboxyls. A greater probability of fatty acids in the metabolite of region 8 (cerecidins do not contain acyl chains derived from fatty acids) and only region 8 presents a probability of fatty amides (amides formed between fatty acid and amine, as for example present in surfactin) and N-acyl amines (in fact, both are also not present in cerecidins).

The three specific differences observed in region 8 when compared to the cerecidin cluster derive from the same root in the CHAMOIS hierarchical tree and are thus related. There is a 0.97 probability that the metabolite produced by region 8 contains fatty acyls, due to the presence of a fatty amine (0.85), which in turn would be an N-acyl amine (0.85). Chemically and molecularly speaking, it is possible for a ribosomal peptide to undergo modification with a fatty chain; however, an analysis of the genes in region 8 (Fig. 10A) did not indicate the presence of any genes with pfam domains for acetylation with a long fatty acid chain (such as GNAT or ACP). We then used RiPPMiner-Genome 2.0 (Agrawal et al., 2021) to predict the structure produced by region 8 (Fig. 10B) and compare it with the structure of cerecidin (Fig. 10C).

Manual analysis of BGC showed the presence of all components necessary for cluster functionality, including precursor peptides (with sequential copies, as in cerecidin BGC), LanM, responsible for peptide modification reactions (dehydration, elimination, and cyclization), ABC transporters and C39 and S8 peptidase (responsible for export and cleavage of the peptide core), and regulation based on two components as well as HTH (Lagedroste et al., 2020; Castro et al., 2021). All these components together suggest, in addition to cluster functionality, the antibacterial activity of its product, mainly due to the presence of peptidases along with ABC transporters, the main self-immunity mechanism of lanthipeptides (Smits et al., 2020). The putative structure (1, Fig. 10B), is very similar to cerecidin A1 and A7, having one more carbon than A1 (which still has an additional hydroxyl) and two more than the A7. The potential production of a cerecidin analog is corroborated by the phenotype of the isolate, which showed activity against S. aureus MRAS, although it did not show activity against E. faecalis, which further explains the specific difference between cerecidin A1, since when compared with A7, only the former showed activity against vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis (WANG et al., 2014).

Our results highlight the significant biotechnological potential of microorganisms isolated from honey from native Brazilian stingless bees, species still underexplored but with rich microbial diversity associated. Genome mining revealed significant diversity and complexity in the biosynthetic clusters present in the genomes of the two most promising isolates. This approach allowed us to identify possible pathways for the production of specialized metabolites and raise the hypothesis that one of the isolates may synthesize a putative cerecidine analog which could explain the observed antimicrobial phenotype. Thus, we highlight the importance of integrating experimental and computational approaches in the search for new antimicrobial compounds and highlight the unique potential of seeking new sources for bioprospecting specialized metabolites.

Discussion

We observed a diverse microbiota isolated from commercial honeys from Brazilian native stingless bees, in spite of the harsh nature of this environment. The literature points to a strong symbiotic relationship between bees and microbiota, which help bees in the fermentation of honey (for preservation in the hives), in the transformation of pollen into stored food, in defense against pathogens through the release of metabolites and also as food for bees (de Paula et al., 2021). However, the composition of this population might vary significantly from the hive and through the processing and prolonged storage of the honey samples, therefore the present work does not attempt to provide insights on the microbiota of the hive, but on a subset of bacteria with remarkable stress tolerance and that are amenable to cultivation under laboratory conditions.

Previous published work on the microbiota present in fresh pollen and propolis indicate a presence of actinobacteria, especially Streptomyces, most of which have great antimicrobial potential (Garcia-Mazcorro et al., 2019; Ersoy Omeroglu et al., 2023; Grubbs et al., 2021). However, reports of actinobacteria in honey samples are very scarce, and all are from fresh samples (Reichardt et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2019). When working with processed commercial honey, we noticed the predominance of bacteria of the genus Bacillus, which are remarkably resistant to high temperatures and the honey fermentation process. Although the specialized metabolism of Bacillus strains is more restricted than that of Streptomyces, bacteria of this genus are still recognized as robust producers of antibiotics against clinically important bacterial species (Freitas-Silva et al., 2024; Hallaj-Nezhadi et al., 2022; Naglimat et al., 2019; Zulkhairi Amin et al., 2020). Thus, it is possible to envision the potential for the discovery of new antibiotics, given the variety of microorganisms isolated from commercial honeys with activity against the various pathogens tested, including multidrug-resistant strains. Previous studies, such as that of Oliveira et al. (2026), highlight the large quantity of Bacillus present in samples of commercial honeys and their biotechnological applications.

We decided to use live cells of isolates on ATCC-matched plates instead of crude bacterial extracts or fractions for antibacterial activity tests, since many biosynthetic clusters are not expressed under conditions that facilitate bacterial growth of single cultures (Murtaza et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2018). Thus, when co-culture is used as an analytical method, we expect to uncover a larger proportion of strains with antibacterial activity.

Multidrug resistant K. pneumoniae presents one of the largest threats to human lives in hospital environments, thus it was encouraging to identify two isolates with activity against this pathogen. Whole genome sequence, followed by phylogenetic analysis predicted that Bacillus sp. M14–41 is a potential new species or subspecies, highlighting that the use of little-explored products as a source for bioprospecting opens up a vast opportunity for the discovery of new organisms with potential antibacterial activity. By genome mining these isolates, we identified a consistent number of biosynthetic clusters for the genera in question and used a series of tools and in silico pipelines to understand the biosynthetic potential of our isolates. We identified a large number of biosynthetic clusters that have not yet been characterized, which did not show clustering with the MIBiG 4.0 repository (the most accepted method for predicting BGC products, using a cutoff of 0.3). This number is most significant in the S. rhizosphaerae M12–28 isolate, none of whose BGCs showed similarity to MIBiG clusters, demonstrating potential for chemical novelty, which is being explored by our group. Furthermore, there are no reports in the literature of the use of the Shouchella genus for the discovery of new antimicrobials, which further increases its biotechnological potential.

One limitation of this work is the lack of chemical analyses through isolating and identifying metabolites in active fractions, requiring further research to address this need. To date, no experimental relationship has been established between the cluster in question and the compound produced, nor is there any experimental proof of its responsibility for the observed antimicrobial phenotype; only an assumption based on in silico analyses and experimental data in the literature suggests the cluster as a candidate to produce an antimicrobial compound. Therefore, we will now focus our activities on isolating and characterizing, guided by genomes, the potential metabolites responsible for the antibacterial activity of the two isolates.

Employing a number of in silico resources to perform detailed analysis of a Bacillus sp. 14–41 BCG with similarity to the characterized cerecidin cluster, we observed that our isolate encodes genes for the production of a putative cerecidin analog, which may be related to its antimicrobial phenotype against K. pneumoniae BAA-2146. All of this was only possible thanks to advances in bioinformatics, which have proven crucial to modern natural product chemistry as a way to address the MDR bacteria crisis.

Conclusions

We isolated 378 microorganisms from commercial honeys from Brazilian bees, 27 % of which exhibited antimicrobial activity against E. coli DH5α, which was a proxy for predicting biological activity against ATCC strains. For example, we observed strong activity of our isolates against clinically important species, such as a S. aureus MRSA strain, where 61 % of the prospected were active. Through bioprospection, allied with phenotypic characterization and genome mining to explore the cryptic BCGs of the isolated strains, we were able to identify a potentially new Bacillus species active against the multidrug resistant K. pneumoniae BAA-2146. In depth genome analysis of Bacillus sp. 14–41 also allowed the identification of a BCG responsible for the production of a putative cerecidin analogue.

All analyses related to the putative compounds produced by the isolates were performed using bioinformatics tools and still lack experimental data for confirmation. The in silico analysis can now guide the search for the individual molecules by UHPLC-MS, to dereplicate their chemical novelty and molecular identification of metabolites with biological activity. This should aid in the development of genome scale metabolic models to direct media optimization or strain engineering, with the goal of enhancing the production of the desired antibiotics.

Materials and methods

Isolation of microorganisms present in commercial honeys

For the isolation of microorganisms, 12 samples of honey from the companies “Abelha Brasil” (AB) and “Mbee” were used, which were from native stingless bees, as shown in Table S5. The access to the Brazilian biodiversity was registered with the “Sistema Nacional de Gestão do Patrimônio Genético e do Conhecimento Tradicional Associado”(SisGen) under the project A4CC0B1, in compliance with the Nagoya protocol. We chose these companies due to the honey preparation cycle, with free access to the plants and low-scale production, ensuring care for the hives and an environment as close to natural as possible.

Different solid culture media (agar) were used to selectively promote the growth of microorganisms present in honey samples, with all growth carried out inside a laminar flow chamber. The culture media used were: YPD, LB, TMA, TSA, HS-citric acid, HS-Ethanol, ISP-2, CSA, BHI, MCK, MSA, Benett and Mueller-Hinton. We plated the honey-derived samples on August 4, 2023, corresponding to 682 days after the manufacturing date of Guaraipo honey (Abelha Brasil), 681 days after the manufacturing date of Tubuna honey (Abelha Brasil), 676 days after the manufacturing date of Mandaçaia honey (Abelha Brasil), and 477 days after the manufacturing date of the honeys from mBee. To culture the microorganisms, the honey samples, removed from the sealed packaging inside the laminar flow chamber, were diluted 1:3 with pure water (HPLC level) sterile, with 100 μL of diluted sample being applied to each plate. They were then incubated for 3 days at 30 °C. After the microorganisms grew, they were isolated in new Petri dishes and also inoculated in their respective liquid media to carry out disk diffusion experiments.

Antimicrobial activity of isolates against E. coli DH5α and ATCCs strains

We tested the antimicrobial activity of the isolates through inhibition halo experiments, adapting the protocol present in BrCast (Brazilian Committee for Antimicrobial Sensitivity Tests), version 2024, for antibiograms.

Eight ATCCs (American Type Culture Collection) strains were used for the test, namely: E. coli strain 1532 (ATCC 35,218, resistant to ampicillin), E. coli strain Seattle 1946 (ATCC 25,922), Staphylococcus aureus subsp. aureus strain F-182 (ATCC 43,300, resistant to methicillin and oxacillin), Enterococcus faecalis strain NJ-3 (ATCC 51,299, resistant to vancomycin and susceptible to teicoplanin), Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Enteritidis strain CDC K-1891 (ATCC 13,076), K. pneumoniae strain NCTC 9633 (ATCC 13,883), K. quasipneumoniae K6 strain (ATCC 700,603), which produces extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL), and K. pneumoniae 1000,523 (ATCC BAA-2146), which is resistant to several classes of antibiotics.

ATCCs were cultured in Petri dishes with Mueller-Hinton agar and incubated for overnight growth. We then suspended colonies of each strain in 10x PBS (diluted 1:10 and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter) adjusting the inoculum to obtain turbidity with an OD600 nm of 0.1.

Having all the inocula ready, we started testing: we dipped a sterile cotton swab in the ATCC suspension, spread the inoculum evenly over the Mueller-Hinton agar plates in three directions, applied 5 mm sterile paper discs on the plate and then we transferred 5 μL of each isolate to the paper discs. We incubated the plates at 35 °C for 18 h. The halos were then recorded, measured and analyzed.

For statistical analysis of the effectiveness of E. coli DH5α, we used 100 isolates (50 with activity and 50 without) for tests with ATCCs. Then, we separated this sum into two groups: those with activity against E. coli DH5α and those without activity against the laboratory strain, and compared them with the results against ATCCs.

Molecular identification of isolates

The gDNA of each isolate was extracted to sequence the gene encoding 16S rRNA, following the LiAc protocol (Clark, 2022). Amplification of the 16S region was carried out by PCR, using primers described in Table S6 (Sambo et al., 2018). For the PCR reaction, we used New England Biolabs protocol (2015). Pools of F primers are assembled for each R primer used. After amplification of the 16S region, a 1.5 % agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide is used to confirm the amplification, subjecting it to electrophoresis at 110 V for 100 min.

Then, we purified the PCR product, using the Biospin gel and PCR Purification Kit – BioFlux or equivalent, eluting the samples with ultrapure water, for sequencing them, by Sanger (Centro Genomics – USP). Sequencing results are opened using SnapGene View v. software. 7.0.2 and then compared with the NCBI databases, using nucleotide BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?CMD=Web&PAGE_TYPE =BlastHome) and EZBioCloud, database data set for 16S sequences. After surveying the species with the greatest similarity, sequencing reading errors are corrected manually, comparing the alignment between the isolate and the similar species and the fluorescence peaks observed in that base pair, resulting in the final fragment of 16S rRNA elucidated.

Phylogenomic and genome mining

To sequence the whole genome of our two isolates, we extracted gDNA using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit, following the brand's protocol. The genomes were hybrid sequenced by Illumina and Nanopore, annotated by Bakta (Schwengers et al., 2021), and quality checked by CheckM (Parks et al., 2015). Sequencing services were performed by Plasmidisaurus (OR, USA).

With the genomes assembled and polished, we used TYGS for phylogenomic analysis to identify the isolates' species. TYGS calculates the digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) between the genome of our strain and several reference species with close TETRA coding sequences. For phylogenomic analyses we used the web versions of TYGS (tygs.dsmz.de) and JSpeciesWS (https://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws).

For genome mining, we used web versions of the following software: antiSMASH 8.0.4 (antismash.secondarymetabolites.org) in strict mode, with the additional features KnownClusterBlast, MIBiG cluster comparison, ClusterBlast, ActiveSiteFinder, Pfam-based GO term annotation, NCBI genomic context links, RREFinder, and TIGRFam analysis enabled, ARTS 2.0 (https://arts.ziemertlab.com) with Duplication & BGC Proximity screen, Phylogeny screen, Resistance Model screen Domain of unknown function screen (DUF) and Exploration mode active, and RiPPMiner-Genome (http://www.nii.ac.in/rippminer2.html). We used BiG-SCAPE/CORASON cluster mode (https://github.com/medema-group/BiG-SCAPE) with a 0.3 default cutoff, –mix and –mibig-version 4.0, and NPBdetect (https://github.com/cbl-nabi/NPBDetect), with prediction type HC. For CHAMOIS, we followed the pipeline available at https://git.lumc.nl/mflarralde/chamois with default parameters.
