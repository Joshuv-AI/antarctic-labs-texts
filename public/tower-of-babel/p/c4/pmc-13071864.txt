Temporal Changes in Vertebral Morphology of the Free‐Ranging Rhesus Macaques From Cayo Santiago, Puerto Rico
Authors: Catalina I Villamil, Jeziel J Negrón, Emily R Middleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe objective of this study is to assess temporal change in vertebral size and shape in macaques born on Cayo Santiago from 1951 to 2002 in order to clarify patterns of temporal change identified in the cranium, appendicular skeleton, and body mass.Materials and MethodsWe assessed overall size, vertebral body height, and overall shape using a published dataset of 3D coordinate data representing six vertebrae from 132 female and 78 male Rhesus macaques from Cayo Santiago. We performed multiple linear regression with year of birth and age at death as independent variables, and assessed differences between early (1950–1965) and late (1985–2002) cohorts using standard statistical methods.ResultsWe found decreases in overall size and vertebral body height over time across vertebrae. Females decreased more than males, resulting in greater sexual dimorphism over time. We found no evidence of vertebral shape change. Incidentally, we found that thoracolumbar vertebral body height is not sexually dimorphic in this group and that males largely maintain vertebral body height over time.DiscussionOur results are consistent with an overall reduction of the skeleton in this population rather than localized reductions in specific body regions or in soft tissue mass. Females displayed more extreme changes than males, and this pattern may suggest that females are under stronger selection for environmental reasons, possibly due to the physiological demands of pregnancy. Female–male differences may also reflect early female age at first birth, which occurs prior to full skeletal maturation and may directly affect female growth.Keywords: acclimation, phenotypic plasticity, phenotypic variation, sexual dimorphism, vertebral column

Introduction

Understanding phenotypic variation is of fundamental importance in the evolutionary sciences, including biological anthropology. Phenotypic variation may have multiple origins at the population level, which are often dichotomized into long‐term adaptive processes that occur over generations and shift population medians, and shorter‐term processes such as phenotypic plasticity and acclimation, which occur within a single individual's lifetime (West‐Eberhard 1989; Wilson and Franklin 2002; Lickliter 2014; Grenier et al. 2016; Collier et al. 2019; Klingenberg 2019). On a theoretical level, understanding phenotypic variation can provide insight into evolutionary and developmental processes both in general and within specific taxa (Richardson 1999; Stern 2000; Klingenberg 2019). On a practical level, understanding phenotypic variation provides a basis for assessing traits that may be of evolutionary significance in comparative studies, and for making species delimitations in the living and fossil record, especially for skeletal materials for which behavioral and soft tissue data are often absent (Plavcan and Cope 2001; Antón et al. 2016). From this perspective, it is especially important to understand how the phenotype changes over time within populations, as both our living skeletal samples and fossil samples generally reflect restricted time points, with little clarity on the processes and dynamics at play “in between.” However, studies of within‐population phenotypic change over time in primates are generally rare.

The free‐ranging Macaca mulatta population on Cayo Santiago, Puerto Rico, presents a unique opportunity to study temporal changes within a single, isolated population. A group of about 400 Indian‐origin Rhesus macaques was brought to the Cayo Santiago island in 1938, where they have lived and reproduced since that time (Carpenter 1972). The colony underwent at least one major population bottleneck in its early period, dwindling to about 150 individuals in the 1950s, then increasing again in the 1960s (Carpenter 1972). A formal dietary supplementation plan was started in 1956, and in the 1960s, the colony began to receive commercial monkey chow (Windle 1980; Kessler and Rawlins 2016). This monkey chow, in turn, switched from a 15% protein mix to a 25% protein mix in 1969 (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987). The colony has also undergone various culls that have removed large numbers of individuals at various points in its history, most notably in 1944, 1968–1972, and 1984 (Hernandez‐Pacheco et al. 2016; Kessler and Rawlins 2016), but has maintained high genetic diversity (Widdig et al. 2016; Kanthaswamy et al. 2017), although, to our knowledge, no studies have explicitly addressed phenotypic variation before and after these bottlenecks and culls. Cayo Santiago represents a more tropical climate than the population's origin region in India (Francis and Wang 2023) and has also experienced several hurricanes throughout its history, with significant impacts on the vegetation and topology of the island (Kessler and Rawlins 2016). Skeletons began to be systematically collected from the island in the 1970s, with some skeletons preserved before that time (Kessler and Rawlins 2016).

Previous studies in this population have assessed changes in body size and both cranial and postcranial elements. One study identified increases in limb length and cranial size, and changes in limb proportions, between 1951 and 1979 (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987). These increases were attributed to changes in access to food, reflecting the interplay between improved access to protein and calories from monkey chow and changes in population density that might affect access to the chow (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987). Further, DeRousseau and Reichs (1987) also identified differences between females and males, which could signal a differential response to improved resource access across the sexes. Weinstein (2001) and, to a more limited extent, Antón et al. (2016), also compared body size, limb lengths, and limb proportions in the Cayo Santiago macaques in comparison to M. mulatta in other regions and climates, and found a pattern of larger bodies and longer limbs with no changes in limb proportions, with these differences again attributed to steady food provisioning. A third study attempted to assess selection pressures in female macaques from Cayo Santiago and determined there was significant selection in favor of larger body size, especially cranial size (Blomquist and Turnquist 2011). Regardless, this same study identified a significant decline in female body size from 1959 to 1987, contrary to previous researchers, and linked this pattern to increasing population density on the island (Blomquist and Turnquist 2011). A more recent study expanded both the timeline (1951–2002) and the sample size (635 total individuals) to assess temporal changes in limb lengths, limb ratios, and body weight, explicitly testing whether patterns of change were consistent with acclimation to a tropical climate (Francis and Wang 2023). This study found a significant decline in body mass and long bone diaphyseal breadth measurements, with greater overall decreases in body size in females than in males (Francis and Wang 2023). This decline was associated with the maintenance of long bone lengths, resulting in a longer, leaner body, which the authors attribute to acclimation consistent with Bergmann's and Allen's rules (Francis and Wang 2023).

Previous studies on temporal changes in the morphology of the Cayo Santiago macaques have focused on body mass or on skeletal measurements of the limbs and crania as proxies for overall skeletal size. However, no studies have assessed temporal change in the vertebral column. The vertebral column is an important component of overall body size, contributing to torso length, and is heavily involved in positional behavior and in supporting the body's soft tissues in various stages of motion (Slijper 1946; Schilling and Hackert 2006; Schilling 2011; Williams et al. 2019). The vertebral column additionally undergoes significant evolutionary changes among taxa with different locomotor repertoires and body sizes (Shapiro 1993, 1995, 2007; Johnson and Shapiro 1998; Nalley and Grider‐Potter 2015; Williams et al. 2019; Kinoshita and Hirasaki 2024). As a result, assessing temporal change in vertebral size and morphology may clarify some of the patterns observed in previous studies for the appendicular and cranial skeleton, and can also help clarify what patterns we should expect from other populations with poorer sampling, such as fossils.

Here, we present a study on changes in the vertebral column of the Cayo Santiago population born between 1951 and 2002, representing 10–12 generations. We test whether these changes are consistent with those observed for other body structures by other authors. Our current data are insufficient to test whether selection is acting on these structures, so we assess only whether changes have occurred. We test the following hypotheses:

Overall vertebral size and vertebral body height have decreased on average from 1951 to 2002, reflecting overall trends in decreasing body mass and diaphyseal diameter (Blomquist and Turnquist 2011; Francis and Wang 2023). This finding would suggest that there is an overall reduction in macaque size, and not a specific reduction in limb and cranial measurements or in soft tissue mass.

Vertebral shape has changed from 1951 to 2002. Changes in overall body size may result in allometric changes to vertebral form (Rose 1975; Manfreda et al. 2006; Hernandez et al. 2009; Kikuchi and Ogihara 2021). Further, although no one has explicitly tested changes in positional behavior over time on Cayo Santiago, data from other taxa suggest such changes may have occurred. The optimal size of the Cayo Santiago population has been estimated at about 300, but the population has risen to nearly 2000 during the period represented by our study sample (Hernandez‐Pacheco et al. 2016), which may affect group range sizes and thus how far individuals move on a daily basis (Wrangham et al. 1993; Ruehe and Hohmann 2004). In addition, changes in temperature, substrate availability, and other climatic variables, as the Cayo Santiago macaques experienced in the transition from India to Puerto Rico, may affect locomotor behaviors across taxa (Miles 1994; Feder et al. 2010; Eppley et al. 2022).

Materials and Methods

Sample and Data

The sample consists of 132 female and 78 male adult M. mulatta from Cayo Santiago, Puerto Rico, with known birthdate and age at death, born between 1951 and 2002. Cayo Santiago macaque females have an average age at first birth of 4.3 years (Hoffman et al. 2010), so this sample encompasses 10 to 12 generations, an estimate that can be confirmed from pedigree and catalog data. We examine a previously published dataset of three‐dimensional coordinate data from six typical vertebrae: cervical vertebrae 3 and 5 (C3 and C5), thoracic vertebrae 3 and 9 (T3 and T9), and lumbar vertebrae 2 and 5 (L2 and L5) (Villamil et al. 2024a, 2024b).

Mature status was determined by a combination of known age at death, eruption of the third molar, fusion of the sphenooccipital synchondrosis, and epiphyseal fusion. However, it is possible that female macaques continue growing until 15 years of age even after achieving traditional markers of skeletal maturity (Wang et al. 2006, 2007).

The coordinate data capture aspects of the vertebral centrum and neural arch, including the pedicles, transverse and spinous processes, and zygapophyses (Figure 1). We also analyzed the ventral craniocaudal body height of each vertebra separately, as this measurement directly contributes to overall body length (e.g., crown‐rump length). Intraobserver error is estimated at between 1.55% and 3.33%, with data from each vertebral level collected by a single individual (Villamil et al. 2024a, 2024b). Due to the nature of the dataset, the individuals are known to be related.

Coordinate data used in this study. Shown on a Rhesus macaque thoracic vertebra in (A) dorsal, (B) lateral, (C) cranial, and (D) caudal views. Vertebral body height was calculated as the distance between landmarks 1 and 44. Full landmark definitions are available with the original dataset (Villamil et al. 2024a, 2024b).

We assessed and corrected the data for landmark inversions (e.g., right–left inversions) and eliminated individuals with any missing landmarks from analyses. For analyses of coordinate data, we did not omit any landmarks, which may result in some local error for less reliable landmarks. However, this is unlikely to affect overall results.

Preliminary Analysis

Each vertebral level was examined separately for all analyses.

For coordinate data, we used MorphoJ v.1.08.02 (Klingenberg 2011) to perform a Generalized Procrustes Analysis on each dataset, which removes differences between individuals that are due to orientation, position, or overall size. We then performed a principal component analysis (PCA) on the symmetric component of shape variation, also in MorphoJ. All subsequent analyses involving principal component (PC) scores were carried out on those PCs accounting for at least 5% of variance.

PC scores were then analyzed in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024). PC scores were first regressed on centroid size using the “lm” function to assess the effects of size on shape for the whole sample and within females and males separately. We then performed a Multiple Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) on these PC scores to assess sex differences using the “manova” function. We also compared female and male centroid sizes and vertebral body height using standard parametric (Student's t‐test, “t.test” function) or nonparametric (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, “wilcox.test” function) statistical methods as appropriate (Tables S1 and S2). Since our purpose in these comparisons is not to assess differences in specific measurements, but rather to help determine whether a mixed‐sex or sex‐divided approach is more informative with regard to assessing secular change, we do not apply any corrections for multiple tests.

Finally, we assessed the relationship of year of birth to age at death using the “lm” function in base R to create a simple linear model, as age at death could be a confounding factor in the dataset.

Assessing Change Over Time

We assessed change over time in two ways, via multiple regression and paired t‐tests. Based on preliminary analyses indicating sex differences across the board (see Section 3), we analyzed females and males separately. For analyses involving PC scores, we analyzed a mixed‐sex model and also analyzed females and males separately, but used PC scores from the combined sex dataset, given that visual inspection of PC plots indicated no broad‐level differences in the shape of the various distributions within or between females and males.

We performed a multiple regression using the “lm” function in R, with age at death, year of birth, and their interaction as independent variables and each centroid size, vertebral body height, or PC score as the dependent variable, following Francis and Wang (2023). Throughout, we report adjusted R 2. In the case of mixed‐sex regressions for PC scores, we also included sex and the interaction of age at death and sex as independent variables. We performed a Holm–Bonferroni correction (Holm 1979) with target p < 0.05. We performed a Holm–Bonferroni correction for centroid size and vertebral body height results across vertebrae and sex (N = 12) and within vertebrae for PC scores (e.g., for all C3 PC scores, N = 10–12). For significant models where both age at death and year of birth were significant contributors, or where neither was a significant contributor, which may suggest multicollinearity, we performed simple linear models separately for each of these variables to clarify their contributions.

For PC scores, we also performed a pooled linear regression in MorphoJ. We compared all PCs accounting for 5% or more of total variance to year of birth, with PC scores pooled by sex and 10,000 permutations to calculate significance. MorphoJ implements a regression score in order to analyze multiple PC scores simultaneously (Drake and Klingenberg 2008).

We also calculated the average centroid size and PC score for Early individuals (born 1950–1965; F = 22–27, M = 6–7) and Late individuals (born 1985–2002; F = 20–24, M = 17–20) to assess the magnitude of change, if present. We chose these cutoffs to ensure a reasonable sample size for the estimation of the mean and the variance (Cardini and Elton 2007), although the male Early sample is quite small regardless. We compared Early and Late averages using a two‐tailed paired t‐test in R, using the “t.test” function.

Finally, we estimated the magnitude of sexual size dimorphism in Early and Late individuals by dividing the male average centroid size or vertebral body height by the female average. We then compared the Early and Late magnitudes using a two‐tailed paired t‐test in R as well.

Results

Sample Characteristics: Size, Sex, and Age

Across vertebrae, the first five or six PCs each accounted for 5% or more variance (Table S3). There are significant interactions between size and shape for all vertebral elements assessed here, but these interactions are inconsistent across groups, often weak, and do not always involve PC1 (Table S4). C3, C5, and T3 all show consistent relationships of PC1 to log centroid size across mixed, female, and male samples. Differences between small and large specimens are concentrated in the relative length and orientation of the spinous process (Figure S1).

Centroid size and vertebral shape exhibit significant sex differences across the board (Table S5). Males tend to be larger than females (Table S5). Vertebral body height is only significantly different among females and males for the two cervical vertebrae, C3 and C5 (Table S5).

There is a significant relationship between age at death and year of birth (Adj. R 2 = 0.1335; F = 33.83; p = 2.19 × 10−8), most likely indicating sampling bias in the collection (e.g., individuals born in later years had not yet reached old age at the time the collection was made). The relationship is not significant for males (Adj. R 2 = 0.0228; F = 2.84; p = 0.096) but is significant for females (Adj. R 2 = 0.1274; F = 20.41; p = 1.37 × 10−5). Although the middle decades (1960–1990) have a representative spread of ages from 5 to about 30 years old, individuals born between 1950 and 1960 are between 14 and 24 years of age, and those born between 1990 and 2005 are between 7 and 15 years of age. This distribution is largely representative of the materials available in the collection itself for both 1950s and 1990s birth dates. Because of this, we are unable to adjust the sampling scheme for either of these decades at this time.

Size and Shape Changes Over Time

Overall, there are weak trends towards smaller sizes over time in both females and males across vertebrae (Table 1; Figure 2). Significant models display adjusted R 2 between 0.074 and 0.174 (Table 1). Year of birth is the only significant contributor to most size trends in females, while in males, at least some models displayed a significant effect of age at death as well (Table 1, Tables S6 and S7). For female L2 centroid size, neither age at death nor year of birth were significant contributors in the multiple regression model, and a simplified linear model did not clarify their contributions because both factors were statistically significant when analyzed independently (age at death F = 9.51, p = 0.003, Adj. R 2 = 0.066; year of birth F = 7.23, p = 0.008, Adj. R 2 = 0.057).

PCs 1 and 2, which represent about 30% of the variance in each vertebra, do not capture any temporal patterns on visual inspection (Figure 3). A handful of PC score models were significant, but there is no consistent pattern to their significance in terms of vertebra, sex, or the specific PCs that show significance across the sexes within vertebrae (Table 2; Tables S8–S11). Multiple regression models and the subsequent simple linear regression models suggest that only a handful of PC scores are significantly associated with year of birth independently of age at death (Table 2, Tables S8–S11). These include: C3 PC1 and PC4 across analyses; mixed‐sex C5 PC4; mixed‐sex T3 PC4, female T3 PC4 and PC5 and male T3 PC2; mixed‐sex and male T9 PC2; mixed‐sex L2 PC2 and female L2 PC4; and mixed‐sex L5 PC5. Of these, almost all of the female PCs show significant relationships to size (Table S4), which may suggest a confounding effect. In general, these PCs represent a low proportion of variance (5%–15%), and even significant models have relatively low R 2 < 0.10, with simple linear models displaying even lower R 2 in general (Tables S8–S11), suggesting an overall weak and inconsistent relationship of vertebral shape to year of birth. Sex‐pooled regression models similarly suggest that any significant relationships of vertebral shape to year of birth are weak, and the two vertebrae with the highest scores, C3 and T3, show only about 5%–7% of variation predicted by year of birth (Table 3). Shape changes in C3 and T3, the two vertebrae with the strongest relationships to year of birth, are shown in Figures S2 and S3. These changes generally show relative shortening of the spinous process and of the vertebral body from early to late time periods, as well as variable and sometimes contradictory changes in the orientation of the spinous and transverse processes (Figures S2 and S3).

Sexual Dimorphism

There is sexual size dimorphism overall and within both Early and Late groups (Table 5). On average, male vertebrae are larger than female vertebrae by about 10%–15%. When assessing vertebral body height, sexual dimorphism is more marked in the cranial segments of the column and less marked in the caudal segments (Table 5). There is a significant increase in sexual dimorphism from Early to Late groups (T = −7.27, p = 0.0008, D.F. = 5), with an average increase in sexual dimorphism of 0.08 (approximately 8% overall).

Discussion

Changes Over Time

We hypothesized that vertebral size decreased from 1951 to 2002 and found significant but weak decreases in overall vertebral size over time, which vary by sex. This is consistent with Francis and Wang's (2023) and Blomquist and Turnquist's (2011) findings of an overall decrease in body weight among females and males, and suggests that these findings reflect an overall reduction in size and not merely soft tissue changes (e.g., muscle mass or fat) or localized skeletal changes. This is contrary to previous authors who had suggested that we may be observing localized variation in the developmental response of limbs and trunk to specific environmental factors (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987), following findings in humans (Gonzales et al. 1982, 1984; Pomeroy et al. 2012). Further, previous authors had hypothesized that an increase in size occurred as a result of improvements in diet (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987), but our results are contrary to this hypothesis. Rather, our results, in agreement with others (Blomquist and Turnquist 2011; Francis and Wang 2023), suggest that additional environmental or selection factors are acting to reduce body size in this macaque population despite increased access to nutritional resources overall. Compared to later studies, DeRousseau and Reichs' (1987) study was limited to 101 animals born between 1951 and 1972, and, as they themselves suggest, this short time period most likely limited their ability to identify and differentiate dietary and environmental trends that were occurring in tandem. Long‐term trends may not yet have become visible in the skeletal collection, or short‐term fluctuations, like those identified by DeRousseau and Reichs (1987) for individuals born in the late 1960s, may have obscured longer‐term trends identified by this study and other authors.

We also hypothesized that vertebral body height decreased from 1951 to 2002 and found a significant decrease in vertebral body height over time, which is most marked in females and less marked and less clearly associated with year of birth in males. This suggests that there is not just a decrease in body mass or overall body size, but that females are getting shorter, while males are maintaining overall body length. Previous researchers have identified sex differences in temporal changes in this population, with females showing a more marked change overall, regardless of the direction of change (DeRousseau and Reichs 1987; Francis and Wang 2023). Francis and Wang (2023), who found that females maintained long bone lengths while males decreased long bone lengths, hypothesized that observed changes in limb proportions and overall body mass are associated with temperature and climate, and represent a way to increase surface area relative to volume, following Bergmann's and Allen's rules. Taken together, our results suggest an even more extreme change in body proportions in females than in males.

We hypothesized that vertebral shape changed from 1951 to 2002, reflecting shifts in positional behavior in this time period. However, we found no consistent changes in vertebral shape over time, with only a handful of PCs in either sex displaying a significant relationship to year of birth. These results suggest overall that no significant changes in vertebral function are occurring in the Cayo Santiago population, despite changes in the external environment and body characteristics.

Finally, we hypothesized that sexual dimorphism increased from 1951 to 2002. Our results support this hypothesis, as we found a significant increase in sexual dimorphism over time, both when measured through centroid size and when measured through vertebral body height. This appears to be driven mainly by females becoming smaller over time, while males better maintain their overall size. This is consistent with findings in Turcotte et al. (2022) of greater sexual dimorphism in the Cayo Santiago population when compared to other Rhesus macaque populations and may suggest a mechanism for this shift, for example, an increase in sexual dimorphism occurs in this population due to a disproportionate reduction in female size over time.

Size Reductions and Female and Male Differences

General decreases in vertebral size and body mass may be broadly linked to the “island rule,” a tendency for large species to become smaller on islands, and for small species to become larger (Foster 1964). This pattern has been confirmed in a broad variety of mammals (Lister 1989; Anderson and Handley 2002; Lomolino 2005; Herridge and Lister 2012; McFadden and Meiri 2013), including primates (Bromham and Cardillo 2007; Masters et al. 2014), and size reduction in this context is usually attributed to selection in response to a reduction in resources and predators. This kind of size reduction is evident within periods of less than 200 years (Rozzi and Lomolino 2017). However, observed patterns of insular dwarfing are highly variable in their origin, with a diverse array of selective and non‐selective pressures influencing body size (Lomolino 2005). On Cayo Santiago, for example, it is unlikely that size reductions can be attributed to reduced resources, since the monkeys are provisioned. As others have suggested, climatic variables and increasing population density are more likely to play a role in size changes on the island (Blomquist and Turnquist 2011; Francis and Wang 2023). Studies of captive macaques have shown a positive relationship between increasing population density and higher glucocorticoid levels as measured via hair cortisol (Dettmer et al. 2014). There is also evidence of increased stress over time at Cayo Santiago specifically, as indicated by the frequency of linear enamel hypoplasia, which has also been attributed to worsening climatic conditions (Guatelli‐Steinberg et al. 2025). A combination of increasing population density and adverse climate may thus have had distinct physiological impacts on the Cayo Santiago population. Further, in our study, we find an overall pattern of size reduction over time, but with marked sex differences, with greater reduction in overall size and, specifically, in vertebral body length, in females, likely resulting in more extreme proportional changes in females than in males.

It is possible that females experience greater negative effects from external environmental conditions, such as temperature and humidity, and have acclimated or adapted more strongly to the environment at Cayo Santiago than have males. These differences could be due to the physiological requirements of pregnancy or lactation, which have been shown to be adversely affected by heat stress and other environmental factors in humans (Chersich et al. 2020; Yüzen et al. 2023; Howells et al. 2025). Reproduction is in itself quite costly for female macaques (Maestripieri and Georgiev 2016): the birth season is associated with the highest adult female mortality rates in Cayo Santiago despite food provisioning, reflecting high maternal mortality during pregnancy and birth, and lactating females on Cayo Santiago have also been shown to display higher cortisol than non‐lactating females (Hoffman and Maestripieri 2012). Previous studies have established a shift in the birth season in the Cayo Santiago macaques to accommodate environmental variables (Rawlins and Kessler 1985; Hoffman and Maestripieri 2012; Hernández‐Pacheco et al. 2016), and shifting body proportions and body size may be an additional response to mitigate the effects of heat stress or other environmental factors on pregnancy in females specifically. Studies assessing heat stress and pregnancy outcomes in the Cayo Santiago macaques may provide insight into this possibility.

Another possibility is that female–male differences in change over time result incidentally from differences in developmental trajectories. Researchers have identified trends of earlier reproduction over time among the Cayo Santiago macaques compared to other Rhesus macaques (Lee 2019) and significant birth cohort effects on age at first reproduction (Blomquist 2012). Whereas reproduction begins at about 4 years of age in female macaques (Hoffman et al. 2010), skeletal maturation is only achieved at about 7 years of age (Cheverud 1981; Wang et al. 2007). This growth period is both extended and accelerated in males (Turcotte et al. 2022). Stress associated with pregnancy and lactation may thus disproportionately affect growth in females in their final stages of development. Thus, as suggested by DeRousseau and Reichs (1987), some of the apparent changes over time may reflect differential effects on development rather than underlying shifts in population characteristics.

Of course, multiple factors could play a role in the observed pattern of size reduction. External environmental stressors and their acclimatizing or selective effects, which may be compounded by the negative effects of increasing population density, may especially affect young females and, in conjunction with the developmental side effects of early age at first reproduction, create a marked sex effect.

Incidental Findings

Interestingly, and unexpectedly, despite broad sexual dimorphism in vertebral shape and overall size, we found no significant sex differences in vertebral body height of the thoracic and lumbar elements. Previously, Villamil et al. (2024a) identified a pattern where males tended to add thoracolumbar segments to the vertebral column and hypothesized that adding segments may be a way to increase overall body size when the size of individual vertebrae is constrained. The lack of sexual dimorphism in vertebral body height for thoracolumbar elements is consistent with this hypothesis, since in this scenario, larger male body size (in this case, crown‐rump length) could result only from the addition of vertebral segments. Together, this trend towards increasing body size by adding vertebral segments in the face of other constraints, and findings that male macaques either maintain their overall size or experience smaller reductions over time than females, may suggest that although male Rhesus macaques do not rely on body size for reproductive success (Higham and Maestripieri 2014; Kimock et al. 2019), there is still substantial selective pressure favoring the maintenance of relatively larger bodies overall.

Limitations

We cannot rule out that there is an independent relationship between age at death and vertebral size, such that the age skew in the sample, where individuals from the earlier sample are older, is impacting these results. Such a relationship between age and vertebral size may result from continued maturational (Wang et al. 2007) or degenerative changes (Goh et al. 2000; Kwok et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2013), which may occur in the absence of obvious external pathology. It may also be that, prior to the systematic collection of skeletons in the 1970s, small individuals were more easily lost and were not collected as frequently as large individuals. A larger sample, with a more representative age distribution, may clarify these results. The current sample available in the skeletal collection precludes this reanalysis, as there are few or no younger individuals with vertebrae available for early time periods and no older individuals available for more recent time periods. Such a reanalysis may be possible soon as more recent individuals are added to the Cayo Santiago collection. The small sample size for the early male cohort may also affect our results, skewing the male distribution.

In addition, the sample was originally designed to test heritability and other factors relying on pedigree data, so that many individuals were specifically chosen because they were related. This may also affect our results. However, this sample represents 22 distinct matrilines and is representative of all the matrilines found in the Cayo Santiago collection. Further, this sample is generally representative of the age distribution for different time periods of the collection.

Conclusions

Our findings are consistent with a scenario where there are overall reductions in size over time in the Cayo Santiago Rhesus macaques, with changes more marked among females than in males. Given their access to a high‐quality diet, which should act to increase overall body size, this suggests that external factors, including climate, are at work to reduce body size in this population. These factors may particularly affect females, who are at especially high risk during pregnancy and lactation.
