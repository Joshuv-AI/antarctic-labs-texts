Effect of photoperiod duration during incubation on hatch traits, growth, and behavior of Leghorn chicks to 21 days of age☆
Authors: Celma E Ferreira dos Santos, Tory Shynkaruk, Eugenia Herwig, Bruce Rathgeber, Trever G Crowe, Henry L Classen, Karen Schwean-Lardner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study determined the effects of photoperiod duration during incubation on Lohmann LSL-Lite hatch traits, growth, and behavior. Eggs (n = 1,200) were randomly assigned to one of four treatments: 0L:24D (0L), 6L:18D (6L), 12L:12D (12L), or 18L:6D (18L). Light was provided via red (641 nm) LED lamps. Data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA in a randomized complete block design with significance declared when P < 0.05. In-ovo photoperiod did not affect embryo mortality, hatchability, or hatch window. However, 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 90% of the eggs hatched earlier in the 18 L treatment compared to 0 L. Male chicks in the 0 L treatment were heavier than males from 12 or 18 L. Female chicks in the 0 L treatment were heavier than females from 6, 12, or 18 L. Treatment did not affect navel closure, female body length, heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio, composite asymmetry, or relative yolk-free BW and yolk sac residue. Relative weights of liver, heart, duodenum, ileum, and cecum were heaviest under 18 L. During rearing, BW was higher in the 0 L birds compared to 6 and 18 L at 7 d, 6 and 12 L at 14 d, and 18 L at 21 d, however, flock uniformity was unaffected. No effects were observed on composite asymmetry or H/L ratio at 21 d post-hatch. At 0 d, chicks incubated under 18 L spent a higher percentage of time running and a lower percentage of time resting. At 1 d, chicks in the 18 L treatment spent more time resting, walking, and foraging than those incubated under 0 L. At 2 d, chicks in the 18 L treatment spent a lower percentage of time resting and higher percentage of time foraging compared to 0 L. By 3 d, only standing behaviour was affected with chicks in the 6 L treatment standing for a higher percentage of time than chicks in the 0 and 12 L treatments. In conclusion, the use of photoperiods during incubation impacted duration of incubation without negatively affecting the development of the embryo or pullet stress and behaviour.Keywords: In-ovo, Photostimulation, Hatch window, Chick quality, Stress

Introduction

Traditionally, commercial poultry eggs are incubated under complete darkness. During natural incubation, the egg is exposed to light when the eggs are rotated by the hen and when the hen leaves the nest (Tainika and Bayraktar, 2022). The past few decades have shown progress in understanding the benefit of using in-ovo lighting to improve bird development and health (Rogers and Krebs, 1996; Özkan et al., 2012a, 2012b; Archer and Mench, 2014). Evidence suggests that the effects of in-ovo lighting on the embryo can differ with the duration of the photoperiod. A significant increase in White Leghorn embryo weight was observed as early as 4.5 d of incubation when eggs were incubated under continuous (24 L) incandescent light (Lauber, 1975). Similarly, the exposure of broiler eggs to a photoperiod of 16L:8D, using fluorescent light, significantly increased embryo weight (21 d of incubation) relative to egg weight and reduced residual yolk weight compared to eggs incubated under darkness (Özkan et al., 2012a). In house sparrows (Paser domesticus), a longer photoperiod (18 vs 12 L) resulted in a shorter incubation period. This effect was likely due to increased multiplication of cells and accelerated embryo development and metabolism (Cooper et al., 2011).

Differences in photoperiod duration during incubation can also affect bird health and stress levels (Archer and Mench, 2013). Photoperiod duration affects the rhythm of melatonin hormone syntheses (Zawilska et al., 2006), and melatonin can have beneficial effects on the immune system (Çalişlar et al., 2018; Horodincu and Solcan, 2023). Exposure of fertile broiler eggs to 12 L using fluorescent light stimulated the immune system as reflected by a higher production of antibodies measured 3 wk post-hatch compared to birds incubated under 0 or 1 L. Additionally, the use of a 12 L (fluorescent light) program during broiler egg incubation reduced the levels of corticosterone in 4 wk old broilers as a post-stress response to crating, and these birds showed the lowest composite asymmetry scores compared to photoperiods of 0, 1, and 6 L (Archer and Mench, 2013).

These physiological responses signal a reduction in stress, through activation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, a primary regulator of stress in birds (Scanes, 2016). Stress promotes the release of glucocorticoids, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and corticotropin-releasing hormone into the bloodstream. High levels of glucocorticoids in the body can result in depression of the immune system and elevate the proportion of heterophils to lymphocytes (Scanes, 2016).

Many previous studies investigating in-ovo lighting focused on providing white light via fluorescent or incandescent bulbs. More recently, focus has shifted to the use of monochromatic LED lighting during incubation. Kankova et al. (2022) suggested that exposure to red light during incubation, compared to blue light, may stimulate the immune response in the gut after hatching. Archer (2018) compared two white LED light sources, with high levels of either red or blue light and found that both the red and blue light treatments increased hatchability of fertile eggs compared to dark incubation. Red light during incubation has been shown to improve hatchability and chick quality (Archer, 2015), while also reducing fear and stress later in life (Archer, 2017a).

The use of red LED light during incubation has been shown to have beneficial impacts compared to other wavelengths. However, to the author’s knowledge, no studies have identified an optimal photoperiod using red-light spectrum LEDs for hatch and early brooding parameters in layer-type chicks. Therefore, the objective of this work was to investigate the impact of a range of photoperiod duration using a red (641 nm) LED lighting system on Lohmann LSL-Lite fertilized eggs during 21.5 d of incubation, on hatch traits, chick growth, and behavioral expression.

Materials and methods

The research and experimental procedures described in this manuscript were approved by the University of Saskatchewan’s Animal Care Committee following the guidelines set out by the Canadian Council on Animal Care (2009). The approved animal use protocol number for this experiment was 19940248.

Experimental design

A total of 1,200 Lohmann LSL-Lite fertile eggs were obtained from a commercial hatchery. Upon arrival, the eggs were stored in a cooler at 13.3°C, until the start of incubation (maximum storage of 4 d past day of lay). The trial included three replicate hatches, set in a completely randomized block design. In each hatch, 400 eggs were randomly distributed between four incubators (100 eggs per treatment per hatch). Breeder flock age variability was minimized as much as possible when sourcing from a local hatchery to reduce the effect of breeder flock age on the studied parameters (breeder flocks ages were 46, 35, and 45 wk of age, respectively).

Four incubators (1502 Sportsman, GQF Manufacturing Co., Savannah, GA), with windows blocked to eliminate external light, were outfitted with three dim-to-red (641 nm) 7 W LED lighting tubes (AgriShift® TLL, Once Inc., Plymouth, MN). In addition, each incubator was outfitted with an automatic lighting timer (Noma® Outdoor Heavy-Duty Timer) to provide one of four in-ovo photoperiods: 0L:24D (0L), 6L:18D (6L), 12L:12D (12L), or 18L:6D (18L) at an average intensity of 3,844 gallilux (2,050 lux) at egg level. Photoperiods were in place from the day of set until hatch.

Incubator and pullet management

On the day of set of incubation, eggs were removed from the cooler 2 to 3 h prior to being set, individually marked, weighed as a group, and placed with the air cell orientated upward in incubator trays. One temperature and humidity miniature data logger (HygrochronTM DS1923-F5#, Maxim Integrated, San Jose, CA) was placed among the eggs on the top shelf near the back of each incubator to record temperature and humidity readings every 20 min. Incubation conditions were set to 37.8°C and 50-55% RH from 0 to 18 d of incubation. On d 18 of incubation, the eggs were transferred to hatch trays located at the lower level of the incubator. The temperature was maintained at 37.8°C and RH was increased to 55-65% until the hatch endpoint. Incubator conditions were monitored twice daily.

In-ovo sexing did not occur, so both males and female chicks hatched and were used for naval scores and chick BW and length. Male chicks were used for terminal procedures including blood and tissue collections and any remaining males were euthanized on day of hatch. Only females chicks remained on trial for brooding and rearing data collections. One hundred and twenty female chicks (30 per treatment) from each hatch were housed in one of four floor pens (one per treatment, 4.6 m2) located in the same room. Wheat straw (10 cm depth) was used as bedding material, and a cardboard brooder ring was placed in each pen during the first week to contain the chicks for ease of video observation. Each pen contained a tube feeder and nipple drinker line, with a supplemental feeder and drinker available for the first 4 d. Feed (commercial wheat based starter diet with 2,738 kcal/kg metabolizable energy and 19.2% crude protein) and water were provided ad libitum.

The photoperiod in the room was set to 23L:1D (30 lux) from 0 to 2 d, followed by 20L:4D (30 lux) from 3 to 6 d, 16L:8D (20 lux) from 7 to 14 d, and 13L:11D (20 lux) from 15 to 21 d. White light was provided via incandescent bulbs, and light intensity was measured at bird level. Room temperature was set at 34°C from 0 to 2 d and decreased gradually to reach 28°C at 2 wk and 26°C at 3 wk. Relative humidity was maintained at 55-60% throughout the brooding period.

Data collection

Embryo Mortality. At 9 and 18 d of incubation, all eggs were weighed and individually candled. All clear eggs and dead embryos were removed and fertility and age of embroynic death were assessed. This was repeated at the hatch endpoint with all unhatched eggs.

Hatch Window, Hatch Time, and Hatchability. From 480 h (20 d) until 516 h (21.5 d) all incubators were checked for hatchlings every 4 h. During each check, all fully hatched chicks were counted and moved to a hatcher (without a source of light) until incubation was complete, with 516 h considered the expected end of hatch. Lights in the incubator room were switched off during the hatch check when incubator doors needed to be opened, with light for checks provided only by a small headlamp with a red LED light. At the hatch endpoint, all hatched chicks were counted, and the hatchability of set and fertile eggs was calculated.

Navel Score. At the end of hatch, all chicks were feather sexed (Tierzucht, 2014) and wing banded for individual identification. The navels of 30 female and 30 male chicks were inspected by treatment. A score of 1 was assigned to flat, closed, and clean navels. A dried membrane in the navel and a black button of up to 2 millimeters, where swelling, redness, and a string may be present, were assigned a score of 2. Navels with black buttons exceeding 2 millimeters or an open navel were scored 3 (Molenaar et al., 2010).

Chick BW and Length. Following navel scoring, the same chicks were individually weighed, and their lengths measured as described by Hill (2001). Briefly, chicks were positioned ventrally with their necks and right legs extended and the length from the tip of the beak to the end of the middle toe (excluding the nail) was recorded. All measurements were performed by the same individual to ensure consistency.

Chick H/L Ratio. Blood samples (0.3 millilitres) were taken from the jugular vein of 15 male chicks immediately after the hatch endpoint using 1-cc syringes and once collected, the samples were transferred to EDTA coated vacutainer tubes. Blood smears were then created in duplicate and stained using a PROTOCOLTM Hema 3TM stain set (Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, ON, Canada). A total of 100 heterophil or lymphocyte cells per slide were counted under 100x oil immersion magnification, and the heterophil to lymphocyte (H/L) ratio was determined.

Tissue Collection and Morphological Asymmetry. Twenty male chicks per treatment were euthanized via cervical dislocation and yolk sac residue was removed. Yolk-free body and yolk sac residue weights were recorded. The heart, liver, and gastrointestinal tract (GIT) were removed and weighed. The GIT was separated into duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, and colon. Each section was weighed individually, and its length measured. All organs and GIT weights were corrected for live body weight. In addition, the left and right femur, tibiotarsus, and metatarsus were removed. Femur (from the Trochanter major to the Gondylus fibularis), tibiotarsus (from the Processus cnemialis to the Trochlea tibiotarsi), and tarsometatarsus (from proximal to distal end) lengths were measured using a digital calliper (Driesch, 1976).

Pullet Data Collection. Birds were individually weighed at 7, 14, and 21 d of age to determine flock uniformity. In addition, chick behavior was recorded for analyses from 0 to 3 d using infrared video cameras (Bosch WZ45 Integrated IR Dome; Bosch Security Systems, Inc., Fairport, New York, USA) mounted on the ceiling above each pen. Videos were scan sampled at 20 min intervals to determine the behaviour being performed by each bird in the pen (ethogram presented in Table 1). At 21 d, blood samples (2.0 millilitres) were collected from the brachial vein of each pullet using 22-gauge 1″ vacutainer needles and EDTA coated vacutainer tubes for determination of the H/L ratio as described above. Following blood collection, 20 birds per pen were euthanized via cervical dislocation and the heart, liver, GIT, and right and left femur, tarsometatarsus, and tibiotarsus bones were removed and assessed as previously described to determine organ and tissue weights relative to body weight and morphological asymmetry.

Statistical analyses

This study was analyzed as a randomized complete block design (replicate hatch as block) with photoperiod as a fixed effect and hatch as the random effect. The experimental unit was incubator (n = 3). Data were pooled from each block for analyses. The hatch time and hatch window data were analyzed as a generalized linear mixed model using the Glimmix procedure in SAS® 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Asymmetry data were analyzed using a two-sample comparison t-test, followed by an ANOVA using the mixed procedure in SAS® 9.4. The daily behavioral mean output was analyzed using the Glimmix procedure with a Gaussian distribution and Identity link function. All other data were tested with a one-way ANOVA using the mixed procedure. Statistical assumptions were assessed prior to analyses, and a log (y + 1) transformation was applied to the data when necessary. Results were declared significantly different when P < 0.05, and a trend was noted when 0.10>P > 0.05. Tukey’s range test was used for mean separation.

Results

Incubation and hatch

Hatch Traits. Incubator ambient temperature was not affected by in-ovo photoperiod (37.90±0.26⁰C, P = 0.44). Embryo mortality (early, mid, and late) and hatchability of set and fertile eggs were unaffected by in-ovo photoperiod (Table 2). The impact of photoperiod on hatch time can be found in Table 3. Eggs incubated under 18 L hatched earlier (P < 0.01), with 5% of chicks hatching at 481.6 h of incubation compared to those incubated under 0 L (489.2 h) or 6 L (485.6 h). Chicks in the 12 L treatment (484.6 h) did not differ from those in the 6 or 18 L treatments, however they hatched earlier than chicks incubated under 0 L. Under the 18 L treatment 10, 25, 50 and 75% of the eggs also hatched significantly earlier than any other treatment (P < 0.01). All photoperiods providing light had an earlier hatching time compared to dark incubation for 5, 10, 25, 50, and 75% of eggs hatched (P < 0.01). It took longer for 90% of the eggs to hatch under 0 L compared to 18 L (P = 0.02), but no differences were observed for the time for 95% of the eggs to hatch. No effect of photoperiod duration on hatch window was found (Table 4).

Discussion

Incubation and hatch

In this study, photoperiod primarily affected the duration of incubation, with longer photoperiods (up to 18 L) resulting in shorter hatch times. Incubation under red light for 18 L resulted in 5% of the chicks hatched 3-7.6 h earlier, 10% of the chicks hatched 3.7-7.7 h earlier, 25% of the chicks hatched 3.3-7.8 h earlier, 50% of the chicks hatched 3.5-8.1 h earlier, 75% of the chicks hatched 3.3-7.1 h earlier, 90% of the chicks hatched 3-6.9 h earlier, and 95% of chicks hatched 3.8-6.6 h earlier. The shortening of the hatch time with 18 L suggests the photoperiod duration may influence the entire hatching process. A study evaluating the use of green light during incubation of laying hen eggs found that eggs exposed to 12 L had a shorter hatch time than those incubated under dark conditions (Wang et al., 2020). Shafey and Al-mohsen (2002) reported shorter incubation times for broiler eggs incubated under 24 L using green light compared to complete darkness. Hannah et al. (2020) reported that use of a 12 L photoperiod reduced the hatch window compared to dark incubation in laying hen eggs. However, not all studies agree with the acceleration of incubation under longer photoperiods. Özkan et al. (2012a) found no differences in incubation duration for broiler eggs incubated under white light for 16 L compared to 0 L. The incubation of broiler eggs under an intermittent photoperiod of 15 min light and dark periods using green LED light also had no effect on incubation duration or hatch time (Rozenboim et al., 2004). Li et al. (2021) reported no difference in hatch window for broiler chicks incubated under darkness or a 12 L photoperiod under red, blue, or white light.

The mechanism(s) by which longer photoperiods accelerate embryonic development have yet to be idenitified. One possible explanation for an earlier hatch initiation could be an increase in incubator temperature caused by the lighting systems. This is more likely to occur with the use of incandescent bulbs, which produce heat, whereas LED bulbs emit a negligible amount of heat. However, Rozenboim et al. (2004) observed that the use of continuous lighting (green 560 nm LED) during incubation increased broiler egg yolk temperature by 0.01% for every 1 min of exposure to the light and suggested that an intermittent lighting program would suppress the production of heat. These authors did not specify whether the heat was produced by the bulb or if an increase in embryo metabolism could be the source. In the current study, our results were similar to those reported by Walter and Voitle (1972) for broilers and Fairchild and Christensen (2000) for turkeys, who reported that temperature among the incubating eggs was not affected by photoperiod. Interestingly, although photoperiod duration under red light in the current research affected incubation length, it did not affect the hatch window, meaning that the variability in time from start to end of hatch did not differ. Previous research has shown that chicks from older (59 wk) breeder flocks hatch earlier than those from younger (29 wk) parent flocks (Ulmer-Franco et al., 2010). Breeder performance objectives state that Lohmann LSL-Lite hens reach peak production at 30 weeks. The breeder flock ages of the eggs used in this study were 35, 45, and 46 wk. During statistical analyses, data were blocked by breeder flock age to minimize any age effects.

Previous studies investigating impacts of light wavelength during incubation have shown red light can improve hatchability in layers, broiler, and ducks (Archer, 2015; 2017a, 2017b). When combining wavelength and photoperiod, Li et al. (2023) compared 12 and 18 L photoperiods under blue light and found no impact on broiler hatchability. In the current trial, incubation under red LED light, did not affect the hatchability of either fertile or set eggs under any photoperiod. Breeder performance objectives state that the expected hatchability of Lohmann LSL-Lite eggs is 83.3% and in the current study hatchability of fertile eggs ranged from 87.25 to 92.09%. The current results indicate that the timing of hatch can be shortened without any negative impacts on hatchability using a photoperiod of 18 L. Furthermore, despite the earlier hatch observed when a light-dark cycle was used, particularly the 18 L photoperiod, embryo and chick health was not negatively affected in this study. No impact on mortality or navel healing was noted in the current study. These findings agree with data reported by Huth and Archer (2015), who found no differences in embryonic death under white light (0 or 12 L). Similarity, Li et al. (2023) found no impact on mortality under blue light (12 or 18 L). Archer et al. (2017b) reported reduced embryo mortality (early and overall), unhealed navels, and chicks with defects when chicks were incubated under 12 L red/white light compared to dark, while Li et al. (2021) found no difference in mortality between dark, red, white, or blue light incubation.

Walter and Voitle (1972) observed no differences in broiler chick body weight at hatch when embryos were exposed to 0 or 24 L during incubation under incandescent white light. Huth and Archer (2015) also reported no difference in body weight at hatch for chicks incubated under 0 and 12 L photoperiods (white LED light). Shafey and Al-mohsen (2002) weighed chicks when checking the hatch and observed lower chick weight in hatchlings exposed to continuous green light compared to those incubated under dark. However, Abdulateef et al. (2021) reported that both embryos at 19 d of incubation and chicks at hatch incubated under dark conditions were lighter compared to those incubated under red, blue, or green light for 12 L. No difference in chick body weight was reported by Archer et al. (2017b) when providing a red/white light combination for 12 L during incubation compared to dark for White Leghorn chicks. In the current work, chick body weight was measured at the hatch endpoint (516 h) for all treatments, independent of hatching time, and chicks incubated under 18 L were lighter compared to the shortest photoperiod, however these chicks also hatched earlier. Post-hatch body weights of chicks who hatched early vs. late can differ up to 10% (Careghi et al., 2005; Bergoug et al., 2013).

Along with the reduction in incubation time and decrease in body weight at hatch, a reduction in absolute yolk sac residue and yolk-free body weight at the hatch endpoint was observed with increasing photoperiod duration in the present study. However, when correcting for live body weight, the relative yolk sac residue and yolk-free body weight at the hatch endpoint were not different between photoperiods. Özkan et al. (2012a) reported similar results, and these authors also observed a lower yolk sac residue weight (corrected for embryo weight) in broiler embryos at 18 d of incubation when incubated under a 16 L photoperiod.

Results of this study showed relative weights of the duodenum, jejunum, and cecum in chicks incubated under 12 and 18 L were heaviest. This could be related to the observed differences in the hatching time between treatments (chicks in the 18 L started hatching up to 7.6 h earlier), as the small intestine, and particularly the duodenum, develops rapidly after hatch (Geyra et al., 2001). The reported increase in the relative weight of the digestive tract sections may also be due to the reduction in yolk sac weight in chicks that hatched earlier, in addition to dehydration and tissue utilization, as those chicks waited longer to be processed without access to feed and water. Heavier relative intestinal weight at hatch has been linked to an improvement in broiler performance through improvements in weight gain and feed conversion (Ipek and Sozcu, 2015; Villanueva et al., 2016), however in this study, impacts on post-hatch body weight at 7, 14, and 21 d were small.

Fairchild and Christensen (2000) observed no effects of incubation photoperiod on absolute liver and heart weights for turkey poults, which agrees with the results of this study. However, when the liver and the heart weights were corrected for BW, both were heavier in the 18 L treatment compared to 0 and 6 L in the current study. Wang et al. (2021) reported heavier livers in chicks incubated under 12 L compared to 0 L, which they suggested may have been related to the observed increase in insulin-like growth factor 1 as this is primarily secreted by the liver. Tong et al. (2017) reported heavier heart weights at hatch in chicks incubated under 24 L green light vs 12 L green light. The authors suggested that the increase in heart weight could be due to melatonin possibly impacting embryo cardiac function. Additionally, artificial light from the incubator may accelerate physiological processes as the requirements for maintenance energy increase under light (Cooper et al., 2011). Özkan et al. (2012a) found that a 16 L incubation photoperiod illuminated by white (fluorescent) light did not affect broiler chick`s relative liver and heart weights at embryonic day 13 or 18 compared to embryos incubated under dark. However, the current research observed an increase in relative liver and heart weights in hatchlings when embryos were exposed to an 18 L photoperiod after 21 d of incubation. These findings suggest that if a change in relative liver weight occurs, it could be during the last 3 d of incubation, when yolk sac absorption increases due to changes in metabolism processes, increased energy requirements, and increased need for mobilization of glucose. The chicks in the current study that were incubated under 12 and 18 L photoperiods began hatching earlier and had lower yolk-free body weight at hatch. This suggests an altered metabolic state, which may have been due to an impact of photoperiod duration on embryo development.

Brooding and rearing

During the brooding and rearing period up to 21 d post-hatch, birds incubated under 0 L were the heaviest. Future research could be performed to determine if this effect continued as pullets aged. Broilers hatched under 0, 12, and 24 L (fluorescent light) photoperiods and reared under a 12 L schedule did not differ in body weight from hatch to 42 d of age (Archer et al., 2009). In addition, no differences in BW were observed in broiler chicks hatched under 0 and 12 L (cool white LED) incubation photoperiods and reared under 12 L (Huth and Archer, 2015). This could be due to genetic differences between meat- and layer-type chickens.

Chronic stress was not impacted by in-ovo photoperiod, as no effect was noted on H/L ratio or composite asymmetry levels in male chicks at hatch or female pullets at 21 d in this study. However, at 21 d, pullets from the 12 L treatment showed a tendency for higher asymmetry scores which would indicate an increased stress level. This is contrary to previous studies that found a reduction in asymmetry scores in birds incubated under 12 L compared to 0, 1, and 6 L (Archer and Mench, 2013, 2014; Huth and Archer, 2015).

Rearing poultry under different photoperiod durations can cause significant changes to their behavior (Schwean-Lardner et al., 2012). With evidence that light can reach embryos through the shell (Shafey and Al-mohsen, 2002), it is of interest to know whether adjusting photoperiod duration during incubation can alter chick behavior soon after hatch. In this study, there was little impact on nutritive and comfort behaviors during the first 3 d post-hatch. However, increases in active behaviours were observed under photoperiods longer than 12 L, while photoperiods less than 6 L decreased active behaviors on the first day post-hatch. The higher percentage of time spent foraging by chicks incubated under 18 L could be the result of the earlier hatch time and higher absorption of the yolk sac that was observed. The increase in locomotor behavior might be positive for bird development and well-being as an increase in physical activity can increase bone health and strength (Shipov et al., 2010).

One of the limitations of this study was the small number of eggs incubated (1,200) and the use of small non-commercial incubators. Future research conducted on a commercial scale would be beneficial prior to adoption of this lighting strategy by industry.

In conclusion, the current research indicates that the use of longer photoperiods (12 and 18 L) can reduce hatch time in White Leghorn chicks without detrimental impacts on embryonic or chick health and stress. Shortened hatch times would result in shorter turnaround times for commercial hatcheries. On-farm application was highlighted behaviorally, with chicks incubated under longer photoperiods being more active, which could be beneficial in finding feed and water and stimulating bone development and strength under commercial conditions.
