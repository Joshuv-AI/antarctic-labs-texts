The Impact of Nordic Pole Walk Advice Alone or in Combination With Exercise Strategies on Daily Physical Activity in Patients With Intermittent Claudication: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Anna Sandberg, Joakim Nordanstig, Åsa Cider, Lennart Jivegård, Maria Hagströmer, Maria Bäck
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe impact of exercise interventions on physical activity (PA) remains undetermined in intermittent claudication, which is why it is important to include objectively measured PA as an additional endpoint. The aim of this prespecified secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial was to investigate the impact of unsupervised Nordic pole walk advice (WA) alone or in combination with hospital-based supervised exercise (SEP) or home-based structured exercise (HSEP) on PA in patients with intermittent claudication.MethodsIn total, 166 patients with intermittent claudication (mean age = 72 [SD = 7.4] y; 41% women) were randomized to 3 intermittent claudication-treatment strategies: WA, WA + SEP, or WA + HSEP. All patients received Nordic poles and standardized WA (≥30 min, 3 times weekly). Patients randomized to HSEP and SEP accepted participation in an additional 6-months exercise program. PA was measured with an accelerometer-based activPAL3 monitor for 7 days at baseline and at 3, 6, and 12 months. PA outcomes were steps per day, time spent within a stepping cadence ≥100 steps per minute, time spent upright, number of body transitions from sitting to standing, and number of sitting bouts of >30 minutes and >60 minutes.ResultsAt 1 year, no intergroup differences were observed in any of the PA variables, whereas significant intergroup differences were observed at 3 months regarding time spent within a stepping time cadence ≥100 steps per minute. The mean change for HSEP (2.47 [SD = 10.85] min) was significantly different from the mean change for WA (−3.20 [SD = 6.24] min). At 6 months, the number of sitting bouts (>60 min) for SEP was significantly different from WA (mean change = 0.24 [SD = 0.69] vs −0.23 [SD = 0.81]).ConclusionThis study indicates that the addition of 6 months of HSEP or SEP does not improve PA at 1 year, as compared to unsupervised WA alone. Factors of importance for increasing PA in patients with intermittent claudication require further investigation.ImpactAt the 1-year follow-up, the addition of intermittent claudication-tailored additional exercise strategies did not improve daily PA in patients with intermittent claudication compared with unsupervised Nordic pole WA alone. Future studies may explore the role of behavior change techniques to increase PA in this patient group.Keywords: Accelerometry, Gait Speed, Peripheral Arterial Disease, Sedentary Behavior, Walking

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease is a manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis, and intermittent claudication is the most classical symptom.1 Intermittent claudication is defined as pain in the calf which occurs during exercise and is relieved by rest.2 Patients with intermittent claudication have a higher cardiovascular event rate and mortality as compared with individuals without intermittent claudication.2 Patients with intermittent claudication have a poorer health-related quality of life, in the aspects of physical functioning rather than mental functioning as compared with healthy individuals.3

Moreover, substantially lower levels of physical activity (PA) were observed in a subgroup of patients with intermittent claudication with severe functional impairments as compared with healthy adults,4 including 29% lower daily step count when compared with gender- and age-matched controls.5 Hospital-based supervised exercise programs (SEPs) are recommended in current guidelines as a first-line intermittent claudication treatment to improve walking distance, health-related quality of life, and self-reported functional impairment.6,7 Additionally, home-based structured exercise programs (HSEPs) should be considered to improve walking distance when SEP is not available.6 The exercise regimen in HSEP is similar to SEP, but it is self-directed and organized in a personal setting. Both exercise regimes may include behavioral change techniques.6

Substantial evidence demonstrates the health benefits of regular PA in the general population,8–10 and a primary rehabilitation goal for older adults is often to maintain or increase PA to achieve health benefits.11 Epidemiological studies in patients with peripheral artery disease have shown that higher levels of PA during daily life are associated with improved survival12 and less yearly functional performance decline.13 A cohort study of patients with intermittent claudication also showed that any PA beyond light intensity at baseline was associated with lower mortality during 5 years of follow-up.14 The updated PA guidelines for the general population show evidence that even small amounts of moderate-to-vigorous PA can achieve health benefits when compared with individuals who do not engage in moderate-to vigorous PA.9,10 Moreover, in patients with intermittent claudication, studies have shown an association between PA and a lower annual risk of all-cause mortality, with the greatest risk reduction found when activities were performed with at least moderate intensity.14,15

PA is a complex health behavior that can be difficult to measure.16 In clinical trials, activity monitors such as pedometers and accelerometers are promising as reliable tools for the objective measurement of PA and further allow clinicians to evaluate walking patterns, which is of clinical interest for patients with intermittent claudication.17

Exercise interventions can be evaluated with different walk tests, eg, a standardized treadmill test7 or the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) to assess walking distance in patients with intermittent claudication.18 Recent research suggests that increased pain-free and maximum walking distance after SEP does not automatically lead to an increase in daily PA.19 Overall, only very weak to moderate correlations between exercise capacity outcomes and objectively measured PA have been observed, which indicates the need to use PA as a novel outcome measurement.20 In spite of this, only a few randomized clinical trials have investigated the effect of concurrent intermittent claudication treatment options (SEP, HSEP, control/usual care, endovascular revascularization) on everyday PA, and it remains unclear whether exercise interventions positively influence PA.21

We have previously reported results regarding 6MWT distances and muscle endurance from the SUNFIT trial (Supervised or UNsupervised exercise training For Intermittent claudicaTion), an assessor-blinded, 3-armed, multicenter, randomized clinical trial that investigated the 1-year effectiveness of 6 months of best medical treatment including unsupervised Nordic pole walk advice (WA), WA + SEP or WA + HSEP in patients with lifestyle-limiting intermittent claudication. Our results showed that 6 months of HSEP was non-inferior to SEP, whereas neither of these treatment strategies was superior to an unsupervised WA strategy.22 The aim of this prespecified secondary analysis of the SUNFIT trial was to evaluate the impact of 6 months of WA alone or in combination with HSEP or SEP on objectively assessed PA in patients with intermittent claudication at 1 year, following random allocation into 1 of the 3 above-mentioned treatment strategies.

Methods

Participants

Patients were recruited in Sweden by a vascular surgeon from outpatient clinics at the Departments of Vascular Surgery at Sahlgrenska University Hospital (Gothenburg), Karlstad Central Hospital (Karlstad), and Södra Älvsborg Hospital (Borås) between September 2014 and February 2018. Patients with an established mild to severe intermittent claudication for >6 months, confirmed to be of vascular origin, with an ankle-brachial index of <0.9, and/or a postexercise ankle-brachial index drop of ≥30% were included in this study. The exclusion criteria included previous revascularization for intermittent claudication performed within 3 months before the enrollment visit, a revascularization procedure deemed necessary by the vascular surgeon during the 12 subsequent months after the enrollment visit, cognitive dysfunction, inability to perform the 6MWT, and inability to speak or understand the Swedish language. The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Gothenburg (Entry No. 349-14). All the procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and all the participants provided written informed consent before entering the study. This study protocol is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02341716), and full study details and previous results are reported elsewhere.22

Treatment Strategies

Patients were randomized to 1 of 3 strategies: WA, WA + SEP, or WA + HSEP. A full description of the treatment strategies content and arrangement has previously been published.22 Briefly, all patients received the best medical treatment from a vascular surgeon, including verbal and written disease information, pharmacological risk factor intervention, and a 1-time unsupervised WA that encouraged all the patients to perform limb symptom-inducing Nordic pole walking sessions (3 times weekly, ≥ 30 minutes; WA strategy). There were 2 teams of physical therapists in this study; team A (n = 6) was blinded to the patients’ group affiliation and was responsible for outcome assessments, and team B (n = 8) was responsible for the exercise programs and follow-up. Two weeks after randomization, all the patients visited physical therapist (A) for baseline assessments. Patients allocated to SEP and HSEP strategies were than scheduled to visit physical therapist (B) for the additional 6-month exercise program (3 times weekly; 50 min/session), performed either at the hospital in a group session under the direct supervision of a physical therapist (SEP strategy) or at home with regular feedback (phone calls and face-to-face visits) from a physical therapist (HSEP strategy). Both exercise programs were individually adapted to each patient and consisted of aerobic walking exercises and resistance exercises. After the scheduled 6-month exercise period, patients in SEP and HSEP were instructed to continue with the exercise program at home for 6 months, with no further feedback. Adherence to the SEP and HSEP exercise procedures was measured during the first 6 months using logbooks. To keep the WA unsupervised, adherence to the WA was not assessed in any of the groups.22

Accelerometer-Measured PA

PA was objectively measured with an accelerometer-based activPAL3 monitor (PAL Technologies Ltd, Glasgow, UK). By using proprietary algorithms, activPAL3 is able to determine body posture (standing and sitting/lying), body transitions (sitting to standing and vice versa), stepping, and stepping cadence by measuring acceleration and determining the orientation of the thigh.23 ActivPAL3 is a reliable, valid monitor for detecting posture and purposeful stepping activities in healthy adults.24 One physical therapist from team A placed the activPAL3 in a waterproof sleeve and attached it with Tegaderm™ (3M Health Care Business, Neuss, Germany) to the midline on the patient’s right thigh. Patients were instructed to wear the activPAL3 continuously for 7 full days (ie, 9 days in total) and then to return it in a prepaid padded envelope to the Department of Physiotherapy for data registration. Data were collected at baseline (prior to the initiation of SEP and HSEP) and subsequently at 3, 6, and 12 months (Fig. 1).

Data Analysis

The activPAL3 data were downloaded from the device using PAL software Suite (PAL Technologies Ltd, version 7, Glasgow, UK). First, the data were processed to create “event files,” followed by the processing of the event files with the PALbatch “CREA awake wear-time classification algorithm” and the 14-h protocol (allow up to 10 h continuous stillness; PAL Technologies Ltd, 2020, PAL, PALbatch Software Suite, version 8.10.12.57, CREA version 1.2, Glasgow, UK), thereby creating day-level variables. To enable an evaluation of 7 full days of continuously wearing the activPAL3, the first and last day of the recordings were removed for each patient. To filter out incorrect or unrepresentative data, non-valid days and days with non-wear time exceeding 10 h/day were removed from the calculations and patients with fewer than 4 days of activPAL3 recording per follow-up were excluded from the analysis.25 All PA outcome measurements were calculated as average daily data during each monitoring period.

Outcomes

The outcomes were the change in steps/day, time spent within a stepping cadence of ≥100 steps/min, time spent in an upright position (ie, standing still and stepping), number of transitions (sitting to standing), the number of sitting bouts spent in bouts of >30 min, and the number of sitting bouts spent in bouts >60 min, assessed with activPAL3 for the 3 treatment strategies from baseline to 3, 6, and 12 months.

A stepping cadence of >100 steps/min is a threshold reported to equal moderate intensity in an elderly population.26 A study of patients with intermittent claudication and healthy controls demonstrated that patients accumulated an approximately equal number of minutes per day stepping, with an only slightly lower cadence.27 Therefore, a stepping cadence of >100 steps/min (approximately 4 km/h) was found to be reasonable to describe time in at least moderate intensity activities.

In addition, steps/day was dichotomized into 2 categories: <7000 steps/day and ≥7000 steps/day. Normative data in healthy older adults average 2000 to 9000 steps/day and special populations with disability and/or chronic illness average 1200 to 8800 steps/day. The broad range of PA level reflects the natural diversity of abilities in the older population.28 A study by Gardner et al5 showed that patients with intermittent claudication take an average of 6722 steps/day, and for this reason, 7000 steps/day was regarded as a reasonable threshold in this study.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint in the overall SUNFIT study was a change in the 6MWT from baseline to 1 year, and the study sample size was determined based on a non-inferiority hypothesis comparing SEP with HSEP, which explains why no formal power calculation underpins this secondary analysis of daily PA.22 Categorical data are presented as absolute and relative frequencies, whereas continuous data are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs) or 95% CI as appropriate. Intergroup comparisons were performed with 1-way analysis of variance and observed significant intergroup differences were further analyzed with pairwise post hoc tests, adjusted by the Bonferroni correction for multiple tests. The outcome analysis was performed on the initial treatment assignment (intention to treat), and we used the full dataset without any imputation of missing values. The statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).

Role of the Funding Source

The funders played no role in the design, conduct, or reporting of this study.

Results

A total of 166 patients with intermittent claudication that had been randomly allocated to the 3 different treatment strategies, ie, HSEP (n = 56), SEP (n = 54), and WA (n = 56), were included in this preregistered secondary analysis of the SUNFIT trial. During the course of the study 14 patients (8.4%) withdrew their consent. At the 1-year follow-up visit, 139 patients (HSEP:47, SEP:46, WA:46) had complete accelerometer data and were included in the analyses. The patient flow chart is shown in Figure 2. Patient characteristics in terms of demographics, risk factors, and comorbidities at baseline are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 72.1 (SD = 7.4) years, and 41% were women. Most patients had moderate claudication according to the Rutherford classification,29 cardiac disease was present in 31.9%, and almost 30% had diabetes mellitus. No significant intergroup differences were observed at baseline. Full exercise adherence (ie, participated in ≥80% of the scheduled exercise sessions at the recommended intensity according to the Borg rating of perceived exertion for ≥30 min per session) was shown in 9 patients (24%) in HSEP and in 14 patients (26%) in SEP. The corresponding numbers for partial exercise participation (ie, attendance at ≥20% to <80% of exercise sessions, irrespective of the Borg rating of perceived exertion) were 27 patients (71%) in HSEP and 26 patients (48%) in SEP.22

Over time, the number of valid recorded days was an average of 328 days for HSEP, 317 days for SEP, and 334 days for WA. On 3% of the occasions (n = 18), data from the activPAL3 device could not be accessed due to the activPAL3 being broken (n = 5), the activPAL3 getting lost in the mail (n = 3), or patients being unable to wear the activPAL3 due to surgery or angiography (n = 2), among other reasons (Fig. 2). A total of 0.8% (n = 30) nonvalid days were removed from calculations. A total of 3 days had >10 h/day non-wear time and these days were consequently removed from analysis. Overall, 5 patients had fewer than 4 days of activPAL3 recording and were thus excluded from the analysis. At baseline, no intergroup differences were observed in any of the activPAL3 outcomes (P > .05; data not shown).

Discussion

This analysis of SUNFIT trial data demonstrated no intergroup difference for change in steps per day from baseline until 1 year.

In a study by Fokkenrood et al,19 no increase in daily PA level was found after 3 months of SEP, despite an increase in pain-free and maximum walking distance. The same pattern has been observed in patients with knee and hip osteoarthritis30 and chronic heart failure.31 These results potentially indicate that patients who increase their walking distance will not use this capacity to walk more often or with longer duration in their daily life.19 Additionally, we found that the WA strategy significantly spent less time sitting (in bouts >60 min), as compared to SEP at 6 months. A similar pattern was shown for patients in both SEP and HSEP who, over time, spent more time sitting, as compared with WA. To this end, Whipple et al32 implied that patients with intermittent claudication participating in SEP may compensate for the extra activity by reducing their free-living PA. As an increase in physical capacity following participation in an exercise program does not necessarily translates into changes in daily life PA behavior, it may be important, from a clinical perspective, to study both of these outcomes in future studies.

At 3 months, a significant intergroup difference was observed in favor of HSEP compared with WA, in terms of time spent within a stepping cadence of ≥100 steps/min. One possible explanation of this result could be that patients in HSEP were instructed to perform a moderate-to-vigorous exercise program 3 times weekly, including different over ground walking and jogging exercises. It can be hypothesized that the patients in HSEP placed a greater focus on increasing their overall PA in the home environment, which may explain this observation. Overall, our patients spent approximately 10 min/day in a stepping cadence of ≥100 steps/min. Furthermore, when evaluating stepping activity in healthy adults, activPAL3 detects >90% of steps taken at a cadence of ≥69 steps/min but it detects no steps at a cadence of <24 steps/min. The results of a stepping cadence of <69 steps/min should therefore be interpreted with caution.33 Additionally, Gardner et al34 have suggested that another threshold of >60 steps/min is equivalent to walking at moderate intensity in patients with intermittent claudication. However, as this threshold is calculated from step accumulation (total number of steps taken per minute) and not on step cadence (total number of steps divided by the stepping time), it will generate different outcomes and is not directly comparable to our results.27 In the study by Gardner et al,34 positive associations of better vascular outcomes were found in patients with intermittent claudication taking >1000 steps/day at a cadence (ie, step accumulation) of >60 steps/min as compared with patients with intermittent claudication taking approximately 450 steps/day. Although stepping cadence has previously been studied in patients with intermittent claudication,27,35 to the best of our knowledge, no study has previously evaluated the impact of SEP, HSEP, and WA on stepping cadence.

Improved ambulatory function, assessed with different walk tests for maximum and pain-free walking distance, gait speed, cadence and stride length, and health-related quality of life are seen in patients with intermittent claudication who take >7000 steps/day as compared with those who do not reach this threshold.36 The dichotomization of our patients into taking <7000 steps/day or ≥ 7000 steps/day showed that most patients (63–72%) took an average of fewer than 7000 steps/day at baseline. These results are in line with the results reported by Gardner et al5 that approximately 62% of patients with intermittent claudication took <7000 steps/day and further, had a 62% lower chance of attaining 7000 steps/day as compared with age- and gender-matched controls. Furthermore, a curvilinear relationship has been observed between PA and health benefits in adults, with the greatest relative risk reduction in mortality and morbidity occurring when shifting from being inactive to more active.37 However, only a few of our patients taking <7000 steps/day at baseline increased to >7000 steps/day at 1 year. Additionally, all our patients were given a 1-time WA, and in order to keep the WA strategy unsupervised, no further feedback was given to this advice. Therefore, increasing daily PA was not the subject of particular focus in our study, per se. A systematic review concludes that in order to engage in and adhere to walking recommendations, patients with intermittent claudication need continuous encouragement, support, and feedback.38

Moreover, interventions combining self-monitoring with other components such as goal-setting and feedback on behavior change have been found to be effective for increasing PA in healthy adults.39 In patients with intermittent claudication, only a few studies have evaluated the effects of behavior change techniques to increase PA. In a small cohort study where patients with intermittent claudication received an activity tracker and weekly follow-up for input about goal setting, overcoming barriers, and preventing relapse, a non-significant change in steps/day between baseline and 6-months follow-up was observed.40 Cunningham et al41,42 found that a brief psychological intervention including motivational interviewing and action planning significantly increased steps/day at 4 months in the intervention group as compared with controls, and this difference was sustained at 1- and 2-year follow-ups. A better future understanding of the possible role played by behavioral change interventions in increasing PA may inform and improve secondary prevention strategies of patients with intermittent claudication.

The baseline assessment of activPAL3 in our study was measured after the WA were given to all patients and furthermore, with the intention of keeping the WA completely unsupervised, patient adherence to the WA was not monitored in any of the strategies. This results in some uncertainty regarding whether—and to what extent—patients adhered to the WA. The baseline 6219.7 (SD = 2804) steps/day in our study population can be compared with other studies that used different types of accelerometers or pedometers. In a recent study by Gardner et al,5 patients with intermittent claudication reported a daily step count of 6722 (SD = 3393), which compares reasonably well with our findings. On the other hand, other studies in patients with intermittent claudication present lower steps/day counts at baseline.5,21,43 Since the WA in our study was given before the baseline activPAL3 device assessment, it can be hypothesized that our patients might therefore have already become slightly more physically active at the baseline registration of daily activity.

Recently, a systematic review evaluated studies in which wearable activity monitors were incorporated in HSEP interventions for patients with intermittent claudication.44 In addition to wearable activity monitors, regular follow-up by face-to-face visits or by telephone was provided for exercise progression and adjustments of prescribed exercise dose. Their findings suggested some evidence that interventions that incorporated wearable activity monitors may be beneficial for improving intermittent claudication walking ability. This could also be an important future area of research.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the long-term follow-up at 1 year, the blinding of assessors, and the randomized trial design, which generated closely comparable groups at baseline. Another strength is the methodology to control the accelerometer data using predefined rules of excluding nonvalid days and days with ≥10 hours of non-wear time, and the lowest acceptable minimum wear-days threshold (>4 days).17 One study limitation is that the sample size calculation was based on the 6-minute walking distance endpoint rather than on the daily PA endpoint that was explored in this secondary analysis. As a result, the possibility that this study may have been underpowered for the performed analysis cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, an important finding is that only a few patients in HSEP (24%) and SEP (26%) groups were fully adherent to the prescribed exercise strategies. Wear time diaries in addition to the activPAL may be important tools to verify wear time validation and the collection of information on wake and sleeping time or other contexts of interest.45 As patients in the present study did not fill out wear time diaries, this may be considered as a limitation. We therefore used the CREA algorithm and the 14-h protocol, which automatically removes ≤10 h of continuous time spent lying from the calculation. Additionally, to our knowledge, no validity study has been published for the CREA algorithm and the 14-h protocol, ultimately resulting in some remaining uncertainty about accelerometer algorithm data accuracy. In 2021, Carlson et al46 compared the CREA algorithm with the participants’ diaries. Based on agreement with diaries, the CREA algorithm showed good validity for classifying out-of-bed time, in-bed time, and periods over 2.5 to 2.75 h of non-wear time. In addition, activPAL has not been validated for patients with intermittent claudication and there are no standardized guidelines, established consensus documents, or best practice recommendations for reporting activPAL outcomes for PA in older adults.47

Conclusion

This prespecified secondary analysis of patients with intermittent claudication suggests that the addition of a 6-month HSEP or a hospital-based SEP does not improve daily PA at 1 year, as compared with patients receiving unsupervised WA alone. Significant intergroup differences were observed at 3 months in time spent in a stepping cadence of >100 steps/min, in favor of HSEP as compared with WA and at 6 months, regarding sitting bouts of >60 min duration, where WA spent less time sitting as compared with SEP. Future studies should focus on elaborating on factors that increase daily PA in patients with intermittent claudication.
