Contactin-2 mitigates heart failure and cardiac remodeling via regulation of NUPR1 and ferroptosis
Authors: Yu-Dong Fei, Zhi-Xing Wei, Tai-Zhong Chen, Liu-Hui Zhang, Yi-Chao Zhang, Jian-Wen Hou, Qian Wang, Xing-Xing Cai, Mu Chen, Yu-Li Yang, Peng-Cheng Yao, Zhen-Tao Fei, Yangjinming Bai, Ting-Ting Zhao, Ji Yan, Wei Li, Mei Yang, Rui Zhang, Xiang-Dong Liu, Ming-Zhe Zhao, Xiao-Lei Xu, Jian Sun, Yue-Peng Wang, Yi-Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe roles of Contactin-2 (CNTN2) and ferroptosis in heart failure and cardiac remodeling remain incompletely understood.ResultsCNTN2 was significantly upregulated in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patients and heart failure mice. In cardiomyocyte specific CNTN2 conditional knockout (CNTN2 cKO) mice, transverse aortic constriction (TAC) induced markedly exacerbated heart failure, cardiac remodeling and ferroptosis compared to control mice. Ferroptosis inhibition substantially attenuated heart failure in CNTN2 cKO mice subjected to TAC, indicating that enhanced ferroptosis contributes to the detrimental effects of CNTN2 deficiency. RNA sequencing identified NUPR1, a ferroptosis repressor, as a downstream molecule of CNTN2. Mechanistically, CNTN2 activated the Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4 pathway to regulate NUPR1. CNTN2 overexpression attenuated Angiotensin II-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and pathological remodeling, whereas these protective effects were abolished by Lyn or NUPR1 inhibitors. We further revealed CNTN2 and Lyn interacted with each other, and that CNTN2 interacted with Lyn through its 1-328aa domain. In vivo NUPR1 overexpression via AAV9 significantly mitigated TAC-induced heart failure and cardiac remodeling in CNTN2 cKO mice.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that CNTN2 protects against pressure overload induced heart failure and cardiac remodeling by regulating ferroptosis through the Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 pathway, suggesting CNTN2 as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00890-5.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of cardiovascular death worldwide [1]. Although several new therapeutic approaches have been developed, the prognosis for HF patients remains poor [2]. Identifying new therapeutic targets is crucial for improving the outcomes of HF patient. Cardiac remodeling, characterized by structural and functional changes in response to mechanical stress and myocardial injury, leads to progressively impaired heart function and is a key pathological process in HF [3]. Under ischemic conditions, the release of damage-associated molecular patterns or alarmins from dying cells triggers inflammation and cardiac remodeling. While in non-ischemic conditions, including pressure overload-induced HF, other mechanisms play significant roles [4]. Programmed cell death has been extensively studied including apoptosis [5], but the benefit of apoptosis blockade may be attributed to non-myocytes [6]. Other forms of programmed cell death have been recognized [7], and accumulating evidence supports their pathophysiological roles in HF.

Recently, evidence has identified ferroptosis as an important mode of programmed cell death. Ferroptosis is widely involved in stress response and organ dysfunction [8]. Cardiomyocyte ferroptosis mediates doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy, and inhibition of ferroptosis effectively ameliorated HF induced by doxorubicin or ischemia/reperfusion [9]. However, the roles of ferroptosis in cardiac remodeling and pressure overload-induced HF are not yet fully understood.

Contactin-2 (CNTN2), a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, is expressed in the nervous system and heart. It is involved in neurogenesis [10], neuronal migration [11] and regulation of potassium channels [12]. Pallante et al. demonstrated that CNTN2 is expressed in the heart, and is richly expressed in the cardiac conduction system [13]. It is reported that in a coculture condition, cardiomyocytes adjacent to cardiac fibroblasts showed increased expression of CNTN2, but the effects of increased expression of CNTN2 was not further explored [14]. Zhou et al. showed that CNTN2 protects against aortic valve calcification [15]. However, the functions of CNTN2 in HF have not been fully investigated.

In this study, we showed that CNTN2 expression is significantly upregulated in both human and mouse models of HF. Cardiomyocyte specific CNTN2 conditional knockout (CNTN2 cKO) exacerbated HF and ferroptosis in a mouse HF model induced by transverse aortic constriction (TAC). NUPR1, a repressor of ferroptosis [16], is a downstream molecule of CNTN2, and its regulatory roles were verified both in vivo and in vitro. Our results demonstrate the novel functions of CNTN2 in pressure overload-induced HF and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, providing experimental evidence for new therapeutic targets against HF and cardiac remodeling.

Methods

Detailed experimental protocols are presented in the Supplemental Materials.

Induction of TAC in mice

CNTN2 cKO and CNTN2flox/flox (control) male mice aged 8–10 weeks were randomized to receive TAC to induce HF models or sham operation. Briefly, after pentobarbital sodium anesthesia and mechanical ventilation, TAC was performed by ligation around a 27G needle with a 7 − 0 silk suture. Then the needle was promptly removed to induce pressure overload heart failure. Sham-operated animals receive identical procedures except for constriction step. The operators were blinded to the randomization.

Cell isolation and culture

Different protocols were performed to obtain cardiomyocytes: neonatal mice ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMVMs) were obtained from 1- to 2-day-old neonatal C57BL/6 mice [17], and primary adult cardiomyocytes were isolated using Langendorff apparatus [18, 19]. Cells were treated with CNTN2 overexpression adenovirus (Ad-CNTN2) or negative control (Ad-NC) (Hanbio Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). To investigate the signaling pathways, PP2 (10µM) [20] or ZZW-115 (5µM) [21] was added to the medium to inhibit Lyn or NUPR1.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as means ± SEM of at least three independent experiments unless otherwise indicated. Data analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed t-tests for dual-group comparisons or analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc analysis for multi-group analysis. A threshold of p < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

CNTN2 is critically involved in heart failure

Firstly, we analyzed a previously published single-cell sequencing dataset of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) patients and healthy controls [22]. Of the 55,122 cells, we selected cardiomyocytes for further analysis, comprising 27.8% of healthy samples and 14.8% of HCM samples. CNTN2 was significantly upregulated in HCM cardiomyocytes (p = 3.2 × 10− 10, Figure S1A-C).

Next, we analyzed the expression of the contactin family in the ventricle tissues of sham-operated and heart failure model mice. Quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR) showed that only CNTN2 expression was significantly increased in the heart failure model (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis confirmed that CNTN2 was significantly upregulated in TAC-induced heart failure mouse ventricle tissues compared to the sham group (p = 0.015, Fig. 1B). Additionally, immunostaining of ventricle tissue revealed increased CNTN2 expression in HF patient compared to control (Figure S1D-E), and increased CNTN2 expression in the mouse HF model (Fig. 1C and D).

Given that previous reports indicated CNTN2 is highly expressed in the cardiac conduction system, we performed additional experiments to confirm the expression changes of CNTN2 in cardiomyocytes. We enzymatically perfused hearts using the Langendorff apparatus, carefully removed the endocardium, where most cardiac conduction cells are located, and extracted protein from epicardial cardiomyocytes. Western blot analysis showed significantly increased expression of CNTN2 in the TAC group (Figure S1F). Additionally, wild-type (WT) NMVMs were treated with Angiotensin-II (AngII, 1 µM) or vehicle. CNTN2 expression was also upregulated in the AngII group (Figure S1G). Moreover, we isolated different cell types from mouse ventricular tissue, and Western blot results showed that CNTN2 is mainly expressed in cardiomyocytes, but not endothelial cells, fibroblasts or macrophages (Figure S1H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CNTN2 expression is significantly upregulated in cardiomyocytes in heart failure.

To analyze the roles of CNTN2 in vivo, we performed TAC to induce HF in CNTN2 cKO and CNTN2flox/flox (control) littermates. The decreased expression of CNTN2 in the CNTN2 cKO group compared to the control group was verified by qPCR (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.004, Fig. 1E), Western blot (p = 0.004, Figs. 1F), and immunofluorescence staining (Figure S1I).

CNTN2 conditional knockout aggravated heart failure and cardiac remodeling

Heart function and structural remodeling were assessed by echocardiography. TAC induced decreased ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) in control mice (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 2A-C). The EF (46.203 ± 1.277% vs. 38.511 ± 1.535%, post-hoc p = 0.010) and FS (22.846 ± 0.782% vs. 18.565 ± 0.846%, post-hoc p = 0.036) were further decreased in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to the control TAC group. The left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) was significantly increased (CNTN2 cKO TAC group vs. control TAC group, 4.193 ± 0.071 mm vs. 4.517 ± 0.080 mm, ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.047, Fig. 2D). The CNTN2 cKO TAC group showed higher levels of heart weight (HW)/ body weight (BW) (5.341 ± 0.103 mg/g vs. 5.795 ± 0.110 mg/g, ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.014, Fig. 2E) and HW/ tibia length (TL) (7.184 ± 0.185 mg/mm vs. 8.619 ± 0.222 mg/mm, ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 2F) compared to the control TAC group. Mortality rates tended to be higher in CNTN2 cKO mice compared to control mice following TAC (Figure S2A).

Next, we performed ex vivo experiments to further investigate the roles of CNTN2 deficiency in heart failure. Cardiac remodeling was assessed by histological staining. HE staining demonstrated more pronounced cardiac hypertrophy in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group (Fig. 2G). Cross-sectional area measurements confirmed more significant cardiac hypertrophy (335.201 ± 9.285 µm2 vs. 431.054 ± 11.701 µm2, ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Figs. 2H-I) in CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to control TAC group. Also, Masson`s trichrome staining revealed that cardiac fibrosis was significantly aggravated in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to Ctl TAC group (Figure S2B-C). Pathological cardiomyocyte gene expressions were analyzed by qPCR, revealing significant increases in NPPA (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.002, Fig. 2J), NPPB (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 2K), and MYH7 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.002, Fig. 2L). Taken together, our results demonstrated that CNTN2 cKO aggravates pressure overload induced heart failure and cardiac pathological remodeling.

Due to the high expression of CNTN2 in the cardiac conduction system, we monitored mouse ECG in vivo. The ECG parameters were comparable between the CNTN2 cKO TAC and control TAC groups (Figure S2D-E), indicating that cardiac conduction dysfunction was unlikely to be the mechanism leading to exacerbated heart failure.

Programmed cardiomyocyte death is critically involved in the progression of heart failure, particularly under non-ischemic conditions [23]. To investigate this, we examined different types of programmed cardiomyocyte death. As shown in Fig. 2, the levels of pyroptosis markers (cleaved Caspase-1 and IL18) and apoptosis markers (cleaved Caspase-3 and Bcl-2) were significantly elevated in control TAC groups compared to control sham groups. However, the expressions were comparable between the CNTN2 cKO TAC and control TAC groups (Fig. 2M-Q). These findings suggest that CNTN2 deficiency exacerbated heart failure by inducing cardiac pathological remodeling, with no substantial contribution from increased cardiomyocyte pyroptosis or apoptosis.

CNTN2 conditional knockout significantly aggravated ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is recently recognized as an important form of programmed cell death, and is involved in cardiac remodeling. We further analyzed ferroptosis levels in the different groups by assessing multiple markers. MDA, an indicator of ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation, was significantly increased in the control TAC group compared to the control Sham group (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.020) and further augmented in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to the control TAC group (post-hoc p = 0.001, Fig. 3A). Cardiac Ptgs2 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.006, Fig. 3B) and non-heme iron (Figure S3A) were also increased in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to control TAC group. To further investigate the roles of CNTN2 in heart failure and its regulation of ferroptosis, we performed experiments on NMVMs. CNTN2 cKO and control NMVMs were treated with AngII or vehicle. Analysis of ferroptosis markers showed significantly higher levels of MDA (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.007) and 8-OHDG (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.001) in control + AngII group compared to control + vehicle group. Furthermore, levels of MDA (post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 3C) and 8-OHDG (post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 3D) were further increased in CNTN2 cKO AngII group compared to control AngII group.

Ferroptosis markers were also analyzed by Western blot. The results showed that compared to the control TAC group, ACSL4 was significantly induced in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.027), while GPX4 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.047) were downregulated (Fig. 3E-G). Immunohistochemical staining of mouse ventricles showed higher levels of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), a product of lipid peroxidation, in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group (Figure S3B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed significantly disorganized mitochondria in TAC groups. Notably, in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group, characteristic features of ferroptosis were observed, including increased mitochondrial membrane density, mitochondrial shrinkage and loss of cristae (Fig. 3H, Figure S3C-D). The Flameng score was increased in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to Ctl TAC group (p < 0.001, Figure S3E). Moreover, NMVMs of CNTN2 cKO and control mice were treated with AngII or vehicle, and C11-BODIPY 581/591 fluorescence probe was used to assess lipid perioxidation. Staining images showed a marked increase in green fluorescence intensity in CNTN2 cKO NMVMs following AngII treatment, indicating enhanced lipid ROS production in CNTN2 cKO AngII group compared with control AngII group (post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 3I and J).

Ferroptosis inhibition attenuates heart failure in CNTN2 cKO mice subjected to TAC

To further determine whether enhanced ferroptosis mediates the aggravated cardiac dysfunction observed in CNTN2 cKO mice after pressure overload, CNTN2 cKO mice subjected to TAC were randomly assigned to receive the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) or vehicle. Western blot analysis confirmed upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 in cKO TAC + Fer-1 group compared with cKO TAC + vehicle group (Fig. 4A-C). Consistently, the lipid peroxidation marker 4-HNE was markedly reduced in cKO TAC + Fer-1 group compared to cKO TAC + vehicle group (Fig. 4D).

Furthermore, CNTN2 cKO NMVMs were treated with AngII or vehicle in the presence or absence of Fer-1. C11-BODIPY probe staining showed that AngII markedly increased oxidized BODIPY fluorescence in CNTN2-deficient cardiomyocytes, indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation. Next, we analyzed whether inhibition of ferroptosis could ameliorate TAC induced heart failure in CNTN2 cKO mice. Echocardiographic analysis demonstrated that Fer-1 treatment significantly improved cardiac function in CNTN2 cKO TAC mice, as evidenced by increased EF and FS, along with reduced LVEDD, compared with cKO TAC + vehicle group (Fig. 4G-J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that loss of CNTN2 exacerbates pressure overload–induced heart failure primarily by promoting ferroptosis.

CNTN2 activates Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 pathway to regulate ferroptosis

Next, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CNTN2 mitigates heart failure and ferroptosis. RNA sequencing on ventricle tissue from CNTN2 cKO TAC and control TAC mice identified 251 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To identify ferroptosis-related genes among the 251 DEGs, we utilized the FerrDb V2 database [24] and intersected the 251 DEGs with the 621 ferroptosis-related genes annotated in FerrDb, yielding 5 overlapping genes (Figs. 5A). Among these candidates, NUPR1 was demonstrated to play a critical role in both cardiovascular disease and ferroptosis [25]. Notably, NUPR1 was also included in the most significantly downregulated Gene Ontology biological process “defense response” (p = 1.1 × 10− 4, Figs. 5B), and is the only one of the 5 genes encoding a transcriptional regulator. So we hypothesized that NUPR1 may serve as a key downstream mediator of CNTN2. The downregulation of NUPR1 in the CNTN2 cKO TAC group compared to the control TAC group was verified by qPCR (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.002, Fig. 5C) and Western blot (p = 0.005, Fig. 5D). Together, these findings identify NUPR1 as a potential downstream effector through which CNTN2 regulates cardiac remodeling and ferroptosis.

Next, we explored the molecular mechanism by which CNTN2 regulates NUPR1 and ferroptosis. CNTN2 is known to activate Lyn, a member of the Syk kinase family [26], which has been implicated in mediating the endoplasmic reticulum stress response [27]. The eIF2α/ATF4 pathway is critically involved in endoplasmic reticulum stress response, which has been shown to induce NUPR1 [16] and mitigate ferroptosis [28, 29]. Based on this, we hypothesized that CNTN2 activates Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4 signaling pathway to regulate NUPR1 and ferroptosis. We analyzed the protein levels of the Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 pathway in mouse ventricle tissues (Fig. 5E). Our results showed that the expression levels of phosphorylated eIF2α (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001), ATF4 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.010), and NUPR1 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001) were significantly increased in control TAC group compared to control Sham group. Compared to control TAC group, CNTN2 cKO TAC group showed comparable levels of total Lyn expression (Figure S4A), but significantly decreased levels of phosphorylated Lyn (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.005, Fig. 5F), phosphorylated eIF2α (post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 5G), ATF4 (post-hoc p = 0.003, Fig. 5H), and NUPR1 (post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 5I).

Furthermore, we performed experiments on NMVMs to confirm the signaling pathway. CNTN2 cKO NMVMs treated with vehicle showed significant decreased CNTN2 compared to control NMVMs treated with vehicle (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.009, Figure S4B-C), and the expression is also decreased in CNTN2 cKO AngII group compared to control AngII group (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001). The expression levels of phosphorylated Lyn (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Figure S4D), phosphorylated eIF2α (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.121, Figure S4E), ATF4 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Figure S4F), and NUPR1 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.003, Figure S4G) were decreased in CNTN2 cKO + vehicle group compared to control + vehicle group. Consistent with the results of ventricle tissue, the expression levels of phosphorylated Lyn, phosphorylated eIF2α, ATF4, and NUPR1 (post-hoc p < 0.001) were significantly decreased in CNTN2 cKO AngII group compared to control AngII group.

These results demonstrate that CNTN2 deficiency induces ferroptosis via Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 pathway, which contributes to exacerbated heart failure and cardiac remodeling.

CNTN2 mRNA levels were significantly increased after Ad-CNTN2 treatment, as were NUPR1 mRNA levels (ANOVA p < 0.001, Ad-CNTN2 vs. Ad-NC post-hoc p = 0.001, Fig. 6A and B). Induction of CNTN2 significantly alleviated the increased levels of NPPA (ANOVA p = 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.012, Fig. 6C), NPPB (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 6D) and MYH7 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.004, Fig. 6E) in Ad-CNTN2 AngII group compared to Ad-NC AngII group. Markers of ferroptosis were also assessed, revealing a significant reduction in the upregulated levels of MDA (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.018, Fig. 6F) and Ptgs2 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 6G) in Ad-CNTN2 AngII group compared to Ad-NC AngII group. Furthermore, we analyzed the signaling pathway by Western blot (Fig. 6H). In Ad-CNTN2 AngII group, levels of CNTN2, phosphorylated Lyn, phosphorylated eIF2α, ATF4 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p < 0.001, Fig. 6I and L) were significantly increased compared to Ad-NC AngII group, as well as NUPR1 (ANOVA p < 0.001, post-hoc p = 0.010, Fig. 6M). Total Lyn expression was comparable between groups (Figure S4H).

Discussion

In our study, we elucidated the pivotal roles of CNTN2 in heart failure and pathological cardiac remodeling. We observed that CNTN2 deficiency exacerbated heart dysfunction and cardiac remodeling in a TAC mouse model (Figs. 2 and 3), whereas overexpression of CNTN2 conferred protective effects (Fig. 6). Previously, CNTN2 was primarily recognized for its expression in the nervous system [30], with limited exploration of its involvement in cardiovascular system until Pallante et al. demonstrated its presence in cardiac tissues [13]. Due to its high expression in Purkinje cells compared to ventricles, CNTN2 has been utilized as a marker for the cardiac conduction system [31]. However, its role in cardiac pathologies, particularly heart failure, remained inadequately explored. In our investigation, we initially assessed CNTN2 expression changes in a previously published single cell dataset of HCM patients, and also in human and mouse ventricular tissues during heart failure using immunostaining, qPCR, and Western blot analyses, revealing significant upregulation of CNTN2. Our results are in accordance with previous reports that fibroblast induces expression of CNTN2 in cardiomyocytes [14]. Considering the abundant presence of Purkinje cells in endocardial ventricular tissue, which could influence qPCR and Western blot outcomes, we further examined CNTN2 expression in Purkinje cell-scarce cardiomyocytes from the epicardium and in NMVMs. Collectively, these analyses underscored the upregulation of CNTN2 specifically in cardiomyocytes during heart failure. Moreover, our in vivo findings demonstrated that CNTN2 cKO TAC mice exhibited more pronounced deterioration in heart function and cardiac remodeling compared to control TAC mice, meanwhile ECG parameters remained unaffected. Consequently, we conclude that CNTN2 is upregulated in heart failure and its deficiency exacerbates cardiac dysfunction and remodeling.

Our study highlighted that CNTN2 conditional knockout exacerbates pressure overload induced cardiac remodeling through induction of ferroptosis, a mechanism potentially mediated by the downregulation of NUPR1, a critical repressor of ferroptosis (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). In ischemia-induced heart failure, cardiomyocyte death and inflammatory responses are well-established contributors to pathological hypertrophy and cardiac remodeling [32]. However, the mechanisms by which pressure overload triggers cardiomyocyte death in the absence of ischemia and the release of cell death factors remain incompletely understood. Our results demonstrated that CNTN2 cKO exacerbated heart failure after TAC. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of immune response-related pathways. This finding is noteworthy because CNTN2, a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, has been implicated in immune regulation in multiple sclerosis, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, and other immune-associated disorders [33, 34]. Given the close association between immune activation and regulated cell death, we further evaluated multiple forms of programmed cell death in CNTN2 cKO group. The results revealed selectively enhanced ferroptosis, but not pyroptosis or apoptosis, in the CNTN2 cKO TAC mouse model (Figs. 2 and 3). Ferroptosis, characterized by lipid peroxide accumulation, represents a distinct form of regulated cell death involved in various pathological processes, such as cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury and heart failure [9, 35]. Mechanistically, CNTN2 deficiency downregulated the Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4 pathway, leading to the decrease of NUPR1and enhanced ferroptosis. Conversely, CNTN2 overexpression attenuated cardiomyocyte ferroptosis and pathological remodeling. Given that ventricular remodeling actively contributes to cardiac dysfunction, it remains an attractive target for heart failure therapeutics, though clinical trial outcomes vary, with some proving disappointing [36–38]. Our findings provide further evidence implicating ferroptosis in non-ischemic heart failure and suggest that CNTN2 could be a promising therapeutic target for cardiac remodeling.

Our findings suggest that CNTN2 functions as an upstream modulator of the Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 signaling pathway by regulating the phosphorylation of key signaling molecules. Previous studies have demonstrated that CNTN2 directly interacts with Lyn and promotes phosphorylation of its activation site Y416 in nervous system [26, 39, 40]. However, whether this regulatory mechanism exists in the cardiovascular system and the molecular basis underlying the interaction remained unclear. In the present study, we demonstrated that CNTN2 and Lyn interacts with each other in cardiomyocytes, and identified the 1-328aa region of CNTN2 as the critical region mediating its interaction with Lyn. Functionally, CNTN2 deficiency reduces Lyn phosphorylation, further suppressed phosphorylation of eIF2α and subsequent activation of ATF4 and NUPR1, and aggravates ferroptosis and cardiac remodeling. Conversely, CNTN2 overexpression enhanced Lyn phosphorylation, further activated eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1. As CNTN2 is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored cell adhesion molecule, it may not directly phosphorylate Lyn. A plausible mechanism is that CNTN2 serves as a membrane scaffold that promotes Lyn activation by facilitating protein interactions. Consistent with this hypothesis, previous studies demonstrated that CNTN2 interacted with Lyn in lipid rafts, and lipid raft disruption by MβCD reduced the amounts of Lyn coimmunoprecipitated with CNTN2 [26]. Therefore, CNTN2 may promote Lyn activation by facilitating protein interactions, thereby subsequently enhance eIF2α phosphorylation, leading to activation of ATF4 and NUPR1, and suppression of ferroptosis. Future studies are needed to further clarify the molecular basis of this phosphorylation-dependent mechanism.

Additionally, our study demonstrated that CNTN2 deficiency leads to NUPR1 downregulation, exacerbating heart failure and cardiac hypertrophy (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8). Notably, NUPR1, also known as p8, is a stress-responsive transcription regulator involved in diverse processes such as cell proliferation and inflammation. Consistent with prior reports [25], we observed upregulation of NUPR1 in our TAC-induced heart failure model (Fig. 5). Recent literature further underscores NUPR1’s critical role as a ferroptosis repressor [16], corroborated by our findings demonstrating that NUPR1 downregulation exacerbates cardiomyocyte ferroptosis, whereas its overexpression protects against ferroptosis in vivo. Collectively, our results support the notion that CNTN2 regulates NUPR1 to mitigate heart failure and pathological remodeling primarily by suppressing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.

Limitations

Firstly, most experiments were conducted in animal models, necessitating future clinical trials for validation. Additionally, this study primarily employed a transverse aortic constriction model and Angiotensin II–treated neonatal mouse ventricular myocytes, which mainly represent pressure overload–induced heart failure. Whether the cardioprotective effects of CNTN2 extend to other forms of heart failure, such as diabetic or inflammatory cardiomyopathy, requires further investigation.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that CNTN2 deficiency aggravated heart failure and cardiac hypertrophy by inducing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis via Lyn/eIF2α/ATF4/NUPR1 pathway (Figure S6), and CNTN2 might be an effective therapeutic target of heart failure and cardiac remodeling.
