The mid-term outcomes on reverse total shoulder arthroplasty with isolated latissimus dorsi transfer in patients with isolated external rotation lag without pseudoparalysis: an average follow-up of 5 years
Authors: Yu-Seok Kim, Seung Jin Yoo, Sung-San Park, Jong-Hun Kim, Jae Chul Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) is a well-established replacement strategy to treat irreparable massive rotator cuff tears and cuff tear arthropathy. In patients with forward (FF) deficits combined with a loss of external rotation (ER), RSA with latissimus dorsi transfer (LDT), which was initially used for brachial plexopathies, has been addressed to restore both functional deficits. The aim of the study is to assess mid-term clinical and radiological outcomes of concomitant RSA with LDT in patients with significant preoperative external rotation lag greater than 30 degrees without pseudoparalysis. We hypothesized that RTSA with concomitant isolated LDT can effectively improve ER range of motion (ROM) and ER lag postoperatively.MethodsOur final case series included 26 patients who underwent RTSA concomitant with isolated LDT out of 341 RTSA’s from July 2014 to July 2023. Inclusion criteria were (1) ER lag greater than 30 degrees and Goutallier grade greater than 3 in infrapinatus and teres minor muscle on preoperative MRI (Goutallier in J Shoulder Elbow Surg 12:550–554, 2003), (2) ROM in FF greater than 90 degrees, (3) diagnosis of massive irreparable rotator cuff tears and cuff tear arthropathy, according to Hamada classification (Hamada in Clinical Orthopaedics Related Res 469:2450–2460, 2011), and (4) a minimum postoperative follow-up of 2 years. Clinical outcome measures, which were evaluated preoperatively, postoperative 6 month, 12 month, and annually thereafter, were range of motion, muscle strength, visual analogue scale (VAS), Constant-Murley score, and American Shoulder and Elbow Society (ASES) score as well as complications. Radiologic outcomes measures included preoperative Hamada grade and Goutallier grades.ResultsThe study was consisted of 10 male and 16 female patients, with an average follow-up of 5 years (range, 27 to 150 months). Preoperative range of motion showed FF 136′, ER 28′, internal rotation T9, and abduction 127′; in addition, ER lag was 32.6′ at side and 26.2′ at 90′ abduction. Mean Goutallier grades of infraspinatus and teres minor were 3.9 and 3.7, respectively. Postoperative outcomes revealed that active range of ER did not significantly increase over time but that the degree of ER lag at side and at 90′ abduction both showed statistically significant improvement at their final follow-up. Furthermore, the VAS and clinical scores, including ASES and Constant scores, all showed significant improvement at the final follow-up although muscle power including ER, belly press, lift off showed no improvement.ConclusionIn conclusion, the RTSA with isolated LDT in patients with irreparable massive rotator cuff tears with significant ER lag but without pseudoparalysis showed that ER lag was significantly improved postoperatively and remained stable through the mid-term follow-up without loss of IR. Regardless of prosthesis designs, LDT was able to restore functional ROM in ER and did not sacrifice its function in internal rotation in a combination with RSA.Level of EvidenceCase Series, Level IV.Keywords: Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, Latissimus dorsi transfer, External rotation lag

Introduction

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) is one of the most reliable surgical treatments in patients with irreparable massive rotator cuff tears and cuff tear arthropathy with functional deficits [1]. Because the earlier Grammont-style RTSA was less efficient in restoring external rotation (ER) compared to forward flexion (FF), latissimus dorsi transfer (LDT) was introduced as a RTSA-combined procedure to restore ER, as proposed in irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears [2, 3]. Later, progress in prosthetic designs in RTSA has shifted to an implant with a lateralized center of rotation, which can recruit posterior deltoid muscle more and can re-tension remaining rotator cuffs. Underlying biomechanics in a lateralized prosthesis can achieve better deltoid wrapping to increase not only FF and but also ER [2, 4, 5].

ER is crucial in activities of daily living, and its deficit is detrimental to functional capacity with upper extremities [3]. LDT is a well-known, effective method to restore active ER in irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, and a modified L’Episcopo technique, utilizing either isolated latissimus dorsi or concomitant transfer with teres major, is an appropriate addition to RTSA to manage ER deficits [6] Tendon transfer in a combination with RTSA is a type of surgery that requires considerations in its excursion, its position of fixation on humerus, restoration of the function. Latissimus dorsi is transferred posterior to humerus and inserted onto the lateral aspect of proximal humerus near the teres minor and inferior infraspinatus; as a result, latissimus dorsi is modified in its line of pull and consequently acts as an external rotator [7].

Previous literature on RTSA with LDT primarily focuses on patients with significant ER deficits with a lag associated with active FF deficits, who also exhibit magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed severe fatty degeneration in infraspinatus and teres minor muscle belly [10–8]. Boileau coined the term, combined loss of active elevation and ER (CLEER) deficit, for those who are unable to actively elevated with complete loss of ER, accompanied by a positive Hornblower’s sign [9]. There is also a subset of patients who are capable of functional active FF but have difficulties with ER, so-called isolated loss of ER, “ILER” deficit [2, 3, 9]. Patients with ILER deficit, who are candidates for RTSA, can be managed with several options associated with RTSA, including modifications in its degree of lateralization and various tendon transfer options, to restore the ER deficits.

Our aim of the study is to evaluate midterm clinical outcomes of RTSA with isolated LDT without teres major in patients who preoperatively exhibit ER lag greater than 30 degrees without pseudoparalysis. Our hypothesis was that RTSA with isolated LDT could effectively improve ER range of motion (ROM) and ER lag postoperatively in those with isolated ER deficit at the midterm follow-up.

Methods

The current study was approved by the institutional review board of our institution. Informed consents were waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. We performed a retrospective review of the patient data, who has undergone RTSA with isolated LDT from 2013 to 2023. The study inclusion criteria were (1) ER lag greater than 30 degrees with a positive ER lag sign and Goutallier grade greater than 3 in infrapinatus and teres minor muscle on preoperative MRI [10], (2) ROM in FF greater than 90 degrees, (3) diagnosis of massive irreparable rotator cuff tears and cuff tear arthropathy, according to Hamada classification, [11] and (4) a minimum postoperative follow-up of 2 years. Exclusion criteria include those who underwent RTSA for fracture, inflammatory arthritis, septic arthritis, and revision RTSA (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study inclusion and exclusion

Surgical techniques

All surgical operations were performed by a single senior orthopedic surgeon (J.C.Y.). All patients in the study received RTSA with isolated LDT. RTSA procedure was performed in a beach-chair with a lazy arm position using deltopectoral approach [12]. Preoperatively, humerus retroversion was measured using two-dimensional computed tomography (CT) assessment of humerus including elbow joint [13]. Humeral head was cut using alignment guide based on the pre-measured humeral retroversion angle. Subscapularis tendon was detached and tagged with three No.5 Ethibond strands (Ethicon; Somerville, NJ). After implantation, subscapularis tendon was re-attached to the lesser tuberosity insertion site by 3 non-absorbable trans-osseous sutures when present. Soft tissue biceps tenodesis was done when available.

Tendon graft harvest

The deltopectoral interval was developed distally to expose the pectoralis major tendon at its insertion along the lateral border of the inferior bicipital groove. The superior one-third to one-half of the pectoralis major was released at the musculotendinous junction, preserving a sufficient healthy cuff tissues for later repair. With the arm positioned in external rotation and the pectoralis major retracted medially and inferiorly, the underlying LD tendon was identified, with the teres major (TM) muscle located directly deep to it (Fig. 2). The LD tendon was carefully detached from its humeral insertion while preserving the TM, bluntly separated from the surrounding tissues, and tagged with two nonabsorbable sutures (No. 5 Ethibond) using a Krackow’s locking suture technique. The mobilized tendon was mobilized above teres major tendon and then transferred to the posterior aspect of the humerus.

The harvest of latissimus dorsi tendon during RTSA procedure. (blue triangle: lateral margin of latissimus dorsi tendon before harvest; yellow triangle: humerus shaft)

Tendon graft fixation

To maximize the correction of external rotation lag, the fixation site was determined intraoperatively based on restoration of passive external rotation and appropriate tendon tension. The arm was sequentially positioned in neutral to slight external rotation to assess the optimal balance between excursion and tension of the transferred tendon. Usually, this insertion for the harvested LD tendon overlaps with the insertion of infraspinatus lower portion and teres minor footprint, approximately 1 ~ 1.5 cm lateral in respect to the bicipital groove. Once the ideal fixation point was identified, two bone tunnels were created using a Kirschner wire at the selected site. The sutures were passed through the tunnels and securely tied, achieving stable fixation intended to enhance postoperative external rotation and minimize residual lag (Fig. 3).

The fixation of harvested latissimus dorsi tendon on proximal humerus. (blue triangle: fixated latissimus dorsi tendon on posterior aspect of greater tuberosity; green triangle: lesser tuberosity; yellow triangle: bicipital groove

Rehabilitation

All patients followed a standard postoperative rehabilitation program by maintaining to wear an abduction brace with 45’ external rotation pillow for 6 weeks postoperatively (Fig. 4). Continuous passive motion exercise and passive ROM started from postoperative 2nd week. At postoperative 6th week, after removal of the brace, the patient was instructed to initiate active ROM exercises. Muscle strengthening exercises were allowed at postoperative 8th week.

External rotation abduction pillow brace

Clinical and radiological evaluation

All patients were regularly followed up at the outpatient clinic at postoperative 2, 6, 12 months and annually thereafter. All radiological parameters were measured by two fellowship-trained shoulder surgeons (S.J.Y. and Y.S.K).

Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain and function, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeon (ASES) [14], and Constant score [15], preoperatively and on the regular basis postoperatively at each follow-up. The ROM of each patient was measured using a goniometer active FF, ER, and abduction (ABD) of the shoulder pre- and post-operatively at each follow-up by a shoulder specialized trainer (S.M.L). Internal rotation (IR) was measured by assessing the patients ability to reach the spinal vertebral spinous process with the tip of the thumb; T4–12 to 4–12 points, L1–5 to 13–17 points, and buttock to 18 point [16]. ER lag angle was measured at arm at side and at 90-degree abduction, using goniometer, and ER lag sign was simultaneously evaluated. Muscle power was using a hand-held dynamometer by a shoulder specialized trainer (S.M.L.). Furthermore, A sub-group analysis of postoperative outcomes between the medialized versus lateralized prosthesis used with LDT was performed to assess comparison in pain, function, and clinical scores.

Preoperative simple radiographs (anteroposterior view and axial view) of the affected shoulder were taken to evaluate cuff tear arthropathy, according to Hamada classification, and acromiohumeral distance (AHD) [11]. Postoperative simple radiographs at the final follow-up were obtained to assess any changes in Hamada grade and AHD as well as any complications, such as a loosening of prosthesis, acromial fracture, scapular notching, and heterotrophic ossification. A loosening of humeral prosthesis was defined as subsidence > 5 mm, change in alignment > 5′, or radiolucent line of 2 mm in more than 3 zones on simple radiographs [17]; in addition, the loosening of glenoid prosthesis was defined as any change in component position or evidence of screw and metal failure [18].

T2-weighted MRI (3.0-T scanner, Gyroscan Intera Ahieva; Philips Medical System) was used to evaluate the preoperative conditions of the rotator cuff tendons, especially degree of fatty degeneration of infraspinatus and teres minor. Goutallier grade for rotator cuff tendons was assessed on the preoperative MRI exam [10]. CT (Revolution Frontier Gen 3, GE Health Care System) was used to evaluate the preoperative osseous structure of glenohumeral joints, such as glenoid retroversion and inclination angle and humerus retroversion. In addition, humerus-retracted 3-dimensional CT reconstruction of scapula was performed to assess peri-glenoid osseous structure including bone glenoid bone defect and osteophytes.

Statistical analysis

Demographics data and outcome variables were analyzed using the student t test, Wilcoxon signed rank test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variable and chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. SPSS version 13.0 was used for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In addition, minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for statistically significant parameters were estimated as 0.5 standard deviation of paired changed scores or values. The proportion of patients achieving MCID was calculated by classifying patients as achievers when the improvement was equal or greater than the estimated thresholds.

Results

Among a total of 341 RTSA performed between 2013 and 2023, there were a total 122 medialized prosthesis and 229 lateralized prostheses. Out of the 341 RTSA patients, there were 30 patients who showed significant ER lag without pseudoparalysis with MRI-confirmed severe fatty degeneration of infraspinatus and teres minor. In the current study, the included 26 patients (10 male and 16 female), who underwent RSA concomitant with isolated LD transfer, were included with an average follow-up of 60.4 months (range, 27–150 months). Discussion

The primary finding of the current case series shows that the ER lag at both side and 90′ abduction in those who underwent RTSA with isolated LDT was significantly improved at the final follow-up, and the improvement maintained during a mean follow-up of 5 years. However, ROM in ER showed an improvement until the postoperative 2 years but was characterized by a gradual decrease from then onto the final follow-up while maintaining stable muscle power in ER throughout postoperatively. In addition, postoperative outcomes in pain and functional scores demonstrated significant improvement with a relatively low risk of complications.

With its success in restoring active ER in irreparable massive rotator cuff tears, LDT with or without teres minor concomitant with RTSA has shown conflicting outcomes in the restoration of ER function [6, 9]. There are various previous literature on mid-term effectiveness of LDT concomitant with RTSA in posterior deficient massive rotator cuff tears or arthropathies, which resulted in increased active ER range and improvement in ER lag, along with functional improvement [8, 19, 20]. On the other hand, a short-term outcome with an average follow-up of 25 months on medialized RTSA prosthesis with LDT failed to show improvement in active ER and functional outcomes [21]. A randomized-controlled study of RTSA with LDT in patients with CLEER deficits demonstrated no significant improvement in ROM or patient-reported outcomes, but there was a significantly better resolution of the Hornblower sign compared to the RTSA without LDT [22]. In the current series of RTSA with isolated LDT in ILER deficits, active ROM in FE and ABD showed statistically significant improvement, whereas active ER showed a deterioration in its ROM after early improvement until the postoperative second year. Even though active ER ROM was not significantly improved, postoperative ER muscle power persisted consistently without statistically significant changes throughout the midterm postoperative follow-up.

In a setting of RTSA with concomitant LDT, a decision between an isolated LDT versus a combined LD and teres major transfer (LDTMT) has not reached its consensus, but previous literature reported short- and mid-term outcomes in postoperative function and suggested different techniques for varying indications, which yielded in conflicting recommendations [8, 21]. In the current study, the study population, who exhibited ILER deficit without pseudoparalysis preoperatively, was treated with RTSA with concomitant LDT because the replaced glenohumeral joint is held to have better anterior stability and less resultant IR weakness especially in presence of subscapularis insufficiency when teres major is left intact [21]. A recent biomechanical study on the importance of teres minor in posterior cuff insufficiency concluded that isolated LDT was sufficient to restore and enhance ER and abduction strengths even in the absence of teres minor and that ER strength was effectively enhanced in higher angles of abduction especially when aligned with abduction [23]. A retrospective, comparative study between RTSA with isolated LDT versus RTSA with LDTMT demonstrated equally efficient postoperative functional restoration in ER motion and strengths without significant differences in irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears without osteoarthritis [24]. On the other hand, another mid-term study on massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, RTSA showed superior ROMs in active FF and ABD with isolated LDT than LDTMT, but the strength for ABD was greater with LDTMT than isolated LDT. In addition, EMG analysis on anterior and posterior muscle also showed that comparable muscle activity activation between the native anterior muscle and posterior transferred muscle in both techniques [25]. All in all, there is still no consensus on how to manage patients with isolated loss ER without pseudoparalysis in a setting of RTSA.

In the current study, the average follow-up was 60.4 months among the patients, whose age was 74.2 on average at the time of surgery. In a discussion of long-term clinical outcomes after RTSA, there is still a debate between functional deterioration around a mid-term follow-up versus no functional deterioration even in a long-term follow-up [29–26]. Even though some previous literatures emphasize a significant functional decline at postoperative 5 years [27, 28], recent systemic review of long-term outcome of RTSA longer than 10 years demonstrated that RTSA showed stable clinical and functional improvement without significant deterioration; however, despite the long duration of follow-up period, the weighted follow-loss was reported to be relatively high with 63%, ranging from 47 to 80% [26]. In our study, the patients who performed RTSA with isolated LDT showed constant improvement in range of motion, except ER; however, the postoperative resolution of ER lag after isolated LDT remained stable throughout the follow-up both at side and at 90-degree abduction (Fig. 5). Even though mid-term outcomes showed gradual clinical decline of ER ROM, post-transfer improvement in ER lag remained constant without significant loss of muscle power in ER.

Chronological change in range of motion of external rotation (ER) and ER lag in RTSA with isolated latissimus dorsi transfer

The average improvement in ER lag postoperatively may represent the tenodesis effect of the isolated LDT performed with RTSA, and such improvement continued to be stable throughout the follow-up without significant deterioration of ER muscle power. Isolated LDT was able to maintain postoperative IR muscle power with the remaining teres major tendon after the transfer, which indicates well preservation of IR function with teres major tendon alone with RSA. The isolated LDT was able to maintain its tenodesis effect throughout the mid-term follow-up without significant loss of ER muscle power or compensatory IR loss after LDT, and it showed constant improvement in ROM, pain, and clinical score without significant functional deteriorations at the mid-term follow-up.

In the current study, out of 26 RTSAs, there were 19 lateralized prostheses and 7 medialized prostheses for their RTSA with isolated LDT. The interesting finding was that the lateralized RTSA with the isolated LDT showed slightly better improvement in ER lag at an average follow-up of 5 years even though it failed to reach statistical significance (p = 0.09) and that ER muscle power in the lateralized prostheses group indicated significant improvement compared to the medialized prostheses group until postoperative 2nd year; however, the difference tends to be neutralized in its mid-term follow-up. In addition, the medialized prostheses group had statistically significant improvement of ER ROM compared to the lateralized prostheses group. ASES and Constant scores showed comparable outcomes at the midterm follow-up, and the lateralized prostheses group expressed less pain scores at the final follow-up than the medialized group (p = 0.05). Previous literature on comparison between lateralized versus non-lateralized RTSA prostheses demonstrated not only comparable short-term postoperative clinical outcomes in a randomized controlled trial but also similar postoperative outcomes at an average 10 years follow-ups except for a higher incidence of scapular notching in the medialized group, which showed no correlation to clinical outcomes [29, 30]. Furthermore, a recent study on comparison between lateralized RTSA without LDT versus medialized RTSA with LDT in irreparable massive rotator cuff tears with ILER deficit indicated that lateralized group was as effective not only in improving ER deficit postoperatively but also in preserving IR function as the medialized group [31].

While the status of teres minor integrity plays a critical role in ER lag, the ER gain after the LDT shows comparable ER gain after the RTSA, which continued to be maintained at the mid-term follow-up, in both medialized and lateralized prostheses sub-groups. However, the loss after the re-gain of the ER ROM was greater at the mid-term follow-up in the lateralized group compared to the medialized sub-group. Lateralized protheses provides greater deltoid wrapping over the prostheses which increases the efficiency of biomechanics within the RTSA prostheses; however, a previous study indicated that such deltoid wrapping fails to be effective in the posterior aspect, which may consequently result in so-called “deltoid fatigue” and be associated with advanced age as well as nonphysiological muscle fiber recruitment due to biomechanical changes of RTSA [32].

The current study has few limitations. First, the small sample size of the study had a challenge in providing sufficient power to provide significant statistical differences. Second, due to the retrospective nature of the study, the sub-group analysis between the medialized and lateralized prostheses did not have equal distribution of patients, which needs to be taken into consideration in the analysis. However, it was a noteworthy comparison to find out differences in functional outcomes after lateralized RTSA with isolated LDT form medialized prosthesis. Current evidence is still limited to conclude and generalize that LDT is more efficiently able to restore ER or related functional outcomes in lateralized prosthesis than medialized prosthesis, and an well-structured comparative study design would be highly recommended to draw such clinical evidence on such matter.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the RTSA with isolated LDT in patients with irreparable massive rotator cuff tears with significant ER lag but without pseudoparalysis showed that ER lag was significantly improved postoperatively and remained stable through the mid-term follow-up without loss of IR. Regardless of prosthesis designs, LDT was able to restore functional ROM in ER and did not sacrifice its function in internal rotation in a combination with RSA. Despite late clinical deterioration in ER ROM beyond postoperative 2nd year, the muscle power remained constant throughout the follow-up, suggesting the tenodesis effect of tendon transfer in a setting of RSA.
