Association of the atherogenic index of plasma and its integrative novel adiposity-based composites with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: novel adiposity-derived AIP indices provide modest incremental prognostic information
Authors: Sheng Wei, Jiajun Qiu, Yong Liu, Ning Xu, Zhonghua Sun, Sha Li, Changping He, Xiangping Xie, Yanlang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is recognized as a progressive pathophysiological continuum linking metabolic dysfunction, dysfunctional adiposity, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular injury. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) reflects lipid-related atherogenic burden, whereas novel adiposity indices, including body roundness index (BRI), weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), and a body shape index (ABSI), capture body-shape-related adiposity burden. However, the associations of AIP and AIP-based adiposity composite indices with mortality outcomes across the CKM spectrum remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the associations of AIP and integrative AIP-based composite indices, including AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI, with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals across CKM stages.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis using prospectively collected data from 22,587 US adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018. Following the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory, participants were classified into a hierarchical staging framework (Stages 0–4) to reflect the CKM disease continuum. Primary outcomes were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, identified through linkage to the National Death Index. Integrative AIP-based composite indices were constructed by directly multiplying AIP by each adiposity index, including BRI, WWI, and ABSI, yielding AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI, respectively. These indices were evaluated as integrated exposure variables reflecting the combined burden of atherogenic dyslipidemia and adiposity-related body shape. All analyses incorporated complex survey weights to ensure national representativeness. Survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 2 adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, lifestyle factors, blood pressure, and total cholesterol. Nonlinear associations were examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Incremental prognostic value was assessed using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsDuring follow-up, non-survivors exhibited significantly higher baseline integrative composites than survivors (P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), which adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, lifestyle factors, blood pressure, and total cholesterol, each standard deviation increase in the integrative composites was independently associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality: AIP-BRI (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.05–1.14), AIP-WWI (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.02–1.11), and AIP-ABSI (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.02–1.11). These point estimates were numerically slightly higher than that of stand-alone AIP (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01–1.10), but the differences should be interpreted cautiously. For cardiovascular mortality, the corresponding HRs were 1.17 (95% CI, 1.09–1.27) for AIP-BRI, 1.11 (95% CI, 1.03–1.21) for AIP-WWI, and 1.11 (95% CI, 1.02–1.20) for AIP-ABSI, compared with 1.10 (95% CI, 1.02–1.20) for AIP. RCS analyses revealed J-shaped associations, with a clinical risk threshold for AIP-BRI at 1.57. Subgroup analyses indicated that these associations were most evident in participants aged < 50 years. Adding BRI to AIP assessment yielded a statistically significant but modest NRI of 3.10% for cardiovascular mortality.ConclusionAcross the CKM spectrum, higher AIP-based adiposity composite indices, particularly AIP-BRI, were associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, especially among younger individuals. However, their incremental predictive improvement was modest, suggesting that these indices may serve as supplementary exploratory markers rather than stand-alone tools for CKM risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03277-y.Keywords: CKM syndrome, All-cause mortality, Cardiovascular mortality, Atherogenic index of plasma, Integrative metrics, Risk stratification, Prognostic implications

Introduction

The introduction of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents a paradigm shift by the American Heart Association (AHA) in understanding the systemic interactions between metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1]. Rather than a single entity, CKM encompasses a pathophysiological spectrum involving obesity, diabetes, impaired renal function, and cardiovascular abnormalities, with systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction driven by excess metabolic substrates as its core pathogenesis [2, 3]. Epidemiological data indicate that nearly one-third of U.S. adults are within the CKM spectrum, making it a leading cause of premature mortality and multiorgan failure globally [4]. Given the substantial heterogeneity of CKM, single-dimensional biomarkers often fail to capture the full complexity of its risk profile. Thus, identifying comprehensive assessment tools that integrate both lipid metabolic function and adipose tissue structural burden may improve CKM risk assessment [5].

Atherogenic dyslipidemia plays a central role in the metabolic basis of CKM, characterized by elevated triglycerides (TG) and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)—a pattern that often reflects insulin resistance induced by ectopic fat deposition [6]. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithmic transformation of the molar ratio of TG to HDL-C, has been validated as a superior surrogate for the size of small, dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL) particles [7]. Compared with traditional low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), sdLDL exhibits greater endothelial penetrability and oxidative susceptibility, serving as a key initiator of atherosclerotic plaque formation [8]. Multiple previous population studies have confirmed that elevated AIP independently predicts coronary artery calcification progression [9] and all-cause mortality risk in the general population [10, 11]. Notably, a recent study by Zheng et al. was the first to demonstrate a significant positive correlation between AIP levels and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in CKM patients [12].

Nevertheless, while AIP provides a “functional” readout of lipid metabolism, it fails to reflect the anatomical distribution of adipose tissue, which is a critical limitation because excessive visceral fat accumulation contributes to lipotoxicity and inflammatory activation during CKM progression [13]. Traditional body mass index (BMI) also has important limitations in CKM populations: it cannot distinguish between muscle mass and adipose tissue and may be influenced by kidney disease-related fluid retention. These limitations may contribute to the so-called “obesity paradox,” a phenomenon observed in some cardiovascular or renal disease populations in which higher BMI appears to be associated with neutral or even better outcomes, despite the adverse cardiometabolic effects of excess or dysfunctional adiposity [14, 15]. Therefore, BMI may mask truly high-risk individuals with central obesity or visceral adiposity.

To overcome these drawbacks, several novel anthropometric indices based on body geometry and waist-related measurements have been proposed in recent years. The body roundness index (BRI), derived from an elliptical model using waist circumference and height, is designed to estimate body roundness and central adiposity [16]. The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), calculated from waist circumference and body weight, reflects central obesity while reducing the influence of overall body weight [17]. A body shape index (ABSI), derived by standardizing waist circumference for BMI and height, captures abdominal body shape relatively independent of general body size [18]. Emerging evidence consistently shows that these indices can transcend the limitations of BMI, more accurately quantify central obesity and visceral fat burden, and exhibit superior performance to BMI in predicting cardiovascular mortality [19–21].

Although AIP (a metabolic marker) and novel adiposity indices (morphological markers) have each demonstrated potential in risk prediction, evidence remains limited regarding whether their integration provides additional prognostic information in the complex context of CKM syndrome. Recent studies have begun to construct metabolic-body shape combined indices (e.g., TyG-ABSI), which have shown potential incremental value in cardiovascular risk stratification [22–24]. Furthermore, recent mediation analysis suggests that AIP may partially mediate the effect of body shape indices on cardiovascular events, implying a possible upstream-downstream link in their pathological mechanisms [25]. We hypothesized that a multidimensional assessment strategy integrating AIP with novel adiposity indices (BRI, WWI, ABSI) may simultaneously capture lipid-related atherogenic burden and body-shape-related adiposity burden. Thus, using nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study aimed to evaluate the associations of AIP and AIP-based adiposity composite indices with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals across CKM stages.

Methods

Data sources and study population

This retrospective cohort analysis used prospectively collected data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a series of cross-sectional, nationally representative surveys conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the United States. NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to collect comprehensive demographic, clinical, laboratory, and nutritional data from non-institutionalized U.S. civilians. For the present analysis, we integrated data from the 1999–2018 NHANES cycles, which included a total of 101,316 participants.

Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) insufficient information for CKM stage classification or failure to meet the study eligibility criteria for the analytic CKM-staging cohort; (2) missing data on all-cause or cardiovascular mortality; (3) unavailable data on the AIP; and (4) missing anthropometric measurements (height, weight, or waist circumference) required for calculating novel adiposity indices. After applying these exclusion criteria, 76,093 non-eligible participants, 48 participants with missing mortality data, 1,636 participants with missing AIP values, and 952 participants with missing anthropometric data were excluded. A total of 22,587 participants were finally included in the analytical cohort. During the follow-up period, 3,343 participants died, including 858 deaths attributed to cardiovascular causes. Ethics statement

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and all participants provided written informed consent. This study is a secondary analysis of de-identified data from the publicly available NHANES database and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In line with the institutional policies of Zhengzhou Central Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University, this secondary analysis was deemed exempt from additional ethical review, and the requirement for renewed informed consent was waived.

Definition of CKM

CKM was defined and staged according to the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory, which conceptualizes CKM as a progressive continuum linking metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [26]. In the present study, participants with sufficient information for CKM classification were categorized into five hierarchical stages, from Stage 0 to Stage 4.

Stage 0 was defined as the absence of CKM risk factors, including optimal body mass index (BMI < 25 kg/m²), healthy waist circumference (WC < 102 cm for men and < 88 cm for women), normal glycaemia, normal blood pressure (BP), normal lipid profile, and preserved kidney function. Stage 0 was retained in the analysis as the lowest-risk reference stage within the CKM staging framework.

Stage 1 was defined as excess or dysfunctional adiposity, including overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) or abdominal obesity (WC ≥ 102 cm for men or ≥ 88 cm for women), and/or impaired glucose metabolism, defined as fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%.

Stage 2 included individuals with established metabolic risk factors or moderate-to-high-risk CKD. Metabolic risk factors included hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL), or metabolic syndrome (MS). According to the NCEP-ATP III criteria [27], MS was defined as the presence of at least three of the following: central obesity (WC ≥ 102 cm for men or ≥ 88 cm for women), elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL), low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; <40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women), elevated BP (≥ 140/90 mmHg or antihypertensive medication use), and elevated fasting glucose (≥ 100 mg/dL). CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated using the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation [28], or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g.

Stage 3 represented subclinical CVD or advanced CKD, identified by a 10-year Framingham Risk Score ≥ 21.5% for women or ≥ 21.6% for men [29], or an eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m². Stage 4 was defined as clinical CVD, including heart failure, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, or stroke.

Participants who could be assigned to Stage 0–4 were included in the analytic cohort. In contrast, “non-CKM” exclusions referred to individuals who could not be classified within the operational CKM staging framework because of insufficient information or failure to meet the study eligibility criteria, rather than eligible Stage 0 participants.

Definition of terminal events

All-cause and cardiovascular mortality were the primary outcomes of this study. Mortality status and underlying causes of death were obtained from the NHANES Public-Use Linked Mortality Files (PLMF). These files were linked to the National Death Index (NDI) records up to December 31, 2019, using a probabilistic matching algorithm [30]. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as death primarily attributed to cardiovascular diseases, which was classified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes: I00–I09 (rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart diseases), I11 (hypertensive heart disease), I13 (hypertensive heart and renal disease), and I20–I51 (ischemic heart disease, heart failure, and other cerebrovascular diseases).

Definition of AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI

AIP was calculated using the formula derived from previous epidemiological studies: AIP = log10 (triglycerides [mg/dL] / high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [mg/dL]) [7, 8]. Fasting triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were measured using standardized laboratory methods in the NHANES mobile examination centers (MECs), following NCHS quality control protocols. Three novel adiposity indices were included: BRI, WWI, and ABSI. Their calculations were based on standardized anthropometric measurements (height, weight, waist circumference) obtained during the NHANES MEC examinations:

BRI = 364.2-365.5×√(1-[ WC(cm)/2π]2 / [0.5 ×height(cm)]2) [16].

BRI = 364.2-365.5×√(1-[ WC(cm)/2π]2 / [0.5 ×height(cm)]2) [16].

WWI = WC [cm] /√height [cm] [17].

WWI = WC [cm] /√height [cm] [17].

ABSI = WC（cm）/（BMI 2/3 × height 1/2 [cm]） [18].

ABSI = WC（cm）/（BMI 2/3 × height 1/2 [cm]） [18].

To evaluate the integrated prognostic value of lipid-related atherogenic burden and adiposity-related body shape, we constructed three predefined AIP-based composite indices by directly multiplying AIP by each adiposity index. This construction was based on previously published approaches for modified AIP-related indices and TyG-related derived indices. The combined indices were calculated as follows:

AIP-BRI = AIP × BRI

AIP-BRI = AIP × BRI

AIP-WWI = AIP × WWI

AIP-WWI = AIP × WWI

AIP-ABSI = AIP × ABSI

AIP-ABSI = AIP × ABSI

These composite indices were analyzed as integrated exposure variables rather than formal statistical interaction terms. Therefore, they should be interpreted as pragmatic composite markers reflecting the combined burden of AIP and adiposity-related body shape.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on their established associations with mortality risk in CKM patients and previous NHANES-based studies. All covariates were derived from NHANES interview questionnaires and MEC examinations:

Demographic characteristics: Sex (male/female), age (continuous variable, years), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other races), educational level (less than high school, high school graduate, more than high school), poverty income ratio (PIR: low, PIR < 1.35; medium, 1.35 ≤ PIR < 3.0; high, PIR ≥ 3.0; a ratio of family income to the federal poverty threshold), and marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married). Lifestyle factors: Drinking status (never, former, mild, moderate, heavy), smoking status (never, former, current). Clinical measurements: Systolic blood pressure (SBP, continuous variable, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, continuous variable, mmHg), and total cholesterol (TC, continuous variable, mg/dL). All measurements were performed following standardized NHANES protocols by trained personnel.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Given the complex multistage probability sampling design of NHANES, survey-weighted analyses were implemented for all statistical tests to ensure the national representativeness of the study results. Weight calculations followed the official NHANES guidelines, integrating sampling weights, non-response weights, and post-stratification weights. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Baseline characteristics of the study participants were summarized in Table 1, stratified by all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality status. Continuous variables were presented as weighted mean ± weighted standard deviation, and between-group differences were tested using survey-weighted linear regression. Categorical variables were expressed as weighted counts (weighted percentages), with group comparisons conducted using survey-weighted chi-square tests. Given the large sample size, P values for baseline comparisons were interpreted descriptively, with emphasis placed on the distribution and magnitude of differences rather than statistical significance alone.

Weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to evaluate the associations between AIP-related parameters (AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI) and the risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with results detailed in Table 2. Three multivariable regression models were constructed to adjust for potential confounders sequentially: Crude Model (no covariates adjusted), Model I (adjusted for age and sex), and Model II (fully adjusted for sex, age, race/ethnicity, educational level, PIR, marital status, drinking status, smoking status, SBP, DBP, and TC). Because some covariates in Model II, such as blood pressure and total cholesterol, may partly overlap with cardiometabolic pathways related to AIP and adiposity, Model II was interpreted as a conservative adjustment model. To further assess model stability, collinearity analyses were performed for AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI together with all covariates included in Model II for both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality models. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated, and the results are shown in Supplementary Tables 1–2.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the predictive values of AIP-related parameters for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with results presented in Fig. 5; Table 3. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify discriminative performance, and differences in AUCs between AIP and the composite indices were compared using the DeLong test. For DeLong tests, a Bonferroni-corrected P value of 0.0167 was used as the significance threshold. Harrell’s C-index was further calculated to evaluate discrimination for survival outcomes. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) was computed to assess the incremental reclassification value of the composite indices compared with AIP alone. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the dynamic discriminative performance of AIP-related parameters during follow-up, with results shown in Fig. 6. Decision curve analysis was also conducted to explore the potential net benefit of these indices across different threshold probabilities, as shown in Fig. 5.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Table 1 presents the weighted baseline demographic, lifestyle, anthropometric, and laboratory characteristics of 22,587 participants stratified by all-cause and cardiovascular mortality status. Overall, participants who died during follow-up tended to be older and showed a less favorable cardiometabolic profile than those who survived. For all-cause mortality, deceased participants were older (65.79 vs. 44.85 years), had larger waist circumference (101.37 vs. 98.05 cm), higher triglyceride levels (1.74 vs. 1.47 mmol/L), higher total cholesterol levels (5.14 vs. 5.05 mmol/L), and higher systolic blood pressure (132.92 vs. 120.04 mmHg), but lower diastolic blood pressure (68.01 vs. 70.85 mmHg). They also had higher AIP values (0.41 vs. 0.32), higher stand-alone adiposity indices, including BRI (5.73 vs. 5.18), WWI (11.39 vs. 10.86), and ABSI (0.09 vs. 0.08), as well as higher AIP-based composite indices, including AIP-BRI (2.57 vs. 1.87), AIP-WWI (4.77 vs. 3.51), and AIP-ABSI (0.04 vs. 0.03). Similar patterns were observed when participants were stratified by cardiovascular mortality status. Compared with participants without cardiovascular death, those who died from cardiovascular causes were older (67.65 vs. 46.57 years), had larger waist circumference (104.16 vs. 98.25 cm), higher triglyceride levels (1.74 vs. 1.49 mmol/L), higher AIP values (0.42 vs. 0.32), and higher adiposity-related indices, including BRI (6.12 vs. 5.22), WWI (11.50 vs. 10.90), ABSI (0.09 vs. 0.08), AIP-BRI (2.78 vs. 1.92), AIP-WWI (4.91 vs. 3.61), and AIP-ABSI (0.04 vs. 0.03). Differences in body weight, total cholesterol, and HDL-C were relatively small between cardiovascular mortality groups. For categorical variables, participants who experienced all-cause or cardiovascular death had a higher proportion of men, individuals with lower educational attainment, widowed/divorced/separated participants, those with low or medium poverty income ratio, former drinkers, and former smokers.

Association analysis between AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI with all-cause death and cardiovascular death

Table 2 presents the associations between AIP and the composite indices (AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI) (per 1 standard deviation [SD] increase) and the risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, as evaluated by weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models with three adjustment strategies. For all-cause mortality, all indices were significantly associated with an increased risk of death in the Crude Model. After adjusting for age and sex (Model I) and further adjusting for all covariates in Model II (sex, age, race/ethnicity, educational level, PIR, marital status, drinking status, smoking status, SBP, DBP, and TC), the HRs were attenuated but remained statistically significant. In the fully adjusted Model II, the HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.10) for AIP, 1.09 (95% CI, 1.05–1.14) for AIP-BRI, 1.07 (95% CI, 1.02–1.11) for AIP-WWI, and 1.07 (95% CI, 1.02–1.11) for AIP-ABSI. For cardiovascular mortality, the corresponding fully adjusted HRs were 1.10 (95% CI, 1.02–1.20) for AIP, 1.17 (95% CI, 1.09–1.27) for AIP-BRI, 1.11 (95% CI, 1.03–1.21) for AIP-WWI, and 1.11 (95% CI, 1.02–1.20) for AIP-ABSI. These results indicate significant associations between AIP-related parameters and mortality outcomes, while direct superiority among indices should be interpreted cautiously because formal pairwise comparisons of HRs were not performed.

Dose-response relationship between AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI with all-cause death and cardiovascular death

Figure 2 depicts the dose‒response relationships between AIP-related parameters (AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, AIP-ABSI) and risks of all-cause and cardiovascular death, analyzed via restricted cubic spline models adjusted for all covariates in Model 2. For all-cause death, all parameters exhibited J-shaped associations (P for non-linearity < 0.05). Specifically, AIP (Ref point = 0.32), AIP-BRI (Ref point = 1.57), AIP-WWI (Ref point = 3.54), and AIP-ABSI (Ref point = 0.03) showed a negative association with all-cause death risk below their respective threshold points, while the risk increased significantly in a positive dose-dependent manner beyond these thresholds (P for overall < 0.001 for all).

For cardiovascular death, only AIP-BRI presented a non-linear (J-shaped) relationship (P for non-linearity < 0.05), with a threshold point at 1.57: the risk of cardiovascular death remained relatively stable below this point and increased beyond it (P for overall < 0.001). In contrast, AIP, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI showed linear positive associations with cardiovascular death risk (P for non-linearity > 0.05). These findings suggest that AIP-BRI may capture additional nonlinear risk information, although this threshold should be interpreted cautiously and requires further validation.

Subgroup analysis

Figures 3 and 4 depict subgroup analysis forest plots for the associations between AIP-related parameters (AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, AIP-ABSI) and all-cause or cardiovascular death, respectively, based on the fully adjusted Model II. Age was the only significant effect modifier: for all-cause death (Fig. 3), participants < 50 years showed notably higher hazard ratios (HRs) for AIP-BRI (1.35, 95% CI: 1.22–1.49), AIP-WWI (1.28, 95% CI: 1.14–1.43), and AIP-ABSI (1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.41) compared with those ≥ 50 years (all HRs close to 1, P-interaction < 0.001 for all). For cardiovascular death (Fig. 4), a similar pattern was observed, with participants < 50 years exhibiting significantly higher HRs for AIP-BRI (1.39, 95% CI: 1.12–1.74), AIP-WWI (1.46, 95% CI: 1.17–1.83), and AIP-ABSI (1.43, 95% CI: 1.15–1.79) than those ≥ 50 years (P-interaction < 0.001 for all). Subgroups of sex, ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, drinking status, and smoking status showed consistent positive associations across strata, with P-interaction > 0.05 for all, indicating no significant modification of the associations by these factors.

Comparative value of AIP and AIP-related parameters in predicting all-cause death and cardiovascular death

Figure 5 presents the ROC curves and decision curve analysis of AIP and AIP-related composite indices for predicting all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, while Table 3 summarizes their discrimination, reclassification, and optimal-threshold performance. Figure 6 further shows the time-dependent ROC analysis, illustrating the dynamic discriminative performance of AIP and the composite indices during follow-up.

For all-cause mortality, the AUC of AIP was 0.57 (95% CI, 0.56–0.58), with a Harrell’s C-index of 0.538. Compared with AIP alone, the AIP-based composite indices showed slightly higher AUCs and C-indices: AIP-BRI had an AUC of 0.58 (95% CI, 0.57–0.59) and a C-index of 0.553; AIP-WWI had an AUC of 0.58 (95% CI, 0.57–0.59) and a C-index of 0.553; and AIP-ABSI had an AUC of 0.59 (95% CI, 0.58–0.60) and a C-index of 0.554. DeLong tests indicated statistically higher AUCs for the composite indices than for AIP alone after Bonferroni correction. The NRI values were also statistically significant for all-cause mortality: 1.27% (95% CI, 0.16–2.38) for AIP-BRI, 1.07% (95% CI, 0.58–1.55) for AIP-WWI, and 1.36% (95% CI, 0.89–1.84) for AIP-ABSI. These findings suggest modest incremental discrimination and reclassification improvement beyond AIP alone.

For cardiovascular mortality, AIP had an AUC of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.56–0.59) and a C-index of 0.556. The corresponding AUCs were 0.59 (95% CI, 0.57–0.61) for AIP-BRI, 0.58 (95% CI, 0.57–0.60) for AIP-WWI, and 0.59 (95% CI, 0.57–0.60) for AIP-ABSI, with C-indices of 0.571, 0.570, and 0.571, respectively. DeLong tests showed statistically significant differences compared with AIP alone. For reclassification, AIP-BRI showed a statistically significant NRI of 3.10% (95% CI, 0.53–5.68), whereas the NRI values for AIP-WWI and AIP-ABSI were not statistically significant. The decision curve analysis and time-dependent ROC analysis provided additional evaluation of clinical usefulness and time-varying discrimination, respectively, and generally supported the modest but consistent incremental performance of the composite indices, particularly AIP-BRI, for cardiovascular mortality.

Overall, the composite indices showed statistically significant but modest improvements in discrimination and reclassification compared with AIP alone. Therefore, these indices should be interpreted as supplementary exploratory markers rather than stand-alone prediction tools.

Sensitivity analysis

Table 4 presents the results of sensitivity analyses evaluating the robustness of associations between AIP-related parameters and all-cause or cardiovascular death. For all-cause death, the hazard ratios (HRs) of AIP, AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI remained consistent across Sensitivity 1–4 (e.g., AIP: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11 in all four analyses), indicating stability after addressing missing data. In Sensitivity 5, HRs were slightly attenuated but still statistically significant (e.g., AIP: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.10), suggesting minimal impact of reverse causality. Sensitivity 6 confirmed the consistency of findings (e.g., AIP: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.11). For cardiovascular death, similar patterns were observed: HRs of all parameters were consistent across Sensitivity 1–4 (e.g., AIP-BRI: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.11–1.27 in all four analyses), remained significant in Sensitivity 5 (e.g., AIP-BRI: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.06–1.25), and were validated in Sensitivity 6 (e.g., AIP-BRI: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.02–1.28). These results collectively demonstrate the robustness of the observed associations to various analytical assumptions and data handling strategies.

Discussion

This study evaluated the associations of plasma AIP alone and AIP-based adiposity composite indices (AIP-BRI, AIP-WWI, and AIP-ABSI) with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in a nationally representative cohort of 22,587 US adults across the CKM spectrum. The composite indices, particularly AIP-BRI, were associated with mortality outcomes and showed modest incremental discrimination and reclassification compared with AIP alone. These findings remained generally robust through sensitivity analyses and were more evident in individuals younger than 50 years. However, given the modest AUC values and limited absolute improvements, the composite indices should be interpreted as supplementary exploratory markers rather than stand-alone clinical prediction tools.

As an emerging biomarker for lipid metabolism disorders, AIP has been increasingly utilized in prognostic assessments of CKM populations in previous studies [7, 31]. Zhou et al. reported that elevated AIP levels were closely associated with higher mortality risk in U.S. CKM patients, particularly late all-cause mortality and non-late/late cardiovascular disease mortality [12]. Another study in a Chinese population demonstrated that long-term monitoring of AIP changes facilitates the early identification of individuals with CKM stages 0–3 at high risk of CVD [32]. A key mechanism underlying these associations may be that high AIP reflects the coexistence of hypertriglyceridemia and hypoalphalipoproteinemia: elevated TG levels promote the formation of small, dense low-density lipoprotein (sd-LDL) particles [33], which are prone to oxidation and endocytosis by macrophages, leading to foam cell formation and accelerated atherosclerotic plaque progression—exerting systemic adverse effects on vascular, cardiac, and renal tissues and exacerbating CKM [34]. Additionally, reduced HDL-C impairs reverse cholesterol transport, decreasing cholesterol excretion from peripheral tissues to the liver and further exacerbating atherosclerosis [35].

However, CKM is a complex syndrome driven by multiple pathogenic factors, and prediction based on a single indicator may fail to comprehensively assess disease burden. In recent years, the combination of novel body shape indices with traditional biomarkers has been increasingly employed in CKM risk assessment. A study found that among individuals with CKM stages 0–3, TyG-ABSI exhibited stronger associations with mortality risk and higher predictive ability compared with other TyG-derived indices, with AUCs of 0.688–0.708 for all-cause mortality and 0.696–0.739 for cardiovascular mortality over 5–15 years [36]. Similarly, a Chinese study demonstrated that TyG-BRI, TyG-ABSI, TyG-WWI, and TyG-CVAI had significant and incremental predictive value for stroke risk in patients with CKM stages 0–3 [37]. Notably, no previous study has investigated the prognostic value of combining AIP with novel body shape indices for CKM-related mortality risk. Thus, one of the main findings of our study is that AIP-based composite indices provide modest incremental prognostic information beyond AIP alone. This finding supports the potential relevance of jointly considering abnormal lipid metabolism and body fat distribution in CKM prognosis.

Among the three composite indices, AIP-BRI showed the most consistent incremental performance, possibly because BRI is more highly correlated with visceral adipose tissue (VAT) distribution abnormalities compared with WWI and ABSI. VAT accumulation drives CKM progression and deterioration through multiple interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. A key feature of visceral obesity is a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, driven by adipocyte hypertrophy, macrophage infiltration, and elevated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α and IL-6), which can lead to metabolic dysfunction [38, 39]. This inflammatory environment promotes type 2 diabetes by facilitating the development of insulin resistance (IR) [40]. Inflammation, IR, and obesity interact to form a vicious cycle that further increases the likelihood of advanced CKM progression [41]. Indeed, previous studies have shown that BRI may add information beyond BMI in CKM-related frailty or advanced CKM assessment [42, 43]. Consistent with these prior findings, our study showed that AIP-BRI had numerically higher estimates than AIP-WWI and AIP-ABSI in several analyses. These results suggest that integrating visceral obesity indicators with atherosclerosis-related lipid biomarkers may be useful for broader CKM risk assessment. However, further validation is needed before incorporating this composite indicator into routine clinical risk assessment.

Our dose-response analysis revealed the complexity of risk patterns: all combined indices exhibited a J-shaped association with all-cause mortality, and AIP-BRI also showed a distinct J-shaped relationship with cardiovascular mortality (threshold at 1.57). This non-linear association suggests that at low indicator levels, the risk is relatively stable; once a specific threshold is exceeded, the risk may increase. This phenomenon may be related to adipose tissue decompensation. When adipose tissue expands excessively—particularly when VAT accumulation exceeds its storage capacity—it triggers adipocyte hypoxia, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and cell death, which in turn recruit macrophage infiltration, increase the expression of the inflammatory regulator NF-κB, and produce pro-inflammatory signals such as IL-6, exacerbating the state of chronic low-grade inflammation [44–46]. Meanwhile, spilled lipids deposit in non-adipose tissues such as the liver and skeletal muscle, exacerbating lipotoxic stress and maintaining a pro-inflammatory state, further worsening IR and metabolic disorders [47, 48]. IR and VAT accumulation together form a vicious cycle involving endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, accelerated atherogenesis, hypertension, and thrombosis—all of which increase the risk of CKM-related mortality. However, due to the lack of mechanistic biomarkers, the underlying biological pathways remain unclear and require further investigation. Additional studies are needed to determine whether these thresholds correspond to specific stages of metabolic dysfunction and to validate their relevance in other populations.

In subgroup analysis, we found that age was a significant effect modifier, with stronger associations between combined indices and mortality risk in participants younger than 50 years. These findings suggest that younger individuals with elevated combined indices may represent a subgroup requiring closer risk awareness. This may be attributed to the longer potential exposure of young CKM patients to metabolic damage, higher cumulative burden of subclinical disease, and the fact that they are often underrecognized in routine clinical assessments. Our findings are consistent with previous studies in patients with cardiovascular disease and coronary heart disease: one study found that patients with chronic coronary heart disease younger than 60 years had an increased risk of elevated AIP, carotid atherosclerosis, and increased carotid intima-media thickness [49]; Min et al. demonstrated that no significant difference in AIP was observed in elderly patients older than 60 years [50]; however, Kim et al. found no significant difference in the association between AIP and cardiovascular mortality across age groups [51], while Fu et al. reported a significant positive correlation between AIP and cardiovascular mortality or all-cause mortality in diabetic patients of all ages [52]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in study populations and study periods. Future large-sample population studies are needed to clarify whether there are subgroups that require heightened attention and their characteristics. It is also possible that young individuals with high AIP and adiposity indices represent a phenotype of accelerated metabolic aging, characterized by early VAT accumulation and dyslipidemia, which may not be fully captured by traditional risk factors such as BMI or LDL-C alone.

Although all combined indices showed modest AUC values (0.58–0.59)—a common finding in large-scale epidemiological studies using single biomarkers to predict long-term mortality—the NRI results, particularly the 3.10% NRI of AIP-BRI for cardiovascular mortality, suggest limited but measurable incremental reclassification value. The added Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent ROC analysis, and decision curve analysis further provided a broader evaluation of discrimination and potential clinical usefulness. Nevertheless, the absolute improvement remained modest, and these indices should be regarded as supplementary exploratory markers rather than replacements for established clinical risk models.

Placing our study in a broader literature context, our findings align with the current trend in cardiometabolic risk assessment, which increasingly emphasizes multidimensional markers beyond traditional risk factors. In recent years, several studies have explored integrated indicators that combine metabolic, lipid-related, and adiposity-related information. For example, the visceral adiposity index has been reported to predict adverse cardiovascular events more accurately than traditional abdominal obesity indices [53], and the combined use of BMI and ABSI has been suggested for mortality risk assessment in diabetic populations by capturing both general and central adiposity burden [54]. Similarly, in the field of lipid metabolism, AIP is considered to reflect the overall burden of atherogenic lipoproteins more comprehensively than single lipid components [55, 56]. Recent evidence also suggests that AIP and its modified indices, particularly AIP-WHtR, are associated with incident stroke in individuals with CKM stages 0–3, supporting the potential relevance of integrating lipid-related and adiposity-related measures in CKM risk assessment [57]. In this context, our study extends previous work by evaluating AIP-based composite indices that combine lipid-related atherogenic burden with body-shape-related adiposity burden. From a clinical perspective, these indices, particularly AIP-BRI, may help identify individuals with CKM who have both atherogenic dyslipidemia and unfavorable adiposity distribution. Because AIP, BRI, WWI, and ABSI can be calculated from routinely available lipid and anthropometric measurements, these composite indices may be practical and low-cost supplementary markers in clinical or public health settings. Their potential value may be particularly relevant for early risk recognition among younger individuals, in whom traditional age-driven risk assessment tools may underestimate long-term cardiometabolic risk. Nevertheless, these indices should be interpreted cautiously. Given the modest AUC values and limited incremental predictive improvement observed in this study, AIP-based composite indices should not be considered replacements for established risk prediction models or individualized clinical judgment. Rather, they may serve as exploratory supplementary tools to support broader CKM risk assessment. Further external validation and prospective studies are needed to determine whether incorporating these indices can improve clinical decision-making or patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational retrospective cohort analysis using prospectively collected NHANES data, this study cannot establish causal relationships. Second, although we adjusted for a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. In addition, some covariates included in the fully adjusted model, such as blood pressure and total cholesterol, may partly overlap with cardiometabolic pathways related to AIP, adiposity, and mortality; therefore, potential overadjustment bias should be considered when interpreting the fully adjusted results. Third, although collinearity analyses showed that all variance inflation factor values were < 5, suggesting no substantial multicollinearity among the adjusted variables, collinearity testing cannot fully address conceptual overlap among variables within related biological pathways. Fourth, the calculation of AIP and adiposity-related indices relied on a single baseline measurement during the NHANES survey, which may introduce measurement error and cannot capture long-term changes or trajectories in these indicators. Fifth, although sensitivity analyses excluding participants who died within the first two years of follow-up were conducted, reverse causality cannot be completely ruled out, particularly given the J-shaped associations observed in restricted cubic spline analyses and the complex metabolic characteristics of CKM populations. Sixth, although we supplemented ROC/AUC analysis with Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent ROC analysis, decision curve analysis, and NRI, the overall improvement in discrimination and reclassification remained modest; therefore, these indices should be interpreted as supplementary exploratory markers rather than stand-alone clinical prediction tools. Seventh, NHANES data primarily represent the U.S. population, and the generalizability of the conclusions needs to be further verified in populations of other ethnicities and regions.

The strengths of this study include a large nationally representative sample, long-term mortality follow-up, standardized data collection and quality control procedures, incorporation of the complex NHANES sampling design, and multiple sensitivity analyses supporting the robustness of the main findings.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort analysis using prospectively collected NHANES data, higher AIP-based adiposity composite indices, particularly AIP-BRI, were associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across the CKM spectrum, with more evident associations among participants aged < 50 years. However, the observed improvements in discrimination and reclassification were modest, suggesting that these composite indices should be considered supplementary exploratory markers rather than stand-alone clinical prediction tools. Further external validation and prospective studies are needed to determine whether these indices can improve risk prediction, clinical decision-making, or patient outcomes in CKM populations.
