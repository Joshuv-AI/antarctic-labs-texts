HKU25 clade MERS-related coronaviruses use ACE2 as a functional receptor
Authors: Chen Liu, Young-Jun Park, Cheng-Bao Ma, Risako Gen, Cameron Stewart, Yu-Cheng Sun, Xiao Yang, Mei-Yi Lin, Qing Xiong, Jun-Yu Si, Peng Liu, David Veesler, Huan Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) is an established receptor for Middle East respiratory syndrome-related coronaviruses (MERSr-CoVs), while recent studies have identified angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) usage in multiple merbecovirus clades. Yet, receptor usage of many genetically diverse bat MERSr-CoVs remains unclear. Here we show that broadly distributed HKU25 clade merbecoviruses use ACE2, rather than DPP4, as their receptor. Cryo-electron microscopy revealed that HsItaly2011 and VsCoV-a7 strains engage ACE2 similarly to HKU5 but with remodelled interfaces and distinct orthologue selectivity, suggesting a shared evolutionary origin of ACE2 recognition. EjCoV-3, a close relative of the DPP4-using BtCoV422, showed broad multi-species ACE2 tropism and preadaptation to human ACE2. Several ACE2 glycans and residues within or near the binding interface were identified as determinants of orthologue selectivity. These viruses remain sensitive to several broadly neutralizing antibodies and entry inhibitors, indicating potential countermeasures for future outbreaks. These findings highlight the versatility of ACE2 as a functional receptor for diverse coronaviruses.

Main

Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) is a highly pathogenic merbecovirus with a case fatality rate of 36% (ref. 1). According to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (https://ictv.global/taxonomy), the merbecovirus subgenus comprises four species: Betacoronavirus cameli (MERSr-CoV), Betacoronavirus erinacei (EriCoV), Betacoronavirus pipistrelli (HKU5) and Betacoronavirus tylonycteridis (HKU4). MERS-CoV belongs to B. cameli, along with viruses circulating in vespertilionid bats, yet a phylogenetic gap remains between these diverse merbecoviruses2,3. The closest known relative of MERS-CoV is NeoCoV, which was discovered in Neoromicia capensis (Cape serotine) in Africa, shares only 85.5% genome identity with MERS-CoV and exhibits substantial divergence in the spike (S) glycoprotein S1 subunit3,4.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) was identified as the entry receptor for MERS-CoV and was subsequently shown to mediate entry of HKU4-related viruses (B. tylonycteridis)5–9. Whereas HKU4 and a few bat MERSr-CoVs, such as BtCoV-422 (ref. 10), share similar receptor-binding domain (RBD) features with human/camel MERS-CoV, many other MERSr-CoVs exhibit highly divergent RBD sequences, implying the use of alternative receptors4. Indeed, the extraordinary RBD genetic diversity complicates efforts to predict the zoonotic risks of these viruses3,4. As a result, we previously classified merbecovirus RBDs into six phylogenetic clades, providing a framework for investigating receptor usage and informing vaccine design and pandemic preparedness efforts11.

We and others recently revealed that merbecoviruses from the NeoCoV, MOW15-22 and HKU5 clades, found across three continents, independently evolved the ability to use angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as a receptor through entirely distinct binding modes11–16. The receptor switch history of merbecovirus remains unclear, although recombination likely plays a crucial role3,13,17. Therefore, merbecovirus receptor usage can markedly deviate from the taxonomy of viral species based on the conservation of five open reading frame 1ab (ORF1ab) concatenated replicase domains18.

Although MERSr-CoV HKU25 has been proposed to use DPP4 for entry, the supporting evidence is weak and lacks structural confirmation19. The uncertainty of receptor usage extends to the HKU25 clade, including viruses discovered in Italy20, Switzerland21, China10,19,22,23 and Japan24, limiting our ability to predict their spillover potential. Based on phylogenetic relatedness to HKU5, we hypothesized that members of the HKU25 clade coronaviruses use ACE2, rather than DPP4, for entry. Screening an ACE2 orthologue library revealed that most, but probably not all, HKU25 clade coronaviruses can engage ACE2 as receptor. EjCoV-3, a strain closely related to the DPP4-using MERSr-CoV BtCoV-422 at the whole genome level, showed broad ACE2 orthologue tropism and suboptimal human ACE2 (hACE2) utilization. Cryo-electron microscopy of the ACE2-bound HsItaly2011 and VsCoV-a7 RBDs showed that these viruses engage ACE2 with a binding pose reminiscent of that observed for HKU511.

Results

Prediction of ACE2 utilization

To investigate receptor usage among diverse merbecoviruses, we retrieved 1,117 publicly available merbecovirus S sequences from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Then 152 non-redundant S sequences were selected (Supplementary Table 1) for multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction to identify representative strains (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Phylogenetic trees based on S (Extended Data Fig. 1b) and RBD (Fig. 1a) were constructed, with a focus on representative strains without confirmed receptors, including the bat coronavirus NsGHA201025, hedgehog coronaviruses (EriCoVs)26–28 and 15 non-redundant bat coronaviruses classified as HKU25 clade coronaviruses10,19,23. Analysis of amino acid sequences from five concatenated ORF1ab replicase domains confirmed that all HKU25 clade coronaviruses belong to MERSr-CoVs18,29 (Fig. 1b). Comparison of trees based on whole-genome and S/RBD amino acid sequences revealed phylogenetic incongruencies (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1b). For example, EjCoV-324, BtCoV-42210 and VmSL202021 clustered together, sharing 84.6–89.4% genome-wide nucleotide sequence identity, while exhibiting marked S/RBD divergence and clustering into two distinct RBD clades. RBD phylogeny reveals a close relationship between HKU5 and HKU25 clade coronaviruses, suggesting that HKU25 clade coronaviruses may also use ACE2 for entry11,15,16. Whereas HKU5 (lineage 1) was predominantly sampled in Pipistrellus abramus in Southeast China, HKU25 clade coronaviruses have been identified in a wide range of vespertilionid bat species across Eurasia. Pairwise amino acid sequence analysis showed that HKU25 clade S glycoproteins share 65–68% identity with MERS-CoV S, 67–70% with HKU4-1 S and 69–73% with HKU5-1 S, respectively. HKU25 clade RBDs share 62–73% identity with HKU5-1 but only 48–54% with HKU4-1 and 32–36% with NeoCoV and MOW15-22, suggesting distinct receptor recognition modes12,13 (Extended Data Fig. 1c). HKU25 clade RBDs harbour insertion–deletion (indel) patterns similar to HKU5 (for example, two indels at HKU5 S residues 513–522 and 543–552, respectively) but distinct from that of MERS-CoV, HKU4-1, BtCoV-422, NeoCoV, MOW15-22 and HKU31 (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Additional indels are present in PaGB01, SC2013, VsCoV-kj15 and other strains. It is worth noting that up to 15 out of 24 ACE2-interacting HKU5-19s residues are conserved in HKU25 clade RBDs, suggesting a potentially shared receptor usage mode (Fig. 1d). Simplot analysis comparing several viral genome sequences with EjCoV-3 reveals high similarity to BtCoV-422 and VmSL2020 except in the divergent S1 region. Accordingly, the EjCoV-3 RBD is more closely related to the ACE2-using HKU5-1 RBD than to the DPP4-utilizing BtCoV-422 RBD8, suggesting possible recombination events among ancestral strains (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Furthermore, comparing AlphaFold3-predicted and experimentally determined RBD structures indicate that HKU25 clade RBDs resemble HKU5-1 in terms of overall receptor binding motif (RBM) architecture, particularly in indel 2 and 3 located at the tip11, with PaGB01 harbouring a shortened indel 3 (Fig. 1e,f).

Multi-species ACE2 tropism

To investigate the receptor usage of HKU25 clade coronaviruses, we tested binding of RBD-human Immunoglobulin G fragment crystallizable (RBD-hFc) fusion proteins from 11 strains to ACE2 and DPP4 orthologues from P. auritus, P. kuhlii, V. murinus and P. abramus, which are reported host species of these coronaviruses. None of the RBD-hFc recombinant proteins from PaGD2016-Q249, PkItaly2011, VmSL2020, VmSL2021 and PaGB01 bound to ACE2 or DPP4 orthologues from their reported corresponding hosts. However, P. auritus ACE2, but not DPP4, unexpectedly bound RBD-hFc constructs from eight HKU25 clade strains (Extended Data Fig. 2).

To further explore multi-species ACE2 tropism, we assessed RBD-hFc binding and entry of Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) pseudovirus (PSV) harbouring one of 8 representative strains using a well-established ACE2 library comprising 113 orthologues from 59 bats and 54 non-bat mammalian species with validated expression14 (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). HKU25-305, EjCoV-3, SC2013, HsItaly2011 and VsCoV-a7 can efficiently bind to and use multiple ACE2 orthologues from Nycticeius humeralis, Murina feae, Eptesicus fuscus and P. auritus bat ACE2 orthologues, along with several artiodactyl and rodent ACE2s. Among the tested strains, EjCoV-3 exhibited a broad ACE2 tropism across mammalian orders and was the only HKU25 clade coronavirus capable of utilizing hACE2 for entry. Furthermore, flow cytometry confirmed that the RBD-hFc of HKU25 clade coronaviruses bound to ACE2 orthologues from E. fuscus and M. feae, but not to hACE2, human DPP4 (hDPP4), E. fuscus aminopeptidase N (APN) or E. fuscus DPP4 (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3c), contradicting a previous report suggesting HKU25 engagement of hDPP414,19. Using biolayer interferometry (BLI), we showed that the soluble dimeric Rattus norvegicus ACE2 bound to the immobilized HsItaly2011 RBD with apparent affinity (KD,app) of 130 nM and that the dimeric E. fuscus ACE2 ectodomain bound to the immobilized VsCoV-a7 and HKU25-462 RBDs with KD,app values of 1.765 µM and 532 nM, respectively (Fig. 2d–f). No binding of the dimeric hACE2 ectodomain to these RBDs was detected (Fig. 2g).

Both RBDs engage the ACE2 peptidase domain with poses similar to the ACE2-bound HKU5 RBD complex, with which they can be superimposed with root-mean-square distance (r.m.s.d.) values of 0.56 Å (VsCoV-a7) and 0.78 Å (HsItaly2011) over all Cα positions. The interfaces of VsCoV-a7 RBD–E. fuscus ACE2 and HsItaly2011 RBD–R. norvegicus ACE2 complexes bury an average surface of 870 Å2 and 1,016 Å2, respectively, compared with 950 Å2 for HKU5 RBD–P. abramus ACE2 complex11.

E. fuscus ACE2 (E.fusACE2) and R. norvegicus ACE2 (R.norACE2) respectively interact with the VsCoV-a7 RBD and the HsItaly2011 RBD through shared interactions and contacts specific to each complex involving the molecular determinants of receptor species tropism previously identified for HKU511. For instance, P324E.fusACE2, P325R.norACE2 and P324P.abrACE2 insert in a comparable crevice at the surface of each RBM through contacts with Y429VsCoV-a7, Y461HsItaly2011 and Y464HKU5-19s. Conversely, neighbouring interactions are profoundly remodelled relative to HKU5, as exemplified by N328E.fusACE2 harbouring an N-linked oligosaccharide, which is directly interacting with Y425VsCoV-a7, or T329R.norACE2 which is hydrogen-bonded to the equivalent Y457HsItaly2011 residue through a water molecule (Fig. 3b). The N353E.fusACE2 side chain is hydrogen-bonded to the T474VsCoV-a7 backbone carbonyl (similar to N353PabrACE2 and T510HKU5), whereas G354R.norACE2 cannot form similar interactions with the HsItaly2011 RBD. The VsCoV-a7 and HsItaly2011 RBDs respectively accommodate an ACE2 glycan at position N90E.fusACE2 and N90R.norACE2 through F482VsCoV-a7 and Y514HsItaly2011, setting them apart from the P. abramus ACE2–HKU5 interface, which lacks this glycan due to the presence of D90P.abrACE2 (Fig. 3b).

To functionally assess these interactions, we examined HsItaly2011 RBD-hFc binding to R. norvegicus ACE2 mutants with substitutions at key positions (Fig. 3c). Most substitutions reduced binding, except the N90AR.norACE2 glycan knock-out and Q322NR.norACE2 glycan knock-in mutations, suggesting a limited role of these glycans (Fig. 3c). Consistent with the structural data, several alanine/glycine substitutions or charge reversals in the HsItaly2011 RBM dampened RBD-hFc binding and PSV entry, except for Y514A, D517K and T544A mutations, probably due to remodelling of contacts (Fig. 3d–f).

Critical residues and glycans for ACE2 tropism determination

To define determinants of host species tropism for HKU25 clade coronaviruses, we engineered chimeric ACE2 constructs by swapping regions between functional orthologues (P. auritus, Mustela erminea, E. fuscus, M. feae) and the generally non-functional hACE2. Structural analysis revealed that direct virus-interacting residues cluster within positions 1–100 and 301–400. We therefore generated chimeras by swapping three consecutive regions: residues 1–100, 101–300 and 301–400. Swapping residues 101–300 had little effect relative to wild-type (WT) controls, whereas substitutions in residues 1–100 or 301–400 altered receptor functionality. For example, chimeras containing hACE2 residues 1–100 lost RBD-binding ability, indicating the crucial role of this region (Fig. 4a). To identify determinants restricting hACE2 functionality, we replaced hACE2 residues 1–100 with sequences from P. auritus or M. erminea ACE2, which restored binding to several HKU25 clade RBDs, whereas swapping residues 301–400 caused milder phenotypic changes (Fig. 4a). Within residues 301–400, the Q325P mutation enabled detectable HKU25-462 RBD binding, further enhanced by additional E329R/N330D substitutions, more closely matching the P. abramus ACE2 residues (HKU5 receptor; Extended Data Fig. 6a). Fine mapping of residues 1–100 between hACE2 and M. feae or E. fuscus ACE2 revealed that the K26T/D30E hACE2 double mutation supported detectable HKU25-462 RBD binding (Extended Data Figs. 6b and 7a,b), whereas other mutation combinations permitted PSV entry of several HKU25 clade coronaviruses (Extended Data Fig. 7c–f). Previous studies showed that N-linked glycans near the RBD–ACE2 interface can modulate receptor recognition through binding enhancement or steric restriction11,12,13. To assess their functional impact on HKU25 clade receptor utilization, we mutated each of the four glycosylation sites (hACE2 numbering positions 90, 322, 329 and 387) near or within the interaction interface of different ACE2 orthologues (Fig. 4b,c). RBD binding assays showed that removing glycans at these sites generally enhanced binding, whereas glycan knock-in mutations abolished binding in several cases (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, the P. abramus ACE2 S388P glycan knockout and the hACE2 N322S/Q325A/E329N triple mutations promoted detectable HKU25-462/EjCoV-3 and SC2013 RBD binding, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 7h,i). Together, these results suggest that glycans primarily act as host-tropism barriers for HKU25 clade coronaviruses, contrasting with the glycan-dependent binding observed for the NeoCoV RBD–Pipistrellus pipistrellus ACE2 interaction13.

Characterization of S-mediated entry of HKU25 clade coronaviruses

A notable difference between HKU25-related coronaviruses and HKU5 or MERS-CoV is the absence of a polybasic (furin) cleavage site at the S1/S2 junction, a feature critical for proteolytic processing during viral biogenesis31 (Fig. 5a). Accordingly, pseudotyped particles carrying HKU25 clade S glycoproteins were uncleaved when produced in human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK293T) besides minimal processing for EjCoV-3 S (Fig. 5b). HKU25 clade S glycoproteins promoted cell–cell fusion in Caco-2 cells stably expressing functional ACE2 orthologues from several mammalian species, in a trypsin-dependent manner, highlighting a requirement for exogenous protease priming in these conditions (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Only EjCoV-3 S promoted fusion in Caco-2-hACE2 cells albeit weakly. Additional coexpression of human transmembrane serine protease 2 (hTMPRSS2) did not substantially enhance fusion for HKU25-305 or EjCoV-3 S, although a modest effect was observed with E. fuscus ACE2, either with or without trypsin treatment (Fig. 5c). Source data

To evaluate S-mediated viral propagation, we used a replication-competent VSV-CoV-S (rcVSV-S) system in Caco-2 cells stably expressing human or bat ACE2 orthologues. Seven rcVSV-HKU25r-S viruses were successfully rescued and efficiently amplified in Caco-2 cells stably expressing E. fuscus, M. feae or R. norvegicus ACE2 in the presence of N-tosyl-ʟ-phenylalanyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin (Fig. 5d). Only EjCoV-3 exhibited detectable (but weak) propagation in Caco-2 cells endogenously expressing or overexpressing hACE2 at 72 h after infection, consistent with single-round VSV-S PSV entry assays (Fig. 2a,b).

To delineate entry pathways and therapeutic targets, we tested PSV entry and rcVSV-HKU25r-S amplification in the presence of inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies targeting distinct steps of coronavirus entry. rcVSV-HKU25r-S viruses (Fig. 5e) and HKU25 clade VSV PSVs (Extended Data Fig. 8d) were most sensitive to the TMPRSS2 inhibitor Camostat in ACE2-expressing Caco-2 cells and to the cathepsin B/cathepsin L cysteine protease inhibitor E64d only in ACE2-expressing HEK293T cells32 (Extended Data Fig. 8e), although the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations varied across strains. This suggests that HKU25 clade S can be activated either by TMPRSS2 at the plasma membrane or cathepsin B/L in the endolysosome, with the dominant pathway differing in different cell types. The human coronavirus OC43 S heptad-repeat region 2 (HR2)-derived EK1C4 lipopeptide, a broad fusion inhibitor33, potently inhibited rcVSV-HKU25r-S propagation (Fig. 5e) and PSV entry (Extended Data Fig. 8f) with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations below 5 nM. Moreover, the broadly neutralizing antibodiestargeting S2 stem helix (S2P6) (ref. 34) or the conserved S2’ cleavage site (76E1) (ref. 35) hindered propagation of the tested rcVSV-HKU25r-S viruses (Fig. 5f). Finally, the humanized hACE2-blocking monoclonal antibody that potently inhibited SARS-CoV-2 entry, namely, h11B11 (ref. 36), neutralized rcVSVs bearing S glycoproteins from HKU25-305, EjCoV-3 and SC2013, but not MERS-CoV, in Caco-2 cells expressing hDPP4 or the indicated hACE2 mutants (Fig. 5g).

Discussion

DPP4 has been recognized as a canonical receptor for members of the Merbecovirus subgenus since its identification as the MERS-CoV entry receptor. While this likely applies to the entire HKU4 clade, only a few MERS-related coronaviruses have been experimentally confirmed to use DPP46,8,9,19,37. The inability of most merbecoviruses to engage DPP4 has hindered the development of robust infection models, limiting our understanding of their entry mechanisms and our ability to develop and evaluate the efficacy of antiviral therapeutics and vaccines.

Recent studies showed that ACE2 recognition has independently emerged in multiple merbecovirus clades with distinct geographic distributions11,12,13. Our findings extend this paradigm by identifying a subset of HKU25 clade coronaviruses as group 3 ACE2-using merbecoviruses, challenging the previously proposed DPP4 dependence of this clade19 and supporting the prevalence of ACE2 usage among merbecoviruses. Moreover, the overall similar but divergent ACE2 binding mode used by HKU5 and HKU25 clades concurs with their close RBD phylogenetic relationships4,11. The recognition of largely overlapping surfaces for merbecoviruses (HKU5/HKU25), sarbecoviruses (SARS-CoV-1/SARS-CoV-2) and setracoviruses (NL63) with entirely distinct RBM architectures suggests fulfillment of specific geometric constraints required for efficient viral entry38,39.

Some merbecoviruses may use receptors other than ACE2 or DPP4. For instance, EriCoVs, NsGHA2010 and several HKU25 clade merbecoviruses exhibit distinct RBM indels and are not known to use any tested ACE2 or DPP4 orthologues (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Notably, recent advances in the field indicate that hedgehog APN is a functional receptor for EriCoVs40,41. While three HKU25 clade coronaviruses (VsCoV-1, VmSL2020 and VmSL2021) were not confirmed to use ACE2 in this study, their reliance on untested ACE2 orthologues from V. murinus and V. sinensis cannot be excluded. Similarly, polymorphisms in the P. auritus ACE2 allele may have influenced receptor functionality for PaGB0142,43, as previously observed for bat ACE2 in sarbecovirus entry42. We also cannot rule out that ACE2-using HKU25 coronaviruses can exploit alternative receptors under certain conditions, as suggested by a recent study44. Definitive confirmation of host ACE2/DPP4 orthologue usage, or identification of additional receptors, will be critical for a comprehensive understanding of receptor utilization and species tropism for these merbecoviruses.

The evolutionary history of receptor usage shift among merbecoviruses remains unclear. Accumulating evidence suggests that S recombination, with breakpoints between N-terminal domain (or domain A) and S2 subunit, plays a key role in receptor switching3,45. For instance, DPP4-using MERS-CoV and BtCoV-422 are proposed to have acquired their RBDs through recombination of HKU4 clade viruses with NeoCoV-like and EjCoV-3-like ACE2-using ancestors, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 9)3. This evolutionary model is supported by shared sequence signatures of indels 2 and 3 among DPP4-using viruses (for example, MERS-CoV, HKU4, BtCoV-422; Fig. 1d,e). While RBD recombination facilitates receptor switching, this mechanism alone does not establish novel receptor recognition modalities. Instead, adaptive sequence changes, such as RBM indels and antigenic drift, appear to remodel interactions and drive the emergence of new receptor binding modes46. Although ACE2 utilization has emerged independently across diverse bat species, the evolutionary origin of the conserved DPP4-binding mode remains enigmatic. The phylogenetic gap between MERS-CoV and currently known HKU4 strains, alongside the absence of viruses highly similar to MERS-CoV in bat virome surveys47–49, implies the existence of unsampled merbecovirus reservoirs in uncharacterized ecological niches that may have donated the DPP4-binding RBD sequences to MERS-CoV, as exemplified by the recently described mBSS strain in Brazil50.

The HKU25 clade comprises genetically diverse MERSr-CoVs circulating among vespertilionid bats across Eurasia. Host migration and cross-species transmission appear to drive diversification and recurrent recombination. Despite being phylogenetically classified within MERSr-CoVs based on ORF1ab sequences, HKU25 clade coronaviruses resemble HKU5 in terms of ACE2 binding mode and orthologue tropism. As for other merbecovirus clades, critical interface residues and glycans govern their ACE2 specificity and zoonotic potential12,13,14. For example, the P. abramus ACE2-specific N386-glycan, which is accommodated upon HKU5 RBD binding, appears to sterically prevent HKU25 recognition (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7h). Unlike HKU5, which has spilled over into minks and evolved hACE2-compatible lineages15,51, currently sampled HKU25 clade coronaviruses remain bat-restricted and poorly adapted to hACE2. The absence of furin cleavage sites in HKU25 clade S proteins may further constrain human adaptation. Nevertheless, the broad ACE2 orthologue tropism and weak hACE2 utilization of EjCoV-3 reveal pre-adaptive potential for host switching and underscore the need for close monitoring. Lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic and proactive investigations of transmissibility, pathogenicity and therapeutic vulnerabilities of ACE2-using merbecoviruses should be prioritized to strengthen preparedness for potential future outbreaks52,53.

Methods

Cell lines

HEK293T (CRL-3216 or CRL-11268), Caco-2 (HTB-37), BHK-21 (CCL-10) and I1-Hybridoma (CRL-2700) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. All the above cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Monad) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The I1-Hybridoma cell line, which produces a neutralizing antibody targeting the VSV glycoprotein (VSV-G), was maintained in minimum essential medium with Earles’s balanced salts, 2.0 mM of L-glutamine (Gibico) and 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and routinely passaged every 2–3 days. HEK293T or Caco-2 cell lines overexpressing various receptors were generated using lentivirus transduction followed by the puromycin (1 μg ml−1) or Zeocin (100 μg ml−1) selection. Expi293F (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14527) was used for protein production.

Construct design

Plasmids for expressing WT or mutated mammalian ACE2 orthologues or ACE2 chimera were constructed by inserting human codon-optimized coding sequences into the lentiviral transfer vector (pLVX-EF1a-Puro, Genewiz) with C-terminally fused 3× FLAG tags (DYKDHD-G-DYKDHD-I-DYKDDDDK) for bat ACE2 and single FLAG tags (DYKDDDDK) for non-bat mammalian ACE2 orthologues14,54. Plasmids for expressing human or bat DPP4, or bat APN were generated similarly to ACE2 orthologues with C-terminally fused 3× FLAG tags. Plasmid for human TMPRSS2 expression was constructed to pLVX-EF1a-Zeocin (clone based on pLVX-EF1a-Puro with the resistance gene replaced with Zeocin) with C-terminally fused ALFA-tag (PSRLEEELRRRLTE)55. For S-incorporated VSV PSV production, human codon-optimized S sequences from MERS-CoV (YP_009047204.1), HKU5-1 (YP_001039962), SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1 (UFO69279.1), HKU1 (YP_173238.1), HKU25-462 (ASL68953.1), HKU25-305 (ASL68941.1), EjCoV-3 (BDD37132.1), SC2013 (AHY61337.1), VsCoV-kj15 (BDI08820.1), VsCoV-a7 (BDI08829.1), HsItaly2011 (AUM60014.1), VmSL2020 (USF97409.1), VsCoV-1 (BBJ36008.1) and PaGB01 (WDQ26963.1) were commercially synthesized and subcloned into the mammalian expression vector that uses the CAG promoter (pCAGGS) with 13–15 C-terminal residues replaced by a hemagglutinin (HA) tag (YPYDVPDYA) to facilitate S incorporation56. For the expression of recombinant CoVs RBD-hFc fusion proteins, plasmids were constructed by inserting commercially synthesized RBD coding sequences from HKU5-19s (AGP04932.1) (residues 385–586), HKU25-462 (residues 385–587), HKU25-892 (AWH65943.1) (residues 385–587), HKU25-305 (residues 384–586), PaGD2016-Q249 (WCC63256.1) (residues 383–584), EjCoV-3 (residues 383–585), SC2013 (residues 382–588), VsCoV-kj15 (residues 388–594), HsItaly2011 (residues 383–586), PkItaly2011 (AUM60024.1) (residues 383–586), VsCoV-a7 (residues 385–587), VmSL2020 (residues 389–590), VmSL2021 (USF97418.1) (residues 387–588), VsCoV-1 (residues 382–583) and PaGB01 (residues 377–572) into the pCAGGS vector containing an N-terminal CD5 secretion signal peptide (MPMGSLQPLATLYLLGMLVASVL) and C-terminal hFc-twin-strep-3× FLAG tags (WSHPQFEKGGGSGGGSGGSAWSHPQFEK-GGGRSDYKDHDGDYKDHDIDYKDDDDK) for purification and detection. DNA fragments for cloning chimera or mutants were generated by overlap extension PCR or gene synthesis and verified by commercial DNA sequencing.

For BLI and cryo-EM analysis, the native ACE2 ectodomain dimers encoding residues of R. norvegicus (1–741) and E. fuscus (1–747) were subcloned into a mammalian expression vector with the human cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter (pcDNA3.1(+)) by Genscript with a C-terminal Avi tag, GGS flexible linker and octa-histidine tag. The native hACE2 ectodomain dimer encoding residues 19–740 were synthesized and subcloned into a mammalian expression vector with the human cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter (pCMV) by Genscript with N-terminal signal peptide sequence (MGILPSPGMPALLSLVSLLSVLLMGCVAQ) and C-terminal octa-histidine tag, flexible linker (GGSSG) and Avi tag. The RBD encoding residues of HsItaly2011 (388–589) and VsCoV-a7 (356–556) were subcloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) with N-terminal signal peptide (MGILPSPGMPALLSLVSLLSVLLMGCVAETGT) and C-terminal thrombin cleavage sequence, 8 flexible GS linker sequence (GSSSGGSG) and an Avi tag followed by octa-histidine tag. The RBD residues of HKU25-462 (386–590) were synthesized and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid by Genscript with N-terminal signal peptide (MGILPSPGMPALLSLVSLLSVLLMGCVAETGT) and C-terminal thrombin cleavage sequence, an 8 flexible GS linker sequence (GSSSGGSG) and an Avi tag followed by an octa-histidine tag.

Recombinant protein production

For producing proteins or antibodies for biochemical or neutralization assays, corresponding plasmids expressing proteins were transfected using GeneTwin reagent (Biomed, TG101-01) in HEK293T cells or Expi293F cells. At 4–6 h post-transfection (hpt), culture medium was replenished with the SMM 293-TII Expression Medium (Sino Biological, M293TII). Protein-containing supernatant was collected every 3 days for 2–3 batches. Fc-tagged proteins (antibodies or recombinant RBD-hFc) were purified using Pierce Protein A/G Plus Agarose (Thermo Scientific, 20424). In general, proteins were enriched by agarose, washed with wash buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA), eluted using glycine buffer (100 mM in H2O, pH3.0) and immediately neutralized with 1/10 volume of 1 M Tris–HCl, pH8.0 (15568025, Thermo Scientific). Proteins with twin-strep tag were purified using Strep-Tactin XT 4Flow high-capacity resin (IBA, 2-5030-002), washed with wash buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) and then eluted with buffer BXT (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM biotin). Purified proteins were concentrated using ultrafiltration tubes, buffer-changed to PBS and stored at −80 °C. Concentrations were determined by the Omni-Easy Instant BCA Protein Assay Kit (Epizyme, ZJ102).

For recombinant glycoprotein production for BLI and cryo-EM analysis, each construct was expressed in Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific), cultured at 37 °C with constant rotation at 130 revolutions per minute (r.p.m.) in a humidified incubator with 80% relative humidity and 8% CO2. DNA was transfected using Expifectamine 293 Reagent following the protocol outlined by the manufacturer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and grown for 4 days before collection. Cell culture supernatants were clarified by centrifugation and collected using either HisTrap HP Ni Sepharose Columns (Cytiva) or Ni Sepharose excel resin (Cytiva). The resin was washed with 10–50 column volumes of 25 mM Tris–HCl 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole pH8.0, followed by a 15 column-volume elution using 25 mM Tris–HCl 150 mM NaCl, 400 mM imidazole pH8.0. Afterwards, the proteins were buffer exchanged into 25 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl pH8.0 using 10 kDa or 100 kDa Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (Millipore) for RBDs or ACE2s, respectively. A portion of proteins was also set aside and biotinylated using a biotin ligase (BirA) reaction kit (Avidity). These biotinylated RBDs were adjusted to a final concentration of 40 μM with all the provided reagents, and the reaction was carried out at room temperature for 30 min, followed by 10 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, proteins were each purified by gel filtration using a Superose-6 Increase 10/300 column (ACE2s) or Superdex-200 10/300 column (RBDs) (Cytiva) equilibrated in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl pH8.0. The main peak was collected, flash-frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use.

RBD-hFc live-cell binding assay

HEK293T cells transiently expressing the indicated receptors were incubated with 8 μg ml−1 RBD-hFc proteins (diluted in DMEM) for 30 min at 37 °C (36 hpt). Subsequently, cells were washed once with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) and incubated with 2 μg ml−1 of Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; A11013, 1:1,000 diluted in HBSS/1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 1 h at 37 °C. After additional washing with HBSS, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000 dilution in HBSS) for 30 min at 37 °C. The images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (MI52-N). Relative fluorescence units were quantified using a Varioskan LUX Multi-well Luminometer (Thermo Scientific).

PSV production and entry assays

Single-round VSV-ΔG PSVs carrying trans-complemented S glycoproteins from various coronaviruses were produced following a modified protocol, as previously described57. Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding S glycoproteins. At 24 hpt, cells were transduced with VSV-G trans-complemented VSV-ΔG encoding green fluorescence protein (GFP) and firefly luciferase (fLuc)(VSV-ΔG-fLuc-GFP, constructed and produced in-house) at 1.5 × 106 TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose), diluted in DMEM with 8 μg ml−1 polybrene and incubated at 37 °C for 4–6 h. After washing once with DMEM, the culture medium was replaced with SMM 293-TII Expression Medium (Sino Biological, M293TII), along with the presence of the I1 neutralizing antibody targeting the VSV-G to eliminate background entry signal from the residual VSV-G-harbouring PSV. Supernatants containing S-incorporated VSV PSV were collected 24 h later, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min (4 °C), aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. The TCID50 of the PSV was calculated using the Reed–Muench method58,59.

For single-round VSV PSV entry assays, HEK293T or Caco-2 cells transiently/stably expressing different receptors (3 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates) were incubated with PSV (2 × 105 TCID50 per 100 μl). PSVs produced in serum-free SMM 293-TII Expression Medium were typically pretreated with 100 μg ml−1 TPCK–trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, T8802) for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by 10% FBS in the culture medium to inactivate the protease activity. The I1-neutralizing antibody was added to the trypsin-treated PSVs again to reduce the background before use. Luciferase activity (relative light units, RLU) was measured at 18 h post-infection (hpi) using the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay Kit (Promega, E2620) and detected with a GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega) or Varioskan LUX Multi-well Luminometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

To examine the S glycoprotein packaging and cleavage efficiency, the S-incorporated VSV PSVs were concentrated using a 30% sucrose cushion (30% sucrose, 15 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA) at 20,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer, vortexed and boiled (95 °C, 10 min), followed by western blot detecting the S glycoproteins by C-terminally fused HA tags (anti-HA-tag mAb, MBL, M180-3; 1:10,000 diluted in PBST containing 1% milk) and with the VSV-M (anti-VSV-M antibody, kerafast, EB0011; 1:10,000 diluted in PBST containing 1% milk) serving as a loading control.

For EK1C4 inhibition assays, single-round PSVs pretreated with 100 μg ml−1 TPCK–trypsin were incubated with threefold serial dilutions of 200 nM EK1C4 in DMEM for 1 h at 25 °C. This mixture was then combined with trypsin-detached ACE2-expressing Caco-2 cells in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, in 96-well plates. For experiments involving Camostat or E64d, ACE2-expressing Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate 1 day before infection. Threefold serial dilutions of 200 μM Camostat (MCE, HY-13512) or E64d (MCE, HY-100229) in DMEM with 1% DMSO were incubated with the cells for 1 h at 37 °C. Afterwards, single-round PSVs were added to the cells along with the previously added inhibitors. Infection was assessed 18–24 hpi by measuring intracellular luciferase activity (RLU). Nonlinear regression with ‘[inhibitor] versus response (three parameters)’ was used for curve fitting.

Cell–cell fusion assays

Dual-split proteins-based cell–cell fusion assays were performed in Caco-2 cells stably expressing ACE2 receptors. Two groups of cells stably expressing the indicated receptors were transiently transfected with different plasmids to assess the S protein and receptor interaction-mediated membrane fusion. Group A cells were transfected with plasmids encoding S and rLucN(1-155)-sfGFP1-7(1-157), while group B cells were transfected with plasmids encoding S and sfGFP8-11(158-231)-rLucC (156-311) expressing plasmids. At 12 hpt, two groups of cells were trypsinized, combined and seeded into a 96-well plate at 8 × 104 cells per well. At 24 hpt, the cells were washed twice with DMEM and then incubated with 50 μl DMEM with or without indicated concentrations of TPCK-treated trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, T8802) for 20 min at 37 °C. The cells were supplemented with 100 μl DMEM/10% FBS to inactivate trypsin activity. Syncytia formation with green fluorescence was assessed 2–12 h later using Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining (1:5,000 dilution in HBSS for 30 min at 37 °C) and fluorescence microscopy (MI52-N; Mshot).

Immunofluorescence assay

For assessing the expression of membrane anchored receptors, cells transiently or stably expressing the indicated receptors with C-terminally fused 3× FLAG tags (for ACE2 and DPP4) or ALFA-tags (for hTMPRSS2) were fixed and permeabilized by incubation with 100% methanol (10 min at 25 °C), washed by PBS and incubated with 2 μg ml−1 mouse anti-FLAG antibody M2 (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804; 1:1,000 diluted in PBS/1% BSA), or 1 μg ml−1 homemade anti-ALFA-tag nanobody-hFc recombinant protein (1:1,000 diluted in PBS/1% BSA) for 1 h at 37 °C (ref. 55). After washing once with PBS, the cells were incubated with 2 μg ml−1 Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32742) or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A11013) 1:1,000 diluted in 1% BSA/PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. The images were captured with a fluorescence microscope (Mshot, MI52-N) after the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 reagent (1:1,000 dilution in PBS).

BLI

Biotinylated HKU25-462, HsItaly2011 and VsCoV-a7 RBD were diluted into 10× Octet Kinetics Buffer (Sartorius) and loaded onto pre-hydrated streptavidin biosensors to a 1 nm shift. The tips were then re-equilibrated in the kinetics buffer before being dipped into a serial dilution of R. norvegicus or E. fuscus ACE2 dimers for 300 to 500 s, followed by another incubation in kinetics buffer to assess the dissociation. The ACE2 starting concentrations ranged between 3,000 and 900 nM, and we used two- or threefold dilution series in kinetics buffer, leaving one well without any dilution as a background control. Binding was assessed at 30 °C and 1,000 r.p.m. using an Octet Red96. The binding data were baseline subtracted and evaluated using Octet Data Analysis 11.1 software with steady-state kinetics using the binding response for the last 5 s of the association phase and plotted in GraphPad 10.4.

Flow cytometry

Plasmids for overexpression of ACE2, DPP4 or APN orthologues were constructed by inserting human codon-optimized coding sequences into the vector (pLVX-IRES-ZsGreen) with C-terminally fused FLAG tags (DYKDDDDK). At 36 hpt, cells transiently expressing the indicated receptors were washed twice with cold PBS and incubated with 10 μg ml−1 of the indicated RBD-hFc proteins at 4 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 2 μg ml−1 Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (1:1,000 diluted in HBSS/1% BSA) to stain the bound RBD-hFc (Thermo Fisher Scientific; SA5-10136) at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were detached with 5 mM EDTA/PBS. For all samples, 10,000 receptor-expressing live cells gated based on Side Scatter (SSC)/Forward Scatter (FSC) and ZsGreen-fluorescence intensity were analysed using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman). Gating strategy is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

rcVSV-S amplification and inhibition assays

The experiments of replication-competent VSV-S (rcVSV-S) were authorized by the Biosafety Committee of the State Key Laboratory of Virology and Biosafety, Wuhan University, and conducted under Biosafety Level 2 conditions. To construct plasmids for rescuing rcVSV-S expressing HKU25 clade S glycoproteins, the fLuc encoding sequences of pVSV-ΔG-fLuc-GFP39 were replaced with the indicated coronavirus spike sequences. Reverse genetics was applied to rescue rcVSV-S pseudotypes expressing HKU25 clade S glycoproteins along with a GFP reporter, following a modified protocol from previous descriptions57. Briefly, BHK-21 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at 80% confluence and inoculated with 5 multiplicity of infection (MOI) of recombinant vaccinia virus expressing T7 RNA polymerase (vvT7, a kind gift from Mingzhou Chen’s lab, Hubei University) for 45 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were transfected with pVSV-ΔG-GFP-S vector plasmids and helper plasmids (pVSV-ΔG-GFP-S: pBS-N: pBS-P: pBS-G: pBS-L = 5:3:5:8:1) after washing with DMEM. The rcVSV-S containing supernatant (P0) was filtered (0.22 μm) and amplified in Caco-2 cells transiently expressing VSV-G (P1). Subsequently, P2 viruses were generated in Caco-2 cells stably expressing indicated ACE2, without the ectopic expression of VSV-G and in the presence of anti-VSVG antibody (I1-Hybridoma supernatant) to produce viruses without VSV-G contamination. For amplification assay, 3 × 104 Caco-2 cells stably expressing the indicated ACE2 were incubated with rcVSV-S (1 × 104 TCID50 per 100 μl) in a 96-well plate in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS with or without the treatment of indicated concentrations of TPCK-treated trypsin. For EK1C4 (ref. 33), S2P6 (ref. 34) or 76E1 (ref. 35) inhibition assays, the rcVSV-S were incubated with indicated antibodies (final concentration of 100 nM for EK1C4 and 50 μg ml⁻¹ for S2P6 and 76E1) in DMEM for 1 h at 25 °C, mixed with trypsin-detached ACE2-expressing Caco-2 cells in DMEM with a final concentration of 2% FBS and 20 μg ml−1 TPCK-treated trypsin in 96-well plates. For the h11B11 (ref. 36) or protease inhibitors, ACE2-expressing Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate 1 day before infection. h11B11 (final concentration of 50 μg ml⁻¹) or inhibitors (final concentration of 100 nM) were diluted in culture medium and then incubated with the cells for 1 h at 37 °C. The rcVSV-S viruses were then added to the cells in the presence of previously added inhibitors. At the indicated time after infection, the cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000 dilution in HBSS) for 30 min at 37 °C, and the fluorescence images were taken by a fluorescence microscope (MI52-N).

Cryo-electron microscopy data collection, processing and model building

The E. fuscus ACE2 ectodomain-bound VsCoV-a7 RBD complex was prepared by mixing at 1:1.2 molar ratio, followed by 1 h incubation at room temperature. Then 3 µl of 5 mg ml−1 complex with 6 mM 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio]-2-hydroxy-1-propanesulfonate (CHAPSO) was applied onto freshly glow-discharged R 2/2 UltrAuFoil grids60 before plunge freezing using a vitrobot MarkIV (ThermoFisher Scientific) with a blot force of 0 and 5.5 s blot time at 100% humidity and 22 °C. The data were acquired using an FEI Titan Krios transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV and equipped with a Gatan K3 direct detector and Gatan Quantum GIF energy filter, operated in zero-loss mode with a slit width of 20 eV. Automated data collection was carried out using Leginon (v.3.5)61 at a nominal magnification of ×105,000 with a pixel size of 0.843 Å. The dose rate was adjusted to 9 counts per pixel per s, and each movie was acquired in counting mode fractionated in 100 frames of 40 ms. A total 14,595 micrographs were collected with a defocus range between −0.2 and −3 μm. Movie frame alignment, estimation of the microscope contrast-transfer function parameters, particle picking and extraction were carried out using Warp (version v1.1.0 beta)62. Particles were extracted with a box size of 192 pixels at a down-sampled pixel size of 1.686 Å. Two rounds of reference-free two-dimensional (2D) classification were performed using cryoSPARC (v4.4.0)63 to select well-defined particle images. Initial model generation was carried out using ab initio reconstruction in cryoSPARC, and the resulting maps were used as references for heterogeneous three-dimensional (3D) refinement. Particles belonging to classes with the best resolved RBD and ACE2 density were selected. To further improve the data, we trained the Topaz model64 on Warp-picked particle sets belonging to the best classes after two rounds of reference-free 2D classification. Particles picked using Topaz were extracted and subjected to two rounds of reference-free 2D-classification, ab initio reconstruction and heterogenous 3D refinement. The two different particle sets from the Warp and Topaz picking strategies were merged, and duplicates were removed using a minimum distance cutoff of 90 Å. After two rounds of ab initio reconstruction–heterogeneous refinements, 3D refinement was carried out using non-uniform refinement with C2 symmetry and per-particle defocus refinement in cryoSPARC65. The dataset was transferred from cryoSPARC to Relion (v5.0) using the pyem (version 0.5) program package, and particle images were subjected to the Bayesian polishing procedure implemented in Relion66 during which particles were re-extracted with a box size of 320 pixels and a pixel size of 1.0 Å. To further improve the map quality, ab initio reconstruction in cryoSPARC was used to classify the data in three bins, and the generated models were used as references for heterogeneous 3D refinement. Non-uniform refinement of the ACE2 dimer-bound RBD structure was carried out using non-uniform refinement applying C2 symmetry and using per-particle defocus refinement in cryoSPARC to yield a reconstruction at 2.7 Å resolution comprising 941,663 particles. To further improve the density of the RBD and ACE2 domain interface, the particles were symmetry expanded and subjected to focused 3D classification without refining angles and shifts using a soft mask encompassing the RBD and ACE2 peptidase domain using a tau value of 40 in Relion. Particles belonging to classes with the best resolved RBD–ACE2 domain interface density were selected and then subjected to local refinement using CryoSPARC with the same soft mask. The final dataset contained 578,871 asymmetric units and led to a 2.5 Å resolution reconstruction. Reported resolutions are based on the gold standard FSC of 0.143 criterion, and FSC curves were corrected for the effects of soft masking by high-resolution noise substitution67,68. Local resolution estimation, filtering and sharpening were carried out using cryoSPARC.

For the structure of the R. norvegicus ACE2 ectodomain dimer bound to the HsItaly2011 RBD, the complex was prepared by mixing at 1:1.2 molar ratio followed by a 1 h incubation at room temperature. Then 3 µl of 5.3 mg ml−1 complex with 6 mM CHAPSO was applied onto freshly glow-discharged R 2/2 UltrAuFoil grids before plunge freezing using a vitrobot MarkIV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a blot force of 0 and 5.5 s blot time at 100% humidity and 22 °C. Then 8,298 movies were collected with a defocus range between −0.2 and −3.0 μm. The overall data processing methods were the same as those for the E. fuscus ACE2 ectodomain-bound VsCoV-a7 RBD complex except that focused 3D classification used a soft mask encompassing the RBD and part of the ACE2 peptidase domain with a tau value of 90 in Relion. The final dataset contained 831,432 asymmetric units and was used for the final local refinement with a soft mask comprising one R. norvegicus ACE2 peptidase domain and the bound HsItaly2011 RBD, resulting in a 2.5 Å resolution reconstruction. More details are shown in Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5.

UCSF Chimera (v1.7)69, Coot (version 0.9.8.93)70, AlphaFold371 and Phenix (version 1.21rc1-5109-000)72 were used to fit, build and refine the models using sharpened and unsharpened cryo-EM maps. Validation used Phenix72, MolProbity73 and Privateer (MKIV)74.

Bioinformatic and structural analysis

Merbecovirus S glycoprotein sequences were retrieved from the NCBI Virus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/) on 9 August 2024. A total of 3,308 unique Betacoronavirus S glycoprotein entries were obtained through search terms ‘Betacoronavirus’ or ‘BetaCoV’ with advanced filters ‘sequence length between 1,200-1,400’ and ‘exclude SARS-CoV-2’. Phylogenetic analysis in Geneious Prime (version 2023.0.4) software identified merbecovirus sequences, which were refined to 150 non-redundant entries after excluding sequences with identical amino acid compositions and over-sampled MERS-CoV strains (retaining two representatives: one human-derived and one camel-derived). Subsequent NCBI BLAST (v2.16.0) searches using MERSr-CoV S protein sequences identified two additional MERS-related coronaviruses (PDF-2180 and VsCoV-1), yielding a final dataset of 152 merbecoviruses (accession numbers and sequences in Supplementary Table 1). The RBM sequences in Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1d were aligned via MAFFT (version 7) with manual adjustments to optimize indel positioning. Fully and partially conserved residues were highlighted with red and green backgrounds, respectively. The RBD sequences (relative to MERS-CoV residues 377–588, NC_019843.3) used for evolutionary analysis in Fig. 1a were aligned via MAFFT-DASH (v1.1). Phylogenetic trees were generated with IQ-TREE (version 2.0.6) using a maximum likelihood model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Evolutionary model selection was performed using ModelFinder Plus in IQ-TREE with Bayesian information criterion optimization. The models implemented were WAG + G4 model for RBDs, TIM2 + F + I + R4 model for the whole genomes and LG + F + I + R4 model for spikes. Pairwise sequence identities were calculated in Geneious Prime (version 2023.0.4) (https://www.geneious.com/) following MAFFT alignment. The structures of HKU25-305, SC2013, HsItaly2011, PaGB01 and HKU31 RBDs were predicted using AlphaFold371. Experimentally resolved structures included the NeoCoV RBD–P. pipistrellus ACE2 complex (PDB 7WPO), MERS-CoV RBD (PDB 4KQZ), HKU4 RBD (PDB 4QZV), HKU5 RBD (PDB 9D32) and MOW15-22 RBD (PDB 9C6O). All these RBDs were visualized and analysed in ChimeraX (v1.7.1). Analysis of whole-genome nucleotide sequence similarity of indicated merbecoviruses was calculated in Simplot (version 3.5.1) following MAFFT alignment.

Statistics and reproducibility

Most experiments were performed two to three times with two to three biological repeats unless otherwise specified. Representative results are shown as means ± standard deviation (s.d.) as indicated in the figure legends. Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were conducted for statistical analyses using GraphPad Prism 10. P < 0.05 was considered significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; NS, not significant).
