Biochemical changes and phytoextraction potential of quinoa and wheat for their resilience to salt stress
Authors: Sepideh Hosseini, Roxana Moogouei, Shahla Teymoori, Roshanak Moogouei, Mehdi Borghei, Ololade Latinwo, Keyura Katam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhytoextraction presents a promising alternative for desalinating saline environments. Our study investigated the phytoremediation efficiency and ion uptake mechanisms of Chenopodium quinoa (Quinoa) and Triticum aestivum (wheat) in response to salt stress. The plants were subjected to NaCl-induced salinity levels of 5, 10, and 15 dS m⁻1 in a hydroponic system, and we measured the remediation efficiency for sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and chloride ions. The solutions incubated with wheat plants exhibited higher ion concentrations than those with quinoa. Chenopodium showed significantly higher bioaccumulation of ions (Mg2⁺, Ca2⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺) in its roots and leaves compared to Triticum. Chenopodium demonstrated greater ion uptake efficiency than Triticum. Under control conditions, both plants effectively contributed to desalination, as indicated by their translocation factor values. In contrast, Chenopodium showed higher TF under salt stress than Triticum for the measured ions. Salinity did not significantly affect potassium accumulation in quinoa shoots, which helped maintain membrane integrity compared to wheat. The analysis of the oxidative status revealed that wheat accumulated higher levels of hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxidation, especially in the roots. The activities of antioxidative enzymes superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, catalase, ascorbic peroxidase, and glutathione reductase showed a significant increase in the roots and leaves of Chenopodium under salt stress, providing essential protection against reactive oxygen species and lipid peroxidation. Introduction

Climate change and global warming significantly affect arid and semi-arid regions, leading to an alarming rise in the salinization of surface waters and agricultural soils, undermining global agricultural sustainability and food security1. Soil salinization, predominantly affecting the topsoil layer, results in excessive accumulation of soluble salts. Despite mitigation strategies like drip irrigation, subsurface drainage, and deep plowing, salinization remains a critical challenge. One significant impact is the rising sea levels, which allow seawater to infiltrate groundwater and coastal aquifers. This infiltration increases the salinity of these water resources, adversely affecting crop yields2. Salinity impairs root water uptake by reducing soil osmotic potential and limiting water availability. High soil salinity adversely affects various physiological processes, leading to deficiencies in water and nutrients and metabolic alterations. This can inhibit seed germination and photosynthesis, ultimately impacting plant growth and development3. As salinity levels rise, salt is absorbed and transported to the shoots, accumulating in the leaves, causing the ionic imbalance within cells and producing ion toxicity4. When subjected to salinity stress, plants respond with widespread changes in gene expression, primarily due to ionic imbalance and osmotic pressure. With global food production demand projected to increase by 60% by the year 2050, salinity poses one of the significant limitations in cereal cultivation, reducing the crop yield of plants like wheat by up to 65%5,6. Wheat faces multiple stresses such as drought, salinity, and flooding at various growth stages, with predictions suggesting a potential 4% decline in wheat cultivation by 20307,8.

Advancements in systems biology and high-throughput phenotyping have uncovered mechanisms related to cross-tolerance for multiple stresses and establishing stress memory9. In response to NaCl, genes encoding antioxidant enzymes are upregulated, enhancing the activity of superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and ascorbate peroxidase to mitigate hydrogen peroxide generated under high salinity10. Plant responses to salinity frequently involve proteins linked to signal perception, transduction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification, NaCl compartmentalization, changes in protein expression, and metabolism related to carbohydrates and salt11. Salinity levels significantly decreased wheat plants’ growth and output characteristics, gas exchange, membrane stability, photosynthetic pigments, plant water content, K+ and Ca2+ contents, and K+/Na+ ratio12. Proline and soluble sugar concentrations, Na+ and oxidative stress biomarkers, and antioxidant activities were increased by salinity compared to the control. An increased concentration of 300 mM NaCl increased the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide, causing oxidative stress in wheat seedlings13. Salt-tolerant wheat showed overexpression of proteins and metabolites associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, starch, sucrose metabolism, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway14.

Nanoparticles (NPs), including metallic, metal oxide, and carbon-based varieties, are being utilized to enhance salt tolerance in crops due to their ability to penetrate plant tissues and improve morphology, physiology, and biochemical responses15. This results in better ion regulation and osmotic adjustment, fostering healthier growth and higher yields under saline conditions. NPs also enhance the expression of stress-responsive genes and antioxidants, fortifying the plants’ defenses. Biochar is similarly acclaimed for sustaining agricultural productivity amidst climate variability, while alternate cropping patterns are proposed as direct strategies for managing salinity stress and ensuring ongoing food production16. Phytodesalination has emerged as an economical and sustainable method for handling saline soils and restoring salt-affected lands17. Effective phytoremediation species must tolerate environmental pollutants and produce high biomass, improving soil conditions18. Halophytes are adept at absorbing significant levels of elements associated with salinity, such as sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium, making them suitable for use as NaCl remediation crops19.

Halophytes, such as Portulaca oleracea L., showcase remarkable resilience and are recognized as practical solutions for sustainable agriculture and food security20. Chenopodium quinoa (Quinoa), a facultative halophyte, is a promising cash crop for marginalized areas and possesses phytoextraction capabilities for heavy metals, making it suitable for reclaiming polluted soils. Certain halophytes are not just resilient plants; they represent a promising solution for sustainable agriculture and food security. These remarkable salt-tolerant species thrive in challenging coastal and arid-saline environments, making them invaluable in the face of climate change. It is known to have phytoextraction potential of heavy metals in leaves and thus can be used to reclamation polluted soil21. Quinoa seeds and leaves are rich in vitamin E and omega 3, have a lower sodium content, and their minerals are much higher than wheat, barley, and corn, so the amount of calcium, zinc, and iron in quinoa is higher than in other cereals22. The NaCl application positively impacted plant growth and cesium absorption by quinoa23. Quinoa can survive in soils with salinity levels as high as 51.5 dSm−124. Thus, quinoa has been valued as a model food crop due to its exceptional nutritive value and adaptability to various abiotic stresses25.

Triticum aestivum (wheat) and Chenopodium quinoa (quinoa) are the two promising crops that have emerged as exceptional plants for their impressive tolerance to salinity, affordability, and remarkable adaptability to harsh conditions26,27. These crops’ resilience and ability to thrive in waterlogged conditions are vital for ensuring food security in challenging environments. With their remarkable growth rates and impressive yields, these crops promise significant economic benefits and contribute to sustainability. Moreover, the by-products generated from phytoremediation can be harnessed to create forage or renewable energy, solidifying their essential role in advancing sustainable agricultural practices28. Hence, this study aimed to determine the efficiency of Triticum aestivum and Chenopodium quinoa plants in the uptake and accumulation of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and chloride and their ion translocation factors under different salinity stresses. Furthermore, the antioxidant activity and phytoremediation efficiency were evaluated for both wheat and quinoa.

Material and methods

Plant material and hydroponics growth conditions

The seeds of Triticum aestivum and Chenopodium Quinoa were surface sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (10%) for 30 min, washed with distilled water, and germinated in petri dishes in a dark incubator at 35 °C and 60% relative humidity. One-week-old seedlings were transplanted to pots containing sterile coco peat and perlite (pH 5.5) and maintained in the greenhouse. A minimum temperature of 16 °C and a maximum temperature of 26 °C with 40% relative humidity was maintained in the greenhouse. Sixty-day-old plants were removed from coco peat and perlite (pH 5.5), washed with distilled water, transferred to Hoagland’s solution, and incubated for 14 days29. Salinity treatments of plants

After 14 days of incubation in Hoagland solution, the roots were washed in distilled water. The plants were transferred to containers with four different salinity levels: 0 dS m−1 (control), 5 dS m−1, 10 dS m−1, and 15 dS m−1 based on their electric conductivity. NaCl concentrations were added at four-day intervals to minimize osmotic shock, and the pH of the solutions was maintained between 5.5–6 throughout the experiment30. During the remediation process, the plants were incubated for 16 h in daylight and 8 h in the dark with minimum temperatures ranging from 15 to 24 °C and maximum temperature of 28 to 36 °C.

Measurement of physiological traits of plants treated under salinity treatments

The most critical morphological characteristics that determine the behavior of the plant during the phytoremediation process include the average leaf area, dry weight, root length, and shoot length. This trait monitors plant changes and the influence of salinity and its fluctuation. After the remediation period of 28 days, the plants were removed from the culture medium, and the average leaf area, dry weight, root length, and shoot length were measured31. To measure the average leaf area, the tested plants were taken from solutions with specific salinity, and their leaf area was measured in square centimeters by the leaf area measuring device32. The containers were oven-dried for two days at a temperature of 80 °C ± 5, and their dry weight was determined with a digital scale.

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activity

The tissue samples (both root and aerial tissues) (1 g) were milled using a cold mortar and pestle, homogenized in 5 ml of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) to determine the enzyme activity, and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used to measure the activities of superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, catalase, and ascorbic peroxidase enzymes34. Glutathione reductase activity was determined by measuring the reduction of GSSG by NADPH at 30 °C, indicated by a decrease in absorbance at 340 nm35. The assay mixture contained 0.2 M potassium phosphate, 0.2 mM Na2EDTA, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 25 μm NADPH, 0.25 mM GSSH, pH 7.5, and 50 μl of enzyme extract in a 1 ml final volume. The addition of NADPH initiated the reaction.

Lipid peroxidation:

Lipid peroxidation was determined by measuring the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances assay. Frozen root samples (1 g) were milled separately in 5 ml of 1% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C 36. The MDA concentration was calculated using the following equation: MDA (μmol g−1 FW) = 6.45(OD532 − OD600)—0.56 OD450.

Measurement of hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide content in the leaves and roots of both plants was determined using trichloroacetic acid (TCA) method37. Frozen samples (0.2 g) were homogenized in an ice bath with 5 ml of 0.1% TCA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Then, 0.5 ml of the supernatant was added to 0.5 ml of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 ml of 1 M KI. The supernatant was taken, and its absorbance was read at 390 nm. The content was measured using a standard calibration curve of known H2O2 concentrations.

Measurement of Ascorbic acid

About 250 mg of frozen leaf and root samples were extracted with 10 ml of 6% trichloroacetic acid. Two milliliters of 2% dinitrophenylhydrazine were mixed with 4 ml of the extract, and one drop of 10% thiourea solution in ethanol (70%) was added and then boiled in 100 °C water bath for 15 min and cooled at room temperature. Subsequently, 5 ml of cold H2SO4 (80%) was added to the mixture, and the absorbance of the solution was determined at 530 nm using a spectrometer38.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) software package. The average of the data was compared using the minimum significant difference of the FLSD test at the level of 5%. The measured traits were analyzed separately in a randomized complete block design to determine the response of each plant to salinity stress. All the data were subjected to GIM-ANOVA, and the least significant differences (LSD) after the variance analysis were applied to allow multiple comparisons of the mean of the data (p = 0.05).

Results

Morphological changes in Triticum and Chenopodium in response to salt stress

Triticum showed more significant root and shoot lengths than Chenopodium under normal conditions (Fig. 2). Under salt stress, Triticum experienced a significant reduction in both root length (42.5%) and shoot length (24%). In contrast, Chenopodium showed only a 12% decrease in shoot length, while its root length remained unchanged compared to the control. Triticum’s leaf area decreased by 66% with rising salinity, whereas Chenopodium showed an increase in leaf area, specifically a 19% increase at a salinity stress level of 15 dsm−1. Furthermore, the dry weight of Triticum was reduced by 54%, while Chenopodium demonstrated an increase of 15% (Table 1).

Remediation of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and chloride ions from saline solution using hydroponics-grown plants

The phytoremediation efficiency of Triticum and Chenopodium was evaluated after 28 days of incubation in saline solutions at various concentrations. The levels of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and chloride ions in the saline solutions were measured before and after the incubation of the plants (Supplement Tables 1–5). In Triticum, sodium ion uptake increased at a salinity of 5 dSm−1. However, it showed a slight decrease at salinities of 10 and 15 dSm−1, resulting in a final remediation efficiency of 12.3% (Fig. 3). In contrast, potassium ion uptake significantly declined with increasing salinity, leading to a remediation efficiency of only 5.03%. The highest remediation efficiency for magnesium ions, at 21.89%, was observed at 15 dSm−1 NaCl. Calcium ion uptake also decreased, resulting in a remediation efficiency of 14.97%. Chlorine uptake efficiency increased to 12.20% as salinity increased to 15 dS m−1. In Chenopodium, magnesium ion uptake reached the highest level at 24.5%, followed by sodium at 23.96% and chlorine at 18.4%. All these ions showed increased uptake with rising salinity. However, potassium and calcium uptakes decreased with the increasing saline concentration. Overall, Chenopodium displayed a greater ion uptake capacity than Triticum.

Accumulation of ions− in shoots and roots of hydroponics-grown plants under salt stress

The accumulation of ions in the root and leaf tissues was analyzed to assess the impact of salt stress on the physiology of wheat and quinoa exposed to varying salt concentrations. In wheat, the Na+, Mg2+, and Cl− ions levels in the leaf and roots increased progressively with higher salinity concentrations. Conversely, the bioaccumulation of K+ and Ca2+ ions decreased as salinity levels increased (Supplement Table 6). Similarly, quinoa exhibited responses analogous to those seen in wheat. The accumulation of Na+, Mg2+, and Cl− ions in the leaves and roots of quinoa increased with rising salinity, and the K+ and Ca2+ ions levels decreased as salinity increased (Supplement Table 7).

Comparative analysis of ion bioaccumulation efficiency in Triticum and Chenopodium to salt stress

Sodium ion accumulation tends to be higher in leaves than in the roots, with increased salt concentration in both plant species. (Fig. 4). At the control level, Na+ concentration was lower in the aerial tissues of wheat than in the roots. In contrast, quinoa exhibited higher Na+ levels in its aerial tissues than in the roots. At the highest level (15 dSm−1), the Na+ in the aerial tissues surpassed those in the roots for both plants, with a more significant accumulation of the ion in the quinoa leaf, whereas the roots of both plants showed a similar increase in Na+ to high salt stress. At the same time, no significant change occurs in the roots of either plant in response to salt treatment. Magnesium ion accumulation tends to rise in both the roots and shoots of the plants. Additionally, the bioaccumulation of chloride ions in the roots and shoots of both Triticum aestivum and Chenopodium quinoa increases with higher saline concentration in the solution. Notably, at a salinity level of 15 dSm−1, quinoa leaves exhibit the maximum chloride concentration compared to wheat. Translocation factors (TF) to salt stress

The translocation factors for the ions studied—sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and chloride- were greater than 1, indicating their potential for effective plant remediation (Supplement Table 8). Under controlled conditions, the translocation factor (TF) values for sodium, magnesium, calcium, and chloride were twice as high in Chenopodium compared to Triticum; in contrast, the TF for potassium was lower in Chenopodium than in Triticum (Fig. 5). In Triticum, the TF for sodium, magnesium, and chloride increased with higher salinity levels. However, the TF for calcium decreased as salinity increased, while potassium showed a slight increase in TF up to a salinity level of 10 dSm−1, after which the TF declined. In Chenopodium, the TF values for sodium, magnesium, chloride, and calcium were higher at low salt concentrations but decreased with increasing salinity. Conversely, the TF for potassium consistently increased with higher salinity levels.

Accumulation of hydrogen peroxide, ascorbic acid, and lipid peroxidation to salt stress

The accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ascorbic acid (ASA), and lipid peroxidation are considered the primary biomarkers of oxidative status in plants39. Salt stress significantly increased H₂O₂ levels in wheat and quinoa’s root and aerial tissues. In control groups, H₂O₂ levels were higher in the root tissues than in the leaves of both plants. At high salt concentrations, wheat exhibited the most significant increase in H₂O₂ levels, with at least a fourfold increase in the roots and a twofold increase in the shoots (Fig. 6). In contrast, quinoa experienced approximately a twofold rise in H₂O₂ levels in both roots and shoots compared to the control plants. Ascorbic acid content decreased with salinity treatments. Leaf tissues contained higher levels of AsA than root tissues in both plants. At 5 dSm−1 of NaCl, no significant difference was observed. However, at a salinity level of 15 dSm−1, the highest amounts of AsA were found in the leaves and roots of quinoa compared to wheat (Fig. 7). Lipid peroxidation levels, measured by malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, were higher in leaves than in roots under control conditions in both plant species (Fig. 8). MDA levels increased linearly from low to high concentrations of NaCl in both plants, generally being higher in leaf tissues than in root tissues. In leaf tissue, MDA levels at 15 dS m−1 NaCl treatment were highest in wheat, showing approximately a fourfold increase in both leaves and roots, followed by quinoa, which showed a threefold increase in both tissues.

The antioxidative enzyme activity is notably higher in leaves compared to roots for both standard and saline treatments across the plants observed (Fig. 9). In particular, quinoa’s leaf SOD levels rose by 2.8-fold, while wheat’s rose by 1.5-fold at a salinity of 10 dSm−1. However, at 15 dSm−1, there was a decline in SOD activity for both plants, with quinoa exhibiting the highest overall activity. For root tissues, SOD activity also increased under salt stress, peaking in quinoa at 10 dSm−1 with twofold increase, but declining at 15 dSm−1. Wheat roots exhibited a marginal increase at 5 dSm−1, followed by a decrease after reaching 10 dSm−1.

Regarding peroxidase (POD) activity, both quinoa’s root and leaf tissues presented lower levels than wheat under control conditions. However, under 15 dSm−1 NaCl treatment, quinoa preserved higher enzymatic activity levels in leaves (1.5-fold) and roots (1.6-fold) compared to its controls. Discussion

Soil and surface water salinity significantly threaten environmental sustainability, sustainable agriculture, and food security. Salt stress caused a more significant reduction in the root and shoot lengths, leaf area, and dry weight in Triticum, while a significant increase in leaf area and dry weight was observed in Chenopodium (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Higher remediation efficiency of Chenopodium

Our study measured the differences in ion concentrations of solutions after incubating Triticum and Chenopodium plants. The results indicated that ion concentrations in solutions with Triticum were higher than those in solutions with Chenopodium (Fig. 3). This suggests that Triticum has a lower uptake of these ions, corresponding to the greater remediation efficiency observed in Chenopodium. Our data indicate that Chenopodium exhibits higher remediation efficiency for all five ions across various salt concentrations. Recent hydroponic studies have shown that higher NaCl concentrations in the culture medium significantly increase plant tissue’s sodium, potassium, crude ash, and hemicellulose levels while calcium, sulfur, nitrogen, and carbon levels decline40. Sodium stress raises the cytosolic calcium concentration, enhancing plants’ salt tolerance through the SOS pathway and the Na+/H+ antiporter SOS141. These findings highlight the vital role of Ca2+ ions in regulating transport processes under salinity, suggesting higher ion sequestering by Chenopodium promising avenues for improving crop resilience in saline conditions42. Three halophytic species from the Chenopodiaceae family have effectively reduced salinity to acceptable levels when grown under various salt concentrations in constructed wetlands43. Additionally, the halophytes Ipomoea aquatica, Alternanthera philoxeroides, and Ludwigia adscendens demonstrated a decrease in electrical conductivity (EC) of the water with increasing exposure time when treated with 0 and 7 dSm−1 salinity, further illustrating their capability to promote phyto desalinization and improve water quality44. Furthermore, the halophytes Bambusa vulgaris and Phragmites australis, when exposed to NaCl concentrations of 1000 mg/L, showed phytoremediation efficiencies of 32% and 19%, respectively45.

Ion homeostasis during salt stress:

Under salinity stress, ion homeostasis is crucial to plant cell function. High salinity levels lead to a notable increase in Na+ and Cl− accumulation while simultaneously inhibiting the uptake of K+ and Ca2+. This results in ion imbalances that can cause cell oxidative stress46. Our research demonstrated marked differences in the accumulation of ions in the roots and shoots of two plant species, revealing higher concentrations in the shoots than in the roots (refer to Supplement Tables 6 and 7). In both Triticum and Chenopodium species, Na, Mg, and Cl ion accumulation increased in response to salt stress, while the levels of K+ and Ca2+ were decreased. Chenopodium showed a higher Na, Mg, and Cl ion accumulation in leaf and root tissue and more significant reductions in K+ and Ca2+ than in Triticum (Fig. 4A, B). Thus, Na+ poses a significant challenge to K+ homeostasis, making the maintenance of a balanced cytosolic Na+/K+ ratio vital for plant salinity tolerance, affecting the functioning of Na+ and K+ transporters and channels in channels in uptake, translocation, and intracellular homeostasis. This observation complements the findings regarding Chenopodium, indicating an increase in ion accumulation, particularly Na+, Mg2+, and Cl− to elevated salt concentrations47. Earlier studies have also shown increased magnesium accumulation in the shoots of halophytic species like Arthrocnemum indicum and Suaeda fruticosa, suggesting their potential for use in phytodesalination48.

The elevated Na+ uptake in both roots and shoots negatively impacted the absorption of Ca2+ and K+, disrupting normal cellular functions49. The influx of Ca2⁺ activates calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) to help initiate the downstream signaling cascades and regulate the ion transport and the gene expression involved with the salt stress tolerance50. This ion balance is closely regulated by Ca2⁺ signaling, which influences key transport proteins like Na⁺/K⁺ transporters. Additionally, the interaction between secondary metabolites and calcium signaling is vital for salt tolerance. Ca2⁺ regulates the biosynthesis of these metabolites through transcription factors like MYB and WRKY.

The accumulation of Na+ and Cl− in the roots of both Triticum and Chenopodium implies an active exclusion mechanism, which helps facilitate water uptake against concentration gradients more efficiently in Chenopodium as these ions accumulate at a greater quantity than in wheat. Chenopodium quinoa has been noted for its higher diversity of K+, Na+, and Cl− transporters51, which may affect its response to salt stress. Recent studies on salt bladders found on the surfaces of quinoa leaves have shown that these structures secrete Na + as a mechanism to adapt to salt stress52.

The excessive uptake of NaCl in saline conditions competes with K+ uptake, leading to a deficiency of K ions53. In our studies, quinoa maintained a relatively higher K+ concentration than wheat in leaves and roots, suggesting a superior ability to uphold a stable cytosolic K+/Na+ ratio in saline environments (Fig. 4A, B). As salinity levels increased, we observed decreased K+ accumulation in wheat and quinoa leaves and roots. Similar findings have been documented, indicating that Na+ competes with K+ for root uptake under saline conditions54. Maintaining elevated levels of K+ under saline conditions is indicative of a salt tolerance mechanism55. The significance of potassium cannot be overstated, as it serves as an activator for several essential enzymes and directly influences processes such as photosynthesis, nitrogen and carbon metabolism, sugar transport, and protein synthesis by regulating ATPase and stomatal function56. The preferential transport of K+ over Na+ can be considered a valuable criterion for identifying salt-resistant genotypes, representing an effective strategy for adaptation to saline environments while ensuring optimal K+ and Na+ homeostasis.

Higher Transfer factor of ions is a crucial index for phytoremediation potential

The translocation factor (TF), also known as the concentration ratio (CR), represents the relationship between ion concentrations in the shoots and roots of plants57. Some plants can inhibit the upward movement of metals, using this mechanism as a form of tolerance. Generally, successful phytoremediation depends on efficiently transporting metals to plant parts that can be easily harvested. Plants that limit soil and root translocation often exhibit reduced biomass accumulation, while hyperaccumulators are known for efficiently taking up metals into their aerial biomass.

Our findings indicate that, except for Na+ and K+ control samples, both plants exhibited TF values greater than one (TF > 1) for ions at all saline concentrations, suggesting that Triticum and Chenopodium demonstrate practical desalination efficiencies under normal soil conditions (Fig. 5). The TF is an index greater than 1 implies high efficiency, as this was emphasized in most recent literature58. For various salinity concentrations, TFs greater than one indicate that the aerial parts of these plants accumulate more ions than their roots. Remarkably, Chenopodium plants exposed to different salinity levels showed increased dry weight and higher Na+ and Cl− concentrations in both roots and shoots (Table 1 and Fig. 3). The data revealed that increasing salinity from 5 dSm−1 initially enhanced root length at 10 dS m−1 before gradually causing a decline. In contrast, shoot length decreased with rising salinity levels, while leaf area and dry weight increased. Previous research on the salt-resistant Chenopodium cultivar UDEC-5 indicated that the accumulation of NaCl in plant tissues promoted water uptake, enhanced indirect photosynthesis, and reduced ionic stress59. The same studies comparing quinoa genotypes under varying salinity levels (0, 100, and 300 mM NaCl) found that the salt-resistant UDEC-5 exhibited significant growth stimulation, outperforming the salt-sensitive Kcoito variety.

Scavenging ROS to salt stress and detoxification process in wheat and quinoa

The accumulation of Na+ and Cl− ions in the leaves and roots increased oxidative stress markers, particularly H2O2. Quinoa exhibited a lower concentration of H2O2, while wheat demonstrated an accumulation of photo-reducing power, resulting in higher production of H2O260. Salinity stress induces ROS generation and disrupts the lipid bilayer, potentially causing significant peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids due to hydrogen expulsion, thereby increasing the production of free oxygen radicals and reactive aldehydes61. The adverse effects of ROS accumulation were more pronounced in the roots than in the shoots. This is primarily because roots are the initial plant tissues to encounter saline toxicity and are in direct contact with elevated salt levels in the soil. Our results showed higher ROS production in wheat compared to the H2O2 levels in quinoa (Fig. 5).

Furthermore, the detoxification mechanisms involve significant ion acclimatization within root tissues. Moderate to high salinity affected the ascorbic acid (AsA) content in both leaves and roots of the studied plants (Fig. 6). Despite the reduction under high salinity levels (15 dSm−1), quinoa managed to maintain a higher AsA content compared to wheat. The reduction of AsA under salinity stress may be directly attributed to its degradation by ROS, as observed previously in wheat62. The generation and accumulation of oxygen-free radicals and H2O2 are known to lead to membrane distortion and protein degeneration, which are often associated with lipid peroxidation63. AsA and GSH both have high redox potentials, by which AsA and GSH detoxify H2O2 and at the same time, both AsA and GSH are regenerated. Both AsA and GSH are potent antioxidants, but maintaining their redox state is important in conferring stress tolerance in plants, which largely depends on the activities of the four enzymes associated with the AsA-GSH cycle64. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification is linked to increased ascorbic acid (AsA) during salt stress. This process regulates the expression of genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism in quinoa65. Carbohydrate metabolism is essential for managing osmotic potential and supplying energy and carbon sources when photosynthesis is limited during stress66. This dynamic is further supported by the higher Na and Cl ion accumulation both in root and aerial shoot tissues of quinoa and wheat plants.

Compared to the control group, MDA increments were higher in leaves than in roots, suggesting that the leaves were more adversely affected by oxidative damage due to salinity (Fig. 7). These findings align with those results of severe lipid peroxidation in pepper plants under salt stress67. It is generally acknowledged that excessive ROS production may lead to oxidative stress, resulting in lipid peroxidation of membranes and compromising their integrity and chloroplast ultrastructure68. In this study, wheat exhibited the highest MDA content in leaves and roots (Fig. 8), leading to increased ROS and H2O2 (Fig. 6), which function as signal transduction molecules and probably cause a higher reduction in photosynthetic activity, resulting in the reduction of leaf biomass, area, and significant reduction in plant growth (Table 1; Fig. 2). Elevated ROS levels contribute to membrane lipid peroxidation, with peak MDA levels attributed to the peroxidation of essential lipid membranes resulting from ROS generation and acclimatization under salt stress69. The production of excessive ROS accelerated lipid peroxidation (MDA), accumulation of Na+ to toxic levels in plant tissues, and disturbance in the antioxidant response of peas reduced the photosynthetic ability of plants, resulting in reduced growth and development under salinity stress. The salinity-induced oxidative stress (H2O2 and MDA) caused photoinhibition of PSII at the donor and acceptor ends70. The elevated MDA levels This increase in MDA damaged the membrane of cells and might have caused up to a 50 percent leaf loss in wheat (Table 1). The mitigating role of Moringa olifera leaf extract applied as a seed priming and foliar spray in peas grown under salinity stress is reported71. The excessive production of ROS-induced lipid peroxidation chain reactions disrupts the bio-membranes and induces cell death. At the same time, quinoa had the lowest MDA content when exposed to high stress, indicating reduced membrane damage and highlighting quinoa’s superior tolerance to saline conditions. In quinoa, the levels of H2O2 and MDA in leaves were low, contributing to the protection of chloroplast ultrastructure and PSII photochemical efficiency.

Elevation of antioxidant activities in Chenopodium

The antioxidant activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) were observed to be higher in leaves compared to the roots of both wheat and quinoa, under control conditions. However, glutathione reductase (GR) exhibited higher activity in the roots than in the leaves for both plants. Under low salinity, there was generally an increase in the activities of these enzymes. At the same time, medium to high salt stress resulted in differential expression based on the plant tissue (Fig. 8). Specifically, both wheat leaves and roots experienced a decline in antioxidant activities at 10 dSm−1. However, APX levels increased in the roots at that stage. Previous studies indicate that various wheat varieties demonstrate differing antioxidant enzyme activities and sensitivity to salt stress, often showing reduced enzyme activities72.

In contrast, quinoa displayed increased enzyme activities in both tissues up to 10 dSm−1, followed by a decline after 15 dSm−1. A highly evolved sophisticated antioxidant system has been shown to protect the cells from oxidative damage. Notably, CAT activity declined at 10 dSm−1, while APX continued to increase to 15 dSm−1 in leaf and root tissues. Previous studies reveal that the protective role of CAT in maize leaves seems effective only up to 80 mM NaCl, as the synthesis of a specific form of CAT appears limited at higher salt concentrations73. This increase in antioxidant enzyme activity indicates that quinoa has activated its defense mechanisms to cope with the oxidative stress caused by salt stress. SOD activity increased consistently, by 1.5X, in leaves exposed to 100 mM NaCl, enhancing protection against harmful effects and representing an adaptive response that contributes to improved salt tolerance74. Moreover, GR activity was consistently higher in the roots than in the leaves for both species, particularly in quinoa, where higher ROS production likely induces GR through salinity stress-related gene expression, potentially mitigating damages to cell membrane integrity caused by salt stress75. A slight increase in GR activity at 100 mM NaCl may be attributed to the detrimental effects of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions or the restricted availability of NADPH due to impaired photosynthetic processes under salt stress76.

The overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) releases a cascade of toxic effects that include lipid peroxidation, leading to the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Fig. 8). This situation induces a powerful defense mechanism, activating robust antioxidant responses designed to mitigate oxidative stress. In a remarkable feedback loop, the rising levels of ROS stimulate the activation of vital antioxidant enzymes: superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX). These enzymes serve as protectors, scavenging the ROS to shrink their abundance. The overall increase in antioxidant enzymes in quinoa is consistent with earlier findings, which act as vigilant defenders, reducing oxidative threats and ensuring the delicate balance in antioxidant molecules and enzymes in response to NaCl77. This suggests that stress conditions can trigger the production of specific secondary metabolites. The salt treatment induced sodium accumulation in the shoots, functioning as a nutrient while heightening antioxidant enzyme activities and promoting the production of bioactive molecules.

Conclusion

An increase in salinity in the solution showed a positive correlation with the phytoremediation efficiency for sodium, magnesium, and chlorine. However, the efficiency of sodium and calcium decreased in both plant species. High salt regimes in the soil enhanced membrane permeability, leading to a loss of membrane integrity in both wheat and quinoa tissues caused by oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation. This change correlates with the increased acquisition of Na, Cl, and Mg ions, with quinoa exhibiting a more significant accumulation of these ions. In contrast, quinoa experiences only a slight decline in potassium accumulation in the shoots, while the roots show more significant reductions. This difference allows quinoa to maintain higher membrane integrity concerning the K+/Na+ ratio, resulting in less damage from salt stress. The more significant accumulation of Na+ and Cl− in the roots of Chenopodium suggests that it employs a more active exclusion mechanism, enhancing its ability to uptake water against concentration gradients.

Additionally, the increase in leaf area and dry weight in Chenopodium enables it to accumulate ions at higher concentrations, indicating its greater phytoremediation efficiency for these ions than Triticum. Wheat leaves have experienced a more excellent production of H2O2, causing higher MDA levels in response to medium to high salt levels, which might have inhibited photosynthetic activity, resulting in a significant reduction in leaf area and biomass. Sodium bioaccumulation is higher in the shoots of both plants than in their roots, implying that harvesting the aerial parts of these plants can effectively aid in removing sodium pollution from the environment. The results reveal that salt stress leads to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation while hindering the antioxidant defense system in wheat. Furthermore, quinoa responded to salt stress by alleviating the intricate antioxidative enzyme systems such as SOD, GR, and APX. However, CAT levels were not high, which collaborated to regulate ROS levels. Due to salt stress, the elevated activities of antioxidant enzymes and ascorbic acid in Chenopodium protect against ROS and lipid peroxidation.

Future perspective

Hyperaccumulator plants can absorb and store large amounts of heavy metals, making them essential for phytoremediation. However, despite their potential, phytoremediation encounters challenges related to plant selection, soil conditions, and the time required for remediation. While these plants effectively clean contaminated soils and waters, they must be managed carefully to prevent environmental risks, such as entering heavy metals into the food chain. A significant advancement in this field is the introduction of nanoparticles (NPs). The strategic application of NPs can protect the photosynthetic system from salinity damage, enhance membrane integrity, and improve nutrient absorption. NPs boost the production of stress-responsive genes and antioxidants, reinforcing plants’ natural defenses. These particles bind to pollutants, enhancing their absorption by plants and directing them to critical areas, which improves remediation efficiency. Combining nanotechnology with artificial intelligence (AI) and remote sensing technologies has great promise, as it can optimize plant selection and monitor large-scale remediation efforts in real time.

Additionally, biochar plays a crucial role in immobilizing cationic heavy metals in polluted soils, especially in acidic environments. It not only improves soil structure and fertility but also supports microbial life. However, carefully selecting biochar is vital to maximize its effectiveness against various metal pollutants. Ongoing research into engineered plants and microbe-assisted methods aims to enhance phytoremediation efficiency further. In conclusion, phytoremediation is a powerful, sustainable, cost-effective environmental cleanup solution. Its ability to remove a wide range of pollutants makes it an essential tool in the battle against global pollution, with continued research paving the way for a healthier future.
