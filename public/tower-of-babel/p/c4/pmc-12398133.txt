Shear wave elastography for noninvasive assessment of intracompartmental pressure in tibial plateau fractures: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Heng Zhang, Luqi Li, Huiyang Jia, Haofei Wang, Qi Dong, Jialiang Guo, Zhiyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the utility of shear wave elastography (SWE) as a noninvasive diagnostic tool for assessing anterior compartment intracompartmental pressure (ICP) in patients with tibial plateau fractures, facilitating early detection of high compartment pressure (HCP) to mitigate acute compartment syndrome (ACS) risks.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study conducted from January 2024 to March 2025 at a tertiary hospital in China, 170 patients aged 18–65 years with tibial plateau fractures diagnosed within 48 h were enrolled. Demographic data, injury mechanisms, Schatzker classification, and laboratory parameters were collected. shear wave velocity (SWV) was measured via SWE on both injured and contralateral anterior compartment muscles, alongside invasive ICP monitoring. Statistical analyses encompassed Spearman’s correlations, stepwise logistic regression for a multivariate model incorporating SWV, age, and fracture type, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for diagnostic performance.ResultsAmong participants (57.1% male, median age 45 years), 44 exhibited HCP (ICP ≥ 30 mmHg). Injury-side SWV and SWV differential showed strong positive correlations with ICP (ρ = 0.704 and 0.535, respectively; both P < 0.001). Significant SWV disparities were observed between HCP and non-HCP groups (P < 0.001). The multivariate model yielded an AUC of 0.880, with 93.2% sensitivity and 71.4% specificity, demonstrating consistent performance across gender subgroups. Adjusted odds ratios highlighted injury-side SWV (aOR 5.99) and Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ fractures (aOR 4.14) as key predictors.ConclusionOur study results indicate that SWE can reliably and noninvasively detect HCP in tibial plateau fractures, providing superior diagnostic accuracy and clinical applicability.Keywords: Acute compartment syndrome (ACS), Intracompartmental pressure (ICP), Shear wave elastography (SWE), Tibial plateau fractures, Ultrasound

Introduction

Acute compartment syndrome (ACS) is a surgical emergency, with its pathophysiology involves a vicious cycle of increased intracompartmental pressure (ICP), reduced microcirculation, cellular hypoxia, and subsequent edema, which can rapidly progress to rhabdomyolysis and myonecrosis if untreated [1–6]. Timely recognition and emergent fasciotomy are essential, as delays beyond critical thresholds—often within six hours—can result in permanent functional deficits, limb loss, or death [7–9]. The lower leg is particularly susceptible due to its rigid osteofascial compartments and limited compliance [10–12]. Tibial plateau fractures, commonly resulting from high-energy trauma, have been reported to complicate ACS in 11–27% of cases, especially in Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ fracture types, underscoring the clinical importance of vigilance in this population [13, 14]. Early and accurate diagnosis remains crucial to ameliorate outcomes and reduce the significant morbidity associated with this limb-threatening condition [9, 15].

Traditional diagnosis of ACS primarily relies on clinical symptoms and invasive ICP measurements using devices such as needle manometers or wick catheters [2, 10]. However, clinical assessment is often subjective and challenging, particularly in patients with impaired consciousness, leading to delayed or inaccurate diagnosis [16–18]. Moreover, invasive ICP measurement, the current gold standard, carries risks including pain, infection, iatrogenic injury, and potential alteration of compartment pressure due to technique limitations and the need for saline injections [19, 20]. The accuracy and reliability of both clinical and invasive pressure assessments remain debated, limiting their routine and repeated use, especially in vulnerable populations or during continuous monitoring [2, 17, 21]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop real-time, noninvasive, safe, and reliable diagnostic methods to improve early detection and timely intervention of ACS, ultimately reducing morbidity and improving prognosis [10, 19].

Ultrasonography is a widely accessible and non-invasive imaging modality with proven advantages in evaluating superficial soft-tissue diseases, offering real-time, radiation-free assessment with ease of use and minimal patient discomfort [8, 10, 22]. Shear wave elastography (SWE), an advanced ultrasound technique, quantitatively measures tissue stiffness by inducing and tracking shear wave propagation, providing objective data on mechanical properties, which closely correlates with tissue elasticity and biomechanical status [8, 23, 24]. SWE extends traditional ultrasound by enabling dynamic, quantitative evaluation of musculoskeletal structures, as well as predicting fall risk in older adults, with demonstrated utility in diagnosing conditions like sarcopenia, muscle spasticity, and chronic pain syndromes with less operator dependence and high reproducibility [8, 25–27]. Though the application of SWE in musculoskeletal disease diagnostics is well established, its role in detecting ACS remains largely experimental. Animal studies have confirmed strong correlations between ICP and SWE in ACS models, highlighting SWE’s potential as a real-time, noninvasive tool for early ACS assessment [16, 19, 28–30]. However, clinical validation of SWE for ACS diagnosis in human subjects is currently lacking.

In summary, the anterior compartment of the lower leg is particularly susceptible to ACS due to its rigid osteofascial boundaries and frequent exposure to injury-related elevated pressures. Shear wave velocity (SWV) serves as the primary parameter in SWE, reliably quantifying tissue stiffness independent of density or anisotropy, thereby providing a sensitive indicator of biomechanical changes [31]. This study aims to explore the potential utility of SWV as a noninvasive, objective diagnostic tool for assessing anterior compartment ICP in patients with tibial plateau fractures.

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2024 to March 2025 at the Trauma Emergency Center of a tertiary hospital in China. We consecutively enrolled patients aged 18–65 years admitted within 48 h after tibial plateau fracture injury. Exclusion criteria included: (1) open fractures; (2) concurrent fractures in other locations or multiple injuries; (3) history of lower limb injuries or surgeries, musculoskeletal system diseases, or neurological system diseases; (4) presence of tension blisters or abrasions at the examination site; (5) inability to maintain a neutral leg position during examination; and (6) refusal to participate in the study. The required sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2) based on preliminary data, targeting an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.8, α = 0.05 (two-sided), and a statistical power of 0.90. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Ke 2023-107-1). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This study utilized SWV measured by SWE as the primary predictor variable, with ICP as the main outcome measure. Patients were divided into the high compartment pressure group (HCP group, ICP > 30 mmHg) and the non-high compartment pressure group (Non-HCP group, ICP ≤ 30 mmHg) [2, 4].

Clinical data

For patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria, demographic information, medical history, and biochemical test data were collected through the medical record system. Demographic characteristics included gender, age, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Medical history encompassed the Schatzker classification and injury mechanism. The Schatzker classification was categorized into two groups: Schatzker types Ⅰ-Ⅲ and Schatzker types Ⅳ-Ⅵ [32, 33]. Injury mechanisms included falling injury, smash injury, electric bicycle accident injury, and motor vehicle accident injury. Blood test results, obtained within 24 h of admission, included white blood cell count (WBC), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and potassium ions (K⁺), as these biomarkers have been shown to be associated with muscle injury [34–38].

Ultrasound examination

All ultrasound examinations were performed by sonographers with over 3 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasound, using the Aixplorer ultrasound system (Supersonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) equipped with an SL10-2 linear array probe (6–10 MHz) preset to “musculoskeletal (MSK)” parameters. The sonographer was blinded to the participants’ clinical history. Participants were positioned supine with both lower limbs in a naturally extended neutral position and muscles relaxed. The measurement site was located at the midpoint of the anterior compartment at the proximal 30% level along the line from the head of fibula to the tip of lateral malleolus (Fig. 1a) [39].

A generous amount of coupling gel was applied to the examination site to minimize near-field ghosting and enhance acoustic coupling, while avoiding probe pressure on the skin. Initially, the tibialis anterior muscle was used as the target muscle, grayscale ultrasound mode was used to obtain a long-axis section parallel to the muscle fibers, identifying the muscle site and fiber direction. The “general” default mode in the musculoskeletal setting was used, with depth and focus adjusted to suit the deep fibers of target muscle. After obtaining satisfactory two-dimensional grayscale images of tibialis anterior muscle, the system was switched to SWE mode, displaying both grayscale and SWE images simultaneously. The SWE color scale was set to 0-180 kPa, with colors ranging from blue (soft) to red (hard) based on Young’s modulus. A 2 × 2 cm sampling box was placed in the center of the anterior fascial compartment. After the image showed uniform color filling and stabilized for at least 5 s, the image was frozen, and three 4-mm diameter circular Q-Boxes were selected within the muscle area of the sampling box, avoiding large blood vessels and acoustic shadows. Shear wave velocity was automatically calculated in m/s (Fig. 1b and c). Measurements were repeated three times at intervals of at least 3 s, and the average was recorded.

Ultrasound examinations of both lower limbs were sequentially performed by two sonographers with equivalent qualifications, with consistent settings across all patients. All ultrasound images were reviewed by a senior sonographer with over 10 years of experience. SWV for both sides were recorded, and the SWV difference between the affected and unaffected sides was calculated.

Intracompartmental pressure measurement

Within 5 min of completing the ultrasound examination, ICP was measured using the Stryker Intra-Compartmental Pressure Monitor (STIC; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI), a widely accepted and commonly used device with validated accuracy in previous studies [40–42]. The measurement site was identical to that used for ultrasound examination and was performed by a rigorously trained physician who was blinded to the SWE results. After connecting the device to a disposable assembly, air was purged from the tubing. The selected site was disinfected, and the pressure monitor’s needle was positioned near the site, oriented toward the proximal limb at a 45° angle to the skin. The device was zeroed by pressing the reset button, and the needle was inserted into the muscle, followed by the injection of 0.3 ml saline. Once the reading stabilized, the ICP was recorded in mmHg.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n, %). For comparisons between groups, the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and chi-square/Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Effect sizes quantified significant group differences: Cohen’s d for normal data (≥ 0.20 small, ≥ 0.50 medium, ≥ 0.80 large), Cliff’s Delta for non-parametric data (≥ 0.15 small, ≥ 0.33 medium, ≥ 0.48 large) [43], and Cramér’s V for categorical variables (weak ≥ 0.10, moderate ≥ 0.30, strong ≥ 0.50) [44]. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to evaluate the consistency of SWV measurements within and between sonographers. To assess the absolute reliability of the SWV measurements, we additionally calculated the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimal detectable change at the 95% confidence level (MDC95). Spearman correlation coefficients was applied to assess associations between ultrasound features, pressure, and clinical indicators, with correlation strength categorized as weak (< 0.4), moderate (0.4–0.7), or strong (> 0.7). Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis or deemed clinically significant were included in a multivariable logistic regression model. Backward stepwise regression (removal criterion P > 0.05) was used to select variables and construct the final predictive model. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for the independent variables in the final model. The model’s ability to identify HCP was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The Youden index was calculated as sensitivity plus specificity to determine the optimal cutoff value. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.0, The R Foundation) and SPSS (IBM, SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.1, USA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study enrolled 170 patients with tibial plateau fractures (57.1% male, median age 45 years). The detailed flowchart of patient screening is presented in Fig. 2. Participants were stratified by ICP into HCP (n = 44) and Non-HCP (n = 126) groups. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The HCP group was significantly younger (median 43.0 vs. 47.0 years, P = 0.041, Cliff’s Delta = -0.21), with no differences in gender or BMI (both P > 0.78). Injury mechanisms differed significantly (P = 0.015, Cramér’s V = 0.25): electric bicycle collisions were more frequent in HCP patients (36.4% vs. 23.0%), while motor vehicle collisions predominated in Non-HCP patients (18.2% vs. 35.7%). Severe fractures (Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ) were more common in the HCP group (77.3% vs. 49.2%, P = 0.002, Cramér’s V = 0.23). Laboratory analysis showed higher CK in the HCP group (median 398.0 vs. 239.0 U/L, P = 0.012, Cliff’s Delta = 0.26). Ultrasound revealed significantly greater injury-side SWV (median 3.7 vs. 3.3 m/s, P < 0.001, Cliff’s Delta = 0.67) and SWV difference (mean 0.9 ± 0.4 vs. 0.6 ± 0.3 m/s, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.95) in HCP patients. Healthy-side SWV did not differ (P = 0.326).

The ICC for SWV measurements of injured-side muscles obtained by the same ultrasonographer was 0.973 (95% CI: 0.965–0.979). For measurements performed by two equally qualified sonographers, the ICC values were 0.914 (95% CI: 0.886–0.936). The absolute reliability analysis for injured-side SWV yielded an SEM of 0.048 m/s and an MDC95 of 0.133 m/s.

Correlations between clinical characteristics and SWV

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient revealed significant associations between SWV measurements and key clinical variables (Fig. 3). Notably, injury-side SWV demonstrated strong positive correlations with ICP (ρ = 0.704, P < 0.001) and CK (ρ = 0.261, P < 0.001), and weaker but significant correlations with LDH (ρ = 0.216, P = 0.005) and WBC (ρ = 0.164, P = 0.032). Similarly, SWV difference showed moderate-to-strong correlations with ICP (ρ = 0.535, P < 0.001) and CK (ρ = 0.277, P < 0.001), along with significant associations with LDH (ρ = 0.189, P = 0.013) and WBC (ρ = 0.173, P = 0.024). Healthy-side SWV showed no significant correlations with any clinical characteristics (all P > 0.05). Age, BMI, and K⁺ were not significantly associated with any SWV measurements (all P > 0.15). Validity of SWV screening for HCP

In univariate logistic regression, variables with P < 0.1 included injury-side SWV, SWV difference, age, smash injury, Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ classification, CK, and LDH. These features were subsequently entered into multivariable analysis. Stepwise regression identified three independent predictors of HCP (Table 2): injury-site SWV, age, and Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ fracture classification. Due to substantial scale differences between variables, injury-side SWV and age were standardized by dividing by their respective sample standard deviations (0.29 m/s for SWV and 11.6 years for age) for the final model. Thus, the adjusted odds ratios represent the change in odds per one standard deviation increase in each variable. Injury-side SWV demonstrated the strongest association with HCP (adjusted OR = 5.99, 95% CI: 3.42–11.65; P < 0.001). Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ fractures significantly increased HCP risk (aOR = 4.14, 95% CI: 1.63–11.58; P = 0.004), while older age was protective (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.37–0.98; P = 0.046). No multicollinearity was detected in the predictive model (VIFs ≤ 1.092). The model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.880 (95% CI: 0.826–0.935) (Fig. 5a). At the optimal cutoff (0.165), sensitivity was 93.2% and specificity was 71.4%. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed good calibration (χ² = 7.76, P = 0.457). Subgroup analyses showed consistent performance in both males (Fig. 5b) and females (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

This investigation pioneers the clinical validation of SWE for noninvasive evaluation of ICP and its diagnostic efficacy in detecting HCP in patients with tibial plateau fractures. Our results revealed robust positive associations between injury-side SWV or SWV differential and ICP, establishing SWV as a dependable noninvasive proxy for ICP monitoring. Moreover, SWV metrics exhibited substantial disparities between participants with ICP surpassing 30 mmHg and those without. Particularly compelling, a refined logistic regression model integrating injury-side SWV with clinical variables such as age and fracture type showed outstanding sensitivity and specificity for HCP identification, yielding a remarkable AUC.

The observed positive correlations between SWV and ICP align with the biomechanical principles underlying ACS, where elevated ICP induces progressive tissue alterations. Elevated ICP increases muscle stiffness through ischemia and edema, which collectively alter mechanical properties of muscle [16, 19]. This heightened stiffness accelerates shear wave propagation, as SWV directly reflects tissue elasticity, with faster velocities indicating greater rigidity independent of operator influence [23, 24]. Experimental evidence supports this mechanism, demonstrating strong associations between ICP and SWV in ACS models, where pressure-induced tension linearly elevates shear modulus [30, 31].

Our results are consistent with animal studies on SWE in ACS models, which uniformly demonstrated strong ICP-SWV correlations. For instance, Zhang et al. [45] reported r = 0.942 (P < 0.001) in rabbit compression models with histological necrosis, while Ren et al. [19] found r = 0.802 (P < 0.001) in turkey infusions, including post-fasciotomy declines. Ichiro et al. [29] showed R² = 0.91 with functional impairments, Xing et al. [30] ρ = 0.927 in rabbits, and Kang et al. [16] significant shear modulus increases (P < 0.001) in pigs, consistent with our positive associations (ρ = 0.704 for injury-side SWV). However, these controlled experimental settings differ from our clinical cohort by lacking human variability, such as comorbid factors (age-related changes, obesity, or preexisting conditions), soft tissue heterogeneity (variations in muscle composition and edema distribution), and physiological diversity (differences in injury severity and baseline perfusion). This real-world heterogeneity likely explains our moderately lower correlation coefficients, yet it enhances the clinical relevance of our findings by demonstrating SWE’s utility in diverse patient populations, bridging the gap from animal proof-of-concept to practical human application. Yuri et al. [46] showed strong SWV-pressure links (r > 0.80, P < 0.05) in healthy volunteers under cuff simulation, and Zhang et al. [10] noted elevated elasticity in suspected ACS cases necessitating fasciotomy, aligning with our SWV disparities between high and normal ICP groups. Compared to these conclusions based on several cases, we expanded the sample size and integrated clinical covariates (age, fracture type) into a multivariate model (AUC 0.880), thereby improving diagnostic accuracy beyond these preliminary human explorations, highlighting the critical value of clinical validation for translational applications.

Beyond the evaluation of ACS, the application scope of SWE has extended to various musculoskeletal disorders, highlighting its versatility in quantifying biomechanical changes. For instance, Liu et al. [47] reported elevated shear modulus in knee osteoarthritis, negatively correlating with functional scores, while Gao et al. [24] achieved high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.86) for neck somatic dysfunction via SWV. Similarly, An et al. [48] integrated SWV into a sarcopenia model with AUC 0.946, and Wei et al. [49] linked reduced SWE values to low muscle mass (AUC 0.800). These findings affirm SWE’s broad utility in reflecting pathological stiffness changes from inflammation, degeneration, or atrophy.

Our findings demonstrate that injury-side SWV, integrated into a multivariate model, enables early, noninvasive detection of HCP in tibial plateau fracture patients, potentially mitigating ACS complications. With an AUC of 0.880, the model facilitates effective risk stratification, while consistent performance across male and female subgroups ensures broad applicability. High intra- and inter-observer reliability (ICCs 0.914–0.973) and low MDC95 value (0.133 m/s) underscore SWV’s robustness and practicality for clinical use. Broader implications include incorporating SWE into emergency protocols to surmount limitations of invasive monitoring, such as pain and infection risks. In our cross-sectional cohort of 170 patients (57.1% male, median age 45 years), SWV strongly predicted HCP in high-risk groups like those with Schatzker Ⅳ-Ⅵ fractures (aOR 4.14). This cost-effective approach is particularly valuable in resource-limited settings, promoting timely interventions and reducing morbidity.

Despite these strengths, our study has limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding SWV and HCP progression. Conducted at a single center, results may lack generalizability. Exclusion of open fractures and polytrauma patients limits applicability to broader trauma scenarios. Reliance on a 30 mmHg ICP threshold is contentious in the literature, and the lack of long-term outcomes restricts comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, the limited availability of SWE equipment and trained operators in some trauma centers may hinder its routine use in all clinical settings. Furthermore, our SWE measurements focused exclusively on the deep fibers of the tibialis anterior muscle, which may limit insights into superficial fiber stiffness and overall muscle heterogeneity in pressure distribution. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate the model, assess longitudinal SWV changes, incorporate additional biomarkers, compare both layers of muscle, and test in diverse populations, such as other fracture types, alongside efforts to enhance SWE accessibility and training in resource-limited trauma centers to facilitate broader clinical adoption. Nevertheless, this work lays a foundational step toward noninvasive ACS diagnosis, offering a promising tool for early detection and improved patient outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study indicates that SWV, integrated into a multivariate model with age and fracture type, facilitates noninvasive detection of HCP in tibial plateau fracture patients. These findings suggest SWV’s potential for early ACS risk stratification, especially in high-risk groups, while surmounting the drawbacks of invasive monitoring. Despite inherent limitations, this work provides a foundational framework for noninvasive diagnostics, calling for prospective multicenter studies to validate and broaden its clinical application for improved patient outcomes.
