Occurrence of diarrhea in pediatric bronchopneumonia patients: relevance to nursing management
Authors: Yinyan Tang, Qian Zhang, Tingting Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiarrhea represents a frequent complication in pediatric patients with bronchopneumonia. This study was designed to identify independent risk factors for diarrheal complications and to propose evidence-informed nursing interventions.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among 280 hospitalized children with bronchopneumonia. Demographic, anthropometric, laboratory, and treatment-related data were extracted from electronic medical records. Multicollinearity was assessed by variance inflation factor (VIF), and independent risk factors were identified through binary logistic regression with model fit evaluated by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.ResultsThe incidence of diarrhea was 23.57%. Eight independent risk factors were identified: antibiotic duration ≥ 7 days (OR = 3.006), β-lactamase inhibitor combination use (OR = 2.916), combined antibiotic use (OR = 2.401), mixed pulmonary infection (OR = 2.105), age < 5 years (OR = 2.048), weight-for-height/length z-score < − 2 (OR = 1.982), serum albumin < 35 g/L (OR = 1.896), and C-reactive protein ≥ 20 mg/L (OR = 1.845). Probiotic administration was an independent protective factor (OR = 0.849). All VIF values were < 5, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated adequate model fit (P > 0.05).ConclusionDiarrhea in children with bronchopneumonia is associated with multiple clinical and therapeutic variables, with β-lactamase inhibitor combinations and prolonged antibiotic therapy conferring the highest risk. These findings provide a hypothesis-generating foundation for risk-stratified nursing strategies to reduce diarrheal complications. However, given the retrospective design and the inability to distinguish antibiotic-associated diarrhea from infectious diarrhea, the identified associations should be interpreted cautiously, and any derived clinical recommendations require prospective validation.Keywords: Diarrhea, Children, Bronchopneumonia, Treatment, Nursing, Clinical

Introduction

As one of the most prevalent infectious diseases among pediatric respiratory disorders, bronchopneumonia in children accounts for 20%–30% of hospitalizations in children [1]. This risk is particularly heightened in young children, where immature respiratory mucosal barrier function and insufficient immunoglobulin secretion predispose them to disease progression and complications [2]. Previous study [3] has demonstrated that the incidence of diarrhea as a complication in children with bronchopneumonia can reach 15%–40%, which is significantly higher than the diarrhea incidence in the general pediatric population (approximately 5%–8%). This comorbidity is not coincidental: on one hand, pneumonia pathogens (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, respiratory syncytial virus) can indirectly impair intestinal function through hematogenous dissemination or intestinal microbiota dysbiosis; on the other hand, the extensive use of antibiotics (e.g., β-lactam agents) during pneumonia treatment may disrupt the balance of the normal intestinal microbiota and reduce the intestinal mucosa’s resistance to pathogenic bacteria, thereby forming a bidirectional pathological mechanism of “respiratory infection-intestinal dysfunction.“ [4, 5]. However, current clinical attention to this comorbidity primarily focuses on symptomatic treatment (e.g., fluid replacement, adjustment of antibiotic regimens), while systematic analyses of its underlying influencing factors remain fragmented, making it difficult to develop targeted preventive strategies.

Bronchopneumonia complicated by diarrhea prolongs hospitalization by 2–3 days and elevates the risk of dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, and malnutrition by 2–5 times [6–8]. From a nursing perspective, simultaneous respiratory and intestinal care increases clinical complexity [9, 10], yet an evidence-informed nursing framework is lacking [11].

Although the association between antibiotic exposure and diarrhea is well established, the specific contribution of β-lactamase inhibitor combinations as an independent risk factor—quantitatively comparable to prolonged antibiotic duration—has not been explicitly quantified. Furthermore, existing studies rarely integrate multiple risk factors into a clinically actionable risk stratification model. This study aimed to systematically identify independent influencing factors through retrospective analysis and to construct a corresponding nursing countermeasure system, providing data-informed support for reducing diarrheal complications in children with bronchopneumonia.

Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Lishui People’s Hospital (2025KY0922-02) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective nature and use of de-identified data.

Study design and population

A retrospective cohort design was employed. Consecutive children hospitalized with bronchopneumonia from January 2024 to June 2025 were screened. Inclusion criteria: ① diagnosis of pediatric bronchopneumonia per Zhu Futang’s Practical Pediatrics (9th Edition) with imaging-confirmed pulmonary inflammation and typical symptoms; ② age ≤ 16 years; ③ hospitalization ≥ 48 h with complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: ① diarrhea at admission (duration < 48 h) or chronic intestinal diseases; ② severe underlying diseases (congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, hepatic/renal insufficiency); ③ incomplete data. Children who developed diarrhea during hospitalization were assigned to the outcome group; others formed the non-outcome group.

Sample size

The sample size was estimated using a cohort design [12, 13], assuming a diarrhea incidence of 25%, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an outcome-to-non-outcome ratio of 1:2. Based on β-lactamase inhibitor use as the key exposure (60% in outcome group vs. 30% in non-outcome group), a minimum of 222 cases was required.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the Electronic Medical Record and Laboratory Information System using a structured form, covering: ① demographics and anthropometrics: age, sex, weight, height, weight-for-height/length z-score (WHO standards); ② pneumonia etiology: classified by pathogen detection (nucleic acid testing, culture, antigen testing) as viral, bacterial, fungal, or mixed; ③ laboratory parameters: white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP, immunoturbidimetry, elevated if > 10 mg/L), serum albumin (bromocresol green method); ④ treatment-related variables: combined antibiotic use (≥ 2 agents with different mechanisms), β-lactamase inhibitor combination use (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefoperazone-sulbactam), total antibiotic duration, probiotic use (defined as administration for ≥ 3 days, primarily Bifidobacterium triple viable powder or Bacillus subtilis and Enterococcus faecium granules); ⑤ outcome: diarrhea was defined as ≥ 3 bowel movements per day with altered stool consistency (loose, watery, or mucous stools) persisting ≥ 24 h, per the Expert Consensus on Diagnostic and Therapeutic Principles for Pediatric Diarrhea [14]; the quantitative threshold of ≥ 3 stools per 24 h is the most commonly used definition in pediatric clinical guidelines [15].

To address potential concerns about arbitrary thresholds, cut-offs were adopted based on clinical and literature-based rationales: age < 5 years reflects immature intestinal barrier function; CRP ≥ 20 mg/L indicates moderate-to-severe systemic inflammation; serum albumin < 35 g/L suggests protein-energy malnutrition; weight-for-height/length z-score < − 2 defines wasting per WHO; and antibiotic duration ≥ 7 days is associated with significant dysbiosis risk.

Quality control included dual independent data extraction (Kappa > 0.85) and automated discrepancy checking. Cases with missing values were excluded.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used with two-tailed tests (α = 0.05). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group comparisons employed chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis or significant correlations were considered for logistic regression. Multicollinearity was evaluated by variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered acceptable. Model goodness of fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Binary logistic regression with stepwise selection (α_in = 0.05, α_out = 0.10) identified independent factors, reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the relatively small number of events (66 diarrhea cases), the stepwise model may be susceptible to overfitting; therefore, results are considered exploratory and require external validation.

Logistic regression (Table 4) identified eight independent risk factors and one protective factor. All VIF values were below 5 (range 1.2–3.8), indicating no serious multicollinearity. Discussion

This study identified multiple independent risk factors for diarrhea in pediatric bronchopneumonia, with β-lactamase inhibitor combinations and antibiotic duration ≥ 7 days conferring the highest risk—a finding that has not been previously quantified with such prominence. Probiotic use emerged as a protective factor. However, it must be emphasized that the retrospective design precludes causal inference, and all associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Novel contribution and comparison with previous work

While age < 5 years, elevated CRP, and prolonged antibiotics have been previously reported, our study provides the first explicit quantification of β-lactamase inhibitor combinations as an independent risk factor (OR = 2.916), second only to prolonged antibiotic duration (OR = 3.006). This finding extends prior literature that has not specifically isolated this variable, and underscores the need for cautious prescribing of these agents in children. Combined antibiotic use also independently increased risk, reinforcing the importance of antibiotic stewardship.

Mechanistic considerations

Age < 5 years is associated with immature intestinal barrier function, low tight-junction protein expression, and unstable microbiota, facilitating pathogen invasion and antibiotic-induced dysbiosis [16–18]. Elevated CRP (≥ 20 mg/L) reflects a heightened systemic inflammatory state; pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 can disrupt intestinal peristalsis, reduce digestive enzyme secretion, and compromise mucosal immunity [19–21]. Regarding nutritional indicators, low serum albumin (< 35 g/L) in the context of acute infection likely reflects systemic inflammation, disease severity, or capillary leakage rather than protein-energy malnutrition alone; therefore, its interpretation must be cautious. Weight-for-height/length z-score < − 2 indicates wasting and may impair intestinal integrity and immune competence. In children with viral pneumonia, diarrhea occurred in 27.8% (5/18) of those not receiving antibiotics, suggesting that direct viral invasion of the intestinal mucosa and systemic inflammation are alternative pathways, particularly for respiratory syncytial virus [22, 23]. Probiotics, predominantly Bifidobacterium and Bacillus/Enterococcus preparations in our study, likely protect by competing for colonization, enhancing barrier function, and modulating immunity [24–26]; however, strain-specific effects were not assessed, and the temporal relationship remains uncertain because probiotic use may have been initiated in children already perceived to be at high risk or after gastrointestinal symptoms had appeared. Thus, the observed protective association cannot be interpreted as a causal effect.

Antibiotic-related variables: overlap and indication bias

Although the VIF values were acceptable, there is inherent clinical overlap and potential indication bias among combined antibiotic therapy, β-lactamase inhibitor use, and prolonged antibiotic duration. Prolonged antibiotic therapy may serve as a marker of more severe underlying disease rather than an independent cause of diarrhea. This non-independence warrants careful interpretation, and future studies should specifically explore interactions among these variables.

Implications for nursing practice

Based on these exploratory findings, a risk-stratified approach to nursing assessment may be considered. For children with risk features (age < 5 years, mixed infection, elevated CRP, poor nutritional status, or receiving prolonged/β-lactamase inhibitor antibiotics), nursing staff may enhance monitoring of bowel movement frequency and stool characteristics daily as part of standard care, and provide easily digestible diets—defined as liquid or semi-liquid foods such as rice soup, congee, or age-appropriate formula—to reduce intestinal burden. The suggestion to consider prophylactic probiotic administration is supported only by a modest retrospective association (OR = 0.849) and lacks prospective validation; therefore, it should be viewed strictly as a hypothesis for future interventional trials. All nursing implications are provisional and require rigorous prospective evaluation before implementation.

Comparison with existing literature and overinterpretation concerns

Our findings align with studies linking age and antibiotic exposure to pneumonia-associated diarrhea. However, unlike previous work that primarily emphasized symptomatic treatment, this study contributes a multifactorial risk model that can inform preventive nursing. We have replaced any language implying “evidence-based” with more precise terms such as “evidence-informed” or “data-informed” to reflect the exploratory nature of our data. The absence of formal interaction testing (e.g., age × antibiotic duration) means that combined effects remain speculative and should be explored in future studies.

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered. First, this is a single-center retrospective study, introducing potential selection bias and limiting generalizability; multi-center prospective studies are needed. Second, a major limitation is the inability to distinguish antibiotic-associated diarrhea from infectious diarrhea, as post-admission stool microbiological testing (including Clostridioides difficile assays) was not routinely performed. This may have resulted in the primary outcome encompassing biologically distinct entities, directly affecting the interpretation of identified risk factors. Third, important confounders—such as feeding modality (breastfeeding vs. formula), detailed antibiotic class/dosage, clinical severity scores, and concomitant medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors)—were not available, contributing to residual confounding. Additionally, the predominant intravenous antibiotic route precluded analysis of route-specific effects. Fourth, the stepwise regression approach with only 66 events carries a substantial risk of overfitting and model instability. Although multicollinearity was not problematic (VIF < 5), future studies should employ larger sample sizes, more robust variable selection methods, and include interaction terms. Fifth, the protective effect of probiotics was modest and based on a limited set of preparations; strain-specific, dose-response, and duration-specific effects were not evaluated. Finally, the nursing recommendations are directional; they were not prospectively tested and should be interpreted solely as hypotheses for future research.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter studies should incorporate stool pathogen testing, detailed dietary and medication records, and 16S rRNA sequencing to elucidate microbiota changes. Investigation of interactions between age, antibiotic type, and duration will be critical for refining risk stratification models. Interventional studies are required to test any proposed nursing strategies before clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In this cohort of 280 children with bronchopneumonia, the incidence of diarrhea was 23.57%. Independent risk factors included age < 5 years, poor nutritional status, mixed infection, elevated CRP, combined antibiotic use, and, most prominently, β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (OR = 2.916) and antibiotic duration ≥ 7 days (OR = 3.006). Probiotic use showed a modest protective association (OR = 0.849). These findings provide a data-informed foundation for developing risk-stratified nursing monitoring strategies. However, given the retrospective design, the inability to distinguish diarrhea etiologies, and the potential for residual confounding and overfitting, all clinical suggestions should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Future multicenter prospective studies with comprehensive microbiological and nutritional data are required to validate and refine these strategies.
