Pathogenic potential of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from aquatic environment: a study of multidrug resistance and virulence
Authors: Vikar Ahmed, Mohammad Tahir Siddiqui, Asghar Ali, Syed Ahmed Rizvi, Mohammad Saif, Shayan Ahmed, Qazi Mohd Rizwanul Haq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmoxicillin is among the most frequently prescribed antibiotics globally, either as monotherapy or in combination with clavulanic acid as amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AMC). However, the prolonged use of AMC and other antibiotics has intensified selection pressure, accelerating the emergence of AMC-resistant and multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. Klebsiella, a member of the ESKAPE pathogens, employs diverse resistance mechanisms against multiple classes of antibiotics. This study was aimed to identify environmental Klebsiella isolates resistant to AMC with MDR phenotype and to investigate the underlying genetic determinants contributing to their resistance and virulence.MethodologyWater samples were collected from 14 sites, encompassing both wastewater and natural aquatic environments, and screened for AMC resistance on AMC supplemented Klebsiella-Selective agar base media. Antibiotic profiling of AMC resistant isolates was done by Kirby-Bauer’s disc diffusion test. Phenotypically positive MDR isolates were identified by MALDI-ToF MS. Furthermore, Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were selected for PCR based detection of antibiotic resistance and virulence factor associated genes using plasmid and genomic DNA as a template respectively. Horizontal gene transfer experiment was carried out using K. pneumoniae isolates as donor and plasmid-free and antibiotic sensitive Escherichia coli J53R strain as a recipient. Biofilm formation was detected by crystal violet assay and visualised in SEM. The hypermucoviscosity of K. pneumoniae (hmvKp) was confirmed by string test.ResultsOf the total 178 AMC resistant bacterial isolates, 119 displayed MDR phenotype. Among 63 putative AMC-resistant, MDR isolates exhibiting a non-metallic sheen on EMB agar, MALDI-TOF MS-based identification confirmed 33 to be Klebsiella pneumoniae. PCR based screening for resistance determinants revealed the presence of blaTEM (100%), blaSHV (75.75%), blaCTX−M (54.54%), blaNDM (27.27%), blaOXA−48 (39.39%), blaCMY (48.48%), qnrA (6.06%), qnrB (87.87%), qnrS (93.93%), tetA (81.81%), and tetB (27.27%), alongside sul1 (90.90%) and dfrA12 (9.09%) genes. Additionally, virulence-associated genes viz., fimH (33.33%), mrkD (78.78%), ecpA (54.54%), iucC (54.54%), and rmpA (6.06%) were also detected. Furthermore, biofilm formation assay demonstrated that 24 (72.72%) isolates were strong biofilm-formers, indicating their potential for pathogenicity.ConclusionOccurrence of hypervirulent, AMC resistant and MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae in aquatic environment is a concern and further studies are required to explore their potential threat in dissemination of resistance and clinical implications.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11806-5.Keywords: ESKAPE pathogens, K. pneumoniae, Virulence, Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, Aquatic environment, Multidrug resistance

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance is an urgent health concern, expected to cause 10 million deaths worldwide by the year 2050, if the current trend of overuse and misuse of antibiotics keeps on going [1]. The use of antibiotics in human healthcare, animal farming and agriculture selects for antibiotic resistance and therefore, a leading cause of emergence of antibiotic resistance. Antibiotic resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) are transmitted among humans, animals and the environment [2, 3].

The β-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems, are extensively prescribed drugs worldwide [4]. Amoxicillin is the most often used penicillin, both alone and with the β-lactamase inhibitor as amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [5]. Extensive antibiotic use, as well as long-term exposure of Klebsiella to the various class of antimicrobials has contributed to the development of multidrug-resistant strains [5]. Substantial amount of literature shows that Klebsiella exhibits a range of antibiotic resistance mechanisms against key accessible class of antibiotic drugs, including carbapenems, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and fosfomycin, resulting in therapeutic failure [6].

Klebsiella, a Gram-negative, lactose fermenting bacteria belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae is a member of ESKAPE pathogens viz. Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. It has wide ecological distribution in nature and can be detected from food, sewage, soil, plants and also forms gut microbiota of animals and humans [7]. Klebsiella is an opportunistic pathogen colonizing mucosal surfaces without causing any disease [8]. Moreover, it is also known to be the second most common cause of urinary tract infections (UTIs) after E. coli [9]. High frequency and diversity of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and numerous genetic factors are reported in Klebsiella that contribute to its ability to cause disease in human. Furthermore, Klebsiella is known to have a role in the transmission of drug resistance genes from environmental to clinically important bacteria [10]. The global resistance and the infection-related mortality rate of K. pneumoniae has reached 70%, and 40%~70% respectively [11].

The aquatic environment may play an important role in transmission of ARGs, since it is frequently affected by human activity. Surface waterways are a major source of drinkable water for human and animal usage. As a result, contaminated water can spread antimicrobial resistance and virulence factors in Klebsiella [12]. ARB and associated ARGs can be disseminated by routes such as use of antibiotics in healthcare, animal farming, agriculture as well as residual discharge of antibiotics in domestic and production waste [13].

Animals and humans cannot fully absorb and metabolize antibiotics, and ARGs developed in bacteria enter into WWTPs [14, 15]. As a result, WWTPs are hotspots for pathogens, antibiotics, and ARGs [16, 17]. The amounts of antibiotic residues are often well below therapeutic concentrations; they may still be high enough to exert selection pressure on bacteria that might evolve antibiotic resistance [18]. K. pneumoniae has been found to possess a number of virulence factors. The lipopolysaccharide layer and the prominent polysaccharide capsule that the majority of isolates express shield the bacteria from phagocytosis and the bactericidal effects of serum. Type 1 and Type 3 fimbriae are the two main fimbrial adhesion organelles produced by K. pneumoniae [19]. The ecpA gene is involved in biofilm formation, which aids the bacteria to colonise [20]. Moreover, the iucC gene encoding a siderophore aerobactin is highly expressed and plays a virulence role and acts as a virulence determinant during hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) infection [21]. K. pneumoniae can be classified as either hvKp or classical Klebsiella pneumoniae (cKp) based on virulence traits. Although cKp infections contribute to the majority of K. pneumoniae infections worldwide, cKP can developed into hvKp by acquiring virulence factors like plasmids [22].

There are number of reports on biofilms related ailments from Europe. According to National Institutes of Health (NIH) estimates, upto 75% of infectious diseases are caused by biofilms producing bacteria [23, 24]. K. pneumoniae has been identified as a potential biofilm producer [25]. The development of biofilm increases the resistance against antimicrobials and external stress and is significantly influenced by various virulence factors like capsule, fimbriae, pili, and O-lipopolysaccharide present in K. pneumoniae [23].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is increasingly recognised as a global health challenge that exceeds clinical boundaries and requires a One Health approach, acknowledging the interconnectedness of humans, animals, and the environment [24, 26]. K. pneumoniae circulates across these compartments, and the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains highlights the need for integrated surveillance and collaborative efforts among healthcare, veterinary, and environmental sectors. Investigating environmental isolates is therefore essential to understand resistance reservoirs, track dissemination pathways, and inform effective antimicrobial stewardship and control strategies.

India is among the largest consumers of antibiotics globally, and increasing evidence points to widespread antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in both clinical and community settings [27]. The Delhi-NCR region, with its dense population, rapid urbanization, and extensive discharge of untreated hospital and municipal wastewater into natural water bodies, represents a critical hotspot for the emergence and dissemination of resistant pathogens [28]. While national AMR surveillance programs provide data on clinical isolates, there is a paucity of studies examining environmental reservoirs of resistance in this region. Recent investigations have documented the presence of multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, including Klebsiella pneumoniae as well as carbapenemase genes (e.g., blaNDM) in sewage, river water, and hospital effluents in Delhi and other Indian cities [29, 30]. Understanding the burden of multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae in aquatic environments of Delhi-NCR is therefore essential to capture the environmental dimension of AMR within a One Health framework.

We hypothesised that K. pneumoniae strains from aquatic environments and wastewater in the Delhi-NCR region harbour significant AMC resistance genes and virulence determinants, thereby representing a potential public health risk within a One Health framework. This study therefore aimed to characterize AMC resistance, multidrug resistance patterns, and virulence genes in environmental K. pneumoniae isolates, and to evaluate their potential for horizontal gene transfer and biofilm formation.

Materials and methods

Isolation of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AMC) resistant bacteria

The water samples were collected from 14 different sites (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1), including wastewater and natural aquatic environment during January 2023-July 2024. Collected water samples were aseptically transported to the laboratory. Samples were serially diluted, spread on AMC (32/16 µg/mL) amended Klebsiella-Selective Agar base media (Hi-Media, India) plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in an incubator (Scigenics Biotech). Post incubation, the bacterial colonies exhibiting purple magenta colour were streaked on Luria Agar (LA) plates for purification.

Preliminary screening for AMC resistance and MDR through antibiotic susceptibility test (AST)

Pure colonies were inoculated into Luria Broth (LB) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in an incubator shaker at 120 rpm. A secondary culture with 0.5 McFarland was spread on Muller Hinton Agar (MHA) plates for antibiotic susceptibility test (AST) through Kirby-Bauer’s disc diffusion test. In order to screen isolates for multidrug resistance, different class of antibiotics (penicillin + inhibitor, third-generation cephalosporins, carbapenem, fluoroquinolones and antifolates) were used to screen multidrug resistant (MDR) phenotypes. The antibiotics used were amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AMC, 30 µg), cefotaxime (CTX,30 µg), ertapenem (ETP,10 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP,30 µg) and co-trimoxazole (COT,25 µg). After overnight incubation, the diameter of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was measured in millimetres (mm). The values recorded were compared with standard chart and isolates were designated resistant/intermediate/susceptible as per clinical laboratory standard institute (CLSI) guidelines-2023 (33rd edition M100) [31]. The isolates showing resistance for AMC and at least three other class of antibiotics were identified by Matrix Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization-Time of Flight Mass Spectroscopy (MALDI-ToF MS).

Identification of bacterial isolates

In order to exclude any possible contamination such as E. coli and other spp., selected isolates were further streaked on Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar plates. The isolates bearing mucoidal, pinkish-black and non-green metallic sheen phenotypes, suspected to be Klebsiella were selected for identification through MALDI-ToF MS at Dr. Lal Path Labs., New Delhi.

Phenotypic test for extended spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemase production

The K. pneumoniae isolates were further tested for ESBL production through Phenotypic Disc Diffusion Confirmatory Test (PDCT) as per CLSI guidelines-2023 (33rd edition M100) [31]. The test inoculum of 0.5 McFarland was spread on MHA plates using sterile glass beads. The test was performed using third-generation cephalosporins cefotaxime (CTX,30 µg) and ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg) with their β-lactamase inhibitor counterpart cefotaxime-clavulanic acid (CEC,30 + 10 µg) and ceftazidime-clavulanic acid (CAC,30 + 10 µg) respectively. The antibiotic discs were placed 30 mm apart from each other. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, the isolates displaying a difference of ≥ 5 mm for antibiotic + clavulanic acid and antibiotics alone were inferred as phenotypically ESBL positive. For carbapenemase production (CPE), disc diffusion method was used to assess the sensitivity of imipenem and ertapenem in accordance with the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST). Isolates having inhibitory zone widths of ≤ 19 mm for both imipenem (10 µg) ertapenem (10 µg) wereclassified as carbapenemase positive [32, 33]. For positive and negative control, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 and E. coli ATCC 25,922 were used respectively.

Antibiotic profiling

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of K. pneumoniae isolates against a wide range of antibiotics (HiMedia, India) was carried out through Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion test as per CLSI guidelines-2023 (33rd edition M100) [31]. A total 6 different, commonly used classes of antibiotics (penicillin, cephalosporins, monobactam, carbapenem, antifolate, fluoroquinolone, tetracycline, chloramphenicol and aminoglycoside) were selected. The antibiotic disc used were ampicillin (10 µg), ampicillin-sulbactam (A/S,10/10µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (PIT,100/10µg), ceftriaxone (CTR,30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ,30 µg), aztreonam (AT,30 µg), imipenem (IPM,10 µg), levofloxacin (LE,5 µg), tetracycline (TE,30 µg) chloramphenicol (C,30 µg), amikacin (AK,30 µg), streptomycin (S,10 µg) and gentamycin (GEN,10 µg). Fresh cultures were obtained by inoculating pure isolates into the LB by incubating at 37 °C in an automated incubator shaker at 120 rpm till 0.5 McFarland. A total 50 µl of the cultures was spread on MHA fresh plates and spread through sterile glass beads. The antibiotics discs were placed at an appropriate distance from each other and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 14–16 h. Post incubation, the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was measured in millimetres (mm) and the susceptibility was determined against the tested antibiotics. The test was performed in triplicates and the isolates were designated as resistant/intermediate resistant/susceptible. The standard control used was E. coli ATCC 25,922.

Multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index

MAR index was calculated as described by Krumperman (1983). The overall resistance of a single isolate towards 18 different tested antibiotics is represented as MAR Index. The MAR index value > 0.2 indicates the overuse of antimicrobial drug and high selection pressure in the source water body, whereas MAR index < 0.2 value indicates the low risk of source contamination.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

The MIC of different antibiotics was determined in a 96 well microtiter plate using broth dilution method as per CSLI guidelines-2023 (33rd edition M100) [31]. MIC of each antimicrobial agent was defined as the lowest concentration which inhibited visible growth of the organism. Three antibiotic classes viz., penicillin (AMC), third-generation cephalosporins (CTX, and CTR), carbapenem (ETP), fluoroquinolones (CIP), monobactam (AT) and antifolates (COT) were used to investigate the presence multidrug resistance. Luria Broth (LB) was used to culture the bacteria and the stock solutions of the antibiotics were prepared and loaded into the first wells of column of the microtiter plate. As stated earlier, serial dilutions (1024–2 µg/mL) of the antibiotics were made and inoculated with the 10 µl of 1000 times diluted 0.5 McFarland culture [34]. Microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 14–16 h and optical density (OD) was recorded in a Microplate Reader (Thermo Scientific Multiscan Go) at 600 nm. The experiment was performed in duplicates. E. coli ATCC 25,922 was used as standard control.

Molecular investigations of resistance and virulence genes among Klebsiella isolates

For molecular detection of drug resistance and virulence associated genes, plasmid and genomic DNA was extracted. Genomic DNA was isolated through Phenol: Chloroform: Isoamyl alcohol (PCI) method and plasmid DNA was extracted through Kit based method by using HiPurA Plasmid DNA Miniprep purification kit (Hi-Media MB508-50PR). The quality and concentration of extracted DNA was analysed through Nanodrop (Eppendorf BioPhotometer plus).

PCR based amplification of resistance genes

PCR based amplification of the genes encoding ESBLs as class A (blaCTX−M, blaTEM, blaSHV), and class C (blaCMY) and carbapenemases class A (blaKPC), class B (blaNDM, blaIMP), and class D (blaOXA−48) was carried out using plasmid DNA as template and gene specific primers (Table 1). Furthermore, antibiotic resistance genes conferring resistance to fluoroquinolone (plasmid mediated genes qnrA, qnrB, and qnrS), tetracycline (tetA, tetB), aminoglycosides (aac(3)-II), trimethoprim (dfrA12, dfrA17) and sulfamethoxazole (sul1) were also investigated. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) -integrons 1 and 2 (intl1 and intl2) facilitating the antibiotic resistance dissemination were also amplified using plasmid DNA as template and gene specific primers. PCR reaction was carried out in 25µL reaction volume, comprising 12.5µL of Master mix (Takara, Japan), 0.25µL (1µM) of primers (both reverse and forward), 0.5 µl (30 ng/µl) of template DNA and the final volume was adjusted by adding milli-Q water. The PCR cycling conditions for each reaction were standardised as initial denaturation (95 °C for 5 min) followed by 35 cycles of denaturation (94 °C for 1 min), annealing at respective temperature of each primer for 1 min, extension (72 °C for 1 min) and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The staining agent used was Ethidium Bromide. The amplified gene products were visualised on 1% agarose gel (Hi-Media, India) at 120 Volts/hr and photographed (BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA, USA). (Supplementary Table S2) shows the PCR conditions of all amplified resistance genes, MGEs and virulence genes.

PCR based amplification of virulence factor associated genes

Detection of virulence associated genes fimH, mrkD, rmpA, ecpA and iucC in MDR K. pneumoniae isolates was carried out using genomic DNA as template and gene specific primers (Table 1).

Transconjugation experiment

Five isolates of K. pneumoniae (Od-2, Jv-92, Sn-38, Sn-51 and Sn-72) harbouring maximum number of antibiotic resistance genes were selected as donors. A plasmid-free and antibiotic sensitive Escherichia coli J53R strain resistant to sodium azide (NaN3) was selected as a recipient. The secondary cultures of donor and recipient bacterial isolates were mixed in a 1:1 ratio in LB (Hi-Media, India) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h without any shaking [48]. The transconjugants were serially diluted and spread on LA plates containing AMC (32/16 µg/mL) and NaN3 (100 µg/mL) and selected for MIC studies and plasmid isolation. MIC test against five class of antibiotics viz. penicillin (AMC), third-generation cephalosporins (CTX, and CTR), carbapenem (ETP), fluoroquinolones (CIP) and monobactam (AT) against donors and transconjugants were determined to investigate the occurrence of drug resistance. PCR based amplification was done using transconjugant plasmid as template to confirm the presence of antibiotic resistance genes.

Determination of biofilm formation

Crystal violet assay (CV) was used to study the biofilm formation. Secondary culture and Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) media in 1:1 ratio was added in 96 well plate and incubated overnight. The planktonic cells were discarded and methanol was added to the wells and left at room temperature for 15–30 min. The wells were washed thrice with 200 µL of 1X PBS (phosphate buffer saline). 200 µL of 0.1% crystal violet was added to the wells and incubated for 30 min. Thereafter, the wells were rewashed thrice with 1X PBS and air-dried for 20 min. For quantification of biofilm, OD was recorded at 590 nm in microplate reader (TECAN Infinite M200, Switzerland) after dissolving in 200 µL of 95% ethanol. The experiment was carried out in triplicates and the results were averaged. The strength of biofilm formation was calculated as per the formula: OD ≤ ODc (non-biofilm former), ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc (weak), 2×ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc (moderate) and 4 × ODc < OD (strong biofilm former) as previously described [49]. ODc = average OD of negative control + 3x standard deviation (SD) of negative control. Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC19606 was used as positive control.

Biofilm visualization through scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The biofilm was visualised through SEM. For SEM study, the strong biofilm former isolate (Jv-92) was selected. The fresh culture (0.5 McFarland) was mixed with TSB media into the ratio of 1:1 in a 12- well sterile plate. A sterile glass cover slip was placed in the centre of the well to colonize the biofilm forming bacteria. The culture was incubated at 37 °C without any shaking for 36 h. Post incubation, the planktonic cells were washed with sterile 1% phosphate buffer saline (PBS). The fixing of biofilm was performed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for 3 h. The extra fixative was rinsed thrice with buffer, followed by gradual dehydration with 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100% ethanol for 10 min each. Finally, the glass cover slips were air dried overnight. Biofilm was visualised through Scanning Electron Microscope (Thermo Scientific Apreo 2 S) at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi.

Mucoviscosity assay for hypervirulent phenotypes

The hypermucoviscosity of K. pneumoniae (hmvKp) was confirmed by string test as suggested by earlier study [50]. After stretching with a loop, the strings of ≥ 5 mm were labelled as hypermucoviscous phenotypes [50]. A string test that showed hypermucoviscosity and the presence of the siderophore aerobactin producing gene iucC suggested hypervirulence [51].

Statistical analysis

To assess whether the distribution of detected resistance genes deviated from the expected uniform frequency, we applied a chi-square goodness-of-fit test. The expected frequency was calculated and set at 15 for each gene category. Gene-wise contributions to the overall χ² statistic were also determined. The test was performed using MS Office Excel 365 (Microsoft, USA).

Results

Initial screening and identification of bacterial isolates

Of the total 178 AMC resistant isolates, 119 (67%) isolates displayed AMC and MDR phenotype. Upon counterselection on EMB agar, 63 presumptive Klebsiella were processed for identification.

Among selected isolates, 33 (52%), 17 (27%), 7 (11%) and 3 (5%) isolates were found to be Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, Citrobacter freundii and Enterobacter hormaechei respectively. One isolate each of Enterobacter bugendensis, Enterobacter kobii and Hafnia alvei were also detected.

Antibiotic profiling of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates

Antibiotic profiling of K. pneumoniae isolates showed that after amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (since, it was used in initial screening), ampicillin showed 100% (n = 33) resistance, followed by ampicillin-sulbactam, cefotaxime, ceftazidime 97% (n = 32), ciprofloxacin 94% (n = 31), ceftriaxone, levofloxacin 82% (n = 27), ertapenem 79% (n = 26), co-trimoxazole 73% (n = 24), aztreonam 67% (n = 22), imipenem 58% (n = 19), amikacin 45% (n = 15), tetracycline 39% (n = 13), gentamycin 36% (n = 12) and, both streptomycin and chloramphenicol as 12% (n = 4) (Figs. 2 and 3).

Heatmap showing ESBL, carbapenemase production, antibiotic profiling, MAR index and MIC values of K. pneumoniae isolates

Phenotypic test for ESBL and carbapenemase production

The PDCT of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates confirmed, 20 out of 33 isolates (61%) were ESBL producers. Using discs of ETP and IPM, 21 (64%) isolates displaying zone of inhibition ≤ 19 mm for both ETP and IPM were classified as carbapenemase positive (CPE) (Fig. 3).

MAR index calculation

MAR index of different bacteria was in the range of 0.94–0.27. Majority (85%) of the tested isolates showed MAR index value ≥ 0.5. One isolate (Oh-7) and three others (Va-24, Sn-39, and Sn-51) exhibited high MAR index values of 1 and 0.94, indicating resistance to 100% and 94% of the tested antibiotics, respectively (Fig. 3). Overall, 91% (n = 30) displayed MAR index values ≥ 0.5, suggesting widespread resistance across multiple antibiotic classes. These findings indicate a significant level of contamination of highly resistant strains in environmental settings.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

MIC of different drugs varied between 2 to ≥ 1024 µg/mL. Higher MIC values were recorded for AMC, CTX and COT. Ten isolates were found to tolerate 64 µg/mL or higher concentration of all the tested antibiotics. However, the antibiotics CIP, AT, ETP and CTR displayed relatively lower MIC values. The study demonstrated that 11, 11, 10 and 6 isolates were able to tolerate ≤ 32 µg/mL of CIP, AT, ETP and CTR respectively (Fig. 3). The heatmap was generated using MS Office Excel 365 (Microsoft, USA).

Molecular detection of drug resistance genes

Detection of ESBL and carbapenem resistance genes

The ESBL genes (blaCTX−M, blaTEM and blaSHV) were detected using specific primers and plasmid DNA as template. blaTEM was detected in all isolates. However, blaSHV and blaCTX−M were detected in 76% (n = 25) and 55% (n = 18) isolates respectively (Fig. 4). There were 45% (n = 15) isolates harbouring all the ESBLs (blaCTX−M + blaTEM + blaSHV) genes. blaNDM, and blaOXA−48, were detected in 27% (n = 9) and 39% (n = 13) isolates respectively. blaCMY was detected among 48% (n = 16) isolates and 15% (n = 5) isolates were found to harbour all the β-lactamase (blaCTX−M + blaTEM + blaSHV + blaNDM + blaOXA−48 + blaCMY) encoding genes (Fig. 5A).

Detection of fluoroquinolone, tetracycline and antifolate resistance genes

Among plasmid-mediated fluoroquinolone resistance (qnrA, qnrB and qnrS) genes, the qnrS was most prevalent in 31 (94%), followed by qnrB in 88% (n = 29) and qnrA in 6% (n = 2) (Fig. 4). The combinations (qnrB + qnrS) were detected among 82% (n = 27) and (qnrA + qnrB + qnrS) were seen in 6% (n = 2) isolates. The tetracycline resistance gene tetA was amplified in 82% (n = 27) and tetB in 24% (n = 8) and there were 18% (n = 6) isolates harbouring (tetA + tetB) combinations. Among antifolates genes, the sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim genes were detected as sul1 and dfrA12 in 91% (n = 30) and 9% (n = 3) isolates respectively and 9% (n = 3) isolates harboured (sul1 + dfrA12) combinations (Fig. 5A). Amplification of mobile genetic elements (MGEs)

To investigate the potential for gene transfer, the existence of mobile genetic elements- integrons was assessed among the Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates. Remarkably, class 1 integron (intI1) was detected in 100% (n = 33) isolates indicating its ubiquitous distribution in the studied environmental strains. Class 2 integron (intI2) was identified in 21% (n = 7), all of which also harboured intI1, demonstrating a co-occurrence rate of 21% (Figs. 4 and 5B).

Amplification of virulence factor encoding genes

The most prevalent virulence associated gene detected was mrkD 79% (n = 26) followed by ecpA and iucC 55% (n = 18), fimH 33% (n = 11) with least frequent rmpA l6% (n = 2) (Fig. 4). 15% (n = 5) isolates co-harboured (fimH + ecpA + iucC + mrkD) (Fig. 5C). Two isolates possessing rmpA gene were positive for string test and strong biofilm forming phenotypes. A total of 42% (n = 14) isolates harbouring iucC gene and positive for string test were labelled as hvKp phenotypes and 58% (n = 19) negative for either iucC or string test or both as cKp based on earlier study [46].

The observed distribution of genes demonstrated significant variations relative to the expected uniform distribution (χ² = 217.07, df = 23, p < 0.0001). This indicates that certain determinants were more common while others were less common. The examination of the χ² statistic at the gene level revealed that a limited subset of genes accounted for the predominant observed deviation. The most important positive contributors were blaTEM (21.6), intl1 (21.6), qnrS (17.1), sul1 (15.0), blaIMP (15.0), blaKPC (15.0), dfrA17 (15.0), and aac(3)-II (15.0). On the other hand, qnrA (11.3), rmpA (11.3), and dfrA12 (9.6) were all important negative contributors. The combined analysis of these genes constituted the predominant portion of the χ² deviance, indicating the existence of non-random occurrence patterns.

Determination of biofilm formation and hypervirulence

In our study, 64% (n = 21), 15% (n = 5), and 12% (n = 4) isolates displayed strong, moderate and weak biofilm formation respectively. However, 9% (n = 3) were non biofilm formers. A total of 79% (n = 26) isolates were positive for hypermucoviscosity in string test (Fig. 4).

Transconjugation experiment

Successful transconjugation was performed and high frequency of transconjugation was obtained. All the transconjugants showed a significant increase in MIC values indicating the acquisition of resistance. The MIC values of the transconjugant of Od- 2 and Jv-92 were increased for AMC and CTR equal to their donor counterpart. Presence of resistance genes among transconjugants was confirmed through PCR based amplification. The isolates J1, J2, J3, J4 and J5 are the respective recipient counterparts of the donor isolates Od-2, Jv-92, Sn-38, Sn-51 and Sn-72 respectively.

We observed an increase in the MIC values of recipient isolates in conjugation experiment and some of the isolates attained the MIC values even equal to their donor counterparts. Furthermore, the distribution of resistance genes among recipients was investigated through PCR based amplification. A successful transfer of resistance determinants from donor to recipient isolates was seen. The transfer rate of genes blaTEM and intl1was seen to be 100%. However, the gene qnrB present in donor isolates was not seen among recipient isolates. Discussion

Klebsiella pneumoniae thrives in aquatic environments [52]. Antibiotic residues found in WWTPs and hospital effluents increase the selection pressure, which causes common aquatic bacteria to become resistant through a variety of transfer mechanisms. Apart from hospital wastewater, the resistant bacteria also proliferate in urban water, municipal wastewater, aquaculture systems, and agricultural settings [53]. MDR K. pneumoniae has also been identified from sewage plants [54] and raw wastewater [55].

In the present study, we investigated the presence AMC and multidrug resistance and virulence associated genes among environmental isolates of K. pneumoniae from Delhi NCR. Our investigation demonstrated that all (n = 33) AMC and MDR K. pneumoniae strains displayed 100% resistance to ampicillin (AMP) and 97% resistant to ampicillin-sulbactam (A/S). These results correlate with earlier reports showing resistance among K. pneumoniae isolates. For instance, a report from a poultry farm showed 96.6% and 55.9% resistance to AMP and A/S [56]. The intrinsic resistance of K. pneumoniae to AMP is well-defined and is primarily connected with the chromosomal based resistance of a penicillinase (blaSHV−1), which allows K. pneumoniae to hydrolyse penicillin [10]. The increased degree of A/S resistance found in our study implies that selective pressure or bacterial strains vary in clinical and environmental settings.

We observed higher resistance to third generation cephalosporins as 97% of the isolates showed resistance to CTX and CAZ. Similar results were reported by [57], where a significant degree of CAZ resistance in K. pneumoniae isolated from a river in Portugal, pointing attention to the alarming issue of cephalosporin resistance worldwide. Furthermore, we found 94% of K. pneumoniae isolates to be resistant to CIP, 79% to ETP and PIT, 58% to IMP, and 36% to GEN. The findings pointed to a significant rise in the prevalence of multidrug resistance, especially to fluoroquinolones, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and carbapenems. In comparison, a study by Savin et al. on Klebsiella from slaughterhouse and municipal wastewater, reported a high resistance for CIP (96.4%), ETP (96.7%), PIT (50%), GEN (23.3%) and IMP (16.7%) [58]. Even though the resistance to CIP was in the same range in both studies, our study observed greater resistance to IPM which indicates emergence and spread of carbapenemase producing Klebsiella strains in Delhi NCR. We observed 60.60% of isolates to be ESBL producers in phenotypic testing. However, a previous study carried out on Klebsiella from Yamuna River reported only ⁓30% isolates to be ESBL producers [59]. In carbapenemase production test, we observed 64% of isolates were positive. In a phenotypic testing for carbapenemase production on the Enterobacterales from a clinical origin reported 18% isolates to be resistant, out of them, the highest 24% were K. pneumoniae [60].

PCR based amplification of resistance genes, demonstrated that blaTEM was the most prevalent (100%) gene followed by blaSHV (75.75%) and blaCTX−M (54.54%). In an earlier study ESBL producing K. pneumoniae isolates from animal, human and environment were reported to harbour both blaTEM and blaSHV in all environmental isolates (100%). However, human samples showed blaTEM and blaSHV in 85% and 81% isolates respectively [61]. A study conducted on the ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae from recreational and hospital wastewater reported that both blaTEM and blaSHV were the most frequently detected co-existing genes, while blaCTX−M was exclusive to E. coli [62].We observed blaNDM, and blaOXA−48 among 27.27% and 39.39% environmental isolates respectively. A sewage based study reported blaOXA−48−like as most prevalent (55%) [63]. An other study from a treatment plant from Delhi, reported 71% of blaNDM−1 [30]. Lamba et al. reported blaNDM−1 as a commonly found gene in hospital effluents from Delhi, India [29]. These findings indicate wide distribution of β-lactamase encoding genes in various environmental settings. In this work, we found the highest incidences of qnrS (94%), followed by qnrB (88%) and qnrA (6%), indicating an alarming rate of rise in fluoroquinolones resistance. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that 45% and 36% of isolates, had qnrB and qnrS coexisting with ESBLs. Co-occurrence of antibiotic resistance genes from multiple classes make treatment more cumbersome and possess a serious threat to public health [64]. Additionally, among antifolates sul1 and dfrA12 were found to be present among 90.90% and 9.09% isolates respectively. This further demonstrates the variety of resistance strategies against almost all the key accessible class of antibiotics adopted by K. pneumoniae. We evaluated the presence of MGEs and observed intI1 in 100% and intl2 in 21% along with the co-exitance of intI1 + intI2 in 21% of the isolates. Our findings are similar with previous studies identifying several classes of integron class as key players in transmission of ARGs [65].The transferability of multidrug resistance determinants was successfully performed through conjugation experiment. A successful transfer of resistance determinants from donor to recipient isolates was observed. The transfer rate of genes blaTEM and intl1was 100%. However, the gene qnrB present in donor isolates was not observed among recipient isolates. This conjugation experiment’s outcome raises the prospect that different bacteria may pass on resistance genes under environmental conditions. The likelihood that infections may develop resistance may be greatly increased by the transmission of resistance determinants in the aquatic environment [66]. Type 1 and Type 3 are the two primary kinds of fimbriae expressed by K. pneumoniae. Fimbriae serve as adhesins, encouraging adherence to abiotic surfaces, such as medical equipment, where bacteria build biofilm, as well as biological surfaces with subsequent tissue invasion [67]. Our investigation of virulence associated genes found mrkD (78.78%) as the most prevalent gene followed by ecpA and iucC (54.54%) each, fimH (33.33%) with least frequent being rmpA (6.06%). A similar work was carried out on clinical K. pneumoniae and reported fimH-1, iucA and rmpA genes among 94%, 37% and 5% respectively [68]. In a study carried out on 50 clinical isolates, fimH was reported among (100%) followed by mrkD (86%) the isolates [69]. Similarly, a Brazilian study on MDR K. pneumoniae from Intensive Care Unit reported mrkD (96%) and fimH-1 (88%) [6]. The higher incidences of mrkD among bacteria from different origin underscores the significance in mediating the biofilm formation. The variability in the prevalence of fimH may reflect the differences in the strain specificity, geographical distribution. We noticed 83.33% of the iucC harbouring isolates were strong biofilm formers. Two isolates possessing rmpA gene were positive in string test and strong biofilm forming phenotypes. The isolates were considered hvKp as per the criteria described in a previous study [68]. A total of 14 (42%) isolates harbouring iucC gene and positive in string test were considered as hvKp phenotypes. The chi-square analysis revealed that the distribution of resistance genes among the investigated bacterial isolates is not random, it is shaped by selective pressure such as antibiotic use and environmental exposure. Significant over-representation was seen in notable genes like blaTEM, intl1, qnrS, and sul1, highlighting their established roles in horizontal gene transfer, environmental persistence, and co-selection driven by frequently used antibiotics. For instance, intl1 is a sign of pollution caused by people and helps bacteria become resistant to many drugs [70]. On the other hand, blaTEM is still one of the most common β-lactamases in the world [61]. Conversely, clinically relevant genes like as blaKPC, blaIMP, dfrA17, and aac(3)-II were rarely detected, suggesting potential localised selective pressures, confined dissemination, or fitness costs linked to environmental strains. The negligible presence of virulence factor like rmpA underscores the ecological barriers that restrict their survival beyond host settings. The χ² decomposition underscores distinct gene-specific contributions that affect the organisation of the resistome and virulome in the studied K. pneumoniae isolates.

The biofilm forming ability shields Klebsiella from various stress factors such temperature variations, pH swings, food availability, and fluid dynamics, making it a crucial survival strategy [71]. By enhancing bacterial survival, persistence and antibiotic tolerance, biofilms make infections more challenging to treat [72]. We found 63.63%, 15.15% and 12.12% were strong, moderate and weak biofilm formers respectively. This heterogeneity reflects strain-specific differences in the underlying genetic and regulatory mechanisms of biofilm development [73]. As per Araújo et al. 100% of the Klebsiella isolates from surface waters of Northern Portugal were biofilms producers [74]. Conversely, Seifi et al. found K. pneumoniae isolates from animal food sources, only 15%, 40% and 30% produced strong, moderate and weak biofilms, and 15% did not create any biofilms [75]. A study on wound associated 130 K. pneumoniae isolates, showed 80% to be strong biofilm formers suggesting a great impact on health [76]. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) estimates that up to 75% of infectious diseases are caused by bacterial biofilms, including tissue associated infections and implant related infections [77]. Furthermore, the SEM analysis revealed K. pneumoniae (Jv-92) to be a strong biofilm former. The visible thick cell aggregates assist in the development of resistance and virulence. Through SEM analysis, a study from United Kingdom reported that K. pneumoniae formed a mature dry surface biofilm and bacteria remained viable up to four weeks, although the culturability was low [78]. Biofilm maturation correlates with increasing matrix density, as evidenced by the progression from isolated cells to tightly packed biofilm embedded within extra cellular polymeric substances matrices. SEM imaging effectively distinguishes different stages of biofilm formation, emphasizing changes in spatial cell arrangement and extracellular material. Although, biofilms formed by clinical isolates are typically more robust, homogeneous, and resistant, while environmental isolates display more dynamic and variable structures [79]. Biofilms are responsible for 8.9 million healthcare-associated ailments annually in the European Union and European Economic Area [22]. The above findings indicate the biofilm producer K. pneumoniae are prevalent not only in clinical settings, but also in aquatic environment posing a significant threat as they may act as persistent reservoir of biofilm former strains and potential pathogens, underscoring the need for careful environmental surveillances and antibiofilm therapeutic approaches.

The detection of MDR K. pneumoniae carrying virulence genes and capable of transconjugation in environmental samples underscores the role of the environment as a critical AMR reservoir. Our findings reinforce the One Health perspective, where resistant K. pneumoniae can circulate between humans, animals, and ecosystems, amplifying transmission risks [80, 81]. Evidence from wastewater and surface water studies further highlights the environmental dissemination of mobile resistance determinants, strengthening the need for integrated surveillance and control strategies across sectors [82, 83]. Our results emphasize that importance of One Health approach in addressing MDR K. pneumoniae to safeguard public and environmental health.

Limitations and future directions

It is critical to acknowledge the limitations of the study, it was a cross-sectional study and was based on a modest number of isolates, which may not fully capture the complete diversity of the region. Resistance profiling relied on PCR-based detection, which, while robust for identifying targeted genes, might not resolve their genomic context or plasmid diversity. Future research should concentrate on molecular epidemiology such as whole genome sequencing, plasmid typing and conjugation mapping, longitudinal studies to monitor persistence, functional assays for virulence (In vivo models or cell lines) and metagenomics to study the microbial community dynamics and resistome profiling which can provide detailed insights into genetic diversity of K. pneumoniae in different environments.

Conclusion

The present study revealed a significant incidence of K. pneumoniae with the AMC and MDR and virulent, biofilm former phenotypes in waste water and natural aquatic environment in Delhi, NCR. The presence of ESBL determinants and other multidrug resistance genes in coexistence with virulence associated genes suggest that these sites are potent reservoir of antibiotic resistant and potential pathogenic K. pneumoniae, posing a threat to public health. The study also emphasizes the critical need of monitoring the use of various antimicrobials, which aids in the establishment of selection pressure that contributes to the rise in resistance. In view of our findings, we can infer that healthcare professionals should adopt standard operating procedures before discharging hospital effluents directly into the environment which may be helpful to limit the global burden of antimicrobial resistance. To the best of our knowledge this is the first report on genetic mechanisms of AMC resistance in waterborne K. pneumoniae from India. Additional research is needed to evaluate their occurrence in different environments.
