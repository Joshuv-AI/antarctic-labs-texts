Mental Health Literacy and Associated Factors in Ethnic Minority Border Regions of Guangxi, China: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jun-Lin Zhu, Hong-Heng Li, Yu Chen, Fan-Pei Ye, A-Jun Ma, Zhi-Hua Li, Jin-Pei Li, Yu-Xin Yang, Sara Sulieman, Si-Yuan Liu, Yao-Ming Tian, Meng-Kai Xie, Peng Cui, Hong-Ye Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to assess mental health literacy (MHL) in Guangxi, a border province in China with one of the largest ethnic minority populations, and to analyze its influencing factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used a multistage stratified cluster sampling approach to conduct face-to-face interviews with 9,744 residents aged 18 years and older in Guangxi. Guided by the socio-ecological model (SEM), we evaluated individual, interpersonal, community, and structural variables. Binary logistic regression and optimal scaling regression were used to identify independent factors associated with MHL and their relative importance.ResultsOf the 9,444 residents included, the overall MHL attainment rate was 12.7%; attainment in the mental health knowledge dimension was 16.6%. Recognition rates were 27.4% for depression and 46.2% for social anxiety disorder. MHL scores were significantly lower in rural ethnic minority areas than in urban areas (P < 0.05). Regression analyses showed that older age, being from the Dong ethnic group, lower education, a history of psychological treatment, and poor self-reported health were risk factors for low MHL. In contrast, male gender, employment in healthcare or technical professions, higher income, and daily internet use of less than three hours were protective factors.ConclusionMHL levels in Guangxi are suboptimal, particularly for recognizing depression and social anxiety disorder, with insufficient mental health knowledge emerging as a key barrier. Digital access and socio-economic advantages play a pivotal role in enhancing MHL. Strengthening mental health education in ethnic minority regions through culturally tailored, internet-based interventions and prioritizing high-risk groups, such as older adults, individuals with lower education or income, and those in rural Dong communities, may help reduce mental health inequalities.Keywords: mental health literacy, socio-ecological model, ethnic minorities, mental health knowledge, influencing factors, Guangxi, China

Introduction

Mental health issues are widespread globally, with approximately one in eight individuals affected by mental disorders. During the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of anxiety and depressive disorders increased by more than 25%.1 In recent years, adverse events such as the refugee crisis, public health emergencies, and the intensifying climate crisis have gradually posed significant threats to mental health systems.2–4 These interconnected risk factors not only increase the risks of mental health problems but also place additional strain on mental health systems. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study revealed that mental disorders remain among the top ten leading causes of the global disease burden, with no evidence of a decline in the burden since 1990.5 While structural barriers exist, enhancing individual awareness is essential for improving service utilization. Therefore, mental health literacy (MHL) has emerged as a crucial target for intervention, serving as a foundational prerequisite for bridging the gap between symptom onset and professional care.6

Jorm and colleagues first introduced the concept of MHL as an extension of health literacy.7 MHL refers to the knowledge and beliefs that enable individuals to recognize, manage, and prevent mental disorders. A study conducted in Iran demonstrated a significant positive correlation between MHL and quality of life.8 Higher levels of MHL are associated with a reduction in stigma and social distance related to mental illness,9 improved self-awareness of mental health and greater confidence in providing mental health first aid when supporting peers with mental health challenges.10 Previous studies in countries such as Switzerland11 and South Korea12 have extensively utilized tools like the HLS-EU-Q47 to assess MHL, consistently revealing suboptimal levels across diverse populations. However, mere assessment is no longer sufficient. To address these persistent deficits, the research focus has recently shifted towards two critical dimensions. First, there is a growing emphasis on digital health literacy, recognizing that digital platforms have become a primary source for health information seeking, especially in the post-pandemic era.13 Second, there is a paradigm shift from individual-level analysis to structural and social determinants, positing that mental health outcomes are deeply embedded in community contexts.14 These evolving frontiers underscore the necessity of adopting multi-dimensional frameworks to understand MHL comprehensively.

Recent epidemiological studies on mental disorders in China have revealed that the 12-month prevalence of all mental disorders (excluding dementia) is 9.3%, while the lifetime prevalence is 16.6%.15 These results underscore the significant prevalence of mental disorders in China in recent years. In 2019, the Chinese government incorporated the “Mental Health Promotion Action” into the 15 major initiatives outlined in the “Healthy China Action Plan (2019–2030)” and designated MHL as the first key outcome indicator.16 These findings highlight the significant theoretical and practical value of studying MHL.

Past studies have indicated that the overall health literacy of ethnic minority groups in remote areas of China is far from satisfactory, and low health literacy contributes to inefficient self-health management.17 Ethnic minorities are more susceptible to depressive disorders18 and common mental health disorders in these groups tend to be more persistent.19 The utilization of mental health services among ethnic minorities with poor mental health is notably low,20 often falling below that of the majority ethnic groups.21 These patterns exacerbate regional disparities in mental health issues. Moreover, ethnic minority groups in Guangxi, particularly the elderly, exhibit a higher prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms.22 These findings underscore the urgent need for increased attention to mental health interventions and education in this region.

Situated in Southern China, the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region is home to more than ten million people from ethnic minority groups, accounting for 37.6% of the region’s total population.23 It has the largest ethnic minority population among all of China’s provinces. Home to 12 recognized ethnic minorities, Guangxi faces specific challenges typical of border regions, including a shortage of professional mental health services and relative economic underdevelopment. These structural constraints, combined with significant linguistic diversity, make it a representative case for studying culturally mediated barriers to MHL. Geographically adjacent to Southeast Asian nations, Guangxi shares similar socio-cultural environments with the region. Research in neighboring countries, such as Vietnam, has indicated that MHL is often compromised by limited resources and social stigma,24 highlighting the need to investigate these factors in Guangxi.

Unlike approaches that focus solely on individual characteristics, the socio-ecological model (SEM) theory emphasizes the dynamic interaction between individuals and their environments. Salihu et al highlighted that the SEM is particularly effective for identifying barriers in minority and underserved communities by categorizing factors into multiple levels.25 Applying this framework allows for a holistic examination of how individual demographics, interpersonal connections, community contexts, and broader structural factors in border regions interact to shape MHL.

However, few studies have comprehensively examined the multi-level determinants of MHL using an SEM in ethnic minority border regions of China, where digital disparities and cultural barriers coexist. This study seeks to bridge this gap by assessing MHL levels among the residents of Guangxi and identifying key socio-demographic, behavioral, and cultural factors influencing MHL. By integrating socio-ecological theory with empirical data from Guangxi, this study contributes to both theory-building and the development of culturally adapted public mental health strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study, based on the primary indicators of the Mental Health Promotion Action in Guangxi, was conducted between September and December 2021. Participants were selected based on the following criteria: Inclusion criteria: (1) Residents aged 18 years and older; (2) Individuals who had lived in the region for at least six months. Exclusion criteria: (1) Individuals unable to communicate effectively or complete the survey due to severe physical or mental conditions (eg, severe cognitive impairment or acute psychosis); (2) Individuals who could not be located after at least three follow-up visits.

Survey Tool and Variable Selection

We adopted the Chinese National MHL Questionnaire for the survey, which is the designated monitoring tool when implementing the “Mental Health Promotion Action”. It comprises three dimensions: judgment questions on mental health knowledge (dimension 1), self-evaluation questions (dimension 2), and situational case questions (dimension 3). Dimension 1 consists of 20 questions, which are based on the “Ten Principles of MHL” and other fundamental psychological concepts. Each correct answer is awarded 5 points, while incorrect responses or “do not know” answers receive 0 points. The total score is the sum of the points from all 20 questions, with a score ranging from 0 to 100. A score of ≥80 points is considered acceptable. Dimension 2 includes eight questions assessing factors such as positive mindset, mental health awareness, psychological knowledge acquisition, and help-seeking behavior. Each question offers four response options, which are scored from 1 to 4 points. The highest score of 4 points is given for “Always” or “Strongly Agree,” with the remaining options scored in intervals of 1 point according to the respondent’s degree of agreement. The total score is the sum of the points from all 8 questions, with a score ranging from 8 to 32. A score of ≥24 points is considered acceptable. Dimension 3 features two questions with the same structure but different scenarios. Each scenario includes four subquestions designed to assess mental health skills, such as identifying mental illnesses and overcoming self-stigma and public stigma. The total score for this dimension is 40 points, with a score of ≥28 points considered acceptable. To meet the MHL standard, all three dimensions must be attained. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was 0.824. Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded P < 0.001, and the KMO value was 0.835.

The nested structure of the SEM guided the selection of variables potentially influencing MHL.25 Variables were systematically identified across four hierarchical levels: the individual level (gender, age, chronic disease status, history of mental health treatment, alcohol consumption frequency, smoking status, exercise status, average daily internet use time, self-reported health status); the interpersonal level (marital status, family history of mental illness); the community level (residence type, ethnicity); and the social structural level (education level, occupation, average monthly income per household member). Specifically, average daily internet use time was defined as the self-reported average daily duration of internet use over the past month, excluding time spent at work or study.

Field Survey and Quality Control

The survey was organized by the Guangxi Mental Health Center, with local survey departments in each city overseeing the organization, implementation, quality control, supervision, and technical support within their respective regions. A pilot survey was conducted to ensure the scientific rigor of the survey design before the official data collection began. Surveyors were selected from mental health institutions at the county (district) level, as well as from township health centers and community health service centers. To ensure effective communication with ethnic minority residents, we prioritized recruiting surveyors proficient in local dialects/languages. All survey personnel, including supervisors at each site, received standardized training to ensure consistent operations across all survey points, as well as proficiency in using the survey instrument and a clear understanding of the survey’s objectives. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews, with surveyors visiting respondents’ households to administer the questionnaire, ensuring thorough and accurate data collection.

Statistical Analysis

Data entry was performed using EpiData 3.1, and analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0. ArcGIS 10.8 was utilized to generate a geographic distribution map of MHL levels. Continuous variables were described as means ± standard deviations, while categorical data were expressed as relative frequencies. Independent samples t-tests were employed to compare scores for positive mindset, mental health awareness, and other items between males and females, as well as differences in mental health knowledge, self-evaluations, and health skills scores between urban and rural residents. Chi-square tests were applied to compare characteristics between those who met and those who did not meet the MHL standard, as well as to examine regional differences in attainment rates. To assess the relationships between baseline characteristics and MHL levels, logistic regression models were employed, reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These relationships were visualized using subgroup forest plots. Omnibus and Hosmer–Lemeshow tests were conducted to evaluate model fit. Additionally, an optimal scaling regression model was employed to identify the factors influencing MHL and evaluate their relative importance. Tolerance tests were conducted to assess multicollinearity, and F-tests were used to evaluate the model. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Respondent Demographics

A total of 9,744 questionnaires were distributed, and 9,444 valid responses were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 96.9%. The gender distribution among the respondents was balanced, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.02. The largest age group was 45–59 years, comprising 40.7% of the respondents. The urban-to-rural ratio was 1:1.17. The majority of participants were Han (61.2%) or Zhuang (32.7%). The most common education level was junior high school (39.1%). Most respondents were married (81.9%). Farming was the primary occupation (66.6%). The majority of participants reported an average monthly income per household member of 2,999 CNY or less (74.3%). The highest proportion of respondents came from Guilin city (11.2%). Detailed data can be found in Table 1.

Current Status of MHL

Among the 9,444 residents surveyed in Guangxi, the average MHL score was 109.25 ± 24.15. A total of 1,201 participants met the MHL standard, corresponding to an overall attainment rate of 12.7%. Respondents scored an average of 57.06 ± 18.28 on knowledge judgment items, with an attainment rate of 16.6% (1,568/9,444); 25.67 ± 3.96 on self-evaluation items, with an attainment rate of 74.9% (7,078/9,444); and 26.52 ± 7.55 on case-based items, with an attainment rate of 48.7% (4,601/9,444). The recognition rates were 27.4% for depressive disorder (2,589/9,444) and 46.2% for social anxiety disorder (4,359/9,444).

As shown in Figure 2, the average correct response rate across the 20 mental health knowledge items was 56.2%. Specifically, the accuracy rates for knowledge related to the causes and prevention of mental disorders, treatment of mental disorders, child protection and education, mind–body health associations, symptoms and identification of mental disorders, and other general knowledge and principles were 64.6%, 74.0%, 49.0%, 70.9%, 45.6%, and 33.6%, respectively.

Gender Differences in MHL

Men showed higher scores than women in three areas: positive mindset, mental health knowledge acquisition, and skills for seeking professional psychological help (P < 0.05). In contrast, women scored higher than men in mental health awareness, overcoming self-stigma, and overcoming public stigma related to mental health (P < 0.05). Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis of Factors Affecting MHL

The dependent variable was whether MHL met the standard (not meeting the standard = 1, meeting the standard = 0). Independent variables with a P-value less than 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model (see Supplementary Table S3). All categorical variables, except for age groups, were set as dummy variables. The forward LR method was used for logistic regression analysis, with inclusion and exclusion stepwise probabilities set at 0.05 and 0.10, respectively. The omnibus test of the model’s coefficients indicated that the regression model was significant overall (chi-square = 259.681, P < 0.001), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed that the model fit well (chi-square = 11.115, P > 0.05). The logistic regression results revealed that being older, being from the Dong ethnic group, having a lower education level, having a history of mental health treatment, and having poor self-reported health status were risk factors for low MHL. Being male, working as medical personnel or other professional technicians, having an average monthly income per household member (CNY) ranging between 3,000 and 7,999, and spending less than 3 hours on the internet daily were protective factors for MHL (P < 0.05) (Figure 5).

Discussion

MHL is a critical component of health literacy and plays a significant role in promoting changes in mental health behaviors. This study offers valuable insights into the MHL among residents of Guangxi, revealing a generally low level of awareness, particularly regarding depression and social anxiety disorder. Awareness of mental disorders is essential for early diagnosis and intervention. However, our study indicates that a significant portion of people cannot accurately identify or distinguish between common mental disorders. This finding aligns with the research by Yu et al27 on rural adults’ awareness of depression and anxiety in China, which is likely influenced by the public cultural beliefs of ethnic minorities and the diverse, complex presentation of mental disorder symptoms. For example, due to cultural practices, customs, and stigma surrounding mental disorders, many ethnic minority residents experiencing symptoms of depression or anxiety may report conditions such as neurasthenia or sleep disorders.28 The overall MHL attainment rate of 12.7% in Guangxi is in line with the baseline level set in the “Healthy China Action Plan (2019–2030)”.16 While this finding is comparable to MHL levels in other provinces, such as Guangdong (12.1%),29 it is significantly lower than the rates observed in cities on China’s eastern coast, such as Jiaxing (28.68%).30 The gap between the target of reaching 20% to 30% of MHL by 2022 and 2030, as proposed by the “Healthy China Action Plan” and the “Healthy Guangxi Action Plan”,16,31 remains substantial. The relatively low attainment rate underscores the need for targeted interventions to improve MHL, especially in underdeveloped and ethnically diverse regions like Guangxi.

Respondents performed well in the psychological self-evaluation dimension, which aligns with the findings of He et al.32 This suggests a growing self-awareness of mental health. However, a critical gap remains: the attainment rate for mental health knowledge was only 16.6%. According to Sørensen’s framework,33 this discrepancy indicates that although residents have the motivation (high self-evaluation), they lack the necessary understanding (factual knowledge). This gap limits their ability to effectively apply mental health skills in real-world situations. This limitation is particularly evident in child protection and education, highlighting an urgent need to strengthen training for parents and educators. Among the cities, the highest attainment rate (29.3%) was found in Fangchenggang, Guangxi. This may be attributed to the city’s previous efforts to reinforce health poverty alleviation policies, which included a series of public health consultations, health education lectures, and rural health outreach activities, as part of its role as an international medical open pilot zone. However, cities with low MHL still require more attention. Additionally, urban residents exhibited significantly higher attainment rates and scores across all dimensions than rural residents, a conclusion that is consistent with previous studies.34,35 Urban residents benefit from greater socioeconomic advantages, including better access to mental health resources, superior educational facilities, improved living environments,36 and broader internet access.37 This disparity highlights the need for mental health education and interventions in rural areas with ethnic minorities, fostering the equitable development of MHL between urban and rural regions.

Both logistic regression and optimal scaling regression consistently identified the independent influencing factors of MHL. Logistic regression facilitates subgroup analysis, while optimal scaling regression ranks these factors, offering valuable insights for policy prioritization. Regarding individual factors, we found that average daily internet use is the most significant factor influencing MHL. In this study, individuals who spend no more than three hours online per day (excluding work and professional study time) exhibited higher MHL levels compared to non-users. A reasonable explanation for this is that many individuals today seek mental health support and information online,38 while those who do not use the internet may miss valuable opportunities to access mental health education. Similarly, a study in Ethiopia on traditional healers found a significant positive correlation between MHL and the acquisition of mental health information via media.39 This suggests that the internet should play a more prominent role in the dissemination of mental health knowledge, particularly by promoting online, cost-effective mental health education resources to underserved populations.

Furthermore, we identified several individual risk factors for low MHL, including advanced age, poor self-reported health status, and a history of psychological treatment, while being male was found to be a protective factor. China has the largest elderly population in the world, and cognitive and mental disorders among the elderly are becoming increasingly prevalent.40,41 Future research should prioritize mental health issues among the elderly, focusing on bridging knowledge gaps, challenging negative attitudes, and fostering proactive behavioral habits.42 An English longitudinal study on ageing has shown that an individual’s prior physical health status can influence their current mental health.43 Our study further revealed that individuals reporting poor physical health had a 37.7% increased odds of not meeting MHL standards compared to those who reported good health. This may be attributed to the fact that chronic diseases, long-term pain, and other forms of physical discomfort can impair psychological adaptability and emotional regulation.44 Additionally, individuals with poor physical health often experience greater limitations in daily life, such as reduced social interactions and lower levels of physical activity, which further hinder their access to mental health support. Future health interventions for individuals with impaired physical health should consider integrating physical health management with psychological education. Remarkably, we discovered that individuals with a history of psychological treatment tend to have lower MHL. This may be influenced by several factors, including the psychological impact of the illness, limited access to high-quality mental health services, and stigma encountered during treatment.45 We advocate for improving the quality of mental health services by strengthening psychological counselling during treatment and incorporating interactions with individuals from similar cultural backgrounds to reduce mistrust.46 Our comparative analysis showed that female residents in Guangxi had significantly lower MHL attainment rates than their male counterparts, consistent with findings from a study in Mianyang, China.47 However, this result contrasts with the conclusions of Cotton et al48 on gender differences in MHL among young people in Australia. This discrepancy may arise from differences in the age inclusion criteria between the study samples. Based on these findings, future MHL interventions should be tailored to address the specific needs of both males and females.49 Key indicators revealed that, compared to males, females scored lower in areas such as positive mindset, psychological knowledge acquisition, and help-seeking skills. These insights provide valuable guidance for designing interventions aimed at improving female MHL. Such interventions could include educational programs focused on building emotional resilience, workshops to enhance psychological knowledge, and campaigns to promote help-seeking behaviors and raise awareness of available mental health resources.

Chinese ethnic minorities experience different medical resource distributions, health equity, and mental health prejudices.50 Improving MHL can contribute to reducing mental health disparities between ethnic groups.17 At the community level, we found that ethnic diversity adds complexity to mental health education. For example, the MHL attainment rate among the Dong ethnic group (2.6%) was significantly lower than that of the Han ethnic group. This discrepancy may be partly due to traditional prejudices against mental illness, insufficient mental health education, and barriers to accessing psychological services.22 A previous study has shown that the Dong community faces a high risk of depression and anxiety symptoms.28 These findings underscore the urgency of spreading psychological knowledge within the Dong community. It is recommended to integrate mental health promotion programs into ethnic cultural activities, such as utilizing the Dong people’s Grand Songs51 for emotional regulation and leveraging social media to spread Dong mental health culture.52 Moreover, as many ethnic minorities reside in remote mountainous areas, where access to information is limited, utilizing local media for mental health education could help bridge the gap in information access. Future mental health interventions in ethnic minority areas should be tailored to the cultural needs and beliefs of each group.

In terms of social structural factors, we found that a lower education level significantly increases the risk of poor MHL. This finding aligns with evidence from communities in Ethiopia.53 It may be attributed to disparities in access to mental health information, cognitive abilities, and the emphasis placed on mental health by individuals with varying educational backgrounds. Educational inequalities may further exacerbate disparities in mental health care utilization.54 Interventions targeting individuals with lower educational levels should prioritize accessible, easy-to-understand public science education campaigns and community-based initiatives. Additionally, we found that working in healthcare professions has a protective effect on MHL. Medical personnel typically receive specialized mental health training, which enables them to better identify, understand, and address mental health issues. The experience accumulated through clinical psychological practice also enhances their MHL. However, evidence suggests that medical personnel often face significant work-related stress,55 and encounter barriers when seeking professional psychological help, such as doubts about the effectiveness of psychological services, concerns over privacy, and time constraints.56 This indicates that, for medical personnel, having high MHL may not always translate into effective self-management of their mental health. Research suggests that implementing targeted interventions to reduce stigma and promote social support can improve help-seeking behaviors among medical personnel.55 Regarding economic status, we found that individuals with an average monthly income per household member (CNY) between 3,000 and 7,999 demonstrated a higher MHL attainment rate compared to those with an income below 2,999, which is consistent with a previous study.57 Higher-income individuals generally lead healthier lifestyles and have greater access to mental health resources.32 Notably, the protective effect of average monthly income per household member was significant only in the middle-income range. This may reflect the nonlinear relationship between economic resources and MHL. Additionally, society should enhance mental health support for low-income groups by providing more accessible and affordable services.

Based on our research findings, several policy recommendations can be made. First, efforts to promote mental health education in ethnic minority regions should be intensified, focusing on raising awareness about recognizing symptoms of mental disorders and promoting child protection and education. Second, ethnic minority areas could screen high-risk groups for MHL based on the identified factors. For example, priority should be given to populations who do not use the internet, have low education levels, are elderly, have low incomes, have poor self-reported health status, are female, or have a history of psychological treatment. Targeted interventions should be designed and implemented according to the relative importance of these factors. Third, when enhancing MHL, priority should be given to resource-limited rural areas and Dong communities. Mental health interventions should be integrated with traditional cultural activities of ethnic minorities, and the accessibility of digital mental health resources should be improved to ensure broader access to fast, convenient, and low-cost services. Fourth, efforts should focus on eliminating the stigma associated with mental health issues, encouraging early identification and promoting early help-seeking behaviors.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first large-scale survey of MHL in provinces with significant ethnic minority populations in China, characterized by a robust sample size and scientifically rigorous sampling methods. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships. Second, certain variables, such as self-reported health status, rely on subjective data, which may introduce biases such as social desirability bias and recall bias. Although the MHL assessment tool used is both reliable and valid, it does not capture important aspects such as the unique cultural backgrounds of ethnic minorities or the accessibility of mental health services. While these limitations are acknowledged, they do not substantially undermine the study’s conclusions. Rather, they point to important directions for future research aimed at improving the comprehensiveness of MHL assessments. Future research should focus on longitudinal intervention studies (eg, assessing digital education programs) and mixed-method studies to explore cultural meanings and the evolution of MHL, as well as how factors such as internet usage habits, educational background, and occupation interact to influence its development. Moreover, there is an urgent need to develop MHL assessment tools tailored to the cultural contexts of different ethnic groups. Additionally, investigating the relationship between MHL and actual mental health-promoting behaviors will not only highlight barriers and facilitators but also provide scientific evidence for designing more effective intervention programs.

Conclusions

This study systematically highlights the relatively low levels of MHL in the Guangxi region, with a lack of psychological knowledge identified as the primary bottleneck. Additionally, the public’s ability to recognize mental disorders is generally inadequate. Theoretically, our findings extend the application of the SEM by empirically demonstrating the multi-level interaction of digital access, occupational structure, and cultural background in shaping MHL. The main risk factors include advanced age, being from the Dong ethnic group, low educational level, a history of psychological treatment, and poor self-reported health status. In contrast, protective factors include male gender, employment in healthcare or professional technical fields, higher income, and daily internet use of less than 3 hours (the most influential factor). These findings suggest that, in the digital age, internet usage and social structural factors have a dominant influence on public mental health awareness. They also reflect the constraints imposed by unequal resource distribution, which hinder the improvement of MHL. The key value of this study lies in providing evidence for mental health interventions and public policy development for border regions with similar ethnic and cultural compositions. This research emphasizes the need to strengthen mental health education, promote online mental health resources, target high-risk populations, optimize promotion activities in rural ethnic minority regions (particularly Dong communities), integrate mental health education with traditional ethnic culture, and reduce stigma. Ultimately, these efforts will help address mental health inequalities and foster more equitable health outcomes in border regions.
