Growing up giving care: economic and educational outcomes for youth and young-adult caregivers
Authors: Katherine E M Miller, Elise M Parrish, Rohini Achal, Regina Shih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFamily and friends play a crucial role in supporting people with disabilities by providing unpaid help with activities ranging from personal care to housekeeping to wound care.MethodsWe examined associations between earliest age of first unpaid caregiving episode and educational and economic outcomes by age 60 years using nationally representative survey data from the Health and Retirement Study.ResultsWe found that youth/young-adult caregivers (became a caregiver on or before age 25) have 47% more caregiving episodes and 244% more caregiving years compared with those who become caregivers after age 25+ years. Compared with people who never become caregivers and caregivers aged 26+ years, youth/young-adult caregivers have lower levels of postsecondary education and by age 60 have lower predicted Social Security value (−12% vs caregivers aged 26+ years and −23% compared to non-caregivers).ConclusionGrowing up giving care is a reality for many youth and young adults in the United States today. Investing in youth caregiving research is critical to help develop targeted policies and interventions to meet their unique needs.

Introduction

Family and friends play a crucial role in supporting older adults and people with disabilities by providing unpaid help with activities ranging from personal care to housekeeping to wound care.1 In 2025, an estimated 59 million adults provided unpaid help to individuals with functional or cognitive limitations.2 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention declared family caregiving a public health issue due to the deleterious economic and health impacts of providing unpaid care.3 While the physical, mental, and economic effects of caregiving for an aging spouse or parent are well established, the evidence examining differential effects of caregiving conditional on the age of caregiving is sparse.4-8 Early life (ages 5–25 years) is a critical developmental period marked by milestones including completing primary and secondary education, developing a sense of identity, transitioning to adult life, and initiating career plans.9,10 Thus, the consequences of caregiving in early life as a youth caregiver may differ from the effects of caregiving as a middle-aged or older adult.

While limited evidence examines youth/young-adult caregivers in the United States, evidence from Australia and Europe suggests that caregiving in early life is associated with greater anxiety and depressive symptoms, lower self-esteem, and worse self-reported health status.6 Additionally, youth caregivers spend significantly less time on schooling and completing homework and are less likely to be enrolled in full-time school compared with their non-caregiving peers.11 The long-term economic outcomes of youth and young-adult caregivers compared with non-caregiving peers, such as impacts on wealth accumulation, remain unclear, as early financial losses may compound over time.9,12 Studies that use a life-course perspective contend that later-life experiences are a function of opportunities and constraints experienced earlier in life that may compound over time.13 The contemporaneous educational and health disadvantages of early-life caregiving may constitute substantial constraints that pattern later-life outcomes and experiences.

Our objective was to describe the associations between educational and economic outcomes by earliest age of first unpaid caregiving episode. Understanding the association between caregiving across the life course and long-term economic outcomes is an important first step to inform policy supports for caregivers of all ages and future research directions to elucidate the causal pathways between caregiving and economic outcomes to refine the economic valuation of unpaid care.

Methods

Data and Sample

We used survey-weighted, nationally representative data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) to identify age of first caregiving episode and document patterns in late-life outcomes.14,15 We used the HRS Life History Mail Survey (LHMS) cross-wave file to identify the age, timing, and duration of caregiving episodes across the life course. The HRS LHMS was sent to respondents in 2015, 2017, and 2019 and includes retrospective questions about education, work history, health events, and unpaid caregiving. We then linked observations from the HRS-LHMS Questionnaire to the RAND longitudinal file, which captures later-life financial measures, sociodemographic characteristics, birth cohort, and biennial survey weights for the wave prior to the HRS-LHMS questionnaire completion. While the LHMS questionnaire may be completed by proxy respondents, we excluded respondents who did not complete the survey themselves, given our focus on distal life events that proxy respondents may not be able to complete accurately (n = 230). We also excluded individuals who skipped the caregiving questions in the LHMS questionnaire (n = 1921) or who had a missing sample weight for the survey wave prior to the LHMS (n = 509). We had an analytic sample of 12 120 respondents with respondents from all HRS sample cohorts.

Measures

Outcomes

Early-life outcomes include the highest level of education attained (less than high school, high school/GED [General Educational Development], some college or Associate's degree, Bachelor's degree or greater) and indicators for whether the respondent participated in high school clubs or sports, took college prep courses in high school, and initiated post–secondary education within 2 years of high school graduation.

Outcomes related to transitioning to adulthood are median age at first job; whether the first job was full-time or part-time; median age of first household; ever had living, biological children (ie, not including step, stillbirth, miscarriage); and median age at first marriage. Because later-life outcomes (eg, wealth) are collected biennially, we report outcomes from the wave corresponding to age 60 or 61 years to allow for comparisons over time as participants enter the study in different periods. Outcomes examined at age 60 include marital status, homeownership, total net wealth, and predicted Social Security value. We adjusted total net wealth and Social Security income for inflation using the Consumer Price Index to 2019 US dollars.

The key explanatory variable is the first age at which someone reports providing unpaid care to an individual “with some sort of special need” for a period of 6 or more months. We created a 3-level indicator that denotes whether someone never reports being a caregiver; becoming a caregiver where the earliest age of caregiving is 25 years or younger (hereafter called youth/young-adult caregivers); and becoming a caregiver where the earliest age of caregiving is after age 25 years (hereafter called adult caregivers). We excluded individuals who had implausible care episodes (eg, age at first caregiving year begins before birth year or before age 5 years) (see the survey questionnaire language in the Supplemental Materials).

Additional explanatory variables include respondent's sociodemographic characteristics (eg, sex and race) and self-reported economic characteristics from childhood (childhood family socioeconomic status of poor/average/well-off, whether they moved during childhood due to financial difficulties, and if the family ever received financial help during childhood).

Among family caregivers only, we also describe the relationship of caregiver to care-recipient (not mutually exclusive categories), number of caregiving episodes, and number of lifetime years spent caregiving. To calculate the number of lifetime years spent caregiving, we double counted overlapping episodes to better capture the intensity of caregiving experiences—for example, if person A cared for mother during 1990–1992 and father during 1989–1991 then total number of episodes would be 2 and caregiving years would equal 6. As a sensitivity analysis, we also calculated episodes without allowing for overlapping calendar years.

Empirical Approach

First, we calculated the percentage of respondents who reported caregiving responsibilities by age of earliest caregiving episode (<18, 18–25, 26–35, 36–45, 46–65, and >65 years of age). Second, we describe differences in caregiving experiences of youth/young-adult caregivers compared with adult caregivers. Third, we describe the sociodemographic characteristics of the overall sample and age of earliest caregiving episode, using chi-square tests for differences in categorical characteristics and t tests for continuous variables across groups. Due to small cell sizes, we grouped individuals who are never caregivers, caregivers by age 25 years, and caregivers after age 25 years. We report unweighted frequencies and weighted percentages.

Because caregiving has historically been provided by women, generational shifts in gender norms and increased female labor force participation may influence the prevalence of caregiving and who becomes a caregiver. Therefore, we replicated all descriptive analyses across grouped birth cohorts. First, we examined caregiving experiences among respondents from the first HRS cohort (born 1931–1941), Asset and Health Dynamics Among the Oldest Old (AHEAD) cohort (born before 1924), and the Children of the Depression (CODA) cohort (born 1924–1930). Then, we examined outcomes among the War Babies cohort (born 1942–1947) and the Early Baby Boomers (born 1948–1953). Finally, we examined outcomes among the Mid Baby Boomers (born 1954–1959) and the Late Baby Boomers (1960–1965).

We used Stata SE, version 18.5 (StataCorp LLC). This research was not considered human subjects research and was therefore exempt from Institutional Review Board approval by 45 CFR Part 46.

Results

Among respondents (n = 12 120), 27.9% of individuals reported being a caregiver at some point during their life course. Among respondents who had ever been caregivers, 2.1% reported beginning caregiving before age 18 years, 4.3% between ages 18 and 25, 11.3% between 26 and 35, 17.9% between 36 and 45, 56.2% between 46 and 65, and 8.2% at age 65 years or older (Figure 1).

When examining differences in demographic characteristics, early-life experiences, and transitions to adulthood, we found significant differences between youth/young-adult caregivers compared with adult caregivers and non-caregivers (Table 2). Compared with non-caregivers, individuals who are a youth/young-adult or adult caregiver are more likely to be female (76.3% vs 49.4%; P < .001). We also found that youth/young-adult caregivers are more likely to have no more than a high-school degree/GED compared with adult caregivers and non-caregivers (52.6% vs 33.3% [P < .001] and 40.1% [P < .01], respectively). We found no consistent evidence of differences in childhood family socioeconomic status of moving during childhood due to financial difficulties, or if the family ever received financial help during childhood. Youth/young-adult caregivers are less likely to have participated in high school clubs or sports, taken college prep courses in high school, to have ever had children, or to initiate post-secondary education within 2 years of graduating high school, but these differences were not statistically significant. However, compared with non-caregivers, youth/young-adult caregivers got married and started their first job at younger ages (P < .05). Youth/young-adult caregivers’ median age at their first full-time job was also significantly lower than the median age at first job for caregivers who began providing unpaid care later in the life course (18 vs 19 years; P < .05).

Other job history experiences also vary by caregiving status. Compared with non-caregivers, youth/young adults are less likely to work their first job as a full-time job and more likely to ever work part-time, stop working to care for children, and cut hours to care for children (P < .05). Youth/young-adult caregivers also exhibit job history disadvantages relative to adult caregivers: for example, compared with adult caregivers, youth/young-adult caregivers are nearly 11 percentage points more likely to ever stop working to care for children (P < .05).

Youth/young-adult caregivers’ later-life educational, health, and economic outcomes are even more striking compared with adult caregivers and non-caregivers (see Table 3). Youth/young-adult caregivers are significantly more likely to report at least 2 chronic conditions by age 60 compared with non-caregivers (56.2% vs 43.1%; P < .001) and more likely to report at least 2 chronic conditions by age 60 compared with adult caregivers (56.2% vs 47.0%), but these differences were not statistically significant (P > .05). Median wealth was significantly lower for youth/young-adult caregivers compared with adult caregivers (P < .05). Similarly, median predicted Social Security value at age 60 was also significantly lower for youth/young-adult caregivers compared with adult caregivers and non-caregivers ($156 994 vs $204 280 [P < .001] and $178 386 [P < .05], respectively).

Results are largely consistent across HRS cohorts examined (see Supplementary Materials). Most notably, the prevalence of early-life caregiving is nearly double in the youngest cohort examined (Mid- and Late Baby Boomers) compared with early cohorts, which may reflect cultural, societal, and economic shifts in American society.

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of older adults, we found that respondents with a history of being a youth/young-adult caregivers have 47% more caregiving episodes and 244% more caregiving years compared with those who become caregivers later in life. Youth/young-adult caregivers have lower levels of post–secondary education than non-caregivers or later-life caregivers. When considering later-life economic outcomes, compared with non-caregivers and adult caregivers, youth/young-adult caregivers have lower median predicted Social Security value. These findings have important implications for (1) understanding the long-term outcomes observed of family caregivers and (2) considering how researchers calculate the economic valuation of care.

While the health and economic consequences of caregiving as an adult for an aging parent or older spouse are well researched, the long-term health and economic outcomes of youth/young-adult caregivers compared with adult caregivers and non-caregivers are underresearched in the United States. We show that youth/young-adult caregivers have lower levels of post–secondary education, reduced opportunities for full-time employment, and significantly diminished wealth accumulation in later life compared with both non-caregivers as well as adults who become caregivers later in life. This aligns with prior research finding that youth/young-adult caregivers spend less time in school and are less likely to be enrolled in school compared with their non-caregiving counterparts.11 While our estimates are descriptive, it is unsurprising to observe that later-life economic outcomes for those who began caregiving early in life are consistently worse than their non-caregiving peers or peers who provided care as an adult. To further place our findings in the context of the existing literature, we found that adult caregivers have worse economic outcomes than non-caregivers, which is aligned with prior caregiving research on adults,17-21 but that being a caregiver earlier in life is associated with worse economic outcomes even when compared with later-life caregiving. For example, youth/young-adult caregivers have 12% and 23% less in median predicted Social Security value compared with older-adult caregivers and non-caregivers, respectively. While youth/young-adult caregivers may have fewer economic resources than non-caregiving peers, the mechanism explaining these differences is unknown. For example, individuals may opt into caregiving roles instead of employment due to preferences and therefore engage in multiple caregiving experiences. Alternatively, youth/young-adult caregivers may have fewer opportunities to engage in wealth-generating activities earlier in life and disrupt one's human capital investment at an early stage, which may compound over time. Collectively, these results raise concerns about the long-term economic outcomes of individuals who begin caregiving in childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood. Systematic policy interventions are needed to address the burdens of caregiving for youth/young adults.

The often overlooked and understudied economic outcomes of youth/young-adult caregivers highlight the potential underestimation of the valuation of unpaid care in the United States. Estimating the economic valuation of unpaid care is extremely difficult to do in the long term as short-term costs, such as lost wages or reduced hours of work, can translate into longer-term economic costs that can be challenging to attribute directly to caregiving (eg, getting passed over for a promotion, working an extra year past retirement due to financial strain from caregiving episodes compounding over years).22 Additionally, researchers are faced with how to value the opportunity cost of caregiving. This challenge is amplified when considering the costs of caregiving as a youth/young adult. While positive aspects of caregiving exist, understanding the tradeoffs being made by providing care as a youth is critical to optimize individual economic outcomes but can also inform policy interventions in the direct-care workforce and formal (ie, paid) home- and community-based services to complement unpaid care.

State and federal governments have recently increased caregiving policy activity, including enacting family and medical leave laws and offering payments to family caregivers of Medicaid recipients; however, few of these policies extend support to youth/young-adult caregivers.23-26 In the absence of a federal strategy, several states have pioneered innovations that may serve as a roadmap for other states and federal efforts. First, developing more effective and equitable policies that address the diverse needs of these caregivers begins with collecting more rigorous and representative data of youth/young-adult caregivers’ health and educational experiences. Several states, including Rhode Island, Colorado, and Florida, mandate school-based behavioral surveys to track caregiving trends among middle and high school students. Importantly, these surveys identify student caregivers to inform the design of caregiver-friendly school environments that support students’ needs. National implementation of validated questions across all school-based surveys would help generate more representative data, thereby advancing needed data collection. Second, there are potential opportunities to leverage existing infrastructure and pilot programs to support youth/young-adult caregivers. For example, Rhode Island is developing policies that allow students to count caregiving hours towards community service requirements and is designing more flexible class schedules, skills-building programs, and support groups for caregiving students.27,28 Other states, such as Florida, have implemented school-based interventions (eg, Caregiving Youth Project) aimed at supporting caregiving students. This program includes individualized coaching and counseling sessions in addition to educational workshops designed to reduce stress and support academic progress. School systems could adopt additional strategies, such as flexible deadline and attendance policies, revising tuition policies and withdrawal policies to avoid penalizing youth caregivers.27 Former First Lady of California Maria Shriver has also proposed higher-education scholarships for those who undertake caregiving activities for 2 years.29 Educational institutions could also accept caregiving as relevant clinical experience for nursing programs, such as CNA, LPN, or other health care degrees.30 These educational policies may benefit both caregivers, by facilitating degree completion, and care recipients, who receive more informed care. While such certifications offer valuable pathways to post–secondary education, policymakers must also invest in building robust professional ladders that facilitate long-term professional advancement to mitigate potential unintended consequences (eg, programs may unintentionally reinforce low-wage jobs with poor working conditions).31 While great progress is needed to support youth/young-adult caregivers, existing infrastructure and data-collection efforts could be leveraged to begin to meet their needs.

Limitations

This analysis is subject to several limitations. First, our analyses are descriptive, not causal. Second, the HRS sample yields small cell sizes when examining particularly young ages of first caregiving episode. Consequently, we collapse categories and are unable to describe nuanced health and economic statuses by age of each respondent's first episode of caregiving and disentangle likely important heterogeneity between youth vs young-adult caregivers, as well as differences within youth and young-adult caregivers by sociodemographic characteristics. Similarly, to facilitate comparisons, we examined outcomes observed at age 60–61 years, which may mask broader temporal trends (eg, probability of homeownership). Fourth, our analysis is subject to recall bias as respondents are asked to reflect upon their life history experiences. However, recall bias would likely result in underestimating the prevalence of early-life caregiving and thus underestimate the associations with later-life outcomes. Finally, while we use age cutoffs aligned with prior research and life course theory to identify youth/young-adult caregivers, future research is needed to help refine age cutoffs.11,16 Despite these limitations, these descriptive statistics on education and economic experiences across the life course stratified by age of first caregiving episode provide key information in one of the largest, nationally representative surveys with longitudinal follow-up into late life.

Conclusion

Without a cohesive long-term care system, family caregivers of all ages will continue to play a disproportionate role in delivering long-term care in the United States. While the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention deems caregiving a public health issue, youth/young-adult caregivers are overlooked in research and policy.3 Rigorous, nationally representative studies are key to elucidating diverse experiences of youth/young-adult caregivers and the health and economic outcomes in the short- and long-term. Investment in comprehensive data collection will allow for targeted, evidence-based interventions to ensure health, educational, and economic success for youth/young-adult caregivers—particularly given the dynamic relationship between health end finances throughout one's life course. Simultaneously, policymakers can leverage existing school-based supports to help create an environment supportive of caregiving students. Growing up giving care is a reality for many youth and young adults in the United States today and there is an urgent need to invest in youth caregiving research to inform the development of targeted policies and interventions to promote the long-term health of caregivers.
