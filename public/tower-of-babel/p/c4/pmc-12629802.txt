A nanobody-based tri-specific NK cell engager targeting CD5 triggers antitumor immunity
Authors: Chen Yang, Ping Wang, Mingjun Yang, Huaqing Lu, Qizhong Lu, Zongliang Zhang, Zeng Wang, Zhixiong Zhu, Hexian Li, Wanqin Zeng, Zhengyu Yu, Meng Li, Lizhou Zhao, Bo Ling, Wei Wang, Hai Yi, Yisong Xiong, Ting Niu, Aiping Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe poor prognosis of patients with recurrent or refractory T cell malignancies emphasizes the need for improved immunotherapies. CD5 is a characteristic marker of malignant T cells and is expressed on almost all normal T cells. Therefore, for treating T cell malignancies, focusing on natural killer (NK) cells lacking CD5 expression may elicit a better safety profile than that by T cell-based therapies. We generate a CD5-targeted NK cell engager (NKCE) through the specific binding of CD16a nanobody and a high-affinity anti-CD5 antibody. Its antitumor potency is demonstrated in vitro. After incorporating interleukin (IL)-15Rα/IL-15, the modified tri-NKCE exhibits stronger antitumor efficacy against CD5+ malignant tumor cells, with the production of more cytokines and chemokines. In vivo, tri-NKCE exhibits stronger cytotoxicity by enhancing NK cell proliferation. Compared with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, this tri-NKCE exhibits no toxicity to normal T cells. In conclusion, tri-NKCE offers a safer and cost-effective immunotherapy against T cell malignancies.

Introduction

The overall prognosis of patients with T cell malignancies, a heterogeneous group of diseases with high rates of recurrence and mortality, is generally poor, particularly when compared to that of patients with B cell malignancies.1 Although current strategies such as chemotherapy have been beneficial for certain disease subsets, their effectiveness in patients with relapsed or refractory diseases remains limited.2,3 Therefore, it is imperative to develop innovative therapies for T cell malignancies.

CD5, a type-I transmembrane glycosylated protein,4 is expressed on normal T cells and a small group of B cells known as B1 cells.5,6 Owing to its high accumulation in most T cell malignancies but not on the surface of hematopoietic stem cells, CD5 is considered an ideal therapeutic target.7,8 Previous preclinical studies have shown that the CD5 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-transduced T cells exert antitumor effects in CD5-positive T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) models.9 However, as CAR-modified T cells express the targeted antigen as well, traditional CD5-targeting CAR-T cells are unable to precisely identify tumor cells and result in fratricide,10 ultimately limiting the expansion of CAR-T cells. Although this adverse effect has been reduced by employing the CRISPR-Cas9 technology to knockout CD5 expression in T cells before transduction with CD5-CAR,11,12 CD5 plays a crucial role in maintaining the function of T cells,13 whether the loss of CD5 affects the normal function of CAR-T cells remains to be further confirmed. In addition, CD5-CAR-T cells can also target normal T cells, resulting in unintended T cell aplasia.10 These limitations highlight the need for finding an alternative cell type that may overcome the disadvantages of CAR-T cells.

Natural killer (NK) cells are essential components of the innate immune system and have the ability to identify and eliminate virus-infected and tumor cells.14 Adoptive NK-cell transfer has been shown to be safe in clinical trials, with no risk of graft-versus-host disease.14 Furthermore, antigens targeted on T cell malignancies were not detected on NK cells, with the exception of CD7.1 CAR-modified NK cells are promising approaches for treating T cell malignancies while avoiding the mutual killing of CAR-T cells.15 However, NK cells derived from peripheral blood are difficult to genetically modify, and their short lifespan reduces their antitumor potency.16 NK-92 cells have been used as an alternative cell line for CD5-CAR transduction, and their effectiveness has been demonstrated against CD5+ malignant cells.15 Nonetheless, because NK-92 cells are derived from lymphoma, they must be irradiated before clinical use,17 which limits their persistence in vivo. Considering these complicated manufacturing procedures and the need for vector-mediated gene transfer, recombinant proteins known as NK cell engagers (NKCEs)18,19 have emerged as a promising alternative. NKCEs induce antitumor immunity by co-engaging with antigens on tumor cells and activating receptors on NK cells, demonstrating several advantages over CAR-NK cells and inducing CAR-like activity.

NK effector cell function is regulated by a complex array of activation and inhibition receptors.20 The activating receptors such as CD16, NKG2D, and NKp46 can trigger NK cell-mediated antitumor immune responses.21 Unlike other activating receptors, CD16 (also known as FcγRIII) can fully activate NK cells22 and is considered the most effective activating receptor. Several products, including AFM13 (anti-CD30/CD16a), AFM24 (anti-EGFR/CD16a), and AFM28 (anti-CD123/CD16a) are in preclinical development or clinical trials.23,24,25,26 CD16-targeted NKCEs are particularly effective because, unlike monoclonal antibody-based antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), their function is not affected by CD16a polymorphism.27,28 Human CD16 exists in two isoforms (i.e., CD16a [FcγRIIIa] and CD16b [FcγRIIIb]) that share 96% sequence identity in their extracellular immunoglobulin-binding regions.29 However, CD16b cannot trigger antitumor apoptosis and may cause adverse effects in vivo.30 Therefore, selecting antibodies specifically for binding to CD16a is important in developing NKCEs.

NK cell function, survival, and proliferation are physiologically regulated and enhanced by cytokines, particularly interleukin (IL)-15 and IL-2.31,32 Compared with IL-2, IL-15 is more favorable for NK cell immunotherapy as it avoids regulatory T cell-mediated immune suppression.33 Some IL-15-NKCEs assembling the cytokine signal and bi-specific antibodies into a single recombinant protein have demonstrated enhanced delivery of IL-15 to NK cells and potentiate NK cell-based immunotherapies.34,35,36,37,38,39,40

Nanobodies are the recombinant variable domains of heavy-chain-only antibodies and possess several advantages such as small size, high thermostability, short development cycle, and deep tissue penetration.41 These characteristics make them well suited for therapeutic applications. This study aimed to develop a potential therapeutic strategy for T cell malignancies using NKCE. We created an NKCE containing an anti-CD16a nanobody and a high-affinity anti-CD5 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) identified through in-house screening. This NKCE showed effective and specific cell lysis against tumor cells. By fusing with the IL-15 receptor α (IL-15Rα; sushi domain)/IL-15 domain, we constructed a modified NKCE called 1H6-15-NKCE. In addition to improving cytotoxicity, 1H6-15-NKCE significantly improved NK cell survival and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, unlike CD5-CAR-T cells, this tri-specific antibody has no on-target off-tumor cytotoxicity toward normal T cells. Collectively, these findings support the efficacy and safety of this treatment strategy for T cell malignancies.

Results

Characteristics of anti-CD5 single-chain antibody fragments and anti-CD16a nanobody

Previous studies have shown that CD5 is a therapeutic target for T cell malignancies42 but is constitutively expressed in normal T cells (Figure S1). In this study, flow cytometry detected CD5 expression in several target cell lines (Figure S2A), including CCRF-CEM, Jurkat, and MOLT-4 cells, but not in NK cells (Figure S1). Based on this, we planned to develop CD5-targeted NKCEs by primarily screening for monoclonal antibodies with a high affinity for CD5, and four positive clones were verified (data not shown). The heavy-chain variable region (VH) and light-chain variable region (VL) sequences of these clones were assembled using a linker ((G4S)3) and fused with human immunoglobulin (Ig)G4 Fc fragments (hFc) to form recombinant proteins. Two of these were successfully expressed, and their purification was validated through sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Figure S2B). Their binding specificity to CD5+ tumor cell lines were confirmed by flow cytometry with clone H65 used as a positive control (Figure 1A),12 and binding capacity was further quantified to CCRF-CEM cells via immunofluorescence (IF) (Figure S2C). Furthermore, a variable domain of heavy chain of heavy-chain antibody (VHH) phage library was constructed to screen for nanobodies that specifically bind to CD16a (Figure 1D). We first immunized camels with the CD16a extracellular domain (ECD) and then isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the blood, followed by cDNA and phagemid library generation. After four rounds of phage panning, eight candidate clones were observed to bind CD16a (158V/F). Only one clone (P60) showed no binding to CD16b (NA1/NA2) in phage ELISA, in contrast to clone 3G8,43 which could bind both CD16a and CD16b (Figure 1E). The VHH sequence of P60 was fused to hFc to generate a recombinant protein, and its expression was validated by SDS-PAGE (Figure S2E). Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) results demonstrated that P60 specifically bound to CD16a (158V/F) but not to CD16b (NA1/NA2) (Figures 1F and S2F). The IF results also confirmed this finding (Figure 1G). Therefore, we focused on the clones 1H6 and P60 for further studies.

In vitro effectiveness of CD5-CD16 NKCE

To evaluate whether our NKCE could activate NK cells and induce cell lysis of CD5+ malignant cells, we constructed a CD5−CD16 NKCE, namely 1H6-NKCE, by linking anti-CD16-VHH with anti-CD5 scFv through the human IgG4 Fc protein (Figure 2A). The generated NKCE was successfully purified with the correct molecular weight indicated by SDS-PAGE (Figure S3A). The binding capacity to CD5 and CD16a (158V/F) was confirmed using ELISA (Figures 2B, 2C, and S3B), and binding affinity of CD5 or of CD16a (158V/F) was measured using SPR or BLI (Figures S3C and S3D). After NK cells were isolated and the surface expression of CD16 was verified (Figure 2D), 1H6-NKCE binding to NK cells via CD16 was validated using flow cytometry (Figure 2E). The cytotoxic effects of 1H6-NKCE on CD5+ target cells, including CCRF-CEM, MOLT-4, and Jurkat cells, were assessed. The results showed that 1H6-NKCE lysed these target cells to different degrees and that cytotoxicity mediated by 1H6-NKCE was significantly higher than the treatment of 1H6 and P60 (Figure 2F). However, the killing effect mediated by 1H6-NKCE was not observed in tumor cells lacking CD5 expression (Figure S3E). To evaluate this function over time, real-time cellular analysis (RTCA) was conducted. CD5-transfected HeLa cells were used as target cells, and their transduction efficiency was confirmed through flow cytometry (Figure S4). The long-time killing assays showed that 1H6-NKCE induced more robust killing than the treatment without antibody, 1H6, and P60 at all time points tested (Figure 2G). In contrast, in the absence of CD5 expression, HeLa cells were not lysed by 1H6-NKCE-treated NK cells (Figure S3F). The production of cytotoxic granules by NK cells was quantified by the induction of CD107a in a degranulation assay. When co-cultured with CD5+ target cells, the number of CD107a+ cells significantly increased following treatment with 1H6-NKCE (Figures 2H, 2I, and S5). Furthermore, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to identify the gene expression profile of NK cells induced by 1H6-NKCE, with no antibody treatment as reference. The results showed that more than 1,900 genes were differentially expressed (Figure S6A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that some pathways involved in NK cell function, such as the cellular response to type II interferon and the tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-non canonical nuclear factor κB pathways, were upregulated in the 1H6-NKCE treatment group (Figure S6B). The expression analysis of all samples revealed that some genes associated with NK cell cytotoxicity were also upregulated following treatment with 1H6-NKCE (Figure S6C), confirming the antitumor effect of 1H6-NKCE.

1H6-15-NKCE shows enhanced anti-CD5+ malignant cell activity over 1H6-NKCE in vitro

IL-15, a pleiotropic cytokine, enhances NK cell proliferation, survival, and cytotoxicity.44 Thus, the effects of recombinant human (rh) IL-15 on NK cell cytotoxicity were initially evaluated by supplementing NK cells with rhIL-15 or rhIL-15 plus 1H6-NKCE. The results indicated that the addition of rhIL-15 increased NK cell cytotoxicity (Figures S7A–S7C). However, there are some limitations in applying the monomeric recombinant cytokines, such as the short serum half-life and the difficulty in producing sufficient quantities in standard mammalian cell expression systems.45 To overcome these drawbacks, we linked soluble IL-15Rα-sushi domain to IL-15 as the IL-15Rα/IL-15 complex to the C terminus of P60 and fused it to the human Fc protein. A modified NKCE, 1H6-15-NKCE, was synthesized (Figure 3A). SDS-PAGE confirmed the size and purity of the fusion protein (Figure S8A). IL-15 content was then detected through ELISA (Figure S8B). Next, we investigated whether the IL-15Ra/IL-15 in this backbone could drive potent IL-15 signaling, thus increasing the antitumor efficacy of NK cells. The IL-15-Fc (IL-15Rα/IL-15 fused with human IgG4 Fc fragments), 1H6-NKCE, 1H6-15-NKCE, or only NK cells were incubated with CD5+ target cells. The results of flow cytometry showed that the number of residual target cells (CD56−) was lower after treatment with IL-15-Fc than that with only NK cells. Compared with IL-15-Fc, the fewer target cells alive were observed with the treatment of 1H6-15-NKCE, which was caused by the specific tumor lysis of cross-linking of CD16 in the presence of the CD5-targeting function of 1H6-NKCE. Furthermore, we found that 1H6-15-NKCE drove the stronger activity of NK cells isolated from different donors against CCRF-CEM, Jurkat, and MOLT-4 cells at a series of concentrations (Figures 3C and S10), suggesting a wide application scope for this therapy. Lysis of CCRF-CEM, MOLT-4, and Jurkat target cells using fresh PBMCs from healthy donors was assessed. The results revealed that 1H6-15-NKCE induced more robust NK cell-mediated killing than did 1H6-NKCE, particularly at a 10:1 (effector-to-target [E:T]) ratio (Figure 3D). Although a trend of enhanced tumor lysis was observed by the IL-15-Fc compared with NK cells, the strongest cytotoxicity of 1H6-15-NKCE indicating the CD5-targeted cell killing elicited by 1H6-NKCE was important to NK cell function. RTCA was used to investigate the long-time killing efficacy of NK cells, which were activated by 1H6-15-NKCE treatment and eliminated more HeLa-CD5 cells than those activated by 1H6-NKCE, and this enhanced NK cell function was more evident at later time points (Figure 3E). The augmented cell killing of IL-15-Fc, in comparison to the addition of only NK cells, can be attributed to the IL-15 signaling. But due to the lack of the combination of 1H6-NKCE, its NK cell function was weaker than that activated by 1H6-15-NKCE (Figure 3E). Additionally, antibody-mediated NK-NK cell lysis was assessed through a calcein-release assay, which revealed that NKCEs could not induce fratricidal NK cell killing (Figure 3F).

To evaluate their ability to eliminate primary tumors, three primary T-ALL samples with CD5+ blasts were used in this study (Figures 3G and S11A). Compared with no treatments, allogeneic NK cells targeted by our NKCEs could eliminate CD5+ primary tumors, and higher tumor lysis was observed by NK cells mediated by 1H6-15-NKCE (Figures 3H and 3I), suggesting the greater potential capacity of 1H6-15-NKCE applied in the “off-the-shelf” therapy by allogeneic infusion. To further evaluate whether 1H6-15-NKCE could engage autologous NK cells, the proportion of CD56+ NK cells from PBMCs was compared between patients and healthy donors. The results showed that, although the CD56+NK cell frequencies were weakly declined in patients, the proportion of CD56bright NK cells and CD56medium NK cells from patients was not changed compared with that from healthy donors (Figures S11B and S11C). We further characterized the expression of CD16, CD57, KIR2DL1, CD94, and NKG2A on NK cells from healthy donors and patients. No significant difference was observed for CD16, CD57, KIR2DL1, and NKG2A between patients and healthy donors, but the expression of CD94 was reduced in CD56+ T-ALL NK cells (Figure S11D). Next, the results of calcein-release assay indicated that autologous T-ALL patient PBMCs could fight against primary tumor cells by antibody-dependent cytotoxicity when mediated by NKCEs, especially the 1H6-15-NKCE (Figure S11E). Taken together, 1H6-15-NKCE exhibited more potent allogeneic and autologous antitumor activity against CD5+ tumors in vitro.

Transcriptomic signature and cytokine secretion profile induced by 1H6-15-NKCE

Next, we performed transcriptome analysis to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the effects of 1H6-NKCE, 1H6-15-NKCE, and no antibodies on NK cells in the presence of CD5+ target cells. Approximately 1,400 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were induced by both 1H6-NKCE and 1H6-15-NKCE, and more than 2,000 genes were either upregulated or downregulated by 1H6-15-NKCE (Figure 4A). The volcano plot in Figure 4B shows that, in NK cells, the gene expression pattern induced by 1H6-15-NKCE was distinct from that induced by 1H6-NKCE. Particularly, we found greater enrichment of NK cell-mediated antitumor function pathways such as immune system process, response to cytokines, and chemotaxis in the DEGs based on Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis (Figure 4C). Further unsupervised clustering analysis illustrated that some genes involved in cytokine signaling pathways, including interferon gamma (IFNG), granzyme B (GZMB), and TNF (Figure 4E), had higher expression levels in 1H6-15-NKCE-activated NK cells, as confirmed through cytokine release assays (Figures 4F–4H). Thus, 1H6-15-NKCE poised NK cells for more effective antitumor action by enhancing cytokine production. In addition, some genes enriched in the chemokine activity pathway, such as CCL3, CCL4, and CXCR6, were only upregulated by 1H6-15-NKCE (Figures 4C and 4E). This suggested that 1H6-15-NKCE-activated NK cells have the potential to recruit dendritic cells (DCs), thereby enhancing the tumor-specific adaptive immune response.46 Flow cytometry analysis using the activation marker CD69 and the degranulation marker CD107a demonstrated that 1H6-15-NKCE induced stronger antitumor effects in NK cells than did 1H6-NKCE treatment (Figures 4I–4K). Interestingly, 1H6-NKCE was also able to slightly activate T cells (Figure S12), suggesting that NKCEs have the potential to engage a broader range of immune cells.

1H6-15-NKCE induces NK cell survival and proliferation in vitro

To investigate the role of 1H6-15-NKCE in NK cell survival and proliferation, we analyzed changes in the transcriptional levels of genes related to NK cell proliferation. GO functional enrichment analysis showed that cell proliferation-related pathways were enriched in DEGs between 1H6-15-NKCE- and 1H6-NKCE-treated NK cells (Figure 5A). 1H6-15-NKCE improves antitumor activity and persistence of NK cells in vivo

After assessing the antitumor function of NKCEs in vitro, a T-ALL xenograft model was established to evaluate the cytotoxicity of NK cells induced by NKCEs in vivo. Non-obese diabetic-severe combined immunodeficiency IL2Rgnull (NSG) mice were injected with CCRF-CEM-ffLuc cells and randomly divided into the following groups: untreated, NK cells, NK cells with 1H6-NKCE, or NK cells with 1H6-15-NKCE. Mice were imaged for approximately 4 weeks to monitor tumor growth (Figure 6A). The tumor burden increased rapidly in untreated mice, with a median survival of 26 days (Figures 6B–6D). Mice that received only NK cells exhibited mild tumor control and eventually succumbed to tumor progression (Figures 6B–6D). Combination treatment with NK cells and 1H6-NKCE induced stronger antitumor responses than treatment with NK cells alone, especially at the early time points (Figures 6B and 6C). However, mice treated with NK cells and 1H6 or P60 showed a similar tumor burden to that of mice receiving NK cells alone, as assessed by an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). This indicates that effective control of T-ALL tumor progression could only be achieved by 1H6-NKCE, which crosslinks CD16 in the presence of target cells (Figures S16A and S16B). Notably, likely because of the limited survival of NK cells, the tumors could not be suppressed in the 1H6-NKCE-treated group starting from day 14 (Figure 6B). 1H6-15-NKCE exhibits strong antitumor activities without on-target off-tumor lysis against autologous T cells

CAR-T cell therapy, a promising approach, has been widely used for treating T cell malignancies, and its ability to lyse tumors has been demonstrated in preclinical research. However, on-target off-tumor toxicity remains a major challenge of CAR-T cell therapy for T cell malignancies.9,48 Due to NK cell function being controlled by surface receptors, which distinguish between healthy and stressed cells, we further explored whether 1H6-15-NKCE holds greater potential for clinical safety compared with CAR-T therapy. We designed a second-generation anti-CD5-CAR consisting of anti-CD5 scFv, a CD28 transmembrane spacer, the intracellular signaling domain 4-1BB, and CD3ζ. This CAR molecule was then linked to the same anti-CD5 scFv joined to the sequences encoding endoplasmic reticulum (ER) retention peptides through the 2A sequence and coupled in-frame with the GFP gene via another 2A (Figure S17A). The transduction efficiency of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells was characterized by detecting the expression of anti-CD5 scFv and GFP on T cells detected via flow cytometry (Figure S17B). As the anti-CD5 scFv was linked to an ER retention domain, this construct fastens the CD5 of CAR-T cells to the ER/Golgi,49,50 the surface expression of CD5 was blocked as expected, and these anti-CD5-CAR-T cells could proliferate while minimizing unintended fratricide (Figures S17C and S17D). Next, the cytotoxicities of 1H6-15-NKCE and anti-CD5-CAR-T cells to target and non-target tumor cells were compared at various E:T ratios. The results showed that the 1H6-15-NKCE induced stronger specific antitumor efficacy in vitro when NK cells and anti-CD5-CAR-T cells were added at equivalent doses (Figures 7A and S17E). Given that CD5 is expressed on both malignant and normal T cells, on-target off-tumor toxicity that may cause T cell aplasia of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells and NKCE-mediated-NK cells against autologous T cells was evaluated. The extent of cytotoxicity against ffLuc+ normal T cells was assessed after they were co-cultured with anti-CD5-CAR-T or 1H6-15-NKCE-induced NK cells for 48 h. In this assay, CCRF-CEM-ffLuc cells were included as a control to better understand whether there exists a difference in cell lysis generated by the CAR-T cells and antibody-mediated NK cells in tumor and normal cells. The results showed that the number of both residual T cells and tumor cells were significantly reduced after incubation with anti-CD5-CAR-T cells compared with incubation with mock-transduced T cells (MOCK-T) cells. However, when co-cultured with NK cells or NK cells treated with 1H6-15-NKCE, the cytotoxicity was only observed in tumor cells rather than normal T cells, indicating that 1H6-15-NKCE had no side effect on normal T cells (Figure 7C). To exclude any cytotoxicity of T cells masked by the proliferation stimulated by 1H6-15-NKCE, the killing assay of T cells was carried out by incubating freshly isolated PBMCs from healthy donors with 1H6-NKCE or 1H6-15-NKCE for 4 h. By comparing the proportion of CD5+CD3+ T cells at the beginning and end of the experiment, we found that the percentage of CD5+CD3+ T cells did not decrease after treatment with 1H6-NKCE or 1H6-15-NKCE (Figures S18A–S18C). Furthermore, isolated NK cells were co-cultured with activated normal T cells in the presence or absence of NKCEs for 24 h. The results showed that the proportion of viable T cells was not affected by adding NKCEs, when compared with adding only NK cells (Figures S18D–S18F). Therefore, the in vitro data indicated that the application of 1H6-15-NKCE to treat T cell malignancies is likely a safer immunotherapeutic alternative to anti-CD5-CAR-T therapy.

As NK cell function is regulated by the balance between activating and inhibitory receptor-ligand interactions on target cells, we further elucidate the possible mechanisms underlying this difference by phenotypic characterizing the inhibiting ligands (“classical” and “non-classical” major histocompatibility complex [MHC] class I molecules) and activating ligands on both normal T cells and tumor cells. Interestingly, cell-surface human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-E expression of T cells was higher compared with tumor cells, with an upregulation but no statistically significant difference observed for HLA-ABC (Figure S19A). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis of the expression of ligands for activating receptors, such as NKG2D, NKP30, and DNAM-1, confirmed the lower expression of some activating ligands, including B7-H6, ULBP1, ULBP2/5/6, and CD155, on T cells (Figures S19B and S19C). Although NKCEs may provide activation signals to normal T cells via CD16, the higher expression of inhibitory ligands and lower expression of certain activating ligands on normal T cells result in an overall activating signal that may not be sufficient to tip the balance toward NK cell activation, thus leading to no response to T cells.

1H6-15-NKCE triggers comparable in vivo antitumor activities with those of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells

The ability to suppress or eliminate malignant cells in vivo in xenograft mouse models may serve as a valuable predictor of therapeutic efficacy in patients. Therefore, we established an aggressive T-ALL xenograft mouse model to assess the antitumor effects of 1H6-15-NKCE in comparison with anti-CD5-CAR T cells. NSG mice were injected with MOLT-4-ffLuc cells, followed 3 days later by the infusion of equal numbers of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells, MOCK-T cells, NK cells, and NK cells with 1H6-15-NKCE (Figures 7D, S20A, and S20B). Both anti-CD5-CAR-T cells and NK cells with 1H6-15-NKCE demonstrated a potent ability to suppress the growth of tumor cells (Figures 7E and S20B–S20D). Twenty-eight days after tumor transplantation, the body weight of mice in the no treatment, MOCK-T cell, and NK cell groups began to sharply decline until death, whereas that of mice treated with either CAR-T cells or NK cells combined with 1H6-15-NKCE was not significantly reduced (Figure 7F). A direct comparison of mice treated with NK cells in combination with 1H6-15-NKCE and anti-CD5-CAR-T cells demonstrated improved survival time of NK cell-treated mice, with 2 of 5 mice that received NK cells and antibodies remaining alive 60 days post-treatment, whereas the group of anti-CD5-CAR-T cell-treated mice had a median survival of 52 days (Figure 7G). We further evaluated cytokine production release in mice serum, including interferon (IFN)-γ and IL-6. Both anti-CD5-CAR-T cells and NK cells treated with 1H6-15-NKCE produced IFN-γ detected in the serum, but anti-CD5-CAR-T cell treatment resulted in an increasing level of cytokine from day 3 to day 20. As for the production of IL-6, the limited level was detected in the treatment of NK cells in combination with 1H6-15-NKCE (Figure 7H). However, anti-CD5-CAR-T cells led to persistence from day 3 to day 20 that was associated with cytokine release syndrome.51 These results further confirm that co-administration of NK cells with 1H6-15-NKCE achieves durable antitumor efficacy in vivo with minimal toxicity, comparable to that observed with anti-CD5-CAR-T cells.

Discussion

T cell malignancies are a heterogeneous group of aggressive hematological tumors.1 NK cells, as critical effectors of the innate immune system, have been recognized for their crucial role in antitumor responses.14 However, the therapeutic potential of NK cells in treating T cell malignancies remains underutilized. In this study, we designed CD5-targeted NKCEs to trigger NK cells against CD5+ tumor cells. Our in vitro and in vivo data showed that NKCEs, particularly 1H6-15-NKCE, could mediate a notable antitumor response. Additionally, in vitro analyses showed that these NKCEs exhibited no on-target off-tumor effects, indicating that employing the NKCE to treat T cell malignancies is a promising therapeutic strategy.

In recent decades, T cell-based treatment strategies, including T cell-engaging antibody constructs, CAR-T cells, and checkpoint inhibitors, have been rapidly developed for treating hematological and solid malignancies.52,53,54 CAR-T cell therapy has been successfully used in treating patients with aggressive B cell malignancies,54 with Food and Drug Administration-approved products such as Yescarta (Kite pharma, USA) and Kymriah (Novartis, Switzerland).55 Although CAR-T cell therapy against T cell malignancies has been demonstrated to have antitumor efficacy in preclinical studies, the “on-target off-tumor” effect of CAR-T cells on autogenous T cells resulting from the common target antigens expressed on both cells raises concern about its potent safety problems, such as T cell aplasia.10,56 To overcome this obstacle, one alternative is to target antigens with limited expression in the normal T cell populations, such as CD30, CD1a, and TRBC157,58,59; however, these antigens are only expressed in a restricted subset of malignant cells, limiting the applicability of the strategy to all T cell malignancies.60 Additionally, isolating healthy T cells from malignant T cells is a complicated procedure that increases the risk of tumor cell modification by CAR.60 In the present study, we developed NKCEs to trigger NK cells to fight against CD5+ malignant T cells, which have rarely been utilized in treating T cell malignancies. In vitro results showed that our NKCEs could mediate superior antitumor efficacy while avoiding the killing of normal T cells in comparison with CD5-CAR-T cells. This is likely due to the NK cell recognition, and the integration of signals transmitted by activating and inhibitory receptors is complex. Except for the inhibitory MHC class I receptors on NK cells, which serve as a rheostat, regulating and dampening signals transduced through activating receptors, the outcome of NK cell response, when interacting with expressing ligands for both inhibitory and activating receptors, is determined by the balance of signals. Based on it, the expression of ligands on target tumor cells and T cells for activating and inhibitory receptors was analyzed.61,62 Our results showed that, compared with the expression in target tumor cells, the expression of non-classical MHC I molecules was higher and the expression of B7-H6, ULBP1, ULBP2/5/6, and CD155 was lower in T cells, indicating that the likely lifted inhibitory NKG2A-HLA-E axis and dropped activating signals by NKP30, NKG2D, and DNAM-1 receptors may determine the non-activation status of NK cells. Notably, when transfecting NK cells with CAR constructs containing intracytoplasmic immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs, the NK cells may be directly activated by the intracellular signal domain and lose their ability to recognize healthy cells controlled by surface receptors, leading to the NK cell-mediated killing of normal T cells.15 Unlike CAR-NK treatment, which requires complicated gene manipulation and exerts side effects on normal T cells, NKCEs are produced through a simple manufacturing process that does not modify NK cells.

Although 1H6-15-NKCE shares structural similarities with some IL-15-NKCEs, such as GTB-3650 developed by GT Biopharma,63 which has been reported to be composed of a VHH targeting CD16 (both CD16a and CD16b), a wild-type rhIL-15, and an scFv targeting a tumor antigen, our design incorporates several improvements and advantages. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD16b, existing as a soluble receptor in serum, may trigger side effects via the formation of immune complexes in vivo upon binding to antibodies.30,64 In addition, antibodies that bind to the CD16b on neutrophils could restrict the ADCC activity of NK cells against cancer cells.65 To overcome these limitations, we developed a CD16a-engaged NKCE by screening for a VHH that could bind specifically to CD16a, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential side effects. Most CD16-NKCEs that are scFv linked or scFv armed with cytokines, lack the Fc domain and may have a short lifespan in the serum.66,67,68,69,70 In contrast, our NKCEs were designed to fuse the VHH to a human Fc domain, allowing FcRn binding and extending serum half-life.71 Importantly, incorporation of the Fc domain confers bivalency to our antibody design, but NK-NK lysis was not observed in this study. Thus, our NKCEs could prevent the reduction of NK cells’ retention time. Furthermore, IL-15 and IL-15 Rα interaction domains were used as the functional protein scaffold for generating 1H6-15-NKCE. Pre-associating IL-15 to the soluble IL-15Rα in a single-chain format can markedly increase the biological activity and longer half-life compared with that of IL-15 alone.72 Our in vitro and in vivo data showed that the cytotoxicity of NK cells could be improved in the presence of 1H6-15-NKCE. This may result from a higher number of NK cell-mediated antitumor signaling pathways induced by 1H6-15-NKCE compared with that induced by 1H6-NKCE, including cytokine signaling pathways. Transcriptomic differences also suggested that the regulatory mechanism of IL-15 is different from that of CD16. In addition, genes involved in DNA replication and the NK cell cycle were upregulated following 1H6-15-NKCE treatment, demonstrating the positive regulatory function of IL-15 on NK cell proliferation. These findings indicate that our design optimizations not only provided our NKCEs with better therapeutic effects but also conferred benefits to 1H6-15-NKCE at the molecular level.

Our results also demonstrated the ability of NKCEs to activate NK cells through ADCC activity. Notably, by detecting the activation marker CD69 on the surface of PBMCs, we observed weaker activation of T cells. This is likely because CD16a is also expressed on a subset of T cells, such as γδ T cells.73 Although our experiments did not demonstrate the killing effect of activated T cells on tumor cells, our data still suggested that CD16 could serve as a dual-activating target for NK cells and a small subset of T cells. In addition, 1H6-15-NKCE promoted proliferation of normal T cells, likely due to CD5 expression, which allows 1H6-15-NKCE to bind and activate the IL-15 signaling pathway via receptor chains on the T cell surface. Collectively, these findings highlight an approach that may enable future combination immunotherapies involving both T cells and NK cells.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. The specific anti-CD16a VHH used in our study could bind to and activate NK cells; however, the binding affinity of CD16a (158V)-ECD was 9.6 nM. If affinity maturation strategies,74 including randomization mutation, antibody phage display, and artificial intelligence tools,75 are applied to improve the affinity of this antibody, stronger potency of our NKCEs may be observed. Although CD16 induces strong NK cell antitumor functions, its expression is negatively regulated by metalloproteinases, such as ADAM17. This regulation leads to decreased CD16a expression and makes CD16a-engaged NKCEs less effective.76 Incorporating other NK cell receptors, such as NKG2D and NKp46, into NKCE design may help overcome the limitations associated with CD16a dysfunction. Furthermore, although our results demonstrated that NK cells mediated by 1H6-15-NKCE could trigger durable antitumor efficacy with minimal toxicities compared with CD5-CAR-T cells in vivo, a multi-dimensional evaluation for potential toxicity studies by other preclinical models, including human immune system-engrafted models, genetically engineered mouse models, and even syngeneic mouse models, could be combined in the future.77,78,79 Additionally, as NK cells and T cells represent different immune cell types, comparing their antitumor efficacy is complex and can be affected by various factors, such as differences in cell phenotype caused by generation and expansion methods and the number of effector cells used in pharmacodynamic evaluations. For a more rigorous head-to-head comparison, further standardization and experimental considerations could be required.80 Despite these limitations, our findings support the application of NKCEs as a promising approach to treat T cell malignancies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and primary tumor cells

The CCRF-CEM, Jurkat, MOLT-4, Raji, HEK293T, SP2/0, Sup-T1, MAVER-1, and HeLa cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). SP2/0, CCRF-CEM, Jurkat, MOLT-4, Sup-T1, MAVER-1, and Raji cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). HeLa and HEK293T cells were cultured with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and GlutaMax (Gibco, USA). All cells were incubated at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HeLa cells transduced with a human CD5-expressing lentivirus vector or a human CD16a/CD16b expressing lentivirus vector were named HeLa-CD5 or HeLa-CD16a/CD16b, respectively. CCRF-CEM-ffLuc, Jurkat-ffLuc, MOLT-4-ffLuc, and Raji-FFluc cells were stable lines transfected with the lentiviral vector containing the luciferase reporter gene. Peripheral blood samples or bone marrow aspirates from patients with T-ALL received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the West China Hospital of Sichuan University. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For this study, six human participants were included. Four male patients, aged 18, 22, 29, and 37 years. Two female patients, aged 19 and 31 years. The potential influence of sex or gender on the results was not assessed due to limited sample size.

Mice and In vivo studies

Female NSG mice and BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). All experimental procedures were performed in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Sichuan University and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University. Only female mice were used in this study to minimize variability. Therefore, potential sex-based differences were not evaluated. To assess the anti-tumor effect of NKCEs in vivo, CCRF-CEM or MOLT-4 xenograft models were established. NSG mice were injected with 4× 105 CCRF-ffLuc cells or 1× 106 MOLT-4-ffLuc via tail vein (i.v.). After allowing the tumor cells to establish for 3 days, tumor-bearing mice were randomized into different groups and received NK cell or T cell population (5× 106 cells/mouse). NK cell therapy was supported with human IL-2 (10000 IU/mouse, every 2–3 days for 2 weeks). For some assays, CAR-T therapy was also supported with the same dose of IL-2. Following the injection of NK cells, NKCEs (20 μg/mouse) were administered intravenously every other day for 2 weeks. IVIS Imaging was performed on the mice using a Tanon ABL X5 (Tanan Science & Technology Co. Ltd. China) or an IVScope 8500 (CLiNX Co. Ltd. China) in vivo imaging system. For experiments tracking the in vivo persistence of NK cells, other groups of tumor-bearing mice were used. Blood was drawn weekly, and the spleens were taken out after the mice were sacrificed. The data was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Method details

Plasmids

The pCDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen, USA) was used for the expression of recombinant proteins. The pVAX1 vector (Invitrogen, USA) was utilized for the expression of antibodies. The pMECS vector used for the construction of the VHH phage library was kindly provided by Professor Qin Zhao (College of Veterinary Medicine, Northwest A&F University). The PLVX-puro was employed for the generation of stable transfected cells. The pSLCAR-CD19-BBz vector (Addgene, USA) was used as the backbone for construction of anti-CD5-CAR.

Human recombinant protein production and purification

The DNA sequences encoding the human CD5-ECD, human NKP46-ECD, human CD16a (158F/158V)-ECD, and human CD16b (NA1/NA2)-ECD were synthesized (Sangon Biotech, China) and subcloned into the pCDNA3.1 vector with a C-terminal 6×His tag for purification. The recombinant proteins were obtained by transiently transforming the constructed vector into HEK293T cells using polyethylenimine (PEI, Polysciences, USA) and cultured in 293 Freestyle medium (Gibco, USA) for 5 days. The proteins were purified from the culture supernatant with Ni-NTA beads (GE Healthcare, USA). The molecular weight and purity of proteins were determined by SDS-PAGE.

Generation of anti-human CD5-scFv fusion proteins

Female BALB/c mice were immunized with CD5–ECD–His recombinant protein every 2 weeks for a total of five injections. The antigen used for immunization was mixed with either a complete (first injection) or incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (second to fifth injections) to improve the immune response. After evaluating the immune titer of the serum, hybridoma cells were produced by fusing spleen cells with the mouse myeloma cell line SP2/0. Positive hybridoma clones producing the target antibodies were subcloned by limiting dilution (two to three rounds) to ensure monoclonality. The sequence of the anti-CD5-scFv was obtained by linking the amplified variable regions of the light and heavy chains from positive clones using a (G4S)3 linker and then cloned to the pVAX-hFc (human IgG4) vector, followed by validation via sequencing. Finally, the anti-CD5-scFv-hFc fusion proteins were transiently expressed by CHO-S cells and purified using Protein A + G Agarose (Beyotime, China). The purity of all recombinant proteins was analyzed via SDS-PAGE.

VHH library construction and identification of anti-human CD16 nanobodies

The VHH phage library screening of anti-CD16a antibodies was constructed following our previously reported lab protocol.81 After four rounds of phage panning, ELISA was conducted to identify the clones that were only able to bind CD16a. The only candidate clone was selected and then sequenced based on the amino acid sequences of its complementarity-determining regions.

Specificity and affinity analysis of anti-human CD5-scFv and anti-human CD16a-VHH fusion antibodies

To determine the binding specificity of anti-CD5-scFv fusion proteins, ELISA was conducted. The CD5-ECD-His protein (2 μg/mL, 100 μL) was coated on the ELISA plates (ThermoFisher, USA) overnight at 4°C. On the following day, serially diluted fusion antibodies were added to the plate and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. After washing the plates, HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (H + L) (Jackson, USA) was used to detect the substrate TMB in a chromogenic reaction. The binding specificity of anti-CD16a-VHH fusion antibodies was tested similarly using the above method. As for phage ELISA for P60, HRP-conjugated anti-M13 antibody was used to test the substrate TMB in a chromogenic reaction. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was then used to determine whether anti-CD5-scFv or anti-CD16a-VHH fusion antibodies could bind innate CD5+ or CD16+ cells, respectively. FITC-conjugated goat anti-human IgG(H + L) antibody (Proteintech, USA) or FITC-AffiniPure F(ab')2 Fragment Goat Anti-Human IgG (H + L) antibody (Jackson, USA) was used as the secondary detection antibody. Finally, IF assays were used for further evaluation of the binding specificity. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany). Affinity analysis of NKCEs was also conducted using this approach.

Production and purification of NKCEs

The sequence encoding anti-CD16a VHH or anti-human CD16a VHH-IL15Rα/IL15 was amplified and spliced into the pVAX-hFc (human IgG4) vector between the Age I/ECOR I restriction endonuclease sites (N-terminal of the Fc fragment) to construct a plasmid called pVAX-16 or pVAX-16-15. Next, the sequence of anti-CD5 scFv was cloned into pVAX-16 or pVAX-16-15 at the Sal I restriction endonuclease site (C-terminal of the Fc fragment) to generate the final vector called 1H6-NKCE or 1H6-15-NKCE. The DNA sequence was verified to confirm the sequence and location of gene insertion. Production and purification of NKCEs were performed as described for anti-CD5-scFv proteins.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The affinity constants (KD values) of anti-CD5-scFv fusion proteins and the NKCE to the CD5-ECD protein were determined via SPR using the Biacore 8K instrument (GE Healthcare, USA), following the previously published method.82 Briefly, anti-mouse antibodies were immobilized onto a CM5 sensor chip using the Amine Coupling Kit Type 2 (Cytiva, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, purified recombinant CD5-ECD-mFc protein was captured onto the immobilized antibodies. For kinetic analysis, serial dilutions of antibodies (from 0.78 nM to 12.5 nM) were flow through the immobilized proteins and allowed to dissociate before regeneration, with a reference channel serving as the control. Kinetic constants were calculated using the BIAevaluation software (GE Healthcare, USA).

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI)

BLI measurements were performed using GatorPrime (GatorBio, USA). Protein A probes (GatorBio, USA) were soaked for at least 10 min in PBST (PBS supplement with 0.02% Tween 20). The anti-CD16a VHH fusion protein and the NKCE were diluted to 5 μg/mL in PBST and loaded onto the Protein A probe until the shift reached 0.5–1 nm. After a 60s equilibration phase, the binding kinetics of the CD16a (158V), CD16a (158F), CD16b (NA1), and CD16b (NA2) antigens were monitored at multiple concentrations (from 6.25 nM to 100 nM). Each concentration was tested for 180 s of association and 300 s of disassociation. The obtained data were subtracted from the reference and fitted to a 1:1 binding model using the GatorOne software (GatorBio, USA).

Isolation of human NK cells

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll (GE Healthcare, USA). NK cells were enriched from PBMCs through magnetic selection with CytoSinct CD3 human nanobeads (Genscript, China) and CytoSinct CD56 human nanobeads (Genscript, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of NK cells was determined via flow cytometry.

Generation of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells

CAR-expressing lentivirus was generated by transforming HEK293T cells with pSLCAR lentivirus plasmids, gag/pol-expressing plasmids psPAX2, and packaging plasmids pMD2G using PEI reagent (Polysciences, USA), as previously described.81 PBMCs isolated from healthy donors were cultured in X-VIVO 15 medium (Lonza, Switzerland) supplemented with heat-inactivated 10% FBS and activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) for 72 h. Subsequently, T cells were transduced with CAR-expressing lentivirus in a plate pre-coated with RetroNectin protein (Takara, Japan) in the presence of interleukin-7 (IL-7) and IL-15 (10 ng/mL; Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). The transfection efficiency of anti-CD5-CAR-T cells was determined by the expression of GFP and anti-CD5 scFv using FACS. For some experiments, activated T cells were transduced with a luciferase reporter gene-encoding lentivirus to obtain luciferase+ T cells.

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested and stained according to related protocols. Phenotypic analysis is based on positive gating cell populations. All flow cytometry was conducted on a NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (Agilent, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software or NovoCyte Express software.

Cytotoxicity assays

Analysis of direct cytotoxicity via FACS

NK cells were incubated with target tumor cell lines or primary tumor cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1 for 4 h at 37°C in the presence or absence of NKCEs. Cell mixtures at the beginning and end of incubation were harvested and stained with APC-conjugated anti-human CD56 antibody (Biolegend, USA) or BV421-conjugated anti-human CD56 antibody (BD, USA) for 30 min at 4°C. The percentage of CD56− cells represented the residual level of target cells. For the evaluation of the potential side effects of NKCE-redirected NK cells on normal T cells, cell mixtures collected at the start and end of incubation were harvested and stained with PE-conjugated anti-human CD5 and BV421-conjugated anti-human CD3 antibodies (Biolegend, USA).

Luciferase-based cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity of NKCEs against target tumor cells was evaluated using NK cells or PBMCs as effector cells. CCRF-CEM-ffLuc, Jurkat-ffLuc, MOLT-4-ffLuc, or Raji-ffLuc cells were incubated with NK cells or PBMCs at different E:T ratios in the presence of a defined concentration of NKCEs for 4 h at 37°C. For anti-CD5-CAR-T cell killing assays, target cells were incubated with CAR-T cells or MOCK-T cells at E:T ratios ranging from 1:2 to 4:1 for 4 h. For the on-target off-tumor killing lysis for autologous T cells, T cells transfected with the luciferase gene were incubated with immune effector cells at the E:T ratio of 1:1 for 48 h. Luciferase-based assays were performed using the Steady-Glo Luciferase assay system (Promega, USA), and the value of luminescence was detected using a Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (CLARIOstar, Germany).

Real-time cell analysis (RTCA)

The in vitro long-time cytotoxicity of NKCE-redirected NK cells or anti-CD5-CAR-T cells was assessed using the label-free RTCA instrument (ACEA Biosciences, USA). Initially, the baseline was determined by adding 50 μL of cell-culture medium to each well of the 96-E-Plate (ACEA Biosciences, USA). Target cells at a density of 5×104 cells/mL (100 μL) were inoculated in the plate and cultured at 37°C until the cell index (CI) value exceeded 1.0. Next, immune effector cells were added to the plate at an indicated E:T ratio in a total volume of 200 μL. Subsequently, the real-time CI measurements were continuously recorded and monitored for approximately 45–60 h. Finally, the data collection and analysis were carried out using RTCA Software 2.1 (ACEA Biosciences, USA).

Calcein-release cytotoxicity

Calcein-release cytotoxicity assays were performed as previously described.83 Briefly, target cells were labeled with 2 μM calcein AM (Beyotime, China) in RPMI 1640 medium at 37°C for 30 min. After being washed, they were incubated with effector cells in a total volume of 200 μL at various E:T ratios in the absence or presence of NKCEs. For the assessment of NK cell fratricide, calcein-labeled primary human NK cells were incubated with autologous NK cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1 in the absence or presence of antibodies. After 4 h of incubation, the calcein fluorescence released into the supernatant was measured using a plate reader at 520 nm (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices).

NK cell activation assay

This test involved using the CD107a degranulation marker and the CD69 activation marker to assess the activation of NK cells by NKCEs. To detect the CD69 activation marker, fresh PBMCs were co-cultured with or without target cells at an E:T ratio of 10:1 for 24 h in the presence or absence of 50 nM NKCEs. The cells were then washed and stained with PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-human CD3, FITC-conjugated anti-human CD56, and APC-conjugated anti-human CD69 antibodies (Biolegend, USA) at 4°C for 30 min. After washing the cells twice, the percentage of CD3-/CD56+/CD69+ and CD3+/CD69+ cells represented the level of activation of NK and T cells, respectively. For the evaluation of CD107a degranulation marker, purified NK cells were incubated with or without the target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1 at 37°C for 4 h in the presence or absence of 10 nM antibodies. An hour later, GolgiStop (BD, USA) was added to the medium to inhibit intracellular protein transport. Finally, the cells stained with FITC-conjugated anti-human CD56 antibody and APC-conjugated anti-CD107a human antibody (Biolegend, USA) were analyzed via flow cytometry. CD56+/CD107a+ cells were identified as degranulated NK cells.

Cytokine release assay

NK cells and target tumor cells were co-incubated at an E:T ratio of 1:1 in culture medium in the presence or absence of antibodies for 24 h. The levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB were detected by harvesting the supernatant of the co-cultured system using an ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Proliferation assay

NK or T cells isolated from healthy donors were labeled with CellTrace CFSE (Invitrogen, C34554) and then treated with NKCEs or IL-15 in medium supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated AB serum (Access Cell Culture Grifols, USA) at 37°C. After 7 days, cells were washed and stained with Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit (biolegend, USA), followed by anti-human CD56-APC and anti-human CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5 antibodies (biolegend, USA). Viable cells were gated to analyze proliferation.

RNA sequencing

PBMCs isolated from healthy donors were incubated with target CCRF-CEM cells at an E:T ratio of 10:1 for 16 h. After incubation, NK cells were purified with CD3 and CD56 nanobeads, and the purity was verified by flow cytometry. Then, total RNA was extracted from the purified NK cells using TRIzol Reagent. The RNA-seq transcriptome library was prepared following Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation (San Diego, CA) using 1 μg of total RNA. The sequencing library was constructed on a NovaSeq X Plus platform using NovaSeq Reagent K according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality controlled using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) with default parameters. The clean reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens genome (GRCh38.p14, https://mart.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Info/Index) using HiSat2 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml). To identify DEGs between two samples, the expression level of each transcript was calculated according to the transcripts per million reads (TPM) method. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/stats/bioc/DESeq2/). DEGs with |log2(FC)|≥1 and FDR<0.05 were considered to be significant DEGs. In addition, GO (http://geneontology.org/) functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using Goatools (https://github.com/tanghaibao/GOatools). GO terms were considered to have significant enrichment, when the Bonferroni-corrected P-value was <0.05 compared with the whole-transcriptome back. GSEA (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp) was applied to rank genes based on the shrunken limma log2 fold changes. The GSEA tool was used in the “pre-ranked” model with default parameters.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). Sample numbers and other information (number of replicates and specific statistical tests) are indicated in the respective figure legends. Student’s t test was used to compare two groups with normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann-Whitney tests used to compare two groups with non-normally distributed continuous variables. A one-way ANOVA was used to compare three or more groups, and a two-way ANOVA was used to compare three or more groups with two independent variables. The post hoc tests used for multiple comparisons are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Survival curve analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and p-values were determined using the log rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Significance is indicated as follows: p > 0.05 (ns: no significance); ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. For statistical analyses that require pairwise comparisons among multiple groups, the lettering method is also used to indicate significant differences, groups showing significant differences (α = 0.05) are assigned different lowercase letters to indicate statistical significance.
