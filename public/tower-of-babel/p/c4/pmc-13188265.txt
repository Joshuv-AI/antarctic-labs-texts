Atrial fibrillation versus non-atrial fibrillation in postoperative complications following lumbar surgery: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Chaojun Wang, Yiming Mai, Jimei Zhang, Jian Wang, Jiong Lin, Qinfeng Yang, Qin Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith an aging population and high prevalence rate of atrial fibrillation (AF) in older individuals, more elderly patients with AF receive lumbar operations. Nevertheless, there has been little study discussing the perioperative outcomes of AF in lumbar surgery, especially using a national database.MethodsA population-based, retrospective cohort survey was conducted on adults receiving lumbar surgery who were divided into 2 cohorts based on the diagnosis of AF or not, using the 2016–2019 Nationwide Inpatient Sample database. Propensity score matching was applied to eliminate selection bias. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to explore the impact of AF on postoperative complications following AF.ResultsOverall, 222,557 lumbar surgical patients were finally involved in the present study, 10,929 (4.9%) of whom had AF. Irrespective of potential confounders, the in-hospital death risk of patients undergoing lumbar surgery with AF was 1.75-fold compared to those without AF. Additionally, AF was an apparently independent risk factor for cardiac arrest, postoperative delirium, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute respiratory failure, continuous trauma ventilation, acute renal failure, urinary tract infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, blood transfusion, pyemia, septicemia, death, wound infection, and hemorrhage/hematoma/seroma.ConclusionAF seems to be associated with increased perioperative mortality and complications. Orthopedists should be cognizant of the adverse impact on patients with AF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-026-00678-1.

Background

Due to the aging population, the demand for lumbar fusion and decompression procedures for degenerative disease with the symptoms of pain and impairment continuously rises (Alpert 2022, Laubach et al. 2022). There are over 4.83 million patients undergoing spine surgery procedures around the world each year. From 2004 to 2015, the rate of lumbar spine fusion surgeries among US adults has gone up, climbing from 60.4 per 100,000 to 79.8 per 100,000 (Yoshida et al. 2023). As surgical volumes grow, the absolute number of perioperative complications—particularly cardiovascular events—is also expected to rise.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) currently affects approximately 1–2% people of the global population, and its prevalence is estimated to double in the next five decades due to an aging population (Camm et al. 2010). Furthermore, it is associated with serious adverse cardiovascular events, such as thrombo-embolic events and heart failure, affecting the quality of life of these patients. Additionally, AF can lead to dementia or cognitive decline, especially in older patients (Knecht et al. 2008, Bunch 2020).

Postoperative AF poses a significant risk of increased mortality, hospital readmission, stroke, heart failure, and higher healthcare costs in surgical and general populations (LaPar et al. 2014, Keller et al. 2018, Issa et al. 2015). Perioperative AF has also been reported to worsen postoperative outcomes in orthopedic procedures such as knee endoprosthetic surgery (Eun et al. 2011). Beyond general surgical stress, lumbar spine surgery may pose specific cardiovascular risks. Emerging evidence suggests that intraoperative manipulation of the dura mater or nerve roots can trigger a spinal-cardiac reflex, leading to bradycardia and hypotension (Sultan et al. 2023). Additionally, significant intraoperative blood loss has been associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiac events after lumbar spine fusion (Kaye et al. 2018). To date, only isolated case reports have described AF-related complications after lumbar spine surgery, such as thromboembolic events in a patient with chronic AF undergoing posterior lumbar decompression (Wang et al. 2003).

Despite the growing burden of both lumbar spine surgery and AF in the aging population, large-scale studies examining the impact of AF on perioperative outcomes after lumbar procedures remain lacking. Therefore, the objective of this study is to examine the impact of AF on the occurrence of complications among all patients who were hospitalized and underwent lumbar fusion or decompression surgery between 2016 and 2019.

Methods

Data source

The Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) was used in this study to include data on more than 25 million hospitalized patients in the United States from 2016 to 2019. It includes demographics, payment type, and clinical data, including International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis and procedure codes. A key advantage of NIS is that it is the largest all-payer inpatient database in the United States, collecting 20% of medical records from all inpatient discharges in American community hospitals (excluding long-term acute care and rehabilitation hospitals), representing nearly 8 million admissions each year (Conen et al. 2020). Since NIS contains completely de-identified medical record data, this study was granted exempt status by our Institutional Review Board.

Data collection

We initially identified patients aged ≥ 18 years with a primary ICD-10 procedure code for either lumbar fusion (0 S.G0-0 S.G1) or lumbar vertebral decompression (0 S.T2-0 S.T4, 0 S.B2-0 S.B4) from 2016 to 2019 (n = 236,939). We excluded patients with missing data for age or those younger than 18 years (n = 6,049), missing sex (n = 34), missing primary payer (n = 256), or missing race (n = 8,043). The final study cohort comprised 222,557 patients, who were then stratified into two groups based on the presence of an AF diagnosis: an AF cohort (n = 10,929; ICD-10-CM codes I48.0, I48.1, I48.2, I48.91) and a non-AF cohort (n = 211,628).

Outcomes

We used the ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes to identify perioperative complications. The term “any complication” was defined as the occurrence of one or more complications. Medical complications included acute myocardial infarction (AMI), cardiac arrest, acute heart failure, shock, acute cerebrovascular disease, postoperative delirium (POD), pneumonia, pulmonary embolism (PE), acute respiratory failure, continuous trauma ventilation, acute renal failure (ARF), urinary retention, urinary tract infection (UTI), gastrointestinal bleeding (GI bleeding), blood transfusion, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), thrombocytopenia, pyemia and septicemia. Surgical complications included wound infection, wound rupture, hemorrhage/seroma/hematoma, and nerve injuries (Table 1). In addition, all complications and mortality were assessed during the index hospitalization. The NIS does not provide post‑discharge follow‑up; therefore, events occurring after discharge (e.g., 30-or 90-day complications) could not be evaluated.

Propensity score matching analysis

To eliminate the confounding impact of demographic variables and comorbidities, covariates were entered into the propensity score, including demographics (age, race, sex, payment type, and hospital characteristics) and pre-existing comorbidities available in the database. Patients in the AF cohort were matched 1:1 with patients in the non-AF cohort using a caliper width of 0.05 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score via the greedy (nearest-neighbor without replacement) matching method. After matching, two comparable cohorts were created: a matched AF cohort (n = 10,840) and a matched non-AF cohort (n = 10,840).

Data analysis

Between the AF and non-AF cohorts and between the matched AF and matched non-AF cohorts, demographics, comorbidities, perioperative complications, and mortality were compared. The Pearson chi-square test was performed for univariate analysis of all categorical variables. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. To control for demographic variables and comorbidities, covariates were matched in a 1:1 propensity score. Multivariate logistic analyses were used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using R version 4.3.2. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline patient and providers’ characteristics

Using the NIS database, we identified 236,939 patients who underwent lumbar fusion or decompression surgery between 2016 and 2019. After excluding 22,415 patients with missing demographic data, the final analytic cohort comprised 222,557 patients (Fig. 1). Among these, 10,929 patients (4.9%) had a concomitant diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (AF). The proportion of patients with AF undergoing lumbar spine surgery increased from 4.5% in 2016 to 5.1% in 2019 (Fig. 2).

Propensity score-matched cohort analysis

In this study, 10,840 AF patients were successfully matched with non-AF patients (Table 2; see Table S1 for full details). After matching, the absolute standardized difference of all measurement confounding factors was < 10%, indicating successful matching and group balance.

Outcomes after matching

The results (Figs. 3 and 4) of the multivariate logistic analyses were consistent with the propensity-matched analysis. After adjustments for possible confounding factors, the AF cohort demonstrated a higher risk of any complication (OR, 1.301; 95% CI, 1.228 to 1.378; P < 0.001), any medical complication (OR, 1.296; 95% CI, 1.223–1.374; P < 0.001); and any surgical complication (OR, 1.316; 95% CI, 1.098 to 1.578; P = 0.003). Moreover, compared with non-AF, AF patients were more likely to die during hospitalization (OR, 1.751; 95% CI, 1.240 to 2.471; P = 0.001). In addition, AF was a risk factor for the below medical complications: cardiac arrest (OR, 1.748; 95%CI, 1.087–2.810; P = 0.021), POD (OR, 1.252; 95% CI, 1.053 to 1.488; P = 0.011), pneumonia (OR, 1.418; 95% CI, 1.164 to 1.728; P < 0.001), PE (OR, 1.700; 95% CI, 1.258 to 2.298; P < 0.001), acute respiratory failure (OR, 1.469; 95% CI, 1.225 to 1.761; P < 0.001), continuous trauma ventilation (OR, 1.687; 95%CI, 1.359 to 2.093; P < 0.001), acute renal failure (OR, 1.291; 95% CI, 1.168 to 1.426; P < 0.001), UTI (OR, 1.322; 95% CI, 1.158 to 1.509; P < 0.001) GI bleeding (OR, 2.058; 95% CI, 1.204 to 3.519; P = 0.008), blood transfusion (OR, 1.301; 95% CI, 1.228 to 1.291; P < 0.001), pyemia (OR, 1.351; 95% CI, 1.120 to 1.629; P = 0.002) and septicemia (OR, 1.335; 95% CI, 1.107 to 1.610; P = 0.003). It was found that patients with AF have an evaluated risk of such surgical complications: hemorrhage/hematoma/seroma (OR, 1.435; 95%CI, 1.060 to 1.941; P = 0.019), and wound infection (OR, 1.621; 95%CI, 2.489 to 1.378; P = 0.027).

Discussion

The demand for lumbar surgery is steadily increasing, driven by an aging population with persistent pain and impairment due to degenerative diseases that no longer respond to conservative management. Given the increasing prevalence of AF with age (Fig. 5) and its established association with adverse outcomes, the growing demand for lumbar surgery among elderly patients has important implications for this high-risk population. However, few studies have demonstrated the relationship between AF and outcomes in lumbar spine surgery, especially using large-scale databases. A rare case report of a 73-year-old man with AF was detected arterial thromboembolism after lumbar decompressive surgery (Wang et al. 2003). Our study included 10,840 patients with AF and 10,840 matched controls and found that AF patients had a significantly higher mortality and incidence of complications. To our knowledge, it is the first large population-based survey to investigate how AF affects the outcomes of lumbar surgery in matched patients, controlling for various potential confounders. It is also the first study to investigate the complete impact of pre-existing AF on the postoperative outcomes.

AF is a common cardiac arrhythmia characterized by rapid and irregular atrial electrical activity, resulting in ineffective atrial contractions, irregular ventricular rhythm, and decreased cardiac output (Camm et al. 2010). This AF-induced hemodynamic instability may exacerbate the risk of cardiac arrest by predisposing patients to myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, which is in agreement with our result. Wang et al. (Wang et al. 2003) found that 16% of the 921 patients with AF subsequently developed congestive heart failure in the Framingham Heart Study. Conen et al. (Conen et al. 2020) found that patients who develop new postoperative atrial fibrillation face an elevated risk of MI during the initial year following surgery. While the observed increase in postoperative AMI or acute heart failure did not reach statistical significance, this may reflect the relatively low incidence of these events and the corresponding limitations in statistical power. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to definitively assess these risks.

Anticoagulation therapy is essential for AF patients to mitigate acute cerebrovascular disease and thromboembolic risk, yet it invariably escalates bleeding complications. This correlation is well-established in surgical contexts. A propensity score-matched cohort of spine surgery patients revealed that long-term oral anticoagulation confers a 3.4-fold increase in postoperative bleeding risk versus controls (95%CI, 1.3 to 9.0) (Sweda et al. 2024). A population-based study of 83,839 patients undergoing elective spine surgery found that prophylactic aspirin (OR, 1.48; 95%CI, 1.17 to 1.86) and unfractionated heparin (OR, 2.01; 95%CI, 1.81 to 2.24) were significantly associated with increased odds of blood transfusion (Fiasconaro et al. 2021). Our findings are concordant, showing AF association with increased risks of blood transfusion, hemorrhage, hematoma, seroma, and GI bleeding.

Our observation of an increased of PE among patients with AF may be explained by hemodynamics and coagulation related to AF. In AF, the rapid and irregular atria quiver result in ineffective atrial contraction, which decreases cardiac output. Furthermore, it reduces venous return and worsens venous stasis. Additionally, thrombi that form in the atrial appendage can fall off and lead to embolism (Savelieva et al. 2007). Both of these can result in DVT and PE. However, we found no notable differences in the increased risk of DVT or acute cerebrovascular disease. These results are in accordance with findings reported by Wang et al. (Wang et al. 2015), who reported that AF elevated the risk of PE but did not pose a similar threat to DVT without comorbidities. It indicated that the occurrence of DVT may require a combination or complex interaction of AF and other comorbidities. This finding is consistent with a guideline that the cumulative risk of stroke in patients with “AF alone” without a clinical history or echocardiographic evidence of cardiovascular disease is low (Camm et al. 2010). Ammar et al. conducted a case-control study of 110 patients with AF and concluded that AF is secondary to stroke (Killu et al. 2019). However, Leif et al. (Friberg and Svennberg 2020) reported that AF was not associated with a higher risk of PE alone. We speculate that this might be due to the exclusion of atrial fibrillation OAC treatment. Given the limitation of our database, we could not divide the AF patients into the OAC treatment group and without OAC group. Leif et al. (Friberg and Svennberg 2020) concluded OAC treatment was associated with a reduction of PE. However, given the limited evidence specifically examining this relationship in the context of lumbar spine surgery, further studies are warranted to validate our hypothesis. If confirmed, these findings would support the consideration of OAC therapy for PE prophylaxis in high-risk surgical patients with AF.

Our cohort-match study adds evidence that there are significant difference in risk of acute respiratory failure and continuous trauma ventilation with a comparison of L-AF and non-L-AF. Postoperative PE may contribute to acute respiratory failure under the mechanism of ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch resulting from vascular obstruction and pulmonary vasoconstriction (Fukunaga et al. 2012).

Among the general population, atrial fibrillation is related to poor renal function outcomes and is one of the most common disorders in patients at risk for renal impairment (Soliman et al. 2010, Villgran et al. 2022). Consistently, we found that AF was associated with acute renal failure, and the possibility of acute renal failure is 1.3-fold compared with patients without AF (irrespective of basic information and 29 comorbidities). This may be strongly associated with acute/subacute hemodynamic changes due to AF. Furthermore, myocardial fibrosis and similar modifications in the kidney are also promoted by AF (Watanabe et al. 2009, Stamellou and Floege 2021). The hypothesis of pathogenic mechanisms is still to be verified by further studies. Furthermore, consistent with the results shown by Wei-Syun et al., who exclaimed that AF elevated the risk of the aforementioned adverse renal outcomes. In this regard, the clinical relevance and significance are clear; patients after lumbar spine surgery with AF had a higher risk of acute renal failure. Therefore, clinicians should maintain a heightened awareness of this risk when managing patients with AF undergoing lumbar spine surgery. Closer perioperative monitoring of renal function, and consideration of nephroprotective strategies where appropriate, may be warranted in this high-risk population. In addition, we found that AF increased the risk of sepsis.

Limitation

While the large sample size is a strength of this study, several important limitations should be considered. First, as an analysis of administrative data, our findings are associative rather than causal. Moreover, our reliance on ICD-10-CM codes for case ascertainment carries inherent risks of misclassification or undercoding of AF, procedures, and complications.

Second, the NIS lacks granular clinical and intraoperative data. We were unable to account for potential confounders such as surgical technique (e.g., open versus minimally invasive), number of fused levels, operative duration, estimated blood loss, anesthesia type, or surgeon volume. The database also lacks critical clinical details—including AF subtype (e.g., paroxysmal versus persistent), disease severity, perioperative anticoagulation management, and the precise timing of complication onset. The absence of these variables introduces the potential for residual confounding.

Third, because the NIS captures only index hospitalization data, our analysis was restricted to in-hospital complications and mortality. As we were unable to assess medium- to long-term postoperative outcomes (e.g., at 30 days, 90 days, and beyond), late events such as surgical site infections presenting after discharge may be underestimated.

Despite all these limitations, the NIS remains a valuable tool for conducting outcome-based orthopedic surveys. First, its large, multi-institutional design provided a sample size of more than 220,000 lumbar spine procedures, enhancing the generalizability of our findings across diverse hospital settings. Second, this substantial cohort size enabled robust analysis of relatively uncommon perioperative complications in patients with AF. Third, the detailed demographic and comorbidity information in the NIS allowed for adjusted analyses using multivariable logistic regression and propensity score matching, thereby mitigating (although not eliminating) residual confounding.

Conclusion

Patients with AF undergoing lumbar surgery face a significantly increased risk of mortality and perioperative complications, a finding partly attributable to their higher comorbidity burden (Fig. 6). These data support the need for routine preoperative risk assessment and suggest that enhanced perioperative cardiovascular management may improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
