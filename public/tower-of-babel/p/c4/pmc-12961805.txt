Sexual function in breastfeeding women: a systematic review
Authors: Mohadese Adeli, Nazanin Shamaeian-Razavi, Morvarid Irani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexual function during breastfeeding is a clinically significant but under-researched aspect of postpartum health. Hormonal, physical, psychological, and social changes may influence women’s sexual function, affecting marital satisfaction and quality of life. This systematic review aimed to synthesize evidence on sexual function in breastfeeding women and the factors contributing to its variations.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in Persian (SID, MAGIRAN) and international databases (Medline, Scopus, Web of Science, Science Direct, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar) for studies published between 2010 and 9 August 2025. Quantitative and qualitative studies reporting on sexual function, psychological, emotional, and physical effects, and quality of life in breastfeeding women were included. Methodological quality was assessed using the NICE checklist, which was selected due to its comprehensiveness and applicability to both quantitative and qualitative studies, while other common tools such as JBI and Cochrane Risk of Bias exist. Following screening, 22 studies were included. Narrative synthesis was used to address heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and populations.ResultsFindings indicate that breastfeeding is associated with changes in sexual function, including decreased libido, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and reduced sexual satisfaction, with prevalence estimates ranging from 60% to 80% across studies. Hormonal factors, particularly increased prolactin and decreased estrogen, contributed significantly during the early postpartum months. Psychological factors such as stress, postpartum depression, and body image concerns also affected sexual function. Social and relational factors, including partner support and cultural expectations, moderated these effects. Notably, some women reported positive experiences, such as increased emotional intimacy and improved relationship satisfaction, particularly when adequate social and psychological support was available.ConclusionSexual function during breastfeeding results from a complex interplay of hormonal, psychological, social, and relational factors. Although many women face challenges, supportive environments and educational or counseling interventions can mitigate negative effects and enhance marital satisfaction. These findings highlight the need to integrate sexual health counseling into postpartum care programs, emphasizing both physiological and psychosocial dimensions to optimize women’s overall postpartum well-being.Systematic review registration(PROSPERO: CRD42025637151)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04358-6.Keywords: Breastfeeding and sexual function, Sexual desire during breastfeeding, Postpartum sexual challenges, Vaginal dryness after childbirth, Psychological factors and sexual health, Marital satisfaction postpartum, Sexual dysfunction after childbirth

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines sexual health as a harmonious state between the mind, emotions, and body, contributing to the enhancement of individuals’ social, spiritual, and personal aspects and playing a role in their development [1, 2]. Sexual health requires a positive attitude towards sexual relationships, the ability to experience a safe, pleasurable, consensual, and free from coercion, discrimination, and violence [3–5]. Healthy sexual relationships not only strengthen emotional bonds between couples but also solidify family foundations and prevent psychological, social issues, and even divorce [6, 7].

Sexual function is a complex physiological and psychological process that includes phases such as sexual desire, arousal, vaginal lubrication, orgasm, sexual satisfaction, and pain during intercourse [8–10]. Healthy sexual function is considered one of the key elements of the quality of life for women, with numerous studies confirming its positive relationship with general health and quality of life [11, 12]. However, women’s sexual function is dependent on various factors such as the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and menopause [13, 14]. Among these, the postpartum and breastfeeding period is considered a sensitive and challenging phase, marked by profound physiological and psychological changes that can impact women’s sexual function [15, 16].

Sexuality problems have a more dominant impact on women’s sexual function during the third trimester of pregnancy and the breastfeeding period compared to menopause [17, 18]. Scientific evidence has shown that breastfeeding can have both positive and negative effects on marital relationships and sexual function, but in most cases, a decrease in sexual desire and satisfaction has been reported [19–21]. Studies have estimated the prevalence of sexual dysfunction during the first three months postpartum between 41% and 83%, and around 60% in the first year [22, 23]. Hormonal changes due to breastfeeding, such as increased prolactin levels and decreased estrogen, can suppress sexual desire, reduce vaginal lubrication, and cause pain during intercourse [18]. Additionally, factors such as fatigue from baby care, sleep deprivation, postpartum depression, changes in body image, and partner-related hormonal changes such as decreased testosterone in men, may also contribute to reduced sexual desire and satisfaction. Stress can further increase cortisol levels, which negatively affects sexual function [20, 24, 25]. In contrast, Published empirical studies have shown that mother-infant skin contact and breastfeeding can help improve sexual desire and satisfaction in some women by increasing oxytocin secretion, based on measured oxytocin levels and sexual function outcomes in breastfeeding women [16, 21].

Although numerous studies have examined sexual dysfunction in the postpartum period, comprehensive research specifically addressing the impact of breastfeeding on women’s sexual function remains limited, and a thorough synthesis of existing findings has not yet been conducted. Sexual dysfunction is relatively common among breastfeeding women, as reported in several observational and cross-sectional studies [19, 25–30]. Factors such as exclusive breastfeeding, fatigue, and hormonal changes may influence sexual desire and marital satisfaction [16, 19, 26–29]. Considering the importance of sexual relationships in strengthening family foundations, this systematic review aims to synthesize existing evidence on sexual function in breastfeeding women and the factors contributing to its variations.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in both Persian and international electronic databases, including SID, MAGIRAN, MEDLINE, ScienceDirect, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, ProQuest, Wiley, Highwire Press, and Google Scholar.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to breastfeeding and sexual function. Key search terms included, but were not limited to: breastfeeding, sexual function, sexual dysfunction, sexual desire, psychosexual effects, quality of life, marital relationships, and postpartum sexual health. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine search terms. A detailed example of the search strategy for selected databases is provided in Additional File 2.

The final search was completed on 9 August 2025. Reference lists of included studies were manually screened to identify additional relevant publications. Searches were limited to English- and Persian-language publications due to feasibility constraints.

All retrieved records were imported into a shared database, and duplicate records were removed. Following duplicate removal, records underwent an initial relevance screening based on titles to exclude clearly unrelated studies resulting from the broad search strategy. Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality and potential risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using a modified version of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) quality appraisal checklist, adapted for both quantitative and qualitative study designs.

Each study was independently appraised by two reviewers. Discrepancies in quality ratings were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MI). Results

Study selection and characteristics

A comprehensive search across six electronic databases yielded 33,640 records (PubMed = 1,303; Web of Science = 1,207; Scopus = 4,120; Science Direct = 3,735; ProQuest = 1,970; Google Scholar = 23,105). After removing duplicates and non-relevant studies, 633 unique records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 24 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 22 studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Of the included studies, 17 were quantitative and 5 were qualitative. All quantitative studies had a cross-sectional design, and 10 included comparative groups.

The studies were conducted across multiple countries, including Iran [15, 16, 19, 25–28, 30], the United States [31, 32], Germany [20], the Philippines [10], Brazil [22, 33], Japan [34], Spain [35], Ireland [36], and Switzerland [37], and were published between 2010 and 2025.

Quantitative studies employed validated measurement instruments, most commonly the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) [19, 25, 26, 30], as well as breastfeeding-specific questionnaires [15, 16, 33]. Qualitative studies collected data through interviews and focus groups [23, 33, 35, 37].

Across the included quantitative studies, participant characteristics such as maternal parity, length of marriage, duration of postpartum sexual abstinence, and vaginal symptoms (including dryness, pain, and bleeding) were inconsistently reported. When available, these characteristics are presented in the quantitative study characteristics table as reported by the original studies.

Methodological quality was assessed using the modified NICE checklist. Most studies were rated as having good methodological quality, although some demonstrated limitations related to reporting or sample size (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

Sexual function during breastfeeding is influenced by a complex interplay of hormonal, physical, psychological, social, and relational factors. This systematic review synthesizes both quantitative and qualitative evidence, providing a comprehensive understanding of these influences [15, 16, 18–23, 25–37, 45].

Hormonal influences

Breastfeeding, particularly in the early postpartum months, is associated with hormonal changes that affect sexual function. Increased prolactin secretion reduces estrogen levels, which can lead to vaginal dryness and decreased sexual desire [16, 22, 30, 35]. Studies consistently report that women practicing exclusive breastfeeding are more likely to experience reductions in sexual desire and related dysfunctions compared to non-breastfeeding women [16, 22, 30, 35].

A closer analysis indicates that these findings align with the results of Mashoufi et al. [15], who reported decreased sexual desire in breastfeeding women, whereas Sun et al. [31] showed that some breastfeeding women with adequate emotional support did not experience significant reductions in sexual desire. This discrepancy suggests that hormonal changes alone cannot fully explain postpartum sexual alterations, and social and relational factors play a crucial moderating role.

These postpartum sexual changes, influenced by both biological and psychosocial factors, may also affect family planning decisions, including contraceptive use, frequency of sexual activity, and timing of subsequent pregnancies. Recognizing the role of social and relational support can help healthcare providers offer tailored counseling on sexual health and reproductive planning during the postpartum period.

Physical and psychological factors

Physical changes such as vaginal dryness and dyspareunia primarily result from reduced estrogen levels and physiological postpartum adaptations [16, 19, 26, 28, 30]. Psychological factors, including anxiety, postpartum depression, and body image concerns, also significantly affect sexual function [20, 23, 25, 29, 30, 33, 35, 36]. Studies by Wallwiener et al. [21] and Rahmani et al. [37] demonstrate that lack of social and partner support exacerbates these effects, reducing sexual satisfaction and marital quality.

Analytically, the interaction between physical and psychological factors should be examined simultaneously. For instance, women experiencing vaginal dryness but receiving strong emotional support reported higher sexual satisfaction than those with dryness and insufficient support. Therefore, addressing both biological and psychological dimensions provides a more comprehensive understanding and informs effective supportive interventions.

Social and relational factors

Partner and family support, including support from parents and in-laws, together with cultural norms and societal expectations, play a key role in shaping sexual function during breastfeeding. Emotional and practical support from partners is associated with fewer sexual problems and higher marital satisfaction [15, 25, 37, 46]. These social and relational factors may also influence family planning decisions, including contraceptive use, sexual activity frequency, and timing of subsequent pregnancies, highlighting the importance of tailored postpartum counseling. Conversely, societal pressures and cultural expectations regarding postpartum sexual activity can exacerbate sexual dysfunction and influence women’s perceptions of their sexual roles [15, 25, 35, 37, 43].

Comparative analysis with studies by Escasa-Dorne [10] and Matthies et al. [19] indicates that in societies with stronger family and cultural support, sexual dysfunction is less prevalent, whereas in cultures with stronger social pressures, breastfeeding women are more likely to experience reduced sexual desire. These findings emphasize the importance of considering cultural and relational contexts in postpartum care programs.

Positive and divergent experiences

Not all experiences during breastfeeding are negative. Some women report that breastfeeding strengthens emotional intimacy with their partners and enhances body image perception, thereby positively influencing sexual satisfaction [16, 33, 35]. Evidence suggests that when sufficient social and psychological support is available, breastfeeding can be associated with positive sexual and relational experiences [16, 33, 35]. Analytical insights highlight that these positive outcomes help explain divergent findings, underscoring that postpartum sexual experiences cannot be fully attributed to hormonal factors alone and result from a complex interaction of psychological and social determinants.

Study heterogeneity and limitations

Included studies varied substantially in terms of design, measurement tools, postpartum periods, and cultural contexts [10, 15, 16, 18–34, 36, 37], which influenced reported prevalence and types of sexual dysfunction. This variability underscores the need to interpret findings within study-specific contexts. Limitations of this systematic review include the cross-sectional design of most included studies, which restricts causal inferences regarding the relationship between breastfeeding and sexual function. Additional limitations include diversity of measurement instruments and sample sizes, language restrictions, and the inability to perform meta-analysis.

Conclusion

Sexual function during breastfeeding results from a complex interplay of hormonal, physical, psychological, social, and relational factors. While many women experience challenges such as reduced sexual desire, vaginal dryness, and dyspareunia, some report positive experiences when adequate emotional and social support is available. Educational, counseling, and culturally sensitive interventions are essential to promote women’s sexual health and enhance marital satisfaction during the postpartum period.
