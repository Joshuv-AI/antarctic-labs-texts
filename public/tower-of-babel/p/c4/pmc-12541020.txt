Cross domain fault diagnosis in internal combustion engines using multisensor data with transfer federated and transformer based federated transfer learning
Authors: A. Srinivaas, N. R. Sakthivel, Binoy B. Nair
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article presents experimental fault diagnosis research of engines through the integration of bidirectional transfer learning, federated learning, and a new hybrid architecture of Transformer and Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for out-of-distribution (OOD) testing. For the research purpose, two unique engine datasets were chosen: one for standard car engines and generators, and the other for engines of remote-controlled model cars and drones. For the initial step, bidirectional transfer learning through the employment of traditional DNNs was utilized to eliminate the domain difference among these heterogeneous engine datasets so that fault diagnosis can be effectively achieved for small and large engines. The second step was to develop a federated learning system to facilitate collaborative DNN model training on decentralized engine data to ensure data privacy with overall enhanced generalization. Lastly, the research proposes a federated transfer learning approach that employs Transformer-DNN hybrid architecture. The Transformer component, with positional encoding and multi-head attention mechanisms, was tailored to extract sequential dependencies in engine operational data, whereas the DNN part was dedicated to discriminative feature extraction for classification. Outstanding results show that transfer learning and federated transfer learning are highly effective in fault diagnosis of small engines; however, OOD testing based on the hybrid architecture substantially enhances accuracy in fault detection for large engines. Comparative experiments show significant improvements in classification accuracy and robustness in comparison to baseline DNN models. These results form a convincing basis for the development of scalable, privacy-preserving predictive maintenance systems that are flexible for deployment on a range of engine applications within both automotive and unmanned systems environments.

Engine fault diagnosis has evolved significantly in recent years driven by advancements in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence(AI). Initially, traditional diagnostic methods relied heavily on the experience of engineers and manual inspection to identify engine faults, which often resulted in inefficiencies and a high rate of error. The recent advancement is discussed further , exploring ensemble methods, such as the blending technique using Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC) and Random Forest (RF), has significantly improved diagnostic accuracy to 98.43% and reduced construction time to 86 seconds1. Recent advancements in deep learning have enabled the application of autoencoders for vehicle engine fault identification, facilitating automatic feature extraction and dimensionality reduction from complex operational data to distinguish between normal and faulty engine states2. Techniques like the anomaly Transformer Neural Network (TNN) diagnose cooling system faults by reconstructing faulty data without prior samples, enhancing detection capabilities. TNN’s multi-attention head architecture allows efficient data processing, differing from traditional models requiring complex decoders1,3. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has further expanded the capabilities of automatic sound classification systems, achieving fault classification accuracies above 98% in specific applications4.

Transfer learning (TL) is pivotal, enabling the application of knowledge acquired from one domain to another. This approach was particularly beneficial in situations with limited training data, proving instrumental where traditional methods falter due to data distribution discrepancies and operational variability5,6. An innovative fault diagnosis approach that integrates Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNN) with TL to enhance diagnostic accuracy and reduce computational costs in mechanical systems. By transforming one-dimensional time-series signals into grayscale images for feature extraction, the method leverages TL to improve classification accuracy, even with limited data7. Hybrid Approach with fault trees and back propagation neural networks to overcome the limitations of each when used alone used to build an expert system with an integrated database, knowledge base, neural networks, data acquisition system, inference engine, explain procedures, and a human–machine interface8. On a bearing dataset proposed Transformer-based Conditional GAN Transfer Learning Network (TCGANTLN) with wavelet coherence of the time series data achieving higher accuracy in fault classification9. A deep reconstruction convolutional autoencoder (DRTCNN) was employed for feature extraction and data reconstruction of the multi-class confusion matrix was used to evaluate the model’s decision effect on the data, showing the high accuracy diagnosis ability of the model10. A specific challenge: imbalance domain generalization fault diagnosis (IDGFD). This occurs when the number of samples in the normal class significantly outweighs those in the fault class within each source domain is addressed by Domain Mixed-Enhanced Domain Generalization Network (DEMDGN) based on the mixed-up-based data augmentation and domain-based discrepancy metrics to align feature distributions across multiple heterogeneous source domains, creating domain-invariant features for better fault classification on the bearing dataset from Case Western Reserve University (CWRU)11.

Federated Learning (FL) is a decentralized machine learning approach that addresses critical issues related to data privacy and management by enabling multiple parties to collaboratively train a model without sharing their local data12, a multi-scale deep belief network (DBN) method integrated with an attention mechanism for effective bearing fault diagnosis under multiple operating conditions demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy compared to single-scale DBN, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN), and Probabilistic Neural Network (PNN) algorithms13. Federated Transfer Learning (FTL) extends Federated Learning (FL) by adopting transfer learning so model learning can cross share, under the scenario of unrelated datasets, the collaborative knowledge without having access to raw data. This paradigm skilfully overcomes the frictions between data distribution and operational variety, enhancing the productivity of tasks such as fault diagnosis and predictive maintenance, and retaining data privacy15,16. A federated transfer learning strategy based on data restoration (FTLS-DR) to address challenges in fault diagnosis, particularly when dealing with data corruption and privacy concerns to address the negative transfer based on the linear regression completion based on the random forest (RF) with joint domain discrepancy loss was introduced to correct the phenomenon of parameter bias and the Wasserstein Distance (WD) was used to evaluate the dataset discrepancy with a feedback loss and the performance metrics like MAE (Mean Absolute Error), MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error), MBE (Mean Bias Error), RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error), and R2 (coefficient of determination)17.Proposed FTL scheme adopts a federated learning structure where multiple clients work with their local data while a central server coordinates the learning process incorporated a discrepancy-based approach to handle differences between source and target domain data for a bearing fault diagnosis18.

Beyond convolutional and transformer models, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) offer a complementary framework for modelling structured sensor relationships and enhancing out-of-distribution fault detection alongside TL, FL, and FTL approaches. For example, adaptive expert ensembles introduce a graph-causal framework that isolates environment-invariant features via backdoor adjustment and expert weighting to robustly handle distributional shifts in wind-turbine diagnosis19. Energy-Propagation Graph Neural Networks (EGN-OOD) transform vibration signals into maximal-information graphs and iteratively propagate energy scores to distinguish in- and out-of-distribution samples with high precision in construction-machinery applications20.Semi-supervised meta-learning frameworks tackle OOD interference in bearing diagnosis by allocating pseudo-labels to unlabelled data and adapting distance metrics to suppress outliers21, while open-set classifiers built on RNN-derived graph features detect novel fault classes via one-class KL-divergence thresholds22. In unsupervised settings, Graph Sampling and Aggregation network (Graph SAGE) autoencoders trained on normal bearing graphs flag anomalies by reconstruction error23and one-shot GNNs leverage single‐example prototypes for few‐shot rotating-machinery fault classification24. Attention-aware temporal–spatial GNNs fuse multi-sensor graphs through spatial message-passing and temporal attention to achieve an accuracy of 0.96 or higher on wind-turbine and gearbox datasets25. The above recent studies that integrated GANs and Transformers have shown remarkable learning capabilities and highly effective transfer performance, largely in bearing fault diagnosis scenarios; however, several challenges remain. Notably, the discrepancy between generated data and real-world internal combustion engines (ICEs) signals can be substantial due to heterogeneous operational conditions, leading to reduced cross-domain robustness. Moreover, while these hybrid approaches offer rich representational power, many still focus on bearing datasets and thus lack comprehensive validation on more complex ICEs environments with varied load conditions, vibration signatures, and acoustic patterns. Consequently, the poor feature extraction abilities of many cross-domain models and the significant distributional mismatches across datasets underscore the necessity for more targeted solutions in ICEs fault diagnosis—an area where our experimental data and model development aim to fill critical research gaps.

This article aims to compare three approaches—(i) DNN-based bidirectional transfer learning, (ii) federated learning with DNN + ADNN (domain adaptation), and (iii) federated transfer learning using a Transformer-DNN hybrid—across two distinct engine datasets (Engine A and Engine B). Unlike conventional one-way transfer, the proposed solution assesses bidirectional knowledge transfer between Engine A and Engine B. Training includes tri accelerometer vibration signals and sound pressure data under a variety of load settings. In addition to the standard 0% load condition, this work evaluates the fault diagnosis performance at 15% and 30% load conditions (for Engine A)—representing out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios not covered by traditional transfer or federated setups. This ensures a more realistic appraisal of model robustness, the study seeks to highlight a scalable, privacy-preserving, and robust framework for engine fault diagnosis, bridging the gaps identified in prior literature.

Transfer learning ICEs fault diagnosis DNN based framework

Data acquisition involves collecting signals from a tri-accelerometer—capturing the x, y, and z axes—and a sound pressure sensor. From each sensor channel, nine statistical features were computed: mean, median, mode, standard deviation, kurtosis, skewness, range, mean absolute deviation (MAD), and interquartile range (IQR). This yields a total of 4 × 9 = 36 features that comprehensively represent the underlying dynamics of the engine signals.

While MI-based ranking naturally minimizes redundancy to some extent by prioritizing features with higher scores, employing an explicit redundancy control mechanism—such as correlation-based feature elimination—could further enhance the robustness of the selected feature subset.

In the proposed Deep Neural Network (DNN), each computational block begins with a Dense layer, wherein each input is fully connected to every neuron in the subsequent layer, allowing the network to capture complex feature relationships effectively. We employ Xavier initialization also known as glorot initialization to set the initial weights. This initialization approach maintains stable gradient distributions during training and mitigates issues such as vanishing or exploding gradients during backpropagation32. Batch Normalization further stabilizes the internal data distributions, accelerating convergence and improving generalization, while the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function introduces nonlinearity and reduces the risk of vanishing gradients33. Dropout randomly deactivates neurons during training to prevent overfitting by encouraging more robust representations. Finally, the model culminates in a Dense layer with a SoftMax activation, which converts raw logits into a probability distribution across multiple classes, making the network suitable for our multi-class classification tasks34,35. The Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) offers a key advantage by fostering domain-invariant representations, effectively minimizing the impact of domain shift between different engines or datasets40. Rather than requiring a deep, computationally heavy architecture (as seen in our proposed transfer learning setups), the DANN‐based design incorporates a GRL to encourage feature extraction that is both discriminative for classification and robust against domain‐specific variations41,42. Consequently, a lighter DNN can still achieve high classification performance by aligning feature distributions across domains, reducing the need for large numbers of layers or neurons and thereby lowering computational overhead. Dataset details

Tri-axial accelerometer (vibration) and microphone (sound pressure) signals were acquired at a sampling frequency of 25,600 Hz (Δt = 3.90625 × 10⁻5 s), yielding 1,536,000 samples per 60 s recording. The NI 9234 4-Channel DAQ (24-bit ADC) provides a usable bandwidth of 10–20 kHz, which far exceeds the highest fundamental vibration frequencies of our engines (Engine A: 500–5500 rpm ≈ 8.3–91.7 Hz; Engine B: 3200–13,500 rpm ≈ 53.3–225 Hz), ensuring all relevant harmonics are captured without aliasing. Each 60 s trace was divided into non-overlapping 1 s windows (25,600 samples/window), resulting in 60 segments per run. With 25 runs for each of the five operating modes (normal, Cyl 01–04 cut-off), a total of 1500 windows per class were generated. For modelling, we randomly subsampled N windows per class from the pool of 1500 available 1 s segments: Engine A used N = 40, and Engine B used N = 12, yielding feature matrices of size 200 × 36 and 60 × 36, respectively. These subsampling sizes were chosen to ensure equal class representation and manageable training times. From each window, nine statistical descriptors (mean, median, mode, standard deviation, kurtosis, skewness, range, mean absolute deviation, interquartile range) were computed on all four channels, producing a 36-dimensional feature vector per instance. Results

A confusion matrix is a fundamental machine learning evaluation metric that is used for the assessment of the performance of classification algorithms. A confusion matrix is a categorization of model predictions by actual vs. predicted classes. A confusion matrix is intended to display true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN), and provides more insightful information than mere accuracy48,49.

Our engine fault diagnosis is a multi-classification problem, the confusion matrix is useful in the identification of misclassification patterns, enabling a clearer understanding of class-specific performance. It is important in the calculation of critical metrics like precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy, which help in the measurement of model reliability and robustness in various conditions50. With the study of the matrix, we can determine if the model is skewed towards classes, is experiencing difficulty with particular fault categories, or requires additional tuning for better generalization. Hyperparameter optimization of the Engine A → B transfer task was conducted with a fivefold cross-validation protocol to ensure robust, repeatable performance across the TL,FTL and tenfold cross-validation for the FL configurations: the epoch-wise weight-update rule (Eq. 3) sensitivity β was cycled through {0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, with β = 0.75 producing an optimal mean F1-score and recall-balancing per class; focus-loss hyperparameters α ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75} and γ ∈ {1.0, 2.5, 4.0} were attempted, and α = 0.75 with γ = 2.5 produced the greatest gain towards minority-class F1 with minimal majority-class accuracy destabilization; and Transformer sizes d_model ∈ {32, 64, 128} and nhead ∈ {2, 4, 8} (with ff_dim = 2d_model and two encoder levels per Eq. 11) were explored, with d_model = 64 and nhead = 4 producing an optimal validation accuracy and computational cost. Discussion

This article presents a general framework for engine fault diagnosis, with a particular focus on cylinder fault detection using three methodologies: Transfer Learning (TL), Federated Learning (FL), and Federated Transfer Learning (FTL). The main findings are presented as follows:

i.Transfer Learning (TL):The DNN-based bidirectional transfer learning exhibits a pronounced asymmetry: transferring from Engine B → A yields 99.50 % accuracy, while A → B only reaches 93.22 % (Table 6). This outcome can be understood in terms of: Spectral content and class separability:Engine B’s small-scale Drone/RC vehicle engine (3 200–13 500 rpm ≈ 53–225 Hz) generates clear, high-frequency vibration harmonics with strong fault-specific signatures, resulting in tightly clustered feature groups (low within-class variance, high inter-class distance). By contrast, the larger, heavier Engine A (500–5500 rpm ≈ 8–92 Hz) exhibits broader, lower-frequency dynamics and greater background noise, which blur class boundaries and increase within-class variance.Generalization from a simpler source:Models pretrained on Engine B learn robust high-frequency features that also manifest—albeit at lower amplitude—in Engine A’s signals, facilitating seamless B → A transfer. Conversely, features learned from Engine A are dominated by low-frequency, noise-prone components that fail to capture the distinct high-frequency fault markers of Engine B, degrading A → B performance.Signal complexity & noise characteristics:Engine A’s broader dynamic range and mechanical noise introduce greater intra-class variability and lower signal-to-noise ratios, making it harder for the model to extract fault-discriminative features. In contrast, Engine B’s cleaner harmonic patterns yield more distinctive, high-contrast features that are easier to learn and transfer. ii.Federated Learning (FL):Confusion Matrices and Misclassification Analysis: FL using a DNN + DANN hybrid architecture, exhibits robust classification for both Engine A and Engine B, achieving 91.2% and 94.17% accuracy, respectively. The confusion matrices indicate that Engine A has a more stable classification pattern, whereas Engine B presents minor misclassification errors, particularly in Cyl_03 and Cyl_04 categories. Although this method enhances robustness through domain invariance.cross-validation and Performance Dependency: FL necessitates tenfold cross-validation to mitigate domain-specific biases and ensure stable performance across distributed datasets. Conclusions

In this work, an end-to-end framework for engine fault diagnosis was introduced, with a focus on cylinder fault detection based on Transfer Learning (TL), Federated Learning (FL), and Federated Transfer Learning (FTL). The TL approach utilizes an advanced deep neural network to well transfer knowledge among engines but is plagued by domain variations. Conversely, the FL approach maintains data privacy by learning light Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) and a Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) on decentralized data and then centrally aggregating local models, although with increased communication overhead. Our introduced FTL approach, incorporating a Transformer-based module with DNN and DANN components, not only acquires long-range contextual dependencies using self-attention mechanisms but also enhances domain adaptation under various engine conditions. Experimental evidence from multi-cylinder fault cases shows that FTL significantly improves cross-domain generalization and diagnostic accuracy and is a viable solution to resilient engine fault detection. Future work will explore incorporating domain-adaptive few-shot learning to further enhance cross-machine diagnostic performance.
