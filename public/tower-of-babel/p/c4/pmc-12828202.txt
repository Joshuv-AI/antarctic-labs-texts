Imaging and clinical outcomes of arthroscopically assisted lower trapezius tendon transfer using achilles allograft in the treatment of functional irreparable posterior-superior rotator cuff tears
Authors: Cristina Delgado, Gia Rodríguez, Vanesa López, Pablo Jiménez, Emilio Calvo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo assess the imaging and clinical outcomes of the arthroscopy-assisted lower trapezius transfer (aaLTT) in the setting of irreparable or functionally irreparable rotator cuff tears.MethodsBicentric prospective study of patients diagnosed of posterior-superior irreparable or functionally irreparable cuff tears treated with aaLTT and a minimum 2-year follow-up. Patient-reported outcomes, including pain level, range of motion, Constant–Murley Score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, and Subjective Shoulder Value scores, were recorded preoperatively and postoperatively. X-rays were obtained preoperatively and at the final follow-up to evaluate progression of arthropathy. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging or ultrasound was utilized to assess integrity of the transferred tendon at the final follow-up.ResultsSeventeen consecutive patients, median age of 58 (range, 51-70) years, were included. At a median follow-up of 34.5 months (range, 24-61 months), all patients showed a significant improvement in pain scores and patient-reported outcomes, including Constant–Murley Score and Subjective Shoulder Value scores. Significant improvements in forward flexion and external rotation averaged 30° and 35° (P < .001), respectively, were also obtained. External rotation lag sign and pseudoparalysis were reversed in 88.2% (15/17) and 100% (5/5) of patients, respectively. Postoperative imaging studies showed lateral avulsion of the graft in 6 (35.2%) cases.ConclusionThe aaLTT resulted in a significant decrease in pain and improvement in shoulder function with moderate healing rates of the transferred tendon in patients with functional irreparable rotator cuff tear at short-term follow-up.

Rotator cuff tears (RCTs) are a common cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction, affecting around 50% of patients over 60 years,38 with an overall incidence rate of 22%-34%.44 Of these, 25% are considered massive RCTs.44 Initially managed nonsurgically, rotator cuff repair is recommended in patients in whom conservative treatment fails, providing pain relief and functional recovery. However, some RCTs present with advanced fatty infiltration (FI) or severe retraction that will not reach the anatomic footprint (irreparable), while others may reach it but still fail to heal and are considered to be functionally irreparable rotator cuff tears (FIRCTs).21,41

Management of irreparable and FIRCTs is challenging. In elderly patients, reverse shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has shown to be a reliable option with good clinical and functional outcomes.12 However, substantial complication rates and limited survivorship have been reported in young patients.13 Instead, joint-preserving techniques, including rotator cuff débridement,42 partial rotator cuff repair,4 biceps rerouting,35 superior capsular reconstruction,29 implantation of a subacromial spacer,26 and tendon transfers,9,10,14 have emerged as alternative treatments for this population.

Among all these surgical options, tendon transfers represent a promising option to reduce pain and improve shoulder function, especially in young and active patients with massive posterosuperior RCTs and external rotation (ER) weakness, providing a dynamic replacement for the posterior aspect of the force couple. Although the latissimus dorsi transfer (LDT) has shown satisfactory clinical outcomes in selected patients with posterosuperior FIRCT,15 the lower trapezius transfer (LTT) has been suggested to be biomechanically superior, as it provides a more advantageous line of pull for ER compared with the latissimus dorsi.3,31 In addition, successful clinical outcomes of the open LTT have been reported.11 However, there are few studies analyzing the results of the arthroscopically assisted procedure.2,6,9 Moreover, there are even fewer studies analyzing the radiological outcomes and graft healing rates. The role of graft healing in LTT is yet to be determined. Considering that many studies have shown that patients undergoing rotator cuff repair with failed healing exhibit worse clinical outcomes,43 it has been hypothesized that graft healing in LTT may be functionally and clinically significant. Furthermore, understanding the timing and mechanisms of tendon healing is deemed crucial for a better understanding of postoperative recovery, including rehabilitation timelines and return-to-work milestones.

The purpose of this study was to assess the imaging and clinical outcomes of arthroscopy-assisted lower trapezius transfer (aaLTT) in the setting of irreparable or FIRCTs. We hypothesized that the aaLTT would provide improved functional outcomes and range of movement with high healing rate.

Methods

A bicentric prospective study of patients diagnosed of posterosuperior irreparable or FIRCTs who underwent aaLTT from March 2019-March 2023 and had a minimum 2-year follow-up was conducted. The study was approved by the institutional review board under act 06/24. FIRCT was defined as follows: (1) concurrent supraspinatus (SSP) and infraspinatus (ISP) tears, (2) tendon retraction to glenoid according to Patte stage 3 retraction,32 (3) tendon length less than 1.5 cm, (4) high-grade FI of the posteriosuperior cuff (Goutallier classification grades 3 or 4)16; and (5) cuff tears deemed irreparable during intraoperative assessment.

The indications for the aaLTT included: (1) posteriosuperior FIRCT involving the SSP and ISP confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (2) pain and/or shoulder weakness without stiffness; (3) failure of conservative treatment; (4) loss of active ER, ER weakness with the arm at the side and/or the arm in abduction with ER lag sign; (5) no or minimal arthritic changes in the x-ray defined as Hamada ≤ 317; (6) intact subscapularis or repairable subscapularis tear, classified according to Lafosse classification as grade ≤ 324; and (7) intraoperative arthroscopic findings of retracted posterosuperior cuff tendons not being reduced to their original footprint area, with humeral cartilage exposure remaining after tendon release. Availability of postoperative MRI or ultrasound study at the final follow-up was required for inclusion in the study. Patients with infectious or neurologic disorders, those with trapezius or deltoid palsy, patients with severe arthritis, and previous surgeries other than RCT repair were not considered candidates for this surgery and, subsequently for the study.

Surgical technique

All surgical procedures were performed by 2 senior surgeons (GRV, EC) following the technique previously described by the authors.36 Briefly, the LTT was harvested through a transverse skin incision along the inferior border of the scapular spine, 3-4 cm lateral to the medial edge of the scapular spine, and prepared in a Krakow configuration. After preparing the Achilles tendon allograft, the allograft was shuttled from the posterior wound into the joint through an opening in the ISP fascia and an interval between the ISP and deltoid muscles. The graft was then fixed with 2 5.5-mm medial-row suture anchors (5.5-mm Healicoil; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) were placed medially on the footprint. Two additional 5.5-mm knotless anchors (Multifix; Smith & Npehew, Andover, MA, USA) were placed at the lateral footprint to complete a double-row repair. The arm was positioned in maximal ER at 30° flexion and 60° abduction, and the Achilles allograft was sutured to the native trapezius tendon in a Pulvertaft weave with multiple nonabsorbable sutures passing the lower trapezius through the Achilles allograft. Postoperatively, patients were kept in an ER shoulder brace for 6 weeks. Passive range of motion (ROM) was initiated after 4 weeks, avoiding internal rotation (IR) until the sixth postoperative week, when full passive ROM and progressive active ROM was allowed. ER strengthening was avoided until 4 months postoperative. Unrestricted activity was not allowed until 6 months after surgery.

Clinical evaluation

Medical records of all patients were reviewed by 2 independent reviewers from the surgeons performing the intervention (CD, VL). Patient data, including age, gender, smoking habit, body mass index, medical comorbidities, previous surgical history, and type of work, were recorded. Type of work was categorized as sedentary, which included jobs that did not require heavy lifting or the operation of tools or machinery with the upper limbs, or labor-intensive, where heavy lifting or machine operation was required. All patients were evaluated before, at 6 weeks, and at 6 and 12 months after surgery. Patient-reported outcomes collected included pain levels measured with visual analog scale (VAS), Constant–Murley Score (CMS),40 American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES),1 and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV) scores.23 We used the established value of the minimal clinically important difference for the CMS to quantify the proportions of patients that reached or exceeded this threshold value.27,34 Intraoperative and postoperative complications were also determined.

Active ROM of the shoulder, including forward elevation (FE), abduction, ER with the arm at side (ER1) and at 90° of abduction (ER2), were measured using a standardized goniometer, whereas IR was determined by the highest spinous process level the patient's thumb could reach. In this study, pseudoparalysis was defined as the inability to perform active FE and shoulder abduction >90° after subacromial anesthetic injection with full passive FE.9 Isometric strength was assessed with the participant in the same position for ROM with a handheld dynamometer (Lafayette instrument; Lafayette Instrument Company, Lafayette, IN, USA). As there are considered indications for this surgery, all patients showed a positive ER weakness or lag sign preoperatively. ER weakness was defined as the inability to maintain the shoulder at 0° of ER with the arm at the side and the elbow flexed to 90° when moderate strength to IR was applied by the examiner. ER lag sign was considered positive when the patient, with the elbow flexed to 90°, was unable to maintain the shoulder in ER or it drifted into IR. Finally, patients were also assessed for the presence of a Hornblower sign.

Imaging evaluation

A senior shoulder and elbow surgeon (GRV), previously trained by a senior musculoskeletal radiologist, evaluated all imaging studies. True anteroposterior and scapular lateral radiographic views of the shoulder were obtained preoperatively and at the final follow-up. Rotator cuff arthropathy progression was evaluated using the Hamada classification.17 MRI (Siemens Magnetom Verio 3T, A Tim + Dom System; Siemens Healthcare, Munich, Germany) was completed before and 12 months after surgery to characterize RCTs and tendon healing. MRI preoperative evaluation included cuff tear size, muscle atrophy, and the amount of fFI. Tear retraction was evaluated in the coronal T2 view and classified according to Patte classification.32 The amount of FI in the posteriouperior cuff was assessed in the T1 sagittal Y-view and graded from stage 0 to stage 4 as defined by Fuchs.16 Postoperative noncontrast MRI or ultrasound was requested to assess integrity of the transferred tendon at the final follow-up. In the MRI, the integrity of the graft was evaluated both in the graft insertion into the greater tuberosity of the humerus in the coronal view and from the allograft to the LTT on the axial view (Figs. 1 and 2). Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.1 (R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A post hoc power analysis was conducted using a sample size of 17 patients. Assuming an effect size of 1.0 (large), a significance level of 0.05, and a two-tailed paired design, the achieved statistical power was approximately 86%.

Quantitative variables were illustrated by median and interquartile range. Qualitative variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies and compared with the chi-square test. Wilcoxon and the Mann–Whitney U test/Kruskal–Wallis test were used to compare related and unrelated nonparametric data. The significance level was established at P < .05. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate significant associations between preoperative features and retear. The statistical threshold for significance was established at P < .05.

To account for the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. With an overall significance level set at α = 0.05 and 12 statistical tests performed, the corrected significance threshold was set at P < .0042. Only comparisons meeting this adjusted threshold were considered statistically significant.

Results

Twenty-three patients who had undergone aaLTT for FIRCT, were identified. Of these, 17 patients met inclusion criteria. No patient was lost to follow-up (follow-up rate of 100%). Thus, 17 patients, with a median age of 58 (range 51-70) years, were included in the study. Clinical outcomes

At a median follow-up of 34.5 months (range 24-61 months), all patients showed a significant improvement in pain scores and patient-reported outcomes including CMS and SSV score (Table II). All patients achieved an improvement in the CMS that exceeded the minimal clinically important difference. Postoperative ASES score reached 76 points at the final follow-up. Significant improvements in forward flexion and ER averaged 30° and 35° with the elbow at the side (P < .001)), respectively, were also obtained. Fourteen out of the 15 employed patients (93.3%) were able to return to work at the time of final follow-up, of whom 2 were involved in sedentary activities and 12 in labor-intensive occupations. The patient who did not return to full duty was involved in labor-intensive occupations. At the final follow-up, all but 3 patients (82.4%) were satisfied with their outcomes. One unsatisfied patient was unable to return to work at his labor-intensive occupation as he felt his shoulder weak, and another required revision surgery due to graft avulsion.

Imaging outcomes

Postoperative MRI and ultrasound were available in 15 (88.2%) and 2 (11.8%) patients, respectively. Of these, 11 (64.7%) showed healing at both the allograft-to-trapezius and allograft-to–greater tuberosity junctions. Lateral avulsion of the graft was identified in 6 (35.2%) cases. No retears were found in the Achilles tendon allograft medial to the SSP footprint or at the junction with the LTT. Arthrosis progressed in only 1 (5.9%) patient, advancing from stage 2 to stage 3, who also showed a lateral avulsion of the graft on MRI.

Complications

There was one early postoperative complication: seroma that resolved spontaneously at 2 weeks. Failure of the graft was confirmed in the MRI in 6 patients who presented ER weakness. One of them had a traumatic rupture of the graft during the rehabilitation, and revision aaLTT transfer was performed 5 months after the initial surgery. The remaining 5 patients chose not to pursue surgical treatment as they had achieved satisfactory pain relief and clinical outcomes, and their daily activity's function was fully achieved. Conversion to rTSA was not conducted in any patient during the study period.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that reliable objective and subjective improvements in pain level, shoulder mobility, and overall shoulder function were achieved in the short term, despite a moderate rate of complete healing of the transferred tendon after aaLTT. Patients with preoperative Hornblower signs, Tm atrophy, and previous surgeries showed less improvement in ER and FE, respectively, whereas patients with and without preoperative pseudoparalysis achieved similar outcomes.

Numerous treatment options have been proposed for addressing massive irreparable posteriouperior RCTs. rTSA provides satisfactory results for massive RCTs30 and is usually the treatment of choice in elderly, low-demand patients,7 but may not be the optimal treatment choice in young patients.19 Arthroscopic débridement and partial repair can offer some pain relief and improvement in shoulder function, but the results tend to deteriorate over time.22 Subacromial balloon spacer has shown to be an effective alternative in selected patients, both in terms of clinical-functional improvement and short-term isokinetic results,26 but the results in the long-term do not outperform those of rotator cuff repair.28,39 Superior capsular reconstruction can provide static stability, pain relief, and good to excellent clinical results, but graft failure has been described in up to 55% of cases.5 Tendon transfers have emerged as an effective treatment option to reduce pain and improved shoulder function in young and active patients with irreparable RCTs but no associated or minimal arthritis. Although both the latissimus dorsi and lower trapezius have been reported to be a suitable treatment option to restore ER and re-establish the muscular force couples,25 the lower trapezius excursion and line of pull is similar to the ISP, resulting in superior restoration of the shoulder ER, especially with the arm at the side.18,31 Clinically, LTT has also shown to be superior to LDT in terms of ROM, functional outcomes, and progression of arthritis.3 The LTT, although initially described as an open technique,11 was modified by Elhassan in 2016 to an arthroscopic-assisted technique,8 avoiding the need to perform the acromial osteotomy and deltoid detachment.

Satisfactory mid-term clinical outcomes of open LTT have been reported.11 Similarly, the few studies analyzing the effectiveness of aaLTT have reported promising outcomes at short- to mid-term follow-up. Elhassan firstly reported on aaLTT in 2020, showing in a group of 41 patients, at a mean follow-up of 14 months (range 6-19 months), a marked improvement in clinical outcomes including VAS, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand, and SSV scores in all but 5 patients. Significant improvements in FE, abduction, and ER averaged 69°, 45°, and 22° (P < .001), respectively, were also observed. Similarly, Chopra et al6 at an average follow-up of 14.6 months, found significant improvements in postoperative pain VAS (6 vs. 2), ASES score (45 vs. 71), and active ER (10° vs. 40°) in all 19 patients analyzed. More recently, Baek et al, reporting on mid-term results (mean follow-up of 58 months) of 36 patients, showed a significant improvement in all outcome measures, including VAS scores, patient-reported outcome measures, and shoulder motion except IR.2 Our results are consistent with previous studies. In our series clinical and functional outcomes improved at a mean follow-up period of 34.5 months (range, 24-61 months). More specifically, patients achieved higher ROM, VAS, CMS, and SSV score values compared to their preoperative measurements. Interestingly, as in Chopra et al series,6 in our study almost all (88.2%) patients with a preoperative ER lag sign had reversal of their lag sign at the final follow-up. Moreover, pseudoparalysis was present before surgery in 3 (17.6%) patients and was reversed after aaLTT in all shoulders. Elhassan also found satisfactory results in patients with preoperative pseudoparalysis, although the presence of pseudoparalysis was found to have a significant negative impact on clinical outcomes.9 Similarly, in the series by Stone et al, pseudoparalysis was successfully reversed in 4/4 patients.37 On the other hand, in the patient series reported by Baek et al, the Tm hypertrophy group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in The Activities of Daily Living External rotation and ASES scores, as well as in ER at 90° abduction and at the side.2 Our study results corroborate this notion that patients with better preoperative teres minor status—including both clinically (negative Hornblower sign) and in imaging studies (MRI showing no atrophy)—are more likely to achieve desirable outcomes. More specifically, patients without preoperative Hornblower sign or teres minor atrophy achieved improved postoperative ER. However, it is important to highlight that, despite achieving a reduced ROM in ER, patients with worse preoperative teres minor status still showed improvement in VAS, SSV, CMS, and FE compared to their preoperative status. These findings are particularly noteworthy, as they further support the effectiveness of the LTT. Unlike the LDT, which has yielded less satisfactory results in terms of ER, the LTT has emerged as a reliable option for patients with teres minor and subscapularis pathology. Furthermore, its ability to reverse pseudoparalysis further underscores its clinical utility and potential superiority over LDT in certain cases.14,20

Owing to the limited number of studies and their short-term follow-up, there is little information available regarding graft integrity and its clinical implications. In Elhassan's series, out of 41 patients undergoing aaLTT, 2 cases sustained rupture of the transferred tendon, with one undergoing revision surgery.9 Similarly, Stone et al reported failures in 3 patients (20%), all of whom subsequently underwent rTSA.37 However, it is important to note that MRI was not routinely ordered in these series, but only in cases with poor evolution. In contrast, Chopra et al and Baek et al identified graft tears in 2 (10.5%) and 7 (19.4%) MRIs, respectively.2,6 In Chopra's series, conversion to rTSA was conducted in 1 case, whereas in Baek's series, 2 patients, who presented with persistent pain and ER weakness, underwent revision of the aaLTT. Interestingly, even without undergoing revision surgery, patients with retear showed less improvement in clinical outcomes.2 These findings suggest that tendon healing following aaLTT plays a crucial role in postoperative status. In our series, 6 patients experienced retears. When we analyzed graft integrity, we did not find statistically significant differences in postoperative outcome measures. However, the sample size is small; therefore, these trends likely did not reach statistical significance because of the limited number of patients.

The low rate of conversion to rTSA reported in the literature may be attributed not only to the moderate rates of retears but also to minimal progression of arthrosis2,3 as well as the relatively short follow-up. In the current study, only 1 patient (5.9%) exhibited signs of arthritis progression, from Hamada stage 2 to stage 3. Interestingly, a graft retear was identified in this patient. Progression of arthritis was observed in 3 patients (8.3%)—2 from retear and 1 from infection—in Baek series.2 Interestingly, in a comparative study involving 48 patients treated with LDT and 42 treated with aaLTT, the risk of osteoarthritis progression was lower in the aaLTT, with osteoarthritis rates at the end of the follow-up noted at 31% for the LDT group vs. 7% for the aaLTT group.3

Finally, the aaLTT may enable patients to regain enough shoulder function and strength to resume employment. In the current study, despite 76.5% of patients being engaged in labor-intensive occupations, 93.3% were able to successfully return to work. Previous studies have also reported high rates of return to work, although manual workers exhibited the lowest rates of returning to full duty.2,37

This study has several limitations to be acknowledged. First, it is a retrospective study comprising a small patient series with no comparison group. Second, the results were mid-term with a mean follow-up of 34.5 months from surgery and may not be enough to predict the progression of arthrosis. Third, given that this is a multicenter surgical registry dependent on patient enrollment by individual surgeons, selection bias may be present. Finally, postoperative MRI studies analyzing graft integrity were not available for review in 2 cases in whom an ultrasound study was evaluated. Nonetheless, this is one of the largest series with imaging analysis evaluating the aaLTT. Although satisfactory clinical outcomes have been obtained at this early time point were positive, longer-term data and comparative studies between various reconstructive techniques for massive irreparable posterosuperior RCTs are needed.

Conclusion

The aaLTT resulted in a significant decrease in pain and improvement in shoulder function with moderate healing rates of the transferred tendon in patients with functional irreparable RCT at short-term follow-up.
