Comparison of ciprofol versus propofol on gastrointestinal motility in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Xuelian Bao, Jiahui Jin, Yansong Li, Fei Yan, Xinyan Chen, Pingyi Song, Hailiang Du, Leilei Pei, Yaomin Zhu, Jing Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative ileus (POI) is a common complication after abdominal surgery that significantly delays recovery. Evidence suggests that the general anaesthetic propofol inhibits gastrointestinal motility, potentially contributing to POI. Ciprofol, a newer general anaesthetic characterised by the addition of a cyclopropyl group to the propofol structure, has uncertain effects on gastrointestinal recovery. This randomised trial aimed to evaluate the effect of ciprofol compared with propofol on gastrointestinal function in patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal cancer resection.MethodsThis prospective, single-centre, single-blinded, randomised controlled trial will enrol patients aged 18–65 years undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer. A total of 176 participants will be randomly assigned to either the ciprofol group (experimental) or the propofol group (control), with 88 patients in each arm. General anaesthesia will be induced and maintained with ciprofol in the experimental group or propofol in the control group. The primary outcome is the time to first postoperative flatus. Secondary outcomes include key parameters associated with enhanced recovery after surgery. Patients will be followed at baseline and every 24 hours postoperatively.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTUAF2024LSYY-415–03). Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at scientific conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400093434.Keywords: Propofol, Colorectal surgery, Gastrointestinal tumours

Background

Postoperative ileus (POI) is a common complication following abdominal surgery,1 with an incidence of postoperative bowel obstruction ranging from 10% to 30% after colorectal resections.2 POI poses significant clinical challenges. A retrospective study in the USA identified POI as the leading cause of unplanned 30-day readmissions following colorectal surgery, accounting for 33.4% of all readmissions.3 The economic burden is considerable: Asgeirsson et al reported that the mean hospitalisation cost for patients with primary or secondary POI was US$15 914 and US$17 311, respectively, compared with US$8316 for patients without POI.4 Tevis et al further demonstrated that although POI alone does not directly increase postoperative mortality, its interaction with concurrent complications markedly raises mortality risk, with a fourfold increase compared with patients without bowel obstruction.5

Anaesthetic agents play a key role in regulating gastrointestinal function.6 Propofol, the predominant intravenous anaesthetic for maintenance of general anaesthesia, has been linked to gastrointestinal motility inhibition. Patients anaesthetised with a propofol-remifentanil regimen experienced delayed gastric emptying.7 Animal studies have shown that high doses of propofol significantly impair gastric emptying and gastrointestinal transit,8 while in vitro studies on human tissues revealed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on gastric and colonic smooth muscle activity.9

Ciprofol (also known as HSK3486 and cipepofol), a novel 2,6-disubstituted phenol derivative, is a new intravenous anaesthetic that acts by modulating γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA_A) receptors, enhancing receptor activity and promoting chloride ion influx into neurons.10 Although both ciprofol and propofol are GABA_A receptor agonists, ciprofol differs structurally by the addition of a cyclopropyl group to the propofol backbone.11 Its effects on gastrointestinal function remain incompletely defined, and no studies have directly investigated its influence on gastrointestinal recovery. Therefore, this randomised controlled trial will compare ciprofol versus propofol in terms of postoperative gastrointestinal recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

According to the Helsinki Declaration, this study is designed as a prospective, single-centre, single-blinded, randomised controlled trial (V.1.2, dated 17 March 2025). The trial will enrol patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. During the pre-anaesthetic evaluation, a trained researcher (Researcher A) will oversee patient recruitment. Participants will be fully informed of the study purpose, procedures, rights and risks, and Researcher A will obtain written informed consent (see online supplemental file 1) from each participant. Eligible patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the ciprofol group (Group C) or the propofol group (Group P). Simple randomisation will be used, and random numbers will be generated by RStudio (V.4.4.3). General anaesthesia will be induced and maintained with ciprofol in Group Ciprofol and with propofol in Group Propofol. This study will be conducted in the Department of Anesthesiology and Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University from February 2025 to September 2026. Data management and statistics will be performed at the Department of Epidemiology and Health Statistics, School of Public Health, Xi’an Jiaotong University Health Science Centre. The study design and protocol follow the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.12 The experimental workflow is shown in figure 1.

Intervention

All patients will undergo standard preoperative assessments, including ECG, chest X-ray or CT, echocardiography, liver and kidney function tests, electrolyte analysis, complete blood count (CBC) and coagulation profile. On arrival in the operating room, a study researcher (Researcher B) will perform routine monitoring, including ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, invasive blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), body temperature (T°C) and bispectral index (BIS). Patients will be placed in the supine position, and peripheral venous access will be established. Baseline heart rate, blood pressure, SpO₂ and BIS will be recorded. Before induction, oxygen will be administered via a face mask at 6 L/min for 3 min.

Ciprofol group

After preoxygenation and before surgery, induction in Group Ciprofol will be performed with midazolam 0.02–0.04 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3–0.5 μg/kg and ciprofol 0.4–0.6 mg/kg. When BIS is <60, rocuronium 0.45–0.9 mg/kg will be administered. Following induction, a bilateral transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block will be performed using bupivacaine 0.25% (15 mL per side). Anaesthesia maintenance will include ciprofol 0.4–2.4 mg/kg/hour, remifentanil 0.05–0.15 µg/kg/min and rocuronium 5–10 µg/kg/min. BIS will be maintained between 40 and 60. Rocuronium infusion will be stopped 30 min before the end of surgery, while other anaesthetics will be administered until surgery completion. Intraoperative temperature will be maintained with a warming blanket to keep axillary temperature above 36°C. Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) will be mitigated by administering 8 mg of Ondansetron 30 min before surgery completion. For intraoperative fluid management, compound sodium chloride, hydroxyethyl starch and human albumin will be infused as needed. If haemoglobin (Hb) is <70 g/L, blood transfusion will be performed. The amounts of crystalloid fluid, colloid fluid, blood products, bleeding and urine output will be recorded.

Propofol group

After preoxygenation and before surgery, patients in Group Propofol will undergo anaesthesia induction with midazolam 0.05–0.4 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3–0.5 μg/kg and propofol 1.5–2.5 mg/kg. When BIS < 60, rocuronium 0.45–0.9 mg/kg will be administered. A TAP block will then be performed, with the same dose as in Group Ciprofol. Anaesthesia will be maintained with propofol 4–12 mg/kg/hour, remifentanil 0.05–0.15 µg/kg/min and rocuronium 5–10 µg/kg/min. BIS will be maintained between 40 and 60. Rocuronium infusion will be discontinued 30 min before the end of surgery, while other anaesthetics will be continued until surgery completion. All other interventions, including prevention of PONV, intraoperative insulation, fluid management, etc., will be identical to those in Group Ciprofol.

Post-surgery procedures

After surgery, all patients in Group Ciprofol and Group Propofol will receive postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with sufentanil 100 mg and dexamethasone 10 mg diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride (total volume: 100 mL), with a bolus dose of 1.5 mL, background dose of 2 mL/hour and a lockout interval of 15 min. Postoperatively, all patients will be transferred to the Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit (PACU). In PACU, sugammadex, flumazenil and nalmefene will be given according to the patient’s condition for anaesthesia reversal. Extubation will be performed once patients meet the criteria, including full wakefulness, recovery of muscle tone and restoration of cough reflex. All medications administered in PACU will be recorded. Patients will then be transferred to the general ward for further monitoring.

Outcome measurement

Outcome measurements will be assessed at several time points (table 1). The primary outcome is the time to first postoperative flatus. Secondary outcomes include postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction (POGD), postoperative gastrointestinal intolerance (POGI), time to first defecation, time to first meal intake, haemodynamic suppression (incidence of hypotension and sinus bradycardia), hospitalisation costs and postoperative hospital length of stay.

Primary outcome

Time to first postoperative flatus

This outcome will be self-reported by the patient and the follow-up researcher (Researcher C) will record to the nearest hour. Data will be expressed in hours. Follow-up will be performed every 24 hours postoperatively, during which patient and their family members will be asked about the occurrence of first flatus.

Secondary outcomes

POGD

POGD will be assessed using the I-FEED scoring system, which evaluates postoperative gastrointestinal recovery based on intake, nausea, emesis, abdominal findings and symptom duration.13 Each parameter is scored from 0 to 2, for a total score of 0–10. Patients will be classified into three categories based on their total I-FEED score: normal gastrointestinal function: 0–2 points; POGI: 3–5 points; POGD: ≥6 points.14 Assessment will be conducted at the fifth day after operation based on the patient’s condition.

POGI

POGI will be evaluated using the same I-FEED system, with scores of 3–5 points indicating intolerance.

First defecation time after surgery

Patients will self-report the time of first defecation, which researcher C will record to the nearest hour. Data will be collected every 24 hours postoperatively and expressed in hours.

First meal intake time after surgery

Patients will self-report the time of first meal intake, and researcher C will record it to the nearest hour. Data will be collected every 24 hours postoperatively and expressed in hours.

Haemodynamic suppression

Incidence of hypotension: blood pressure will be monitored every 3 min intraoperatively. Hypotension is defined as mean arterial pressure (MAP) <65 mm Hg or a ≥20% decrease from baseline.

Incidence of sinus bradycardia: heart rate will be continuously monitored intraoperatively. Bradycardia is defined as a heart rate <60 beats per minute.

Hospitalisation costs

On the first day after discharge, the hospital information system (HIS) will be reviewed to extract and record total hospitalisation costs, expressed in Chinese Yuan (CNY).

Postoperative hospital length of stay

The total duration of hospitalisation after surgery will be obtained from the HIS system on the first day after discharge and expressed in days.

Sample size

Based on retrospective findings (online supplemental file 2) from our hospital, the mean time to first postoperative flatus following propofol-based anaesthesia was 73.2 hours (3.05 days), while the mean time after ciprofol-based anaesthesia was 63.7 hours (2.65 days), with a SD of 0.88. Calculated through PASS 2021, with a 1:1 allocation ratio, a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80 and accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the required sample size is 88 patients per group (Group C: ciprofol, Group P: propofol). Thus, a total of 176 patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer will be enrolled. This sample size yields a statistical power of 98.5% for the multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA).

Randomisation

After baseline assessment, 176 participants will be randomly assigned 1:1 to either Group Propofol or Group Ciprofol. Statisticians will use SPSS (V.22.0 IBM USA) to generate random numbers. An independent anaesthesia nurse (Nurse A) will sequentially number and seal opaque envelopes containing corresponding random numbers and intervention methods. The envelopes will be stored by Nurse A. Researcher A will recruit patients and confirm eligibility based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. If eligible, Nurse A will provide the envelopes to Researcher B, who will open the envelopes in sequence.

Blinding

The first-level blind background refers to the group corresponding to the random number of each subject (Group A, Group B). The second-level blind code corresponds to the actual treatment (ciprofol or propofol). Random coding will be blinded, and Nurse A will be responsible for storing and distributing random numbers without involvement in other trial processes. Researcher C will conduct postoperative data collection, follow-up and statistical analysis, without participating in anaesthesia management or knowing group allocation. All patients will remain blinded to their group assignment throughout the perioperative period.

After the entire study is completed and the data have been cleaned and locked, group allocation will be disclosed. If adverse reactions occur during postoperative follow-up, Researcher C will report to the principal investigator, who will decide whether unblinding is necessary. If unblinding occurs, the principal investigator will check the allocation and inform Researcher C, but Researcher C will not disclose this to other team members.

Date management and monitoring

All researchers will receive standardised training to ensure uniform procedures. Participant information and outcomes will remain confidential. Researchers B and C will record intraoperative and postoperative data on the case report forms (CRFs). Modifications to CRFs are not allowed. The data on the CRF will be imported into the computer by the principal researcher and saved. The principal investigator will import CRF data into a computer database, and Researcher D will verify the data. Once confirmed, the data will be locked. After locking, any modifications must be traceable. Original CRFs will be stored in the Anesthesiology and Surgery Department of Xi’an Jiaotong University.

The Data Monitoring and Management Committee, composed of the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, will independently review and supervise research data. This committee is a third-party independent of the investigators and sponsors, with no conflict of interest. The committee will visit the clinical research centre every 12 months to review study conduct, including the protocol, ethical approvals, CRFs, informed consent forms and other materials. Reviews will confirm completeness and accuracy of subject information, randomisation, intervention allocation, adverse events and outcomes. The research team will also meet every 2 months to review study progress.

Patient safety and risk mitigation

Adverse events associated with ciprofol or propofol might include allergic reactions, hypotension and respiratory depression. In the event of an allergic reaction, medication will be stopped immediately, and Researcher B will apply standard allergy management. During ciprofol administration, blood pressure will be closely monitored. If hypotension occurs, vasopressors (eg, dopamine, norepinephrine) will be administered as clinically indicated. Post-anaesthesia respiratory function will be monitored; in cases of respiratory depression, airway interventions (eg, jaw thrust, oxygen therapy, assisted ventilation) will be applied. Most patients recover spontaneously, though respiratory support may be required in rare cases.

Unexpected events must be reported to the Data Monitoring and Safety Committee within 24 hours. The committee is composed of the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, and they will determine the causal relationship between the adverse event and the intervention and decide whether the patient should remain in the study. Serious adverse events (SAEs) related to study medications, once confirmed by investigators, will be documented in an adverse event report including onset time, severity, duration, management and outcome. SAEs will be reported to the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. If necessary, unblinding procedures will be followed to ensure patient safety.

Statistical analysis

In this study, the principle of intention-to-treat analysis will be applied to efficacy analysis. The safety analysis set will be used for safety assessment. Data will be analysed using RStudio (V.4.4.3). A two-sided p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant, and between-group differences will be deemed significant at this level.

For quantitative variables (eg, the time to first operative flatus, first defecate time after surgery, first meal intake time after surgery, hospitalisation costs, postoperative hospital length of stay), the Shapiro-Wilk test will be used for normality testing, and the F-test will be used to detect homoscedasticity. If data follow a normal distribution, results will be presented as mean±SD; otherwise, data will be expressed as median and quartiles. Qualitative variables (eg, POGD, POGI, haemodynamic suppression) will be presented as rates. To control the inflation of type I error due to multiple separate tests for differences among secondary outcomes, the MANOVA will be applied, statistical descriptions will be presented using mean vector, covariance matrix and correlation matrix, and the difference between the two groups will be assessed using Hotelling’s T-squared test. For quantitative data meeting normality and homoscedasticity assumptions, independent sample t-tests will be used; otherwise, the rank-sum test will be applied. Comparison of qualitative data between groups will be conducted using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, while ordinal data will be analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Missing data will be handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), under the assumption that data are missing at random. All analyses will be performed using RStudio (V.4.4.3). The imputation model will include clinically relevant variables involved in the analysis, such as group, age, gender and BMI. Linear regression for normally distributed continuous variables, predictive mean matching for non-normally distributed continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables and multinomial logistic regression for multi-category variables. The MICE procedure will be run for 10 iterations to generate 10 imputed datasets. Convergence will be assessed by examining iteration trace plots. The same statistical methods described above in this protocol will be applied to each imputed dataset. Results will then be pooled using Rubin’s rules. To evaluate the robustness of the results following imputation, outcomes from the multiple imputation analysis will be compared with those from a complete-case analysis. Similarity between the results will be regarded as supporting evidence for the robustness of the conclusions.

To assist in interpreting the clinical significance of the anticipated results, there is no universally accepted Minimal Important Difference (MID) for primary outcome. A previous study reported a time to flatus of 75.36 hours following radical colorectal surgery.15 This study will use this value as a minimally acceptable benchmark for flatus time. In the final conclusion, we will consider both clinical significance and statistical significance.

Discussion

Globally, the incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer rank third among all malignancies in both the male and female populations, and in China, the incidence rate of colorectal cancer is the second-highest, and the mortality rate is the third highest.16 The relatively high incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer pose a significant threat to the public health. Surgery remains the primary and effective treatment for colorectal cancer,17 but POI is a common postoperative complication.18 POI increases readmission rates, hospitalisation expenses and even mortality rates.3,5 The aetiopathogenesis of POI remains incompletely elucidated, the pathophysiological mechanism encompasses a complex process of neural reflex, inflammation, ischaemia-reperfusion injury, fluid replacement volume and drug pharmacology factors interacting with each other.18,20 Reported risk factors include male population, fluid overload, alcohol use disorder, a history of abdominal operation and adhesions, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, etc. 21,23 Current non-pharmacological prevention and treatment methods for POI involve gum-chewing, fluid volume restriction, laparoscopic surgery approach selection and the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol. Pharmacological approaches include cyclooxygenase inhibitors, 5-hydroxytryptamine 4 (5-HT4) receptor agonists, μ receptor antagonists, auxin and caffeine.24,26 However, no evidence exists regarding prevention from the perspective of anaesthesia drugs. Propofol, a widely used intravenous anaesthetic for anaesthesia maintenance, has been shown in both animal and clinical studies to inhibit gastrointestinal motility.7,9 Ciprofol is a newly developed intravenous anaesthetic, but no clinical studies have evaluated its effect on gastrointestinal motility following laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer. As previously stated, the choice of anaesthetic has an impact on gastrointestinal function. Postoperative gastrointestinal recovery is of importance for determining the timing of discharge after abdominal surgery, particularly in the context of gastrointestinal operations.19 27 As radical colorectal cancer surgery is a frequently performed procedure. Propofol, commonly used for intravenous anaesthetic maintenance, represents the standard comparative practice in several studies.1928,30 Hence, this study aims to explore the comparative effects of ciprofol versus propofol on gastrointestinal function following laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer. It is important to note that the expected findings of this study are only to be applicable specifically to the patient population who will undergo laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer.

This study has several strengths. First, it evaluates gastrointestinal recovery across multiple dimensions. Outcome measures include time to first flatus, consistent with the studies by Lu et al19 and Arici et al31 as well as use of the I-FEED scoring system within the ERAS protocol.13 This renders the evaluation indicators more comprehensive, rigorous and persuasive. Second, the exclusion criteria for this study are relatively strict, which will endow the research results of subjects meeting the inclusion criteria with greater specificity. And then, this study is a prospective, randomised controlled trial. Randomisation can effectively control patient-level bias and confounding factors in research. Our study can continuously monitor research subjects from the intervention stage to the outcome stage to enhance safety.

However, several limitations exist. The study includes participants aged 18–65 years, excluding older individuals; therefore, the effects of ciprofol versus propofol in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer cannot be evaluated. In addition, being a single-centre study, generalisability is limited by regional and ethnic factors. Several strategies will be adopted to mitigate potential centre-level selection bias, including the use of an independent web-based system that combines randomisation with allocation concealment, rigorous adherence to a standardised enrolment procedures in study protocol, and the blinding of outcome assessors for postoperative evaluations. Future research should involve multicentre, large-scale and potentially international trials. Finally, due to resource and time constraints, this study does not include biomarkers such as serum motilin and gastrin, or measures of organ function, which will be addressed in future research.

In summary, we designed a prospective, single-centre, single-blind, randomised controlled trial to evaluate the effect of ciprofol on postoperative gastrointestinal motility in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer.
