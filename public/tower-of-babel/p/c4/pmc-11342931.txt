Structural and Mutational Analyses of Trehalose Synthase from Deinococcus radiodurans Reveal the Interconversion of Maltose–Trehalose Mechanism
Authors: Li-Ci Ye, Sih-Yao Chow, San-Chi Chang, Chia-Hung Kuo, Yung-Lin Wang, Yong-Jun Wei, Guan-Chiun Lee, Shwu-Huey Liaw, Wen-Ming Chen, Sheng-Chia Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Trehalose synthase (TreS) catalyzes the reversible interconversion of maltose to trehalose, playing a vital role in trehalose production. Understanding the catalytic mechanism of TreS is crucial for optimizing the enzyme activity and enhancing its suitability for industrial applications. Here, we report the crystal structures of both the wild type and the E324D mutant of Deinococcus radiodurans trehalose synthase in complex with the trehalose analogue, validoxylamine A. By employing structure-guided mutagenesis, we identified N253, E320, and E324 as crucial residues within the +1 subsite for isomerase activity. Based on these complex structures, we propose the catalytic mechanism underlying the reversible interconversion of maltose to trehalose. These findings significantly advance our comprehension of the reaction mechanism of TreS.

Introduction

Trehalose is a nonreducing disaccharide composed of two d-glucose molecules linked by a 1,1-glycosidic bond. In biological systems, it plays multiple crucial roles,1,2 such as serving as a carbon and energy source, a structural component of bacterial cell walls, a signaling molecule, and a protectant during environmental stresses such as cold, dehydration, and heat.3−6 Because of its protective ability, stability, and low reactivity, trehalose is widely used in various industries. For example, in the food industry, it is used as a food additive to enhance the flavor and stability of food products.7−9 In medical applications, trehalose can effectively preserve organs and tissues for transplantation.10

Despite its broad applicability, the cost of production of trehalose remains relatively high, prompting investigations into more efficient biosynthetic processes to reduce costs of production. The most common approach for industrial trehalose production is the two-enzyme method which utilizes maltooligosyl trehalose synthase and maltooligosyl trehalose trehalohydrolase to transform maltooligosaccharides or starch into trehalose. This method is favored when using starch and maltodextrins as substrates, with conversion yields exceeding 80%.11,12 However, the efficiency of trehalose production drops when branched starch and maltose are used. This limitation can be overcome using the one-enzyme method, which directly utilizes trehalose synthase (TreS) to enable the interconversion of trehalose and maltose without a coenzyme13−15 (Figure 1).

The enzyme TreS belongs to the glycoside hydrolase family 13 (GH13) and consists of a catalytic (α/β) barrel as the central core with a C-terminal β-sandwich domain. To date, five TreS have been structurally characterized. Based on structure and sequence analysis, TreS from Deinococcus radiodurans ([DrTS], PDB ID: 4TVU) shares high identity with these structures, including those from Mycobacterium smegmatis (PDB ID: 3ZO9, 54.9% identity), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (PDB ID: 4LXF, 55.7% identity), Thermobaculum terrenum (PDB ID: 5X7U, 56.8% identity), and Thermomonospora curvata (PDB ID: 5H2T, 57.4% identity).16−20 TreS from these organisms share high sequence and structural similarities, and a similar catalytic mechanism that involves double displacement with a covalent glycosyl-enzyme intermediate. However, because the available knowledge on the complex structure of TreS is limited, the molecular mechanism underlying the interconversion of maltose and trehalose remains unresolved.17,21−24 Further elucidation of this process is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of trehalose synthesis.

Here, we elucidate the structures of TreS from D. radiodurans and the E324D mutant in a complex with validoxylamine A (VAA). By analyzing these complex structures, we identified N253 and E324 as the key residues within the +1 subsite that maintained the trehalose conformation.20 Furthermore, combining the structural insights and saturation mutational analysis of N253 mutants,25 we propose a molecular mechanism for the interconversion of maltose and trehalose. These present findings advance the understanding of the catalytic mechanism of TreS and pave the way for further studies on engineering TreS for industrial applications.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Ampicillin and isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) were purchased from MDBio (New Taipei, Taiwan), and protease inhibitor cocktail tablets were obtained from Roche (Basel, Switzerland). The restriction enzyme DpnI was purchased from New England BioLabs (Ipswich, MA, USA), and the QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis kit was purchased from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Ni-NTA agarose and Sephacryl S-300 columns were purchased from GenScript (Nanjing, China) and GE Healthcare (Chicago, IL, USA), respectively. All other Chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO, USA).

Protein Expression and Purification of DrTS

Recombinant (rec) wild-type (WT) and mutant DrTS (UniPort ID: I3NX86) were cloned into pET-23a(+) vector (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. The DNA encoding rec-WT was amplified by PCR from D. radiodurans genomic DNA and cloned into the pET-23a(+) vector. DrTS mutants were constructed similarly through site-directed mutagenesis (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The primers used for the site-directed mutagenesis and confirmation of mutants are listed in (Table S1). All PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis was performed using 14 cycles. The amplicon mixtures were digested with DpnI and transformed by heat shock into E. coli cells. Transformants were selected in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin following overnight incubation at 37 °C.

The overexpression of recombinant DrTS was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG when the preculture OD600 reached 0.4–0.6, followed by further incubation at 16 °C for 20 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 16,000g at 4 °C for 20 min and resuspended in buffer A (20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl). The cell suspension was then disrupted through sonication on ice, and the cell debris was separated by centrifugation at 13,865g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting lysate was loaded onto a pre-equilibrated 3 mL nickel–nitrilotriacetic acid agarose column. After washing with 20 mM imidazole in buffer A, the bound protein was eluted with buffer A containing 250 mM imidazole. DrTS was further purified using a Sephacryl S-300 size-exclusion column equilibrated with buffer A. The protein concentration was estimated by measuring the absorbance at OD280. The presence and purity of the protein were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Expression and purification of the mutant DrTS proteins were performed as described above.

Enzyme Activity Assay

The isomerase and hydrolase activities of the rec-WT and mutant DrTS were evaluated by measuring the amount of trehalose and glucose liberated from maltose, respectively. To assess the activity of the TreS mutants, 200 μL of 3.8 μM purified DrTS was mixed with 800 μL of 125 mM maltose and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by heating the mixture in boiling water for 10 min. The quantities of maltose, trehalose, and glucose in each reaction mixture were measured using a high-performance liquid-chromatography (HPLC) system (Schambeck SFD 2100, Bad Honnef, Germany) fitted with a refractive-index detector (SFD, RI 2000) and operating at a flow rate of 1 mL min–1. A carbohydrate-analysis column (6.0 × 150 mm, Shodex SZ5532) pre-equilibrated with 75% acetonitrile, 24% Milli-Q water, and 1% formic acid was used as the mobile phase. The relative activity in this study is defined as one unit of the isomerase or hydrolase activity that catalyzes the formation of 1 nmol of trehalose or glucose per minute.

Crystallization and Data Collection

All batch crystallization experiments of the rec-WT and DrTS mutant proteins were performed by sitting drop vapor diffusion at 15 °C using commercial crystallization screening kits (Hampton Research Corp., CA, USA). Initially, the proteins were adjusted to a working concentration of 30 mg mL–1. The crystallization drop was prepared by mixing 1 μL of either rec-WT or DrTS and mutants with 1 μL of the crystallization mother liquor at a ratio of 1:1. Crystals of rec-WT were previously grown in 0.3 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 0.2 M sodium acetate trihydrate, and 9% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG) 4000. To achieve larger and more uniform protein crystals from drops consisting of 1 μL protein solution and 1 μL reservoir solution, modified reservoir conditions (0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 8.5, and 8% [w/v] PEG 8000) were used, except for the WT–VAA and the mutant E324D–VAA. Specifically, WT–VAA crystals were obtained under reservoir conditions comprised of 0.1 M imidazole (pH 6.5) and 1.0 M sodium acetate trihydrate, while the E324D–VAA crystal was obtained under reservoir conditions of 0.2 M potassium sodium tartrate tetrahydrate and 20% (w/v) PEG 3,350 (pH 7.4). Crystals of DrTS–VAA and E324D–VAA complexes were prepared by incubating in a reservoir solution containing 1 mM VAA for 1 h. All crystals were transferred to a cryoprotectant solution in the reservoir condition, adjusted to contain 25% glycerol, and were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction images of DrTS–VAA and mutant crystals were recorded at the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (Taiwan) in TLS BL15A and TPS BL05A beamlines, respectively. The data were then integrated, scaled, and merged using the HKL2000 software.26

Structure Determination

Crystal structures of DrTS–VAA and mutants were determined using the molecular replacement method with Phaser27 in the PHENIX package.28 The coordinates of WT–DrTS (PDB ID: 4TVU) were used as the search model. The initial structure was refined through iterative cycles of simulated annealing, energy minimization, and manual rebuilding using Refmac refinement and COOT.29,30 Molecular visualizations were generated with PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, version 1.7, Schrödinger, LLC). Table S2 summarizes the data collection and refinement statistics.

VAA Preparation

VAA, a competitive inhibitor of TreS31 is obtained through acid-mediated deglycosylation of validamycin A (VA)32 (Figure 2A). In this study, 2.5 g of VA was first dissolved in 2.5 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, VA hydrolysis was initiated by adding 10 mL of 1 M H2SO4 to the VA solution. The mixture was then heated in boiling water on a hot plate for 1 h. The reaction mixture was then cooled to room temperature and neutralized to a pH of 5.0 by adding Ba(OH)2. After centrifugation at 4,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatant containing a mixture of VA, VAA, and glucose was collected for purification.

Preparation of VAA. (A) VAA was prepared from acid-hydrolyzed VA. (B) VAA was separated by HPLC. (C) VA standard. (D) VAA standard. DrTS; Deinococcus radiodurans trehalose synthase; HPLC, high-performance liquid chromatography; VA, validamycin A; VAA, validoxylamine A.

Since VAA is protonated at an acidic pH (pKa 6.0), the supernatant was loaded onto a cation-exchange column (Amberlite IR120 Na). The bound VAA was then eluted with 1 M of NH4OH. Subsequent purification utilized an anion exchange column (Dowex 1X2 chloride form). Finally, the collected effluent containing purified VAA was lyophilized (freeze-dried) to yield a white powder (0.5 mg).

Purity analysis of the isolated VAA was performed using HPLC equipped with a photodiode array detector (L-2455, Hitachi, Japan) at a flow rate of 1 mL min–1. A Hypersil TM ODS (C18) column (250 × 4.6 mm, Thermo, New Jersey, USA) equilibrated with 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0) and 2.5% methanol was used for separation. The sample was monitored at OD210 and the VA and VAA were identified by chromatography using authentic standards (Figure 2B–D).

Results

Protein Preparation

Protein purification for both rec-WT and mutant proteins was conducted using similar conditions. Approximately 20–30 mg of DrTS protein was obtained from 1 L of LB medium. After verifying protein purity through SDS-PAGE, the protein was quantified and stored at 4 °C for activity analysis. For the rec-WT and mutant proteins used in crystallization, the dialysis buffer was changed to 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM dithiothreitol. Following dialysis, gel filtration was used to enhance protein homogeneity. Fractions containing the recombinant DrTS protein were collected and further concentrated to a final volume of 100–250 μL. Both WT and mutant proteins exhibited stability and were subsequently used for crystallization and activity analysis.

Structure of DrTS–VAA Complex

A previous study demonstrated that VAA is a competitive inhibitor of TreS in the presence of trehalose.33 In this study, the binary DrTS–VAA complex crystal exhibited diffraction to a resolution of 2.83 Å in the presence of VAA. The crystal structure of DrTS–VAA conformed to the P212121 space group, accommodating two DrTS dimers per asymmetric unit. Solution SAXS analysis of DrTS showed that it existed as a dimer in solution (Figure S1). Each DrTS protomer was composed of a catalytic (α/β)8 barrel forming the central core, accompanied by a C-terminal β-sandwich domain (Figure 3A). VAA was accommodated deeply within a pocket formed by subdomain B and the catalytic core domain. The binding of VAA to DrTS involved all hydroxyl groups (OH) on both C7-cyclitol units. Specifically, the C7-cyclitol unit at the −1 subsite stacked against Y66, while O2 of C7-cyclitol formed hydrogen bonds with R207, D209, E251, H318, and D319. O3 of cyclitol interacted closely with H318 and D319, O4 formed hydrogen bonds with D63 and R398, and O6 formed hydrogen bonds with H106 and Q177. The N atom of the nonglycosidic C–N bond interacted with E251 OE1. In the C7-cyclitol unit at the +1 subsite, O2 and O3 made close contact with E320, and O7 formed hydrogen bonds with E324 and N253 (Figure 3B).

Structural comparison between the Apo and complex structures showed that VAA binding did not lead to conformational changes, suggesting that the active site is well-organized in the closed conformation. The structural similarities between trehalose and VAA may explain the resemblance between the DrTS–VAA complex structure and trehalose binding. Notably, N253, E320, and E324 are presumed to play crucial roles in trehalose binding and the interconversion of maltose and trehalose.

Structural and Saturation Mutational Analysis of N253

Structural analysis of the DrTS–VAA complex revealed the potential significance of N253 in trehalose binding and glucose rotation. To confirm the function of N253, a saturated mutant library of N253 was constructed. The results showed that only four mutations (N253C, N253A, N235L, and N253D) retained isomerization activity. Among these, N253C retained about 40% of the trehalose yield, while N253A, N253L, and N253D retained approximately 5–10%. Conversely, the other N253 mutants showed a complete loss of isomerization activity. Interestingly, the hydrolysis activity of N253C, N253A, N253L, N253Q, and N253D was enhanced, whereas the other 14 mutants exhibited hydrolysis activity similar to that of the WT. Importantly, only N253R displayed a complete loss of isomerization and hydrolysis activity (Table 1).

Effects of Mutations in the +1 Subsite of DrTS on Isomerase and Hydrolase Activities

To elucidate the molecular basis of N253 mutants, we determined the crystal structures of N253C, N253E, N253H, N253Q, and N253T. The crystal structures of these mutants exhibited closed conformation similar to that of DrTS–Tris. Further analysis revealed that N253E and N253Q induced steric hindrance at the +1 subsite of glucose, blocking trehalose binding (Figure S2A,B). Additionally, N253T and N253H lost interactions with E324, consequently preventing trehalose binding (Figures S2D,E). The substitution of N253 with Cys enhanced the flexibility of E324, leading to the formation of a small aperture above the active site, facilitating water entry (Figure S2C). This allowed water molecules to access the active site and attack the glycosyl-enzyme intermediate, increasing the maltose hydrolase activity of the mutants. These results suggest that N253 has undergone strong purifying selection during evolution and that this conserved residue is crucial for isomerase activity.

Our previous study has shown that the structures of the WT and the N253A mutant are nearly identical, with the only notable difference being the rotational orientation of the Glu324 side chain. In the N253A mutant, disrupting the interaction between Asn253 and Glu324 led to a positional shift in the Glu324 side chain. This shift created an opening that allowed the solvent molecules to access the enzyme’s active site.18 In this investigation, we found that Asn253 and Glu324 directly interacted with VAA. This suggests that the functional roles of Asn253 and Glu324 are crucial for isomerase activity.

Structure-Guided Mutagenesis Elucidates a Consensus Motif, NHDELTLE, which Is Essential for Isomerase Activity

A previous study highlighted the conservation of several motifs in TreS.34 In the DrTS–VAA complex structure, a consensus motif, NHDELTLE, was identified as being involved in VAA binding at the +1 subsite, mediated by residues E320 and E324 (Figure S3). To elucidate the functional significance of these residues, glutamates were mutated to aspartates, resulting in shortened side chains while maintaining the charge. The findings revealed that the mutants E320D and E324D completely lost their isomerase activity while retaining their hydrolase activity. Interestingly, the hydrolase activity of E324D exhibited a 2-fold increase. The formation of an aperture at the active site in the E324D mutant may facilitate water entry, consequently enhancing hydrolase activity.

To further validate the functional roles of E324, we conducted structural analysis of the E324D–VAA complex. Most interaction networks were similar between WT–VAA and E324D–VAA complexes, with one notable exception: E324D lost interaction with the O7 atom of VAA (Figure 4). This structural alteration may hinder glucose rotation, leading to the loss of isomerase activity. Our structural and mutational analyses provide robust evidence supporting the notion that NHDELTLE is a functional motif essential for isomerase activity.

Comparison with Homologous Structures

Structural comparison of the five TreS revealed that MtTS, MsTS, and TcTS had an open TS conformation, whereas the structures DrTS–VAA and TtTS–Tris had a closed conformation. In the open conformation of TS, subdomain B rotated outward from the active site, and a loop from the subdomain S7 blocked the substrate entry. Conversely, in the DrTS–VAA and TtTS–Tris complexes, the interaction between subdomains B, S7, and the residues in the active site sealed the entrance to the active site. This arrangement resulted in a well-organized active site optimized for isomerase activity (Figure S4A,B). The closed conformation of the active site was consistent between DrTS–VAA and TtTS–Tris. This conformation, induced by the presence of the substrate, is a conserved feature, essential for catalyzing intramolecular isomerization.

A Trehalose and Maltose Model for the Catalytic Interconversion Mechanism

Using the DrTS–VAA structure as a template, we conducted molecular modeling to predict the binding of a trehalose molecule into the active site of DrTS, and used this information to predict energy minimization. Due to the striking similarity in active-site architecture between DrTS and Neisseria polysaccharea amylosucrase (NpAS), we used the nonreducing terminal maltosyl residue from the NpAS–maltoheptaose complex to fit the −1 and +1 subsites. Furthermore, we modeled a maltose molecule into the active site. Based on our findings, we therefore propose a three-step process: glycosylation (glycosyl-TreS + glucose), glucose rotation, and deglycosylation (trehalose formation) (Figure 5).

Two binding models of DrTS. The interaction networks between DrTS and the modeled maltose and trehalose in the +1 subsite. Hydrogen bonds are shown as gray dashed lines. DrTS; Deinococcus radiodurans trehalose synthase.

There are two possible binding modes for TS: maltose and trehalose binding modes. As glucose is liberated from maltose at the +1 subsite, it can switch between these two modes, suggesting that glucose rotation plays a pivotal role in controlling both forward and reverse reactions. Mutation of either E324 or N253 abolished isomerase activity, indicating their critical roles in the process. Moreover, E324 or N253 most likely interacted with O6 of glucose. The interaction between O6 of glucose and the enzyme facilitated glucose rotation mediated by E324 and N253. The E324D–VAA complex structure supports this proposition.

Discussion

Trehalose synthesis is crucial for microorganisms inhabiting extreme environments, thus understanding its mechanism is important.2 The enzyme TreS, characterized by conserved amino acid motifs, exerts its catalytic activity through a dual mechanism akin to that of the members of the GH13 family.35 Given its unique properties, trehalose has found widespread application in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries.36 Our structural and mutational analyses provided insights into the functional roles of the conserved residue N253 in DrTS regarding reaction specificity. Integrating these findings with mechanistic and structural studies of TS and other GH13 enzymes, we propose a reaction mechanism for DrTS (Figure 6A–F).

A proposed catalytic mechanism of DrTS. (A) Trehalose release and the apo state. (B) Maltose binding. (C) Glycosylation state. (D)Hydrolysis and the release of glucose. (E) Glucose rotation. (F) Deglycosylation state. DrTS; Deinococcus radiodurans trehalose synthase.

Upon maltose binding, the nonreducing terminal glucose of the substrate binds to the GH13-conserved residues at the −1 subsite. These enzyme–substrate interactions induce an approximately 9-degree rotation of the subdomain B toward the active site, forming an enclosed active-site conformation. In the +1 subsite, as depicted in the DrTS–maltose complex modeling, the +1 subsite of glucose in maltose directly interacts with Y213, and the unique TS residues E320 and E324. Following substrate binding, the nucleophile D209 attacks the C1 at +1 subsite of glucose, forming a covalent glycosyl-enzyme intermediate. The enclosed active site not only provides an environment that protects the intermediate from nucleophilic attack by water, but also preserves the cleaved +1 subsite of glucose for rotation by N253 and E324. Subsequently, the glucose molecule rotates and attacks the covalent intermediate with its OH1 group, thus completing the intramolecular isomerization, resulting in the formation of trehalose.

In DrTS, the extensive interaction networks above the active site entrance stabilize the closed conformation, particularly by the interactions between R148, N253, E324, and between D153 and N347. The residue N253 plays a crucial role in maintaining the orientation of the E324 side chain. Substituting N253 with Ala, Cys, Glu, His, Gln, and Thr, as well as substituting R148 with Ala, renders the side chain of E324 flexible. Consequently, mutations that induce the formation of active-site apertures for water entry are observed on the protein surface, resulting in higher hydrolytic activity and lower isomerase activity for mutants N253A, N253C, and R148A (Figure S5A–D). Additionally, the DrTS–VAA structure in this study suggests that N253 has a closer binding orientation to trehalose than maltose. There is potential steric hindrance between the +1 subsite of glucose in trehalose and the introduced side chain atoms in the N253Q, N253E, and N253T mutants, providing insights into their activity.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the conserved N253 and E324 residues have evolved to become optimal amino acids at their respective positions for efficient isomerization catalysis. N253 not only participates in forming the closed conformation through interactions with E324 but also plays a critical role in determining isomerase and hydrolase preference. The results of this study provide a structural basis for future engineering and applications of TreS in the biosynthesis of trehalose and other valuable disaccharides.
