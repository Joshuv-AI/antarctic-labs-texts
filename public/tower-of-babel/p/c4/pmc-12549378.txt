Psoas abscess caused by non-typhoid Salmonella in a man with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV): A rare case report
Authors: Maya Habous, Laila Aldabal, Rubina Lone, Adnan Alatoom, Anju Nabi, Rubina Monga, Bhavna Mathur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) typically causes self-limiting gastroenteritis but can rarely present with severe extraintestinal manifestations, especially in immunocompromised individuals. We report a case of a 32-year-old HIV-positive man on antiretroviral therapy, with a history of pulmonary Mycobacterium riyadhense infection, who presented with progressive hip pain, flank swelling, and intermittent low-grade fevers. Imaging revealed a large multiloculated left iliopsoas abscess with secondary extension, and cultures from surgical drainage grew NTS. Despite initial incision and drainage, the patient required repeat open debridement and prolonged antimicrobial therapy with ertapenem and azithromycin, alongside antiretroviral and antimycobacterial regimens. He showed significant clinical and radiological improvement after eight weeks of therapy. This case highlights the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach, given the complexities of treating infections in HIV patients who may have drug–drug interactions and may need prolonged courses of treatment.

Introduction

Salmonella species are Gram-negative, rod-shaped bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. They are broadly classified into Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori, with numerous serovars distinguished by somatic (O) and flagellar (H) antigens [1]. Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) infections are broad ranging from asymptomatic chronic carriage and self-limiting gastroenteritis to more severe forms such as enteric fever, bacteremia, and localized extraintestinal complications, including potentially fatal endovascular infections. While extraintestinal manifestations are generally rare, their incidence and severity are significantly increased in immunocompromised individuals, notably those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/ acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) [2], [3].

A psoas abscess may be classified as primary when no underlying cause is identified, or secondary when it results from direct spread of infection from an adjacent structure [4]. The association between NTS and psoas abscess is rarely reported in the literature. In documented cases, the majority were linked to underlying conditions such as chronic renal failure [5], diabetes mellitus [6], or hematologic disorders like aplastic anemia [7]. The occurrence of NTS as a causative agent of psoas abscess in patients with HIV/AIDS remains poorly studied. In this report, we describe a complex case of a 32-year-old HIV male undergoing treatment for HIV and Mycobacterium riyadhense who developed a psoas abscess resulting from NTS infection.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old man with a known history of HIV infection, diagnosed eight years earlier, presented to the emergency department with a two-week history of progressive left hip pain, swelling over the iliolumbar and flank regions, and intermittent low-grade fevers. He reported that the pain had gradually worsened over the past four months. The patient had not engaged in regular HIV follow-up until 10 months earlier, when he developed pulmonary Mycobacterium riyadhense infection with a CD4 count of 5 cells/mm³ . At that time, he was initiated on antiretroviral therapy (dolutegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir) and concurrent antimycobacterial therapy (rifampicin, ethambutol, and moxifloxacin). On follow-up, his most recent CD4 count was 56 cells/mm³ , consistent with persistent severe immunosuppression.

On examination, his vital signs were: blood pressure 108/67 mmHg, heart rate 137 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, temperature 37.8 °C, and oxygen saturation 98 % on room air. There was localized erythema, swelling, and tenderness over the left hip and iliolumbar region. No focal neurological deficits were noted. Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (white blood cell count, 13.8 × 10⁹/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP, 134.4 mg/L) and a decreased CD4 count (54 cells/mm³). Blood cultures showed no growth. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast demonstrated a multiloculated left iliopsoas abscess measuring 7.3 × 7.0 × 24.0 cm and a left quadratus lumborum abscess measuring 3.4 × 5.3 × 4.2 cm, with retroperitoneal inflammatory changes (Fig. 1).Fig. 1CT scan of the abdomen showing a large multilocular left iliopsoas abscess (yellow arrow) measuring 7.3 × 7 × 24 cm: (a) coronal view, (b) axial view additionally demonstrating a left quadratus lumborum abscess (red arrow) measuring 3.4 × 5.3 × 4.2 cm.Fig. 1

On the day of admission, the patient was administered empiric vancomycin in addition to anti-mycobacterial therapy for Mycobacterium riyadhense and antiretroviral therapy (ART). Furthermore, he underwent incision and drainage (I&D) of the iliopsoas abscess, an intraoperative sample was obtained from the abscess fluid, and a surgical drain was placed. Based on Gram-negative stain and preliminary growth, antibiotic therapy with ertapenem 1 g intravenously (IV) once daily was initiated on day 2, instead of ceftriaxone, due to a documented allergy. On day 3, intraoperative sample cultures of the abscess fluid grew non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica serogroup D (O antigen group 9 positive). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed intermediate susceptibility to fluoroquinolones, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 0.5 µg/ml for both ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin. The isolate was susceptible to carbapenems. Therefore, he was kept on ertapenem, and vancomycin was stopped. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine on day 8 revealed no primary disc involvement but showed secondary extension of the abscess to the L1, L2, L3, and sacral vertebrae. The antibiotic regimen for the psoas abscess remained ertapenem, alongside the patient's existing ART and antimycobacterial treatment. However, due to persistent collection, the ertapenem was switched to meropenem 1 g IV every eight hours on day 11, at which time further wound debridement was performed, and a wound vacuum (VAC) was applied.

On day 16, the patient was discharged in stable condition on ertapenem (for ease of outpatient administration) and azithromycin for a total planned duration of eight weeks, in addition to continuing ART and anti-mycobacterial therapy for Mycobacterium riyadhense. Follow-up CT at week six demonstrated significant regression of both abscesses. Ertapenem and azithromycin were discontinued after a total of eight weeks of targeted antimicrobial therapy. Discussion

This case demonstrated a rare presentation of NTS as psoas muscle abscess in an immunocompromised patient. Although extra-intestinal Salmonella infections, including psoas abscesses, have been described in patients with underlying conditions such as chronic renal failure and diabetes [5], [6], cases of psoas abscess caused by non-typhoidal Salmonella in HIV-positive individuals are extremely uncommon. To our knowledge, this is the first documented instance of NTS causing a psoas abscess in an HIV-positive individual. Recognizing this unusual presentation is crucial for timely diagnosis and management, particularly in patients with low CD4 counts who remain susceptible to atypical infections despite ART. The multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists, surgeons, radiologists, and microbiologists was essential to successfully manage and treat the infection.

The most common clinical manifestation of salmonellosis is gastroenteritis, which accounts for almost 77 % while the remaining cases are attributed to asymptomatic chronic carriage, enteric fever, bacteremia, and extraintestinal localized complications involving the meninges, bones, joints, surgical wounds, or gallbladder [8]. Although NTS is known to cause gastroenteritis, our patient did not have any gastrointestinal symptoms. Instead, his main complaint was psoas abscess, which is an exceedingly rare complication of salmonellosis.

According to Navarro L et al. [9], psoas abscess in HIV patients is most commonly caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus and Mycobacterium avium. Only a limited number of cases have reported psoas abscess in patients with HIV. Simon et al. [10] described a case of bilateral psoas abscess caused by disseminated Mycobacterium avium in a patient with CD4 count of 45 cells/mm³ despite the patient’s compliance with ART. Olasinde et al. [11] reported a case of bilateral psoas abscess in an HIV-positive patient caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, despite the patient’s adherence to ART and a CD4 count of 550 cells/mm³. Similarly, Kozakis L. et al. [12] described a case of tuberculosis-associated bilateral psoas abscess in a newly diagnosed HIV patient with a CD4 count of 84 cells/mm³. A rare case was documented by Corti et al. [13], describing an HIV patient with a CD4 count of 32 cells/mm³ who had never received antiretroviral therapy, presented with a psoas abscess caused by disseminated Nocardia asteroides.

Despite the availability of ART, patients with poorly controlled HIV or low CD4 counts remain at high risk for opportunistic and atypical infections [14]. In our case, the patient was on ART but likely had suboptimal immune recovery, which may have contributed to his susceptibility to this rare NTS psoas abscess. The patient's low CD4 count likely contributed to increased susceptibility to this rare complication. Additionally, a recent infection with Mycobacterium riyadhense may have further compromised immune function or caused structural tissue changes, predisposing to abscess formation.

The clinical presentation of psoas abscess is often nonspecific, typically manifesting as a prolonged infection course associated with back pain. This may be accompanied by fever in pyogenic abscesses or remain afebrile in cold abscesses. In addition, patients often exhibit elevated inflammatory markers, such as increased C-reactive protein levels and leukocytosis; however, these findings are not specific for diagnosis [15]. The diagnosis of psoas abscess in immunocompromised patients can be particularly challenging due to its nonspecific clinical presentation and often negative blood cultures, as seen in our case, this may delay timely diagnosis and intervention [16]. In this case, the diagnosis was further complicated by the patient’s ongoing treatment with moxifloxacin for Mycobacterium riyadhense. Although fluoroquinolones generally have activity against Salmonella species, the isolate demonstrated intermediate susceptibility, with MIC values of 0.5 µg/ml for both ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin. This reduced susceptibility likely contributed to therapeutic failure and may explain the negative blood cultures, as partial suppression of bacterial growth could have occurred. Detailed serotyping confirmed Salmonella enterica serogroup D (O antigen group 9 positive), a serotype frequently associated with invasive disease in immunocompromised hosts. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive microbiological work-up, including susceptibility testing and serotyping, particularly in patients with prior or ongoing antimicrobial exposure. The diagnosis of psoas abscess relies primarily on radiological imaging. While both ultrasound and CT scan can detect a psoas abscess, CT scan offers superior sensitivity. It enables precise evaluation of the abscess extent, identification of underlying lesions, guidance for microbiological sampling via needle aspiration, and planning for therapeutic drainage [17].

Management of psoas abscess involves both targeted antimicrobial therapy and adequate drainage. Due to the limited literature, there are no established guidelines for the management of psoas abscess caused by Salmonella species. However, Peker et al. [5] reported that incision and drainage, combined with ciprofloxacin, was sufficient for successful treatment. In our case the patient did not respond to incision and drainage and therefore required open debridement which could be due to the immunocompromised status of the patient. He subsequently improved on a prolonged course of ertapenem and azithromycin, tailored to the microbiological profile and his need for outpatient compatible therapy.

Managing infections in HIV-positive patients requires careful consideration of potential drug-drug interactions between ART and antibiotics, as well as the need for prolonged antimicrobial courses to ensure adequate pathogen clearance. Balancing effective treatment while minimizing toxicity and resistance is critical, emphasizing the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists, microbiologists, radiologists, and surgeons.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare presentation of Salmonella infection manifesting as a psoas abscess in an HIV patient. The absence of gastrointestinal symptoms and the need for open debridement underscore the importance of considering atypical causes of psoas abscess, such as Salmonella species, especially in immunocompromised patients. Further studies are needed to guide optimal management strategies for such rare presentations.
