Effect of high-quality antenatal care on skilled birth attendant utilization in Ethiopia: a propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Asebe Hagos, Tesfahun Zemene Tafere, Melak Jejaw, Endalkachew Dellie, Kaleb Assegid Demissie, Getachew Teshale, Nigusu Worku, Demiss Mulatu Geberu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting evidence suggests that access to and utilization of skilled birth attendants in Ethiopia remain suboptimal. High-quality antenatal care (ANC), adequate ANC contacts and components, is associated with an increased likelihood of receiving assistance from skilled birth attendants. However, the effect of these ANC interventions on the utilization of skilled birth attendants in Ethiopia has not been thoroughly investigated using rigorous statistical methods. Therefore, this study, aimed to assess the effects of receiving high-quality ANC services, adequate ANC contacts, and comprehensive ANC components on the utilization of skilled birth attendants in Ethiopia employing a robust analytical approach.MethodsWe utilized secondary data from the six-week postpartum survey of the second cohort, alongside the baseline information from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) Ethiopia longitudinal study, which was conducted from November 2021 to October 2022. The study involved selection 1596 participants using a multistage stratified cluster sampling method. To establish rigorous causal estimates, we applied multiple analytical methods to assess the effect of high-quality ANC, adequate ANC contact, and adequate ANC components on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. The primary analysis utilized propensity score matching followed by adjusted regression to account for remaining confounders. Additionally, we performed a supplementary multivariable logistic regression analysis.ResultsAfter adjusting for residual confounding in the matched sample, the adjusted average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) showed that high-quality ANC was associated with a 5.7 percentage point increase in the likelihood of utilizing skilled birth attendants (adjusted ATT = 0.0575; 95% CI 0.024–0.090; p = 0.001). Similarly, the adjusted ATT was 0.0605 (95% CI 0.028–0.092; p < 0.001) for adequate ANC contact and 0.0574 (95% CI 0.024–0.090; p = 0.001) for adequate ANC components, reflecting 6.0 and 5.7 percentage point increases in skilled birth attendant use, respectively. In addition, the average marginal effects (AME) indicated that, on average, high-quality ANC, adequate ANC contact, and adequate ANC components increased the probability of skilled birth attendant utilization by 7.1, 6.0, and 9.2 percentage points, respectively.ConclusionThis study provides robust evidence that high-quality ANC services increase the likelihood of skilled birth attendant utilization during delivery. It also reported a positive association between adequate ANC contacts, the receipt of adequate ANC components, and the use of skilled birth attendants. Therefore, our findings underscore that increasing the number of ANC contacts, ensuring the provision of recommended ANC components, and consistently promoting overall high-quality ANC are critical strategies for improving skilled birth attendant utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13169-8.Keywords: Propensity score matching analysis, Skilled birth attendant, Quality antenatal care, ANC, ANC contact, ANC component, Ethiopia

Introduction

In 2020, approximately 287,000 women worldwide died from maternal-related causes, which equates to nearly 800 deaths daily, or about one every two minutes [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for around 70% (202,000) of these deaths, while Southern Asia contributed about 16% (47,000) [1, 2]. Ethiopia has significantly reduced its maternal mortality ratio from 953 per 100,000 live births in 2000 to 267 in 2020, while neonatal mortality decreased from 48 to 27 per 1,000 live births during the same period [1, 3]. Despite this progress, the country still faced high mortality numbers in 2020, which approximately 10,000 maternal deaths, 83,000 stillbirths, and 104,000 neonatal deaths [1, 3].

Effective maternal health intervention can prevent most of maternal and newborn deaths [4]. A study conducted in 2018 revealed that the primary causes of maternal mortality in Ethiopia included obstetric complications such as hemorrhage, obstructed labor, pregnancy-induced hypertension, puerperal sepsis, and unsafe abortion [5]. Maternal healthcare services provided by skilled health care providers before, during and after childbirth have the potential to significantly decrease maternal and newborn mortality rates [4]. The presence of skilled healthcare provider during childbirth is crucial for saving the lives of both mothers and newborns [6]. Countries with high maternal and neonatal mortality rates often experience low coverage of skilled birth attendants [6, 7]. This gap in coverage contributes to poorer health outcomes for mothers and newborns [6, 7].

Existing evidence reveals that access to and utilization of skilled birth attendants in Ethiopia remains suboptimal [8–10]. In response to this, the Ethiopian government is actively striving to enhance the coverage and utilization of skilled birth attendants as part of its commitment to improving maternal, newborn, and child health outcomes and achieving the Sustainable Development Goals [11]. Ensuring high-quality antenatal care (ANC), along with adherence to the recommended number of contacts and provision of essential components, contributes to improved maternal and neonatal health outcomes. It facilitates the early identification of complication, offers prompt interventions, and nurtures a bond between the mother and health care provider concerning her health and the well-being of her newborn [12]. High-quality ANC interventions, including adequate contact frequency and essential service components, establish a link between the woman and her family and the health care system, increase the likelihood of utilizing skilled birth attendants during delivery, and improve overall health throughout life [13].

Quality ANC encompasses evidence-based services, including appropriate examinations, diagnostic tests, preventive and curative interventions, effective counseling, as well as the recommended number of contacts and essential components [14].

Several studies have reported that women who received high-quality ANC services, adequate ANC contact, and received the recommended ANC component had significantly higher odds utilizing skilled birth attendants and delivering in health facilities [15–18]. However, these ANC interventions alone may not directly lead to the utilization of skilled birth attendants. Instead, mothers who attend ANC services may differ in certain characteristics, such as being more educated or wealthier, which are known to influence in the utilization of skilled birth attendants [13]. To address confounding associations between these ANC interventions and skilled birth attendants use, researchers have traditionally relied on regression analyses. However, this approach may not fully eliminate bias. For instance, the observed positive relationship between ANC and skilled birth attendants use may still be influenced by differences in the distribution of factors affecting ANC utilization among mothers who use high-quality ANC versus those who do not, even after accounting for these factors within regression models [19–22]. In such case, propensity scores are valuable for estimating the effect of a treatment on an outcome in observational studies, particularly when selection bias from nonrandom treatment assignment is a concern [23–25].

In Ethiopia, some studies have investigated the association between the frequency of ANC and the utilization of skilled birth attendants [15, 26]. However, the effects of high-quality ANC, adequate ANC contact, and essential service components on skilled birth attendant utilization have not been thoroughly investigated using rigorous statistical methods. To address this gap, it is crucial to employ a rigorous analytical approach to quantify how these three key dimensions of ANC influence utilization of skilled birth attendance. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association of high-quality ANC, adequate ANC contact, and receipt of adequate ANC components with the utilization of skilled birth attendants in Ethiopia, using propensity score matching (PSM) combined with logistic regression analyses.

Methods

Study settings, design, and data source

Ethiopia, located in eastern Africa, is a landlocked country covering a total area of 1,104,300 square kilometers. As the second most populous country on the content, it has an estimated population of 129.7 million, with approximately 78% living in rural area [27]. Ethiopia is administratively divided into 14 regions: Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples’ (SNNP), Gambella, Harari, Sidama, South west Ethiopia, Central Ethiopia as well as the cities of Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa.

The study used data from the performance monitoring for action (PMA) Ethiopia survey. This survey project was designed to collect information on a range of reproductive, maternal, and newborn health indicators, providing essential insights for national and regional governments. Two rounds of a longitudinal survey were conducted, with the first round spanning from 2019 to 2021 and the second round from 2021 to 2023. In this study, data from the six-week postpartum survey of the second-round cohort dataset was analyzed [28]. The complete dataset was formed by merging the six-week follow-up survey with the baseline data from the second panel cohort of PMA Ethiopia data, using participant identification numbers [29].

Data were collected from the major regions of Ethiopia, including Amhara, Oromia, SNNP, and Addis Ababa city administration. The study participants were selected using multistage, stratified cluster sampling techniques. Initially, Amhara, Oromia, and SNNP were stratified into urban and rural areas, while Addis Ababa city was considered an urban stratum. A sample frame from the central statistical agency (CSA) was utilized to select 162 enumeration areas based on probabilities proportional to their sizes within the strata. In the next stage, 35 households were randomly chosen from each enumeration area [28].

Study population and sample size

We enrolled women aged 15–49 years who reported being pregnant or having given birth within the past six weeks in the baseline survey. For the six-week follow-up, we included only those participants who had consented to follow-up during baseline and subsequently completed the six-week survey [29, 30]. Women who had attended at least one ANC visit were included in this study. The minimum required sample size for this study was estimated using the two-proportion formula for comparing the effect of high- versus poor-quality ANC on skilled birth attendance. The following assumptions were applied: proportion of poor-quality ANC (P1) = 0.49, proportion of high-quality ANC (P2) = 0.60 (absolute effect size = 11) [15], 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), 80% power, and a 1:1 matching ratio for the PSM analysis. To adjust for design complexity [28], a design effect of 1.5 was applied, along with a 10% allowance for non-response. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 1,070 (535 per group). However, to increase the statistical power of the study and improve the robustness of our findings, we included all 1,596 available women in the final analysis. A schematic diagram illustrating the selection of the study population is presented in the supplementary file (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data were gathered using tablet computer through the Open Data Kit (ODK) system. Prior to data collection, the PMA Ethiopia conducted a comprehensive two-week training program for supervisors and resident enumerators (data collectors). The training emphasized a thorough review of survey protocols, questionnaire content, and interview techniques. It involved a combination of classroom exercises and three days of field training. In the baseline survey, the data collectors collected data on various socioeconomic and demographic aspects of women, such as age, education, region, parity, residence, marital status, household wealth status, and migrant status, as well as fertility preferences and birth histories [28, 30]. Similarly, during the six-week postpartum interview, information was collected on important maternal and newborn health indicators. This included data on ANC (timing, number, and contents of ANC), detailed delivery services (facility delivery and skilled birth attendant), and the receipt of immediate postpartum services for both the mother and newborn. Further information can be found in an alternate source [28].

Variables and measurement

Outcome variable

The outcome variable of the study is the utilization of skilled birth attendant. Women are coded as 1 if assisted by doctors, nurses, midwives, and health officers and 0 otherwise.

Treatment variable

The primary treatment variable was high-quality ANC services. Additionally, adequate ANC contact and adequate ANC component also examined as additional treatment variables.

The quality of ANC service was assessed using the quality dimensions defined by the Institute of Medicine (IOM) and the World Health Organization (WHO). These dimensions of health quality include effectiveness, safety, patient-centeredness, timeliness, efficiency, and equity [31, 32]. The PMA data collects information on the dimensions of effectiveness and timeliness; therefore, the study utilized these dimensions to assess the quality of ANC services. Effectiveness involves delivering care based on scientific knowledge and evidence-based practices, including conducting appropriate patient assessments and examinations, performing diagnostic tests, and implementing suitable curative and preventive measures [31, 32]. Timeliness refers to providing healthcare services promptly, minimizing unnecessary delays, and ensuring that patients receive timely care without undue waiting periods [31, 32]. In this study effectiveness was measured by component of ANC services: appropriate maternal assessment and diagnostic test (blood pressure measured, weight measured, blood sample taken, urine sample taken, stool sample taken), screening services (HIV test, syphilis test), appropriate curative and preventive ANC service (two doses of tetanus vaccination, iron supplementation, deworming for intestinal parasite), counseling services (discussed on birth preparedness and complication readiness, discussed on nutrition or diet), ANC contact (at least four ANC contact), and provider type (ANC service provided by skilled provider at least once). Timeliness was measured by early initiation of ANC services (initiation of ANC services within the first three months) [32–35].

A comprehensive index of ANC service quality was developed by combining 15 items from effectiveness and timeliness dimensions. Each item was given an equal weight of “1,“, resulting in scores ranging from 0 to 15. A score of 0 indicated that a woman did not receive any items, while a maximum score of 15 signified the receipt of all available services. After computing the score, each woman was assigned a percentile rank. Women scoring at or above the 75th percentile (receiving ≥ 10 items) were categorized as having received high-quality ANC services, whereas those below the 75th percentile (receiving < 10 items) were classified as having received poor-quality ANC services [18, 36, 37]. To ensure comparability with other studies, we dichotomized the ANC index score using the 75th percentile as the cutoff point [18, 36, 37].

Although both ANC contact (number of visits) and ANC components (service content) are integral parts of overall ANC quality, we conducted separate analyses to examine their individual effects on skilled birth attendant utilization for two reasons. First, since our ANC quality index combines multiple indicators, using a domain-specific approach to analyze these elements helps identify which specific aspects have the strongest impact. Second, this disaggregated analysis provides more targeted evidence for policymakers, revealing whether expanding service coverage (increasing visits), improving service content, or enhancing overall quality would be most effective in increasing skilled delivery attendance. Accordingly, the ANC components was assessed using twelve key service indicators: blood pressure measured, weight measured, blood sample taken, urine sample taken, stool sample taken, HIV test, syphilis test, two doses of tetanus vaccination, iron supplementation, deworming for intestinal parasite, discussed on birth preparedness and complication readiness, and discussed on nutrition. Women who had received more than two doses of the TT vaccine, based on the number of doses and the estimated duration of protection, in previous pregnancies or earlier in life were considered as having received TT vaccination, as their newborns would already be protected against neonatal tetanus. Each item was assigned an equal weight of “1,”, yielding a composite score ranging from 0 to 12. Women scoring at or above the 75th percentile (≥ 8 components) were classified as having received adequate ANC components. ANC contact was measured based on the number of visits. Women who received four or more ANC contacts were considered to have adequate contact, while those with fewer than four were categorized as having inadequate contact. The internal reliability of the items measuring the quality of ANC and the adequate ANC components was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which was found to be 0.76, and 0.72, respectively, both exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 0.7 [38].

Matching variables

The covariates for this study included maternal education level (no education, primary education, secondary education, higher education), wealth index (quintile 1 (poorest), quintile 2, quintile 3, quintile 4, quintile 5 (richest)), family size (< 5, ≥ 5), parity (0, 1–2, 3–4, 5+), intendedness of pregnancy (intended, unintended), partner encouragement to attend ANC (yes, no), history of facility delivery (yes, no), and place of residence (rural, urban), and region (Addis Ababa, Amhara, Oromia, and SNNP). The wealth index was constructed from a number of indicators that are thought to be correlated with a household’s economic status. Component indicators include, for example, possession of household assets (electricity, television, radio, watch, telephone, refrigerator), types of vehicles, water supply and sanitation (the source of drinking water, type of toilet, sharing of toilet facilities), housing condition (material of the principal floor, walls, roof), and ownership of agricultural land, as well as the type and number of animals owned [39, 40]. Principal component analysis was used to construct the wealth index of the household [40].

Statistical analysis

To establish rigorous causal estimates, we employed multiple approaches to assess the effect of high-quality ANC, adequate ANC contact, and adequate ANC components on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. Our primary analysis used PSM with cluster-robust standard errors applied at the enumeration area (EA) level to account for intra-cluster correlation, followed by adjusted regression analysis on the matched samples to control for remaining observed confounders. Propensity scores are an advanced method for matching, designed to balance measured covariates between treatment and comparison groups [23].

Combining matching methods like PSM with regression adjustment further refines causal estimates. After matching, regression analysis can adjust for any residual differences in covariates, increasing the efficiency and accuracy of treatment effect estimation [41–44]. Additionally, a supplementary multivariable logistic regression analysis based on the full sample was conducted to estimate the adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and marginal effects of each treatment variable on skilled birth attendant utilization.

The propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model. The “pscore” command in Stata was applied to calculate the estimated propensity score. The region of common support ranged from 0.076 to 0.852 for quality ANC, 0.154 to 0.918 for adequate of ANC contact, and 0.058 to 0.541 for adequate of ANC components, confirming that the common support assumption was satisfied. The Stata “psmatch2” command was employed to determine the Average Treatment Effect for the treated group (ATT). Several matching algorithms were explored to identify the best approach for balancing baseline characteristics between the treatment and control groups. Theses includes nearest neighbor matching, radius matching, and kernel matching. Ultimately, radius matching without replacement was chosen as it produced the highest quality matches and best ATT estimates (Table 8). A caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of propensity scores (0.1 in this study) was used in the radius matching process. This parameter was selected based on established evidence supporting its effectiveness in producing robust and reliable matching results [45–47].

Balancing tests were conducted using the “pstest” command in Stata. This test evaluated the standardized percentage bias for each covariate included in the propensity score estimation model, both before and after matching. A threshold of less than ± 10% for standardized percentage bias is widely accepted in medical literature [45, 48]. Additionally, t-tests were used to compare the distribution of each covariate between the treatment and control groups. Overall covariate imbalances before and after matching were analyzed using the pseudo-R² statistic. A pseudo-R² value below 0.05 after matching suggests no significant systematic differences in covariate distributions between the groups [49, 50]. While adjusting for covariates and propensity scores can address observed differences between treatment and control group, unobserved differences may still persist. These, unmeasured difference can introduce hidden bias, affecting both treatment assignment and the outcome of interest [51, 52]. To evaluate the robustness of estimates against such hidden bias, a sensitivity analysis is recommended. Since the outcome variable (utilization of skilled birth attendants) was binary, the Mantel-Haenszel (MH) test was applied using the “mhbounds” command in Stata [52], with gamma values ranging from 1 to 2 in increments of 0.05.

In the adjusted regression, we used the “teffects ra” command in Stata to estimate the adjusted ATT. The model controlled for confounders, including education, household wealth index, family size, parity, facility delivery history, pregnancy intention, partner encouragement to attend ANC, place of residence, and region. Stata version 17 statistical software was used for coding and data analysis (Fig. 2).

The mean propensity score was 0.46 (SD = 0.19) for quality ANC, 0.6 (SD = 0.19) for adequate of ANC contact, and 0.22 (SD = 0.12) for adequate of ANC components, indicating moderate variability between the treatment and the control groups.

After accounting for residual confounding in the matched sample, the adjusted average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) indicated that high-quality ANC was associated with a 5.7 percentage point increase in the likelihood of utilizing skilled birth attendants (adjusted ATT = 0.0575; 95% CI 0.024–0.090; p = 0.001). The logistic regression analysis on the full sample further supported this association, with the average marginal effect (AME) showing a 7.1 percentage point increase in the probability of skilled birth attendance (AME = 0.0715; 95% CI 0.014–0.128; p = 0.014). For ANC contact, women who received four or more ANC contacts were significantly more likely to utilize skilled birth attendance. The PSM analyses with adjusted regression estimated a 6.0, percentage point increase in skilled birth attendant utilization (adjusted ATT = 0.0605; 95% CI 0.028–0.092; p < 0.001). This result was consistent with the logistic regression analysis, where the AME showed a nearly identical 6.1 percentage point increase in the probability of skilled birth attendance (AME = 0.0606; 95% CI 0.010–0.110; p = 0.018). Similarly, receiving adequate ANC components was also significantly associated with increased utilization of skilled birth attendants. In the matched sample analysis, the adjusted ATT indicated a 5.7 percentage point increase in skilled birth attendance (adjusted ATT = 0.0574; 95% CI 0.024–0.090; p = 0.001). This association was further supported by the logistic regression model, where the AME showed a 9.2 percentage point increase in the likelihood of skilled birth attendance (AME = 0.0921; 95% CI 0.004–0.180; p = 0.040) (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This study primarily assessed the effect of high-quality ANC on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. Additionally, the effects of adequate ANC contacts and the receipt of adequate ANC components on skilled birth attendance were examined. PSM followed by adjusted regression provides a robust analytical approach for causal inference in observational studies, effectively minimizing selection bias and enhancing the comparability of treatment and control groups [53].

The study found that the coverage of skilled birth attendants was 67% which falls below the targets set by the Ethiopia Second Health Sector Transformation Plan (HSTP II) and the WHO targets [4, 11]. However, this result was higher than the coverage reported in the 2019 MEDHS report (50%) [10], the Cohort one PMA Ethiopia report (54.5%) [9], and the 2023 National Health Equity Survey in Ethiopia (59%) [8]. The variation may be due to differences in study periods, with improvements in skilled birth attendant utilization over time driven by awareness campaigns, better maternal healthcare access, increased government interventions aimed at improving skilled birth attendant utilization, and achieving the goals of the HSTP. Additionally, the current study did not include the pastoralist regions, where skilled birth attendant coverage is expected to be lower compared to the agrarian regions and Addis Ababa City. This exclusion may contribute to the differences observed when compared with the findings from other studies, such as the 2019 MEDHS and the 2023 National Health Equity Survey. Moreover, the present study was conducted among women who had attended at least one ANC visit, which likely contributed to the higher coverage of skilled birth attendants.

Findings from both the PSM and adjusted regression analyses revealed a positive association between high-quality ANC services and the utilization of skilled birth attendants. The PSM analysis estimated an unadjusted ATT of 5.2 percentage points, while the adjusted regression analysis produced a slightly higher adjusted ATT of 5.7 percentage points. These consistent estimates underscore the robustness of the association. Additionally, the AOR of 1.07 and the AME of 7.1 percentage points further supports the causal relationship between high-quality ANC and the increased use of skilled birth attendants. Specifically, the adjusted ATT indicates that utilization of skilled birth attendants was 5.7 percentage points higher among women who received high-quality ANC compared to those who received poor-quality services. Similarly, the AME suggests that, on average, receiving high-quality ANC increases the probability of skilled birth attendant use by 7.1 percentage points.

This finding was consistent with previous studies, which founded those women who received high-quality ANC services were more likely to utilize skilled birth attendants compared to those who received poor-quality ANC [15, 18, 54]. This may be attributed to the fact that appropriate maternal assessments, diagnostic tests, and screening services are critical for identifying pregnancy-related obstetric complications [14]. Early detection of these complications encourages pregnant women to seek delivery in health facilities with skilled birth attendants and facilitates timely referral to higher-level care when needed [14, 55]. Additionally, women who receive high-quality ANC are more likely to be adequately prepared for birth and potential complications. Comprehensive counseling during ANC encourages engagement in birth preparedness activities, such as identifying a skilled provider, saving money for delivery-related expenses, and arranging emergency transportation or care [14, 54, 56]. These activities significantly increase the likelihood of planning for and choosing skilled delivery. Moreover, women who receive high-quality ANC tend to be more satisfied with their care, fostering trust in the healthcare system. This satisfaction increases the likelihood of continued use of essential maternal health services, such as skilled birth attendants and postnatal care, leading to better outcomes for both mothers and newborns [18, 57]. On the other hand, studies indicate that dropout from skilled birth attendance is more likely when mothers receive poor-quality care during their ANC visits [16, 17]. Therefore, the quality of ANC care is essential in influencing the use of skilled birth attendants, facility-based deliveries, postnatal care, and ensuring overall patient satisfaction with maternal health services [18, 58, 59]. However, the quality of ANC services for women in Ethiopia remains critically low, with coverage reported to range between 10.5% and 39% in various studies [13, 26, 36, 37, 60–62]. In general, it can be inferred that high-quality ANC services had a significant positive effect on improving skilled birth attendant utilization.

We also assessed the impact of adequate ANC contact and the receipt of adequate component of ANC services on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. Our findings consistently revealed a positive association between receiving adequate ANC components and the use of skilled birth attendants, although the magnitude of the effect varied across analytical approaches. The unadjusted and adjusted value for adequate ANC component were 9.4 and 5.7 percentage points, respectively. The AOR was 1. 98, while the AME was 9.2 percentage points. All methods consistently demonstrated that receiving adequate ANC components significantly increase the likelihood of utilizing skilled birth attendants. For example, the adjusted ATT indicates a 5.7 percentage point increase in skilled birth attendant utilization attributable to receiving adequate ANC components, suggesting the causal effect among those who actually received the adequate components, while the AME indicates an average increase of 9.2 percentage points in the probability of skilled birth attendant utilization across the full sample (entire population), associated with receiving adequate ANC components.

Similarly, adequate ANC contact also demonstrated a positive effect on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. The adjusted ATT and AME yielded consistent results, each estimating an increase of approximately 6 percentage points in skilled birth attendance among women who had adequate ANC contacts. In comparison, the unadjusted ATT was slightly higher at 8.2percentage points, suggesting a modest overestimation before adjusting for confounders. These findings indicate that adequate ANC contact significantly enhances the likelihood of skilled birth attendance. The adjusted ATT shows that women with adequate ANC contacts were 6.0 percentage points more likely to utilize skilled birth attendants during delivery than those with inadequate contact. This result is further supported by the AME, which also revealed a 6.0 percentage point increase in the probability of skilled birth attendant use, reinforcing the importance of adequate ANC contacts in promoting skilled birth attendants. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in sub-Saharan African countries [59, 63], Uganda [64] and Bangladesh [22], which also reported a positive association between adequate ANC contact and the receipt of essential ANC components with the utilization of skilled birth attendants. This association may be explained by the fact that attending at least four visits offers substantial benefits to pregnant women. It improves their understanding of the importance of skilled birth attendance and institutional delivery, familiarizes them with the healthcare system, and builds confidence and positive relationships with healthcare providers [57, 65]. Furthermore, ANC serves as a platform for pregnant women to build informal support networks, allowing them to share experiences and information about pregnancy and the advantages of delivering in health facilities [65].

Therefore, policymakers, healthcare providers, and other responsible stakeholders need to focus on increasing the number of ANC contacts, ensuring the provision of all WHO-recommended ANC components, and enhancing the overall quality of antenatal care services. These strategies are essential for improving the utilization of skilled birth attendants and advancing maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Strength and limitation

We employed combined analytical approaches to establish rigorous causal estimates of treatment variables on utilization of skilled birth attendants. Additionally, the data was collected postpartum at six weeks so that it reduces recall bias. Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations: First, while propensity score matching address bias from observable characteristics, it does not account for unmeasured confounders, potentially leading to an overestimation of the effect of high-quality ANC on the utilization of skilled birth attendants. Second, the majority of the mothers in the study had no formal education or had only completed primary education, which might have hindered their comprehension of certain questions related to the treatment variable. Lastly, the assessment of treatment variables relied on self-reported information, making it susceptible to both social desirability bias and recall bias.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that high-quality ANC services increase the likelihood of skilled birth attendant utilization during delivery. It also demonstrates a positive association between adequate ANC contacts, the receipt of adequate ANC components, and the use of skilled birth attendants. Therefore, our findings underscore that increasing the number of ANC contacts, ensuring the provision of recommended ANC components, and promoting overall high-quality ANC are critical strategies for improving skilled birth attendant utilization.
