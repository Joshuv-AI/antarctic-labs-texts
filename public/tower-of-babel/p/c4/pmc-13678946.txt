Pathological microenvironment-responsive nano-glycospheres restore disc homeostasis by combinatorial metabolic and genetic regulation
Authors: Lei Zhao, Wenzheng Ma, Wantao Wang, Dan Zhou, Jinghao Fan, Mingkang Liu, Lei Liu, Lin Huang, Xue Wei, Zhaomin Zheng, Hongmei Liu, Decheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is fundamentally driven by a self-perpetuating pathological loop involving metabolic derangement, oxidative stress, and enzymatic hyper-activity. Conventional mono-therapies often fall short of reversing this complex microenvironmental collapse. Here, we developed a smart nano-glycosphere (siMMP13/2-DG NPs) designed for dual-track intervention to restore disc homeostasis. This responsive platform utilized a 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) shell crosslinked via reactive oxygen-sensitive phenylboronic acid moieties, enabling the simultaneous suppression of pathological glycolysis and the neutralization of oxidative stress. Concomitantly, the precise delivery of siMMP13 effectively silenced major catabolic enzymes, thereby arresting extracellular matrix degradation. Our findings demonstrated that this combinatorial approach effectively interrupted the lactate-driven inflammatory cascade, shifting the microenvironment from a hostile catabolic state toward a pro-regenerative metabolic balance. In the lumbar disc degeneration model, the siMMP13/2-DG NPs significantly preserved structural integrity and alleviated discogenic pain by dampening neuro-inflammatory signaling. Introduction

Intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration (IVDD) is a major cause of chronic low back pain (LBP), significantly impacting the quality of life and imposing a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems [1–3]. The pathogenesis of IVDD involves a complex interplay of biological, mechanical, and biochemical factors [4–7]. Among these, the collapse of the microenvironmental homeostasis within the IVD plays a critical role in accelerating IVDD. This pathological state is characterized by a complex interplay between metabolic derangement (notably excessive lactic acid accumulation) and the surge of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which collectively orchestrate a pro-inflammatory and catabolic milieu. Such environmental stress triggers the chronic upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP13, ultimately culminating in the profound degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [8–10]. Effectively regulating these factors is key to treating and slowing down the degenerative process of IVD. Nevertheless, current monotherapies fail to disrupt this multifaceted pathological feedback loop. Therefore, effective modulation of the IVD microenvironment to restore balance and halt or reverse the degenerative process remains a significant challenge in the field of regenerative medicine.

As a competitive glucose analog, 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) effectively bottlenecks glycolysis and curtails lactate buildup [11]. More importantly, 2-DG orchestrates a sophisticated cellular response via the AMP activated protein kinase (AMPK)-silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1) axis [12–15]. Specifically, this response dampens inflammatory output by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) phosphorylation [16, 17] and enhances mitochondrial health through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α) nuclear translocation, ultimately scavenging intracellular ROS [15, 17–19]. Based on these multifaceted properties, we hypothesized that 2-DG could effectively modulate the degenerative microenvironment of IVDD. However, this potential remains untapped in IVDD research. Despite its favorable safety profile in Phase I/II trials as a metabolic modulator, the therapeutic efficacy of 2-DG remains limited by its unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties. Specifically, its rapid in vivo degradation and metabolic turnover necessitate advanced delivery strategies to achieve sustained efficacy at the pathological site [20–22].

To bridge this therapeutic gap, we designed an integrated nano-glycosphere (siMMP13/2-DG NPs) characterized by a ROS-responsive metabolic-genetic co-delivery architecture (Scheme 1). By leveraging the dynamic covalent chemistry of 2-formylphenylboronic acid (2-FPBA), we synthesized a 2-DG-based shell that serves as both a therapeutic agent and a responsive gatekeeper for siMMP13 [23, 24].This design enables precise and localized release in response to the high-ROS microenvironment of the IVD, simultaneously dampening the lactate-driven inflammatory cascade and arresting enzymatic ECM degradation. Using a rat model of anterior lumbar annulus fibrosus (AF) puncture, our study demonstrated that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs remodeled the disc degenerative microenvironment, maintained disc integrity, and alleviated axial pressure-related pain behaviors. The siMMP13/2-DG NPs show promising potential for mitigating IVDD and offer a multifaceted approach to treating this debilitating condition.

Results

Preparation and characterization of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

To fabricate the siMMP13/2-DG NPs, we synthesized polyethyleneimine/2-formylphenylboronic acid/2-Deoxy-D-glucose prodrug polymers (PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG) using a one-pot method (Fig. 1a). The synthetic pathway of the PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG was shown in Fig. S1. Successful synthesis of PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG was confirmed by 1H NMR and Alizarin Red S (ARS) complexation assay, as shown in Figs. S2 and S3. Subsequently, PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG was complexed with siMMP13 to form pathological microenvironment-responsive siMMP13/2-DG NPs via electrostatic interactions between the positive and negative charges (Fig. 1a). Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG efficiently loaded siRNA at an N/P ratio to be more than 30 (Fig. S4a). Based on these findings, an N/P ratio of 40 was selected for subsequent experiments. This selection is further supported by quantitative analyses showing high siRNA encapsulation efficiency (93.0 ± 3.6%) at this ratio, together with a pH-responsive release profile confirming efficient and stimulus-dependent siRNA release from the nano-glycospheres (Fig. S4b, c). To mimic the microenvironment of IVDD, we employed 100 µM tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) to induce a high ROS environment with low cytotoxicity (Fig. S5) [25, 26]. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images revealed that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs were predominantly spherical with a core-shell structure and showed good monodispersity (Fig. 1b). Under TBHP conditions, the siMMP13/2-DG NPs were observed to disassemble their 2-DG shell, leaving behind siMMP13/PEI/2-FPBA nanoparticles with a structure similar to PEI/siMMP13 (Figs. 1c and S6). The hydrated particle sizes and Zeta potential of siMMP13/2-DG NPs were 137.0 ± 4.3 nm (PDI = 0.112 ± 0.017) and 18.7 ± 1.0 mV, respectively (Fig. 1d). Under TBHP conditions, the particle size distribution of siMMP13/2-DG NPs became markedly heterogeneous, indicating that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs were in a disintegrated state (Fig. 1e). The 2-DG encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity of siMMP13/2-DG NPs were 70.0 ± 0.5% and 6.4 ± 0.1%, respectively (Fig. S7). The stability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was evaluated. The findings indicated that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs had good stability, with no obvious alteration in hydrodynamic diameter over a 7-day period (Fig. 1f). Further, drug release from siMMP13/2-DG NPs was evaluated using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) under different microenvironmental conditions. As depicted in Fig. 1g, more than 58.7 ± 4.3% of 2-DG was released within 20 hours in the presence of TBHP, while release remained below 10% in PBS (pH 7.4). The ROS scavenging ability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs was also investigated using free radical scavenging assays with 2,2’-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH). The results indicated that the ROS scavenging capacities of siMMP13/2-DG NPs reached 63.1 ± 1.6% (ABTS method) and 76.4 ± 0.5% (DPPH method), respectively, suggesting that the cleavage of the borate bond contributed to ROS depletion (Figs. 1h, i and S8). These findings collectively confirmed the successful fabrication of pathological microenvironment-responsive siMMP13/2-DG NPs.

MMP13 silencing ability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

We next assessed the efficiency of MMP13 silencing in nucleus pulposus (NP) cells (NPCs) isolated from rat IVD (Fig. 2a). For effective knockdown of MMP13 gene expression in the cytoplasm, it is crucial that siMMP13/2-DG NPs are efficiently internalized by the cells and capable of escaping into the cytoplasm. To evaluate the cellular uptake and endosomal escape of siMMP13/2-DG NPs, Cy5-siRNA was complexed with the PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG to form Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs. As shown in Fig. 2b, Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs exhibited significantly enhanced uptake into NPCs compared with free siRNA. Notably, Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs exhibited enhanced cellular uptake under TBHP conditions compared with normal conditions, likely due to the exposure to boric acid, which facilitates anchoring to sialic acid and enhances cellular internalization of the cargo [27, 28]. We further questioned the reliability of this potentiation process in NPCs by designing and preparing nanoparticles (Cy5-siNC NPs (PEI), and Cy5-siNC NPs (PEI/2-FPBA)) constructed from PEI or PEI/2-FPBA. The results showed that NPCs exhibited stronger cellular uptake of Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs and Cy5-siNC NPs (PEI/2-FPBA), implying that 2-FPBA had a decisive role in promoting cellular internalization (Fig. S9). We subsequently evaluated the endosomal escape capability of Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs. As illustrated in Figs. 2c and S10, the separation between the green fluorescence (LysoTracker Green) and red fluorescence (Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs) was more pronounced, indicating that siMMP13/2-DG NPs successfully escaped from endosomes or early lysosomes into the cytoplasm after 6 h of incubation. These observations suggested that siMMP13 was capable of escaping from lysosomes into the cytoplasm.

To further validate the efficient endosomal or lysosomal escape of siMMP13 and its subsequent gene knockdown capability, we quantified the downregulation of MMP13 mRNA levels in siMMP13-treated cells using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blotting (WB) analysis. Compared with the control group (normal NPCs), TBHP-treated NPCs exhibited a significant increase in MMP13 mRNA expression (181.0 ± 3.2%) (Fig. 2d). Notably, siMMP13 NPs (PEI/2-FPBA/siMMP13 without 2-DG) significantly silenced MMP13 gene expression in TBHP-treated NPCs, resulting in approximately 54.2 ± 0.8% knockdown of MMP13 mRNA. In contrast, the free siRNA group in TBHP-treated NPCs demonstrated no knockdown efficiency. Additionally, MMP13 mRNA expression was moderately reduced in cells treated with siNC NPs (PEI/2-FPBA/nonsense siRNA without 2-DG), potentially due to the scavenging effect of boric acid molecules on ROS in the microenvironment, which may mitigate the TBHP-induced degenerative conditions. WB and immunofluorescence analyses further confirmed that treatment with siMMP13 NPs achieved the highest knockdown efficiency of MMP13, consistent with the MMP13 mRNA expression profiles measured by RT-qPCR (Figs. 2e-g and S11). Compared with both TBHP and siNC/2-DG NPs treatments, siMMP13/2-DG NPs exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory effect on MMP13 expression as well (Fig. S12). Additionally, treatment with siNC/2-DG NPs moderately reduced MMP13 protein expression, an effect likely arising from 2-DG-mediated modulation of the cellular microenvironment and the ROS-scavenging activity of boric acid ester bonds, which act in concert to preserve the normal phenotype of NPCs and attenuate MMP13 expression. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs system exerted an enhanced effect by combining the microenvironment-modulating properties of 2-DG with the targeted silencing capability of siMMP13, thereby achieving potent suppression of MMP13 expression.

AMPK activation ability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

2-DG is known to activate AMPK in chondrocytes, contributing to the treatment of osteoarthritis by regulating cellular energy balance and potentially reducing inflammation and cartilage degradation [29]. Since NPCs share certain similarities with chondrocytes, this prompted us to investigate whether 2-DG could similarly activate AMPK in NPCs to mitigate disc degeneration, a mechanism that has not previously been reported (Fig. 3a) [30–32]. In the present study, we confirmed by immunofluorescence that NPCs normally express AMPK proteins, while 2-DG dramatically increased their phosphorylation at Thr172 (Figs. S13 and 3b-d). As shown in Fig. 3d, the phosphorylation of AMPK (p-AMPK) was significantly increased to 119.4 ± 5.9% and 103.7 ± 9.7% with siMMP13/2-DG NPs and 2-DG treatment, respectively, while the TBHP-treated group decreased to 44.6 ± 10.8%, indicating that 2-DG and siMMP13/2-DG NPs could effectively mediate the activation of AMPK. Additionally, the phosphorylation signal intensity of AMPK in the siMMP13 NPs group (74.4 ± 2.9%) was elevated relative to the TBHP-treated group, likely due to the depletion of the TBHP effect by boric acid. Meanwhile, our RT-qPCR and WB assays revealed that both siMMP13/2-DG NPs and 2-DG promoted the expression of SIRT1, which was otherwise suppressed by TBHP treatment (Figs. S14 and 3e, g).

We next investigated whether AMPK regulation of SIRT1 could influence the expression and phosphorylation of its downstream target, NF-κB. To test this hypothesis, WB and RT-qPCR were used to assess the phosphorylation of NF-κB (p-NF-κB) and the expression of its downstream inflammatory factors, respectively (Fig. 3e, i-k). Compared with TBHP-treated NPCs, the siMMP13/2-DG treatment group demonstrated the most pronounced suppression of NF-κB activation, achieving a non-inflammatory state comparable to the untreated control (Fig. 3e, i). As shown in Fig. 3j, k, treatment with siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively reduced the expression of Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β, 87.1 ± 41.9%) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, 105.2 ± 27.2%) compared to the TBHP-treated group (IL-1β increased to 328.0 ± 68.9%; TNF-α increased to 136.6 ± 29.0%). This reduction was further corroborated by immunofluorescence analysis (Figs. 3l and S15, S16). Furthermore, we verified that the therapeutic effect of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in degenerative microenvironments was consistent with those observed following treatment with AICAR (an AMP analog known to activate AMPK). This was demonstrated by the increase in p-AMPK expression and the suppression of inflammation-associated factors (Fig. S17). Notably, upon pretreatment with Compound C (an ATP-competitive AMPK inhibitor), siMMP13/2-DG NPs could no longer suppress the expression of IL-1β and TNF-α. Collectively, these findings suggested that the anti-inflammatory effects of siMMP13/2-DG NPs were largely mediated through activation of the AMPK pathway.

Furthermore, the AMPK pathway is known to regulate mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism homeostasis by upregulating the expression of SIRT1, which in turn promotes the expression and nuclear translocation of PGC-1α. Our WB and immunofluorescence experiments demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs and 2-DG significantly enhanced PGC-1α expression and its nuclear translocation, indicating that 2-DG was able to activate PGC-1α via SIRT1 regulation (Fig. 3e, h, m). The PGC-1α-dependent mitochondrial protective effect was further validated by assessing mitochondrial membrane potential and intracellular ROS levels. The red/green fluorescence ratio of JC-1 staining in the mitochondrial membrane potential assay was negatively correlated with mitochondrial depolarization. Compared to the TBHP-treated group, siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment increased the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio (Figs. 3n and S18), suggesting that siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively mitigated mitochondrial damage caused by a high ROS environment. Similarly, the reduced DCFH-DA fluorescence signal following siNC/2-DG NPs treatment confirmed their protective effect on NPCs mitochondria and their ability to lower intracellular ROS production (Fig. S19). Taken together, these results indicated that the anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial protective effects driven by the AMPK/SIRT1 axis can be activated by 2-DG delivered via siMMP13/2-DG NPs.

To further evaluate the translational safety of the proposed nano-glycosphere system, we conducted comprehensive systemic biosafety assessments following intradiscal administration. During the experimental period, hematological analysis and serum biochemical indices, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine (CR), remained within normal physiological ranges (Fig. S20a, b). Histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no detectable pathological abnormalities at both 4-week and 8-week time points (Fig. S20c). These findings collectively suggest that the localized intradiscal delivery of siMMP13/2-DG NPs does not induce observable systemic toxicity under the current dosing regimen.

Activation of the AMPK signaling pathway in the lumbar disc

Given the potent activation of the AMPK signaling pathway by siMMP13/2-DG NPs in vitro, we next investigated whether comparable effects could be achieved in vivo within the NP tissue. To minimize confounding effects arising from advanced structural and phenotypic changes typical of late-stage IVDD, the NP tissue was collected at the 2nd week post-anterior AF puncture in rats, representing an early-stage degeneration phenotype (Fig. 4a). Consistent with previous studies demonstrating that needle puncture recapitulates the pathological process of IVDD; immunofluorescence analysis and in vivo imaging system results further confirmed the effective and uniform distribution of siMMP13/2-DG NPs following in situ injection, rather than leaking through the incision site and being subsequently metabolized (Fig. S20d-f) [33–37]. Compared with the Puncture group, treatment with siMMP13/2-DG NPs, siMMP13 NPs, or siNC/2-DG NPs all increased NP water content, with siMMP13/2-DG NPs showing the most pronounced therapeutic benefit (Fig. 4b-d). Histological analysis revealed preserved NP tissue architecture across all treatment groups, enabling subsequent staining assays (Fig. 4e). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs markedly suppressed puncture-induced MMP13 secretion, exerting stronger therapeutic effects than either siMMP13 NPs or siNC/2-DG NPs (Fig. 4e, g). Notably, MMP13 expression was lower in the siNC/2-DG NPs group than in the siMMP13 NPs group, suggesting that 2-DG-mediated microenvironmental remodeling contributes to preserving the normal phenotype of the NP tissue (Fig. 4g). This observation also supports the notion that siRNA-mediated knockdown is temporally restricted, consistent with previous reports indicating that multiple administrations are required to sustain therapeutic effects [38–40]. Together, these findings demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively remodeled the NP microenvironment and delayed IVDD progression through the complementary actions of siRNA-mediated gene silencing and 2-DG-based metabolic regulation.

We next assessed the activation status of the AMPK signaling pathway and its downstream effectors in NP tissue following treatment (Fig. 4e). Compared with the Puncture group, both siMMP13/2-DG NPs and siNC/2-DG NPs markedly increased AMPK expression and phosphorylation, confirming that 2-DG exerts a strong activating effect on AMPK signaling in vivo. The degree of AMPK activation was greater in the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group than in the siNC/2-DG NPs group, suggesting that progressive degeneration in the absence of MMP13 silencing may attenuate AMPK pathway activity. Consistently, siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment induced the strongest positive signals for SIRT1 and PGC-1α, indicating robust activation of the AMPK-SIRT1-PGC-1α axis. We further examined inflammatory signaling in the NP tissue. As shown in Fig. 4e, both siMMP13/2-DG NPs and siNC/2-DG NPs significantly suppressed puncture-induced activation of NF-κB and p-NF-κB, yielding expression profiles comparable to those observed in the Sham group. These results underscored the anti-inflammatory potential of the 2-DG-based nano-glycospheres system. In summary, siMMP13/2-DG NPs protected against early IVDD by concurrently mitigating ECM degradation, activating the AMPK-SIRT1-PGC-1α axis, and suppressing NF-κB-driven inflammation, highlighting the therapeutic potential conferred by the combinatorial design.

Effect of siMMP13/2-DG NPs on ameliorating lumbar disc degeneration

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in mitigating the progression of IVDD in vivo, the long-term anterior lumbar AF puncture model was established in rats. Radiography and histological tests were performed at the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks to evaluate the effect of siMMP13/2-DG NPs on IVDD progression (Fig. 5a). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were first performed to evaluate the water content in the IVD. As shown in Fig. 5b, c, the T2-weighted signal intensity in the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group was the highest among the Puncture, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs groups, similar to the Sham group. Pfirrmann grade, a semi-quantitative visual classification system, is currently recognized as a classification method for diagnosing the degree of IVDD [41]. The Pfirrmann score was mainly Ⅰ and Ⅱ in the Sham and siMMP13/2-DG NPs groups. In contrast, the other three groups exhibited significantly higher grades at the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks (Fig. 5d).

Histological analysis was conducted to examine the microstructure of the IVD. In the Sham and siMMP13/2-DG NPs groups, the IVD exhibited normal structure, with intact NP tissue and a clear boundary with the AF (Fig. S21). Although the IVD in the Puncture, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs groups showed substantial collagen and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) deposition, the IVD in the Puncture and siMMP13 NPs groups were notably disorganized (Figs. 5e-g and S22). Interestingly, the IVD in the siNC/2-DG NPs group remained relatively structurally intact, suggesting that 2-DG may play an essential role in decelerating disc degeneration. The histological scores of the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group were significantly lower than those of the Puncture, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs groups, and were comparable to those of the Sham group at both the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks (Fig. 5h). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively slowed down the progression of long-term IVDD.

To assess the functional consequences of this protective effect, we next evaluated the biomechanics of caudal vertebral motion segments using uniaxial compression and tensile testing (Fig. S23). Puncture-induced IVD damage led to a marked increase in the compressive elastic modulus, consistent with features of late-stage NP fibrosis and disruption of physiological load distribution [42]. In contrast, treatment with siMMP13/2-DG NPs preserved the native compressive viscoelasticity, with elastic moduli remaining comparable to those in the Sham group (Fig. S23b, c, f). At the 4th postoperative week, tensile testing revealed no significant differences in tensile modulus between any treatment group and the Sham group, indicating that needle puncture induced localized NP degradation without compromising the lamellar collagen structure or mechanical toughness of the AF (Fig. S23d, e, g). By the 8th week, the tensile modulus of all treatment groups exceeded that of the non-punctured Sham group, while no differences were detected among the treatment groups themselves. These findings suggested that siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment primarily improves the NP microenvironment but has limited capacity to restore AF structure at later stages. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs attenuated disc fibrosis by restoring NP microenvironmental homeostasis, thereby maintaining the compression-dependent viscoelastic properties of the IVD.

Microenvironment amelioration and inflammation elimination ability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

To further evaluate the microenvironment-modulating capacity of siMMP13/2-DG NPs and their AMPK-mediated anti-inflammatory effects in vivo, IVD tissues were collected at the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks for analysis. Hexokinase 2 (HK2), the first critical rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, was used as a marker to assess glycolytic activity within the tissue [43]. As shown in Fig. S24a, the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group exhibited significantly lower HK2 expression compared to the other groups. Additionally, the glycolytic capacity of NP tissue and lactate production were assessed using phosphofructokinase platelet (PFKP), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA). The expression patterns of these enzymes across the groups were consistent with the HK2 results, suggesting that siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively altered the metabolic state of NP tissue and reduced lactate production (Fig. S24b-d). To further evaluate the functional consequences of glycolytic modulation, lactate production was quantified in NPCs under each drug conditions. Consistent with the expression profiles of glycolysis-associated enzymes, degenerative stimulation significantly increased lactate accumulation, whereas treatment with siMMP13/2-DG NPs and siNC/2-DG NPs markedly reduced lactate production (Fig. S25). These findings suggested that the observed alterations in glycolytic enzyme expression were accompanied by a functional reduction in glycolytic output.

To further explore the biological functions of siMMP13/2-DG NPs, we analyzed the immunohistochemistry results of the activation of the AMPK signaling pathway (Fig. S26). The immunohistochemistry data revealed that the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group had a higher p-AMPK/AMPK ratio and a lower p-NF-κB/NF-κB ratio compared to the Sham group, indicating that siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment activated the AMPK pathway while inhibiting NF-κB signaling in NP tissue (Fig. S26). We subsequently assessed the generation and accumulation of ROS within the NP tissue using the dihydroethidium probe and the oxidative stress markers (8-OHdG). As shown in Figs. S27 and S28, the fluorescence expression pattern of the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group was similar to the Sham group and much lower than that of the Puncture, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs groups, indicating the excellent ROS inhibition potential of the siMMP13/2-DG NPs in vivo. In summary, siMMP13/2-DG NPs reversed the degenerative microenvironment and alleviated IVDD by modifying the metabolic profile of the NP region, activating the AMPK pathway, and inhibiting inflammatory signaling.

Pain phenotype and mechanism of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in alleviating lumbar disc degeneration

Beyond the role of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in altering the microenvironment of the NP, we further investigated their potential to alleviate pain associated with IVDD (Fig. 6a). Notably, patient gender has been identified as a major and important influence on the course of IVDD and the patient’s ability to perceive secondary LBP. To further investigate the pain-relieving effects of siMMP13/2-DG NPs in IVDD, we analyzed the behavioral patterns of rats with different genders with respect to pain separately (Fig. 6). Following the administration of various treatments, we meticulously evaluated the LBP and hind limb pain behaviors in rats. Pressure algometry thresholds (PATs) were employed to measure the axial pressure-related pain changes (Fig. 6b, d) [44]. Baseline PATs measurements taken one week prior to surgery revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups. Postoperatively, all groups exhibited reduced PATs relative to the Sham group, confirming the successful induction of the axial pressure-related pain. We first evaluated pain-associated behavioral changes in male rats, during the first postoperative week, the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group demonstrated significantly higher PATs (599.4 ± 8.7 g) compared to the Puncture group (489.1 ± 31.0 g), siMMP13 NPs (516.1 ± 12.8 g), and siNC/2-DG NPs (521.5 ± 18.3 g). By the third postoperative week, PATs in the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group further increased, reaching 719.1 ± 59.7 g, and remained significantly higher than in the Puncture, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs groups. From the fourth postoperative week onward, PATs stabilized across all groups, indicating that siMMP13/2-DG NPs more effectively alleviated IVDD-associated pain sensitivity compared to other treatments. In addition, we observed a marked increase in PATs in female rats, suggesting potential sex-related differences in pain sensitivity (Fig. 6d). Nonetheless, siMMP13/2-DG NPs still demonstrated superior pain relief effects. As the primary behavioral endpoint, PATs demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment effectively alleviated axial pressure-related pain behaviors associated with IVDD progression. In contrast, the Von Frey test was included to assess potential hindlimb mechanical hypersensitivity resulting from nerve root involvement. Consistent with the anatomical characteristics of the anterior puncture model, no statistically significant differences in hindlimb mechanical sensitivity were observed among groups at any time point, supporting the absence of substantial nerve root compression in this model (Fig. 6c, e). In summary, gender factors can influence the pain behavioral patterns of rats to a certain extent; whereas the treatment of siMMP13/2-DG NPs ensured the integrity of the IVD by maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis, thereby attenuating IVDD-associated pain behaviors, and this pain relief did not depend on the modulation patterns associated with gender.

During IVDD, the ectopic invasion of nerve fibers into the internal regions of the IVD, accompanied by the accumulation of neurotrophic factors such as nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), triggers pain [4]. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that siMMP13/2-DG NPs significantly reduced the expression of NGF and BDNF compared to the Puncture group (Fig. 6f, g). Additionally, the siMMP13 NPs and siNC/2-DG NPs groups demonstrated a reduction in BDNF expression compared to the Puncture group; however, NGF expression remained unchanged, suggesting that these treatments were insufficient to fully suppress pain. Collectively, these findings indicated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs effectively inhibited the production and accumulation of neurotrophic factors, thereby alleviating LBP.

RNA-seq analysis following siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment

To elucidate the mechanism by which siMMP13/2-DG NPs facilitate IVDD remission, we conducted a cluster analysis of differentially expressed genes derived from RNA-seq data (Fig. 7a). NPCs treated with TBHP were designated as the TBHP group, revealing 97 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to the Sham group, including 74 upregulated genes closely associated with inflammation and immune regulation (e.g., Il-11, Nitric oxide synthase (Nos1), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (Cxcl2), among others), suggesting the successful construction of the degenerative model (Fig. 7b). We further analyzed the differentially expressed genes between the TBHP and siMMP13/2-DG NPs groups. As shown in Fig. 7c, the reduced expression of inflammatory factors (e.g., Il-11, Nos2, Cxcl2, and Cxcl3, among others) suggested that siMMP13/2-DG NPs exert their effects by modulating inflammation. Additionally, siMMP13/2-DG NPs treatment shifted the NPC phenotype closer to the Sham group (Figs. S29 and 7d). Molecular function enrichment of the Gene Ontology (GO) showed that the biological function changes were mainly concentrated in “Cytokine activity”, “Signaling receptor activity”, and “Chemokine activity” (Fig. 7e). Notably, alterations in “ATP-dependent activity” highlighted significant changes in NPC energy metabolism. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis showed significant differences not only in cell cycle-related pathways but also in inflammation-related pathways, such as IL-17 and TNF signaling, further supporting the strong anti-inflammatory effects of siMMP13/2-DG NPs (Fig. 7f). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) provided deeper insights into the regulatory roles of key genes. The results showed that extracellular matrix-related “Collagen trimer”, “Extracellular matrix structural constituent”, and chondroprotective “TGF-beta signaling pathway” were up-regulated in the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group compared with the TBHP group, implicating a matrix-preserving effect of siMMP13/2-DG NPs (Fig. 7g-i). Inflammatory signaling pathways, such as “IL-17 signaling pathway” and “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction”, were significantly down-regulated in the siMMP13/2-DG NPs group, confirming its excellent anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 7j, k). Interestingly, upregulation of “Negative regulation of glial cell differentiation” was observed, potentially explaining the mechanism by which siMMP13/2-DG NPs alleviate LBP (Fig. 7l). Collectively, these findings suggested that siMMP13/2-DG NPs adjusted NPCs energy metabolism and modulated inflammatory signaling to achieve therapeutic treatment in the degenerative microenvironment.

Discussion

The social and economic burden of IVDD-induced LBP cannot be ignored [1]. A key challenge in treating IVDD is the complex and multifactorial nature of the disease, which involves disruptions in the IVD microenvironment that exacerbate degeneration [2, 4]. Traditional therapeutic strategies have struggled to simultaneously target the various factors contributing to this imbalance, leading to suboptimal outcomes. In this study, we developed pathological microenvironment-responsive siMMP13/2-DG NPs to remodel the degenerative disc microenvironment and slow the progression of disc degeneration.

The use of 2-DG in our study offers a novel approach, as it addresses multiple pathogenic mechanisms simultaneously. By inhibiting glycolysis, 2-DG reduces lactic acid production, which is a major contributor to the acidic microenvironment that accelerates degeneration. Moreover, 2-DG activation of the AMPK pathway and subsequent promotion of SIRT1 expression attenuate NF-κB phosphorylation, thereby reducing inflammation. Additionally, 2-DG enhances PGC-1α activity, leading to improved mitochondrial function and reduced ROS production. Despite its therapeutic potential, the rapid metabolism of 2-DG in vivo poses a significant limitation to its clinical application [20–22]. Our development of siMMP13/2-DG NPs provides a solution to this challenge, allowing for sustained release and targeted delivery of 2-DG, thereby enhancing its efficacy. The incorporation of siMMP13 further augments the therapeutic effects by specifically targeting the upregulated MMP13, a key enzyme involved in ECM degradation and disc degeneration.

Our in vivo experiments using a rat model of anterior lumbar AF puncture demonstrated that siMMP13/2-DG NPs significantly reduced lactic acid levels, inhibited inflammatory cytokine secretion, decreased ROS production, and suppressed MMP13 activity. These effects collectively contributed to the preservation of disc integrity and the alleviation of axial pressure-associated pain behaviors. Crucially, the sustained 8-week efficacy of this single-dose treatment is fundamentally supported by the interactive behavior of the nanoparticles within the highly anionic, GAG-rich NP microenvironment. The positive surface charge of the nano-glycospheres initiates immediate, non-specific electrostatic tethering to the polyanionic matrix, while the surface-exposed 2-FPBA moieties form dynamic boric acid ester bonds with adjacent diols of matrix glycoproteins. This enhanced “electrostatic trapping and covalent anchoring” network effectively resists rapid clearance, while the outer shell sterically shields the inner PEI/siRNA core from macromolecular GAG displacement. Importantly, our results suggested that siMMP13/2-DG NPs not only slow down the degenerative process but may also promote the preservation of a healthier disc microenvironment, offering a potential strategy for the amelioration of IVDD.

Nevertheless, certain limitations of the current study warrant cautious consideration. While our in vitro mechanistic evaluations provided clear evidence for the therapeutic efficacy of siMMP13/2-DG NPs, these experiments were conducted exclusively using monolayer (2D)-cultured NPCs. The native IVD microenvironment presents a highly complex three-dimensional (3D) structural topography, wherein cell-matrix interactions and mechanical cues act as critical determinatives of NPCs phenotype and drug diffusion kinetics. The phenotypic drift or de-differentiation frequently observed in long-term 2D culture could potentially alter the baseline cellular response to metabolic modulators like 2-DG. Although the 2D monolayer design was strategically leveraged in this study to eliminate the confounding physical barriers of artificial hydrogels, thereby allowing precise quantification of nano-glycospheres internalization, lysosomal escape, and pure signaling cascades, it cannot completely recapitulate the physiological drug-delivery barriers. Future investigations utilizing 3D pellet cultures, biomimetic hydrogel encapsulation, or ex vivo organ explant models under dynamic mechanical loading will be imperative to more accurately simulate the translational performance of these nano-glycospheres.

A discrepancy exists between the significant MMP13 knockdown confirmed by RT-qPCR/WB and its absence of significant differential expression in the RNA-seq data from identical treatments. This inconsistency likely arises from intrinsic differences in assay sensitivity and biological context. Targeted techniques (RT-qPCR/WB) offer higher sensitivity for specific targets, whereas RNA-seq, constrained by background noise and stringent statistical thresholds for genome-wide data, may fail to detect modest but biologically relevant changes. Critically, the nano-glycospheres intervention might induce post-transcriptional regulation, potentially altering MMP13 mRNA stability or translational efficiency, thereby reducing protein levels without commensurate changes in transcript abundance. Additionally, investigating the applicability of this treatment in larger animal models will be crucial for translating these findings into a viable therapeutic option for patients with IVDD.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that siMMP13/2-DG NPs offer a promising multifaceted approach to treating IVDD. By targeting key elements of the degenerative microenvironment, this therapy has the potential to not only alleviate symptoms but also slow or even reverse the progression of IVDD, providing a novel and effective treatment option for this debilitating.

Methods

Animals

Male and female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (300 ± 50 g) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Rats were bred in specific pathogen-free/SPF facilities, and all animals (both experimental and Sham) were co-housed in the 12-hour light/dark cycles at room temperature ranging between 22 and 26 °C and humidity between 40 and 60%. Food and water were provided to the rats unconditionally and regularly. At any significant sign of distress or at the end of the experiments, rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation or CO2 asphyxiation.

Materials

2-FPBA (purity 98%) was purchased from Bidder Medical Technology (Shanghai, CHN) Co., LTD. Hyperbranched PEI (50% (w/v) aqueous solution) (Mw: 5000) was obtained from Yuanye Biotechnology (Shanghai, CHN) Co., LTD. 2-DG (purity 98%) was got from Aladdin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, CHN) Co., LTD. Compound C (purity 99.7%) (catalog number: HY-13418) and AICAR (purity 99.98%) were obtained from MedChemexpress (USA) Co., LTD. Cell Calcein-AM/PI Staining Kit was purchased from Yisheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, CHN) Co., LTD. LysoTracker Green DND-26-Special Packaging Kit, Lipofectamine 3000 Kit, and BCA Protein Assay Kit were Invitrogen (USA) products. Western Cell Lysate (no inhibitor) and Western Monoclonal Diluent were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, CHN) Co., LTD. Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit with ABTS Method and Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit with DPPH Method were purchased from Solarbio Science and Technology (Beijing, CHN) Co., LTD. All siRNAs used in the experiments were synthesized by Biosyntech (Suzhou, CHN) Co., LTD. The sequences used were as follows: (i) MMP13: 5-AGA CUA UGG ACA AAG ACU ATT-3 (sense) and 5-UAG UCU UUG UCC AUA GUC UTT-3 (antisense); (ii) Nonsense: 5-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UTT-3 (sense) and 5-ACG UGA CAC GUU CGG AGA ATT-3 (antisense). The Cy5 dye was introduced at the 5’-end of the sense strand of nonsense siRNA.

Preparation and synthesis of prodrug polymers

The PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG prodrug polymers were synthesized according to the procedure in Fig. S1. First, the synthesis of PEI/2-FPBA prodrug polymers, 50% PEI aqueous solution (10 mg) with 2-FPBA powder (2.5 mg) was dissolved in 3 mL of distillation-distillation H2O, and the reaction was stirred at 60 ℃ for 45 minutes. The solution was kept in a reaction vial for later use. As shown in Fig. S2a, 1H NMR spectra of the products were obtained on a Bruker Advance DRX-400 spectrometer (DE) with D2O as an internal standard. New peaks at 7.5 and 7.6 ppm were detected in PEI/2-FPBA, while the aldehyde peak at 9–10 ppm was lost, which was used to indicate successful grafting of 2-FPBA.

Then, for the synthesis of PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG prodrug polymers, 2-DG powder (2.75 mg) and PEI/2-FPBA prodrug polymers mentioned above reacted at 60 ℃ for 120 minutes. The prodrug polymer solution was ultimately allowed to dilute to 5 mL. Similarly, 1H NMR spectra were used to determine the molecular structure of the products (Fig. S2b). New peaks at 2-2.5 ppm and 3–4 ppm were detected in PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG, suggesting the successful introduction of 2-DG. To further validate the grafting of 2-DG, ARS complexation assay (Beyotime, CHN) was used to determine the exposure of phenylboronic acid. The ARS working solution was intermixed with prodrug polymers (2-FPBA and 2-FPBA/2-DG) in equal volumes. The absorbance of the mixed solution was measured using a UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, JPN) in the wavelength range from 200 to 800 nm. The color change of the mixed solution was recorded. The shift of the maximum absorption peak of the ARS solution in the UV spectrum and the transition from red to yellow represent the exposure of phenylboric acid.

Assembly of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

The siMMP13/2-DG NPs (10 µM) were obtained by mixing 44 µL siRNA solution (an input concentration of 20 µM) with the desired amount of PEI/2-FPBA/2-DG solution (58 µL) mentioned above at room temperature for 15 minutes. 100 nM siMMP13/2-DG NPs (dilution in PBS, pH = 7.4) were used for hydrodynamic diameter and Zeta potential assays. Seven consecutive days of measurements were used to characterize the stability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs. To evaluate the morphology and size distribution of the siMMP13/2-DG NPs, TEM was utilized. The nanoparticles were negatively stained by uranyl acetate solution (2%, wt/wt) to enhance visualization, ensuring consistency with the aforementioned preparation method.

Agarose gel electrophoresis assay

To determine the siRNA loading capacity of PEI grafted with 2-FPBA and 2-DG, siMMP13/2-DG NPs with different N/P ratios (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50) were prepared by gently mixing siRNA with an appropriate amount of polymer as described above. ROS scavenging ability of siMMP13/2-DG NPs

For the ABTS method, the experiment was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the DPPH method, the experiment was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Preparation of siMMP13/2-DG NPs and siMMP13 NPs was performed with three independent replicates for each group. The sample solution (10 µL) was mixed with the ABTS working solution (190 µL) and allowed to stand at room temperature for 6 minutes. The absorbance of the mixture at 515 nm was measured by the microplate reader.

Cell extraction and culture

For the extraction of NPCs, NP tissue from the rat tail vertebrae was collected, and all procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern University of Science and Technology. The NP tissue was cut into pieces and enzymatically digested in trypsin (0.25%) for 30 minutes and then in collagenase Ⅱ (0.2%) for 6 hours. Next, the digestive solution was filtered with a 75 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 2 minutes, then the sediments were collected and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA), as well as 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator. The medium was changed every 3–4 days. When the cell fusion reached 90%, trypsin (Gibco, USA) digested and collected the cells, passaged, and continued the culture. The incubation conditions were replaced: DMEM (low glucose) with 10% FBS, as well as 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator. For this study, only cells of the fourth passage and younger were used.

Cytotoxicity assays

Cytotoxicity of the nano-glycospheres was assessed using an inverted fluorescence microscope post-staining with a Cell Calcein-AM/PI Staining Kit and the CCK-8 kit (Abbkine, CHN). Cells cultured in 96-well plates (50% confluency) were treated with various concentrations of siMMP13/2-DG NPs for 1 or 3 days. Subsequently, the cells were washed and stained using 2 µL of 2 µM Calcein-AM and 5 µL of 4 µM PI diluted in 2 mL of 1× assay buffer. After a 15-minute incubation in the dark, the cells were washed with PBS three times. Live cells emitted green fluorescence (AM), while dead cells exhibited red fluorescence (PI). For the CCK-8 assay, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to 90 µL of culture medium in each well and incubated in the dark at 37 ℃ for 1 hour. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The CCK-8 assay quantifies cellular metabolic activity, with absorbance values proportional to the number of viable cells.

To verify the ability of TBHP to simulate the degenerative microenvironment, a Live/Dead staining assay (Invitrogen, USA) was used. NPCs were seeded in 96-well cell culture plates at 50% confluency and cultured overnight. Different concentrations of TBHP were incubated with NPCs for 24 hors. Subsequently, the cells were washed and stained using 1 mL compound A (Live Green) and 1 µL compound B (Dead Red). After incubation for 30 minutes at room temperature, the fluorescence was observed by inverted fluorescence microscopy.

siRNA tracking and release assays

For assessment of cellular internalization behavior, Cy5- siNC/2-DG NPs (100 nM) were added to the complete medium or simulating degeneration medium (100 µM TBHP), and free Cy5-siRNA or PBS served as control. After 1 hour of co-incubation, cell slides were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Biosharp, CHN), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. FITC-Phalloidin was used to label the cytoskeleton and incubated for 45 minutes at room temperature. Fluorescence imaging was performed by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Carl Zeiss AG, DE).

To investigate the endocytic pathways and intracellular release behavior of siMMP13/2-DG NPs, NPCs were treated with the Cy5-siNC/2-DG NPs at different time nodes. At predetermined times, the treated cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated at 37 ℃ for 45 minutes with 100 nM LysoTracker Green in a cell culture medium. After incubation, the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and subjected to Hoechst33342 staining. Fluorescence imaging was performed by CLSM.

Gene silencing assay

The gene silencing efficiency of siMMP13 NPs targeting the MMP13 gene was evaluated using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). NPCs were seeded at a density of 7 × 105 cells per 100 mm culture dish in the growth medium for 24 hours. Cells were treated with 100 µM TBHP or corresponding drugs (siMMP13 NPs, siNC NPs, and siMMP13) for 48 hours. Then, the cells were washed with PBS, and total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, CHN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed to measure the mRNA levels of MMP13 in NPCs. β-actin was chosen as an internal housekeeping gene to normalize MMP13 mRNA. The mRNA expression level was calculated based on the comparative Ct method (2−△△Ct). RT-qPCR was performed using specific primers (Table S1).

WB analysis

Once reached 70% confluence, NPCs were pretreated with 100 µM TBHP or corresponding drugs (siMMP13 NPs, siNC NPs, and siMMP13) for 72 hours, while untreated NPCs served as the Control group. Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (MedChemexpress, USA). The protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins underwent SDS-PAGE for separation, followed by transfer to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking for 65 minutes with 5% (w/v) skim milk (Epizyme, CHN), primary antibodies against MMP13 were applied overnight at 4 ℃. Following six washes with Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) (Servicebio, CHN), corresponding secondary antibodies were added for 1.5 hours. Protein detection was carried out using the enhanced ECL substrate with a chemiluminescence system (Tanon, CHN).

To assess the AMPK signaling pathway, WB was also employed to detect the expression levels of AMPK, p-AMPK, SIRT1, PGC-1α, NF-κB, and p-NF-κB in NPCs. Analogously, cells were treated with 100 µM TBHP or corresponding drugs (siMMP13/2-DG NPs, siMMP13 NPs, and 2-DG; siMMP13/2-DG NPs, AICAR, siMMP13/2-DG NPs with Compound C) for 72 hours. Next, total cellular proteins were isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Electrophoresis and membrane transfer were performed as previously described analysis. WB was performed using specific antibodies (Table S2).

Immunofluorescence staining

Once reached 70% confluence, NPCs cells were treated with 100 µM TBHP or corresponding drugs (siMMP13/2-DG NPs, siMMP13 NPs, and 2-DG; siMMP13/2-DG NPs, AICAR, siMMP13/2-DG NPs with Compound C) for 24 hours. IVDD model and treatment protocols

The IVDD rat model was established by the following protocol. After a week of adaptive feeding, rats were randomly assigned to five treatment groups, each consisting of five animals. All rats except the Sham group underwent anterior lumbar AF puncture for IVDD modeling. Under anesthesia induced by isoflurane and atmospheric air mixture (2 ± 0.5%), the specific puncture site (L4/5) was identified using the bilateral iliac ridge lines. Following abdominal disinfection, the skin and muscle layers were incised, allowing for blunt dissection of internal organs to expose the vertebral body at the designated site (L4/5). A syringe needle (26-G) was then used to puncture the AF vertically to a depth of approximately 1.5 mm. A successful puncture was confirmed when the syringe needle remained stable without tipping. Subsequently, the needle was rotated clockwise by 360° and retained for 30 seconds. To minimize potential reflux, secondary injury and inflammation, each drug group received a slow injection of the same concentration (100 nM) of their respective treatments (PBS, siMMP13/2-DG NPs, siMMP13 NPs, and siNC/2-DG NPs) at a volume of 2 µL via a microinjector (33-G) through the puncture site. All formulations were prepared and used on-site to ensure there was no noticeable precipitation or aggregation. After the injection, wait until no significant reflux was observed before suturing.

To match the loading module for mechanical testing, we constructed a rat tail vertebral IVDD model. To be specific, all rats except the Sham group underwent the AF puncture for IVDD modeling. Under anesthesia induced by a mixture of isoflurane and air (2 ± 0.5%), the tail was disinfected, the skin and ligament layers were incised, and the vertebral body was separated to expose the designated area (Co 7/8). Model construction and drug administration are consistent with what was mentioned above.

Mechanical testing and analysis

After euthanizing the rats, the tails were excised, and excess muscle tissue was removed. The vertebrae were separated by making an incision through the middle of adjacent vertebrae, creating vertebral-vertebral motion segments. A dual-axis mechanical loading frame was used to test axial mechanical properties (Fig. S23a). The motion segments were secured to the loading frame using a fixation device, wrapped in PBS-soaked wiping material, and then subjected to the following testing protocol. Axial compression/tension was applied under displacement control ranging from 0.2 to -0.2 mm. Compression/tension mechanical properties were calculated using Origin (OriginLab, USA) software, with the slopes of the bottom and top 20% of the curve representing compression and tension stiffness, respectively. To account for inter-animal variability, mechanical results were normalized to the intact segment of the same caudal vertebra.

Radiological analysis of IVDD

MRI was utilized to assess the water content of the IVD at the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks. The rats were anesthetized under isoflurane/atmospheric air mixture (2 ± 0.5%) and subsequently positioned in the MRI scanner (GSMED, CHN). The sagittal position was identified for observation of IVD. The T2-weighted signal intensity of the IVD was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) to evaluate water content. The degree of disc degeneration across the treatment groups was statistically analyzed using the Pfirrmann grading system (Table S3).

Histological analysis of IVDD

IVD tissue samples were collected at the 4th and 8th postoperative weeks. Samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 48 hours, excess muscle tissue was removed, and the samples were decalcified in EDTA decalcification solution for 6 weeks. After decalcification, samples were washed overnight, dehydrated through a gradient of ethanol, clarified in xylene, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were stored at -20 ℃ overnight before being sectioned into 5 μm slices using a tissue microtome (Leica, DE). These slices were baked for 30 minutes and stained with H&E to examine morphological and structural changes in the NPCs. SO-FG and Alcian Blue staining were employed to assess changes in collagen types within the IVD. Additionally, immunohistochemistry was performed to detect the expression of extracellular matrix-related factors (MMP13 and COL2), lactate-related enzymes (HK2, PFKP, PKM2, and LDHA), and AMPK pathway-related proteins (AMPK, p-AMPK, NF-κB, and p-NF-κB). Immunofluorescence was used to evaluate the expression of nerve growth related factors, such as NGF and BDNF. Histological grade was performed strictly in accordance with Table S4.

Behavioral studies

For LBP detection. As described in a previous study by Kim et al., we measured pain thresholds in experimental animals by applying mechanical forces to deep tissue in the back of rats using the algometer (Ugo Basile, IT) to assess their behavior [44]. In simple terms, the tip of the test device was shifted to the L4/5 segment of the lumbar vertebra of the rat, and after the rat was in a correct position and emotionally stable, the vertical downward pressure (50 g/s) was slowly applied. The sound or apparent discomfort of the rat is confirmed as reaching the threshold, the pressure is relieved, and the value is recorded. In addition, each rat was measured 3 times with a time interval of no less than 5 minutes. The results of PATs were employed as the primary behavioral readout to evaluate axial pressure-related LBP associated with IVDD (lower readings represent more severe pain).

For hindlimb pain detection. The Von Frey experiment and equipment (Aesthesio, IT) were used to test whether disc puncture modeling affects the sensitivity of the rat’s hind limb to painful stimuli. Specifically, the rats were housed in a metal-plastic cage with a hollow part at the bottom that allowed the device to pass through and reach the back paws of the rats. The Von Frey test started with a lower score (4.08). If the rat does not respond significantly to the stimulation, the device is replaced in an incremental manner. If the back claw retraction or lifting phenomenon occurs, replace it with a smaller stimulus to verify correctness. In addition, each rat was measured 3 times with a time interval of no less than 5 minutes. The results of the Von Frey test were used to assess the pain sensitivity of the hind limbs due to nerve root involvement (lower readings represent more severe pain).

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from NPCs, which were treated with 100 µM TBHP or 100 nM siMMP13/2-DG NPs, respectively. RNA was sequenced by Gene Denovo (Guangzhou, CHN). The raw sequencing data were purified to remove low-quality sequences, including those with adapters and high rates of unknown nucleotides. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 software. Gene expression levels were quantified by calculating FPKM values with Stringtie software. Differential gene expression was analyzed using the DESeq2 algorithm, with P-values adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR). Criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) included a fold change greater than 2 and an FDR of less than 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to reveal the biological roles of DEGs, while KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified significantly impacted pathways. The hypergeometric test in R software was used for the enrichment analysis, with terms showing an adjusted P-value (P.adj) of less than 0.05 considered significantly enriched.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Two-sided student’s t-test was carried out to assess two-group differences. Two-way ANOVA or One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to assess the statistical significance of differences between multiple groups. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software). “n” represents the number of samples used in the experiments. Unless otherwise specified, “n” refers to independent experiments of the type indicated for each assay: nano-glycosphere synthesis, cell culture, or individual animal, as appropriate. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications [45, 46]. No animals or data were excluded from the analyses. After acclimatization, animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using the SORTBY(RANDARRAY(COUNTA)) function in Microsoft Excel (2023). Pain behavior scoring, Pfirrmann grading, and histological scoring were performed in a double-blinded manner. In cases of disagreement between the two independent evaluators, a third assessor was consulted to reach a consensus. All representative micrographs and data points are derived from at least three independent experiments. Data were assumed to follow a normal distribution, although this was not formally tested. In all cases, statistical significance was set as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, and not significant (ns).
