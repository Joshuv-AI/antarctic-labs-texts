Introduction

Hantaviruses represent a globally distributed yet persistently underrecognized family of rodent-borne RNA viruses responsible for two potentially fatal human diseases: Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS). Unlike most arboviruses, hantaviruses are transmitted not by arthropod vectors but predominantly through inhalation of virus-laden aerosols generated from the dried excreta—urine, feces, and saliva—of chronically infected rodent reservoir hosts. Although rodents develop persistent, asymptomatic infections, humans are dead-end hosts who occasionally encounter these animals in domestic, agricultural, or wilderness settings.

The first formally characterized hantavirus was Hantaan virus (HTNV), isolated in 1978 by Ho Wang Lee and colleagues near the Hantan River in Korea from the striped field mouse Apodemus agrarius—the reservoir responsible for Korean hemorrhagic fever that had afflicted thousands of United Nations troops during the Korean War of the early 1950s.1 Retrospective analyses have since revealed that hantavirus-like illnesses—then called epidemic hemorrhagic fever or nephropathia epidemica — ad been documented in Scandinavia, the Soviet Union, and China for decades prior.2 The North American chapter of the hantavirus story began dramatically in the spring of 1993 when a cluster of young, healthy individuals in the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States succumbed to acute respiratory failure, ultimately linked to a novel hantavirus—Sin Nombre virus (SNV)—harbored by the deer mouse Peromyscus maniculatus.3

The past decade has witnessed several major epidemiological transitions: The global spread of Seoul virus (SEOV) via the cosmopolitan Norway rat; large outbreaks of Puumala virus (PUUV) HFRS in Scandinavia and central Europe (Finland 2020–2021: >1,200 confirmed cases); the documented person-to-person transmission of Andes virus (ANDV) during a 2019 cluster in Argentina; and the emergence of previously unrecognized hantavirus lineages on multiple continents including Africa and Southeast Asia.4,5 Climate change, deforestation, rodent population surges driven by mast-seeding events, and increasing human encroachment into wildlife habitats are collectively amplifying hantavirus transmission risk.6

This comprehensive review synthesizes the current state of knowledge on hantavirus virology, genetic diversity, global epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical presentations across both major syndromes, diagnostic strategies, and evidence-based management—including contemporary intensive care protocols and emerging antiviral therapies. The goal is to provide clinicians, infectious disease specialists, and intensivists with a definitive, up-to-date reference to facilitate prompt recognition and optimal management of this re-emerging pathogen.

Materials and Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, WHO databases, and CDC surveillance reports (1993–2025) to synthesize current evidence on hantavirus taxonomy, epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical features, diagnosis, treatment, and intensive care unit (ICU) management.

Taxonomy and Molecular Virology

Taxonomic Classification

Hantaviruses were historically classified in the genus Hantavirus within the family Bunyaviridae. Following comprehensive phylogenetic re-analyses, the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) restructured this grouping in 2018, elevating hantaviruses to the family Hantaviridae within the order Bunyavirales.7 The type species for the family is Hantaan orthohantavirus, and the current taxonomy encompasses the genus Orthohantavirus, which contains more than 50 recognized species as of 2024.7,8 This taxonomic revision reflects the exceptional diversity of hantaviruses, which are now known to infect not only rodents but also insectivores (shrews and moles) and bats, though only rodent-borne species have been conclusively linked to human disease.

Virion Structure

Hantavirus virions are enveloped, roughly spherical particles measuring 80–120 nm in diameter, with a characteristic buoyant density of approximately 1.16–1.18 gm/cm3 in sucrose gradients.9 The lipid bilayer envelope is derived from the Golgi apparatus of infected cells and is studded with projections formed by two viral glycoproteins, Gn (formerly G1) and Gc (formerly G2), which protrude approximately 5–10 nm from the virion surface.9 These glycoproteins form tetrameric spike complexes arranged in a square lattice pattern visible under cryo-electron microscopy, and they mediate virion attachment, fusion, and cellular entry—making them prime targets for neutralizing antibody responses and vaccine development.10

The internal component of the virion consists of three ribonucleoprotein (RNP) segments, each comprising a genomic RNA segment coated with multiple copies of the nucleocapsid (N) protein and associated with a single copy of the large (L) protein, which functions as the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp).9

Genome Organization

The hantavirus genome consists of three negative-sense, single-stranded RNA segments designated large (L), medium (M), and small (S), encapsidated within the virion.11,12 The L segment (~6.5 kb) encodes the RNA-dependent RdRp (~250 kDa), utilizing a cap-snatching mechanism to prime mRNA synthesis. The M segment (~3.6 kb) encodes the glycoprotein precursor (GPC, ~130 kDa) co-translationally cleaved into Gn (~68 kDa) and Gc (~54 kDa); Gn contains the primary antigenic sites for neutralizing antibodies. The S segment (~1.7 kb) encodes the nucleocapsid protein (N protein, ~48–54 kDa), the most abundantly expressed viral protein and the principal antigen used in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based serological diagnostics.11

Replication Cycle

Hantavirus entry into endothelial cells—the primary target cell type—is mediated through interaction of the Gn/Gc spike complex with cellular integrins, chiefly beta-3 integrin (αvβ3) on endothelial and platelet surfaces and beta-1 integrin (α5β1) on macrophages.13 Following receptor engagement, the virus is internalized via clathrin- and non-clathrin-mediated endocytosis; acidification of the endosome triggers Gc-mediated membrane fusion, releasing the RNP segments into the cytoplasm.14 Unlike many RNA viruses, hantaviruses do not cause pronounced cytopathic effect in-vitro or in-vivo; pathology is predominantly immune-mediated.14 The incubation period in humans ranges from 1 to 8 weeks (mean 2–4 weeks).

Genetic Evolution and Reassortment

Hantaviruses demonstrate strict co-evolution with their rodent reservoirs—a phenomenon termed co-speciation—wherein the phylogenetic trees of virus and host are largely congruent.15 Reassortment events, the exchange of genome segments between co-infecting hantaviruses, contribute to the emergence of novel variants; the Dobrava–Belgrade virus (DOBV) complex provides the clearest natural example.16 The error rate of the hantavirus RdRp (~10−5 substitutions per site per replication cycle) is lower than for many RNA viruses, and the M segment (encoding the neutralization-target glycoproteins) shows the greatest inter-species variation, with important implications for broadly protective vaccine design.15

Genetic Variants, Serotypes, and Rodent Reservoirs

The family Hantaviridae now encompasses more than 50 formally recognized species and numerous provisional variants.7,8 From a clinical perspective, hantaviruses are broadly divided into two ecologically and geographically distinct groups: Old World hantaviruses (primarily causing HFRS) and New World hantaviruses (primarily causing HCPS). Global Distribution and Epidemiology

Overall Burden

Hantavirus infections impose a substantial global public health burden, with an estimated 1,50,000–2,00,000 cases of HFRS reported annually and several hundred to a few thousand cases of HCPS.17 The true burden is likely higher because many infections — particularly mild Puumala nephropathia epidemica and SEOV HFRS—are misdiagnosed or unreported. Mortality is strongly variant- and syndrome-dependent, ranging from less than 0.5% for PUUV HFRS to greater than 70% for Castelo dos Sonhos HCPS.18 A distinctive epidemiological feature is the 3–5 years cyclical pattern of outbreaks observed with PUUV in Scandinavia and Dobrava virus in the Balkans, temporally linked to boom-bust cycles of bank vole and field mouse populations driven by beech mast and oak acorn production.19

HFRS—Country-specific Burden

China bears more than 90% of global HFRS cases, reporting between 40,000 and 1,00,000 cases annually, predominantly caused by HTNV in northern and central China and SEOV in coastal provinces.20 The case fatality rate for Hantaan HFRS in China has declined from approximately 10% in the 1980s to 1–3% with modern supportive care.21 Finland reports the highest per-capita HFRS incidence in Europe, with PUUV causing 1,000–3,000 cases per year in epidemic years and a peak of 2,623 cases during the 2020–2021 outbreak.22 Germany documented 1,742 cases of PUUV HFRS in 2017, the largest single-year epidemic in that country.23 South Korea reports 400–500 annual cases of HTNV HFRS.24 Seoul virus is unique in its cosmopolitan distribution, having been detected on all inhabited continents through the global trade in Rattus norvegicus.25

HCPS—Country-specific Burden

Sin Nombre virus has caused approximately 850 confirmed cases of HCPS in the United States through 2024, with a case fatality rate of approximately 36%—a rate that has not dramatically improved over three decades despite advances in ICU care.26,27 Brazil has the highest total burden of HCPS in South America, with more than 2,500 cumulative confirmed cases as of 2024 and case fatality rates ranging from 36% (Juquitiba) to 72% (Castelo dos Sonhos).28 Argentina and Chile each have approximately 900–1,000 cumulative confirmed cases, caused almost entirely by ANDV.

A landmark epidemiological event was the 2019 El Bolson HCPS cluster in Rio Negro Province, Argentina, which involved 35 cases with 11 deaths and provided the strongest contemporary evidence for ANDV person-to-person transmission, with whole-genome sequencing confirming direct nosocomial and household spread (Table 2 and Figs 1 to 3).5

Mode of Transmission

Rodent-to-human Transmission

The overwhelming majority of hantavirus infections in humans result from inhalation of infectious aerosols generated from the excreta—urine, feces, and saliva—of chronically infected rodent reservoirs.29 Infected rodents shed high titers of virus in their excreta for prolonged periods without developing symptomatic illness. Activities associated with greatest transmission risk include: Sweeping or vacuuming rodent-infested structures without respiratory protection; cleaning barns, sheds, or cabins after winter rodent occupation; agricultural activities (ploughing, harvesting, and cutting vegetation); military training exercises in rodent-endemic fields; camping and outdoor recreation in endemic areas; and residing in poorly constructed dwellings with rodent access.

Person-to-person Transmission (Andes Virus)

With the single, critically important exception of ANDV, no hantavirus has been convincingly demonstrated to spread from person to person. The possibility of interhuman ANDV transmission was first raised during a 1996 Argentine outbreak and subsequently confirmed by epidemiological and phylogenetic analyses of the 2019 El Bolson cluster.5 In that cluster, whole-genome sequencing demonstrated that 14 of 35 cases were linked to person-to-person transmission chains, including at least two healthcare worker infections. The putative mechanism involves respiratory droplets and close contact; airborne transmission has not been definitively established. Critically, no other New World or Old World hantavirus — including SNV—has been reliably documented to transmit between humans.30

Occupational Risk Groups

Populations at highest risk include agricultural and forestry workers, military personnel in field exercises, pest control workers, veterinarians, rodent ecologists, inhabitants of rural dwellings, and persons disturbing long-unoccupied buildings.31 The male sex predominates in most series (approximately 60–70% of cases), likely reflecting differential occupational exposure rather than biological susceptibility. In endemic regions, rodent population surges following El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events are consistently followed by 1–2 year lagged increases in human HCPS cases.32

Pathophysiology

Target Cells and Viral Entry

Endothelial cells lining small vessels throughout the body are the primary target of hantavirus infection.33 Platelets and macrophages are also productively infected. Crucially, hantaviruses are non-cytopathic; they replicate efficiently in endothelial cells without causing direct cell death. Pathology is principally immune-mediated rather than resulting from direct viral cytolysis—a key distinction with clinical importance for therapy.33 Pathogenic hantaviruses preferentially use beta-3 integrin (αvβ3), whose normal function is inhibition of the VEGFR-2-mediated permeability response. By occupying this receptor, pathogenic hantaviruses prevent normal VE-cadherin stabilization and VEGFR-2 attenuation, resulting in sustained VEGF-driven vascular permeability.34

Immune Pathology and the Cytokine Storm

Following endothelial cell infection, a robust innate immune response is initiated. The transition from incubation to clinical illness coincides with a dramatic expansion of hantavirus-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs).35 In severe disease, a dysregulated cytokine storm characterized by massively elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, and MCP-1 amplifies vascular permeability and promotes platelet dysfunction.36 Regulatory T cell (Treg) function is impaired in severe hantavirus disease, contributing to the exaggerated inflammatory response.37 Complement activation via both classical and alternative pathways contributes to endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis.38

Organ-specific Pathology

In HFRS, the hallmark pathological lesion is capillary leak within the renal medulla, producing bilateral hemorrhagic interstitial nephritis. Retroperitoneal edema—sometimes producing a dramatic computed tomographic appearance of bilateral perirenal fluid—is characteristic of severe Hantaan HFRS.39

In HCPS, capillary leak is maximal in the pulmonary microcirculation. Massive transudation of protein-rich fluid into the alveolar space produces noncardiogenic pulmonary edema. Uniquely, hantavirus pulmonary edema is associated with elevated pulmonary capillary wedge pressure—reflecting a cardiogenic component from myocardial depression—superimposed on increased capillary permeability, creating a mixed cardiogenic-permeability edema.40 This hemodynamic pattern informs the critical recommendation to avoid aggressive fluid resuscitation in HCPS.

Clinical Features

Hemorrhagic Fever with Renal Syndrome (HFRS)

Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome presents as a classic febrile illness with acute kidney injury (AKI) and variable hemorrhagic manifestations. The clinical course is described in five sequential phases.

Phase I—Febrile Phase (Days 1–5)

Onset is abrupt with high-grade fever (38.5–41°C), severe headache, backache and loin pain, myalgias, abdominal pain, and nausea. Facial flushing, conjunctival injection, and petechial hemorrhages over the palate and conjunctiva are characteristic early signs. Blurred vision—due to retinal edema and ciliary body swelling—occurs in up to 30% of Hantaan HFRS cases and is a useful clinical pointer.41 Thrombocytopenia (platelet count 50,000–1,00,000/µL) and proteinuria detectable by dipstick are near-universal early findings.

Phase II—Hypotensive Phase (Days 4–8)

In approximately 10–40% of Hantaan HFRS cases, fever defervescence is followed by an abrupt drop in blood pressure, producing hemorrhagic hypotension or frank shock from massive plasma leak into the retroperitoneal space and visceral organs. Severe hypotension at this stage is associated with increased mortality and development of oliguric renal failure. Coagulation disturbances, including elevated D-dimers and frank disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), occur in severe cases.

Phase III—Oliguric Phase (Days 6–12)

Oliguria (urine output <400 mL/24 hours) develops as the dominant clinical feature. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen rise steeply, often reaching 500–1,000 µmol/L in severe Hantaan HFRS. Paradoxical hypertension is common during this phase. Hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is required in 20–40% of hospitalized Hantaan HFRS cases.41

Phase IV—Diuretic Phase (Days 12–15+)

The diuretic phase herald's renal recovery, with urine output increasing dramatically to 3–8 L per day. Paradoxically, this phase carries significant risk: Hypovolemia, hypokalemia, and hypophosphatemia from massive urinary losses may precipitate cardiac arrhythmias and hypotension—deaths from hypokalemia-induced arrhythmia during the diuretic phase have been documented.42

Phase V—Convalescent Phase (Weeks to Months)

Recovery is generally complete, though approximately 30–40% of patients with severe acute disease demonstrate residual proteinuria or reduced GFR at 1 year, and a subset develop chronic kidney disease.43 Hypertension has been documented at elevated rates in long-term follow-up of HFRS survivors.

Hantavirus Cardiopulmonary Syndrome (HCPS)

Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome unfolds in three clinically distinct phases, with the transition from prodrome to cardiopulmonary phase constituting the most dangerous and frequently fatal clinical juncture.44

Phase I—Prodromal Phase (Days 1–5)

The prodrome is characterized by fever, myalgias (often severe, predominantly in the thighs, and lower back), headache, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Respiratory symptoms are absent or minimal. The hematological triad of thrombocytopenia, hemoconcentration, and immunoblasts on peripheral blood smear in a febrile patient with myalgias and rodent exposure history should immediately raise suspicion for HCPS.44

Phase II—Cardiopulmonary Phase (Days 5–10)

Without warning, typically 24–48 hours after initial assessment, the patient deteriorates precipitously. Nonproductive cough, progressive dyspnea, and hypoxaemia develop rapidly, often within hours of the first respiratory symptom. The hemodynamic signature of severe HCPS—low cardiac output (cardiac index often <2.5 L/minute/m2), elevated systemic vascular resistance, elevated pulmonary artery wedge pressure (often 15–25 mm Hg), and severely depressed left ventricular ejection fraction—reflects a profound virus-mediated myocardial depression superimposed on capillary leak.40 This cardiogenic-permeability combination explains why fluid resuscitation is distinctly harmful in HCPS. Bradycardia, wide-complex ventricular rhythm, or complete heart block are hemodynamic warning signs of imminent cardiovascular collapse and should prompt emergency extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) consideration.45

Phase III—Diuretic/Recovery Phase

Survivors who successfully traverse the cardiopulmonary phase typically enter a diuretic phase within 3–7 days of peak illness, with brisk urine output heralding resolution of pulmonary edema. Unlike HFRS, long-term renal sequelae are uncommon in HCPS. Cardiovascular function normalizes within 1–2 weeks in survivors (Table 3 and Fig. 4).46

Diagnosis

Hantavirus infection must be considered in any patient presenting with a febrile illness accompanied by AKI (HFRS) or rapidly progressive respiratory failure and thrombocytopenia (HCPS), particularly with a relevant rodent exposure history. The diagnostic approach is guided by the interval from symptom onset.47

Molecular Diagnostics

RT-PCR/Quantitative RT-PCR

Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction targeting the S or M segment of the hantavirus genome is the most sensitive test during the early febrile phase (days 1–7) and is considered the gold standard for virological confirmation.47 Sensitivity peaks at approximately 90–95% during the first 5 days and declines rapidly thereafter as viraemia wanes. Whole blood collected in EDTA is the preferred sample type. Pan-hantavirus RT-PCR assays using degenerate primers targeting conserved L segment sequences enable detection of phylogenetically diverse species.47,48 Higher viraemia in SNV HCPS correlates with greater disease severity and poorer outcomes.49

Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR)

Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) offers improved sensitivity over conventional qRT-PCR for samples with very low viral loads, such as those collected late in the illness.50 Preliminary data suggest ddPCR may extend the window of RT-PCR positivity by 1–3 days and may be particularly useful in resource-rich settings where maximum sensitivity is required.51

Serological Diagnostics

IgM ELISA

Detection of hantavirus-specific IgM antibodies by ELISA is the most widely used confirmatory test in clinical practice. Immunoglobulin M is detectable from days 3 to 5 of symptoms, peaks during the 2nd week, and may persist for 2–6 months.47 The recombinant N protein is the most commonly used antigen, offering high cross-reactivity among hantavirus species. Sensitivity of IgM ELISA for hospitalized patients exceeds 95% when testing is performed after day 5.48

IgG ELISA and PRNT

Immunoglobulin G antibodies appear from days 7 to 10 and persist for years to decades, providing a serological marker of past infection. A fourfold or greater rise in IgG titre between acute and convalescent samples confirms recent infection.47 The plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) is the gold standard serological method for species-specific antibody detection, measuring the capacity of patient serum to neutralize live virus under biosafety level (BSL-3) conditions. Plaque reduction neutralization test sensitivity reaches 95% by days 14–21, but its requirement for BSL-3 facilities limits its role to reference laboratory confirmation.52

Rapid Antigen and Rapid Antibody Tests

Point-of-care rapid antigen detection tests provide results within 15–30 minutes, potentially useful in resource-limited or remote settings. However, their sensitivity (approximately 60–75%) is inferior to ELISA, and all positive results should be confirmed by standard ELISA or PRNT.53,54

Hematological Markers and Radiology

The hematological triad of thrombocytopenia, hemoconcentration, and immunoblasts on peripheral blood smear is a sensitive (>90%) early indicator of HCPS in an appropriate clinical and epidemiological context.44 In HFRS, computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen demonstrates bilateral perirenal edema virtually pathognomonic of severe Hantaan HFRS.39 In HCPS, chest radiograph rapidly evolves to bilateral interstitial or alveolar infiltrates (the “bat-wing” pattern) during the cardiopulmonary phase. Echocardiography in HCPS typically reveals global left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction 20–40%) (Table 4).45

Treatment

Antiviral Therapy

Ribavirin

Ribavirin, a nucleoside analogue with broad-spectrum antiviral activity, has demonstrated efficacy in-vitro and in animal models against multiple hantavirus species. A landmark placebo-controlled trial by Huggins et al. in Chinese HFRS patients demonstrated significant reductions in case fatality rate, duration of oliguric phase, and need for dialysis in those receiving ribavirin.55,56 Intravenous ribavirin is recommended for hospitalized HFRS patients, with greatest benefit when initiated within the first 5 days of illness. The standard adult dosing regimen is: Loading dose 33 mg/kg IV (maximum 2 gm), followed by 16 mg/kg IV every 6 hours for 4 days, then 8 mg/kg IV every 8 hours for 3 days. For HCPS, two large randomised trials (Mertz et al.) failed to demonstrate benefit for IV ribavirin, and it is currently not recommended for HCPS.57

Favipiravir

Favipiravir (T-705), a broad-spectrum RdRp inhibitor, has demonstrated potent in vitro activity against multiple hantavirus species including SNV, ANDV, HTNV, and PUUV.58 Animal model data from the ANDV hamster model—the most reliable small-animal model of severe hantavirus disease—show significant reductions in viral load, lung pathology, and mortality with favipiravir treatment.58 No completed randomised controlled trial exists as of 2024; favipiravir is currently an investigational agent to be considered in clinical trials or compassionate use protocols.

Monoclonal Antibody Therapies

Several highly potent, broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting ANDV Gn/Gc, including MX10 and JL16, have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in the ANDV hamster model, conferring 100% survival even when administered 5 days post-infection.59 Human anti-ANDV mAbs derived from convalescent donors are in early-phase clinical development. These emerging immunotherapies represent the most exciting therapeutic advance in hantavirus medicine in a decade.

Convalescent Plasma

Convalescent plasma (CP)—plasma from hantavirus-recovered individuals carrying high-titre neutralizing antibodies—has been used anecdotally for both HFRS and HCPS. No completed randomised trial has established its efficacy, and logistical challenges limit routine applicability. Convalescent plasma remains an option for compassionate use in severe cases.60

Vaccines

No hantavirus vaccine is approved for use in Western countries. China has licensed a bivalent inactivated whole-virus vaccine (Hantaan + Seoul strains) since 1994, with field efficacy of 90–95% in endemic areas.61 A DNA vaccine encoding the HTNV M segment entered phase II trials and demonstrated strong neutralizing antibody responses but has not progressed to licensure. Virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines, mRNA-based vaccines, and recombinant adenovirus-vectored constructs are in active preclinical or early clinical development.62

Intensive Care Unit Management

HCPS—Cardiopulmonary Phase

Severe HCPS is one of the most physiologically challenging syndromes encountered in the ICU. The combination of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema with significant cardiomyopathy requires nuanced management that differs importantly from standard ARDS or cardiogenic shock protocols. Patients with HCPS must ideally be managed in centers with ECMO capability.45

Respiratory Management

Invasive mechanical ventilation is required for most patients who progress to the cardiopulmonary phase. Lung-protective ventilation using low tidal volumes (4–6 mL/kg of ideal body weight), plateau pressure limitation (<30 cmH2O), PEEP titration guided by respiratory mechanics, and prone positioning for refractory hypoxaemia (PaO2/FiO2 < 150) should be applied. A critical pitfall is the reflex administration of aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation for hypotension—because the hemodynamic pattern involves cardiomyopathy and capillary permeability rather than vasodilatory distributive shock, fluid administration worsens pulmonary edema and may be lethal.63

Hemodynamic Monitoring and Vasopressor Therapy

Invasive hemodynamic monitoring via pulmonary artery (Swan–Ganz) catheterization is highly informative in HCPS, revealing the pathognomonic pattern of low cardiac index (<2.2 L/minute/m2), elevated pulmonary artery pressure, elevated pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (15–25 mm Hg), and elevated systemic vascular resistance—distinguishing HCPS from distributive (septic) shock.40 Vasopressor therapy with norepinephrine (0.1–1.0 μ/kg/minute) is first-line; vasopressin (0.03–0.04 units/minute) may be added for refractory shock. Inotropic support with dobutamine (2–10 μ/kg/minute) is indicated for severely depressed cardiac output (CI < 1.8 L/minute/m2).45

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation has transformed outcomes in severe HCPS and is the most impactful ICU intervention available for this condition. Because HCPS is characterized by a relatively short (3–7 day) cardiopulmonary phase after which survivors recover rapidly, bridging patients through this acute, potentially reversible phase with ECMO can be lifesaving. Published case series from the USA, Argentina, and Chile report survival rates of 60–75% in HCPS patients managed with ECMO, compared with near-universal mortality in historical pre-ECMO series with similar severity.45,64 Veno-venous (VV) ECMO is appropriate for predominant respiratory failure with preserved cardiac output; veno-arterial (VA) ECMO is required when cardiogenic shock dominates—the more common pattern in severe HCPS. Early ECMO cannulation—before the development of severe lactic acidosis (lactate >8 mmol/L) or cardiac arrest—is associated with better survival.64

Plasmapheresis

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) has been evaluated as an adjunctive strategy to remove circulating cytokines, viral antigens, and inflammatory mediators. Several Argentine and Chilean case series reports improved survival and faster hemodynamic recovery with TPE initiated early in the cardiopulmonary phase of ANDV HCPS.65 Therapeutic plasma exchange may be considered as compassionate therapy in resource-equipped centers for severe ANDV HCPS.67,68

HFRS—ICU Management

Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)

RRT is required in 20–40% of hospitalized Hantaan HFRS cases and is the cornerstone of ICU management for severe HFRS.56 Continuous renal replacement therapy— specifically continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF)—is preferred over intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) in hemodynamically unstable patients.68 Indications for RRT include: Oliguria refractory to conservative measures, life-threatening hyperkalaemia (K+ > 6.0 mEq/L), severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.15), symptomatic uraemia, and fluid overload with pulmonary compromise.56 Citrate anticoagulation is preferred over systemic heparin in thrombocytopenic patients.

Hemostasis Management

Thrombocytopenia is near-universal in HFRS and contributes substantially to hemorrhagic complications. Platelet transfusions are indicated for active significant bleeding with platelets below 20,000/µL or for prophylaxis in patients undergoing invasive procedures at platelets below 50,000/µL.69,70 Disseminated intravascular coagulation—particularly in severe Hantaan and Dobrava HFRS—is managed with fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and cryoprecipitate for fibrinogen replacement; antifibrinolytics may be considered for severe mucosal hemorrhage (Table 5).

Infection Control and Prevention

Personal Protective Equipment for Healthcare Workers

For patients with suspected or confirmed hantavirus infection (all species except ANDV), standard contact and droplet precautions are recommended: Surgical mask, gloves, gown, and eye protection during close contact, aerosol-generating procedures, or handling of specimens.31 For confirmed or strongly suspected ANDV infection — given documented person-to-person transmission — enhanced precautions are warranted: N95 (or equivalent FFP2/FFP3) respirator or powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR), gloves, impermeable gown, and face shield. Negative-pressure isolation rooms are recommended for ANDV patients.31,66

Environmental Rodent Control and Decontamination

Primary prevention of hantavirus infection is fundamentally a rodent control problem. Key environmental measures include: Sealing all gaps and openings in dwellings; maintaining cleanliness of food storage areas; proper disposal of refuse; and using snap traps or anticoagulant rodenticides in a systematic integrated pest management (IPM) programme.29 Rodent-contaminated surfaces should be pre-wetted with a 10% bleach solution, allowed to soak for at least 10 minutes, then wiped up using gloved hands—never swept dry. All materials should be double-bagged and disposed of in accordance with local regulations.29

Laboratory Biosafety

Work with viable hantaviruses requires BSL-3 containment (BSL-4 for high-consequence strains in some national frameworks). Initial diagnostic processing of clinical specimens can be handled at BSL-2 with enhanced respiratory precautions (BSL-2+). All viral propagation, animal inoculations, and PRNT assays require BSL-3 facilities.31,66

Prognosis and Long-term Sequelae

Overall prognosis in hantavirus disease depends critically on the causative species, the clinical syndrome, and the availability of advanced supportive care. Hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome carries an overall CFR of less than 0.5% for Puumala nephropathia epidemica, 1–3% for SEOV HFRS, and 5–15% for severe Hantaan and Dobrava HFRS. In centers with CRRT and modern ICU support, Hantaan HFRS mortality has declined from 10 to 15% in the pre-modern era to approximately 2–5% [21]. Approximately 30% of severe HFRS patients have residual proteinuria or mildly reduced GFR at 12 months, and a small proportion (<5%) develop end-stage renal disease requiring chronic dialysis.43

Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome carries significantly worse prognosis, with overall CFRs of 36–40% for SNV in the USA (largely unchanged over three decades), 25–35% for ANDV in South America, and up to 72% for Castelo dos Sonhos virus in Brazil.27,28 In centers with established ECMO programs for severe HCPS, survival rates approach 60–75%.64 Pulmonary function, assessed spirometrically, is typically normal within 3–6 months; neurological sequelae including cognitive impairment and post-traumatic stress disorder have been documented following HCPS-related prolonged ICU stays.46

Future Research Perspectives

Several areas represent critical priorities for hantavirus research. First, the development and evaluation of specific antiviral therapies — particularly favipiravir and anti-Gn/Gc monoclonal antibodies—in rigorous randomised clinical trials is urgently needed. Second, next-generation vaccine platforms (mRNA, VLP, and adenoviral vector) targeting both Old World and New World hantaviruses offer the realistic prospect of licensable, broadly protective vaccines within the next decade.62 Third, point-of-care molecular diagnostics—isothermal amplification assays such as RT-LAMP—adapted for low-resource settings could dramatically reduce diagnostic delays in endemic regions. Fourth, the mechanisms underlying ANDV person-to-person transmission require elucidation; comparative genomics of respiratory secretions from ANDV vs SNV-infected individuals may reveal the viral or host determinants of transmissibility. Fifth, climate-driven shifts in reservoir rodent ecology will require updated predictive modelling frameworks integrating satellite remote sensing, climate projections, and rodent population surveillance to anticipate future epidemics.20 Finally, One Health surveillance frameworks that integrate animal, environmental, and human health data streams at the human-wildlife interface are essential for early warning of emerging hantavirus threats.50

Conclusion

Hantaviruses are globally significant, re-emerging zoonotic pathogens responsible for two severe and often deadly clinical syndromes—HFRS and HCPS—with a combined annual burden exceeding 2,00,000 cases. The past decade has brought important advances in understanding hantavirus biology, expanding genomic diversity, climate-linked epidemic risk, and the unique epidemiological threat posed by ANDV person-to-person transmission. Diagnosis depends on RT-PCR in the 1st week and IgM serology thereafter, with PRNT providing species-specific serological confirmation in reference laboratories. Treatment remains primarily supportive: Ribavirin has demonstrated mortality benefit in HFRS but not HCPS; ECMO has transformed survival in severe HCPS. Promising therapeutic advances—favipiravir, monoclonal antibodies, and next-generation vaccines—are progressing through the development pipeline.

For the clinician, vigilance is the single most important factor: Hantavirus must be considered in any patient with a compatible febrile syndrome and a potential rodent exposure, whether in an endemic rural region or an urban laboratory environment.70,71 Early diagnosis, timely transfer to centers with ECMO capability for severe HCPS, early ribavirin initiation for HFRS, appropriate infection control, and rodent exposure prevention remain the pillars of optimal management.72,73 Increasing global interconnectedness, climate change, and ongoing habitat disruption make it certain that hantaviruses will remain a persistent and evolving threat to human health—one that demands sustained research investment and clinical preparedness.
