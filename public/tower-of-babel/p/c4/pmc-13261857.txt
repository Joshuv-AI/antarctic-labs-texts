Sinus Node Dysfunction in Takotsubo Syndrome
Authors: Jorge Salamanca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: arrhythmias, bradyarrhythmia, sinus node dysfunction, Takotsubo

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is currently recognized as a distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of acute cardiac syndromes, characterized by transient left ventricular regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMA) extending beyond a single coronary territory in the absence of culprit coronary lesions.1 Although its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, the hallmark of the condition is the reversibility of RWMA, which has traditionally led to its perception as a benign, self-limited, and fully reversible disorder.

However, accumulating evidence suggests that this paradigm may be overly simplistic. TTS carries significant short- and long-term risks, and several studies have shown that its pathophysiological components follow heterogeneous temporal trajectories.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 On the one hand, dynamic abnormalities such as RWMA, alterations in cardiac repolarization (eg, QT prolongation and negative T waves), and myocardial edema tend to improve and resolve over time.1,2 On the other hand, several alterations, including metabolic disturbances, persistent microvascular dysfunction, and subtle abnormalities in systolic function, may extend beyond the normalization of RWMA.3, 4, 5 This dissociation between mechanical recovery and underlying persistent abnormalities provides an important conceptual framework for a more nuanced understanding of TTS and may help explain the sustained risk of adverse events during follow-up.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Zakynthinos et al6 describe a patient who presented with acute dyspnea, a preceding episode of chest pain, and atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, ultimately diagnosed with TTS complicated by severe sinus node dysfunction (SND) requiring permanent pacemaker implantation despite complete recovery of left ventricular RWMA. Although TTS predominantly affects postmenopausal women, the present case involves a male patient, an uncommon but clinically relevant phenotype, as men tend to present with more comorbidities, physical triggers, and a higher risk profile despite limited evidence of major differences in arrhythmic manifestations.7 Beyond its apparent rarity, this case provides several important lessons for clinicians managing patients with TTS.

Arrhythmic complications are a well-recognized component of TTS and may occur in up to a quarter of cases.8,9 In contemporary series, serious arrhythmias have been reported in ∼5% to 10% of hospitalized patients, including ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation in ∼3% to 8%, high-degree atrioventricular block in ∼2% to 3%, and SND in ∼1%.8,9 Ventricular arrhythmias, particularly in the context of QT prolongation, represent the most extensively studied manifestation and typically arise during the subacute phase, when repolarization abnormalities peak.8 Their prognostic relevance is clinically meaningful: in apical TTS, ventricular arrhythmic complications occur in up to 11% of patients and contribute substantially to in-hospital mortality.8

Beyond ventricular arrhythmias, TTS encompasses a broader and more heterogeneous arrhythmic spectrum. Atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter have been described in ∼5% to 15% of patients and are increasingly recognized as markers of clinical vulnerability rather than incidental findings.8, 9, 10 Indeed, atrial arrhythmias have been associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes in TTS.11

Bradyarrhythmias, including atrioventricular block and SND, are less frequent but clinically relevant and may be under-recognized in routine practice.8,9 Notably, they remain less well studied than ventricular arrhythmias, with limited data regarding their incidence, mechanisms, and optimal management.

Importantly, the mechanisms underlying arrhythmias in TTS are complex and multifactorial.1,8 From a pathophysiological perspective, these processes converge to create a dynamic substrate for electrical instability (Figure 1). Catecholamine excess and heightened sympathetic activity promote repolarization abnormalities and triggered activity, while systemic and myocardial inflammation further modulate ion channel function, contributing to action potential prolongation.1 In later phases, shifts in autonomic balance, including enhanced parasympathetic activity, may further influence the cardiac conduction system.8 In parallel, myocardial edema may increase electrical heterogeneity, and microvascular dysfunction may induce transient ischemia at the cellular level.1,2,4 In addition, a preexisting arrhythmogenic substrate may render certain patients more susceptible to both bradyarrhythmic and tachyarrhythmic events in the setting of TTS. Together, these mechanisms generate a transient but evolving arrhythmogenic substrate whose effects on the pacemaking and conduction system may persist beyond the acute phase rather than resolving abruptly.

Mechanisms of Arrhythmogenesis in Takotsubo Syndrome

Schematic representation of the multifactorial mechanisms contributing to arrhythmias in Takotsubo syndrome. A central stress-triggered neurohormonal surge, characterized by sympathetic overactivation with release of catecholamines and neuropeptides, interacts with autonomic imbalance (including enhanced parasympathetic activity in later phases), microvascular dysfunction, and systemic and myocardial inflammation. These processes modulate cardiac ion channels and promote intracellular calcium overload, leading to action potential prolongation and electrical instability. In parallel, myocardial edema increases electrical heterogeneity, while a preexisting arrhythmogenic substrate may further predispose selected patients to both bradyarrhythmic and tachyarrhythmic events. Collectively, these mechanisms generate a dynamic and evolving arrhythmogenic substrate that may persist beyond the acute phase of ventricular dysfunction.

Within this context, the case presented by Zakynthinos et al6 is particularly informative. The persistence of SND despite normalization of ventricular function suggests that electrical recovery may lag behind mechanical recovery or follow a distinct trajectory. This observation aligns with emerging evidence that TTS is not uniformly reversible at all levels of myocardial organization but rather involves a spectrum of transient and persistent alterations affecting contractile, metabolic, microvascular, and electrophysiological domains.

Clinically, this has important implications. Current management strategies in TTS are largely empirical and guided by the expectation of reversibility. According to American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society recommendations,12 in potentially reversible bradycardia, management should prioritize correction of reversible causes and a period of observation before considering permanent pacing, without a predefined duration. In practice, the appropriate length of observation is highly dependent on clinical context, including hemodynamic tolerance and arrhythmic burden, and no specific recommendations exist for TTS.12

In this setting, a conservative approach is generally appropriate, including close observation and temporary pacing only when clinically indicated. Importantly, reassessment of sinus node function should be structured and may include symptom-rhythm correlation, bedside functional maneuvers, pharmacological testing (eg, atropine or β-agonists such as isoproterenol to assess chronotropic response), and, in selected cases, electrophysiological evaluation, although this is rarely performed solely for sinus node assessment. A pragmatic strategy is to defer definitive decisions until the acute phase has resolved—after recovery of RWMA and stabilization of repolarization—given the dynamic nature of TTS.

The present case underscores that recovery of conduction system function cannot be assumed. Absence of sinus node recovery after 14 days of observation following RWMA recovery and persistence at 20-day follow-up suggest that sustained dysfunction beyond the acute phase may reasonably justify consideration of permanent pacing. Contemporary registry data further indicate that device therapy is not infrequent in this context and, notably, that permanent pacemaker implantation is more common than implantable cardioverter-defibrillator use, underscoring the clinical relevance of bradyarrhythmias in TTS.9 In such scenarios, careful longitudinal assessment is essential to guide decision making, avoiding unnecessary or futile device implantation while ensuring timely treatment in patients who may benefit from lifesaving therapy.

More broadly, this case reinforces the need for comprehensive rhythm surveillance in TTS, extending in some cases beyond the acute phase and even beyond the normalization of ventricular function. It also highlights a critical gap in current knowledge: the lack of robust data to inform risk stratification and management of pacemaking and conduction disturbances in this population. Future studies should aim to better define the incidence, predictors, and temporal evolution of different bradyarrhythmias in TTS, as well as their long-term clinical implications.

In summary, although the reversibility of RWMA remains a cornerstone of TTS, it should not be equated with complete biological recovery. Electrical disturbances, including abnormalities of the cardiac pacemaking and conduction system, may persist beyond apparent mechanical normalization. Recognizing this dissociation is essential to refine clinical management and to further advance our understanding of TTS as a complex and multifaceted syndrome.
