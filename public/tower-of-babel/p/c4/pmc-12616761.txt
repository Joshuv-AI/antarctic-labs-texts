Effectiveness of an Online Training Program on Brief Tobacco Intervention (BTI) for Nurses: A Quasi‐Experimental Study. The E‐Learning BTI Project
Authors: Antonio Jesús Ramos‐Morcillo, Maria Ruzafa‐Martinez, Nani Granero‐Moya, Cesar Leal‐Costa, Serafín Fernández‐Salazar, Ana Teresa García‐Moral
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSmoking is the leading cause of preventable deaths. The training of professionals on brief tobacco interventions (BTIs) increases the effectiveness of these interventions.ObjectiveTo assess the effectiveness of an online training program on BTI based on the 5As and 5Rs model in acquiring anti‐tobacco brief advice competencies among nurses.MethodQuasi‐experimental study with a pre‐test and post‐test design, with a control group and without random assignment. In the experimental group (EG), online training was provided in three sections: BTI theoretical content and methodology, clinical scenario videos, and feedback. Each scenario assessed the 5As and 5Rs as a validated instrument (BTI‐Prof(C)). The control group (CG) only assessed the three videos of clinical scenarios. In both groups, competence was measured at the following points in time: T0 (before the training), T1 (at the end of the training), and T2 (after 90 days). The efficacy of the intervention was measured through a two‐way ANOVA, and the variation rate was calculated from T0 to T1 and from T0 to T2.Results236 nurses participated (157 EG; 79 CG). The mean age was 42.9 years, and 76.7% were women. There was a significant group*time interaction in the three cases, indicating that the online BTI training increases the competence of these professionals in clinical scenario 1 (F = 10.210; p ≤ 0.001; η 2 = 0.081), clinical scenario 2 (F = 6.235; p = 0.002; η 2 = 0.051), and clinical scenario 3 (F = 11.271; p ≤ 0.001; η 2 = 0.090).ConclusionA brief, asynchronous, and online intervention using standardized video‐based cases is effective in improving nurses' BTI competence. This type of training can be a useful option for the National Health System as part of a global and continuous strategy for nurses to perform BTI.Clinical RelevanceAn asynchronous online training program provides nurses with standardized, evidence‐based tools to implement brief tobacco interventions in routine care, offering a scalable and practical solution to strengthen preventive strategies in health systems.Keywords: 5A 5R, brief advice, competence, nurses, online learning, smoking, tobacco cessation

Introduction

According to the WHO (World Health Organization 2021), smoking is the leading cause of avoidable deaths worldwide. Each year, it causes the death of 8.7 million individuals and leads to the appearance of avoidable diseases in millions of individuals, both smokers and those who are exposed to second‐hand smoke. Also, it is estimated that the consequences of smoking can result in a worldwide cost of 1.4 billion US dollars each year.

The consumption of tobacco is associated with a broad set of illnesses whose treatment has a very high personal, social, and economic cost. These include not only the well‐known respiratory system diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or lung cancer, but also liver, colon, bladder, mouth/throat, esophagus, cervix, kidney, larynx, and pancreas cancer. In addition, we also find cardiovascular diseases such as myocardial infarction, angina, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and peripheral arterial disease. Chronic diseases like diabetes mellitus and infectious diseases such as tuberculosis are affected by tobacco use (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2014).

Specifically for Spain, the public healthcare system offers partial support for smoking cessation, covering certain medications under specific conditions. Smoking is banned in most public spaces, sales are restricted to individuals aged 18 and above through authorized outlets, and advertising is prohibited. For several years, the National Health System has been delivering training on tobacco cessation to healthcare professionals across both primary and hospital care contexts. The prevalence of individuals who consume tobacco in Spain has progressively decreased in the last four decades, but despite this, it is still found in 28.2% of men and 20.8% of women (Villalbí et al. 2019). Although the data point to a decreasing trend in tobacco consumption, especially in more developed countries (Dai et al. 2022), the burden it implies to health worldwide makes it necessary to increase efforts and continue designing actions destined for the eradication of smoking and the severe consequences derived from it. In this sense, the WHO (World Health Organization 2008) launched the MPOWER strategy, a set of measures to reverse the tobacco epidemic, which includes a series of policies and interventions, among which we find offering help for quitting smoking, especially through brief tobacco interventions (BTI), for which the strategy of the 5As and 5Rs is recommended (World Health Organization 2014). Specifically, this model defines BTI as advice for smoking cessation that is regularly offered during routine clinical consultations, both when patients seek help for quitting smoking and when they do not, for at least 10 min. The 5 A's model (Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange) constitutes a structured clinical protocol for brief tobacco cessation interventions, encompassing the process from smoker identification to follow‐up. In parallel, the 5 R's model (Relevance, Risks, Rewards, Roadblocks, Repetition) offers motivational strategies targeted at individuals not yet ready to quit, emphasizing the personalized discussion of risks and benefits.

The training of health professionals is a key element in addressing the problem of smoking in patients. Many studies have pointed out that professionals perceive it as a facilitating element (Keyworth et al. 2020; Tildy et al. 2023) or acknowledge their lack of knowledge as a barrier when planning to help users abandon smoking habits (Meijer et al. 2019). Many studies have shown that the training of professionals in smoking cessation is important for increasing the number and quality of the interventions performed by health professionals. Concerning the effectiveness of different training interventions, a systematic review concluded that health professionals who had received training were more likely to perform tasks to provide help to those who wanted to quit smoking, as compared to those who had not received it. Among the tasks they performed, we found advising smokers, asking to set a date to quit smoking, or providing self‐help materials (Carson et al. 2012). Another systematic review (Lindson et al. 2021) conducted to understand the effectiveness of interventions designed to promote the abandonment of tobacco in primary care found clues that the training of professionals increases the provision of support for quitting smoking. In hospital nurses, a training intervention was assessed (Boni et al. 2022), which obtained a positive effect, increasing the frequency of actions directed at the dishabituation of smoking implemented by the nursing team.

Knowing what type of training best prepares and supports healthcare professionals to provide competent and effective care to smokers is essential for successful smoking cessation. Presently, given the importance of new technologies in the continuous education of health professionals, knowing the effectiveness of online training strategies on this type of intervention is a key aspect that has been studied at the European level (Girvalaki et al. 2020). Also, the literature review suggests that digital education is at least as efficient as habitual learning to improve health professionals' knowledge and skills in administering interventions to stop smoking. However, the limitations of these studies make it difficult to interpret the conclusions cautiously (Semwal et al. 2019).

The research conducted until the present has been mainly centered on assessing the effectiveness of face‐to‐face training strategies (Duffy et al. 2016; Sheffer et al. 2011; Taniguchi et al. 2022) or mixed strategies (Boni et al. 2022; Tamí‐Maury et al. 2023) and has been performed with different health professionals, obtaining favorable results in general. Most of these studies, independently of the type of strategy utilized, showed some methodological weaknesses, such as the use of self‐reported questionnaires to measure the effectiveness of the intervention and the lack of a control group (Ye et al. 2018). In addition, it must also be mentioned that the literature on the subject underlines the importance of conducting studies that describe the content of the training provided, detailing the education method and the strategies and theories utilized to train the professionals so that the interventions can be reproduced in clinical practice (Carson et al. 2012).

As of the completion of this study, studies on the training of health professionals in smoking cessation strategies in an exclusively online format and their methodological quality are insufficient to propose conclusive recommendations (Martínez et al. 2019; Moeteke et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2018). There is limited evidence from studies involving nurses that employ an online methodology and are based on the 5As model for the design and evaluation of interventions (Bialous et al. 2020; Mizuno et al. 2022; Sarna et al. 2016, 2018). Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the effectiveness of online training on BTI, based on the 5As and 5Rs model, in the acquisition of anti‐tobacco brief advice competence among nurses.

Methods

Research Design

A quasi‐experimental study was conducted using a pre‐test and post‐test design, with a control group to which participants were nonrandomly assigned to both the experimental and control groups.

Hypothesis

Hypothesis: Online training based on the 5As and 5Rs model significantly improves nurses' competence in delivering brief tobacco cessation advice compared to no training.

Settings and Sample

The study population consisted of nurses working throughout Spain at the time of the research. To be included in the study, nurses had to meet the inclusion criteria: working in the National Health System, in both primary care and hospital care, with at least 1 year of clinical experience. Enrolling in another class on an intervention for smoking cessation during the study was considered an exclusion criterion.

The calculation of the sample size (Bausell and Li 2002) was performed for a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) design (factor between subjects: intervention with BTI, yes or no, intra‐subject factor: repeated measures). For the calculation of the sample size, concerning a study of equivalence of means between the two groups (control and intervention), assuming a standard deviation of 5 and that the maximum difference between the groups for them to be considered equivalent is 2 points in the questionnaire score, with a power of 80% and a two‐tailed confidence level of 90%, 78 subjects would be needed for each group. In this case, a 2:1 ratio has been chosen for participant allocation to allow a greater proportion of nurses to receive the training, which resulted in 156 participants in the experimental group and 78 participants in the control group. To compensate for potential losses, the calculated sample size was increased by 30%, leading to a final required sample of 203 participants in the experimental group and 101 participants in the control group, for a total of 304 participants. This decision increases the generalizability of the results by including a larger number of participants in the intervention group. It maximizes the resources available for training, ensuring that more nurses directly benefit from the educational intervention.

Intentional sampling was performed through social networks between July and December 2022 to select the sample. The assignment to the groups was not random. The experimental group (EG) consisted of nurses who had volunteered to participate in the online training, and the control group (CG) comprised nurses who did not participate in the training but agreed to participate in the study.

Intervention in the Groups

The EG took part in a free online training course accredited by the University of Murcia (Spain). The course was conducted through a dedicated online platform and lasted 10 h. Participants had a maximum of 7 days to complete the training, and they could distribute the hours based on their availability. With teaching and clinical experience in brief smoking cessation interventions, the research team developed the content.

The platform included three sections: (1) BTI theoretical contents and methodology, (2) clinical scenarios, and (3) feedback to the student.

Section 1 included the theoretical contents, which provided the epidemiology, current context of smoking, clinical approach to smoking, and effectiveness of smoking cessation therapies.

In section 2, the nurses had access to three clinical scenarios of standardized patients recorded in the video, which showed three habitual situations that may be encountered in clinical practice, including patients in different phases of change: contemplation and preparation for action. These scenarios could be watched as many times as desired, and the student had control of the progress bar. Each clinical scenario was evaluated with the BTI‐Prof(C) tool (Ramos‐Morcillo et al. 2022). This instrument evaluates, with 23 dichotomous answers (yes/no) items, each of the aspects of the 5As and 5Rs model. The website provided the option of answering the items while the video was being watched.

Lastly, section 3 provided feedback for each clinical scenario. The video reflected on what was occurring in each of the most significant aspects of the 5As and 5Rs and indicated their correct and incorrect aspects.

In addition to providing their sociodemographic and employment variables, the CG evaluated the same three clinical scenarios using the BTI‐Prof(C) in an online platform created exclusively for this task.

In both groups, the platform managed the deadlines and reminders for the measurement: T0 (before the training), T1 (at the end of the training), and T2 (90 days after the training).

Variables and Data Collection Instrument

Sociodemographic and Employment Variables

The following variables were collected: Age, sex, Master's degree, Doctorate, and nursing specialty. Work environment (Hospital Care [HC], Primary Care [PC]); rural/urban environment (Rural, Urban [> 50,000 inhabitants], Urban [< 50,000 inhabitants]), hours of previous training in smoking cessation, years of overall professional experience in HC and PC, type of contract (temporary, interim, permanent).

Main Outcome

Score in BTI competence measured with the BTI‐Prof(C) tool (Ramos‐Morcillo et al. 2022). It is an instrument that assesses the performance of healthcare professionals in BTI and identifies areas for improvement. The BTI‐Prof(C) is a criterion‐referenced tool developed to evaluate competence in Brief Tobacco Intervention (BTI) among primary care physicians and nurses, adapted from the validated BTI‐St(C) for nursing students (Ramos‐Morcillo et al. 2019). A central step in its validation was the development of three video‐based clinical scenarios that simulated real primary care encounters with patients either willing or unwilling to quit smoking. The scenarios were designed through a Delphi process involving six experts from medicine, nursing, and psychology, each with more than 10 years of clinical experience. Scripts were written and independently assessed by the experts, with content validity indexes (CVI) ranging from 0.80 to 0.87 in the first round. After two rounds of refinement, full consensus was reached, achieving CVI = 1 for all three scenarios. The scenarios were recorded in an actual health center, ensuring gender balance in the roles and limiting duration to under 3 min. A pilot study with 11 primary care professionals confirmed their face validity, rating them as highly comprehensible, realistic, technically sound, and feasible for online use. Subsequently, 155 professionals completed the BTI‐Prof(C) after viewing each scenario. Internal consistency was strong across the three cases (KR‐20 = 0.826–0.880), and test–retest reliability demonstrated high temporal stability (ICC = 0.829–0.869).

The BTI‐Prof(C) comprises 23 dichotomous items organized according to the 5As and 5Rs model and has adequate validity and reliability. The professionals must watch a clinical scenario with a standardized patient recorded in a video and answer whether the brief smoking cessation intervention criteria are met, as defined in the questionnaire. The answers from the nurses are corrected according to the gold standard pattern defined in the validation of the scenarios; the score range is 0 “no competencies” to 23 “maximum competencies.” Access to the tool and the scenarios are available in the validation publications (Ramos‐Morcillo et al. 2019, 2022).

Data Collection Procedure

All nursing professionals were invited to participate voluntarily, and a consecutive sampling procedure was conducted until the target sample size was achieved. Participants were informed about the training through institutional email communications directed to healthcare management and professional nursing associations, complemented by a targeted social media campaign (Twitter and Facebook), and encouraged to disseminate information informally among healthcare professionals potentially interested in participating. The web platform anonymously recorded the first day of the class, including both groups' socio‐demographic and employment variables, before allowing access to the contents. Three measurements were performed in both groups: T0, T1, and T2. The data were collected from September 2022 to April 2023. All participants were provided with complete information regarding the ethical considerations of the study. This research has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the province of Jaén (ID: 31052018). Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained and electronically recorded through the training web platform in accordance with established research protocols.

Data Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed through the calculation of percentages and means, and a bivariate analysis (chi‐square, Student's t‐test, and ANOVA) to discover the homogeneity between the experimental and control groups in the sociodemographic and employment variables studied. The efficacy of the intervention was analyzed through a two‐way ANOVA, with an intra‐subject factor (T0, T1, and T2) and an inter‐subject factor (online training: yes/no).

The rate of change was calculated from T0 to T1 and from T0 to T2 to discover the true increase in competence in brief smoking cessation interventions in each group. This rate was calculated by applying the following formula: Rate of change after the class T1−T0T0×100, and Rate of change after 3 months T2−T0T0×100 (Daniel and Cross 2019). The statistical analysis researcher was blinded to the participants' group membership. A significance level of 5% (p ≤ 0.05) was utilized in the statistical analysis. The data were analyzed using the SPSS v.26.0 program.

Results

Sample Description

Initially, 210 nurses were enrolled in the BTI training, designated as the experimental group (EG), and 108 agreed to participate as the control group. In the second round, 11 nurses (5.2%) from the EG and 6 (7.4%) from the CG did not respond to the questionnaire. Finally, at the third evaluation, 90 days after the training, 157 nurses participated in the EG and 79 in the CG, meaning that 42 nurses (21%) in the EG and 23 (22.5%) in the CG did not respond. Table 1 shows the descriptive data of the sample and the comparison between groups. No significant differences were found between the EG and the CG in the sociodemographic and employment variables analyzed. The mean age of the sample was 42.9 years, and 76.7% of the participants were women. Half of the participants held a Master's degree, and 5.5% had a PhD. The mean professional experience was 18.4 years. Also, 87.7% provided care in an urban context.

Effect of the Intervention

The results showed a significant group*time interaction in the three scenarios analyzed, indicating that the online BTI training increased the competence of the professionals in clinical scenario 1 (F = 10.210; p ≤ 0.001; η 2 = 0.081), clinical scenario 2 (F = 6.235; p = 0.002; η 2 = 0.051) and clinical scenario 3 (F = 11.271; p ≤ 0.001; η 2 = 0.090) (Figure 1).

Line graphs of changes in three BTI measurements across the experimental and control groups in the three scenarios.

Table 2 shows the mean scores of the participants in the three scenarios and the three measurements. The within‐subjects analysis showed that statistically significant changes were produced in the EG in the three scenarios, while no changes were observed in the CG. A statistically significant increase is observed in scenario 1, from the initial moment (T0) to after the training (T1), showing an 18% increase in EG competence. This increase remains statistically significant 3 months after T0, although it is reduced to 12.60% (T2). A similar result was observed in scenario 3, with a statistically significant increase in the competence of nurses who participated in the training. This increase was 9.2% between T0 and T1, while after 3 months, the increase was approximately 5%. Lastly, scenario 2 shows a statistically significant increase from T0 to T1, with a percentage of 7.3%, although a significant difference was not observed between T0 and T2.

Discussion

The objective of the study was to determine the effectiveness of an online training activity based on the 5As and 5Rs model. In addition, it had an asynchronous format and was at the student's own pace, which included videos of simulations with standardized patients in the acquisition of BTI competencies of nurses. The study included 236 nurses with a mean age of 42.9 years and a mean professional experience of 18.4 years who worked in hospitals and primary care. The sociodemographic factors and professional experience were similar in both groups compared. The results show a statistically significant increase in the BTI competence of nurses who had taken part in the training right after it had ended, which was reduced in all three scenarios evaluated after 3 months, although the competence score was still greater and statistically significant compared to the initial moment in scenarios 1 and 3.

Despite the differences in the teaching methodology and the evaluation tool used in other studies conducted with nurses, our findings are consistent with them, as they show that face‐to‐face training interventions lasting 1–6 h can have positive effects after 3 months (Matten et al. 2011; Sarna et al. 2014; Taniguchi et al. 2022), with these effects maintained up to 12–15 months after the training (Matten et al. 2011; Sarna et al. 2014). Other studies with nurses from different geographical areas and diverse clinical contexts in which the training was performed exclusively online and the effectiveness was assessed through the 5As model also found positive results in 3 months (Bialous et al. 2020; Mizuno et al. 2022; Sarna et al. 2016). Similar results have also been found in the exclusively‐online training of primary care physicians (Moeteke et al. 2024), and, in general, among health professionals in the hospital setting (Martínez et al. 2019).

The increase in the competence of the trained professionals was moderate‐low, with a maximum value of 18% in scenario one and a minimum value of 7% in scenario 2. It must be taken into consideration that in measurement T0, before the start of the intervention, the scores were very high (a mean of 19.30 points out of 23 possible), which limits the range of improvement of the intervention. This could be related to the profile of the professionals who participated, of which more than 60% had previous training, perhaps due to the care and training that have taken place in Spain concerning smoking (Ministerio de Sanidad. Dirección General de Salud Pública y Equidad en Salud 2024). A possible explanation for the lack of significance in T2 is that each case had its own design and a different difficulty level.

Our study has many strengths that increase the robustness of the findings obtained. In the first place, as opposed to most of the studies conducted with nurses, which utilize a pre‐post design that is inadequate for performing causal inferences (Bialous et al. 2020; Mizuno et al. 2022; Sarna et al. 2016), our case used a design with a control group that showed characteristics very similar to the experimental group. In the second place, the training was exclusively online, allowing the participants to access and watch the content at their own pace. This type of exclusively online training shows a higher acceptance and adherence by professionals compared to mixed or only face‐to‐face learning (Boni et al. 2022). In third place, it uses video‐based learning with standardized patients, an aspect that has been shown to increase the flexibility, availability, and personalized pace of the learning, as it provides the possibility of stopping, starting, and moving the time bar of the video to cater to more specific needs and to improve the self‐efficacy of the nurses concerning BTI (Kalampakorn et al. 2023). The video‐based scenarios provided a coherent, realistic, and measurable learning experience, which has a very positive effect on learning nursing skills (Clerkin et al. 2022).

Lastly, the intervention performed was evaluated with a tool (BTI‐Prof(C)) that was validated by nursing and medical professionals. It utilizes a criterion‐based test, which allows for evaluating education interventions and verifying if competencies were obtained for certification (Martínez Arias et al. 2014). This type of evaluation, as opposed to diverse self‐perception tools that are widely utilized in the studies conducted until now (Barta and Stacy 2005; Kalampakorn et al. 2023; Matten et al. 2011; Mizuno et al. 2022; Taniguchi et al. 2022), measures the correct answer and the errors of students in an objective and standardized manner, concerning the actions of the professional in a simulated clinical scenario. The scarce availability of standardized tools brings an important limitation when specifically comparing the effectiveness of the learning of this type of training and the use of tools such as BTI‐Prof(C), which will allow the comparative analysis with other studies.

These findings have important implications for managers and policymakers, without overlooking the fundamental role of nursing managers in facilitating training, integrating it into clinical practice, and prioritizing tobacco cessation within their respective healthcare settings. It is known that nursing professionals best apply the care needed to cease smoking and also have the fewest barriers (Meijer et al. 2019). Thus, the addition of education strategies, such as the one studied, at the level of National Health, offered to all professionals in a Massive Open Online Course (MOOC) format that is permanently open and that includes an adequate variety of validated clinical cases, may be the strategy with the greatest reach in the provision of BTI by clinical nurses. In addition, an online format that is asynchronous and allows the professionals to manage their own time will facilitate the continuous support by health systems that, in the last instance, will facilitate the sustainability of the changes achieved (Bialous et al. 2017; Semwal et al. 2019). Likewise, a recommendation is made for the inclusion of automated tests for the evaluation of BTI training in these courses so that health professionals obtain immediate feedback concerning their learning (Kalampakorn et al. 2023).

Study Limitations

The intervention performed was dependent on participants watching all the online content, but it was not possible to determine the training time dedicated by the students, although these were real training conditions for professionals. The participating nurses chose whether or not to take part in the training, which could introduce a self‐selection bias. However, comparison of the main socio‐demographic and employment characteristics showed that the groups were homogeneous. Additionally, a higher representation of women and nurses from urban areas was observed, which may influence the scores; however, this is consistent with the typical distribution of nurses in Spain. On the other hand, the present study only monitored the learning of BTI competencies, although the study did not provide direct proof that the training truly increased the frequency and quality of these interventions in clinical practice. Further research is necessary to provide additional evidence of this aspect. Another potential limitation is that a high number of nurses who participated in the study had previous training on BTI, so it would be important for new studies to evaluate the impact of this training on nurses without this BTI training or in other countries with different health services.

Conclusions

The present study shows that an online, brief, and asynchronous intervention using standardized patients is effective for increasing the BTI competence of nurses. If they are given adequate training and tools, they can be prepared to play a significant role in the efforts against smoking. This type of training can be a useful and interesting option for the National Health System as part of a global and continuous strategy that promotes nurses to improve their competence in BTI.
