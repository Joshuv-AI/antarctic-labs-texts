Polyclonal antibodies and probiotics improve immune dysfunction in a mouse model of inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Sirous Ghaedi, Zohreh Harsij, Maryam Dianati Rizi, Mehrdad Rezaei, Fatemeh Moein, Zahra Mirasadi, Nima Tanhaeivash, Nazanin Jamshidi, Mohammad Hossein Nasr-Esfahani, Mehdi Hajian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic digestive disorder caused by the overactivity of the mucosal immune system in response to natural intestinal bacteria and environmental factors, affecting genetically susceptible individuals. This research investigates the preventive effect of probiotics (P), specifically Lactobacillus fermentum, Lactobacillus L. reuteri, and polyclonal antibodies (AB), on morphological changes and markers of intestinal inflammation in the IBD mice model. Our study spanned 54 days. For the first 21 days, we observed microbial populations and immunoglobulin levels. Then, we induced chronic inflammation over three 11-day cycles. In each cycle, subjects received 2% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) for 4 days, followed by 7 days of normal water. 75 C57BL/6 mice have been used in 5 groups: DSS, control, DSS + P, DSS + AB, and a combined treatment group of antibodies and probiotics (DSS + ABP). Throughout the study period, clinical symptoms were monitored, and morphological changes and histological scores were assessed after the course. Levels of IBD biomarkers in serum such as nitrite, malondialdehyde enzyme (MDA), and myeloperoxidase enzyme (MPO), were measured along with alterations in cytokines Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-4, IL-6, and IL-22, IgA and IgG in the blood serum. Results showed that All treatment groups, significantly reduced the Disease Activity Index (DAI), total histological score and oxidative stress markers MDA and MPO compared to the DSS group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the oral administration of polyclonal antibodies increased mucosal immunoglobulin levels, helping to prevent the rise in serum immunoglobulin levels and the associated increase in IL-4 (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, treatment with probiotics significantly (p < 0.05) reduced inflammation by modulating cytokine levels (TNF-α and IL-6). In conclusion, the co-administration of probiotics and polyclonal antibodies effectively mitigates IBD progression by synergistically improving histological parameters and regulating systemic immune responses, offering a more potent preventive strategy than individual treatments.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic and reversible illness that can affect the digestive system. Numerous studies have been conducted on IBD, but its causative factors aren’t yet fully identified1. According to the report by Altab et al. 2020, approximately 3.9 million women and 3 million men are affected by IBD. The rate of disease growth is increasing in areas that previously had a low frequency. This disease imposes a significant cost on healthcare systems and treatment in many countries2,3. Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) are the two main types of IBD that are well-recognized. The clinical course of both diseases is identical, but the differences are due to the location and nature of inflammation4.

Researchers have extensively examined the significance of environmental, genetic, and microbial factors in the development of IBD. Among these factors, several ecological elements have been identified as crucial in increasing the risk of IBD5. Alterations in the makeup of the intestinal microbiota play a significant role in the progression of IBD. When the integrity of the intestinal epithelium is compromised, more microorganisms can infiltrate the gut, triggering an exaggerated immune response. Moreover, when normally harmonious bacteria become dominant, this imbalance shifts the microbiome toward dysbiosis6.

The human gut has over 500 species of microbes that offer various benefits to the host. Antibiotics, prebiotics, and probiotics can alter the composition of gut microflora. Probiotic groups help improve the balance of intestinal microbes, inhibit the growth of harmful bacteria, and regulate the passage of food antigens. Additionally, they stimulate the immune responses of the host7. Lactobacillus species are extensively used in probiotic groups in diverse food items globally. This group plays a crucial role in food fermentation. It can be detected in varying quantities in the gastrointestinal systems of both humans and animals, depending on factors such as species, host age, and gut location8. Lactobacilli have health benefits for the gastrointestinal and genitourinary systems. They can prevent and manage intestinal infections, diarrhea, and inflammatory bowel disease.

Lactobacillus L. reuteri, a species of Lactobacillus, is a natural component of the intestinal microflora that can protect against IBD by modulating the intestinal microbial environment and the immune system9. It produces various metabolites, including organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, bacteriocins, and other antagonistic substances10. In a study in mice, L. reuteri was found to reduce inflammation in the intestinal mucosa by decreasing the levels of pro-inflammatory markers such as Myeloperoxidase (MPO), IL-1β, and IL-6 and preventing thinning of the mucosa caused by dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)11. L. reuteri inhibits various pathogens and has anti-inflammatory effects in a mouse model of DSS-induced colitis when given preventively12.

L. fermentum was deemed a safe probiotic strain by the European Food Safety Authority in 2009, as it was added to the default list of qualified safety strains13. Furthermore, evidence indicates the significant contribution of L. fermentum in mitigating inflammation-associated conditions such as colitis, respiratory tract infections, and hepatic injury14. Recent research has validated the interaction of L. fermentum with macrophages, dendritic cells, and hepatocytes, thereby engaging in the modulation of various inflammatory cytokines15. This intervention reduces the inflammatory response and modulates several mediators linked to colitis. Observations indicate that it favorably alters the levels of key mediators, including PGE2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, TNF-α, and IFN-γ16,17. Colitis resulted in elevated IL-10 levels in colon tissue. Meanwhile, treatment with probiotics modulated several cytokines, including IL-1, IL-4, and IL-5, potentially reducing inflammation18.

Natural antibodies (NAbs) constitute a primary line of defense for establishing immune tolerance, providing protection, and maintaining immune homeostasis within the gastrointestinal tract19–21. As a fundamental component of the innate immune system, these molecules provide immediate, pre-existing defense characterized by their polyreactivity and non-specific recognition of a broad spectrum of antigens19,21. Primarily produced by B-1 cells, NAbs facilitate mucosal stability by recognizing microbial flora and clearing apoptotic cell debris, which effectively suppresses pro-inflammatory responses20–22. Immunoglobulin Y (IgY), the predominant polyclonal antibody in egg yolk, serves as a viable alternative to these mammalian natural antibodies. Although oral administration leads to partial denaturation and cleavage into Fc, Fab, and F(ab’)2 fragments by gastric acid and proteases, these components retain their antigen-binding and neutralizing capabilities within the gut.

Therefore, this research aims to evaluate the independent and synergistic preventive effects of the probiotic combination of L. fermentum and L. reuteri and egg yolk-derived polyclonal antibodies (IgY) in C57BL/6 mice with DSS-induced IBD. The study specifically investigates the combined and individual treatments by aassessing the following types of markers: clinical markers, morphological and histological markers, oxidative stress markers, and immunological markers. Overall, the present study aims to evaluate the potential of the treatments in preventing the progression of the disease.

Materials and methods

Ethics and animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board Royan Institute, Tehran, Iran (IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1400.098). All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines established by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Royan Institute. Also, the manuscript follows the recommendations in the ARRIVE guidelines.

Probiotic and antibody preparation

L. fermentum (1638 PTCC) and L. reuteri (1655 PTCC) were obtained from the Persian Type Culture Collection (PTCC) and cultivated in 5 ml of MRS broth for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 3500 g for 20 min, then washed three times with fresh phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in PBS. For the probiotic treatment, fresh bacterial cultures were prepared daily, and the gavage solution was made with a final concentration of approximately 1 × 109 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml, dissolved in 150 µl of PBS for each bacterium23. Egg yolk polyclonal antibody (IgY) was obtained according to the method in previous study and after freeze-drying process, the final preparation process involved the addition of 25 mg of polyclonal antibody powder, derived from egg yolk via freeze-drying, into each gram of feed in polyclonal antibody treatments (active feed) and in the other groups antibodies was inactivated with autoclaving (121 °C, 15 min) process24. This feed was prepared by Royan Biotechnologist Immuno company.

Experimental animal model

Seventy-five female C57BL/6 mice were provided ad libitum access to a normal feed and water, and all experimental procedures were carried out according to guidelines set by the Animal Care Council. After adaptation period, mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 15 per group), control (mice received normal feed with inactive antibody), DSS (mice received normal feed with inactive antibody and colitis was induced by oral administration of DSS), DSS + P (mice received normal feed with inactive antibody and bacterial suspension with gavage and also colitis was induced by oral administration of DSS), DSS + AB (mice received active feed and colitis was induced by oral administration of DSS) and DSS + ABP (mice received active feed and bacterial suspension with gavage and also colitis was induced by oral administration of DSS). To induce a variety of treatments involving antibodies and bacterial suspension, the animals were given oral feed and gavage bacterial treatments over 21 days. Subsequently, for the chronic DSS-induced colitis, mice underwent 3 cycles of DSS for 4 days, followed by 7 days of drinking water between each cycle. Body weight, fecal consistency, and fecal bleeding were recorded daily during the DSS and recovery cycles. Evaluating colitis induced by DSS involved using the DAI score. This index is calculated by adding the scores for body weight loss, stool consistency, and fecal bleeding, then dividing the sum by 3. In this study, a DAI score of zero was assigned to the control group, with a DAI score of 1.5 or more indicating the presence of colitis disease. In this study, a mixture of 75 mg/kg of ketamine (10%) and 2.5 mg/kg of xylazine (2%) was administered via intraperitoneal injection for general anesthesia in mice weighing between 25 and 30 g. After the induction of anesthesia, the mice were humanely sacrificed by complete blood removal from the heart. Death was confirmed through the cessation of both cardiac and respiratory functions.

Morphological evidence and the histological characteristics

On the 54th day of the study, the mice were sacrificed. Then, the mice were dissected, and the weight and length of the large intestine, small intestine, and spleen were recorded. The distal part of the colon samples was prepared for histological examination. The resected segments were opened lengthwise, embedded in paraffin, fixed, and sectioned. Hematoxylin–eosin staining was performed. Briefly, the degree of colon inflammation was scored using light microscopy to look for signs of the extent of damage to the mucosal architecture (0, normal; 3, maximal damage), the goblet cells depletion (0, absent; 1, present), the thickness of muscle tissue (0, normal; 3, maximal thickness), presence or absence of crypt abscesses (0, absent; 1, present) and cellular infiltration (0, normal; 3, maximal infiltration). The degree of colon inflammation was scored using a total histological score. This index is calculated in each case, a numerical score is given with a maximum score of 11 for all criteria25.

Oxidative stress biomarkers (malondialdehyde (MDA), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and nitrite)

MDA levels were measured as a representative of lipid peroxidation using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method (Nalondi™-Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit-MDA). The MDA levels were reported as nmol/mg. MPO enzyme activity was assessed by the commercial kit NampoxTM, and the MPO levels were reported as U/mg. Nitrite levels were measured in serum as a proxy of NO using the Griess method, and the results were expressed in ng/ml (Navand Salamat, commercial kit). All testing procedures were performed following the respective company’s instructions. Samples from nine animals in each study group were used for these experiments.

TNF-α, IL-4, IL-6 and IL-22 measurement

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-4, IL-6, and IL-22 were purchased from R&D Systems (Bio-Techne Co.). Cytokines were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

IgG and IgA assay

The quantification of IgG levels was performed utilizing the Mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) ELISA Kit supplied by CUSABIO, in conjunction with the Mouse immunoglobulin A (IgA) ELISA Kit from the same manufacturer. Measurement of optical absorption was observed at 450 nm and created a standard curve to calculate IgG and IgA concentrations.

Statistical analysis of data

The means and standard errors (SE) for all data were displayed. Prior to conducting parametric analysis, the normality of the data distribution and the homogeneity of variances were confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests, respectively. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to quantitatively analyze the data with SPSS software. Graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism (version 6.0.1). Tukey’s tests were used to identify statistical differences in group means and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Understanding, recognizing, and addressing the risk factors associated with IBD can lead to more effective preventive strategies. Since there is currently no definitive cure for IBD, managing these risk factors can help to lessen the overall impact of the disease26.

Alterations in clinical symptoms, such as weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding, are quantified through the disease activity index (DAI)27. which simultaneously evaluates various several histological and morphological parameters, as well as biomarkers of oxidative stress and inflammation for the assessment of the extent of disease and the effectiveness of the therapeutic regimen28.

In the present study, the observed reduction in Disease Activity Index (DAI) scores across all treatment groups was primarily characterized by attenuated weight loss and significant improvements in fecal consistency and rectal bleeding, particularly within the DSS + AB and DSS + ABP cohorts. These clinical improvements were closely mirrored by a substantial modulation of inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers; while the untreated DSS group exhibited the highest levels of myeloperoxidase (MPO), malondialdehyde (MDA), and nitric oxide, therapeutic interventions—most notably the DSS + ABP treatment—demonstrated a significant enhancement in reducing these markers and restoring mucosal homeostasis. Following our results regarding probiotic effects, Jang and his colleagues observed similar weight changes in their study upon treatment with L. fermentum and L. reuteri29. Furthermore, previous studies indicate that feeding antibodies from dried sheep serum can improve growth, increase the weight of digestive organs, improve intestinal structure, and reduce intestinal inflammation and oxidative stress in mice)30–32. Consistently, our findings show that the histological and morphological parameters of treatment groups (especially the DSS + ABP) significantly ameliorated compared to the DSS group.

In accordance with research works on the dynamic role of natural antibodies33. the oral administration of polyclonal antibodies can effectively mimic the functional properties of polyreactive naturally secreted immunoglobulins from B1 cells, thus enhancing the level of immunity through various innate-like mechanisms. Specifically, these antibodies provide a first line of defense by low-affinity binding to interact with a broad bacterial and viral antigens33,34. Such interactions prevent bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) from binding to the mucosa effectively reducing bacterial adhesion—while simultaneously facilitating pathogen opsonization and the recruitment of the complement system. These processes lead to microbial lysis or enhanced macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, creating a vital opportunity for the host to mount a highly specific adaptive immune response33,34. Additionally, concerning the synergistic activity of polyclonal antibodies and probiotics, these studies suggest that orally administered immunoglobulins upregulate mucin gene expression, including Muc2, Muc3, and Muc435. In line with this, supplements containing probiotics upregulate mucin gene expression and inhibit the adherence of Escherichia coli to the intestinal epithelial surface, as reported by Mack et al.36.

Gouni-Berthold et al. demonstrated that alterations in the concentration of immunoglobulins in the bloodstream are recognized as significant clinical characteristics of IBD37. In alignment with our result, Song et al. demonstrated a correlation between serum IgA and IgG levels and disease activity in patients with Crohn’s disease38. However, in patients with active ulcerative colitis, the serum IgA level was significantly lower than that in patients with less active disease. And also, no significant differences in serum concentrations of total IgG, IgG3, IgG4, and IgM were found between groups of patients with ulcerative colitis but, the differences observed in IgG1, IgG2, and IgA concentrations37.

Balan indicated that, in the jejunum, ileum and plasma, rats fed with dried immunoglobulin produced higher concentrations of secretory IgA (sIgA) than rats fed inactive immunoglobulin with basal diet, whereas, his results in IgG concentration were in line with our finding in antibody treatment (DSS + AB), he observed that Rats fed the dried immunoglobulin supplemented diet had lower plasma IgG concentrations than rats fed either the inactive immunoglobulin diet with basal diet. In conclusion, an ovine Ig fraction selectively modulated various indices of immune function39.

Torrallardona et al. showed that serum Ig may provide passive antimicrobial protection and also from subclinical infection by exclusion of opportunistic pathogens and also help in preventing mucosal damage40(as we observed in the histological result of this study). The gut is a battleground for beneficial and harmful microbes and toxins. If pathogens are suppressed by Ig, there may be a possibility for the growth of beneficial microbes. A possible explanation for this could be that oral feeding of the immunoglobulin supplement may remove pathogens or microorganisms in the GIT, resulting in fewer pathogenic antigens being transported across the gut to blood vessels, resulting in a lower production of plasma IgG and IgA. Furthermore, our findings in the present study show that this lower stimulation of the B cells to react and release the immunoglobulin is the reason for the lower concentration of IL-4 in the antibody treatment group (DSS + AB). Given the fact that IL-4 is capable of proliferating and altering the class of immunoglobulins41.

Our findings also show that oral probiotics ameliorate the IL-4 concentration and reduce the inflammatory cytokine release. Similarly, Sinulingga et al. reported that L. reuteri can effectively augment the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-4, thereby mitigating inflammation in patients suffering from periodontitis42. Research by Parisinos et al. confirmed that IL-6 influences the progression of IBD43. Similarly, Jiang et al. found that L. reuteri can boost IgA production, which helps counteract the effects of the disease by increasing immunoglobulin levels and IL-6 secretion44. Additionally, Algieri et al. reported that administering L. fermentum reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α while increasing mucin production to improve intestinal barrier integrity45. Li et al. proposed that the presence of specific IgY can regulate the immune responses of the intestinal mucosa during an infection caused by S. typhimurium through augmentation of IFN-γ and TNF-α levels46. Yi et al. found that L. fermentum can lower the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, and elevate the levels of IL-4 and IL-10 in the bloodstream of mice47.

IL-22, as a regenerative cytokine, is an essential mediator for inflammation and tissue repair. Sakemi et al. demonstrated a consistent decrease in serum IL-22 levels among individuals with IBD compared to the normal control group. This supports the notion of IL-22 dysfunction in patients with IBD, which aligns with our research findings48. These results suggest a possible explanation that the reduced presence of IL-22 in the bloodstream may be attributed to its heightened utilization at the site of IBD49. One limitation of this study is the absence of control groups receiving water plus probiotics or water plus antibodies; therefore, comparisons between DSS and treatment groups inherently involve two variables (DSS + treatment) versus one variable (water alone). Readers should consider this when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

The findings of our study indicate that the separate or combined administration of probiotics and polyclonal antibodies can effectively prevent the development of IBD and improve clinical, morphological, and histological parameters in the animal model. Notably, the concurrent use of both probiotic and polyclonal antibodies showed superior efficacy compared to their individual use. Furthermore, our observations confirmed that antibody therapy positively influenced the regulation of immune factors.
