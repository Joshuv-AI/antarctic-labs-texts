Apolipoprotein B is considered a potential therapeutic drug target for the treatment of AML
Authors: Qi Meng, Zihao Zhou, Wanchao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) faces significant challenges in the development of novel therapeutic strategies due to drug resistance and disease relapse. Therefore, this study aims to identify new therapeutic targets for AML using a drug-target Mendelian randomization approach.MethodsSummary-level data on low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-related traits were obtained from the GWAS data available on the IEU Open GWAS platform, and AML-associated genetic data were sourced from the GWAS Catalog. Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant hematologic disorder caused by mutations in hematopoietic stem cells and represents the most common type of acute leukemia in adult [1–4]. According to the most recent epidemiological data, the global burden of AML continues to rise. A 2023 study estimated that approximately 20,380 new cases of AML were diagnosed in the United States that year, with nearly 11,310 deaths attributed to the disease [2].Clinically, the standard treatment regimen for AML remains intensive induction chemotherapy, followed by consolidation therapy tailored to the patient’s risk profile or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in high-risk individuals. However, treatment in elderly patients is particularly challenging due to age-related immunosenescence and the presence of multiple comorbidities, which significantly reduce the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy and increase treatment-related toxicities, resulting in poor overall tolerance [2].Although recent advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies have shown promise in subsets of AML patients, current treatment strategies still fail to achieve durable disease control in the majority of cases. Moreover, drug resistance and disease relapse remain major clinical obstacles [3, 5].

Previous studies have shown that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a complex protein network involved in the regulation of cell development, growth, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis. Dysregulation of this pathway has been closely associated with a variety of diseases, including AML [6, 7]. In addition, Apolipoprotein A4 (APOA4), APOB, APOC1, APOE, Cholesteryl Ester Transfer Protein (CETP), and Low-Density Lipoprotein Receptor (LDLR) play critical roles in lipid transport and metabolism, particularly in the synthesis and metabolism of important lipoproteins such as LDL [6–15]. Moreover, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has been shown to enhance AML cell uptake of LDL via LDLR, potentially influencing both AML pathogenesis and drug resistance [16–18]. Therefore, we aimed to conduct a Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, with LDL as the exposure factor and APOA4, APOB, APOC1, APOE, CETP, and LDLR as the targets, to explore their causal relationship with AML as the outcome.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method that utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. Analogous to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), MR is not confounded by environmental factors and thus provides a powerful approach for causal inference [19–24]. This makes MR particularly valuable in situations where traditional RCTs are infeasible, such as in investigating the mechanisms underlying complex diseases. Therefore, this study used a drug-target Mendelian randomization approach to identify potential therapeutic targets, thereby providing new strategies for the treatment of AML.

Drug-target Mendelian randomization analysis

Data acquisition

We further retrieved data on the genes APOA4, APOB, APOC1, APOE, CETP, and LDLR, as well as their respective gene loci, from the official National Library of Medicine website (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/), to conduct the subsequent Drug-Target MR Analysis.

Data related to LDL(ID: ieu-b-110) were obtained from the GWAS-IEU website (gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk), which includes data from 440,546 cases and 12,321,875 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). AML-related data(ID: GCST90042758) were sourced from the official GWAS Catalog (ebi.ac.uk), containing data from 312 European ancestry cases and 456,036 European ancestry controls.

Selection of instrumental variables

The selected instrumental variables (IVs) must meet the three fundamental principles of Mendelian randomization analysis: relevance, independence, and exclusion of confounding [19, 22, 24].First, we selected SNPs significantly associated with LDL from the GWAS summary data as IVs. The screening criteria were set at a P-value of < 5 × 10− 8, with a linkage disequilibrium coefficient (r²) of 0.001 and a linkage disequilibrium block width greater than 10,000 kb to ensure that the IVs were independent of each other. Next, we performed a correlation analysis and retained SNPs with an F-statistic greater than 10 to ensure that the selected IVs were strongly correlated with the exposure (LDL). Finally, we excluded SNPs that were associated with the outcome or potential confounders, and removed palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies during the harmonization of exposure and outcome data [19–21, 23, 24]. Because an insufficient number of SNPs met the conventional genome-wide significance threshold, we applied a relaxed significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ for instrument selection(Please see Supplementary Materials S1 for details.).

Mendelian randomization analysis

In this study, we performed data analysis using the TwoSampleMR package in R software (version 4.4.1). To further minimize confounding biases, we primarily employed the Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) method for causal inference. In addition, we conducted supplementary analyses using MR Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods to assess the robustness of the results [19–21, 23, 24].

Furthermore, to evaluate the heterogeneity of the IVW model, we conducted the Cochran Q test and used the MR Egger method to detect directional pleiotropy and causal effects. We also performed a leave-one-out analysis to assess whether any single SNP strongly influenced the causal relationship between exposure and outcome. Additionally, we applied the MR-PRESSO method to identify and remove potential outliers. The results corresponding to MR-PRESSO are reflected in the leave-one-out analyses and on the y-axis of the forest plots for each MR analysis. To provide a more intuitive assessment of the reliability of the results, we generated funnel plots to detect potential biases or heterogeneity, and scatter plots to visually display the estimated effect sizes and their variation [19–21, 23, 24].

Colocalization analysis

The genomic location of APOB (chromosome 2, positions 21,224,301–21,266,945) was obtained from the PubMed website (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), and colocalization analysis was performed within a ± 50 kb window around this gene region. We retrieved the corresponding LDL identifiers from the eQTL database (eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html) and then located the corresponding datasets in the IEU OpenGWAS project (mrcieu.ac.uk), such as eqtl-a-ENSG00000157978, among others. Colocalization analysis was performed using the locuscomparer and gassocplot packages in the R software to identify potential drug targets [20, 23, 25].

Validation of Mendelian randomization analysis results

To further validate the results of the drug-target Mendelian randomization analysis, APOB was used as the exposure, with the APOB gene itself as the target, to further assess its effect on the risk of AML. We retrieved APOB-related data from the GWAS IEU website, which includes data from 439,214 cases(ID: ieu-b-108), with AML patient data consistent with the previous analysis. The remaining experimental steps followed the same procedure as the drug-target MR analysis.

Selection of instrumental variables

For the six target genes mentioned above, we ultimately retained 12, 29, 46, 45, 13, and 43 SNPs significantly associated with LDL(Please see Supplementary Materials S2-S7 for details.).

Impact of exposure on outcome

The results of the drug-target MR analysis suggested that targeting APOA4, APOC1, APOE, CETP, and LDLR did not significantly affect the prognosis of AML patients. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the Weighted median, IVW, Simple mode, and Weighted mode methods all included 1, with P-values > 0.05, indicating that the effects of targeting these five genes were not statistically significant. Only APOB-mediated LDL metabolism was associated with a reduced risk of AML, showing a statistically significant effect(OR = 0.222267042 [0.089378764 to 0.55273351], P = 0.00121). Except for the MR Egger analysis (P > 0.05), all other methods (Weighted median, IVW, Simple mode, and Weighted mode) yielded P-values < 0.05 (Table 1). Forest plots for APOA4, APOC1, APOE, CETP, and LDLR (Fig. 2A, C, D and E, and 2F) and leave-one-out analysis (Fig. 3A, C, D and E, and 3F) indicated that the 95% CI for the IVW results included 1 and spanned across both sides of the dashed line, indicating that targeting these five genes to modulate LDL levels does not have a significant effect on the risk of AML. In contrast, the forest plots for APOB (Fig. 2B) and the leave-one-out analysis (Fig. 3B) showed that the 95% CI did not include 1 and was positioned to the left of the dashed line, further supporting the conclusion that APOB-mediated LDL metabolism can reduce the risk of AML. This also confirmed the validity of the IVW results. Since the IVW results for APOA4, APOC1, APOE, CETP, and LDLR were not statistically significant, the scatter plots for these genes (Fig. 4A, C, D and E, and 4F) did not show any statistically significant trends. However, the scatter plots for APOB indicated that, except for MR Egger, the lines representing the other four methods showed a downward trend, strongly suggesting that APOB-mediated LDL metabolism can reduce the risk of AML. The funnel plots showed that SNPs for APOA4 (Fig. 5A), APOB (Fig. 5B), APOC1 (Fig. 5C), APOE (Fig. 5D), CETP (Fig. 5E), and LDLR (Fig. 5F) were symmetrically distributed at both ends of the IVW line. Additionally, Moreover, the Cochran’s Q test P values for both the IVW and MR-Egger methods were all > 0.05 (Table 2), indicating no significant heterogeneity in the overall analyses. The Egger intercepts for APOA4, APOB, and CETP were also > 0.05, further suggesting the absence of significant directional pleiotropy or heterogeneity [26]. However, the Egger intercepts for APOC1, APOE, and LDLR were < 0.05, implying the presence of some heterogeneity, which may be attributable to the limited number of instrumental variables or differences in measurements across datasets or laboratories. After applying APOB inhibitors, the OR values for the other four methods, except for MR Egger, were all > 1, with 95% CIs not including 1 (Fig. 6). This indicates that inhibition of APOB-mediated LDL metabolism may increase the risk of AML.

Impact of exposure on outcome

The IVW results showed that APOB can reduce the risk of AML(OR = 0.396318 [0.1992 to 0.7885], P = 0.0084). In this analysis, all methods—Weighted median, IVW, Simple mode, and Weighted mode—yielded P-values < 0.05, except for MR Egger, which showed a P-value > 0.05 (Table 3). These findings demonstrate that the MR analysis results are statistically significant. The forest plots (Fig. 8) and leave-one-out analysis (Fig. 9) further support this conclusion, as the 95% CIs for the IVW method did not include 1 and was positioned to the left of the null line. This provides additional evidence suggesting that APOB may be associated with a reduced risk of AML and further supports the validity of our previous MR findings. The scatter plots (Fig. 10) showed a consistent downward trend in the lines representing the four MR methods other than MR Egger, suggesting the stability and reliability of the MR results. The funnel plots (Fig. 11) demonstrated a symmetrical distribution of SNPs on either side of the vertical line. The Cochran’s Q test P-values for IVW and MR Egger were 0.1380 and 0.1923, respectively, and the Egger-intercept P-value was 0.1197 (Table 4), all of which indicate the absence of significant heterogeneity in the analysis. Furthermore, when simulating the effect of APOB inhibition, the orDrug values from all four methods—except MR Egger—were greater than 1(Table 3), and the 95% CIs did not include 1 (Fig. 12), suggesting that inhibiting APOB itself to reduce its levels may increase the risk of AML.

Discussion

Our experimental results suggest that APOB-mediated LDL metabolism reduces the risk of AML. The results from IVW, Weighted Median, Simple Mode, and Weighted Mode showed P-values < 0.05, with 95% CIs not including 1, indicating strong statistical significance. Furthermore, the Cochran Q test P-values from both IVW and MR Egger were > 0.05, and the Egger-intercept P-value was also > 0.05, suggesting that the results were minimally affected by heterogeneity. The colocalization analysis results suggest that rs13392272 may be a potential functional locus through which APOB-mediated LDL metabolism influences AML. Targeting and activating rs13392272 to enhance this metabolic process may therefore confer a potential therapeutic benefit in AML. Additionally, we validated these MR analysis results, which confirmed that APOB is a protective factor for AML, with rs693 being the potential causal variant between the two.

AML is a malignant disease centered on Leukemic Stem Cells (LSCs), characterized by abnormal expansion of hematopoietic stem cells and impaired differentiation [1, 27–29]. Currently, the treatment of AML faces numerous challenges, particularly issues related to chemotherapy resistance and disease relapse [2, 3, 30, 31]. In recent years, researchers have discovered that LDL plays a critical role in the onset, progression, and chemotherapy resistance of AML. LDL not only serves as a key carrier of cholesterol but also regulates AML cell proliferation, survival, and immune evasion through multiple mechanisms [32–35]. APOB is a major protein synthesized in the liver that participates in the formation of LDL particles, maintenance of their stability, and cholesterol transport. It facilitates the internalization of LDL particles into cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis via interaction with LDLR [6–11, 36]. As a core protein of LDL, APOB’s role in LDL metabolism and its potential to improve AML prognosis through the regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway have increasingly become research hotspots in recent years. In plasma, LDL particles enter cells by binding to LDLR, thereby participating in cholesterol metabolic balance [6, 11, 34, 37]. AML cells are significantly more dependent on LDL than normal cells, especially during the proliferative phase. This is because LDL provides essential cholesterol for AML cells, supporting membrane synthesis, stability, and signal transduction [32, 34, 35, 37]. Moreover, the increased cholesterol content in AML cells can upregulate P-glycoprotein expression, enhancing the efflux of chemotherapy drugs from the cells, leading to chemotherapy resistance. Additionally, in AML cells, LDLR expression is significantly elevated compared to normal cells. The interaction between LDL and LDLR not only facilitates the uptake of cholesterol by cells but also influences the growth and survival of AML cells by modulating various cell signaling pathways [16, 34, 37, 38]. In addition, the study by Su et al. further demonstrated that regulation of oxidative stress–related signaling pathways can significantly influence the sensitivity of AML cells to cytarabine, highlighting the critical role of metabolic and stress-response pathways in therapeutic responses in AML. These findings provide important mechanistic support for the notion that LDL-related or APOB-mediated metabolic pathways may affect AML treatment outcomes by modulating intracellular homeostasis [39]. Beyond leukemic cell biology, treatment-related metabolic and vascular complications may also influence AML outcomes, as exemplified by carfilzomib-induced endothelial injury and microvascular dysfunction reported by Fang et al. In this context, our findings suggest that genetically proxied LDL metabolism may be related to treatment response and post-chemotherapy complications, although our MR analysis does not directly address prognosis [40].

Accumulating evidence from previous studies indicates that LDL uptake in AML is tightly linked to activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These studies have shown that LDL can activate this pathway by binding to its receptor LRP6, thereby promoting AML cell proliferation, survival, and self-renewal capacity [16, 41, 42]. Notably, in LSCs, sustained activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is considered one of the key contributors to AML relapse and chemotherapy resistance [29, 43–45]. For example, LDL has been found to upregulate Protease-Activated Receptor 2(PAR2) expression, which in turn suppresses Dickkopf-1(DKK1)—a known inhibitor of the Wnt pathway—thus enhancing Wnt signaling to regulate cell proliferation and differentiation. These findings suggest that dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway not only sustains the malignant characteristics of AML cells but also supports the self-renewal and survival of LSCs, further contributing to treatment resistance [41]. Therefore, we propose that disrupting the interaction between LDL and Wnt signaling may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming AML progression and drug resistance.

Although previous studies have demonstrated that APOB plays a central role in LDL assembly and mediates cellular lipid uptake through binding to LDLR, our Mendelian randomization results indicate that genetically proxied APOB-mediated LDL metabolism is associated with a reduced risk of AML. This finding appears to be inconsistent with earlier reports suggesting that LDL may promote leukemic cell proliferation. However, this apparent discrepancy is likely attributable to fundamental differences in the biological context captured by genetic instruments versus experimental models. Most prior evidence is derived from in vitro or cellular studies that focus on short-term LDL exposure under artificial conditions, whereas Mendelian randomization reflects the impact of lifelong, systemic modulation of APOB-related lipid metabolism. Moreover, genetic proxies of APOB capture modest and chronic alterations in lipid transport and homeostasis, which may influence immune regulation, membrane dynamics, and the leukemic microenvironment in a manner that differs substantially from the effects of acute or high-dose LDL exposure observed in experimental settings. Therefore, we believe that the protective association observed in our study is more appropriately interpreted as reflecting the long-term physiological role of APOB-mediated lipid metabolism, rather than the direct effects of circulating LDL at the cellular level. In a recent large-scale cohort study involving several hundred thousand participants, higher levels of LDL and APOB were reported to be inversely associated with the risk of hematological malignancies, including AML, suggesting a trend toward reduced AML risk with elevated LDL/APOB levels. APOB itself also exhibited a trend toward a lower risk in statistical analyses, although the association did not reach full significance across all confidence intervals [46]. These findings appear to support the validity of our results. Nevertheless, definitive conclusions regarding the roles of APOB and LDL in the pathogenesis of AML have yet to be established. APOB-mediated LDL metabolism may involve other, as yet unidentified pathways that reduce the risk of AML, which warrants further investigation in future studies. These findings provide hypothesis-generating evidence that lipid-lowering strategies involving APOB inhibition may have complex effects in AML, highlighting the need for careful evaluation in future studies. This strategy has the potential to provide a novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of AML, particularly in improving responses to chemotherapy and reducing drug resistance. Despite the therapeutic potential of targeting LDL levels to suppress the Wnt signaling pathway, there remain significant challenges in clinical translation. Firstly, LDL metabolism involves multiple organs and cell types, and systemic interventions targeting LDL could lead to undesired side effects. Secondly, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays essential roles in normal physiological processes, and its excessive inhibition may impair the function of normal cells. Therefore, precise modulation of these signaling pathways—balancing efficacy with safety—will be a critical focus in future research and clinical application.

With the rapid development of molecular therapies and gene therapy technologies, research on precision treatment for hematopoietic diseases has entered a new stage. Gene therapy approaches, such as the use of engineered lentiviral vectors for β-globin gene addition, have demonstrated both safety and efficacy in patients with β-thalassemia, providing an important paradigm for the clinical translation of genetic modification of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells [47]. As a highly heterogeneous malignancy, AML requires the identification of molecular therapeutic targets beyond classical genetic mutations, encompassing emerging regulatory mechanisms. Jeyachristy et al. reported that N-CoR is specifically degraded by the OSGEP protein in the AML-M5 subtype, suggesting that dysregulation of protein degradation pathways and transcriptional control may represent novel candidate targets [48]. At the same time, increasing attention has been paid to the role of signaling pathway activity in AML prognosis. The study by Márton et al. demonstrated that the phosphorylation status of signaling proteins such as Akt, Erk, and Rb is significantly associated with patient survival, highlighting the importance of signaling-level molecular markers in AML biology and prognostic prediction. In addition to metabolic and genetic factors, alterations in the immune microenvironment are also critical determinants of AML prognosis [49]. The work by Zahran et al. showed that immune dysregulation can lead to substantial heterogeneity in clinical outcomes among AML patients. Specifically, immune dysregulation may modulate leukemic cell survival, therapeutic responsiveness, and disease progression, thereby amplifying or attenuating the effects of underlying genetic and metabolic alterations [50]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the treatment and prognostic assessment of AML should not rely on a single molecule or metabolic pathway, but rather reflect the integrated effects of multiple biological systems. We hope that our study may contribute, at least in part, to improving therapeutic strategies and prognostic evaluation in AML.

Overall, our study utilized drug target MR analysis, which significantly minimizes the influence of confounding factors commonly encountered in traditional clinical cohort studies, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings. However, several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the genetic data used in the analysis were restricted to individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the constructed models and identified therapeutic targets to other populations. Further studies are needed to determine whether these findings are applicable to other populations and ethnic groups. Second, the number of IVs available for MR analysis was relatively limited, which could potentially introduce bias and affect the robustness of the results. Because we applied a relaxed selection threshold when screening instrumental variables, this may have had some impact on the accuracy of the results. Third, our study did not incorporate AML subtype-specific analyses, which may overlook the heterogeneity within AML and the subtype-specific effects of the identified targets. Fourth, because the Mendelian randomization analysis in this study was conducted at the overall AML level using currently available large-scale GWAS summary statistics, these data do not provide sufficient sample size or harmonized datasets to support robust subtype-specific MR analyses. Fifth, although the GWAS summary statistics for the exposures (LDL and APOB) and the outcome (AML) were obtained from independent large-scale consortia, the possibility of minimal sample overlap cannot be entirely excluded when using publicly available data, which may have introduced some degree of bias into the accuracy of the results. Further studies are therefore required to determine whether these findings are applicable to individual AML subtypes.

In conclusion, LDL has been reported to support AML cell growth by serving as a source of cholesterol and to influence cellular proliferation and self-renewal through pathways such as Wnt signaling. In this study, our Mendelian randomization results suggest that genetically proxied APOB-related LDL metabolism is associated with a reduced risk of AML. However, these findings do not imply variant-level causality and should be interpreted at the exposure level under standard MR assumptions. From a clinical perspective, our results raise the hypothesis that lipid-lowering strategies targeting APOB-mediated LDL metabolism may have complex and context-dependent effects in AML. Therefore, the potential impact of APOB inhibition on AML outcomes warrants further investigation rather than direct clinical extrapolation. Future studies incorporating fine-mapping, functional experiments, and prospective clinical data are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to assess the safety and efficacy of such approaches, ultimately enabling more precise and individualized therapeutic strategies for AML patients.
