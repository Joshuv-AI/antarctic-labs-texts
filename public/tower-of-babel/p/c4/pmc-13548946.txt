Defect‐Healed Perovskite Quantum Dots for Optoelectronic Applications in Indoor Photovoltaics and Photoplethysmography
Authors: Seon Joong Kim, Junhyeok Park, Byung Ku Jung, Ohhyun Kwon, Sang Heon Lee, Hyung Jin Choi, Seongyong Hong, Min Jong Lee, Soong Ju Oh, Jae Won Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetal–halide perovskite (Pe) quantum dots (QDs) offer composition‐ and size‐tunable bandgaps, high absorption coefficients, defect tolerance, and solution processability, rendering them compelling photoactive materials for thin film photovoltaics (PVs). Nevertheless, their synthesis and purification introduce traps that lower device performance and reduce stability. In this study, a dispersion‐phase defect‐healing strategy was developed that combines a low loading of lead sulfide (PbS) QDs with PeQDs. In the mixed dispersion, S2− coordinates with the exposed Pb on the PeQDs, while Pb2+ and Br− from the Br‐doped PbS QDs are incorporated into the PeQDs in a manner consistent with vacancy‐related defect passivation. This stabilizes the Pe phase, reduces trap density, and enhances carrier mobility. PeQD PVs achieve a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 15.1% ± 0.2% under 1‐sun illumination and deliver an output power density of 118.8 ± 1.5 µW cm−2 (PCE = 40.9% ± 0.5%) under light‐emitting diode illumination at 6500 K and 1000 lx. PeQD PVs were applied as photodetectors for noncontact reflection‐mode photoplethysmography under low‐intensity reflected light. Unencapsulated devices retained >80% of their initial PCE after 1000 h in ambient air at 30%–40% relative humidity. This approach provides a scalable route for defect control in PeQDs for high‐performance, durable PVs.

Introduction

Metal halide perovskite (Pe) quantum dots (QDs) are promising candidates for next‐generation optoelectronic materials owing to their high absorption coefficients and defect tolerance, solution processability, and compatibility with flexible substrates [1, 2, 3, 4]. These unique characteristics facilitate their widespread application in various fields, such as light‐emitting diodes (LEDs), lasers, sensors, and photovoltaics (PVs) [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In the context of PV devices, PeQDs demonstrate outstanding power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) that currently outperform previously reported QD‐based PVs [3, 11, 13, 14]. At present, surface ligand engineering has been widely used to improve the performance and stability of PeQD PVs, as suitable ligands can protect the ionic lattice and suppress surface vacancies and ion migration [10, 15, 16, 17, 18].

In parallel, indoor PVs have gained strong interest as a practical power source for distributed sensors and Internet of Things nodes operating under ubiquitous low‐intensity artificial lighting [19, 20, 21]. The optimal absorber bandgap under typical indoor LED spectra has been estimated to lie in a wider range of approximately 1.8–2.0 eV [22, 23], which can be closely matched by CsPbI3 PeQDs through bandgap tunability—a strategy that intrinsically suppresses structural defects compared to halide composition tuning [3, 8, 24, 25]. Under these conditions, indoor PeQD PVs can achieve efficiencies competitive with high‐performing bulk Pe devices [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32].

Despite these advantages, the as‐synthesized PeQDs intrinsically contain a high trap‐state density and require mandatory post‐synthetic purification. These purification protocols rely on precipitation‐redispersion, during which polar solvent washing can perturb surface stoichiometry, exposing undercoordinated lead sites and halide vacancies, which lowers the photoluminescence (PL) yield and introduces nonradiative recombination centers [15, 33, 34]. These trap populations broaden subgap absorption, increase the voltage deficit, and facilitate ion migration under light, heat, and humidity, linking surface defect chemistry to both performance loss and instability in PeQD PVs.

Post‐synthetic defect healing has recently been developed to remove or neutralize these defects while preserving the stoichiometry and optical properties of colloidal dispersions. For example, the addition of thiocyanate to CsPbX3 systems restores PL and suppresses blinking [35]. Furthermore, the in situ formation of zwitterionic ligands imparts strong bidentate binding, which stabilizes the nanocrystals against ligand loss and improves their optical stability [36]. Moreover, halide‐supplying reagents and metal‐halide salts, such as trimethylsilyl halides or ZnX2, replenish surface halides and passivate vacancies during post‐synthetic processing [37]. Nevertheless, halide‐rich passivation must be carefully controlled, as excessive halide chemical potential can drive halide redistribution and phase segregation or promote interfacial halide accumulation that increases ionic conductivity or forms electronically resistive interphases, counteracting the benefits of vacancy passivation [38, 39, 40].

The aim of this study is therefore to develop a post‐synthetic defect healing process using Br‐doped lead sulfide (PbS) QDs and CsPbI3 PeQDs to produce high‐performance PeQD PVs. In the proposed system, Br‐doped PbS supplies both S2− and Pb2+: S2− coordinates with exposed Pb sites on the PeQDs and suppresses surface defect formation, while Pb2+ is suggested to diffuse in solution and passivate Pb vacancies in the PeQD lattice. In parallel, Br− from the Br‐doped PbS QDs likely diffuses into the PeQD lattice, consistent with the passivation of internal halide vacancies. It is expected that these coupled processes will stabilize the Pe phase and curb ion migration. Furthermore, comprehensive optical and structural studies are conducted to demonstrate the post‐synthetic defect‐healing process. Subsequently, the device PCE and output power density (P out) are evaluated at AM 1.5G and 1000 lx (6500 K LED). Importantly, the enhanced indoor PV performance suggests improved low‐intensity photoresponse. The PeQD thin‐film devices are also operated in photodetector mode and employed as non‐contact photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors, where an external light source provides illumination. Finally, the ability of unencapsulated cells to retain their initial efficiency after 1000 h at 30%–40% relative humidity (RH) is evaluated.

Results and Discussion

Dispersion‐Phase Defect Healing in PeQD

CsPbI3 PeQDs and PbS QDs possess long‐chain alkyl ligands on their surfaces, which enable their stable dispersion in n‐octane. These QDs were used to prepare two separate dispersions prior to subsequent mixing, as shown in Figure S1. In this work, three types of samples were prepared: (1) neat PeQDs, (2) PeQDs mixed with PbS QDs, and (3) PeQDs mixed with Br‐doped PbS QDs. Throughout this manuscript, these samples are referred to as neat PeQDs, PeQDs‐PbS QDs, and PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br, respectively.

To evaluate the dispersion behavior, colloidal stability, and morphology of the QDs, time‐dependent optical inspection and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were performed over a period of 15 days (Figures 1a and S2–S5). Neat PeQDs initially exhibit a well‐defined cubic morphology with an average size of 10 nm. After 15 days, progressive sedimentation of the neat PeQD dispersion was observed, indicating low colloidal stability. Correspondingly, pronounced agglomeration was observed in TEM images, accompanied by a loss of the cubic morphology, reflecting intrinsically limited structural stability. PbS QDs and Br‐doped PbS QDs exhibit a spherical morphology with an average size of 3 nm that remains unchanged after 15 days (Figure S2), allowing a clear distinction between the two QD components in mixed systems.

(a) TEM images of the PeQDs on day 1 and after 15 days. The insets show the corresponding PeQDs. Scale bars = 20 nm. (b) Pb 4f spectra of the PeQDs and (c) corresponding deconvoluted peak area ratios. (d) XRD patterns of the PeQDs.

In the PeQDs‐PbS QDs system, cubic Pe domains and spherical PbS QD‐rich regions coexist at day 1, indicating the emergence of structural mixing. PbS QDs are observed both as aggregated domains and in direct contact with the surfaces of PeQDs. High‐angle annular dark‐field (HAADF) STEM and elemental mapping confirm the presence of PbS QDs in both configurations (Figures S3a and S3b). Over time, the mixed dispersion remains visually stable without apparent sedimentation, and after 15 days, discrete PbS QD‐rich regions are no longer detected in TEM images, while the cubic morphology of the PeQDs is retained. Elemental mapping further reveals a homogeneous sulfur distribution throughout the PeQDs (Figures S3c and S3d).

In the PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br system, PbS QD‐rich regions are already substantially reduced at day 1, and PbS QDs are predominantly distributed in close association with the surface of PeQDs. This spatial distribution is consistently observed in HAADF‐STEM images and supported by elemental mapping showing extensive overlap between PbS‐derived elements and the Pe phase (Figures S4a and S4b). After 15 days, discrete PbS QD domains are no longer observed, while the cubic morphology of the PeQDs remains preserved. Compared to the pristine PbS QDs‐mixed sample, the Br‐doped system exhibits a more homogeneous distribution of PbS QDs from the early stage (Figures S4c and S4d). High‐magnification images further show that PbS QDs present on the PeQDs surfaces at day 1 are absent after 15 days (Figures S4e and S4f). EDS line‐scan profiles across individual PeQDs were performed on Day 1 and Day 15 (Figure S5). On day 1, the S and Br signals are localized at the PeQD surface, co‐localized with the attached PbS QDs‐Br. After 15 days, the S and Br signals are distributed homogeneously throughout the PeQDs' interior. The homogeneous distribution indicated that S and Br have migrated from the PbS QDs‐Br into the PeQDs in a manner consistent with vacancy‐related defect passivation.

The x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to examine time‐dependent changes in the chemical‐state of Pb2+ and the defect‐passivation induced by the incorporation of PbS QDs (Figures 1b,c). For neat PeQDs at day 1, a distinct low‐binding‐energy Pb 4f component attributed to uncoordinated Pb2+ is observed. This feature is well known as a signature of Pb‐related surface defects originating from halide vacancies and associated with trap states. Quantitative peak deconvolution reveals a contribution of 10.95% from the defect‐related component to the total Pb signal (Figure S6 and Table S1). After 15 days, the relative contribution of uncoordinated Pb2+ decreased to 6.03%. This reduction is attributed primarily to agglomeration‐induced surface area reduction.

In contrast, incorporation of PbS QDs leads to a pronounced suppression of the uncoordinated Pb component at day 1 (7.06% for PeQDs‐PbS QDs and 3.88% for PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br). Notably, the Br‐doped PbS system exhibits the lowest fraction of uncoordinated Pb2+ at the initial stage, demonstrating that defect density is most effectively minimized upon incorporation of Br. The enhanced defect suppression originates from direct chemical interactions at the PeQD surface. Sulfide derived from PbS QDs coordinates with uncoordinated Pb2+, while Br− further contributes to the passivation of residual halide vacancies, likely through Pb‐Br coordination. Such coordination between chalcogenide anions and undercoordinated Pb2+ effectively passivates defects and improves carrier transport in QD/perovskite systems [41]. In addition, in the PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br system, TEM images indicate that PbS QDs are predominately located on the surface of PeQDs rather than forming isolated domains. This spatial distribution suggests that Br incorporation strengthens PbS‐Pe interfacial interactions and thereby enhances defect passivation. As a result of these chemical interactions, the uncoordinated Pb2+ component becomes undetectable in both PbS QD‐containing systems after 15 days within the detection limit of XPS (Figure S6 and Table S1). The apparent shift of the Pb 4f spectral envelope toward higher binding energy originates from changes in the relative weighting of Pb chemical states rather than a rigid core‐level shift [42, 43]. Consistently, S 2p spectra reveal the disappearance of PbS‐related S2− after 15 days (Figure S7), while the remaining S2− signal is characteristic of S2− incorporated within the Pe lattice [44]. Br 3d spectra further confirm the presence of Br in the doped samples already at day 1 (Figure S8).

The x‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis shows that all samples exhibit cubic Pe diffraction peaks at day 1 (Figure 1d). After 15 days, neat PeQDs exhibit the emergence of δ‐phase reflections [45, 46], whereas both PbS QD‐containing systems retain the cubic Pe phase without detectable secondary phases, indicating enhanced phase stability upon incorporation of PbS QDs.

As a control, the intrinsic stability of PbS QDs was examined separately. Independent XPS and XRD analyses confirm that standalone PbS QDs retain their chemical states and crystal structure over the same period (Figures S9 and S10), excluding intrinsic PbS degradation as the origin of the observed structural changes in the mixed systems.

These observations collectively support a sequential adsorption and diffusion mechanism. We propose that the PbS QDs‐Br first adsorb onto the PeQD surface, and their small equilibrium population of ligand‐coordinated surface ions is subsequently captured by vacancy sites continuously generated on the ageing PeQD surface, driving a Le Chatelier‐type ion redistribution into the PeQD lattice over 15 days (Supplementary Note 1) [47]. Importantly, this mechanism does not invoke breakdown of the bulk PbS lattice but operates through the well‐documented surface ion‐equilibrium population of colloidal PbS QDs.

Collectively, the structural, chemical, and crystallographic analyses demonstrate that incorporation of PbS QDs effectively suppresses defect‐related instabilities in PeQDs. The progressive elimination of uncoordinated Pb ions, homogeneous S distribution within the Pe lattice, and retention of the cubic phase together indicate substantial defect reduction achieved through PbS QDs mixing, with additional Br incorporation further enhancing defect passivation.

Optical Characterization of Defect Reduction

To examine changes in optical properties associated with defect reduction induced by PbS QDs mixing, UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy and photoluminescence (PL) measurements were conducted. All PeQDs exhibit absorption spectra in the visible region with a band‐edge feature near 650 nm, corresponding to the optical absorption of CsPbI3 PeQDs (Figure 2a). The absorption peak remains unchanged upon incorporation of PbS QDs and Br‐doped PbS QDs, indicating that the band structure of the PeQDs is preserved despite QD mixing (Figure S10). After 15 days, a pronounced reduction in absorption spectra is observed for neat PeQDs, whereas the absorption spectra of the PbS QDs‐mixed PeQDs are preserved.

UV‐Vis absorption spectra of the PeQDs (a) in the visible spectral region and (b) in the NIR spectral region. (c) PL spectra of the PeQDs. (d) TR‐PL decay profiles monitored at 675 nm.

In the near‐infrared (NIR) spectral region, additional absorption features are observed for the PeQDs‐PbS QDs and PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br (Figure 2b). These NIR spectra originate from the band absorption of PbS QDs, which exhibit absorption peaks at 950 nm for PbS QDs and 930 nm for Br‐doped PbS QDs, respectively, and remain unchanged (Figure S12). With increasing time, a progressive blue shift of the NIR absorption peaks was observed, accompanied by a gradual decrease in absorption intensity, and the corresponding peaks fell below the detection limit after 15 days. Considering quantum confinement effects, this spectral shift suggests a progressive reduction in the effective size of the PbS QDs, consistent with the disappearance of PbS QD domains observed in the TEM analysis.

PL spectroscopy was performed as demonstrated in Figures 2c and S13. The PeQDs exhibited a narrow emission peak centered at 675 nm. For neat PeQDs, the PL intensity decreased monotonically with time and was reduced to 5% of its initial value after 15 days, showing poor stability. In contrast, PeQD‐PbS QDs exhibited substantially enhanced PL stability, exhibiting approximately 112% of the initial PL intensity after 15 days. Furthermore, PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br exhibited a continuous increase in PL intensity with time, reaching approximately 157% of the initial value after 15 days.

Time‐resolved photoluminescence (TR‐PL) measurements were performed by monitoring the PeQDs band emission at 675 nm to elucidate the recombination dynamics (Figure 2d). For neat PeQDs, the average exciton lifetime (τ avg) decreased from 6.89 ns on day 1 to 5.14 ns after 15 days, indicating progressively enhanced nonradiative recombination. In contrast, PeQDs‐PbS QDs exhibited an increase in the τ avg from 7.86 to 10.7 ns over the same period, while a further increase from 8.15 to 11.5 ns was observed for PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br. These trends indicate that PbS QD incorporation suppresses trap‐assisted nonradiative recombination by reducing uncoordinated Pb2+‐related defect states, consistent with the XPS analysis. Additional Br incorporation further enhances this effect by compensating residual halide vacancies, resulting in the most prolonged exciton lifetimes among the samples.

In addition, TR‐PL measurements were conducted by monitoring the emission of PbS QDs at 1050 nm for the PeQD‐PbS QDs and PeQD‐PbS QDs‐Br (Figure S14). For the PeQDs‐PbS QDs, the τ avg decreased from 975 ns on day 1 to 134 ns after 15 days, while a corresponding decrease from 1309 to 150 ns was observed for the PeQDs‐PbS QDs‐Br. The progressive reduction in the τ avg of PbS QDs with time is consistent with the gradual consumption of PbS QD domains, concomitant with the reinforcement of the optical properties of PeQDs.

These observations indicate that PbS QDs mixing suppresses defect‐assisted nonradiative recombination, thereby stabilizing the optical emission of PeQDs, while additional Br incorporation further enhances radiative recombination through effective compensation of halide vacancies.

Evaluation of the PeQD PV Device Performance

To verify property changes at the device level, the performance of the corresponding PeQD PV devices was evaluated. The PV measurement setup is illustrated schematically in Figure 3a, while the device architecture and cross‐sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images are presented in Figures 3b and S15. Additionally, Figure 3c shows the current density‐voltage (J–V) characteristics of the PeQD PV under 1‐sun conditions (air mass 1.5G, AM 1.5G); the corresponding PV parameters are listed in Table 1. The PeQD PV incorporating neat PeQDs exhibited a PCE of 13.1% ± 0.1%, a fill factor (FF) of 70.3% ± 0.5%, a short‐circuit current density (J SC) of 15.5 ± 0.2 mA cm−2, and an open‐circuit voltage (V OC) of 1201 ± 4 mV. In contrast, the device containing both PbS QDs and PeQDs demonstrated a PCE of 14.2% ± 0.2%, FF of 72.2% ± 0.7%, J SC of 15.9 ± 0.2 mA cm−2, and V OC of 1229 ± 7 mV. Concurrently, the PV incorporating Br‐doped PbS QDs and PeQDs exhibited a PCE of 15.1% ± 0.2%, FF of 75.2% ± 0.7%, J SC of 16.1 ± 0.2 mA cm−2, and V OC of 1248 ± 5 mV. These enhancements in V OC, J SC, and FF can be attributed to defect reduction and lattice relaxation in the mixed QD systems, which suppress nonradiative recombination.

Initially, the indoor PeQD PV performance was systematically investigated using LED illumination at 6500 K, with an illuminance of 1000 lx (E v) and an irradiance (E) of 0.290 mW cm−2. The spectral irradiance results for standard and measured indoor light sources with different color temperatures are shown in Figure S16. The PeQD PV incorporating neat PeQDs exhibited a PCE of 32.9% ± 0.3% (P out = 95.7 ± 0.9 µW cm−2), FF of 71.2% ± 0.6%, J SC of 139.7 ± 1.2 µA cm−2, and V OC of 962 ± 7 mV. In contrast, the PV containing mixed PbS QDs and PeQDs demonstrated a PCE of 37.6% ± 0.4% (P out = 109.1 ± 1.2 µW cm−2), FF of 74.6% ± 0.8%, J SC of 147.0 ± 1.1 µA cm−2, and V OC of 994 ± 6 mV. Furthermore, the PV based on PbS QDs‐Br and PeQDs exhibited a PCE of 40.9 ± 0.5% (P out = 118.8 ± 1.5 µW cm−2), FF of 77.9% ± 0.9%, J SC of 151.8 ± 0.9 µA cm−2, and V OC of 1005 ± 6 mV. In accordance with the 1‐sun trend, the PV parameters of the PeQD PVs increased with the degree of interfacial defect reduction and lattice relaxation under LED illumination at 6500 K and 1000 lx. These trends were consistently observed across LED lamps of different color temperatures (2700 K; E v = 1000 lx, E = 0.295 mW cm−2). Under 6500 K LED illumination, the superior PCE is attributed to the favorable spectral overlap between the spectral power distribution of the light source and the absorption window of the PeQD photoactive layer. Figures S17–S19 present the statistical values of the PV parameters for each PeQD PV cell under all light sources. The optimal concentration for PbS QD addition was determined to be 0.01 mg mL−1, and the detailed J–V curves are shown in Figure S20.

The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra were measured to assess photocurrent generation under 1‐sun conditions (Figure 3e). The J SC values obtained by integrating the EQE with the incident spectrum correlated well with the measured J SC values across all conditions. In comparison with the neat PeQD PV, the PbS QD‐containing device exhibited enhanced EQE, with the most significant enhancement being observed near 675 nm. The EQE peak also shifted toward shorter wavelengths, consistent with the trends observed in the absorption spectra. Additionally, to evaluate photocurrent generation under LED illumination, the spectral power distribution of each source was converted to the spectral photon flux density, as shown in Figure 3f. This was then multiplied by the EQE and integrated to yield J SC. The calculated J SC was 10% smaller than the measured J SC due to aperture usage and sidewall edge effects in the PeQD PV devices.

Figure S21 illustrates the J–V hysteresis observed during the forward and reverse scans under each illumination condition, while Figure 3g summarizes the hysteresis indices (HIs) of the devices. A low HI indicates suppression of ionic migration and defect‐assisted nonequilibrium responses in the PeQD film. In the present comparison, HI decreased monotonically from the neat PeQD PV to the PbS QD mixed case and reached a minimum with the Br‐doped PbS QDs. This is consistent with previous reports that have identified ion migration and defect processes as the main contributors to hysteresis in PeQD PVs [26, 51].

Electrical Properties of the PeQD PVs

Subsequently, device‐level analysis was performed to assess the defects in neat PeQDs and their mitigation upon mixing with PbS QDs. Initially, space‐charge‐limited current (SCLC) analysis was conducted, and the electron‐only device is shown in Figure 4a [27, 52, 53]. The trap‐filled limit voltage (V TFL) was identified from the dark J–V characteristics at the transition from the Ohmic regime (J ∝ V) to the trap‐filled regime. NT was calculated using the following equation: NT = 2ε 0 ε r V TFL/(qL2), where ε 0 is the vacuum permittivity, εr is the relative dielectric constant (assumed to be 6.32 for the CsPbI3 PeQD layer) [27, 54], q is the elementary charge, and L is the active layer thickness (confirmed by SEM to be 300 nm). Compared to neat PeQDs, PeQDs mixed with PbS QDs exhibited a lower V TFL, with an even larger reduction being observed upon incorporating Br‐doped PbS QDs, indicating progressively enhanced trap suppression within the photoactive layer. Additionally, the electron mobility (µ e) was obtained from the trap‐free SCLC regime (J ∝ V 2) using the Mott–Gurney equation. The NT and µe values for each device, obtained from SCLC analysis, are summarized in Figure 4b. Relative to the neat PeQDs, µ e increased upon mixing with PbS QDs, although the greatest improvement was observed upon Br‐doped PbS QD incorporation, indicating more efficient charge transport. This enhanced mobility reduced the series resistance (Rs ) and simultaneously improved the FF and J SC.

To precisely determine the spatial distribution of N T within the PeQD layer, drive‐level capacitance profiling (DLCP) was performed at an alternating current (AC) modulation frequency of 20 kHz (Figure 4c) [55, 56, 57, 58]. Compared with neat PeQDs, the PeQD–PbS QD mixture exhibited lower NT throughout the photoactive layer. In particular, incorporation of Br‐doped PbS QDs yielded the lowest NT due to a reduced trap state density caused by fewer defects in the PeQDs. The photoactive layer thickness determined by DLCP (∼300 nm) was consistent with the cross‐sectional SEM results (Figure S15).

To assess the impact of N T on recombination losses, V OC was measured as a function of I L to extract the diode ideality factor (n) (Figure 4d). Plotting V OC against ln(I L) yields a linear relationship, where the slope (m = (kT/q)·n; k, Boltzmann constant; T, absolute temperature; q, elementary charge) gives n. In an ideal device dominated by bimolecular recombination, n approaches 1, whereas larger values suggest trap‐assisted monomolecular loss. The neat PeQD device gave an n value of ∼1.41, indicating increased contribution from trap‐assisted recombination. Mixing with PbS QDs reduced n to ∼1.27, reflecting partial trap suppression, while mixing with Br‐doped PbS QDs further lowered n to ∼1.19, demonstrating effective minimization of V OC losses, primarily through suppression of trap‐induced nonradiative recombination.

To evaluate the impact of recombination on photocurrent generation, J SC was also measured as a function of I L (Figure 4e). In the log–log plot of J SC versus I L, the power‐law exponent α (where J SC ∝ I L α) quantifies the extent of recombination losses [59]. As α approaches 1, trap‐assisted and bimolecular recombination are both minimized. Conversely, smaller α values indicate an increase in pathways that impede carrier collection. Specifically, the PeQD device exhibits an α value of ∼0.94. A mixture of PeQDs and PbS QDs resulted in partial trap suppression and enhanced carrier extraction due to fewer defects within the PeQDs. When incorporating Br‐doped PbS QDs, α approaches 0.98, indicating ideal linearity and confirming that trap suppression within the PeQDs effectively minimizes recombination losses.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed to elucidate the charge transport and recombination processes (Figure 4f) [28, 60]. The resulting Nyquist plot was fitted to an equivalent circuit consisting of the R s, recombination resistance (R rec), and chemical capacitance. In the neat PeQD‐based device, a low R rec combined with a high R s implied a high density of electrically active defects, primarily originating from lead and halide (iodine) vacancies; this promoted nonradiative recombination while impeding charge transport. Upon mixing the PeQDs with PbS QDs, R rec increased while R s decreased, indicating the partial mitigation of vacancy‐related traps and improved carrier extraction. Notably, devices incorporating Br‐doped PbS QDs exhibited the highest R rec and lowest R s, consistent with the more effective vacancy‐related defect passivation of Pb and halide (see Figure 4g), demonstrating effective suppression of trap‐assisted recombination and enhanced charge transport.

Application of the PeQD PVs in a PPG sensor

In addition to energy harvesting, the capability of the PeQD PV devices to operate as photodetectors under low‐intensity illumination was evaluated through PPG measurements (Figure 5). PPG is an optical sensing technique that detects periodic variations in blood volume by monitoring the temporal modulation of light interacting with tissue. In this study, PPG measurements were conducted in a noncontact configuration, enabling signal acquisition without physical attachment of either the light source or the photodetector to the human body. Figure 5a illustrates a schematic overview of the noncontact PPG measurement configuration employed, while a detailed experimental setup is provided in Figure S22. Incident light interacts with human tissue, and the reflected optical signal modulated by pulsatile blood flow is detected by the PeQD PV device. As schematically depicted in the lower panel of Figure 5a, periodic expansion and contraction of arterial blood vessels modulate the intensity of reflected light, producing a time‐dependent electrical signal characteristic of PPG.

(a) Schematic illustration of the noncontact mode PPG measurement setup, in which reflected light is modulated by pulsatile blood flow. (b) Representative PPG signals measured using PeQD PV devices with different photoactive compositions.

The representative time‐dependent PPG signals acquired using PeQD PVs with different photoactive compositions are shown in Figure 5b. The neat PeQD device exhibits weak and irregular signal fluctuations, indicating limited sensitivity to reflected optical modulation under the present measurement conditions. In contrast, PeQD PV devices incorporating PbS QDs exhibit clear periodic oscillations corresponding to cardiac pulsation, demonstrating substantially enhanced photodetection capability. Among these devices, the Br‐doped PbS QDs‐mixed PeQD PV yields the highest signal amplitude and the most distinct periodic features over the measurement duration. This improved PPG response is consistent with reduced trap density and enhanced carrier transport in the PeQD PV devices, resulting in an improved signal‐to‐noise ratio under low‐intensity reflected light.

The PeQD‐PbS QDs‐Br device additionally satisfies the figures of merit of a photodetector, exhibiting a responsivity of 0.328 A W−1 under illumination (Figure S23 and Table S2). Detailed photodetector characterizations, including noise‐equivalent power, specific detectivity, linear dynamic range, rise/decay time, and operational stability, are provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Figure S23.

For comparison, transmission‐mode PPG measurements were conducted with fingers positioned directly above the PeQD PV device (Figure S24). Although pulsatile signals were observed in this configuration, the noncontact operation mitigated direct contact with Pb‐containing materials and permitted remote signal acquisition. These results demonstrate that high performance indoor PeQD PV devices can function as photodetectors for noncontact PPG sensing, extending their applicability to low‐power optical sensing.

Operational Stability of the PeQD PVs

In Pe lattice systems such as PeQDs, the presence of defects reduces the local activation energy at the defect sites. This increases chemical reactivity and substantially compromises stability under various stressors, including oxidative, photoinduced, thermal, and moisture‐induced decomposition [61, 62, 63, 64]. Following the above time‐dependent stability analysis of the PeQDs, the storage stabilities of the corresponding PeQD PV devices were further evaluated to assess how these material‐level degradation processes translate to device‐level performance. As illustrated in Figure 6a, the stabilities of the unencapsulated devices stored under ambient conditions and 30%–40% RH vary with storage time. Specifically, the neat PeQD PV exhibited the lowest stability, whereas mixing with PbS QDs enhanced stability. Incorporating Br‐doped PbS QDs afforded the highest stability, retaining >80% of the initial efficiency after 1000 h. Subsequently, the device stability was evaluated at 50°C under an N2 atmosphere (Figure 6b). The neat PeQD device retained ∼38% of its initial PCE after 1000 h, whereas mixing with PbS QDs improved stability. In particular, the Br‐doped PbS QD device maintained ∼74% of its initial PCE after 1000 h.

Normalized PCE versus time under (a) ambient air and 30%–40% RH and (b) N2 at 50°C. (c) MPPT measurements of normalized PCE versus time for the unencapsulated devices under ambient air and 30%–40% RH.

Maximum power point tracking (MPPT) was performed to evaluate device stability under illumination in air at 30%–40% RH (Figure 6c), demonstrating that photochemical degradation accelerated the decline in device stability. The efficiency of the neat PeQD device dropped to ∼52% of its initial value within 50 h. In contrast, the incorporation of PbS QDs markedly enhanced stability; after 50 h, devices containing undoped PbS QDs retained ∼66% of their initial efficiency, whereas those with Br‐doped PbS QDs retained ∼85%. Collectively, these results demonstrate that defect healing via PbS QD incorporation, particularly with Br‐doped PbS QDs, suppresses moisture‐, heat‐, and light‐induced degradation, markedly improving the storage, thermal, and operational stabilities of PeQD PV devices.

Conclusion

In this study, a dispersion‐phase defect‐healing route for PeQDs was demonstrated by co‐mixing with a small amount of PbS QDs, wherein Br‐doped PbS QDs imparted the strongest effect. In the mixed dispersion, S2− coordinates with the exposed Pb sites on the PeQDs, Pb2 + is incorporated consistent with Pb vacancy passivation, and Br− diffuses into the lattice to occupy the internal halide vacancies, yielding a more stable PeQD phase and cleaner charge transport. Consistent with this, the N T decreased from ∼3.18 × 1015 (neat) to ∼2.87 × 1015 cm−3 (with PbS QDs) and ∼2.3 × 1015 cm−3 (with Br‐doped PbS QDs). Additionally, the electron mobility increased, with the largest gain being observed for the Br‐doped PbS QDs. In the corresponding PeQD PV device, the PCE reached 15.1% ± 0.2% under AM 1.5G illumination. Furthermore, using a 6500 K LED at 1000 lx, P out was 118.8 ± 1.5 µW cm−2 with a PCE of 40.9% ± 0.5%. Beyond energy harvesting, the PeQD PV devices were demonstrated to operate as photodetectors for noncontact photoplethysmography under low‐intensity reflected light. The unencapsulated device retained >80% of its initial efficiency after 1000 h at 30%–40% RH, and ∼74% after 1000 h at 50°C in N2, reflecting durable operation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Br‐doped PbS QD co‐mixing represents a scalable dispersion phase route for defect control in PeQDs.

Experimental Section

Materials

Cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3; 99.9%), lead iodide (PbI2; 99.999%), lead bromide (PbBr2; 99.999%), lead(II) oxide (PbO; 99.999%), 1‐octadecene (ODE; 90% technical grade), oleylamine (OAm; 70% technical grade), oleic acid (OA; 90%), methyl acetate (MeOAc; anhydrous, 99.5%), n‐hexane (anhydrous, 95%), toluene (anhydrous, 99.8%), lead nitrate [Pb(NO3)2; 99.999%], titanium ethoxide (≥97%), bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide lithium salt, 4‐tert‐butylpyridine (4‐tBP; 96%), acetonitrile (anhydrous, 99.8%), and bis(trimethylsilyl) sulfide ((TMS)2S, 98%) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Furthermore, 2,2',7,7'‐tetrakis(N,N‐di‐p‐methoxyphenylamine)‐9,9'‐spirobifluorene (Spiro‐OMeTAD, ≥99.5%) was purchased from Lumtec. Indium tin oxide (ITO, ∼10 Ω·cm−1) substrates were procured from AMG, and Ag (99.99%) was purchased from iTASCO.

Synthesis of the CsPbI3 PeQDs

The CsPbI3 PeQDs were synthesized according to a previously reported method [27], with modifications. Specifically, a cesium oleate solution was prepared by dissolving Cs2CO3 (0.407 g) and OA (1.25 mL) in ODE (20 mL) in a 100 mL three‐neck flask. The mixture was heated under vacuum at 120°C for 1 h, then purged with N2 and heated to 140°C. In another 100 mL three‐neck flask, PbI2 (0.5 g), OA (2.5 mL), OAm (2.5 mL), and ODE (25 mL) were combined, evacuated under vacuum at 120°C for 1 h, purged with N2, and heated to 175°C. At this temperature, an aliquot (2 mL) of the cesium oleate solution was rapidly injected. After 5 s, the flasks were cooled in an ice bath. Subsequently, the resulting crude CsPbI3 PeQD dispersion (15 mL) was mixed with MeOAc (35 mL) and subjected to centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and the CsPbI3 PeQDs were redispersed in n‐hexane (6 mL). This process was repeated using a further portion of MeOAc (10 mL) and centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 3 min. Purified CsPbI3 PeQDs were obtained by discarding the supernatant, centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 3 min, and redispersing in n‐hexane (5 mL). The dispersion was refrigerated at 5°C overnight, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 3 min, filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter, and stored under N2 atmosphere.

Synthesis of PbS QDs

The PbS QDs were synthesized by dissolving PbO (0.9 g) in OA (3.0 mL) and ODE (20.0 mL) in a 100 mL four‐neck flask, followed by degassing for 2 h at 110°C. The solution was then heated to 120°C under an N2 atmosphere. A sulfur precursor was prepared by dissolving (TMS)2S (240 µL) in ODE (8.0 mL), and rapidly injected into the flask. The reaction mixture was then allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The resulting QDs were purified three times by precipitation‐redispersion using acetone and ethanol, and finally dispersed in octane.

Synthesis of Br‐doped PbS QDs

Br precursor was prepared by dissolving PbBr2 (0.037 g) in OAm (10 mL). During the natural cooling step of the PbS QD synthesis described above, the Br precursor (2 mL) was rapidly injected when the reaction mixture reached 80°C. The Br‐doped PbS QDs were subsequently purified following the same procedure used for the PbS QDs.

Fabrication of the CsPbI3 PeQD PV

An Indium tin oxide (ITO) substrate was ultrasonically cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 20 min each, followed by 3 min of O2 plasma treatment. The SnO2 solution was then spin‐coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 200°C for 30 min. The redispersed CsPbI3 PeQD solution (80 mg mL−1 in octane) was spin‐coated at 1000 rpm for 10 s and 2000 rpm for 10 s. The CsPbI3 PeQD thin film was wetted with a Pb(NO3)2 solution (1 mg mL−1 in MeOAc) for 3 s, spin‐dried at 3000 rpm for 10 s, and rinsed with pure MeOAc at 3000 rpm for 10 s. This process was repeated four times to obtain a film with ∼300 nm thickness. For PbS QD mixing, PeQD and Br‐doped PbS QD dispersions in n‐octane were combined at an optimal PbS QD concentration of 0.01 mg mL−1 (Figure S20), and allowed to sit for 15 d before device fabrication. A Spiro‐OMeTAD solution (72.3 mg Spiro‐OMeTAD, 28.8 µL 4‐tBP, 17.5 µL Li TFSI stock solution (520 mg mL−1 in acetonitrile) in 1 mL chlorobenzene) was subsequently spin‐coated onto the device at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, patterned MoO3 (10 nm)/Ag (100 nm) electrodes were deposited on the sample by thermal evaporation under ∼10−7 hPa vacuum. The active area defined by the aperture mask was 0.045 cm2.

PPG measurements

Noncontact PPG measurements were conducted using the PeQD PV devices as photodetectors. Incident light was directed toward human skin without physical contact, and the reflected optical signal was collected by a device positioned at a fixed distance from the measurement site. The generated photocurrent was amplified using a low‐noise current preamplifier (Stanford Research Systems, SR‐570) and recorded using an oscilloscope (DSOX1204G, Keysight). A detailed schematic of the experimental setup is shown in Figure S21.

Characterization

The structural properties were investigated using TEM (Tecnai 20, Thermo Fisher Scientific), XPS (Nexsa, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and XRD (SmartLab, Rigaku). HAADF‐STEM and EDX spectral imaging were performed using a Talos F200X instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The optical properties were analyzed using UV–Vis spectroscopy (Cary 5000, Agilent Technologies) and PL spectroscopy (FP‐8500, JASCO). TR‐PL was performed using a spectrofluorometer (Fluorolog‐3, HORIBA). The J‐V characteristics were examined using a Keithley 2401 source meter (Keithley Instruments, Cleveland, OH, USA), controlled by the K730 program (McScience, Republic of Korea) under a scan rate of 0.2 V s−1, and with bias sweeping from 1.5 to −0.2 V (reverse bias) and −0.2 to 1.5 V (forward bias). To evaluate the multicomponent CsPbI3 PeQD PV cells under different illumination conditions, two light sources were used, namely an AM 1.5G solar simulator (McScience, Republic of Korea) and an LED lamp (McScience K3000, 6500 or 2700 K). The I L was calibrated using a Si reference cell (K801S‐K058, McScience). The EQE spectra of the CsPbI3 PeQD PVs were obtained using a spectral solar cell quantum efficiency measurement system (K3100, McScience). SEM images were recorded using an S‐4300SE instrument (Hitachi). The 2D GIWAXS measurements were performed at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory, Republic of Korea.
