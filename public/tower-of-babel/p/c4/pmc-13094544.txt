Evaluation of the tolerance and effects of 1-deoxynojirimycin on insulin and glucose dynamics in healthy horses and horses at risk for insulin dysregulation
Authors: Canaan Whitfield-Cargile, Michelle Coleman, Kelsey Hart, Daniel Gomes, Londa Berghaus, Kylee Jo Duberstein, Kenzie Ellis, Amanda Tinkle, Roya Shirzad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin dysregulation (ID) is common and diminishes welfare of horses. Current management relies on diet and exercise, with variable responses and limited medical options. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors (AGIs) might provide adjunctive therapy.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo evaluate the tolerance and effects of Reducose®, a Morus alba leaf extract standardized to 5% 1-deoxynojirimycin (DNJ), in healthy horses and horses at risk of ID. Phase I assessed tolerance of escalating oral DNJ doses (0-5000 mg q12h, 7 days each). Phase II evaluated dose-dependent effects on OST response (0-5000 mg q12h, 7 days each). Introduction

Equine metabolic syndrome is a common endocrinopathy characterized by insulin dysregulation (ID)1–3 ID is characterized by disturbances in insulin and glucose regulation, including basal hyperinsulinemia, exaggerated post-prandial insulin responses, delayed insulin clearance, and tissue insulin resistance.2 Dietary management centers on limiting non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) rich feeds such as pasture and high-starch concentrates. Restricting grazing, however, poses logistical challenges, raises welfare concerns, and increases the risk of colic.3,4 Among the clinical consequences of ID, endocrinopathic laminitis is the most devastating, often resulting in chronic pain, loss of use, or euthanasia.3 Hyperinsulinemia is the key driver of this condition, making control of insulin response a critical therapeutic goal.

Dietary modification alone is often insufficient to control hyperinsulinemia and additional pharmacologic therapies might be needed to provide additional control. However, current pharmaceutical options have limitations. Metformin has poor oral bioavailability and inconsistent efficacy.5,6 Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors show promise in horses but have high cost, limited availability, and potential adverse effects, including hypertriglyceridemia.7–9 Thus, there is interest in identifying other strategies to modulate glycemic responses safely and affordably.

α-Glucosidases are brush border enzymes in the small intestine that break down oligosaccharides and disaccharides into monosaccharides.10 α-Glucosidase inhibitors (AGIs) are a class of compounds used in people and companion animals that act locally within the small intestine to inhibit these enzymes.11 By blocking the final step of carbohydrate digestion, AGIs reduce the formation of absorbable monosaccharides, attenuating postprandial glucose absorption and subsequent insulin response. 1-Deoxynojirimycin (DNJ) is a naturally occurring AGI that acts locally in the intestine to block the breakdown of disaccharides and oligosaccharides into absorbable monosaccharides.12,13 In people and laboratory animals, DNJ modulates post-prandial insulin response.14–16 Reducose®, a proprietary M. alba (white mulberry) leaf extract standardized to contain 5% DNJ, is a naturally occurring and reversible AGI.17 While crude mulberry leaf powders are marketed as glucose-lowering supplements, they are poorly characterized and vary widely in composition. In contrast, DNJ (ie, Reducose®) offers a standardized, practical intervention to reduce post-prandial insulin responses in horses. However, the tolerance and metabolic effects of DNJ in horses remain unknown. Therefore, the overarching objective of this study was to evaluate the tolerability and metabolic effects of DNJ in healthy horses and horses at risk of ID. We hypothesized that DNJ would be well tolerated and would reduce post-prandial insulin responses. Specifically, the aims of this study were:

Materials and methods

Horses

This exploratory study was conducted in 3 phases to assess DNJ tolerability, dose-dependent effects, and administration timing in horses. Twenty-seven horses from the University of Georgia research herd were included across all phases. All phases were approved by the University of Georgia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Phase I assessed DNJ tolerance in 6 healthy horses (4 geldings, 2 mares; 7-20 years; 3 Quarter Horses, 1 Warmblood, 1 Thoroughbred/Quarter Horse cross, 1 Pony of the Americas). Horses were considered healthy if they had no history of gastrointestinal or endocrine disease in the previous 12 months, were not receiving medications other than routine anthelmintics and vaccinations, and had normal findings on physical examination, complete blood count (CBC), and biochemistry panel. Horses were maintained as a group on a dry lot where timothy hay from the same cutting (~5 kg twice daily) was provided. They were brought into individual stalls twice daily to consume 1.4 kg of Omolene 100 (Purina Mills, Gray Summit, MO) for approximately 10 minutes before being returned to the dry lot. Bodyweight was measured weekly using a calibrated scale, and body condition score (BCS) assigned on the 9-point Henneke scale.18

Phases II and III examined DNJ’s tolerance and metabolic effects in horses at risk for ID. Horses were considered at risk if they had a prior diagnosis of ID or pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID), a condition commonly associated with ID, within 6 months of enrollment. For these phases, all horses were screened for ID using an OST and for PPID with a thyrotropin-releasing hormone stimulation test (Table S1). All testing and interpretations followed current Equine Endocrinology Group guidelines.19,20

Phase II included 5 horses (4 Quarter Horses, 1 Warmblood; 4 geldings, 1 mare), previously diagnosed with ID but not PPID. With the exception of the previous diagnosis of ID, these horses met the inclusion criteria as in Phase I. Bodyweight using a weight tape and BCS were assessed weekly. Unlike Phase I, the Phase II horses were not managed as a group. Each horse lived and was handled individually according to its usual routine at the facility. Diet, turnout schedules, and exercise varied among horses but were kept consistent within each horse throughout the study. All horses were stalled overnight, and feed was withheld during this period before OSTs while they remained in their usual stalls.

Phase III included 16 horses (8 Quarter Horses, 2 Warmbloods, 2 Haflingers, and 1 each Arabian, Dartmoor, Welsh-cross, and mixed breed; 9 mares, 7 geldings; 8-29 years). Six horses had a previous diagnosis of ID only, 7 had PPID only, and 3 had concurrent ID and PPID. These horses were maintained exclusively on pasture (low-moderate quality mixed late-summer/early-autumn grass; NSC 9%-13% by biweekly sampling). Feed was withheld overnight before OSTs by moving the herd to an adjacent dry lot. All PPID horses received pergolide mesylate (2 μg/kg PO q24h; Boehringer Ingelheim, Duluth, GA) for ≥3 months before and throughout the study. Body weight was measured weekly using a scale, with BCS and cresty neck scores (CNS) assessed by a single trained observer.18,21 Horses were monitored daily by physical examination and visual assessment.

Study design

Phase I assessed DNJ tolerance in healthy adult horses using an escalating dosage regimen over 4 weeks: 0 g in week 1, 500 mg q12h in week 2, 2500 mg q12h in week 3, and 5000 mg q12h in week 4. Each dose was administered for 7 consecutive days by top-dressing concentrate feed, with complete consumption confirmed within 10 min. Horses were monitored twice daily by physical examination for systemic and gastrointestinal effects. On day 7 of each week, additional evaluations included CBC, biochemistry panel, and plasma concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Insulin was measured weekly and processed as described below. After feed was withheld overnight (drylot turnout with evening hay, then no feed until morning), baseline insulin was collected before the DNJ-supplemented meal, and postprandial glucose was measured at 0, 60, and 90 min. Postprandial sampling was limited to glucose, as this phase was designed to assess tolerance rather than endocrine responses.

Phase II evaluated dose-dependent effects of DNJ in at-risk horses using a stepwise dosing protocol. Each horse received 0, 500, 2500, 3750, and 5000 mg PO q12h, with each dosage administered for 7 consecutive days. Dosing was performed as in Phase I. Tolerance assessments on day 7 included CBC, biochemistry panel, and plasma TNF-α and LBP; IL-1β and CRP were not repeated based on Phase I findings. OSTs were performed after feed was withheld overnight on days 1 and 7 of each week. Immediately before each OST, DNJ was administered via syringe in 10 mL water. Plasma glucose and insulin concentrations were measured before and at 60 and 90 min after oral administration of 0.15 mL/kg corn syrup (Karo® Light Corn Syrup, ACH Food Companies, Inc, Oakbrook Terrace, IL), as previously described.2 OSTs responses were averaged to generate a single weekly value per horse. Averaging was selected a priori to reduce within-week biological and sampling variability and to obtain a more stable estimate of each horse’s response to that dosage, consistent with the known variability of OST results in horses.22 The sugar profile of the syrup lots used in this study was confirmed by commercial analysis (Eurofins Nutrition Analysis Center, Des Moines, IA) as 23% total sugar (14% glucose, 9% maltose).

Phase III evaluated the duration of DNJ’s effects in grazing horses at risk for ID using a randomized within-subject study using fixed-sequence, dose-escalation design.23,24 A baseline OST was performed before DNJ administration. Horses then received 2500 mg PO q12h for 28 days, followed by a 2-week washout and then 5000 mg PO q12h for 28 days. OSTs were performed after feed was withheld overnight each week (ie, days 7, 14, 21, 28). Within each dosing phase, horses were randomly assigned to complete an OST at 1 of 4 time points relative to DNJ administration (0, 1, 2, or 3 h post-dose), such that each horse experienced each timing once. DNJ was administered twice daily by top-dressing 0.45 kg of a complete pelleted feed (Senior Formula, Seminole Feed, Ocala, FL), which was offered in individual feeding pans, except on OST mornings, when it was given via syringe dissolved in 10 mL water.

Blood and fecal collection

Blood samples were obtained by jugular venipuncture after alcohol preparation. EDTA tubes were used for CBC, glucose, insulin, and inflammatory markers, and lithium-heparin tubes for the biochemistry panel. Samples were processed as detailed below.

During OSTs, blood was collected at baseline, 60 min, and 90 min into EDTA tubes and immediately placed on ice. Glucose was measured at the time of collection using a handheld glucometer (Precision Xtra, Abbott Laboratories, Abbot Park, IL).25 All samples were centrifuged within 6 h of collection and plasma was aliquoted and stored at -80°C until batch analysis. Plasma insulin concentrations were measured at Cornell University’s Animal Health Diagnostic Center Endocrinology Laboratory using a commercially available radioimmunoassay (RIA; EMD Millipore Corp, Billerica, MA). Insulin and glucose responses during OST were summarized using area under the curve (AUC) and incremental AUC (iAUC), calculated by the trapezoidal method. AUC represents the total area under the concentration-time curve. iAUC represents the area above baseline (time 0). Peak insulin was defined as the highest concentration at 60 or 90 min after Karo® syrup.

During Phases I and II, fecal samples were collected on days 1 and 7 of each week to monitor microbiota changes. Freshly voided feces were used when available; otherwise, rectal collection was performed by a veterinarian, using a gloved and lubricated hand.

Laboratory analyses

Systemic health was assessed though CBC and biochemistry performed at the Clinical Pathology Laboratory at the University of Georgia Veterinary Teaching Hospital. Circulating inflammatory markers including TNF-α and IL-1β (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) and CRP (Abcam, Waltham, MA), were quantified via validated equine ELISA, as previously described.26 Evidence of LPS translocation was assessed by measuring plasma LBP via ELISA (Hycult Biotech, Wayne, PA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. pH on fecal liquid was measured using a pH meter (Accumet Research Model AR25, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburg, PA), as previously described.27 Plasma concentrations of equine resistin were measured with a commercially available ELISA, according to manufacturer instructions (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Carlsbad, CA). All ELISA assays and sample measurements performed in our laboratory were conducted in duplicate.

Data analysis

Health, Endocrinologic, and Inflammatory Markers: All statistical analyses were performed using commercial software (GraphPad Prism v10.4.1). Data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA), assumptions were evaluated by inspecting residual plots; when sphericity was violated, the Geisser–Greenhouse correction was applied. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± SD; non-normal data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Ordinal measures were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Repeated measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) was performed using a within-subjects design, with time and dosage as factors. All horses received each dosage level, allowing both factors to be treated as within-subjects. Post hoc analyses compared each time point to baseline rather than all pairwise combinations, to assess treatment effects while minimizing the risk of Type I error. Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used when comparing each treatment group or dosage level to baseline. Šídák’s correction was applied for analyses involving multiple time points within a given dosage. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Microbiome Data: Fecal microbiota composition (Phases I and II) was assessed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V3-V4 hypervariable region. DNA was extracted from 200 mg of feces using a commercial kit (QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Library preparation and sequencing (250 bp paired-end reads) were performed commercially(MRDNA, Shallow Water, TX) using an Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw sequencing data are available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) BioProject ID PRJNA1283217.

Sequence data were processed using QIIME 2 (version 2024.10).28 Primer trimming was performed using cutadapt, followed by quality filtering and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference the dada2 plugin.29,30 Chimeric sequences were removed using the consensus method. Representative sequences were assigned taxonomy using a Naive Bayes classifier trained against the Genome Taxonomy Database, version 220, at 97% identity.31 A rooted phylogenetic tree was constructed via MAFFT alignment and FastTree.32,33 QIIME2 artifacts were exported for downstream statistical analysis in R (version 4.4.2, Windows 10) using the phyloseq package (v1.48.0).34 Negative control samples were used to identify and remove likely contaminant ASVs using the prevalence-based method implemented in the decontam package (v1.24.0).35 Remaining ASVs were agglomerated to the genus level. Alpha diversity (Shannon index and observed richness) and beta diversity (weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances) were calculated and visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.1).36 Beta diversity was explored via Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), and differences in community composition across groups were tested using PERMANOVA (adonis2, vegan package v2.6-8).37

Results

Phase I: tolerance study

Clinical variables

No adverse effects were observed at any dosage of DNJ. Physical examination findings remained within normal limits throughout the study in all horses. Bloodwork including CBC and biochemistry results remained within reference ranges for all horses at all time points (data not shown). Bodyweights remained stable throughout Phase I, with no differences between week 1 (465 ± 59 kg) and subsequent weeks (week 2: 470 ± 60 kg; week 3: 472 ± 62 kg; week 4: 476 ± 62 kg; all P > .1). BCSs were consistent across all horses (BCS 5 to 6 out of 9) at each time point.

Markers of systemic inflammation

Plasma CRP concentrations did not differ across dosages, with no consistent change from baseline (Table S2). TNF-α was undetectable in one horse; in the remaining horses, concentrations expressed relative to baseline decreased at all DNJ dosages (P = .02-.04; Figure 1A, Table S2).

Gastrointestinal response

Fecal pH remained stable, with no differences from baseline at any dosage (Table S2). In contrast, plasma LBP concentrations decreased at the 5000 mg q12h dosage (mean diff. −3.17 μg/mL, 95% CI, −6.30 to −0.04; P = .04), while lower dosages were not associated with change (Figure 1B, Table S2). Phase II: dose selection in horses at risk for ID

Clinical variables

No adverse effects or changes in physical examination were observed at any dosage of DNJ throughout the duration of the study in any horse. Bloodwork, including CBC and biochemistry results remained within reference ranges for all horses at all time points. Bodyweights remained stable compared baseline bodyweight (514 ± 80 kg at baseline, 526 ± 74 kg at 500 mg q12h, 517 ± 72 kg at 2500 mg q12h, and 503 ± 72 kg at 5000 mg q12h. BCS ranged from 5 to 8 out of 9 at study initiation (1 horse 5/9, 3 horses 6/9, 1 horse 7/9, 1 horse 8/9) and remained unchanged during the study.

Markers of systemic inflammation and gastrointestinal response

A limited evaluation of systemic inflammation and gastrointestinal response was performed in Phase II. TNF-α was below the limit of detection in 2 of 5 horses at all time points. There was a reduction at the 500 mg q12h dosage (P = 0.02) but no differences from baseline at other doses. Plasma LBP concentrations remained stable (Figure S1; Table S3). There were no changes in α diversity, β diversity, or differential taxonomic abundance across dosing intervals (Figure S2, Table S3).

Endocrine response in horses at risk for ID

An OST was performed on days 1 and 7 of each dosage week. The weekly mean response for each horse was used for inferential analysis. After 7 days of DNJ administration, multiple indices of insulin and glucose regulation improved in a dose-dependent fashion, with the largest reductions occurring at higher dosages (Figure 4). Specifically, baseline insulin concentrations were lower than baseline after 7 days at 3750 mg q12h (P = .04) and 5000 mg q12h (P = .01) (Figure 4A). Insulin AUC was reduced at 2500 mg q12h (P = .02), 3750 mg q12h (P = .01), and 5000 mg q12h (P = .02) (Figure 4B). Peak insulin concentrations were also reduced at dosages ≥2500 mg q12h (Figure 4C; P = .02 and 0.01 for 3750 and 5000 mg q12h, respectively). Glucose AUC showed a modest reduction observed only at 5000 mg q12h (P = .05) (Figure 4D). Similar patterns were observed for insulin iAUC, glucose iAUC, and peak glucose (Figure S3, Table S3).

Phase III: Evaluating DNJ effect by dosage and timing

As continuous grazers, horses require sustained modulation of postprandial glucose to effectively regulate insulin. Based on the observed effects of various dosages in Phases I and II, 2 dosages (2500 mg and 5000 mg q12h) were selected to assess the duration of DNJ’s effects and determine whether a higher dosage prolonged its impact on glucose and insulin responses in horses with or at risk for ID.

Clinical variables, inflammation, and gastrointestinal response

BCS remained unchanged throughout the study, with no differences between week 1 and week 4 at either dosage (2500 mg BID: median 6 [IQR 5-7] at both time points; 5000 mg BID: median 7 [IQR 5-7] at both time points). Similarly, CNS scores remained unchanged during the study, with no differences between week 1 and week 4 at either dosage (median 2, IQR 2-4 at all times). Bodyweight did not change over time in the 2500 mg q12h group (P = .86), but at the 5000 mg q12h dosing, there was a modest decrease between week 1 and week 3 (mean difference: −9.4 kg; 95% CI, −13.7 to −5.1; P < .001) and between week 1 and week 4 (mean difference: −8.8 kg; 95% CI, −14.6 to −3.0; P < .001). Only a limited evaluation of systemic responses to DNJ treatment was performed in Phase III due to results in Phases I and II. There was no change in LBP concentrations over time for the 2500 mg q12h dosage (P = .80), but a decrease was observed for the 5000 mg q12h dosage (mean difference: −11.81 ng/mL; 95% CI, −22.13 to −1.50; P = .02).

Endocrine responses

Within each dosing phase, OSTs were performed at the same time DNJ was administered (0 h) or at 1, 2, or 3 h after DNJ administration (1, 2, 3 h, respectively), with each horse completing all time points in randomized order. Insulin AUC was reduced from baseline only when DNJ was administered concurrently with the OST (0 h), both at 2500 mg q12h (P = .02) and 5000 mg q12h (P = .05) (Figure 5A, Table S4A and B). Peak insulin concentrations were also reduced from baseline at 0 h during the 2500 mg q12h period (P < .001), but the effect was more sustained at 5000 mg q12h, with reductions observed at both 0 h (P < .001) and 1 h (P = .02) (Figure 5B, Table S4A and B). Glucose AUC was reduced from baseline only when DNJ was administered concurrently (0 h) at 2500 mg q12h (P = .02), whereas at 5000 mg q12h reductions were observed at all time points (0 h P < .001; 1 h P = .01; 2 h P = .01; 3 h P = .01) (Figure 5C, Table S4A and B). Baseline insulin remained unchanged at 2500 mg q12h, but was reduced after 4 weeks of 5000 mg q12h compared to week 1 (P = .03) (Figure 5D; Table S4A and S4B). Similar patterns were observed for insulin iAUC, glucose iAUC, and peak glucose (Figure S4, Table S4A and B). Resistin concentrations were unchanged at 2500 mg q12h (week 1: 1213 ± 2427 ng/mL; week 4: 1643 ± 3410 ng/mL; P = .77), but were significantly reduced from baseline after 4 weeks of 5000 mg q12h compared to week 1 (week 1: 2121 ± 3330 ng/mL; week 4: 985 ± 2223 ng/mL; P < 0.001). The mean difference at 5000 mg q12h was −1136 ng/mL (95% CI, 17-2255).

Discussion

This study assessed DNJ’s tolerability and metabolic effects in healthy horses and horses at risk for ID. DNJ was well tolerated in all horses, at all doses, with stable clinical, hematologic, biochemical, and gastrointestinal variables. Endocrine responses showed dose-dependent improvements in glucose and insulin dynamics. Resistin, a pro-inflammatory adipokine associated with ID,38 declined with treatment. Taken together, these findings support DNJ as a safe, practical adjunct for managing ID in horses.

Several pharmaceutical AGIs, including acarbose and miglitol, are used in people with type 2 diabetes to manage post-prandial hyperglycemia39,40 and have shown promise in diabetic dogs.41 The most common adverse effects are gastrointestinal disturbances from undigested carbohydrates reaching the hindgut, where they might undergo fermentation and cause bloat, dysbiosis, or mucosal injury.42 This is particularly concerning in horses making tolerability a key focus. Across all phases, DNJ was well tolerated: systemic inflammation markers were unchanged or decreased, fecal microbiota and pH remained stable, and clinical, hematologic, and biochemical analytes showed no adverse effects even at 5000 mg q12h. These findings indicate a favorable safety profile consistent with AGIs in other species.

Metabolic responses to DNJ were dose-dependent in both healthy and ID-risk horses. In Phase I, baseline insulin declined at 2500 and 5000 mg q12h and glucose AUC decreased at the highest dose. In Phase II, improvements were more pronounced, with baseline insulin reduced at ≥2500 mg q12h and insulin AUC, iAUC, and peak concentrations lowered across all doses, maximal at 3750-5000 mg q12h. Glucose AUC declined modestly at 5000 mg q12h, while glucose iAUC improved at all doses, indicating DNJ enhances glycemic control even at lower doses. In Phase III grazing horses, both glucose and insulin AUCs declined at 2500 and 5000 mg q12h, with the higher dose producing more sustained effects across timepoints. Collectively, these findings support DNJ’s dose-dependent attenuation of post-prandial hyperinsulinemia and glycemic peaks. Although DNJ’s known mechanism primarily affects post-prandial carbohydrate digestion, the observed reductions in baseline insulin suggest changes in basal glucose metabolism, such as alteration to endogenous glucose production, insulin-mediated or non-insulin mediated glucose update, or insulin clearance.43 Beyond glycemia, DNJ reduced resistin and LBP after 4 weeks at 5000 mg q12h, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory benefits.

The observed effects are consistent with DNJ’s mechanism of action as an AGI. Laboratory analysis showed that the Karo® syrup used for OSTs contained 14% glucose and 9% maltose, corresponding to a sugar profile of approximately 61% glucose and 39% maltose by weight. The absorption of glucose, a monosaccharide, was likely unaffected, whereas DNJ might have limited maltose breakdown, which could contribute to reduced post-prandial glycemic and insulinemic responses. This mechanism underscores the relevance of carbohydrate composition when evaluating DNJ’s effects. Similar improvement in glycemic responses were seen with both the textured sweet-feed meal (Phase I) and grazing conditions (Phase III), suggesting the metabolic effects of DNJ might extend beyond controlled OST challenges to practical feeding conditions. Importantly, the composition reported here reflects the product used in this study and might not represent all commercial Karo® Light Corn Syrup products.44

DNJ’s mechanism of action, together with feeding practice recommendations for ID horses, introduces important considerations in the context of our study design. Horses with ID are often managed with low-NCS concentrates or continuous access to low-NSC forage. In this study, DNJ was administered only twice daily. To completely attenuate post-prandial insulin responses, more frequent dosing might be required, and the tolerability and safety of such dosing regimens remain unknown. In addition, DNJ inhibits brush-border α-glucosidases primarily affecting the digestion of sucrose and other α-glucans. The glucose and fructose fractions of forage and commercially available low-starch concentrates would be expected to contribute to insulin responses independent of DNJ’s activity. Thus, in ideally managed ID horses the efficacy of DNJ might be diminished. This requires further investigation; however, its use as an adjunct to dietary management might still offer benefit, especially when ideal management is difficult to achieve.

Dose selection for our study was extrapolated from therapeutic dosages in people where oral DNJ doses of 50-100 mg (0.7-1.4 mg/kg) reduce post-prandial glycemia.45 Using body surface area scaling (human Km ≈ 37; horse Km ≈ 25-30), this corresponds to ~ 1-3 mg/kg in horses.46 However, DNJ exerts its glucose-lowering effect by competitively inhibiting brush-border α-glucosidases in the intestinal lumen, making luminal exposure, and therefore intestinal surface area, a more physiologically relevant scaling factor than systemic dose. Human small-intestinal surface area is approximately 30 m2 and horse small-intestinal surface area is approximately 450 m2.47 Using this relationship, human DNJ doses of 50-100 mg correspond to ~1.7-3.3 mg/m2, whereas the 500-5000 mg doses used in horses correspond to ~1.2-12.5 mg/m2. Thus, the horse dose remains within the same order of magnitude when normalized to intestinal surface area. Horse doses equivalent to the human mg/m2 range produced only minimal effects, suggesting that species-specific differences likely necessitate higher luminal DNJ concentrations. The dose-dependent improvements in glycemic control, without adverse effects, support the physiological relevance and tolerability of this range.

Although mulberry leaf powders are widely marketed for glycemic control, their DNJ content is low and highly variable due to plant and processing factors.48 In contrast, Reducose® is a standardized extract that provides consistent levels of DNJ and well-characterized bioactive compounds, including flavonoids and alkaloids, which might contribute antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic benefits.49 Given these differences, the findings of this study should not be extrapolated to other mulberry products.

Comparison to existing therapies further highlights DNJ’s potential role in the management of ID in horses. Metformin, a commonly used pharmacologic agent in horses, has variable efficacy, particularly due to its poor oral bioavailability, rapid systemic clearance, and limited impact on post-prandial insulin responses.50,51 SGLT2 inhibitors, such as velagliflozin and canagliflozin, reduce insulin responses,8,52,53 but raise concerns about long-term safety, with adverse effects including hypertriglyceridemia and polyuria.9 In contrast, DNJ acts locally in the small intestine with minimal absorption, offering a targeted mechanism with potentially fewer systemic risks. Due to the intended local action, the oral bioavailability of this compound was not assessed in this study.

There are several important limitations to this study. First, small sample sizes limit generalizability. All horses were university-owned and located in a single geographic region. While this minimized confounding, it limits extrapolation to horses managed under typical field conditions. Second, this work evaluated short-term tolerability and metabolic effects rather than safety; the study did not evaluate supra-therapeutic dosing or include post-mortem evaluation. Studies in other species support prolonged tolerance,14 but horse-specific research is needed to assess cumulative or long-term effects and delayed outcomes.16 Third, horses in Phases II and III were only mildly ID or considered at risk based on historical diagnoses rather than results of current testing. Given fluctuations in insulin status over time,22 limited repeatability of OST results,54 and the uniformly low-NSC diets of included horses, some horses were not dysregulated at the time of treatment. While this might have attenuated the magnitude of the observed effects, it reflects a clinically relevant target population for a therapeutic supplement. Horses with mild or fluctuating ID remain at risk for progression of ID, particularly when managed with free access to pasture as our Phase III horses were. In such settings, adjunctive therapeutics might provide valuable support. Severely affected ID horses represent the group with the greatest need for pharmacologic intervention, but they are challenging to study. Thus, this work intentionally focused on horses with mild or fluctuating ID to establish tolerability and proof-of concept efficacy, providing an essential foundation for future studies in severely affected horses. Furthermore, Phase III included horses with a prior diagnosis of PPID, a phenotype predisposed to ID55 further supporting the applicability of DNJ to at-risk populations commonly encountered in practice. In addition, the dose-escalation trial might have introduced minor carryover effects. However, the initiation of Phase III at 2500 mg q12h was well tolerated, suggesting this was unlikely to affect tolerance or effects.56

Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence that DNJ is well-tolerated and effective in improving glucose and insulin in horses. DNJ blunts post-prandial hyperinsulinemia in a dose-dependent manner without adverse gastrointestinal or systemic effects. A strength of this work is inclusion of grazing horses at risk for ID, a clinically relevant scenario reflecting real-world management challenges, enhancing the translational relevance of the findings. DNJ holds promise as an adjunctive therapy for ID in horses.
