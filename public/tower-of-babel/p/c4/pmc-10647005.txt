Circannual seizure provocation as the day lengthens in the northern and southern hemispheres
Authors: Marcus C. Ng, Tony Zhang, Darion Toutant, Milena K. Pavlova, Peter Bergin, Mark Quigg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircannual status epilepticus (SE) patterns in communities near Earth's poles best test the hypothesis that SE susceptibility varies with light exposure because these communities are routinely subject to large changes in annual light exposure, which may result in changes to daily sleep time. We compared northern hemispheric circannual SE occurrence in Kivalliq, Canada (latitude‐62.8° N) to southern hemispheric Auckland, New Zealand (latitude‐36.9° S). Instead of peaking at a similar calendar time, SE peaked at a similar solar time during the increasing daylight phase after each region's respective winter solstice. This demonstrates that cumulative effects of increasing light exposure can mediate SE susceptibility.

Introduction

Seizures and status epilepticus (SE) do not occur purely at random, but are mediated by cumulative precipitants and inhibitors that can arise from the environment or from endogenous factors. 1 Since many of these factors occur rhythmically, their chronobiological patterns can reveal which factors are important in seizure precipitation. Communities near the Earth's poles supply a unique resource to evaluate the “weight” of light exposure in seizure or SE precipitation. For example, in the Arctic, solstices can be characterized by 24 h of sunlight in summer and 24 h of darkness in winter. In the Kivalliq region of the Canadian Arctic territory of Nunavut, there is a circannual peak of SE cases maximally after the winter solstice, through the spring equinox, and before the summer solstice 2 as daily light exposure increases. While supporting the notion that re‐emergence of the sun following near‐24 h of darkness interrupts sleep to facilitate occurrence and severity of seizures, 3 , 4 , 5 these data were also subject to numerous factors that potentially confounded the timing of SE occurrence. One way to test the hypothesis that SE susceptibility worsens as the day lengthens is to find comparable communities near the South Pole in which peak SE incidence would also be expected to occur following the winter solstice (albeit in June instead of December). Given the lack of permanent human habitation in the Antarctic, we compared previously published Canadian Arctic circannual SE data with a rigorous and well‐defined epidemiological SE cohort from the southern hemisphere; namely the Greater Auckland region of New Zealand. 6

Methods

We conducted secondary analysis of previously collected SE cases at a northern and a southern hemispheric site. The northern Arctic site of Kivalliq was exclusively served by the provincial neurosciences hospital in Winnipeg, Canada. 7 The southern site comprised five public hospitals and the major private hospital in Greater Auckland, New Zealand. 6 Methods used to identify SE cases have been published previously. 2 , 6 At the northern site, an EEG database identified 99 persons aged ≥16 years who had seizures requiring 117 medical evacuations from the Arctic from September 2009 to November 2020 for subsequent EEG. 7 At the southern site, a prospective study was undertaken (with retrospective chart review to ensure no cases were missed) to identify 367 persons aged 4 weeks or older from 6 April 2015 to 5 April 2016 who had 477 presentations of continuous seizure lasting at least 10 minutes, or repeated seizures exceeding 10 min without recovery. 6 Data were entered into the EpiNet database. 6 We used Stata 14 (College Station, USA) to perform t‐tests and two‐sample proportion tests as appropriate. To evaluate chronological patterns of SE cases, we used MATLAB (Natick, USA) routines to calculate the mean resultant length (MRL) of circular data based on SE frequency per month, week, or day of each year. 8 , 9 The MRL is equivalent to the phase locking value (PLV) at a static interval. We also conducted Rayleigh's test in MATLAB using the CircStat toolbox to assess for statistical departure from circular uniformity with significance at p ≤ 0.05. 8 , 9

Results

The northern site was assigned a latitude of 62.8° N (where the largest settlement in Kivalliq of Rankin Inlet was situated; total Kivalliq population 10,413). The southern site was assigned a latitude 36.9° S (greater Auckland, New Zealand, population 1,615,000). Demographics are in Table 1. As noted from our previously published earlier study, the circannual SE acrophase in the Canadian Arctic peaked from February to June when daily light exposure increases after the winter solstice, through the spring equinox, and prior to the summer solstice (Figs. 1A,B and 2A–C). 10 Similarly, the circannual SE acrophase in Auckland peaked from July to August when daily light exposure increases after the winter solstice (Figs. 1C,D and 2A–C). 11 In the Canadian Arctic (Fig. 2D–F), the MRL vector was consistently clustered at April with length of 0.17 on monthly analysis (p = 0.03), 0.16 on weekly analysis (p = 0.04), and 0.16 on daily analysis (p = 0.04). On circular analysis (Fig. 2D–F), the MRL vector in Auckland was consistently clustered at July with length of 0.14 on monthly analysis (p = 0.0008), 0.14 on weekly analysis (p = 0.001), and 0.14 on daily analysis (p = 0.001).

Discussion

Situated on opposite sides of the globe, we found that SE in the northerly Canadian Arctic and the southerly New Zealand city of Auckland peak at different calendar times of the year. However, we also found that SE peaks at a similar solar time for both the Kivalliq and Auckland cohorts because the circannual acrophases at each site corresponded to the same rising phase of increasing daily light exposure after the winter solstice (June in the southern hemisphere and December in the northern hemisphere). This finding suggests a common mechanism of seizure provocation related to changes in circannual light exposure that can override substantial heterogeneity in patient and SE demographics across both sites due to vastly different social determinants of health, United Nations human development indices, healthcare systems, environmental conditions (such as degree of urbanization, climate, land area, and population density), and sociocultural contexts.

Although SE cases in both Kivalliq and Auckland maximally clustered after the winter solstice, Kivalliq SE cases peaked 4 months after the winter solstice (in April) while Auckland cases peaked 1 month after the winter solstice (in July). We believe that maximal clustering between the two sites was not exactly 180° out of phase (i.e., at 1 month after the winter solstice, or 4 months after the winter solstice) due to the relative latitudinal difference between sites (Fig. 1E). In other words, while Kivalliq is situated at 62.8° N, Auckland is not situated at 62.8° S. Similarly, while Auckland is situated at 36.9° S, Kivalliq is not situated at 36.9° N. The relatively double‐distance from the equator to Kivalliq than to Auckland (equivalent to a relative half‐distance from the equator to Auckland than to Kivalliq) may explain why maximal clustering between the two sites was 90° out of phase instead of 180°.

The precise mediator(s) of SE precipitation after the winter solstice remains unknown. A reasonable candidate is cumulative sleep interruption and deprivation corresponding to increasing daily light exposure. Further to previous descriptions of how both sunlight and moonlight can influence epileptic phenomena, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 the disruptive effect of even a very low amount of ambient light on sleep quality has also been recently described. 16 Moreover, because rapid eye movement (REM) sleep tends to occur in the latter third of the night, this anti‐epileptic stage of sleep may be preferentially disrupted as sleep durations are interrupted or cut short by re‐emergence of sunlight. 17

While we believe that a consistent circannual SE peak shared by two distinct and heterogeneous sites on opposite sides of the world supports our hypothesis of circannual seizure provocation as the day lengthens, we cannot rule out the less likely possibility that our finding was a coincidence generated by the heterogeneity between these two sites. To mitigate this possibility going forward, a future international multi‐center study should standardize SE definitions and inclusion criteria, while carefully selecting sites across a variety of latitudes with matched antipodal geographies.
