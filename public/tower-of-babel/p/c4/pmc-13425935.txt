A randomized controlled trial of brachial plexus block manipulation versus steroid injection in conscious patients for moderate to severe shoulder stiffness
Authors: Zheng Yan, Yinzhe Cui, Kairui Zhang, Jia Ma, Zhefeng Jin, Jiangtao Si, Zhijun Zhang, Jiawen Zhan, Xu Wei, Jie Yu, Yongli Dong, Liguo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntra-articular corticosteroid injection in combination with rehabilitation is a prevalent treatment modality for moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness. However, this approach frequently results in delayed and incomplete recovery. Conversely, arthroscopic capsular release is associated with inherent surgical risks. Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA) presents a minimally invasive alternative; however, concerns regarding intravenous anaesthesia and iatrogenic rotator cuff injury have served to limit its adoption. This randomised controlled trial aimed to evaluate the hypothesis that an Enhanced Recovery After Manipulation (ERAM) protocol incorporating an ultrasound-guided superior trunk brachial plexus block, manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA), an intra-articular corticosteroid injection and standardized rehabilitation is more effective than a corticosteroid injection and standardized rehabilitation.MethodsIn this prospective, single-center, parallel-group superiority trial, 112 patients with moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness were randomized 1:1 to the ERAM protocol (n = 56) or intra-articular compound betamethasone injection plus standardized rehabilitation (n = 56). The primary outcome was the Constant-Murley shoulder function score. Secondary outcomes included visual analog scale (VAS) pain, passive range of motion (ROM) scores (forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation), and Patient Global Impression of Change (PGI-C), assessed at baseline, 1 week, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 6 months. Safety was evaluated with ultrasound and MRI at 8 weeks using the Snyder classification. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; within-group changes were analyzed by repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction.ResultsIn total, 106 patients (53 per group) completed the 6-month follow-up (5.4% attrition). Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. The ERAM group demonstrated significantly superior Constant-Murley scores, VAS pain scores, passive range of motion (composite score, forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation), and PGI-C ratings compared with the control group at every post-treatment time point (all P < 0.05; exact p-values reported in Table 2). Shoulder function in the ERAM group at 1 week was comparable to that of the control group at 6 months, with the greatest ROM gains occurring in forward flexion and abduction and no evidence of recurrent capsular contracture throughout follow-up. No severe adverse events occurred. One ERAM-group patient developed transient deltoid weakness attributed to perineural adhesions involving the axillary nerve, which resolved spontaneously within one day. Ultrasound and MRI at 8 weeks confirmed intact rotator cuff structures with unchanged Snyder grades in all patients.ConclusionFor patients with moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness, including frozen shoulder with or without partial-thickness rotator cuff tears (Snyder grades 1–2), the ERAM protocol substantially shortens the clinical course and achieves accelerated, durable functional recovery compared with corticosteroid injection and standardized rehabilitation. The protocol is compatible with an ambulatory care model and demonstrates a favorable safety profile.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at the (https://www.chictr.org.cn/), (ChiCTR2400083123) on 16/4/2024.Keywords: Shoulder stiffness, Brachial plexus block, Partial-thickness rotator cuff tear, Manipulation under anaesthesia

Background

Shoulder stiffness is a prevalent condition accompanied by varying degrees of shoulder pain, affecting approximately 8% of the population [1]. The highest prevalence is observed among women aged 40–60 years [2]. The condition imposes substantial limitations on daily activities and quality of life. Conservative treatment remains the primary therapeutic approach, encompassing patient education, physical therapy, medication, and functional exercises [3]. Intra-articular corticosteroid injection combined with rehabilitation is widely used; however, this strategy frequently yields delayed efficacy and a high recurrence rate [4]. Arthroscopic capsular release improves mobility rapidly [5] but carries surgical risks and substantial costs. Manipulation Under Anesthesia (MUA) is a minimally invasive intervention that rapidly improves joint mobility in conditions such as frozen shoulder [6, 7]. Nevertheless, concerns regarding intravenous anesthesia-related complications and iatrogenic rotator cuff injury from non-image-guided manipulation have limited its adoption as a first-line option [8]. An optimized approach is therefore needed that retains the minimally invasive advantages of MUA while improving its precision and safety.

The present study introduces the Enhanced Recovery After Manipulation (ERAM) protocol, which refines the conventional MUA pathway in three key respects. Although brachial plexus block has been utilized as an adjunct to manual therapy since the mid-1990s [9, 10], the majority of supporting evidence derives from retrospective cohort studies and uncontrolled case series [11, 12]. Furthermore, previous studies utilized a conventional interscalene block, which carries a risk of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis [13]. In contrast, ERAM employs ultrasound-guided superior trunk block, a diaphragm-sparing technique that provides equivalent surgical anesthesia with a significantly lower incidence of phrenic nerve palsy [13]. ERAM is performed with the patient awake under regional anesthesia, enabling real-time feedback to limit excessive force. This is a safety mechanism absent during MUA under general anesthesia, for which iatrogenic fractures and rotator cuff tears have been reported [14]. The protocol integrates ultrasound-guided superior trunk block, low-force progressive capsular release, intra-articular corticosteroid injection, and immediate standardized rehabilitation to disrupt the pain-immobilization-adhesion cycle. The present randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate whether ERAM improves clinical outcomes in comparison with corticosteroid injection plus standardized rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

This prospective, single-center, randomized controlled trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 statement (Fig. 1). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. WJEC-KT-2023-044-P002). All participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400083123).

CONSORT guideline flow diagram

Patients diagnosed with moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness who attended the hospital between March 2024 and March 2025 were enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged 40–75 years; (2) Diagnosis of shoulder stiffness based on: (a) insidious onset of shoulder pain with progressive restriction of both active and passive glenohumeral range of motion in at least two planes (forward flexion ≤ 120°, external rotation ≤ 30° at 0° abduction, or internal rotation ≤L3 vertebral level); (b) symptom duration ≥ 1 month; (c) radiographic and MRI examination excluding glenohumeral osteoarthritis, calcific tendinitis, and full-thickness rotator cuff tears (Snyder grades 3–4); (d) moderate-to-severe stiffness, defined as a Shoulder Joint Passive Range of Motion Score ≤ 75 points (Fig. 2); (3) Voluntary written informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe skin damage or dermatological conditions at the treatment site; (2) Allergic constitution or hypersensitivity to multiple medications; (3) Coexists with other serious heart, liver, kidney, and other important organic diseases and blood diseases; (4) Specific glenohumeral pathology other than adhesive capsulitis or partial-thickness rotator cuff tears, including inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis), infectious arthritis, crystal-induced arthropathies (gout, pseudogout), advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥ 3), or neuropathic arthropathy; (5) Pregnancy or lactation; (6) Unwillingness to participate. Participants who had received intra-articular corticosteroids, oral NSAIDs, acupuncture, or physical therapy within 7 days prior to enrollment completed a washout period.

Shoulder joint passive range of motion score

Following the exclusion of 13 patients, 112 participants were randomly allocated 1:1 using computer-generated block randomization (block size = 4). Allocation was concealed via sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by an independent biostatistician; envelopes were opened only after completion of baseline assessments. Owing to the nature of the intervention, clinicians could not be blinded to treatment allocation. Patients were instructed not to disclose their group assignment to the assessor. Data were entered into separate datasheets by an external staff member to ensure analysts remained blinded. All clinical assessments were performed by an experienced clinician blinded to allocation. All brachial plexus blocks were performed by a senior anesthesiologist with over 10 years of experience in ultrasound-guided interventional procedures, while all intra-articular injections and MUA procedures were performed by a senior orthopedic surgeon who had completed over 500 intra-articular injections and MUA procedures for adhesive capsulitis prior to the start of the trial. Standardization of procedural techniques was ensured through written procedural checklists and pre-trial calibration meetings.

The ERAM group received ultrasound-guided superior trunk brachial plexus block, MUA, intra-articular injection of compound betamethasone (1 mL of 2% lidocaine + 1 mL of compound betamethasone, equivalent to 5 mg betamethasone dipropionate and 2 mg betamethasone sodium phosphate), and standardized rehabilitation. The control group received the identical intra-articular injection and rehabilitation without brachial plexus block and MUA.

Brachial plexus anesthesia was achieved via ultrasound-guided single-injection superior trunk block targeting the C5–C6 nerve roots using an in-plane approach (needle-to-skin angle 15°–40°, depth 3 cm). Following the administration of negative aspiration, 10 mL of 1% lidocaine was injected circumferentially around the superior trunk under real-time ultrasound visualization. Block success was confirmed at 15–20 min by complete loss of active upper limb motor power and pinprick sensation over the lateral shoulder and proximal arm. No patient required supplemental injection.

The MUA procedure was performed in accordance with previous studies [15] and standardized protocols for traditional Chinese medicine. The patient was positioned laterally, with the physician standing behind the affected shoulder. One hand stabilized the scapula while the other held the elbow. (1) The patient’s arm was subjected to an elastic downward force, after which it was gradually lifted to 180° in abduction (towards the ear). Repeat three times when the sound of the adhesion loosening can be heard and the feeling can be felt. (2) Extend the affected shoulder joint to 90°, bend the elbow, and rotate the affected shoulder joint outward to 45 degrees. Repeat three times. (3) With the shoulder joint in a 90° abduction position, external rotation is performed. Repeat three times. Force was titrated according to tissue resistance and real-time patient feedback. A single manipulation session was conducted, after which the limb was immobilized with a sling until resolution of the anesthetic block.

Both groups underwent an identical two-stage standardized rehabilitation commencing within 24 h of the intervention [12, 15]. Stage 1—Passive mobilization (Days 1–7): Passive shoulder ROM exercises were delivered twice daily (20–30 min per session) by the attending physician or a licensed rehabilitation therapist within pain tolerance. The protocol consisted of forward elevation, forward flexion with shoulder support, and extension with internal rotation (10–15 repetitions each). Stage 2—Active-assisted and progressive resistance (Days 8–21): Supervised sessions were conducted every other day (seven sessions total, 30 min each) by a licensed rehabilitation therapist. On non-supervised days, patients independently performed Stage 1 exercises at home. Stage 2 introduced active-assisted ROM exercises using an overhead pulley, unassisted repetitions of Stage 1 exercises, progressive elastic resistance training (TheraBand) for shoulder flexors, abductors, and rotators, submaximal isometric strengthening (5–10 s holds, 8–10 repetitions), and scapular stabilization exercises. Resistance was advanced as tolerated without eliciting pain.

The primary outcome was the Constant-Murley shoulder function score. Secondary outcomes included VAS pain scores, passive range of motion (composite score, forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation), and PGI-C scores, assessed at baseline, 1 week, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 6 months. The Shoulder Joint Passive Range of Motion Score was derived from the active ROM subscale of the Constant-Murley Score (maximum 100 points across forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation; each scored 0–25). Severity was classified as severe (0–50 points), moderate (51–75), or mild (76–100) [16–18]. Moderate-to-severe stiffness eligible for enrollment was defined as ≤ 75 points (Fig. 2). Passive ROM was measured by the same blinded assessor using a standardized manual goniometer (1-degree increments). During follow-up, participants were instructed to refrain from additional treatments (acupuncture, manual therapy, additional corticosteroid injections, oral anti-inflammatory medications, or surgery). Co-interventions were systematically queried at each follow-up visit, and any protocol deviations were documented. Safety was evaluated by ultrasound and MRI at 8 weeks to assess rotator cuff integrity (Snyder classification [19]), and evidence of iatrogenic injury (new or propagating rotator cuff tears, labral injury, or humeral fracture).

The study employed a parallel-group superiority design with 1:1 allocation. Sample size was calculated using PASS 11: assuming α = 0.025 (one-sided, corresponding to two-sided P < 0.05), 90% power [15, 20], and a between-group difference of 10 Constant-Murley points as the clinically meaningful effect [21], 50 patients per group were required; allowing for 10% attrition, the final target was 56 per group (112 total). The data were analyzed on a per-protocol (PP) basis using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp.). To assess the robustness of the findings, an intention-to-treat (ITT) sensitivity analysis was performed using multiple imputation for missing outcome data under the missing-at-random assumption. The primary analysis population was the PP population; the ITT analysis served as a confirmatory sensitivity analysis. Patients lost to follow-up were included in the ITT analysis using their last available observation. Baseline demographics were compared using independent sample t-tests or Fisher’s exact test. Within-group comparisons across time points were analyzed by repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Between-group comparisons at each time point used independent sample t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests depending on data distribution. Additionally, two-way mixed repeated measures ANOVA (Group × Time) was performed for each outcome to formally test for interaction effects. Mauchly’s test was used to assess the sphericity assumption, and Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when sphericity was violated. Partial eta-squared (ηp²) was reported as the effect size measure. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 112 patients were enrolled. During the 6-month follow-up, 3 patients from each group were lost to follow-up (contact failure), yielding 53 cases per group for per-protocol analysis (Fig. 1). Intention-to-treat analysis using multiple imputation for missing data produced identical conclusions, confirming robustness. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 1).Two-way mixed repeated measures ANOVA confirmed significant Group × Time interactions for all outcome measures (all P < 0.001; Constant-Murley score: F(4, 416) = 70.03, ηp² = 0.402; VAS: F(4, 416) = 36.60, ηp² = 0.260; passive ROM composite score: F(4, 416) = 93.06, ηp² = 0.472; forward flexion: F(4, 416) = 106.21, ηp² = 0.505; abduction: F(4, 416) = 140.97, ηp² = 0.575; external rotation: F(4, 416) = 49.38, ηp² = 0.322; internal rotation: F(4, 416) = 79.44, ηp² = 0.433), indicating divergent recovery trajectories between the ERAM and control groups. Mauchly’s test indicated violation of sphericity for all outcomes (all P < 0.001); Greenhouse-Geisser corrected degrees of freedom and P-values are reported. Post-hoc between-group comparisons at each time point confirmed that the ERAM group demonstrated significantly superior outcomes at all post-treatment assessments (Table 2; Fig. 3). Passive ROM in the ERAM group at 1 week (77.36 ± 8.24) was comparable to that of the control group at 6 months (77.08 ± 7.50). At 6 months, between-group differences remained significant for all measures (all P < 0.001, except external rotation P = 0.003 and internal rotation P = 0.006).

Within-group paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/4 = 0.0125) revealed distinct recovery trajectories. In the ERAM group, all outcomes improved rapidly from baseline to 4 weeks (all P < 0.001 vs. baseline), after which improvement decelerated. VAS pain showed no significant change from 8 weeks to 6 months (P = 0.333), and forward flexion ROM similarly plateaued (8 weeks to 6 months: P = 0.071). However, the Constant-Murley score and abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation ROM continued to improve significantly through 6 months. In the control group, all outcomes improved gradually and continuously, with all consecutive time-point comparisons for VAS, the Constant-Murley score, flexion, abduction, and external rotation significant after Bonferroni correction (all P < 0.001). Internal rotation showed a slower initial response (0–1 week: corrected P = 0.134), with significant improvement emerging from 1 week onward.

At the 8-week safety evaluation, ultrasound and MRI confirmed intact rotator cuff structures with no Snyder grade progression in any patient. No cases of brachial plexus injury, nerve traction injury, humeral fracture, joint infection, or drug allergy were observed in either group. In the ERAM group, one patient (1.9%) with a 4-year history of shoulder stiffness developed transient deltoid weakness (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade 3/5 on shoulder abduction) and axillary-distribution numbness, which resolved spontaneously within one day. No additional neurological or musculoskeletal complications were observed.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the ERAM protocol—combining ultrasound-guided superior trunk brachial plexus block, MUA, intra-articular corticosteroid injection, and standardized rehabilitation—produced significantly superior outcomes compared with corticosteroid injection and rehabilitation in patients with moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness. The treatment effect was evident across all outcome measures and sustained throughout 6-month follow-up. Notably, the ERAM group achieved shoulder function within 1 week comparable to the control group at 6 months, indicating that mechanical capsular release substantially shortens the natural history of shoulder stiffness [22]; no evidence of re-adhesion was observed during follow-up.

The utilization of brachial plexus block for shoulder manipulation was initially delineated by Roubal [23], who documented immediate passive range of motion (ROM) enhancements of 68°–77° subsequent to glenohumeral gliding manipulation under interscalene block in a cohort of eight patients. Subsequent studies by Placzek [24] confirmed the long-term effectiveness of the treatment in 31 patients, with a mean follow-up period of 14.4 months. More recently, Inglese [11] reported high satisfaction and clinically meaningful range of motion (ROM) gains following awake manipulation with brachial plexus block in phase III adhesive capsulitis, and Zhou and Cheng [12] demonstrated superior efficacy of MUA under brachial plexus block versus conservative treatment in a retrospective cohort. The present study extends the findings of previous studies in several respects. First, the sample size (n = 112) is substantially larger than the sample sizes of prior prospective studies of this technique [13, 25]. Second, the study includes patients with partial-thickness rotator cuff tears (Snyder grades 1–2), which extends the applicability of the study beyond primary frozen shoulder and addresses a common scenario excluded from most prior MUA trials. Third, the superior trunk block represents a technical refinement with safety advantages related to phrenic nerve sparing.

Under ultrasound guidance, the superior trunk block anesthetizes the C5–C6 nerve roots, thereby effectively reducing peri-shoulder pain, particularly posterior pain [25]. First described by Burckett-St Laurent [26], this technique targets the superior trunk distal to the interscalene groove, spatially separating the injectate from the phrenic nerve while maintaining anaesthetic efficacy. A comparison of superior trunk block and conventional interscalene block reveals notable distinctions in their respective impact on the phrenic nerve. A recent meta-analysis has substantiated that superior trunk block demonstrates a significantly reduced incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paralysis when compared with conventional interscalene block (RR 0.07; 95% CI 0.04–0.14). This observation is accompanied by a confirmation of comparable block efficacy between the two methods [27]. This property affords the procedure to be performed in an awake, spontaneously breathing patient without the necessity of general anesthesia or advanced airway management. The awake state provides real-time patient feedback regarding pain and neurological symptoms during manipulation. Prior MUA studies predominantly employed interscalene block [13, 25] or general anesthesia [6]. The present study demonstrates that superior trunk block provides adequate muscular relaxation for effective capsular release while avoiding both the respiratory risks of interscalene block and the systemic risks of general anesthesia.

The manipulation component targets fibrotic contractures of the glenohumeral joint capsule through progressive, low-force stretching. Adhesive capsulitis is histologically characterised by fibroblast proliferation and dense collagen deposition, with the anteroinferior region, including the axillary recess, most severely affected [17]. Consistent with this pathology, the most pronounced ROM improvements were in passive forward flexion and abduction—movements biomechanically restricted by anteroinferior capsular fibrosis [28]. In this MUA procedure, the patient is positioned in a lateral decubitus position, and the doctor stabilizes the scapula to facilitate the release manoeuvre, which may reduce injury to the muscles surrounding the scapula. The sustained improvement over 6 months without re-adhesion suggests that mechanical disruption of the fibrotic capsule, followed by structured rehabilitation, permits durable restoration of joint mobility. Capsular release was not directly visualized; confirmation of the targeted capsular effect therefore relies on inference from the pattern of ROM improvement.

The ERAM protocol integrates three therapeutic components: mechanical capsular release, intra-articular corticosteroid administration, and standardized rehabilitation. Each of these components targets a distinct aspect of the disease process. Compound betamethasone (betamethasone sodium phosphate and betamethasone dipropionate) has been shown to suppress inflammatory mediators, cytokines, and nitric oxide synthase via glucocorticoid receptor-mediated transcriptional regulation [29]. This process leads to a reduction in capillary dilation, edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration, thereby alleviating pain and enhancing tolerance for rehabilitation [30]. The implementation of functional exercises has been demonstrated to exert a positive impact on local muscle circulation, thereby facilitating the resolution of inflammation and gradually restoring range of motion. Moreover, these exercises have been shown to play a crucial role in the prevention of adhesion recurrence [31–33]. The control group, which received an identical corticosteroid injection and rehabilitation without mechanical release, exhibited a more gradual improvement that did not reach the levels observed in the ERAM group during the observation period. This finding supports the hypothesis that capsular release is the critical component driving accelerated recovery.

Compared with arthroscopic capsular release, MUA offers a substantially reduced hospital stay, lower cost, and a shorter rehabilitation period [34]. Recent meta-analyses confirm comparable functional outcomes between the two approaches for frozen shoulder [5, 8], and the UK FROST multicentre RCT demonstrated that both MUA and arthroscopic release produced superior outcomes versus structured physiotherapy alone at 12 months [20]. The ERAM protocol builds on this evidence by incorporating a phrenic-sparing regional anaesthesia technique and extending the indication to patients with concomitant partial-thickness rotator cuff tears. Its compatibility with a day surgery model further enhances feasibility within resource-constrained healthcare systems.

A significant safety concern associated with manipulation for shoulder stiffness, particularly in patients with pre-existing rotator cuff pathology, is the potential for iatrogenic rotator cuff injury. In this study, both ultrasound and MRI at 8 weeks confirmed intact rotator cuff structures with no Snyder grade progression in any patient, consistent with Atoun’s findings [35]. Atoun reported no rotator cuff tears on ultrasound at 3 weeks after MUA in 33 consecutive shoulders. The inclusion of patients with Snyder grade 1–2 partial-thickness tears is a salient addition to the clinical literature, as such patients are commonly encountered but have been excluded from most prior MUA trials [6]. The findings of this study indicate that ERAM can be safely administered to this population, provided that full-thickness tears (Snyder grades 3–4) have been excluded through a comprehensive preoperative MRI evaluation. However, the safety assessment was predicated on qualitative MRI evaluation, excluding quantitative measurement of tear dimensions or capsular thickness. This methodological limitation compromises the precision of the conclusion.

With respect to neurological safety, one patient from the ERAM group exhibited a protracted history of shoulder stiffness, concurrently manifesting transient deltoid weakness and numbness. This phenomenon was attributed to perineural adhesions involving the axillary nerve, given the axillary nerve’s role in providing sensory innervation to the anterior and inferior glenohumeral joint capsule, as well as its contribution to motor innervation of the deltoid and teres minor muscles [22]. The incidence (1.9%) is consistent with previously reported rates following shoulder manipulation [13], and the deficit resolved spontaneously without residual impairment. The present study did not entail a systematic neurosensory assessment; rather, safety evaluation was based on patient self-report, which may have resulted in the omission of subclinical nerve injuries. Future studies should incorporate a structured neurological examination at predefined intervals.

Shoulder stiffness is often precipitated by bursitis-related pain, which can lead patients, particularly those with partial-thickness rotator cuff tears, to avoid movement for fear of exacerbating the tear [36]. This avoidance behavior has been demonstrated to promote progressive adhesions and exacerbate restriction. Therefore, it is imperative to differentiate between primary frozen shoulder and rotator cuff tears. A distinguishing feature of frozen shoulders is the restriction of both active and passive motion, whereas rotator cuff tears frequently spare passive motion. A comprehensive physical examination and MRI are requisite for an accurate diagnosis. Despite the limited sample size, which precluded formal subgroup analysis by etiology, the consistent treatment effect across the cohort suggests that ERAM is broadly effective when capsular contracture, rather than structural tendon insufficiency, constitutes the dominant pathology. For full-thickness rotator cuff tears accompanied by hug-up tests, rotator cuff repair is the recommended treatment [36]. For partial-thickness tears accompanied by substantial active and passive motion restriction, ERAM proffers an efficacious, minimally invasive alternative. A comprehensive investigation is necessary to determine the applicability of ERAM for Snyder grades 3–4 tears that are complicated by capsular adhesions.

This study has several limitations. The single-center design and reliance on a single senior physician for all procedures, while ensuring procedural consistency, limit the generalizability of the findings; multi-center validation is warranted. The 6-month follow-up period is adequate for assessing short-to-medium-term outcomes but insufficient to evaluate long-term recurrence, and extended follow-up to a minimum of 12 months is recommended. The manipulation technique was not standardized with objectively quantified force parameters, which limits reproducibility across operators and settings. Quantitative MRI measurements of capsular thickness were not performed, precluding objective imaging-based safety assessment. Finally, the sample size, although adequately powered for the primary outcome, did not permit subgroup analyses stratified by underlying etiology or by comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus.

Conclusion

For patients with moderate-to-severe shoulder stiffness, including frozen shoulder with or without partial-thickness rotator cuff tears (Snyder grades 1–2), the ERAM protocol substantially shortens the disease course and achieves accelerated, durable functional recovery compared with corticosteroid injection and standardized rehabilitation, with a favorable safety profile. Future research should prioritize multi-center validation, objective force quantification, quantitative imaging assessment of capsular changes, systematic neurological monitoring, and extended follow-up to evaluate recurrence.
