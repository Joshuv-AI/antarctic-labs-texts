Parameter estimation of MO-qGEVL distribution under type II progressive censoring with application in environmental data
Authors: Tmader Alballa, Said G Nassr, Ibrahim A Fares, El Husseini Mustafa Abdel Salam, Shimaa Wasfy Sadk, Rasha Abd El-Wahab Attwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroducing new distributions is crucial for achieving more accurate fits to real-world data. In this context, many researchers have modified existing distributions by incorporating additional parameters. So a new distribution—called the Marshall-Olkin generalized extreme value distribution under linear normalization—is proposed and its parameters are estimated under type II progressive censoring by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and Bayesian estimation. However, the MLE of the original GEVL distribution cannot be obtained using classical numerical methods; so, the Whale optimization algorithm is utilized instead. Since humidity plays a vital role in the spread of forest fires, a real data set representing humidity levels in Los Angeles is investigated, showing the superiority of the proposed distribution compared to existing models. Moreover, both distribution fitting (return levels) and time series analysis are employed to forecast future humidity levels.Keywords: MLE, Bayesian estimation, Marshall Olkin, q-GEVL, Type II progressive censoring, Whale optimization algorithmSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Type II progressive censoring is a technique used in reliability testing and survival analysis in which observing all units until failure isn’t possible due to time and cost limitations. Researchers use censoring schemes as Type I (fixed time) and Type II (fixed number of failures), but these don’t account for unit loss during the experiment. Progressive type II censoring addresses this issue by allowing for the removal of units throughout the test and it described as: let N units undergo an experiment, but only a predetermined number m () are observed until failure. After each failure and units observed until failure, a specified number of remaining units are randomly removed until the m-th failure , marking the end of the experiment.1 proposes the method of implementation type II progressive censoring in the code and many papers utilized it in their investigation as2–6.

To analyze such censored data effectively, choose a suitable distribution that can handle the characteristics of the data. Since the traditional distributions lack the flexibility to fit the real-world data under progressive censoring, especially when extremes. To overcome these limitations, extended distribution families like the Marshall–Olkin (MO) transformation are employed to add flexibility to existing models by incorporating an additional parameter , to a baseline distribution7. The present research examines the generalized extreme value distribution with linear normalization q-GEVL8 as the baseline for the MO transformation, producing a new distribution called the Marshall-Olkin generalized extreme value distribution under linear normalization MO-qGEVL9–15. The process of estimation of MO-qGEVL is considered under type II progressive censoring using both maximum likelihood (MLE) and Bayesian estimation (BE). MLE relies on the observed data, seeking parameter values that maximize the likelihood function, and is known for its asymptotic properties and computational simplicity. However, BE incorporates prior knowledge through a prior distribution using the obtained posterior distribution to offer a more flexible framework. BE often uses the values obtained from MLE for starting values in numerical algorithms for posterior computation, enhancing the efficiency and stability of the BE. But MLE equations for higher parameter distributions are not easy to solve, and usually the traditional numerical methods fail. So, metaheuristic optimization techniques (the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)) for handling the MLE equations, see16. Metaheuristic optimization strategies, such as the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), effectively explore and exploit the solution space by mimicking humpback whales’ social behavior and hunting skills, notably their bubble-net feeding strategy. These algorithms are widely used in a variety of fields, including engineering, machine learning, and economics, due to their ability to identify optimal or near-optimal solutions in enormous, complex search spaces,17,18. Moreover, to demonstrate the effectiveness of the MO-qGEVL distribution beyond its superior fit compared to other distributions, both time series (TS) and return level (RL) analyses are employed to predict future humidity observations in Los Angeles19–21. Conclusion

The increasing frequency of catastrophic wildfires across several U.S. states has resulted in extensive property damage, significant loss of life, and widespread emergency evacuations. These tragic events highlight the urgent need for innovative analytical approaches to enhance our understanding and reduce the likelihood of similar occurrences in the future. The Mo-qGEVL distribution is proposed as a suitable model for representing humidity data associated with such events. Parameter estimation for the Mo-qGEVL distribution is carried out under Type II progressive censoring using both Bayesian Estimation (BE) and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Among these, the MLE method combined with the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) demonstrates highly consistent results compared to the Bayesian approach. Furthermore, informative priors in BE outperform non-informative ones, indicating the effectiveness of careful hyperparameter selection. This study also compares the performance of the Mo-qGEVL distribution with a time series modeling approach based on the ARMA(P,q) model. This comparison facilitates the forecasting of future humidity patterns and helps in assessing the potential risk of wildfire spread across different states.

Moreover, the proposed MO-qGEVL distribution is not limited to the proposed dataset. It can be applied to other datasets, which characteristics and goodness-of-fit measures support the suitability of the model.

For future work, the proposed distribution may be studied under Type-II hybrid censoring, and the resulting estimation performance can be compared with the results obtained in the present study. In addition, a copula-based extension, such as the Gaussian copula, may be investigated to model the dependence structure between variables whose marginal distributions follow the proposed model and other models such as27–29.
