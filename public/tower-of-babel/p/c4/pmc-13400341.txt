Multifocal skeletal tuberculosis with posterior-element spinal, sternal, and pelvic involvement presenting as a pulmonary mass: A malignancy mimic
Authors: Srikanth Nalla, Pravin Sudhakar Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultifocal skeletal tuberculosis is an uncommon manifestation of osteoarticular tuberculosis characterized by noncontiguous involvement of multiple skeletal sites. When associated with a mass-like pulmonary lesion, it may closely simulate disseminated malignancy. We report a 26-year-old immunocompetent male presenting with progressive neck stiffness, fever, cough, weight loss, and dysphagia. Imaging demonstrated a large right upper lobe mass with internal air bronchograms and associated centrilobular tree-in-bud nodules, along with multifocal destructive lesions involving the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine, sternum, sacroiliac joint, and pelvic bones. Extensive posterior-element involvement was present, with associated prevertebral, paraspinal, and epidural soft-tissue collections resulting in early spinal cord compression. Laboratory evaluation revealed elevated inflammatory markers, a strongly positive Mantoux test, and negative HIV serology. Bronchoalveolar lavage was negative for malignancy and acid-fast bacilli. Histopathological examination of a CT-guided biopsy demonstrated granulomatous inflammation with Langhans-type giant cells, while GeneXpert MTB detected Mycobacterium tuberculosis, confirming the diagnosis. The constellation of a pulmonary mass and multifocal destructive skeletal lesions initially raised strong suspicion for disseminated metastatic disease. This case highlights an uncommon radiologic phenotype of tuberculosis that closely mimics malignancy and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and obtaining early tissue diagnosis to facilitate appropriate management.Keywords: Multifocal skeletal tuberculosis, Posterior spinal elements, Sternal tuberculosis, Sacroiliitis, Pulmonary mass, Spinal epidural disease, MRI spine, CT chest

Introduction

Tuberculosis demonstrates diverse clinical and radiologic manifestations and remains a major global health concern, particularly in high-burden countries such as India [1]. Although pulmonary disease predominates, skeletal tuberculosis represents a clinically significant form of extrapulmonary disease [2,3]. Classic spinal tuberculosis (Pott’s disease) characteristically involves the anterior vertebral body and adjacent intervertebral disc [2,3]. In contrast, extensive involvement of posterior spinal elements is distinctly uncommon [4], and sternal tuberculosis remains a rare manifestation [5].

Multifocal skeletal tuberculosis (MSTB), defined as noncontiguous involvement of 2 or more skeletal sites, is infrequently encountered and is typically described in isolated case reports or small series [6,7]. Tuberculosis is also well recognized as a “great mimicker,” frequently simulating malignancy on imaging [8,9]. When presenting with a pulmonary mass and widespread destructive skeletal lesions, differentiation from metastatic disease can be particularly challenging.

We report a case of MSTB in an immunocompetent young adult characterized by the unusual combination of a pulmonary mass, extensive posterior-element spinal involvement, sternal osteomyelitis, sacroiliitis, and multifocal pelvic lesions. The constellation of findings closely simulated disseminated malignancy and posed a significant diagnostic challenge, highlighting the importance of early tissue diagnosis and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Case report

A 26-year-old male presented with progressive restriction of neck movements for 4-5 months, followed by recent onset fever, cough, dysphagia, anorexia, and weight loss. There was no history of tuberculosis, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, prior surgery, or immunosuppression. Family history was noncontributory. HIV serology was nonreactive. Neurological examination revealed restricted cervical mobility without focal deficit. Vital signs were stable on admission, and motor power was preserved (5/5) in all extremities.

Laboratory and ancillary investigations

Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated (ESR 70 mm/h, reference <20 mm/h; CRP 16.3 mg/dL, reference <0.5 mg/dL). Hematologic evaluation showed mild anemia, neutrophilia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytosis, consistent with chronic inflammatory disease. Mantoux testing was strongly positive. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated borderline splenomegaly.

Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage demonstrated inflammatory cells without malignant cells; Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative for acid-fast bacilli. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed for dysphagia revealed gastric antral ulcers and erosive gastritis, with biopsies negative for malignancy or tuberculosis.

CT-guided biopsy of the right upper-lobe lesion demonstrated epithelioid granulomas with Langhans-type giant cells, consistent with chronic granulomatous inflammation. GeneXpert MTB detected Mycobacterium tuberculosis (trace positivity), confirming the diagnosis. Rifampicin resistance was indeterminate due to low bacillary load. Aerobic culture yielded scant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, interpreted as secondary colonization.

MRI pelvis demonstrated right sacroiliitis with surrounding soft-tissue edema involving the piriformis and gluteus medius muscles, along with multiple focal lesions involving the left iliac bone, ischium, pubic symphysis, and visualized lower lumbar vertebrae, consistent with multifocal skeletal involvement.

Following multidisciplinary evaluation involving pulmonology, neurology, neurosurgery, gastroenterology, and orthopedics, tissue diagnosis was prioritized because of the malignancy-mimicking imaging appearance. Anti-tubercular therapy was initiated after confirmation of the diagnosis. Given epidural involvement and early spinal cord compression, neurosurgical consultation recommended conservative management with cervical collar immobilization and close monitoring. The patient demonstrated clinical improvement during hospitalization and was discharged in a hemodynamically stable condition with continued anti-tubercular therapy and outpatient follow-up.

Imaging findings

CT chest

Noncontrast CT revealed a large lobulated right upper-lobe mass measuring approximately 49 × 48 × 38 mm with internal air bronchograms (Fig. 1A), closely simulating primary lung malignancy. An adjacent right paratracheal soft-tissue collection was present and was better delineated on postcontrast MRI (Fig. 2). High-resolution noncontrast CT chest axial images demonstrated multiple centrilobular nodules with a tree-in-bud pattern (Fig. 1B), suggestive of endobronchial dissemination of infection [1].

MRI spine

MRI demonstrated multifocal marrow signal abnormalities involving vertebral bodies and posterior elements at multiple levels (Fig. 3). Extensive destruction of the T2-T3 vertebral bodies with prevertebral, paraspinal, and epidural enhancing collections was seen. Mild spinal cord compression was present at C3-C4. Destructive involvement of the manubrium and distal sternum with associated soft-tissue component was identified.

The prevertebral collection exerted anterior mass effect on the posterior esophageal wall, correlating with the patient's dysphagia.

Extensive posterior-element involvement in addition to vertebral body disease contributed to the unusual imaging appearance compared with classic Pott's disease [4].

MRI pelvis

MRI revealed right-sided sacroiliitis with surrounding soft-tissue edema involving the piriformis and gluteus medius muscles, along with multiple focal lesions in the left iliac bone, ischium, and pubic symphysis (Fig. 4), consistent with MSTB [6,7]. Additional T2/STIR hyperintense lesions were identified within the visualized L4 and L5 vertebral bodies.

Overall, imaging demonstrated extensive noncontiguous skeletal involvement affecting the axial spine, posterior elements, sternum, sacroiliac joint, and pelvic girdle in association with a mass-like pulmonary lesion. The constellation of findings closely mimicked disseminated metastatic disease, which was initially considered the leading differential diagnosis.

Hospital course and management

Following multidisciplinary evaluation, tissue diagnosis was prioritized due to the malignancy-mimicking imaging appearance.

After confirmation of MSTB on histopathology and GeneXpert testing, standard anti-tubercular therapy was initiated. Given epidural involvement and early cord compression, neurosurgical consultation recommended conservative management with close monitoring. Cervical collar immobilization was advised because there was no significant neurological deficit. Neurological status remained stable, and surgical intervention was not required.

The patient demonstrated clinical improvement and was discharged in stable condition with continued anti-tubercular therapy and outpatient follow-up. At discharge, he was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, neurologically intact, and symptomatically improved.

Discussion

Spinal tuberculosis most commonly involves vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs [2,3]. Extensive posterior-element involvement is uncommon [4], and sternal tuberculosis remains uncommon [5]. MSTB with noncontiguous skeletal distribution is also infrequently reported [6,7].

The present case is radiologically distinctive due to multilevel posterior-element involvement, concomitant sternal destruction, pelvic and sacroiliac disease, and a large pulmonary mass closely simulating malignancy. This constellation exemplifies tuberculosis as a malignancy mimic [8,9].

The present case is particularly distinctive because of the simultaneous occurrence of a pulmonary mass, extensive posterior-element spinal involvement, sternal osteomyelitis, sacroiliitis, and multifocal pelvic lesions. Although each of these manifestations has been individually described in the literature, their coexistence in a single immunocompetent patient is exceedingly uncommon and created a compelling radiologic mimic of disseminated malignancy.

Prior studies indicate that a small but clinically meaningful proportion of lesions presumed malignant are ultimately infectious or inflammatory in nature [9]. Misdiagnosis may delay anti-tubercular therapy, lead to unnecessary oncologic interventions, and risk neurological deterioration in the presence of epidural disease [3].

Although tree-in-bud nodules and paraspinal collections favored infection [1], the pulmonary mass and multifocal skeletal destruction made metastatic disease a compelling initial consideration. Early image-guided biopsy was therefore essential to establish the correct diagnosis.

Other infectious etiologies such as brucellosis should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Brucellar spondylitis typically demonstrates a predilection for the lumbar spine and is characterized by localized endplate destruction with relatively limited paraspinal soft-tissue involvement. In contrast, tuberculous spondylitis more commonly affects the thoracic spine and is associated with extensive vertebral destruction, large paraspinal collections, epidural extension, and gibbus deformity. In the present case, predominant thoracic involvement, extensive prevertebral and epidural collections, and multifocal extraspinal disease strongly favored tuberculosis.

The case was diagnosed in India, a tuberculosis-endemic region where atypical manifestations of tuberculosis should remain an important diagnostic consideration in young patients presenting with multifocal destructive skeletal lesions and pulmonary masses (Table 1).

Conclusion

This case illustrates an uncommon presentation of multifocal skeletal tuberculosis characterized by posterior-element spinal, sternal, and pelvic involvement presenting as a pulmonary mass. The striking mimicry of metastatic malignancy reinforces the importance of including tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of young patients with multifocal destructive skeletal lesions and a lung mass, particularly in endemic regions [8,9]. Early tissue remains essential to facilitate timely initiation of therapy and prevent neurological complications [3].
