Climatic controls on water-use efficiency and nitrogen acquisition of Astragalus on southwestern Tibetan Plateau
Authors: Songbo Qu, Xu-Ri, Jiaoneng Yu, Ribu Shama, Haojing Lei, Tian-Liang, Sainbuyan Bayarsaikhan, Azzaya Jukov, Munkhbat Tsendeekhuu, Gundegmaa Vanjil, Almaz Borjigidai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe southwestern Tibetan Plateau (TP) is characterized by cold and dry alpine grasslands, yet how plants and soil nitrogen cycles adapt to conditions that are drier and colder than other parts of the TP remain unclear. We measured δ13C and δ15N in Astragalus, a widespread N-fixing legume, and soils along a 3500–5000 m transect in the southwestern TP.ResultsPlant δ13C and inferred intrinsic water-use efficiency (iWUE, 85.6–114.2 μmol CO2 mol−1 H2O) were primarily controlled by growing-season temperature (GST), with SEM showing a significant direct effect of GST on iWUE (standardized path coefficient = 0.45, p < 0.01). Conversely, soil δ15N (2.8‰ to 11.3‰) was negatively associated with mean annual precipitation (MAP; R2 = 0.26, p < 0.01), while plant–soil Δ15N was mainly driven by mean annual precipitation (MAP, R2 = 0.24, p < 0.01). These Δ15N patterns may reflect shifts in the relative contribution of atmospheric N2 fixation and soil N uptake by Astragalus, as inferred from isotopic evidence.ConclusionsThese results suggest potential responses of plant carbon–water coupling and isotope-inferred N acquisition under future warming and drying in high-elevation ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09356-2.

Introduction

Alpine grasslands are highly sensitive to climate change and play an important role in regulating terrestrial carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) cycling [20, 28, 35]. In cold and dry high-elevation ecosystems, plant growth is jointly constrained by thermal conditions and water availability, which can influence photosynthetic carbon assimilation, water-use regulation, and nitrogen acquisition [18, 31]. However, it remains unclear whether plant carbon–water coupling and isotope-inferred N acquisition respond to the same climatic constraints or are mainly regulated by different climatic drivers in alpine grasslands.

Stable carbon isotopes (δ13C) provide a useful tool for evaluating plant carbon–water relations. In C3 plants, leaf δ13C reflects the balance between photosynthetic carbon assimilation and stomatal conductance, and can be used to infer intrinsic water-use efficiency (iWUE) [7, 8]. At broad spatial scales, increased water availability often leads to lower plant δ13C by promoting stomatal opening and reducing diffusional limitation [2]. However, in cold alpine environments, low temperature may impose strong biochemical constraints on photosynthesis by limiting enzymatic activity and electron transport. Under such conditions, thermal limitation may override moisture effects and generate plant δ13C and iWUE patterns that depart from global moisture-driven relationships [10, 17, 25]. Therefore, distinguishing the relative roles of growing-season temperature (GST), precipitation, and atmospheric dryness is essential for understanding plant carbon–water coupling in high-elevation ecosystems.

Nitrogen isotopes (δ15N) provide complementary information on soil N cycling and plant N source use. Soil δ15N integrates the balance between N inputs, internal transformations, and N losses, and is often used as an indicator of ecosystem N cycling openness [11, 23]. At the global scale, soil δ15N generally decreases with increasing mean annual precipitation (MAP), reflecting changes in N loss pathways and isotopic fractionation [1, 5]. Nevertheless, regional studies have shown that isotope–climate relationships can differ from global patterns because local climate, soil properties, vegetation composition, and N transformation pathways interact to shape soil and plant δ15N [3, 27]. On the Tibetan Plateau, previous studies have reported heterogeneous soil δ15N patterns, suggesting that global relationships may not fully capture regional controls in alpine grasslands [30]. In addition, plant δ15N alone can be difficult to interpret across environmental gradients because the isotopic baseline of soil N also varies spatially. The difference between plant and soil nitrogen isotope composition (plant–soil Δ15N) helps account for variation in soil baseline δ15N and provides an isotope-based indication of plant N source use, including the relative contribution of atmospheric N2 fixation and soil N uptake [6]. This is particularly relevant for N-fixing legumes, whose N acquisition may be sensitive to changes in water availability and soil N cycling [13, 26]. Therefore, combining plant δ13C-derived iWUE with plant–soil Δ15N can help evaluate whether carbon–water regulation and isotope-inferred N acquisition are coordinated or controlled by distinct climatic factors.

The Tibetan Plateau (TP), the youngest and highest plateau on Earth, contains alpine ecosystems that are highly vulnerable to environmental change [9, 22]. Previous isotope studies on the TP have mainly focused on central, eastern, or broader plateau-scale transects [27, 29, 34]. By comparison, the southwestern TP is characterized by colder and drier conditions, with alpine steppe and desert steppe widely distributed, but this region remains underrepresented in isotope-based studies of plant and soil N cycling. The genus Astragalus is a widespread N-fixing legume group in alpine grasslands and provides a suitable system for examining plant carbon–water coupling and isotope-inferred N acquisition along climatic gradients. Focusing on a single genus reduces broad phylogenetic variation, and species identity was further evaluated in sensitivity analyses to assess potential interspecific effects [33].

In this study, we measured δ13C and δ15N in Astragalus and associated soils along a transect of approximately 1000 km in southwestern TP, a region rarely covered by previous isotope transects. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize spatial patterns of plant δ13C, δ15N and plant–soil Δ15N, (2) quantify the relative importance of GST versus moisture supply and atmospheric demand (e.g., MAP, VPD, and ET0) in explaining variation in these isotope metrics, and (3) test whether plant water-use patterns and isotope-inferred N acquisition are coupled across this extreme high-altitude gradient, using joint analyses of carbon and nitrogen isotopes.

Method

Study area

This study was conducted along an altitudinal gradient (3613–5016 m) in the southwestern TP (Fig. 1A), spanning longitudes 79.8–92.2°E and latitudes 28.5–33.2°N. Climatic variables used in this study were compiled from gridded climate products and derived indices (Table S1). Although MAT is widely used in large-scale syntheses, it may not adequately represent the thermal conditions experienced by plants in alpine and high-altitude ecosystems. In these systems, the winter period is long and largely biologically inactive, whereas plant photosynthesis, transpiration, growth, and nitrogen uptake are mainly concentrated during the short growing season. Therefore, growing-season temperature (GST) is more directly relevant to plant physiological activity and was used as the primary thermal index in this study. The explanatory performance of GST and MAT was also compared for key response variables (Table S2). The transect covers a GST (calculated as the mean temperature during the growing season, from May to September) gradient of 2.6–13.9℃ (Fig. 1B) and a MAP gradient of 131.4–591.3 mm (Fig. 1C). MAP increased steadily with longitude (R2 = 0.63, p < 0.001, Fig. 1C) and latitude (R2 = 0.59, p < 0.001), whereas GST was negatively correlated with elevation (R2 = 0.38, p < 0.001, Fig. 1B). Along the transect, the landscape is dominated by alpine steppe and alpine meadow. Steppe occurs at the drier end of the precipitation gradient and is mainly composed of cold-xerophytic monocots such as Stipa purpurea and Carex moorcroftii. Alpine meadow occurs at the wetter end, and is dominated by perennial sedges including Kobresia pygmaea, K. humilis, and K. tibetica [30].Fig. 1A Study area showing the sampling transect and site locations on the southwestern Tibetan Plateau. Exact locations of all sampling points

To minimize anthropogenic disturbance, sampling sites were selected in naturally vegetated areas with limited human activity. Where such areas were difficult to access, sampling locations were placed at least 500–1000 m away from major roads. At each site, 2–3 healthy and mature individuals of each Astragalus species were selected. Diseased individuals were avoided, and senescent, insect-damaged, or physically damaged leaves were excluded. Although plant size was not deliberately standardized, sampled individuals were generally small and comparable in size because of the harsh alpine environment, with aboveground cover ranging approximately from 15 to 30 cm2. Mature leaves were collected from each species and pooled into one species-level composite sample per site. When more than one Astragalus species occurred at the same site, each species was kept as a separate composite sample. Soil samples (within 1 m of sampled Astragalus individuals) were collected from the top 10 cm using a 5-cm diameter soil auger, with one sample taken from each quadrat, resulting in a total of 117 soil samples (3 samples per site). Leaf litter and roots were then removed. All samples were sealed in plastic bags and stored below 0 °C for laboratory measurements. Only three Astragalus species (A. strictus, A. arnoldii, and A. tribulifolius) were sampled along the transect, minimizing interspecific variation within the genus.

Laboratory measurements

All plant and soil samples were dried at 75 °C for 48 h and ground to a fine powder (~ 5 μm) using a laboratory mixer mill (MM400, Retsch). Ground samples were weighed into tin capsules (plant: 2 mg; soil: 20 mg) for stable isotope and elemental analysis. Soil δ13C and δ15N were measured at the Environmental Stable Isotope Laboratory (ESIL), Institute of Environment and Sustainable Development of Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, using an IsoPrime 100 IRMS (Elementar, UK) coupled with an Elementar Vario Cube (Elementar, Germany). Two reference materials USGS40 (δ13Cvpdb = −26.39 ± 0.04‰, δ15Nair = −4.52 ± 0.06‰) and USGS41a (δ13Cvpdb = 36.55 ± 0.08‰, δ15Nair = + 47.55 ± 0.15‰) were used for calibration. Additionally, a laboratory reference-Gelatin from bovine skin (δ13Cvpdb = 14.7 ± 0.2‰, δ15N = 6.9 ± 0.2‰) was analyzed every 12 samples to monitor measurement stability. Soil total C and total N were measured using Elementar Vario Cube (Elementar, Germany) and C:N ratios were calculated as the quotient of total C and N. The measurement errors were less than ± 0.2‰ for the above analyses.

Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 mixture of homogenized soil and deionized water using a PHS-3S pH meter. Soil samples were shaken for 1 h in 2 mol/L KCl solution (5.00 g soil and 30.00 ml KCl solution) to extract NH4+-N and NO3−-N. The extract was filtered and analyzed using a Smartchem200 autoanalyzer (Alliance, France).

Statistical analysis

For plant–soil Δ15N calculations, each plant sample was paired with the corresponding site-level mean soil δ15N value. We used ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis (type I regression) to investigate the relationship between isotope values and climatic factors (i.e., GST) as well as soil variables (i.e., soil C and N content, and soil C:N ratio). To assess potential species effects, we conducted sensitivity analyses by including species identity as a fixed factor in linear models for plant δ13C-derived iWUE and plant–soil Δ15N. Species identity was not treated as a random factor because the number of species was small. Therefore, species-specific regressions were not used as primary evidence.

Prior to regression analyses, predictor variables were checked for collinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF). All retained predictors had low VIF values, ranging from 1.03 to 1.12, indicating no problematic multicollinearity (Fig. S3).

The significance of the regression models was assessed using F-tests. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to explore the direct and indirect effects between variables and plant iWUE, Δ15N. Model fit was evaluated using Fisher’s C statistic and associated P-values, and non-significant paths were removed to obtain the final parsimonious model. We constructed the SEM based on the prior knowledge and the SEM was performed with the R package “piecewiseSEM”. A total of 42 plant composite samples were used for analyses involving plant δ13C and iWUE. Due to missing values in the paired plant–soil dataset, analyses involving plant–soil Δ15N, including the SEM, were conducted using 38 complete paired samples. All statistical calculations and figure plotting were conducted with R 4.5.1.

Discussion

Factors controlling plant δ13C and iWUE along the transect

At the global scale, increased water availability leads to depleted plant δ13C by promoting stomatal conductance [4]. In contrast, our dataset showed a weak relationship between plant δ13C and MAP, indicating a clear deviation from global pattern. Similar departures have been reported in regional studies on the Tibetan Plateau, where δ13C has been shown to increase with precipitation in both the northern [10] and eastern TP [17]. This regional divergence likely reflects the overriding influence of cold-temperature constraints on stomatal regulation in alpine environments, which are underrepresented in global syntheses dominated by low-elevation sites. Collectively, these findings suggest that water availability alone is insufficient to explain spatial variation in plant δ13C across our sites.

By comparison, plant δ13C and iWUE exhibited strong and consistent relationships with GST. Importantly, GST and MAP showed only a weak and statistically non-significant relationship along our transect (R2 = 0.055, p = 0.135), suggesting that temperature and precipitation gradients were not strongly confounded. This pattern provided an opportunity to better distinguish thermal effects from water-availability effects. Structural equation modeling further revealed that temperature exerted a dominant influence on δ13C and iWUE, whereas atmospheric dryness indices, including vapor pressure deficit (VPD) and reference evapotranspiration (ET0), showed no significant direct effects. This likely reflects that stomatal regulation is constrained by low temperatures and short growing seasons, reducing the sensitivity of Ci/Ca to atmospheric demand across sites.

These results indicate that thermal conditions, rather than water limitation or atmospheric dryness, are the primary climatic constraint on carbon isotope discrimination and iWUE in these alpine grasslands. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that high-elevation alpine species, including both legumes and grasses, develop similar and relatively stable water-use strategies under cold and dry conditions, thereby minimizing precipitation-driven differences in δ13C [25].

The observed temperature dependence likely reflects biochemical limitations on photosynthetic carbon assimilation at low temperatures, as colder conditions reduce leaf-level biochemical activity, including Rubisco carboxylation and electron transport, thereby decreasing the assimilation rate (A) relative to stomatal conductance (G). We infer that as temperature rises, the positive effect on A outweighs that on G, resulting in lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{C}_{i}}{{C}_{a}}$$\end{document} and thus higher δ13C and iWUE at warmer sites. Consistent with this mechanism, iWUE inferred from δ13C, decreased at higher elevations and lower GST, demonstrating that thermal constraints on photosynthesis drive the observed variation in carbon–water coupling along the elevational gradient. This interpretation aligns with earlier alpine research demonstrating that biochemical limitation becomes the dominant constraint on photosynthesis when temperatures fall below thresholds necessary for optimal Rubisco activity and electron transport [18]. While studies have shown that grasses tend to increase iWUE with warming whereas sedges and shrubs show weaker responses [31], evidence for legumes has been lacking. Our results provide preliminary support that N-fixing legumes, represented here by the genus Astragalus, may exhibit a similar temperature-driven increase in iWUE, suggesting a broader functional convergence in alpine grasslands. Overall, these findings highlight a regional departure from global patterns and identify GST as the dominant climatic control on plant δ13C and iWUE in this high-elevation system.

Factors controlling soil δ15N along the transect

Soil δ15N is a valuable and non-invasive indicator that provides an efficient means to assess and monitor N dynamics within an ecosystem [14, 27]. Its application holds the potential to advance our understanding of the larger-scale nitrogen cycle within ecosystems [30]. Generally, soil δ15N fluctuates within the range of −6‰ to 16‰ [21]. In our study, the average soil δ15N was determined to be 7.8‰, which aligns with the global range. Soil δ15N is influenced by multiple processes, including gaseous N loss, net plant N uptake, N leaching, atmospheric N deposition, and biological N fixation [11]. Among these processes, gaseous N loss (16–30‰) has the strongest effect on soil δ15N, compared to N leaching (1‰), atmospheric N deposition, and biological N fixation (−2–0‰) [12, 27] and has been identified as a key driver of soil δ15N patterns on TP [15]. Denitrification is usually important under wet conditions while NH3 volatilization is prevalent under dry conditions [16]. In this study, MAP, rather than GST, emerged as the main climatic correlate of soil δ15N in these alpine grasslands. Higher δ15N in drier sites is consistent with stronger expression of fractionating gaseous N loss processes, whereas wetter sites exhibited lower δ15N values, reflecting reduced gaseous losses and more limited N fractionation.

The observed Δ15N patterns are consistent with variation in plant N-use strategies rather than changes in the soil N pool. Across the transect, soil N content and inorganic N concentrations showed no significant difference along the water availability gradient. Although NH4+ and NO3− each showed weak positive relationships with soil total N across sites, their summed inorganic N pool (NH4+ + NO3−) did not vary systematically with MAP, indicating that the precipitation signal in Δ15N is unlikely to be driven by simple changes in bulk inorganic N availability. This pattern indicates that the observed Δ15N reflects changes in N transformation processes rather than shifts in the total soil N pool. It further suggests that plants may respond to water availability not by tracking a shifting soil N pool, but by altering their isotope-inferred N source use or relative N source contribution. In drier sites, Astragalus may rely more on atmospheric N2 fixation, supplying isotopically lighter N to plants and reducing Δ15N; in wetter sites, plants increasingly utilize soil N pools, resulting in higher Δ15N values. This suggests that water availability mediates plant N source selection, shifting the balance between fixation and soil N uptake.

Together, these results highlight precipitation, rather than temperature or elevation, as the primary driver of spatial variation in Δ15N, with hydrologic control over plant N acquisition shaping plant–soil isotope coupling in these alpine grasslands. Identifying precipitation as the dominant control provides a climatic basis for predicting future changes in plant N acquisition strategies under altered hydrological regimes.

Conclusion

In the alpine grasslands of the southwestern Tibetan Plateau, plant δ13C and iWUE are primarily constrained by temperature, whereas soil δ15N and plant–soil Δ15N are regulated by precipitation. By focusing on a single N-fixing genus (Astragalus) along a transect where GST and MAP were only weakly related, this study helps distinguish the effects of thermal conditions and water availability on carbon and nitrogen dynamics. Under projected climate change [32], warming is likely to enhance plant iWUE, while decreasing precipitation may influence the relative contribution of N sources, as inferred from plant–soil Δ15N patterns. Together, these findings highlight that temperature and precipitation exert distinct controls on carbon and nitrogen processes in cold, high-elevation ecosystems.
