On the dynamic caddisfly silk H-fibroin gene: a population study in a net-spinning species
Authors: Russell J Stewart, Ashlyn Powell, Jacqueline Heckenhauer, Steffen U Pauls, Gabriela Jijon, Samantha Standring, Cheryl Y Hayashi, Richard H Baker, Paul B Frandsen, Amanda Larracuente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarvae of the caddisfly Arctopsyche grandis BANKS build protective structures and spin silken capture nets in flowing water. Caddisfly H-fibroin, the major protein component of its silk fibers, has a blocky structure with repeating units defined as beginning with a [(SX)nE]m region followed by a G-rich spacer. Previous observation of H-fibroin allelic variation in haploid-resolved individuals led us to investigate allelic variation within two geographically close but separated natural populations of A. grandis. The genomes of 18 individuals were sequenced, and 34 haploid-resolved H-fibroin sequences were extracted. Twenty-four unique alleles were identified in 18 genomes, revealing the dynamic nature of the H-fibroin gene. H-fibroin length variations of up to 25% were tolerated. The major source of the length variations were large-scale deletions and insertions of entire [(SX)nE]m blocks. Small scale indel events were numerous, nonrandomly distributed, and constrained to a few types. One was a 44 residue indel comprising two (SX)nE motifs that split direct tandem repeats in [(SX)nE]m regions, changing m by ±2 without disrupting tertiary structure or block boundaries. The G-rich spacers are of two types, the first distinguished by repeating GLGPH pentapeptides. Indels within this spacer type occur as multiples of the GLGPH pentapeptide. The other category of G-rich spacer was confined to a narrow length distribution. Overall, the results demonstrate the rapid evolution of the caddisfly H-fibroin gene and the wide range of H-fibroin structural polymorphism tolerated in functional capture net silk. At the same time, the limited nature of the indels point to the critical structural features of H-fibroin.Keywords: silk, H-fibroin, resilin, zona pellucida (ZP) proteins, Trichoptera, caddisflies

Introduction

The silk-spinning larvae of aquatic caddisflies (Insecta: Trichoptera) build precise protective structures underwater with silk and materials gathered from their environment, including stones, twigs, bark, and leaves (Wiggins 2005). The order Trichoptera is divided into two suborders, Annulipalpia and Integripalpia, which are estimated to have diverged from a common trichopteran ancestor around 275 million years ago (Frandsen et al. 2024). Larvae of Annulipalpia assemble stationary retreats, sometimes equipped with suspended, highly ordered, silken nets for capturing food. Larvae of the earliest diverging lineages of Integripalpia, referred to as cocoon makers, include free-living species that use silk only in the final instar to build underwater pupal cocoons within a dome-like rock shelter and species that build portable tortoise or purse cases in larval stages. Larvae in the integripalpian infraorder Phryganides assemble portable tubular cases, like composite portable body armor. Going back further in evolutionary time, the aquatic Trichoptera are a sister order of terrestrial silk-spinning Lepidoptera (moths and butterflies), having diverged from a silk-spinning common ancestor around 310 million years ago (Frandsen et al. 2024). Trichoptera and Lepidoptera together comprise the superorder Amphiesmenoptera.

The major structural components by size and mass of Amphiesmenoptera silks are Heavy chain fibroins (H-fibroins), highly repetitive blocky proteins with molecular weights (Mw) ranging from 600 to 1,000 kDa. Until recently, the accurate assembly of large and repetitive H-fibroin sequences was challenging, but with the advent of high-quality long-read sequencing methods, the full-length assembly of H-fibroins from genomic sequences has become nearly routine (Kawahara et al. 2022; Frandsen et al. 2023; Heckenhauer et al. 2023, 2024; Hotaling et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2025). This has provided a growing library of amphiesmenopteran H-fibroin protein sequences, creating a window into their deep evolutionary history. Investigation into this history may provide insights into the molecular adaptations of their silks that accompanied the divergence of Amphiesmenoptera into different environments and silk usage, i.e. terrestrial versus aquatic in the case of Lepidoptera and Trichoptera, and within Trichoptera deployment of silk for building pupal cocoons, tube cases, fixed retreats, and capture nets.

As general features, Amphiesmenoptera H-fibroin protein sequences comprise two highly conserved, relatively short, and nonrepeating regions at the N- and C-termini. These conserved termini flank a large and highly repetitive central region. Conservation of sequence and function are evident in the N- and C-termini of lepidopteran and trichopteran H-fibroins (Yonemura et al. 2006). H-fibroins dimerize through the N-termini, and the Light chain fibroin (L-fibroin) is covalently coupled to the C-termini through disulfide bonds. There is no sequence conservation within the repetitive central region even within families. A unique sequence feature of caddisfly H-fibroins is the presence of (SX)nE motifs, where n = 2 to 6 and X is often a hydrophobic amino acid (A, V, L, I, or Y). The repeating units of caddisfly H-fibroins comprise a region consisting of two to seven closely spaced (SX)nE motifs, [(SX)nE]m regions, (where m = the number of (SX)nE motifs) and a longer variable length G/L-rich spacer region (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). Multiple spectroscopic and proteomic experimental approaches, in multiple species from both caddisfly suborders, have consistently demonstrated the serine (S) in the (SX)nE motifs are phosphorylated (Stewart and Wang 2010; Addison et al. 2013, 2014; Ohkawa et al. 2013; Ashton and Stewart 2015; Frandsen et al. 2019). In natural spun fibers, the phosphoserines (pS) are complexed with roughly equimolar ratios of multivalent metal ions, predominantly Ca2+ but with smaller ratios of Mg2+, Fe2+, and Zn2+ (Ashton et al. 2013; Ashton and Stewart 2019). The phospho-metal ion complexes, likely in the form of semi-crystalline β-domains, are profoundly responsible for the mechanical strength and other material properties of caddisfly silk fibers (Ashton et al. 2013, 2016; Standring et al. 2025).

H-fibroin sequences from 11 caddisfly species (two pupal cocoon makers, six tube case makers, and three retreat-builder/net-spinners) exhibited remarkable structural and organizational diversity (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). The Mw ranged from 600 to 1,000 kDa, and the size, number, and organization of the [(SX)nE]m repeating modules varied substantially across the phylogeny. The structural polymorphisms implied rapid evolution and structural adaptation of the H-fibroins to the distinct uses of the silks. Subsequently, H-fibroin sequences were extracted from haploid-resolved genomes of individual caddisflies, one each of a cocoon-maker, a tube case maker, and a retreat builder/net spinner (Frandsen et al. 2023; Standring et al. 2025). Striking allelic variation in size and the number and organization of the repeating units was observed within individual animals. Similar allelic variation was observed in an individual butterfly H-fibroin and an individual spider silk fibroin (spidroin). This level of H-fibroin allelic diversity was effectively hidden from investigation before long-read sequencing technology (PacBio HiFi) and assemblers (hifiasm) capable of haploid-resolved genomes became available.

Given the substantial allelic variation in H-fibroin within individual caddisflies, it stands to reason that population-level variation would also be substantial and may hold information useful to understanding the evolution of these large repetitive structural proteins. Motivated by this potential, we report H-fibroin protein sequence diversity within two geographically separated natural populations of a single caddisfly species, retreat-building/net-spinning Arctopsyche grandis BANKS (Fig. 1c). This striking structural polymorphism we observe provides further evidence of highly dynamic evolution of caddisfly H-fibroin.

Results

H-fibroin allelic heterogeneity within two populations of A. grandis

Multiple individuals of A. grandis, a retreat builder/net spinner caddisfly, were collected from two watersheds in the Wasatch mountains in Utah. The watersheds are 47 km apart and separated by several mountain peaks and canyons (Fig. 1b). Eight individuals were collected from the first population and 10 individuals from the second population. Their genomes were sequenced and full-length haplotype-resolved H-fibroin sequences were extracted from each genome (genome assembly statistics can be found in Table S1 and a representative H-fibroin protein sequence can be found in Fig. S1). The H-fibroin sequence was not fully assembled in one of the haplotypes for two individuals (7 and 17); therefore, only one allele from each of these individuals was included in the analysis (total of 34) (Table 1). Alleles are designated by individual number (1 to 8 for population 1 and 9 to 18 for population 2) and by first (.1) or second (.2) allele.

Indel characterization

The MUSCLE multiple sequence alignment (MSA) (Fig. 2) was reduced to one representative from each allele to eliminate redundant analysis of identical indel events. The length, sequence, and location of unique indels were identified relative to a modal locus sequence, i.e. the column-by-column majority state along the alignment axis. This avoided using a single allele as a reference sequence for all other alleles. Indels were counted as unique if they had a unique sequence, occurred at a unique location, or were a unique type (insertion vs. deletion). In total, there were 168 unique indels events (UIE) across the alignment. The sequence of each insertion or deletion was extracted from the MSA. The length and number of occurrences of UIE is plotted in Fig. 3a. Only unique indels <100 residues are included in the plot for visual clarity. There were 17 indels >100 residues, ranging from 102 to 363 residues.

A striking feature of the unique indel length distribution was the number of indels that are a multiple (n) of five residues (Fig. 3a, blue bars). These were due to the presence of a variable number of the pentapeptide, GLGPH. For example, a single copy of GLGPH1 was inserted or deleted 27 times, GLGPH2 was inserted or deleted 12 times. These G-rich regions will be described in more detail in the (SX)nE spacer section.

A second feature that stood out was the number (16) of unique 44-residue indel events (Fig. 3a, red bar). Six unique sequences were identified in this category. Each contained two (SX)nE motifs and hereafter are referred to as SX44 indels (Table 2, U1-6). The repeating units of A. grandis H-fibroin begin with [(SX)nE]m regions. The sequence of a representative [(SX)nE]5 region contains two imperfect direct tandem repeats (DTR). The sequence of a representative DTR is shown in the top row of Table 2. The junction of the repeats is indicated with a red backslash. The (SX)nE pattern within the DTR region is n = 4,4,3,4. The pattern of the first repeat is n = 4,4 and the pattern of the second repeat is 3,4. The six unique SX44-indel sequences are aligned under the DTR region. The SX44 indels are in three different registrations (shifted within the DTR region) all of which span the DTR junction. Single amino acid substitutions of unique SX44 indels with the same registration are indicated with red bold letters. The result of a deletion of any of the SX44 indels is to change a DTR into a single repeat, or vice versa for an insertion. The 4,4,3,4 pattern is converted into a 4,4 pattern, and the reverse for an insertion, a 4,4 into a 4,4,3,4.

To investigate if the SX44 indels were specific to the MUSCLE alignment algorithm, the MSA was generated with the PRANK alignment algorithm. PRANK also identified 44-residue indels with 9 unique sequences (not shown), which differed by registration across the DRT junction. MUSCLE alignments with the H-fibroin in a different order, e.g. sequential vs. allele group order, resulted in SX44 indels that covered the same region but with some additional unique registrations. We conclude that there was a true biological SX44-indel. The indel sequences may vary because the alignment algorithm can place the indel across the DTR junction with any alignment registration that results in the same alignment algorithm score. Alternatively, the biological SX44-indel mechanism may be equally likely to create indels anywhere within the region that crosses the DTR junction precisely splicing the tandem repeats into a single repeat.

The distribution of the unique indel event locations was plotted on the alignment column axis (Fig. 3b), color-coded the same as Fig. 3a. Indel loci that contained SX44 indels are red, loci with indels that contain at least one GLGPHn are blue. Black ticks contain all other indels. The tick marks on the rug plot correspond to the locus midpoint. Multiple unique indels can fall into the same locus; the 168 unique indel events fall into 74 loci. As an example, in one H-fibroin allele there may be an insertion that spans 76 residues, the midpoint of which defines an indel locus. In other alleles there may be a 71-residue insertion within that span of 76 residues, both of which would be included at that locus, etc. The tick mark height is proportional to the number of indel events within the locus, which ranged from 1 to 9.

It is visually apparent from the rug plot (Fig. 3b) that the indel events were not distributed evenly (randomly) across the H-fibroin sequences. Instead, the indel events clustered at “hotspots”; regions that tolerated indels without disrupting viable H-fibroin structure. Indel events were sparse in other regions. Within each indel type (red, blue, or black) the distribution of events was nonrandom. For example, red SX44-indel events were absent from the region 4,200 to 6,800 on the alignment column axis. The blue GLGPHn indel events were largely constrained to the N-terminal (left) side of H-fibroin and contained the highest range (1 to 9) in the number of events per locus. The black ticks were tightly clustered in four regions and generally contain the fewest unique indel events, except in the region 5,500 to 6,500. There were no indels in the stringently conserved N- and C-termini regions.

Distribution of SX44 indels

Caddisfly H-fibroin has a repetitive blocky structure. The repeating structural blocks are defined as beginning with an [(SX)nE]m region followed by a G/P-rich spacer region (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). Compact graphical representations of the block structure of the H-fibroins, which are greater than 6,000 amino acids in length, were created by sliding a sequence window along the entire H-fibroin sequences one residue at a time. Each data point represents the percent identity of the window sequence to the underlying allele sequence, plotted at the center point of the window. A sequence that is part of a repetitive structure will show peaks in the plot at all of its locations. To show the locations of the SX44-indel sequences relative to the overall structure of H-fibroin the SX44-indel sequence best centered over the DTR junction, YSASASVSRERPVYRRPVIVSRPTVKKSASVSYETIQTPTIIT, was used as the scanned window SX44 sequence.

Note that each peak does not represent a unique SX44-indel event identified in the MSA. Rather, the pattern of peaks in an [(SX)nE]m region relative to peaks in the same allele or other alleles show where SX44-indel events occurred and how they affected the underlying [(SX)nE]m regions. The scanned SX44-indel sequence has two (SX)nE motifs, an (SX)4E and an (SX)3E; a 4-3 pattern. Therefore, SX44-indel events changed m by ± 2 in a 4-3 pattern. For example, in allele 1.1 peaks 8, 12-16, 20 and 23 (m = 3) had a 4-3 deletion event relative to peaks 3-7, 9-11, 18, 19, 21, and 22 (m = 5). As another example, peak 2 (m = 7) in allele 13.2 had an SX44 4-3 insertion relative to the m = 5 peaks (Fig. 4). Red ticks occur in the rug plots at many of these positions (Fig. 3b). It appears the SX44-indel events cleanly split or splice [(SX)nE]2 direct repeat units, while preserving the repeat structure and boundaries of the [(SX)nE]m regions. The complete pattern of SX44-indels for Alleles 1.1 and 13.2 are presented In Table S3. Similarly consistent patterns occur in all alleles of both populations. Other types of indel events within the [(SX)nE]m regions were not tolerated.

From the sliding window scans of all 24 alleles from both populations, the structural polymorphism of H-fibroin was readily evident within each individual allele and between population alleles (Fig. 5). Structural changes in the [(SX)nE]m regions throughout the population were evident from the patterns of (SX)3E and (SX)4E motifs (red vertical markers). There was a striking bifurcation of the primary structure into distinct left (toward the N-terminus) and right (toward the C-terminus) repeat patterns. A central transition region separated the left side repeat regime from the right side repeat regime. Based on earlier observations, a bipartite structure with a central transition region was a consistent feature of net-spinning caddisfly H-fibroins (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). The bulk of the length polymorphisms was due to shuffling of entire [(SX)nE]m blocks. The structure of the left side, especially in population 1, was more conserved than the right side. The location of the central transition region was relatively stable in population 1 but wandered considerably in population 2.

Polymorphism of [(SX)nE]m spacers

The sliding window scans provided a compact visualization of the polymorphism in the pattern of the [(SX)nE]m repetitive blocks and revealed the position of SX44-indel events. The [(SX)nE]m clusters likely form highly structured, semi-crystalline β-domains. The [(SX)nE]m regions are separated by G/P-rich spacer regions, which likely form less structured flexible and elastic linkers. Polymorphism in the spacer length, structure, and patterns were evaluated by extracting and sequentially numbering the sequences between [(SX)nE] regions. The length (y axis) of each sequential spacer (x axis) was plotted for a representative allele (1.1 and 13.2) from each population (Fig. 6), and for both populations (Fig. 7) to provide an overall view of spacer heterogeneity in and between both populations.

There are two categories of spacers. The first category has two distinct segments (color coded blue and orange) separated by a single (SX)3E motif (red) near the center. The blue segments are G/P-rich regions with repeating GLGPH pentapeptides. The orange segments are G/P-rich and do not contain histidine. The second category does not have GLGPH blue segments. They have central (SX)3E motifs, usually one, and as many as five, that separate G/P no H orange segments. The spacers ranged in length from 110 to 365 residues and fell into 106 unique types (804 total in both populations) that differed in length, segment pattern, or sequence. There were 58 and 48 unique spacers in population 1 and 2, respectively; none of the unique spacers occurred in both populations. The spacers invariably began with R-rich GPWRRRGP or GPWRRHGP motifs and invariably ended with R-rich RRPRGKV or RPRRGKV motifs (alternatively, the [(SX)nE]m regions could be defined as beginning and ending with the R-rich motifs). The invariant arginine clusters create patches of positive charge that break secondary structure and create “bright” boundary markers in the repetitive block structure. Representative spacer sequences from allele 1.1 are listed in Table S2, one from each length category.

The bipartite structure and heterogeneity of H-fibroin alleles were again readily apparent in the distribution of spacers (Figs. 6 and 7). The blue (GLGPH-containing) spacers occurred predominantly in the left side of the central transition region in population 1 alleles and exclusively in the left side of population 2 alleles. In both populations, the orange (G/P noH) spacers dominated the right side (C-terminal region) of the sequences and occurred in distinct stereotypic patterns and lengths. The central transition region was demarked by extra-long orange spacers that contained multiple (SX)3E motifs, as does the end of the H-fibroin sequences. The length heterogeneity of the spacers was due to the G/P-rich spacer segments. The length distributions of both segment types are shown in Fig. 8. The GLGPH-type were more broadly distributed, suggesting they were more tolerant of overall length changes. The lengths differed by five residues, a GLGPH pentapeptide, with only one exception (107). The blue spacers only tolerated unit GLGPH length changes. The length of the orange spacers was much more narrowly distributed and more constrained than the blue, which is also evident in Figs. 6 and 7.

Polymorphism of comparator proteins

For comparison to H-fibroin, structural heterogeneity was analyzed in two other silk proteins, L-fibroin and PEVK-like. Haplotypes of both proteins were extracted from the genomes of each individual of both populations and aligned (Figs. S2 and S3). Only one L-fibroin allele was recovered for individuals 6 and 14. Of the remaining 16 individuals, all were homozygous with respect to L-fibroin (Table 3). There were only two unique L-fibroin alleles, one in each population. In stark contrast, none of the 16 individuals for which both H-fibroin alleles were recovered were homozygous and there were 24 unique alleles (Table 1). The PEVK-like protein (KM384739.1) was identified by proteomic analysis of caddisfly silk gland proteins and predicted to be an elastic structural protein in the silk fiber (Wang et al. 2014). There were 10 homozygous individuals, 6 unique alleles in total, and little length variation (Table 3). Population 1, with five unique alleles, was more polymorphic than population 2 with only one unique allele.

To investigate if H-fibroin structural polymorphism was a result of its repetitive structure, H-fibroin was compared with three nonsilk, repetitive structural proteins: Resilin A, Resilin B, and Zye (XP_077294013.1). Zye is an uncharacterized protein identified in the A. grandis reference genome assembly (ASM5162203v2). Haplotypes of all three proteins were extracted from the genomes of each individual of both populations and aligned (Figs. S4, S5, and S6).

Polymorphism of Resilin A and B was intermediate with 12 and 10 unique alleles, respectively. Again, the alleles were population specific; none occurred in both populations. Zye was nearly as polymorphic as H-fibroin, with only 2 homozygous individuals and 17 unique alleles in 18 individuals. Length polymorphism and unique indel event (UIE) number and density also followed a similar pattern.

Discussion

The dynamic genetics of caddisfly H-fibroin

Comparing H-fibroin from 18 individuals from two closely separated natural populations of a single caddisfly species, A. grandis, has allowed an unprecedented look at the dynamic genetics, structural polymorphisms, and rapid evolution of H-fibroin. The populations occur in similar habitats with little geographical separation (Fig. 1). Yet, 24 distinct H-fibroin alleles were found in 18 individuals. None of the individuals were homozygous and none of the distinct alleles occurred in both populations. H-fibroin length varied 25% between the shortest (5,704 a.a.) and longest (7,228 a.a.) alleles (Table 1). The greatest source of length and structural heterogeneity was insertion or deletion of entire [(SX)nE]m blocks. The number of blocks varied from 21 for the shortest allele (1.2) to 30 for the longest (14.1). Most of the whole block expansions and contractions occurred in the right (C-terminal) side of the alleles. Mechanistically, these large indels were likely due to unequal crossover during homologous recombination (Manning and Gage 1980; Sehn 2015). Most of the large-scale shuffling was confined to the right side of the sequence, which suggests there were stricter sequence- or structure-dependent constraints on the left (N-terminal) side of the sequence. The small SX44 indels changed the primary structure of the [(SX)nE]m regions by m ± 2, which apparently preserved the functional structure and boundaries of these regions. Overall, it appears silk mechanical properties suitable for the real-world of net-spinning caddisfly larva were obtained without tight constraint on H-fibroin size, or the number and arrangement of [(SX)nE]m blocks.

To our knowledge, the closest previous study of H-fibroin in a single species, 22 inbred stocks of the domesticated silkworm Bombyx mori, was reported in 1980 (Manning and Gage 1980). This was long before the development of long-read sequencing methods that now allow nearly routine extraction of full-length H-fibroin sequences from compete genomes (Hotaling et al. 2023). H-fibroin length heterogeneities of 15% were identified by gel electrophoresis. Heterogeneities in internal structures were identified by restriction mapping. In total, they identified 19 distinct alleles in the 22 inbred stocks. The structural polymorphism was attributed to unequal cross over events within the H-fibroin locus.

Within caddisflies, the retreat-building/net-spinning larvae make the most sophisticated use of their silk (Wiggins 2005). They build a stationary domed retreat with stones joined with silk (Fig. 1). Then with the same silk, they weave an organized net at the opening of the retreat to capture food from flowing water. This correlates with the greater structural complexity of net-spinner H-fibroin relative to the H-fibroins of cocoon makers, and tube case makers. This was most apparent in the bipartite structure of H-fibroin. Based on earlier observations, the bipartite structure was a consistent feature of net-spinning caddisfly H-fibroins, but was not apparent in cocoon or tube case makers (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). Perhaps the additional mechanical demands on a silk net suspended in a flowing water stream required structural and mechanical adaptations in their H-fibroins reflected in their greater structural complexity.

There are two categories of spacers between [(SX)nE]m regions. Both contained central isolated (SX)3E motifs. The first category had a GLGPHn segment on the N-terminal side of the spacer separated by a single (SX)3E motif from a G/P-rich (no H) segment on the C-terminal side. The second contained only the G/P-rich (no H) segments separated by (SX)3E motifs. The length of the GLGPHn segments was not highly constrained by H-fibroin function; it could vary from at least 72 to 137 residues, nearly 100%. However, the lengths only changed in multiples of the GLGPH pentapeptide. The length of the other G/P-rich spacer types were mostly constrained to 42, 54, and 63 residues. The mole fraction of G and P in the spacer types put them in the same G/P content region as resilin, elastin, and close to MaSp2 spider silk (Rauscher et al. 2006), suggesting the spacers are elastomeric (Hayashi and Lewis 1998; Savage and Gosline 2008). A high G:P ratio in the spacers was unique to the net-spinner caddisflies (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). The accumulation of P in the G-rich spacers, with the associated increase in elasticity and resilience, may have been a molecular adaptation of H-fibroin to the mechanical and material requirements of a suspended capture net compared with a cocoon or tube case. Previous research on a tube case maker caddisfly with an unusually flexible case, also showed high proportions of P and G, more closely mimicking net-spinning caddisflies than other tube case makers (Deng et al. 2025).

The GLGPH-type spacers comprised 10 to 12 mol% H in a regular repeating pattern. Mechanistically, the pentapeptide indels in a repeating pentapeptide sequence may be the result of DNA polymerase slippage during replication (Sehn 2015). Also, an extended region of repeating pentapeptides may create a relatively large target for unequal crossover events. The crossovers could result in the insertion or deletion of entire [(SX)nE]m blocks between GLGPH spacers, as well as change the number of GLGPH units in the crossover products.

The high concentration of ordered H has significant implications for caddisfly H-fibroin structure. The H imidazole sidechain has a pKa near 6. Small pH fluctuations near pH 6 changes the H ionization state from positive to neutral, creating the potential for pH-triggered changes in electrostatic interactions within and between the GLGPH segments and other parts of H-fibroin. The pH distribution in the caddisfly silk gland has not been measured, but in B. mori the pH of the middle silk gland was reported to be 5 to 6, while the anterior silk gland was 7 to 8 (Miyake and Azuma 2008). If the caddisfly silk gland has a similar pH range, the GLGPH spacers would undergo a dramatic change in charge density on transit through the silk gland, with consequent changes in electrostatic interactions, repulsion or attraction, within and/or between H-fibroin and other silk components. Potential pH-triggered effects through H would be concentrated in the left N-terminal side of the GLGPH spacers, which occur mostly on the left side of the central transition region of H-fibroin.

If pH-triggered effects mediated through H occur in A. grandis and other net spinner H-fibroins, it is not a mechanism conserved across caddisflies. A high concentration of patterned H in the G-rich spacers is not a general feature of all caddisfly H-fibroin sequences. It varies considerably when compared across caddisfly lifestyle clades—free living, tube case makers, and retreat builder/net spinners (Fig. S7). The highest mol% H occurred in some of the free-living caddisflies. H was nearly absent in most of the tube case makers, but with wide variation. And whereas the H was evenly distributed across the H-fibroin sequence of free-living caddisflies, the H of net-spinners was consistently skewed to the left side of the H-fibroin sequence (Fig. S7). Although the presence and distributions of H in caddisflies was highly variable, it appears to be unique to caddisfly silk H-fibroin. Compared with terrestrial silks, the mol% H in two representative lepidopterans (B. mori (WCB27280.1), Aphomia sociella (KAM3962534.1), is ≤0.1%. The major ampullate silk proteins of the spiders Latrodectus hesperus (ABR68856.1) and Argiope argentata (WEY07498.1) have a single H across the entire sequences.

To gain further insight into the rapid evolution of H-fibroin, we compared H-fibroin population-level polymorphism to polymorphism in several other categories of proteins: two nonrepetitive silk proteins (L-fibroin and PEVK-like), two paralogs of a repetitive nonsilk protein (resilin A and B), and a large repetitive extracellular protein (Zye) identified in a search of the A. grandis annotated reference genome for highly repetitive proteins (Table 3). Beginning with L-fibroin, the low level of heterogeneity relative to H-fibroin, e.g. the absence of heterozygosity and low number of unique alleles in the population, was not unexpected. L-fibroin is a nonrepetitive, structured protein and therefore mechanistically less prone to unequal crossover events during homologous recombination, or strand slippage during DNA replication. The 3D structure of L-fibroin is conserved in trichopterans (caddisflies) and lepidopterans (moths and butterflies) (Stewart et al. 2022), which suggests L-fibroin has a critical function in the synthesis, storage, assembly, or structure of silk fibers in both Trichoptera and Lepidoptera, a critical role that does not tolerate structural polymorphisms and was therefore subject to strict purifying selection.

The PEVK-like protein with a low-complexity, highly charged sequence is intrinsically unstructured. It is not highly repetitive and has structural heterogeneity in between that of H- and L-fibroin. This may be expected because its nonrepetitive sequence is mechanistically less prone to unequal crossover events or strand slippage. As an intrinsically unstructured protein, its role in the silk fibers tolerated some degree of heterogeneity compared with L-fibroin. The resilin paralogs are repetitive structural proteins but not silk proteins. Resilin is an elastomeric protein found primarily in the exoskeleton of insects where it is linked to chitin (Appel et al. 2024). The sequence comprises imperfect repeats of short motifs. The Resilin length variations and other measures of heterogeneity were greater than PEVK-like, likely due to its repetitive sequence prone to unequal crossover, but its length variation (7% to 8%) was roughly 1/3 that of H-fibroin. Resilin's role as elastic elements in the cuticle may restrain viable length polymorphisms.

The A. grandis Zye protein is a large (∼3,000 a.a.), highly repetitive protein that contains a zona pellucida (ZP) binding domain. ZP proteins are a class of glycoproteins essential for egg coat assembly and structure; the zona pellucida in mammals, the vitelline membrane in insects. They are involved in gametic recognition and fertilization. The ZP domain is responsible for polymerization of the egg coat matrix (Bokhove and Jovine 2018). The A. grandis Zye protein comprised a secretion signal peptide (1 to 20 a.a.), a ZP domain (57 to 306), followed by a long repetitive region to the end of the variable length protein. In the repetitive region, tandem repeat cassettes consisting of a 10 a.a. motif (CYxxxxDP(R/S)C) alternating with a 9 a.a. motif ((K/R)P(Q/T)TPAPPN) occurred multiple times (ranging from 91-113 in the population). The tandem repeat cassettes were separated by longer (65 a.a.) proline/threonine-rich spacers to form multiple (ranging from 9 to 13) higher-order repeats. In other words, the Zye repetitive modular architecture was highly similar to the repetitive modular architecture of H-fibroin, and therefore a good comparator. Zye polymorphism was much greater than the other categories of proteins, but still less than H-fibroin except in the density of unique indel events (Table 3). Like H-fibroin, the majority of Zye length polymorphism (15%) was due to insertion or deletion of entire higher-order repeats. Interspecies comparison of reproductive ZP proteins, e.g. mammalian ZP3, has shown that ZP3 and other reproductive proteins evolve rapidly and were subject to adaptive evolution; there appeared to be positive Darwinian selection for structural polymorphism (Swanson et al. 2001; Swanson and Vacquier 2002). It is not known if the A. grandis Zye protein is a reproductive protein and if the magnitude of intraspecific structural polymorphism reflected part of an adaptive evolution process. Nevertheless, intraspecific H-fibroin structural heterogeneity in A. grandis was even greater than A. grandis Zye (Table 3). To summarize, A. grandis H-fibroin was substantially more structurally diverse than nonrepetitive silk genes, L-fibroin and PEVK-like, but more similar in diversity to the repetitive proteins that were not silk related, resilin A/B, and especially Zye. This suggests that the observed H-fibroin diversity was not strictly due to population-level processes, but was driven by repeat-mediated mechanisms, such as unequal exchange, replication slippage, and gene conversion.

The heterogeneity in H-fibroin within a single species population provides a foundation for understanding the interspecific diversity of H-fibroin previously observed across caddisflies (Heckenhauer et al. 2023). Owing to their repetitive structures and accompanying suite of repeat-mediated mechanisms, H-fibroins are well-suited for rapid evolution, accounting for the diversity of silks across Amphiesmenoptera. The heterozygosity observed within a caddisfly population of a single species suggests caution is warranted when drawing conclusions from H-fibroin or spidroin sequence comparisons between species based on single individuals of those species, and even individuals of the same species from dispersed geographical regions. The results suggest population-level studies with haplotype-resolved assemblies are essential for characterizing the molecular evolution of silk proteins. To fully unravel the impact of H-fibroin heterogeneity on the silk phenotype, future work should combine mechanical measurements with genome sequencing.

Conclusion

Our population study of H-fibroin primary structure has revealed an extraordinary level of structural polymorphism and rapid evolution within and between two close but separated populations of a retreat-building/net-spinning caddisfly species. On one hand, the polymorphisms suggest selection is remarkably permissive on variations in the length of H-fibroin (∼25%) and number of repeating blocks (50%). On the other hand, the high-resolution indel analysis revealed strong selective constraints on the type and structure of tolerated indels: indel event loci were not randomly distributed, the bipartite primary structure was strongly conserved, repeating [(SX)nE]m blocks only tolerated a 44-residue indel perhaps necessary to preserve the block's higher-order tertiary structure, the C-terminal side of the primary sequence was more prone to changes in the structure and number of repeating blocks than the N-terminal side, the GLGPH spacer segments tolerated only pentameric indels, and the lengths of other G/P spacers were highly constrained. The limited variety and locations of indels point to critical features of H-fibroin structure that were conserved in functional capture net silk. The results provide a detailed example of how nature does polymer chemistry, which may ultimately provide critical insights into the technological exploitation of caddisfly silks.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Larval individuals of A. grandis were collected from two locations in Utah County, Utah, USA, the American Fork River (40.433333, −111.738611, elev. 1,590 m; eight individuals) and the Diamond Fork River (40.083925, −111.354537, elev. 1,700 m; ten individuals) (Fig. 1b). Collected larvae were first identified morphologically by examining the shape of the apotome and verifying the presence of single setae on setal areas two and three of the abdomen (Wiggins 1996). Post sequencing, identifications were confirmed by extracting the DNA barcode region of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I gene from the genome and comparing it against the Barcode of Life Data Systems (BOLD) (Ratnasingham and Hebert 2007).

Because the H-fibroin gene locus is too long to be obtainable via targeted capture or DNA amplification methods, whole genomes were obtained from each sample. Each sample was flash frozen in liquid N2 followed by DNA from whole bodies using the Qiagen Genomic Tip DNA extraction kit. DNA was visualized with an Agilent Femto Pulse and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer. If DNA was of sufficient quality (high molecular weight and sufficient yield), it was sheared to ∼18 bp with a Diagenode Megarupter. PacBio HiFi libraries were prepared from each sample using the SMRTbell express template prep kit 2.0 (PacBio). Libraries were then sequenced on either the PacBio Sequel II or PacBio Revio instruments. For one individual, Hi-C data was generated to provide chromosome-length scaffolding, following the protocol outlined in Eichert et al. (2025).

Genome assembly

The genomes of each individual were assembled from PacBio HiFi reads using hifiasm 0.19.8 (Cheng et al. 2021), which generates two haplotype assemblies. Chromosome-length scaffolding was also performed on one individual following the protocol outlined in Eichert et al. (2025).

H-fibroin extraction and annotation

The terminal sequences from the previously published H-fibroin protein in A. grandis (Frandsen et al. 2023) were extracted and a tblastn (Camacho et al. 2009) search of the termini against the genomes for the 36 new A. grandis haplotype assemblies was performed. Regions of the genomes where both the N- and C-terminus hit to the same contig in the correct orientation to each other were extracted using the Python package Pyfaidx (Shirley et al. 2015). Two of the A. grandis alternate haplotype assemblies with lower sequencing coverage did not have blast hits from both termini on the same contig and the H-fibroin gene from these genomes could not be extracted. An initial annotation of the extracted region was performed using AUGUSTUS with default parameters (Stanke et al. 2006) followed by a manual check of the annotation in Geneious Prime 2024 (https://www.geneious.com). When manually checking the annotation, it was verified that it included the conserved termini sequences, a single intron at the beginning, and a repetitive middle region which alternated between serine blocks and G-rich regions. If additional introns were detected by AUGUSTUS, removing them did not affect the reading frame of the gene and the resulting translation followed the pattern of the gene's repetitive region, indicating that these were not likely true introns and could be removed from the annotation. To ensure that assembly error did not contribute to the variation that we observed in h-fibroin, we mapped HiFi reads back to the two haplotype H-fibroin assemblies and visually inspected the pile-up images. Lastly, SignalP 6.0 (Teufel et al. 2022) was run on every allele to ensure there was a signal peptide present in each H-fibroin.

Extraction and annotation of comparator proteins

To compare the genetic diversity of H-fibroin compared with other proteins, additional gene sequences were extracted from the A. grandis genomes. These included the silk associated genes, L-fibroin and PEVK-like, and additional repetitive genes, Resilin A/B, and Zye. These were extracted with a tblastn search of previously published sequences from Hesperophylax occidentalis L-fibroin (AIO11229.1) and PEVK-like (AIO11230.1) and B. mori Resilin (NP_001166631.1) against the 36 A. grandis genomes. The gene region and flanking sequences were extracted prior to performing an initial feature annotation with AUGUSTUS (Stanke et al. 2006). If an annotated gene was missing an exon, an additional search for that exon was performed using the reference sequence and it was added to the annotation manually. In search of a highly repetitive structural protein for comparison to H-fibroin, the Drosophila sgs3 protein (NP_524024.1) was used to conduct a BLAST search against the annotated A. grandis reference genome (ASM5162203v2). The search identified the A. grandis Zye protein (XP_077294013.1). The sgs3 protein is a highly repetitive glycoprotein (307 amino acids (a.a.)) component of the adhesive that attaches pupae to a substrate (Borne et al. 2021). Two short (126 a.a. and 99 a.a.) segments in the highly repetitive region of Zye (2,862 a.a.) were 34% identical to sgs3 due to spacing of proline and threonine. Zye is not an ortholog of sgs3. Similar, to the extraction of the other proteins, a tblastn search of the A. grandis Zye protein against the 36 A. grandis genomes was conducted. Annotation of the contig containing the tblastn hit was performed using Helixer (Stiehler et al. 2021) in invertebrate mode.

Indel characterization

H-fibroin protein sequences were aligned with MUSCLE (Edgar 2004) using default parameters. Because some alleles were shared among individuals, identical sequences were deleted from the MUSCLE gapped multiple sequence alignment (MSA) leaving only unique H-fibroin alleles for indel characterization (Table 1). First, instead of using any individual sequence as a reference to determine the direction of insertions and deletions, a modal locus alignment string was constructed. For each alignment column (locus) the gaps (−) and residues (not −) were counted for all sequences. A majority rule (>50%) was applied to determine the mode at each locus. If the number of gaps was greater than the number of residues, the modal locus was identified as a gap. If the number of residues was greater than the number of gaps, the modal locus was identified as a residue. If a column had more than one residue, we set the modal locus to the most common residue. The result was a reference-free, consensus-like gapped sequence built using all sequences from both populations.

Second, each alignment position of each H-fibroin allele was compared with the modal locus. If the allele contained a residue and the modal was a gap, the allele locus was marked as an insertion. If the allele had a gap and the modal a residue, the locus was marked as a deletion. If both the allele and the modal had gaps, it was marked as a gap space. For each allele, a contiguous run of columns marked insertion were merged into a single insertion event with a length equal to the run length. Likewise for deletions, contiguous runs of deletions were identified as a single deletion event with a length equal to the run length. The sequence of each insertion event was extracted. The modal sequence was extracted for deletions. The same indel could occur in multiple alleles. To create a table of unique indels, indels of the same type (insertion vs. deletion), with the same start-end location, and with the same inserted sequence were counted only once (deduplicated). For example, a 44 residue indel occurred as 94 events, only 16 were unique events after deduplication. To investigate if MUSCLE identified indels specific to its alignment algorithm in regions with direct sequence repeats, indels were also identified and extracted from a PRANK (Löytynoja 2014) MSA of the deduplicated H-fibroin alleles. The same 44 residue indels were identified in the PRANK alignment but with some alternate registration shifts.

SX44 motif scans

The 44 residue indels identified in the MUSCLE MSA contained two (SX)nE motifs and are therefore referred to hereafter as SX44 indels. The 16 SX44 indels had the same sequence but the sequence differed in registration (Table 2). This occurs because the alignment algorithm is placing a 44 amino acid insertion or gap into a MSA with direct repeats. The insertion can be slid either direction in the sequence to satisfy the algorithm's criteria. To visualize the locations of the SX44-indel in the H-fibroin sequences, a sliding window scan was done with one of the SX44-indel sequence. Each allele sequence was scanned one residue at a time. At each window start position, the %identity of the SX44 window to the underlying allele sequence was calculated and plotted at the center point of the window.

(SX)nE spacer characterization

To evaluate the non-SX44 indels that occurred in the spacer (or linker) regions between [(SX)nE]m regions, the positions of all (SX)nE motifs along each allele were identified and recorded. The spacers were identified as the sequence beginning with the first residue after the E in each (SX)nE motif and ending with the last residue before the S of the next (SX)nE motif. The spacer sequences were extracted from each allele in both populations. The spacers fell into three categories: short spacers (11, 13, 15, 17, or 18 residues) between closely spaced (SX)nE motifs, and long G-rich spacers that contained repeating GLGPHn motifs, and long G-rich spacers that did not contain H. The long spacers all contained one or more single (SX)3E motifs. The sequences, lengths, and positions of all spacers were tabulated. The short spacers were highly consistent in sequence and order within the [(SX)nE]m regions. Therefore, only the polymorphic long spacers were analyzed in detail.

Use of AI

Much of the sequence analysis was done using Python scripts generated by ChatGPT 5.2. The separate scripts for Indel Characterization, SX44 Motif Scans, and SX Spacer Characterization were downloaded and independently evaluated on test sequences outside of the ChatGPT environment to confirm accuracy. The scripts are available on Dryad (see Data and resource availability). None of the manuscript writing was done using AI.
