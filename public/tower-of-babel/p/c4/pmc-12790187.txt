Multimodal Imaging of Papillary Muscle-Origin Left Ventricular Myxoma
Authors: Lei Liu, Ling Chen, Hui Chen, Lei Tao, Yanfei Mo, Jing Yao, Xuelin Yang, Aijuan Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 49-year-old asymptomatic man with no prior cardiovascular history or symptoms such as fever, syncope, or heart failure was incidentally found to have an intracavitary left ventricular mass during routine transthoracic echocardiography. His vital signs were stable (blood pressure: 117/69 mm Hg, heart rate: 77 beats/min), and physical examination revealed normal cardiopulmonary auscultation without murmurs or lower limb edema. The echocardiogram showed a moderately echogenic 27.5 × 16.5 mm mass in the midlateral left ventricular wall near the anterior papillary muscle, with indistinct borders relative to adjacent structures (Figures 1A and 1B, Videos 1 and 2). Color Doppler examination using parasternal long-axis, modified parasternal short-axis, and apical 4-chamber views revealed no detectable intratumoral flow signals. This absence was likely because of acoustic limitations, such as near-field clutter artifacts and resolution constraints, given the Nyquist limit of 15 to 25 cm/s at this depth.

Medical History

The patient had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or surgery, and denied smoking, alcohol consumption, or other risk factors.

Investigations

Contrast-enhanced echocardiography revealed slightly reduced perfusion within the mass compared with the adjacent myocardium. Computed tomography (CT) detected a low-density lesion (CT value: 54 HU; noncontrast images) with irregular morphology, partially merging with the chordae tendineae (Figures 1C and 1D). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) (3.0-T) identified a 33 × 19 × 20 mm mass adjacent to the left ventricular free wall, appearing homogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequences (Figure 1E). Delayed enhancement imaging showed no early enhancement but exhibited marked “cloud-like” enhancement during the delayed phase (Figure 1F). Additionally, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT showed moderately increased metabolic activity, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 7.8; this finding was observed alongside physiological uptake in the surrounding myocardium (Figure 2). According to the interpretative criteria, the SUVmax fell below the benignity threshold of 10.1 Although some multimodal imaging features raised suspicion of malignancy, the overall findings—including low CT attenuation, homogeneously high T2 signal on CMR, cloud-like delayed enhancement, and moderate metabolic activity on PET/CT—were more consistent with a benign lesion.

Management

The patient had the cardiac mass surgically resected. During surgery, a mass measuring 35 × 30 × 30 mm was identified in the left ventricle. The lesion had a smooth surface, firm consistency, and indistinct margins. It adhered to the papillary muscle and chordae tendineae, with invasive growth into the ventricular wall. Extensive adhesion to the left ventricular myocardium and papillary muscle necessitated a subtotal resection. Pathological examination revealed a gray-white, solid mass with moderate consistency. Microscopy revealed myxoid, edematous stroma with slit-like thin-walled vessels, scattered stellate or short spindle cells, and areas of infarction with both acute and chronic inflammatory infiltrates (Figure 3A). Immunohistochemistry indicated tumor cell expression of SMA+, CD34+++, and CD31+++, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 1% (Figure 3B). The final diagnosis confirmed a left ventricular myxoma.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient recovered satisfactorily after surgery and was discharged 1 week later. Serial surveillance through transthoracic echocardiography conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively demonstrated preserved left ventricular function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% to 65%. There was no indication of mitral regurgitation or recurrence of the mass in the anterior papillary region. Continued annual assessments confirmed sustained stability over 23 months, and comprehensive imaging revealed no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

Cardiac myxoma is one of the most common benign primary cardiac tumors, typically arising in the left atrium.2,3 However, it is rarely found in the left ventricle. Relevant literature indicates that left ventricular myxomas comprise only 3% to 4% of all cardiac myxomas.4 This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of ventricular myxomas mimicking malignancy. The incidental detection of a papillary muscle-anchored tumor with pseudoinvasive growth (initially suggestive of malignant tumor on transthoracic echocardiography) underscores the limitations of echocardiography in characterizing atypical cardiac masses.4 Multimodality imaging played a crucial role in diagnosis. CT demonstrated a low-attenuation lesion (54 HU). CMR revealed homogeneous T2 hyperintensity, indicative of high water content in the myxomatous matrix. The delayed-phase “cloud-like” enhancement on CMR, caused by gadolinium pooling in mucopolysaccharide-rich stroma, was a key benign indicator despite apparent myocardial infiltration.5 PET/CT showed moderate FDG uptake (SUVmax = 7.8), below the malignant threshold of >10.6

The pseudoinvasive growth pattern observed during resection, where the tumor enveloped the papillary muscle and chordae without histological evidence of destructive invasion, represents an underreported phenomenon in ventricular myxomas. Microscopic findings of SMA+/CD34+++ stromal cells and minimal proliferation (Ki-67 = 1%) confirmed the imaging profile and benign nature.7 This asymptomatic ventricular case highlights the tumor's capacity for silent progression. The decision to perform subtotal resection carefully balanced the goal of tumor debulking with the need to preserve mitral apparatus integrity, guided by 3 key principles. First, a risk-benefit analysis revealed that complete excision might compromise papillary muscle integrity, potentially causing severe mitral regurgitation and necessitating valve replacement, as indicated by Fucci et al,8 while also risking ventricular rupture due to deep pseudoinvasive penetration. Second, anatomical considerations highlighted that tumor encasement of the chordae-papillary complex and myocardial penetration over 5 mm justified a conservative resection approach.9 And third, a safety strategy was adopted wherein the residual tumor burden, remaining under 5% volume, was deemed manageable given its low Ki-67 index, especially when compared with the unacceptable operative mortality risk associated with more radical surgery. Overall, residual microscopic disease in these benign, low-proliferation lesions poses a lower risk of recurrence than ventricular dysfunction resulting from aggressive resection.10

This case significantly contributes to medical literature, documenting a rare instance of ventricular myxoma originating from the papillary muscle, with pseudoinvasive features. The patient's 23-month recurrence-free follow-up period reinforces 2 key clinical insights: First, comprehensive multimodality imaging should be prioritized over echocardiography when atypical features suggest malignancy, and second, conservative surgical resection is sufficient for histologically confirmed myxomas with low proliferative activity, despite complex anatomical involvement. These findings advocate for future clinical practice to prioritize timely use of CMR and PET/CT to avoid diagnostic delays, especially when echocardiography identifies ventricular masses attached to subvalvular structures. The case underscores the importance of advanced imaging techniques in differentiating benign from malignant cardiac tumors and supports conservative surgical approaches for selected myxoma cases.

Conclusions

We have described a left ventricular myxoma exhibiting pseudoinvasive growth involving the papillary muscle and subvalvular apparatus, which initially raised concerns for malignancy on echocardiographic evaluation. Comprehensive imaging assessment, especially CMR, revealed distinctive features such as uniform T2 hyperintensity and a cloud-like delayed enhancement pattern, alongside moderate FDG uptake (SUVmax = 7.8). Despite the tumor's concerning morphology, these preoperative findings strongly indicated a benign diagnosis. The patient underwent successful subtotal resection that preserved mitral valve function, with the tumor's indolent nature further supported by a low proliferation rate (Ki-67 = 1%). This case exemplifies an optimal strategy for managing complex ventricular myxomas, highlighting the value of imaging-guided conservative resection over radical surgery when pathology confirms benign disease, as evidenced by the patient's favorable 23-month recurrence-free outcome. The findings underscore how advanced imaging techniques can guide appropriate surgical decision-making in challenging cardiac tumor cases.
