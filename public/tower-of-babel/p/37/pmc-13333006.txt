Challenges in Collaborative Long‐Term Health Management for Childhood Cancer Survivors Between General Physicians and Pediatric Oncologists in Japan: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Satoshi Kanke, Isamu Amir, Tomoko Waragai, Tetsuro Aita, Toru Naganuma, Sei Takahashi, Hideki Sano, Sugihiro Hamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdvances in treatment have improved the survival rates of childhood cancer, which could make long‐term health management (LTHM) a critical challenge. We aimed to identify challenges and perspectives associated with building a collaborative system between pediatric oncologists (POs) and general physicians (GPs) for LTHM of childhood cancer survivors (CCS).MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted as part of a regional initiative in Fukushima, Japan aimed at establishing a community‐based collaboration model between a university hospital‐based specialized center and local GPs. We performed a thematic analysis of focus group discussions involving seven GPs and three POs to identify professional perspectives and barriers for future collaboration.ResultsThree primary themes were identified. First, GPs recognized their potential to contribute by leveraging daily experience in multidisciplinary community networks, yet they acquired a deeper understanding of survivorship‐specific psychosocial needs and clear consultation channels with POs. Second, POs maintained a strong sense of responsibility for lifelong care but harbored anxieties regarding care discontinuity, which were linked to structural problems, including undefined professional roles and the lack of robust information‐sharing mechanisms. Third, both groups identified that the deep emotional bonds formed among CCS, families, and POs could hinder the involvement of new healthcare providers.ConclusionsEstablishing a collaborative system requires bridging knowledge gaps regarding specific needs for CCS, and implementing structural improvements (e.g., clearly defined roles and effective information‐sharing systems). Integrating these changes together with the values of patients and their families is essential for sustainable long‐term care for CCS in the Japanese healthcare system.

Introduction

Pediatric cancer is a rare disease in Japan, with an estimated 2500 children (aged 0–14) being diagnosed with cancer each year, representing an incidence rate of 1–1.5 cases per 10,000 children under 15 years of age [1]. Common types include leukemia, brain tumors, lymphoma, germ cell tumors, and neuroblastoma, with leukemia and brain tumors being the most prevalent [2]. Although the survival rate was historically poor, recent treatment advances have raised the overall five‐year relative survival rate to approximately 80% [3]. Specifically, high survival rates have been reported for major types, including 88.4% for leukemia and 73.5% for brain tumors [3]. Due to these improved outcomes, approximately one in 10,000 community residents is estimated to be a childhood cancer survivor (CCS; plural is the same abbreviation) [4].

Compared with their peers without a history of cancer, CCS have higher rates of metabolic and cardiac diseases when they become adults. Therefore, long‐term health management (LTHM) including late complications, age‐related comorbidities, and life events, is one of the key challenges for CCS [5]. Collaboration between pediatric oncologists (POs) and healthcare providers managing non‐cancer health issues of CCS is crucial for LTHM, yet the establishment of such systems remains inadequate [6, 7]. Involvement of primary care physicians is anticipated as a key action in this collaborative LTHM [8, 9, 10]. Challenges for the LTHM of CCS include (i) increased workload for primary care, (ii) insufficient knowledge among primary care providers, and (iii) insufficient communication between POs and primary care physicians [11]. Unlike other countries, Japan's healthcare system lacks a clearly defined primary care system and the role of general physicians (GPs) within it. Furthermore, a system for sharing medical information across multiple healthcare institutions has not been established yet [12]. Consequently, the present lack of progress in primary care involvement in LTHM is not only the same as challenges common to other countries but also envisaged as the existence of “underlying challenges” specific to Japan's healthcare system. However, no research has addressed these challenges, so they remain unclear.

This study aimed to explore and identify challenges for building a new system (hereinafter referred to as “the system”) involving GPs in LTHM of CCS in Japan, which has been carried out through discussions between GPs and POs. Clarifying these challenges is expected to provide valuable insights for the future development of systems supporting LTHM of CCS.

Methods

Study Setting and Project Overview

This study was conducted as part of the Chikaishi Project, a regional system development initiative led by the Long‐Term Follow‐up Center for AYA and Childhood Cancer (LFCACC) at Fukushima Medical University Hospital. Although the center manages more than 200 survivors, it faces challenges in addressing common non‐cancer health issues as survivors age. Because it is often inappropriate for a university hospital to handle all such conditions, the Chikaishi Project was launched to establish a collaborative model between POs and community‐based GPs. The project encompasses a broad spectrum of survivors, ranging from individuals with a very low risk of late complications, such as those treated with surgery alone, to those at high risk following intensive multimodal therapy. This inclusive scope is central to the project's aim of establishing a flexible system where role allocation between specialists and GPs is based on the specific clinical needs and risk profiles of each survivor. As shown in Figure 1, the project consists of three phases: system development (Phase 1), implementation and evaluation (Phase 2), and improvement (Phase 3). The present study focuses on data obtained from an online kickoff meeting for Phase 1 of the project, conducted via Zoom on July 13, 2024.

Group Discussion

At the kickoff meeting, a PO (WT) from LFCACC gave a presentation entitled “Current Status and Challenges in LTHM for CCS,” and a GP (KS) gave a presentation entitled “Proposed Collaboration Model between LFCACC and Community‐based GPs.” This was followed by a 60‐min focus group discussion with the theme “Concerns and points of interest regarding collaboration,” conducted remotely via Zoom as a free‐flowing discussion among participating GPs and POs. KS facilitated the group discussion, which was audio‐recorded.

Study Participants

The kickoff meeting targeted GPs in Fukushima Prefecture, POs, and staff from the LFCACC. A total of 17 GPs, three POs, and one nurse participated in the meeting. To capture a comprehensive range of stakeholder perspectives at the project's inception, all attendees were encouraged to share their views. The analysis focused on the 10 participants (7 GPs and 3 POs) who actively engaged in the discussion (Table 1). This sample size (n = 10) is consistent with methodological recommendations for focus group discussions, which suggest 6–12 participants to facilitate rich interaction and ensure the depth of qualitative data [13]. The adequacy of this sample size was evaluated based on the concept of ‘information power’ and the structural context of the regional healthcare system [14]. In Fukushima Prefecture, pediatric oncology care is centered at the university hospital, and the local GP network is tightly connected through Fukushima Medical University. Because the participating GPs strategically included both community‐based clinic physicians and core regional hospitalists, the group successfully encompassed the essential stakeholders required to build the collaborative framework. This diverse composition allowed the discussion to capture critical nuances regarding how institutional scale shapes the GP's role, rendering the sample highly informative and structurally sufficient for the project's inception.

Data Analysis

The data were transcribed from the group discussion audio and subjected to thematic analysis. First, to avoid professional bias, KS (GP) and WT (PO) independently read all discussion utterances and performed initial coding. To ensure methodological rigor, a reflexivity statement describing the primary analysts' backgrounds is provided. KS is a male general physician with 20 years of clinical experience in both primary care clinics and university hospitals, currently focusing on general medicine education. WT is a female pediatric oncologist with 19 years of experience as a pediatrician, including 7 years in pediatric oncology and 3 years in managing long‐term follow‐up care. Having collaborated for over a year in planning this regional project, the researchers possessed a well‐established shared understanding, which facilitated consensus but posed a risk of conceptual bias. During data interpretation, the researchers' distinct professional backgrounds influenced how they perceived the data: the GP (KS) placed greater emphasis on the logistical and structural division of labor among medical institutions, whereas the PO (WT) focused intensely on the continuity of patient care and the oncologist's lifelong responsibility.

To mitigate this professional subjectivity and ensure that the findings genuinely reflected the participants' authentic perspectives, AI—a researcher with a PhD in linguistics, specializing in qualitative discourse analysis and communication theory, and having empirical research experience in peer‐support systems for adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer survivors—participated as an external auditor. Importantly, AI was not involved in the focus group discussion itself. The three researchers (KS, WT, and AI) collaboratively reviewed all transcripts and initial codes together. By cross‐examining every utterance against the literal and linguistic meanings of the participants' statements, they resolved discrepancies, refined the codes, and extracted subthemes. Subsequently, KS, WT, and AI then categorized these subthemes to develop the final overarching themes. Regarding the decision that additional discussions were unnecessary, the research team evaluated sample adequacy based on conceptual depth rather than full code‐level exhaustion. Because this study was designed as the initial exploratory phase (Phase 1) to map out the foundational framework for collaboration, the primary focus was on capturing the overarching structural and professional barriers. The facilitator (KS) employed active prompting techniques to ensure all participants articulated their core perspectives. During the analysis, the team determined that this single session yielded sufficient conceptual depth to robustly construct the primary themes and the resulting collaborative care model. Therefore, conducting additional focus groups was deemed unnecessary at this initial stage, as the core systemic challenges had been comprehensively identified to fulfill the study objective. This study was planned based on the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [15].

Ethical Considerations

This research plan was reviewed and approved by the Fukushima Medical University General Research Ethics Committee (REC2024‐189). Following the kickoff meeting, the research plan was explained to the group discussion participants, and permission for the use of their data and consent to participate in the study were obtained.

Perspectives and Concerns by GPs

GPs felt that their practice style, which involves treating the patient as a whole rather than focusing on specific organs, offers (i) “potential contributions” for LTHM of CCS. However, they also expressed (ii) “concerns about engaging with CCS,” including limited experience with these patients and difficulties consulting physicians familiar with their past medical history when challenges arise.

Thoughts and Anxieties by POs

POs felt that watching over the patient's growth was part of their role, in addition to treating the disease. On the one hand, they possessed (i) “pride as pediatricians” and wanted to “provide lifelong care” for CCS. On the other, they also felt (ii) “anxiety about the connection between CCS and healthcare providers” when entrusting long‐term follow‐up to others.

Challenges in Collaboration

Challenges identified in LTHM of CCS included: (i) “Insufficient knowledge and experience among GPs,” (ii) “A distant relationship between GPs and POs,” where mutual understanding of each other's clinical practices, workload, and appropriate communication methods was lacking, as observed in discussions. (iii) “Difficulties in collaboration between university hospitals and community‐based medical institutions,” derived from differences in scale and organizational structure, the lack of direct relationships between physicians, and difficulties in information sharing and coordination.

CCS and their guardians have deep relationships with healthcare providers based on their past treatment experiences. When GPs, who were unfamiliar with the childhood treatment history, became newly involved, (iv) “difficulties in responding to the feelings of patients and families” were also reported.

Discussion

The analysis of the interview data revealed the thoughts and concerns of GPs regarding their involvement in LTHM of CCS; the thoughts and concerns of POs; and the challenges in collaboration between the two groups. Based on these findings, we propose a collaborative care model that integrates university hospital‐based specialized care with community‐based primary care (Figure 2).

Perspectives of GPs

The GPs involved in this study recognized that they “have areas where they can contribute” to LTHM of CCS. This perception is likely related to the characteristics of primary care GPs, such as “first contact care,” “longitudinality,” “comprehensiveness,” and “coordination of care” [16]. Particularly in Fukushima Prefecture, the region of this study, university hospitals cover extensive medical service areas, with a radius of over 100 km. Therefore, involvement by GPs located within the patients' communities offers multiple advantages. These include improved convenience for visits, management of general illnesses not typically handled by oncologists, and support leveraging local resources.

Furthermore, GPs already possess experience in multidisciplinary and multi‐agency collaboration through their daily practices, working with local health, welfare, and educational institutions to support elderly patients and patients with chronic conditions. Therefore, they can be expected to provide support to CCS and their families by leveraging existing community networks. However, addressing challenges specific to CCS, including education, employment, and psychological support, requires a deeper understanding of age‐appropriate developmental tasks and life‐stage‐specific challenges, as well as family backgrounds.

The actual roles GPs can undertake vary depending on their workplace and facility functions. Physicians working in primary care clinics can provide continuous care focused on managing lifestyle‐related diseases and chronic conditions. However, physicians at core regional hospitals, whose work centers on inpatient care and specialized treatment, may face constraints in providing ongoing outpatient support. Considering collaboration in LTHM of CCS, it is crucial to implement matching that consciously considers the appropriate division of labor, based on the working environments of GPs and the regional healthcare system.

One reason GPs may feel uneasy about participating in LTHM of CCS is a lack of knowledge and experience regarding potential future conditions, including late complications and recurrence risks. As identified in the results, some GPs expressed uncertainty about whether observed symptoms were truly unrelated to previous cancer. This suggests that because cancer treatment is rapidly evolving due to advances in drugs and radiation therapy, it is unrealistic for general physicians, who are not cancer specialists, to constantly keep abreast of the side effects and long‐term risks of individual treatments. A system enabling GPs to selectively access the information necessary for individual patient care might potentially reduce their learning burden while improving the quality of care.

Therefore, it is essential to establish an environment in which GPs can easily consult or request assistance from POs when uncertain during consultations. Discussions revealed that physicians who had previously experienced “closed consultation channels after referrals” hesitated to engage in subsequent care. This suggests that establishing a relationship with POs enabling ongoing consultation is key to lowering the psychological barrier for GPs to engage in LTHM for CCS.

Perspectives of POs

It was clarified that POs do not have a clear vision for the division of labor with GPs in LTHM of CCS. Japan has fewer GPs compared with other countries [17], making it difficult for POs to envision concrete collaboration models. This can lead to confusion and hesitation during the initial stages of building collaboration between GPs and POs.

Furthermore, POs have developed deep trust relationships with patients and families they have treated in long‐term, maintaining psychological connections even after treatment ends. Previous research has also shown that CCS who experienced long‐term hospitalization and intensive treatment often develop strong relationships with their PO, resulting in a robust therapeutic alliance [18]. Although such relationships are a source of reassurance for patients and families, they can also intensify physicians' sense of mission and responsibility to “continue providing care themselves,” as well as their anxiety about potential discontinuity in follow‐up.

Against this psychological backdrop, POs may feel uneasy about entrusting the LTHM of CCS to other healthcare providers, fearing that follow‐up might be interrupted. Indeed, this study identified concerns such as “We do not know how they are doing after referral” and “We do not know how they are being treated at other facilities.” This reflects a structural problem derived from unclear methods for sharing medical information and undefined professional responsibilities. Therefore, it is essential for POs to ensure continuity of care for CCS, with clearly defined roles for healthcare providers for collaboration and a robust information‐sharing mechanism.

It is also significant for POs to collaborate with GPs with confidence for ongoing communication and feedback opportunities regarding LTHM of CCS. For example, mechanisms are needed to clarify each party's role during coordination and to share regular progress updates afterward. This would enable POs to entrust care to GPs with relief while enabling GPs to seek advice when needed. Fostering such mutual trust is expected to reduce the psychological burden on POs and provide reassurance to patients and families.

Common Challenges for GPs and POs

A challenge recognized by both GPs and POs was difficulty in addressing the feelings of patients and their families. In particular, CCS who experienced prolonged hospitalization during childhood and their families tend to develop close relationships with healthcare providers throughout the treatment process, often forming strong bonds of trust [19]. Effective collaboration between GPs and POs requires sharing not only medical information but also information grounded in the family's narrative and experiences of battling the disease. Careful dialogue that helps patients and families articulate and share their values also contributes to building trust among them, GPs and POs. However, under the current Japanese medical fee system, there are no explicit additional fees for clinical coordination or multidisciplinary information sharing for CCS, except after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Therefore, it is essential to establish a comprehensive incentive structure that formally recognizes and evaluates the significant time and professional expertise required for such coordination. Implementing a policy‐level framework that appropriately compensates for the temporal and cognitive burden of joint care planning would not only provide a financial foundation but also signal a systemic commitment to long‐term survivorship care, thereby motivating both GPs and POs to sustain active and effective collaboration.

Limitations of the Study

This study has several limitations. First, it is a qualitative survey targeting GPs and POs interested in LTHM of CCS in a single prefecture of Japan. Second, because the data were collected from a single 60‐min focus group session during a project kickoff meeting, full data saturation at a granular code level may not have been completely achieved, meaning that some subtle individual perspectives or minor variations might not have been fully captured. However, the session provided sufficient conceptual depth to identify the primary systemic and professional barriers necessary for the initial phase of the project. Third, this qualitative study focused on healthcare providers and did not directly examine actual clinical practices or patient outcomes. The “collaboration challenges” and “concerns” identified here are based on perceptions by providers. Fourth, this study did not directly collect opinions from the CCS themselves or their families. Therefore, the collaboration challenges described from the providers' perspective may not fully reflect the needs and values of patients and families. Fifth, since the participants were recruited from a project kickoff meeting, they represent healthcare providers who likely have a higher‐than‐average interest in LTHM of childhood cancer survivors. Consequently, the findings may be subject to selection bias, given that the perspectives of physicians who are less engaged or less interested in such collaborative efforts were not captured.

Conclusion

This study identified three key themes regarding LTHM of CCS: (i) GPs' perspectives and concerns, (ii) POs' thoughts and anxieties, and (iii) challenges in collaborations. Although GPs recognized their potential to contribute by leveraging their extensive experience in multidisciplinary community networks, they faced barriers, including the need for a deeper understanding of childhood cancer survivor‐specific developmental issues and uncertainty regarding consultation channels. POs expressed significant anxiety regarding care discontinuity, which was found to be deeply rooted in structural problems, including undefined professional responsibilities and the lack of robust information‐sharing mechanisms.

A shared challenge recognized by both groups was the difficulty of addressing the deep emotional bonds and trust formed among survivors, their families, and original oncologists during treatment. Moving forward, it will be essential to establish a sustainable model that incorporates clearly defined roles for all collaborating healthcare providers and effective information‐sharing systems. Integrating these structural improvements with the lived experiences and values of patients and families will be vital to ensuring seamless continuity of LTHM for CCS in Japan.
