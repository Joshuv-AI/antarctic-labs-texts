Femtosecond-laser-assisted Small Incision Lenticule Extraction to Treat Refractive Error: A Narrative Review
Authors: Ashjan Y. Bamahfouz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
With the approval of the Federal Drug Agency of the USA in 2018, femtosecond laser-assisted small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) became an officially available option in several countries including Saudi Arabia to treat myopia and astigmatism. Intense marketing by industries has placed both clients and corneal surgeons into a dilemma of the plus and minuses of this option. Although several reviews compare SMILE to other conventional methods, especially laser in situ keratomileusis, this narrative review perhaps for the first time offers a detailed summary of this procedure and compares two sets of published literature, by authors from China and other countries. The efficacy and safety of SMILE seem to outnumber the complications and high cost of the investment. Its long-term usefulness for treating high myopia and hyperopia is still a matter of research.

Introduction

The world is facing an epidemic of myopia with an estimated 1406 million people with myopia. This is 23% of the population in the year 2000 and numbers are likely to increase to 4758 million in the year 2050.[1] The public health approach to address myopia mainly focuses on prevention, early detection, and provision of visual aids and thus avoid visual disabilities due to uncorrected myopia.[2] However, more and more young populations with myopia prefer spectacle-free vision using contact lenses or by undergoing refractive surgeries.[3] Corneal refractive surgeries include laser in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), Lenticule extraction (FLEx), and small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE)[4] The advent of femtosecond laser has improved the technique to reshape the cornea by LASIK and extract the intrastromal corneal lenticule.[5] A study in the USA reported that 95% of persons undergoing laser refractive surgeries experienced improved vision-related quality of life in addition to being free from spectacles dependence.[6] The cost of SMILE in Europe was estimated to be 335.5 euros compared to 347 euros for femtosecond-LASIK (FS-LASIK) and 443 euros for photorefractive keratectomy (PRK). This high cost of corneal refractive surgeries needed 155 persons to be treated annually by SMILE, compared to 136 by PRK and 155 by FS-LASIK for these procedures to be cost-effective.[7] Institutions with high-volume corneal surgeries in rapidly evolving economies and developing countries could further afford to upgrade these technologies periodically.

Although several research articles have studied the comparison of SMILE with FS-LASIK the two commonly used corneal refractive surgeries, it is interesting to note that the former technique was introduced in 2012 in other parts of the world, but the Federal Drug Agency (FDA) approved it only in 2016 for myopia and in 2018 for myopic astigmatism. A detailed procedure and management guidelines were also proposed.[8910] Many publications on SMILE are therefore from China where myopia is highly prevalent and this technology was introduced earlier. Keeping this possible publication bias in mind, we reviewed the SMILE surgery for myopia and other types of refractive error.

We present SMILE surgery’s usefulness in addressing refractive errors in Arab patients aiming to achieve spectacle-free vision.

Methods

Ethical issue

The ethical and research board members of Umm Al-Qura University, Makkah, Saudi Arabia approved of this review. Since no patient was directly involved in the study, patient consent was waived. The review was carried out from January to September 2023.

Literature search

We used “femtosecond-laser-assisted Small incision lenticule extraction” + “Human” words to search for the articles. We used PUBMED, Google Scholar, Scopus, Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, and OpenMD search engines to identify relevant published articles. We used a filter of the “English” language for full articles or abstracts on these topics. The collected articles underwent three levels of screening. Of the total 2030 articles, first level of screening was done based on relevant titles. We had 353 articles that underwent second level of screening. There were 171 articles by Chinese authors with full articles in the local language. Another 172 articles were in English and other languages with English translations. The abstract screening (2nd screening) was performed and 9 articles were removed as it was on SMILE but not for refractive surgery. Fifteen case reports were not included for subsequent review. The rest 329 were further studied for their relevance to different themes of refractive surgeries. PDF files of important articles such as systematic reviews, reviews, meta-analyses, book chapters, clinical trials, and cohort studies in English were collected and reviewed.

We limited the article search published in the last 10 years and then studied in detail all the articles on the topic published since 2018. Duplications in articles from different search sites were removed. The collection of research articles was grouped based on the level of evidence [Figure 1]. We used GRADE criteria to assess the quality of evidence in the published literature on the topic.[11] We also referred to the Critical Appraisal Toolkit to assess the evidence on the review topic.[12]

Results and Discussion

History of small incision lenticule extraction

Although keratorefractive surgeries have been widely practiced since the last century, the introduction of an excimer laser for corneal surgeries in 1988 was a game-changer. It was used initially for PRK but approval by the US FDA in 1995 made it the preferred practice for refractive surgery. In the early 1990s, automated lamellar keratoplasty using an excimer laser known as LASIK replaced mechanical debriding of the corneal superficial layers in refractive surgery overcoming its negative side effects. After successful animal trials, femtosecond laser was introduced in corneal surgery at the start of the 21st century. It was used on corneal flaps and LASIK was used for ablation. A company in Germany after exhaustive research introduced a machine for applying a new femtosecond laser to carve out a lenticule within the cornea. The corneal lenticule carved by femtosecond laser can be extracted by creating and lifting a hinged flap (FLEx) or through a small incision in the cornea (SMILE). Femtosecond laser can scan the cornea in 3 dimensions, is more accurate, can produce a lenticule of desired thickness and diameter, and can be tailored for correcting errors in different axes. The entire SMILE procedure can be performed on one platform and avoiding shifting of patients as well as investing in two lasers made it cost-effective and popular among cornea surgeons. With several improvements from 2000 to 2011, it was introduced for refractive surgeries worldwide. US FDA approved SMILE for the treatment of spherical myopia in 2016. Now, more than a million SMILE procedures are carried out annually worldwide.[131415] LASIK using femtosecond was used to treat hyperopia earlier. However, reshaping the lenticule carved by femtosecond laser and reintroducing it in the corneal stromal pocket to correct hyperopia is a more recently practiced procedure.[1617]

Indications

SMILE procedure is indicated in mild-to-moderate myopia correction as it is safe, effective, predictable, and stable both in short-term and long-term follow-ups. In eyes with a mean-5.26D myopia, at a mean 6 years after SMILE, 69% of eyes achieved refractive status within ± 0.50D and 86% within ± 1.00D of the attempted SE correction. There was a myopic regression of 0.19D at the last follow-up.[131819] Short-term outcomes of SMILE to treat moderate and high myopia are promising, long-term outcomes are crucial to review the stability of corrections and note possible long-term complications.[12202122] The FDA has approved the use of SMILE to correct myopia from −10.0D and −3.0D astigmatism.[23] SMILE surgery to treat astigmatism is still controversial. It is noted to be not inferior to other conventional corneal refractive surgeries to treat high astigmatism.[24] Undercorrection is a major challenge in treating high astigmatism and researchers proposed different steps to improve the outcomes.[25]

What about hyperopia?

SMILE can also be performed to correct refractive errors in eyes that have undergone previous corneal surgery like deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty.[252627] The lenticule carved using the SMILE procedure has been used for sealing the perforation, covering the corneal ectasia, after pterygium surgery, and removal of dermoid or other limbal tumors.[282930] The corneal lenticule is extracted by SMILE and is experimented with by extracellular matrix-scaffold-based engineering in regenerative therapeutics in several corneal pathologies.[31]

Contraindications

Like other kerato-refractive surgeries, SMILE also is contraindicated in many conditions. The absolute contraindications include patients aged <18 years, certain corneal dystrophies, keratoconus, poorly controlled dry eye, systemic conditions such as active immunological disease, and uncontrolled diabetes. Relative contraindications include the use of retinoids for acne and amiodarone for cardiac arrhythmia, very thin or very thick central cornea, a person with uveitis or glaucoma, and pregnant and lactating women.[323334]

Patient selection and preoperative assessment

Proper patient selection means understanding patients’ requirements aspirations and expectations and ruling out contraindications known to cause setbacks for achieving emmetropia and avoiding/minimizing complications. Comprehensive preoperative assessment includes current refractive status and its stability, tear film assessment, and ruling out infection in the anterior segment with potential intraoperative infection. The cornea is assessed using a slit-lamp biomicroscope, corneal pachymetry, computed corneal topography, and computed video-keratography. The intraocular pressure is measured to rule out uncontrolled glaucoma. The posterior segment is evaluated using different tools for fundoscopy preferably with digital documentation to monitor the impact of the intervention.[10143435]

Patient counseling

Extensive marketing of this new surgery in the American subcontinent after FDA approval has increased expectations of clients. Proper patient counseling at the time of initial assessment is needed to explain the pluses and minuses of this surgery, possible side effects, complications, and nonavailability of long-term outcomes of this surgery. This will help in reducing dissatisfaction, negative feedback about SMILE, and litigation against service providers.[36]

Tools and steps of surgery

The SMILE surgery is performed using Zeiss SMILE software linked to the VisuMax Femtosecond Laser System (Carl Zeiss Meditech AG).[1037] The laser emits photons at a wavelength of 1043 nm and a frequency of 500 kHz. The laser settings on the standard mode are programmed by the manufacturer. The fast mode is customizable according to the region of the corneal to ablet. The expert mode can be optimized by the surgeon.[38] The steps of SMILE surgery for myopia and astigmatism are described in detail by many publications.[103738]

Steps of SMILE surgery include docking, femtosecond laser application, lenticule dissection, and lenticule extraction. The patients fixate green light of the system and machine fixes on the corneal surface. After proper centration is confirmed, suction is applied. Software-assisted customized setting parameters include an anterior cap depth of 120 µm, a side cut of 2 to 5 mm, 6.0 mm of optical zone, and a 0.10-mm transition zone if the eye has astigmatic refraction. The diameter of the cap is kept 1 mm larger than the lenticule diameter. The laser energy of 100 to 160 nJ is applied in pulses. Intrastromal lenticule is created using a femtosecond laser starting from outward and going to the center. In contrast, the cap is cut from the center to the periphery. The outer edges of uniform bubble layers help the surgeon to identify the lenticule edges. A side cut at the cap, a hooked instrument is inserted to separate the anterior lenticule plane from the overlying cap. The posterior plane is dissected by blunt dissection in the opposite direction to avoid lenticule misdissection. The peripheral part of the lenticule is dissected last. Using the technique for capsulorrhexis in cataract surgery, the lenticule is pulled from the side cut of the cap with care not to damage the stromal bed and break the lenticule. The area is irrigated with a sterile balanced salt solution. The cap is smoothened on the stromal bed using merocel sponges [Figure 2].

Postoperative care

Patients after SMILE surgery should not drive, not use makeup, avoid water sports, rest, avoid reading and writing, watch TV and use digital devices for at least 12 h, not do strenuous exercise for 3 days, and avoid smoke and dusty environment. The patient should apply medications in the operated eye and continue ongoing systemic medications as suggested by the doctor. At the first follow up usually 24 h after surgery, the patient should inform the doctor about symptoms experienced. Fluctuation in vision, halos and glare around light, sensitivity to light causing watering and closure of lids, and feeling of dryness are transient problems and usually go away after 3 to 6 months.[3940]

Preservative-free artificial tears are prescribed after surgery and continued as suggested by the doctor at the follow-up visits. The patient is advised to use protective glasses to avoid bright light and trauma. A detailed slit-lamp assessment of the cornea is carried out after 24 h of surgery. The status of the corneal interphase from where the lenticule was separated and the healing of epithelium at the small corneal wound from where the lenticule was removed is assessed. Based on the amount of inflammation, presence of any foreign body at interphase, and excessive meibomian secretion, further mode of management is decided. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) are assessed, and the patient is assured about the temporary nature of symptoms.

Intra-ocular pressure (IOP) measurement after SMILE surgery is crucial but often challenging. There is a significant reduction in IOP in the operated eye. But of the four tonometry methods, biomechanically corrected IOP by Corvis ST and the dynamic contour tonometer-IOP provided better accuracy of IOP compared to the Goldmann applanation tonometry and by ocular response analyzer.[41] These changes are primarily due to changes in the biomechanical properties of the cornea following the SMILE surgery.[42]

Outcome indicators

To evaluate the outcomes of SMILE surgeries various indicators are used. The goal is to provide spectacle-free distance vision without complications, and offer a high quality of life and patient satisfaction.

Uncorrected visual acuity for distance

Refractive surgeries are performed on persons of younger ages. Their main concern therefore would be emmetropia for distance and esthetic look improvement by becoming free of spectacle and hassles of maintaining contact lenses. The index of good outcomes for SMILE surgery would be getting sustained 20/20 UCVA or better. In cases of moderate and high myopia, SMILE was found to reach this goal and sustain it even 7 years after the procedure.[2043] In another study, of 51 eyes with myopia of more than-10.0D, the surgeon achieved UCVA 0.03 ± 0.12 after SMILE surgery.[44] The UCVA on day 1 after SMILE was 20/20 in 92% of eyes and then declined to 84% at 3 months follow-up.[45] In 374 eyes with mild and moderate hyperopia, 1 year after SMILE surgery, the surgeon achieved 20/20 visual acuity in 83% of operated eyes.[17] Thus, apart from eyes with high astigmatism and high hyperopia, long-term visual gains after SMILE surgery seem to be promising.

Efficacy

Mild and moderate myopia and hyperopia are efficiently corrected by SMILE surgery. Six years after this surgery, the efficacy index was 0.86. Sixty-nine percent of eyes were within ± 0.50D and 86% within ± 1.00D of the attempted SE correction.[19] Using indicators of efficacy like uncorrected distance visual acuity of 20/20 and ± 1.0D refractive status postoperatively, Yan et al. in a meta-analysis noted SMILE surgery to be efficient for treating myopia.[46] Earlier studies were conducted to review SMILE to be noninferior to convectional femtosecond-assisted LASIK surgeries. However, with improved software and upgradation of laser energy application, SMILE surgery became more efficient, and its indication expanded to treat hyperopia and high astigmatism. The spherical equivalent 6 months after SMILE in 51 high myopic eyes was −0.06 ± 0.72D.[44] SMILE surgery seems to provide efficient refractive error corrections in the short term and very few cases with high astigmatism, high hyperopia, and high myopia suffer from regressed refractive correction and need additional concurrent procedures such as collagen cross-linkage (CXL).[47]

Safety

Among all corneal refractive surgeries, SMILE surgery especially for myopia is considered a safe option. This is mainly because of a small incision applied on the cornea through which the lenticule is removed, minimum manipulation at the center of the cornea (visual axis), and highly evolved software for precise creation of the lenticule. The software of the machine has three types of settings for operating surgeons; for those with learning curves, for special situations, and for experienced surgeons to modify the parameters as per each eye need. The outer surface ablation is away from the corneal epithelium and Bowman’s membrane sparring the corneal nerve plexus. The size of the lenticule is kept wider than the pupillary diameter and the optical zone. As a result, glare and spherical aberration are less and transient. The selection of patients is an important factor in making this a safe procedure. Detailed assessment for diagnosing preclinical corneal ailments that could cause ectasia is key to success after surgery. Surgeons with a learning curve should be careful to deal with high astigmatism and ensure suction is in place to avoid ocular torsion, whereas the laser is being applied. Patients’ cooperation is crucial. Explaining surgery details and reducing anxiety on the operating table will minimize eye movements at the time of laser application. Five years of follow-up of myopic eyes treated with SMILE procedure revealed that SMILE is safe since it produces fewer intraoperative complications, dry eye, and absence of flap-related postoperative issues.[4849] SMILE surgery is still not safe for correcting myopia of more than 10D and compound hyperopia with risk of undercorrection, regression of correction by time, and ectasia.[16] The safety index of SMILE was 0.98 in a study with a year’s follow-up.[20] Huang and Melki in their review expressed the need for further evidence to declare SMILE a safe surgery compared to LASIK.[50]

Intraoperative complications and management

The main intraoperative complication noted during SMILE surgery is loss of suction. To avoid movement of the eye and maintain the axis while treating high astigmatism, the cornea is stabilized using suction around the cornea and keeps the operative area free of obstruction. The patient is conscious and being asked to fixate on the green spot in the center enables the laser to make lenticule as per the software instruction. If the suction is lost, there is a risk of decentration and due to torsion movement of the eye, the axis of astigmatism gets disturbed. With the updating of the machine and the experience of the surgeon, the incidence of this complication has declined from the range of 0.9%–4.4% in the past to 0.17%–0.93%.[51] Proper fixation of the eye and counseling of the patient to alleviate pain and fear would reduce the risk of suction loss and resultant damage mainly the anterior surface of the lenticule. Remedial measures are described by manufacturers and to some extent prevent serious negative effects on the outcomes. They depend on the event of suction loss occurring from step 1 to step 6 of the surgery.[52] Another complication the surgeon faces during SMILE surgery is opaque bubble layer formation.[51] If meibomian gland dysfunction is not addressed before surgery, the laser is often not effective in separating the posterior surface of the lenticule forming a black spot and separation becomes difficult. If the spot becomes a large, island, the procedure may have to be aborted. This complication poses difficulty during surgery but does not affect visual outcome. Other minor and manageable complications during surgery include subconjunctival hemorrhage, epithelial defect, dissection of lenticule in the wrong plane, lenticule tear, difficulty in removing from a small incision, cap perforation, and ablation decentration. Some complications take place as part of a learning curve and cause the residual refractive error and the introduction of higher-order aberrations.[5354]

Endothelial changes

Since the laser application on corneal stroma is way from both epithelium and endothelium, the risk of damage to viable corneal cells during SMILE surgery is minimal. There was no significant decline in the endothelial cell density 12 months after SMILE surgery was performed to manage moderate myopia and astigmatism.[55]

Postoperative complications and management

The postoperative complications include dry eye syndrome (DES), keratitis, infection, ectasia, epithelial in-growth, photosensitivity, raised intraocular pressure-related stromal keratopathy, and debris and foreign bodies at the stromal interphase. Other rare complications include Bowman’s membrane distortion, endothelial damage, punctate keratitis, and altered corneal biomechanical properties.[51]

Dry eye syndrome

Dry eyes are a major complication of corneal refractive surgery using the laser. Several mechanisms are postulated to cause corneal nerve damage and then DES. They include corneal epithelium damage due to denervation, corneal sensation reduction affecting the blink reflex, suction-induced impaired functioning of limbal goblet cells, surgery-induced inflammations, and side effects of the medications used during and after surgery.[55] DES is more in the first 6 months and subsequently less due to regeneration of the corneal nerves. In the absence of a flap and only a 2-mm long corneal incision to remove the lenticule, no sutures, and no excimer laser, the corneal damage is the least in SMILE surgery. Hence, the risk of DES after SMILE is also less compared to LASIK and other corneal refractive surgeries.[5758] Using tear substitutes, topical anti-inflammatory eye drops, medications to stimulate mucin secretion such as diquafosol tetrasodium and rebamipide 2% solution, autologous serum, topical nerve growth factor, puncta plugs, and treating blepharitis are proven DES management strategies for few weeks after SMILE. Proper counseling patients about the transient nature of DES will help patients cooperate with using prescribed medication postoperatively.[59]

Keratitis

Diffuse lamellar keratitis is noted mainly in the early postoperative period but it could be delayed manifestation also. Although the pathophysiology of the inflammation is not clear, foreign material, high laser energy, and chemical toxicity are a few proposed risk factors. Intense steroid therapy and if needed irrigation of the stromal interphase are performed to manage this complication.[51] The risk of high-energy laser causing such damage in SMILE surgery is less compared to other laser-induced corneal refractive surgeries. Avoiding meibomian secretion or foreign material entering the interphase during surgery is the best preventive measure.

The risk of infection in SMILE surgery is comparatively less than in LASIK as the incision on the cornea is small. Prompt irrigation of interphase with bactericidal agents and antibiotics reduces serious visual sequelae. Preoperative screening to rule out risk factors such as DED, immunocompromised conditions, and strict aseptic measures during surgery would prevent this complication.[60]

Biomechanical changes in cornea

The biomechanical properties of the cornea change after refractive surgeries. Corneal hysteresis and corneal resistance factor are reduced after SMILE but less compared to after LASIK.[61] The tensile strength of the cornea also reduces after SMILE surgery.[62] Corneal ectasia has been reported as a late complication of SMILE. CXL, using rigid gas permeable contact lenses, and if needed lamellar keratoplasty are measures to manage corneal ectasia.[345163]

Other vision-related changes

SMILE surgery may end up with a residual refractive error, new refractive error induced, change in contrast sensitivity, and induction of higher order aberrations mainly vertical coma and spherical type of aberrations.[43] The contrast sensitivity is reduced soon after surgery but after 1 year of surgery, there was no significant difference in CS.[64]

Vision-related quality of life

Because of advantages like surgery without flap, only 2 mm corneal incision to remove the lenticule, established software-assisted technique, minimum IOP fluctuation during surgery, and millions of eyes treated so far in different countries, service providers are happy to adopt SMILE surgery to treat refractive error.[6465] The patients undergoing SMILE surgery also experienced an impact on vision-related quality of life as reported after LASIK and LASIK surgeries.[666768] A small amount of spherical aberration and good contrast sensitivity under mesopic conditions could be a few reasons for patient satisfaction and high vision-related quality of life after SMILE.[686970] Unfortunately higher orders of aberrations existing before refractive surgeries cannot be corrected by SMILE as the central corneal epithelium is not managed in the surgery. While correcting mild and moderate myopia with SMILE surgery, Zhou et al. noted that HOAs such as spherical aberration and coma were more if the optical zone was 6.5 mm instead of 6.8mm. The VQL was not related to the pupillary diameter in the operated eyes.[71]

Cost-effectiveness

Compared to the other corneal refractive surgeries where different lasers were used in the docking stage and for the stromal ablation, SMILE performed by modern technology uses a single machine for laser-assisted procedures and therefore is cost-effective. Although the high cost of this machine is a concern for surgeons and administrators, it is considered cost-effective in the long term. Costs, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) saved, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) are different indicators used to compare the efficiency of different corneal refractive surgeries to manage myopia. The refractive surgery in Europe cost US $ 4050 to US 4100 in 2021. The QALYs for SMILE were 24. Refractive surgery’s role in the prevention of myopia is limited. The strategies for preventing myopia are more cost-effective than corneal refractive surgeries.[72] A study by Balgos et al. in Spain noted that SMILE has the least ICER and therefore more cost-effective than FS-LASIK and PRK. They also found that at least 155 SMILE surgeries should be performed by one machine in a year to earn profit and this was similar for FS-LASIK surgeries.[7]

Patient satisfaction

One of the important indicators for the success of any refractive surgery is patients’ satisfaction with the quality of vision gained after surgery. Reinstein et al. noted that 1 year after SMILE surgery, the satisfaction score was 8 + out of 10 in 94% of patients and a score of 6 out of 10 in 99% of patients with high myopia.[17] Improvement of visual acuity after SMILE is the main reason for patient satisfaction. 93% of patients expressed willingness to undergo SMILE in the fellow eye.[73] Hardly 6% of patients were not completely satisfied as they had problems related to night vision or driving at night. Few patients due to mild dryness of the eye following surgery were partly satisfied with the outcome of SMILE. In a study, 95% of patients undergoing SMILE, expressed their willingness to recommend this surgery to other patients.[74] A large sample and long-term follow-up showing high patient satisfaction after SMILE is very encouraging.

Duration of follow-up

Being a relatively newly approved surgery, publications on the long-term outcomes of SMILE are few. In two studies with 7-year follow-ups of 26 eyes with moderate and high myopia treated by SMILE surgery, the authors noted that the procedure is effective, safe, and stable.[4375] This procedure is performed in young patients. Therefore, the consequences of surgical trauma will be bothersome to them for the rest of their lives. The risk of regression of refractive correction in all corneal surgery cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the long-term outcomes of SMILE need to be studied.

Comparison of small incision lenticule extraction with other conventional corneal refractive surgeries

With the introduction and subsequent revision of software for SMILE procedure, researchers and industries periodically published their findings to compare methods of this new procedure, and the outcomes to the conventional corneal refractive surgeries such as FS-LASIK, PRK, Phakic ICL for treating myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism.[4976] Ease of surgery, evolving software to improve efficacy and safety, minimum HOA, and other complications in SMILE compared to other surgeries, are the keys to higher vision-related quality-of-life scores and patient satisfaction.[777879] However, corneal surgeons face a challenge in switching from conventional procedures to SMILE given the high cost and learning curve they may face.

Small incision lenticule extraction publications in Chinese versus other countries

In the present review, we identified 172 articles from Chinese authors describing their experiences with SMILE surgery. In contrast, 171 articles were from all other countries mainly from Europe and the Indian subcontinent. Comparatively fewer publications were from the USA. This contrast in the publication rate was possibly due to the late introduction of SMILE in countries after FDA approval. Han et al. also observed this trend of differential publication in China versus other countries.[80] By conducting bibliometric analysis, Shan et al. found that surgical control of myopia in the 20th century was mainly published in the USA, but in the 21st century, the publications on myopia surgeries including SMILE were mainly by authors from China, Singapore, and India.[81] SMILE procedure-related publications had a burst intensity of 16.6 starting from 2015 to 2022.[82] Given the high cost of investment and the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic from 2019 to 2021 on ophthalmic services, the FDA-approved procedure seems to be adopted slowly to replace FS-LASIK refractive surgery globally.

Use of lenticule to treat other ophthalmic morbidities

The corneal lenticule extracted by the SMILE procedure has been used to manage recurrent pterygium to cover the corneal defects.[83] Zhao et al. used donor corneal lenticule during epi-kerato-phakia to treat recurrent corneal dystrophy and found it effective at 6 months follow-up.[84] Such lenticules were also used to seal the corneal perforations and corneal tears following trauma.[85] Long-term outcomes and maintaining harvested lenticule using the SMILE procedure from donor cornea would be a fertile area for future research.

Limitations of review

There were a few limitations in this narrative review. Several studies on SMILE are in countries where English is not the first language. Although these publications are in PUBMED, only abstracts were available in English language, whereas full texts are either in Chinese, German, Spanish, or other languages. Few articles presented outcomes in the short-term and then long-term using data from the same patients of the same institutions causing multiple publication bias. The publications were on Oriental and European populations to a large extent. Therefore, extrapolating the experiences to Arab and African populations with refractive error should be done with caution.

Area of further research

Long-term outcomes from the American continent are yet not available since this surgery was approved by the FDA in 2018. Thus, SMILE surgery outcomes of more than 10 years are crucial since this surgery is performed in persons aged 20 to 25 years and have another at least 30 to 40 years to use visual apparatus. During this period, they may undergo ocular treatments for other age-related and metabolic systemic diseases. The effect of such management on the outcomes of the SMILE procedure will be interesting to study. SMILE surgery in combination with other surgeries such as CXL and corrections of HOA need to be studied as SMILE surgery is unable to strengthen the cornea or reshape the front surface of the cornea.

Message to the refractive surgeon

Because of the high strength of evidence in published literature, SMILE is certainly a better surgical option for treating myopia and it is effective and safe. However, refractive surgeons should note the high cost of infrastructure and the need for supervised training before switching from the present mode of myopia management to SMILE. Long-term outcomes of this procedure are still awaited and this should be stressed while counseling the patient when explaining options available for corneal refractive surgeries. Preparing the corneal lenticule, molding it, and reinserting it into the corneal stromal pocket to manage hyperopia is more complex and although approved, one should be careful before undertaking such a procedure without training. In countries like Saudi Arabia that have reported a higher prevalence of keratoconus than other published literature, a detailed workup must be done to identify preclinical keratoconus and avoid such corneas for SMILE procedures.[86]

Message to clients and relatives

For young patients who want to be free of spectacles and contact lenses, refractive surgery is an option. However, in the modern era of extensive use/abuse of video display terminals (VDTs), which already have created a pandemic of dry eye disease, an additional risk of dry eye as a result of corneal refractive surgery is a major issue of debate.[87] The status of DES and climatic factors causing DES, therefore, should be accounted for before opting for corneal surgery. Among these options, SMILE is a better option with a higher safety index.[56] Although visual recovery is fast after SMILE surgery, patients should not be complacent and avoid periodic eye checkups by cornea surgeons. Most of the symptoms after SMILE is transient, but they should be discussed with the operating surgeon during follow-up.

Hospital administrators, especially private refractive surgeons, will be facing the dilemma of expensive investment and replacing the existing refractive surgery tools. The studies showing the cost–benefit ratio will be helpful to them to justify their demand. With extensive marketing and propaganda of SMILE surgeries through mass media, patients are likely to demand this form of refractive surgery and may opt to undergo surgery by surgeons and institutions with facilities for SMILE surgery.

Message to company

Only one company; Carl Zeiss Meditec (Germany) manufactures the machine for SMILE corneal surgery. It should be their responsibility to ensure the training of corneal surgeons in the procedure to avoid poor outcomes due to the learning curve of new surgeons. Feedback from experienced surgeons would help the industry in improving the software. The industry should also encourage the researchers to generate evidence about SMILE surgery. Corneal surgeons who procure the machine will be busy with patient care and increase the surgery rate to cover the heavy investment done. Providing periodic updates about surgery and revision in software should be part of the sale. The teamwork of scientists who made and updated this machine, operating surgeons, and researchers will make this surgery more effective for addressing all types of refractive error and sustain the achievement for the rest of the patient’s life.

Conclusion

The present narrative review of the SMILE procedure to manage different types of refractive error supports a switch from other corneal surgery procedures to SMILE as it is effective and safe. However, a caution to the corneal surgeons and prospective clients about the nonavailability of long-term outcomes of this relatively new procedure. Careful patient selection to rule out the presence of corneal/ocular conditions that pose a risk of complications following SMILE, patient cooperation for monitoring eyes after surgery and updates plus competition in the industry will help in addressing visual disabilities due to uncorrected refractive error.
