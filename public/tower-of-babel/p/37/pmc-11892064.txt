Clinical applications of immunoglobulin G against different individual Aspergillus species for the diagnosis of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis among at-risk populations
Authors: Li-Ta Keng, Chen-Chieh Lin, Chang-Wei Wu, Chia-Jung Liu, Lih-Yu Chang, Meng-Rui Lee, Jung-Yueh Chen, Jann-Yuan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAspergillus fumigatus-specific IgG is often used as a diagnostic test for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA), but few studies have evaluated the performance and serology of IgGs from species other than A. fumigatus. In this study, we evaluated the serology and performance of different Aspergillus species-specific IgG antibodies in patients with CPA and at-risk populations and whether different Aspergillus species-specific IgGs could be of clinical utility and aid in the diagnosis of CPA caused by all Aspergillus species. A total of 187 participants were included between 2020 and 2022 (12 with CPA, 75 with old tuberculosis [TB], 45 with active TB and 55 with bronchiectasis). We measured the serum Aspergillus fumigatus, flavus, terreus, niger-specific, and mixed Aspergillus IgG levels (Phadia ImmunoCap). The correlation was the strongest between A. fumigatus and A. niger (Spearman’s rank: 0.940), followed by A. niger and A. flavus (Spearman’s rank: 0.915). A. terreus-specific IgG was less strongly correlated with the other three Aspergillus species-specific IgG (Spearman’s rank: 0.828–0.849). A. flavus (4 of 6, 67%) was the dominant species. Using the at-least-one-positive approach, the highest performance was obtained when A. fumigatus and A. flavus IgGs were used (sensitivity, 0.75; specificity, 0.84). Significant cross-reactivity exists among different Aspergillus-species IgGs although the correlation may be less significant for A. terreus. In addition to the commonly used A. fumigatus IgG test, IgGs specific to local prevalent Aspergillus species may provide additional clinical utility.KEYWORDS: Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus terreus, Aspergillus IgG, chronic pulmonary aspergillosis

Introduction

Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is an emerging infectious disease in patients with structural lung disease, particularly those with old tuberculosis (TB) [1]. CPA is a debilitating disease that can lead to life-threatening complications if left untreated [2]. The prevalence of CPA among post-TB patients ranges from 6–13% [3]. Diagnosis of CPA poses challenges due to its imaging characteristics, which often overlap with those of other respiratory infections and preexisting structural lung abnormalities [2]. In addition, mycologic evidence of Aspergillus is difficult to obtain. While galactomannan antigen is often negative in CPA in the absence of angioinvasion, the yield of Aspergillus culture remains low, and biopsy for histopathology proof can be difficult in the presence of respiratory insufficiency [4]. Because CPA tends to have a chronic course Aspergillus-specific IgG (Asp-IgG) has performed better than other antibody tests and has become a test of interest in CPA [5]. The performance of different commercial Asp-IgG kits varies with a sensitivity of 75–96% and a specificity of 81–97% [6]. With accumulating published evidence, serum Aspergillus IgG is now a crucial and the preferred noninvasive diagnostic test for CPA.

Although many commercial ELISA kits were available for CPA diagnosis, researchers and physicians often rely on measurement of Aspergillus fumigatus-specific IgG for CPA diagnosis. The rationale behind this routine clinical practice may stem from the fact that A. fumigatus is the most common Aspergillus species that causes human diseases, including pulmonary aspergillosis. In a French study, A. fumigatus accounted for 60% of all Aspergillus species in CPA [7]. In another study in Ghana, Aspergillus species were isolated from 87% (13 of 15) of CPA patients, and A. fumigatus constituted 69% (9 of 13) of them. Although A. fumigatus remains the leading causative species for all forms of pulmonary aspergillosis, other Aspergillus species including A. flavus, A. terreus, and A. niger still account for a significant proportion of pulmonary aspergillosis [8]. IgG antibody of Aspergillus species other than A. fumigatus can also be measured, but few studies have focused on their clinical relevance and utility [9,10]. Therefore, it is of interest to elucidate how the use of different Aspergillus species IgG affects the clinical diagnosis of CPA.

In this study, we evaluated the serology and performance of different Aspergillus species-specific IgG antibodies in patients with CPA and at-risk populations. Thus, we aimed to evaluate whether different Aspergillus species-specific IgGs could be of clinical utility and aid in the diagnosis of CPA caused by all Aspergillus species.

Materials and methods

Study participants and setting

We examined the stored serum of recruited participants who were patients with CPA, active TB, old TB and bronchiectasis in National Taiwan University Hospital Hsin-Chu branch from 2020–2022. The original cohort was intended for unveiling the seroprevalence of Asp-IgG in Taiwanese population and we utilized the stored residual blood sample for this study.

Measurement of serum Aspergillus IgG level

Aspergillus IgG was measured using an automated ImmunoCAP system (Phadia 100; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden) [11]. We tested each serum sample for the presence of A. fumigatus-specific (gm3), A. flavus-specific (gm228), A. terreus-specific (gm36), A.niger-specific (gm207), and mixed Aspergillus IgGs (mx4).

Diagnosis and data collection

CPA was diagnosed according to the guidelines established by the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases/European Respiratory Society and the Infectious Disease Society of America [4,12]. A diagnosis of CPA requires 1. compatible radiographic patterns, such as cavitation with pericavitary infiltrates; 2. presence of mycologic evidence, including histopathology, positive Aspergillus culture, and elevated galactomannan antigen; 3. presence of symptoms for at least 3 months; and 4. exclusion of other diseases [4,12]. The cutoff level of Aspergillus IgG was 40 mgA/L for A. fumigatus IgG. This cutoff level was chosen based on previously published clinical data [13,14].

For patients with old TB, the test of Asp-IgG should be performed at least 3 months after the completion of anti-TB treatment. For patients with active TB, a positive Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) culture or MTB polymerase chain reaction is required to establishment a diagnosis. Among patients with bronchiectasis, the diagnosis of bronchiectasis was confirmed on chest CT images, with at least one of the following criteria: 1. increased ratio of the cross-sectional diameter of the airway to its adjacent artery; 2. lack of tapering, defined as unchanged airway diameter for 2 cm after branching, and 3. visualization of airways in the periphery of the lung within 1 cm of the pleura [15]. Additionally, these bronchiectasis patients were negative for active TB with negative sputum MTB culture and had no history of old TB. For all participants not considered to have CPA, we followed them for two years or until the end of 2022 to ensure no CPA occurrence.

For clinical data, we collected patients’ age, sex, body mass index (BMI), underlying comorbidities, smoking habit, and imaging findings.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup differences were analyzed using the independent-sampled t test or one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. The correlation between serum IgG titers was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. For CPA diagnosis, we evaluated an additional approach that combines two Asp-IgG kits. The at-least-one-positive approach refers to the method that if either or both of the two Asp-IgG tests were positive, the patient was considered to be positive for Asp-IgG. The patient was considered negative for Asp-IgG if both Asp-IgG kits were negative. Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

Regarding the imaging findings, the CPA group was more likely to be cavitated than the other groups (9/12 [75 %] vs. 34/175 [19.4 %], p < 0.0001). The proportion of pleural thickening also differed between the groups, with the CPA group having the highest (4/12, 33.3%), followed by the active TB group (22/75, 29.3%).

Correlation between different Aspergillus species IgG

The scatterplot and correlation between the different Aspergillus species IgG are shown in Figure 2. The correlation was highest between A. fumigatus and A. niger (Spearman’s rank = 0.940), followed by A. flavus and A. niger (Spearman’s rank = 0.915). The correlation was lowest between A. fumigatus and A. terreus (Spearman’s rank = 0.828). The correlation between A. terreus and the IgG of the other three Aspergillus species was generally lower (Spearman’s rank = 0.828-0.849) than that of the other three Aspergillus species (Spearman’s rank = 0.898-0.940). For mixed Aspergillus IgG, the correlation with the other four species was similar (A. fumigatus/A. flavus/A. niger/A. terreus: 0.899/0.884/0.882/0.850, respectively).

Correlation matrix of immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels against different Aspergillus species.

AUCs of different Aspergillus species IgG for CPA diagnosis

The areas under the receiver operating characteristics curves of CPA diagnosis for A. fumigatus, flavus, terreus, niger and mixed IgG were 0.721 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.517–0.924), 0.692 (95% CI: 0.513–0.871), 0.660 (95% CI: 0.469–0.852), 0.641(95% CI: 0.448–0.835), and 0.661 (95% CI: 0.453–0.868), respectively (Table 2). The cutoff levels of the Youden index for A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. terreus, A. niger, and mixed Aspergillus IgG were 44.1, 53.7, 13.0, 37.9, and 38.6 mgA/L, respectively. Although the sensitivity across species according to Youden’s index was suboptimal in the study, the specificity was maintained for all species, except A. terreus-specific IgG.

Among the four CPA patients with culture positivity for A. flavus, the A. flavus IgG titers were 133.5, 101.6, 53.7, and 8.4 mgA/L. For the patient with culture positivity for A. niger, the A. niger IgG titer was 36.9 mgA/L.

Diagnostic modalities other than aspergillus IgG among the study population and those with high A. fumigatus IgG titers

Among all patients, those diagnosed with CPA had the highest proportion (91.7%, n = 11) of invasive diagnostic procedures, including lung biopsy or bronchoscopy examinations. This was followed by the active TB (42.2%, n = 19), the old TB (30.7%, n = 23), and the bronchiectasis group (27.3%, n = 15). The group with the highest percentage of patients undergoing lung biopsy was the active TB group (17.8%, n = 8), followed by the CPA group (8.3%, n = 1), old TB group (5.3%, n = 4), and bronchiectasis group (3.6%, n = 2). Among all non-CPA patients with A. fumigatus IgG > 40 mgA/L, the proportion undergoing invasive diagnostic procedures was the highest in the old TB group (42.1%, n = 8), followed by the active TB group (27.3%, n = 3), and the bronchiectasis group (25.0%, n = 3). Discussion

Our study revealed significant cross-reactivity between the IgG tests of different Aspergillus species, although the correlation between A. terreus and other Aspergillus species may have been lower. A. flavus was the most prevalent species in this study. Using the at-least-one-positive approach, A. fumigatus combined with A. flavus yielded the best performance with increased sensitivity and maintained specificity. This finding suggests that if Asp-IgG from species other than A. fumigatus is to be examined and utilized, IgG specific to the local prevalent species may be considered.

Although A. fumigatus remains the leading causative species of human disease, geographic variation in species proportions may still exist [16,17]. In an epidemiological study in our country, A. flavus and A. fumigatus were equally prevalent causative agents, each accounting for 40% of Aspergillus infection [18]. In another single-center study in our country, A. flavus ranked the 3rd most common species of Aspergillus, behind A. fumigatus and A. niger [19]. In another study investigating culture-positive influenza-associated aspergillosis in our country, A. flavus accounted for one-fourth of all cases and was second to A. fumigatus [20]. In another multicenter study that included five Asian countries, A. fumigatus (49.2%) was the most commonly isolated Aspergillus in invasive mold infections and A. flavus was the second most common species (38.1%) [21]. These studies suggest that A. flavus is an important Aspergillus species in our country, second only to A. fumigatus. These reports are in concordance with those of our study, in which we found that A. flavus was the most prevalent and important Aspergillus species.

Our study revealed that testing Asp-IgG in the corresponding prevalent Aspergillus species may add clinical utility. The best performance was achieved by combining A. fumigatus-specific and A. flavus-specific IgGs. Sensitivity increased, and specificity was maintained using the at-least-one-positive approach. Two patients were diagnosed with CPA based on negative A. fumigatus-specific IgG and positive A. flavus-specific IgG results. One of these patients was culture-positive for A. flavus. Although our sample size was limited, the findings from this study suggest a potential role for using Asp-IgG from locally prevalent species to increase yield rate.

Accumulating evidence has shown that bronchiectasis is an important risk factor for CPA. In one population-based study in Korea, patients with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis had a 4-fold increased risk of developing pulmonary aspergillosis compared to the matched cohort [22]. Another study showed that patient with bronchiectasis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) overlap have an increased frequency and clinical severity of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) [23]. In another study conducting in patients with COPD, A. fumigatus sensitized patients had a higher risk of bronchiectasis [24]. In our study, we found that patients with bronchiectasis had elevated Asp-IgG titers compared with patients with the active TB, although the titer was slightly lower than in the old TB group, which is the most common underlying disease for CPA. Given the higher risk of developing CPA in patients with bronchiectasis, the clinical significance of elevated Asp-IgG in the bronchiectasis group warrants further investigation.

The cross-reactivity of antibodies to different fungal and Aspergillus species has been reported [25]. In one study investigating precipitins and IgG specific to Aspergillus among patients with ABPA, the presence of A. fumigatus-specific precipitin or IgG was associated with antibodies specific for other Aspergillus species, suggesting cross-reactivity [9]. Furthermore, another study investigating patients with ABPA and asthma revealed that both IgE and IgG were highly cross-reactive among the Aspergillus species [10]. However, in that study, the correlation between A. terreus and the other species was less prominent (A. terreus vs. A. fumigatus/A. flavus/A. niger: 0.747/0.607/0.474). In a study in Taiwan, researchers investigating the optimal cutoff level for CPA revealed that significant cross-reactivity exists between A. fumigatus and A. flavus-specific IgG. However, the correlation between the A. fumigatus and A. flavus-specific IgG was more significant among patients with CPA than in diseased controls [26].

While two of the above mentioned studies reported Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between different Asp-IgG kits, we performed a meta-analysis and estimated the pooled correlation [9,10]. In the random-effects model, the correlation between A. fumigatus/A. flavus, A. fumigatus/A. niger, A.fumigatus/A.terreus, A.flavus/A.niger, A.flavus/A.terreus and A. niger/A. terreus was 0.916, 0.926, 0.847, 0.924, 0.736 and 0.675, respectively (Table 4). Interestingly, the results of the meta-analysis showed a strong correlation between A. fumigatus and other non-fumigatus species. This association was less prominent for A. terreus and A. niger. Indeed, in phylogenetic and taxonomic study, A. terreus and A. niger have been found to be less closely related than A. fumigatus and A. flavus [27]. A Mixed Asp-IgG kit using ImmunoCap has not been reported previously. Mixed Aspergillus IgG was detected in a mixture of A. fumigatus, A. flavus, A. niger and A. terreus. Although the exact mixture of allergens included in this kit was not available, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was similar between mixed Aspergillus IgG and other four individual Aspergillus species. However, the performance of the mixed Aspergillus IgG was not superior to A. fumigatus and the clinical applications of mixed Aspergillus IgG remain unclear.

Our study also has limitations. First, it included a relatively small number of patients with CPA. The calculation of the Youden index could be premature, therefore, this may lead to a lower optimal discriminant power of Asp-IgG compared to previous studies. However, the aims of our study was not to conclude the superiority of any kit over the others. Our study also did not aim at determine the optimal cutoff level of Asp-IgG. Second, although we followed patients for 2 years, we cannot exclude the possibility that patients with high Asp-IgG titers had occult CPA disease. Third, given the retrospective design, not all patients underwent invasive diagnostic procedures, such as lung biopsy or bronchoscopy. This could lead to a potential underdiagnosis of CPA, especially in patients with high Aspergillus IgG titers or a history of old TB. In our study, among non-CPA patients with A. fumigatus IgG titer >40 mgA/L, 25.0%–42.1% underwent invasive diagnostics. Furthermore, 50% (2/4) of the old TB patients with A. fumigatus IgG titers >100 mgA/L underwent invasive diagnostics.

In conclusion, we found that the correlation between different Aspergillus species-specific IgGs was high, although it may be lower in A. niger/A. terreus and A. flavus/A. terreus. IgG titers against different Aspergillus species increased in the CPA at-risk population, including patients with old TB and bronchiectasis. Using the at-least-one-positive approach, the addition of A. flavus-specific IgG, which is also the most prevalent species in our study, may increase the sensitivity of Asp-IgG while maintaining specificity.
