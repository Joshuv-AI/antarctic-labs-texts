Etanercept in Steroid-Refractory Acute Graft Versus Host Disease: Clinical Response, Toxicity, and Long-Term Outcomes
Authors: Akanksha Chichra, Sachin Punatar, Anant Gokarn, Sumeet Mirgh, Nishant Jindal, Gaurav Bain, Vijay Shirure, Vinodhini M, Avinash Bonda, Lingaraj Nayak, Bhausaheb Bagal, Sadhana Kannan, Libin Mathew, Navin Khattry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Steroid refractory acute graft versus host disease (SR-aGVHD) is seen in 30%-40% of patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (allo HSCT). We report our experience with etanercept (ETA) as a first-line treatment for SR-aGVHD during a period when ruxolitinib was not available in India. We retrospectively analysed 43 allo HSCT patients from 2010 to 2019 who received ETA for SR-aGVHD. The primary objective was to assess the overall response rate with etanercept. Secondary objectives included overall survival (OS), organ specific responses, causes of mortality and identification of factors associated with survival and see its utility in the current ruxolitinib era. The response rates (overall response rate [ORR] - complete response [CR] + very good partial response [VGPR] + partial response [PR]) at Day 28 was 69%. The organ-specific ORR was 70% (lower gut), 67% (skin), and 50% (liver). The median OS of responders at Day 28 from ETA start was 6.8 months versus 2.8 months in non-responders (p=0.0008). Patients in whom a reduction in the systemic steroid dose of ≥ 30% by Day 28 from ETA start was possible had a significantly longer median OS (9.5 months versus 3.5 months; p=0.0001). In multivariate analysis, steroid dose reduction of ≥ 30% by Day 28 of the start of ETA was a significant factor for survival (p=0.015). The cause of death in the majority was infection (90%). ETA, used as a second-line agent, has shown an acceptable ORR for SR-aGVHD in our cohort. Despite the good response rates, the group had a high transplantation-related mortality. Infections were the major cause of mortality, and early tapering of systemic steroids may be the key to improving outcomes. In the current ruxolitinib era, etanercept may hold a place in patients where ruxolitinib has failed or is not feasible.

Introduction

Acute graft versus host disease (aGVHD) complicates 30%-50% of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplants (allo HSCT) and 14%-36% develop severe aGVHD (grade III-IV)1. Corticosteroids have been established as the first-line treatment of aGVHD2,3. Response to corticosteroids is seen in about 50%-65% of aGVHD patients with a significant proportion remaining steroid refractory3,4.

Steroid refractory acute GVHD (SR-aGVHD) is defined as progression after 3-5 days of corticosteroids (1-2 mg/kg/day of methylprednisolone or equivalent) or no response after 5-7 days of therapy5. SR-aGVHD is a formidable complication of allo HSCT known to have poor outcomes and high mortality. Several second-line agents have been tried in SR-aGVHD, such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), pentostatin, infliximab, etanercept, alemtuzumab, mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), and extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP) with no standard of care till recently6. The FDA approval of ruxolitinib in 2019, based on the results of the REACH-1 trial, has established its role as a standard of care first-line treatment of SR-aGVHD7.

Our center had a policy of using etanercept as the first-line agent in SR-aGVHD, in the period between 2010 and 2019 due to easy availability, low cost, and non-accessibility of other second-line agents for SR-aGVHD, such as ECP and MSC, during the study period. Ruxolitinib was not approved as a second-line therapy during this period and was not accessible in our country. In this retrospective analysis between 2010 and 2019, we aimed to evaluate the response, toxicities, and long-term outcomes of etanercept in our cohort of patients with SR-aGVHD treated in the pre-ruxolitinib period and to see its possible role in the current ruxolitinib era.

Materials and Methods

In this single-center study we retrospectively analysed the outcomes of allo HSCT patients from 2010 to 2019 who developed Grade II-IV SR-aGVHD and received etanercept, amongst 311 patients who underwent transplant in the same period. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC III of Tata Memorial Centre, protocol number #901181). As it was a retrospective analysis waiver of consent was granted by the IEC.

Conditioning Regimens, GVHD Prophylaxis, and Infection Prophylaxis

Conditioning regimens included total body irradiation (TBI) of 12 Gy-13.2 Gy combined with fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day for 5 days) or cyclophosphamide (60 mg/kg for 2 days), fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day for 5 days) with treosulfan (10-14 g/m2/day for 3 days) and thiotepa (5 mg/kg/day for 2 days), fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day for 5 days) with melphalan (140 mg/m2 for 1 day), and fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day for 5 days) with 7.2 Gy TBI. Unmanipulated T-cell replete peripheral blood stem cell grafts were the predominant stem cell source.

GVHD prophylaxis consisted of calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) - cyclosporine A or tacrolimus combined with short-course methotrexate or MMF in matched related donor (MRD) and matched unrelated donor (MUD) transplants. In addition to CNI and methotrexate/MMF, patients undergoing unrelated donor transplants received rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) at a dose of 2.5 to 5 mg/kg. GVHD prophylaxis for haploidentical transplants consisted of post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) (50 mg/kg/day) on Day 3 and Day 4, and CNI with MMF starting at Day 5.

A standard institutional protocol was used for prophylaxis of fungal, viral, and Pneumocystis jirovecii infections and viral monitoring through the study period. Oral acyclovir was started on admission for transplant and continued for at least 1 year post-transplant. Voriconazole or posaconazole was used as antifungal prophylaxis from Day-1/Day 0 of allo HSCT and continued till stopping of all immunosuppression and resolution of GVHD. Doses of voriconazole/posaconazole were adjusted according to weekly trough level monitoring. Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis was started at Day 28 and continued till at least 1 year post transplant or till systemic steroids were ongoing, whichever was later. All patients underwent weekly cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNA monitoring starting from conditioning therapy till stoppage of immunosuppression. Ganciclovir was used for pre-emptive management of CMV reactivation. (Foscarnet and other anti-CMV medications or virus-specific T-cells were unavailable in India then). All patients who had received rabbit ATG and those who underwent haploidentical transplants also underwent weekly monitoring for adenovirus, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), and BK virus. Additionally, all patients with SR-aGVHD underwent weekly assessments for adenovirus, EBV, and BK virus (irrespective of the donor type or use of ATG). There has been no major change in supportive care policy over the entire study period with regard to conditioning, anti-GVHD, or infection control decisions.

Acute GVHD - Diagnosis, Treatment, and Response Criteria

aGVHD was graded as per the modified Glucksberg criteria8, and the diagnosis of GVHD was confirmed by histology of involved tissues wherever feasible. Additionally, all aGVHD episodes were also classified retrospectively as per the Minnesota Scoring into High Risk (HR) and Standard Risk GVHD9. HR aGVHD, as defined by the Minnesota score, was defined as either skin stage 4, lower gastrointestinal (GI) stage 3-4, or liver stage 3-4, or skin stage 3 along with either lower GI 2-4 or liver stage 2-4 GVHD.

First-line treatment of aGVHD Grade II-IV was systemic steroids with methylprednisolone 1-2 mg/kg/day. SR-aGVHD was defined according to standard criteria as mentioned above. Patients who developed a flare of aGVHD during the steroid taper and required an increase in steroid dose were also defined as SR-aGVHD. Etanercept was started at 25 mg (0.4 mg/kg in children) subcutaneously every 72 hours initially and was tapered gradually as per the physician's discretion, depending on response. Steroid taper was started at 7 days from the start of systemic steroids at 0.2 mg/kg every 8th or 11th day as per institutional policy. The steroid taper plan was modified or kept on hold as per the patient's clinical condition and the physician's discretion. Response to therapy was assessed and recorded by the physician on a weekly basis. Complete response (CR) was the complete resolution of aGVHD manifestations in all organs. Partial response (PR) was an improvement in GVHD stage in at least 1 of the initially involved organs without CR and no worsening in any other organs. A very good partial response (VGPR) was an improvement in GVHD in all initial organs, with maximum stage I involvement in 1 or more organs (except upper gut). No response (NR)/progression/failure of therapy was defined as the same grade or progression of GVHD in any organ or the addition of further GVHD therapy beyond etanercept. Death before first response assessment after starting etanercept was taken as NR.

Response assessment was done at three time points - Day 14, Day 28, and Day 56 after initiation of etanercept. Reduction of systemic steroid dose by 30% or more by Day 28 of starting etanercept was also analysed to assess the impact on outcomes of patients.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint was overall response rate (ORR)-(CR+VGPR+PR) at Day 28 after initiation of etanercept. Secondary end points included ORR at Day 14 and Day 56 after initiation of etanercept, overall survival (OS), infectious complications, and causes of death. Categorical outcomes were reported as frequency and percentage. Response rates were reported as a percentage along with 95% confidence interval (CI). OS was defined as from the time of starting second-line treatment with etanercept until death due to any cause. Survival probabilities were calculated and plotted using the Kaplan-Meier curve. Competing risk analysis was used to calculate the cumulative incidence of relapse and non-relapse mortality (NRM). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Univariate analyses were performed using Gray's test for cumulative incidence functions and the log-rank test for OS, transplantation-related mortality (TRM), and progression gree survival. The Cox proportional-hazards model was used for multivariate regression analysis and included variables associated with an endpoint in univariate analysis, or factors known to predict outcomes. Results were expressed as the hazard ratio (HR) with the 95% CI. All analyses were carried out using SPSS v29.

During this period, 43 (13%) patients developed SR-aGVHD as defined before and received etanercept as first-line treatment. There were 31 males and 12 females in the cohort. The ORR at Day 14, Day 28, and Day 56 were 62% (95% CI 46%-76%), 69% (95% CI 53%-82%) and 64% (95% CI 49%-78%), respectively (Figure 1). The CR rates increased from Day 14 to Day 28 post starting etanercept, pointing to the fact that assessment for GVHD response was probably done best at Day 28. The organ-specific response rates (CR+VGPR+PR) at Day 28 were 70% for the lower gut, 67% for skin, and 50% for liver. The patients who achieved CR at Day 14 sustained their response till Day 56, suggesting that early responders may do better.

Chronic GVHD (cGVHD) was seen in 6 patients in this cohort, of which only one patient had extensive cGVHD. None of the patients developed second malignancies.

CMV reactivation was seen in 64% of the patients. Systemic fungal and bacterial infections were seen in 51% and 78% patients, respectively, after the start of etanercept. Overall, 39 of 43 (91%) patients died. The causes of death were infection in 35 (90%) patients and relapse in 4 (10%) patients.

Amongst the 35 patients who succumbed to infection, 30 had some bacterial infection in the form of culture-proven sepsis (in 10 patients), pneumonia (in 18 patients). Fungal infections were also seen in 22 of these patients, which were usually probable fungal infections involving the lungs and/or paranasal sinuses or both. Two of the patients who died also had adenoviremia. None of the patients succumbed to CMV infection, but the addition of ganciclovir in these patients did predispose them to develop cytopenias and would have contributed to an increased number of bacterial and fungal infections.

Discussion

aGVHD is an important cause of mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing allo HSCT. As shown in an EBMT analysis, the outcome of aGVHD has gradually improved over time10. Patients who develop SR-aGVHD are known to have a high mortality rate and a poor quality of life2,3.

Tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α has a well-established role in the pathogenesis of aGVHD11,12. Etanercept consists of two recombinant human TNFR (p75) monomers fused to the Fc portion of human Ig G1, which binds to TNF-α and makes it inactive13.

We have used etanercept as our first-line drug for SR-aGVHD from 2010 to 2019. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest analysis of etanercept as a first-line agent for treatment of SR-aGVHD from a single center. The study aimed to see the response rates and toxicities of etanercept in our cohort in a systematic manner and to determine its role in the current ruxolitinib era. This retrospective review of 43 patients treated with etanercept shows an ORR (CR+VGPR+PR) of approximately 60% at Day 28 after starting etanercept. This is similar to the results reported in the literature of response rates ranging from 40%-70% with etanercept14-16. In some other studies, etanercept has been combined with other immunosuppressive agents like daclizumab, tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil with similar response rates17,18.

Patients who had SR-aGVHD of the lower gut and skin in our cohort had better responses to etanercept compared to those with liver involvement. Busca et al. reported their results of etanercept in 13 patients with SR-aGVHD. In their cohort too, the gut aGVHD had a higher response rate of 63%, followed by skin (50%) and liver (40%)14. In another study in pediatric patients with SR-aGVHD where etanercept was used as a first-line agent, the overall response rate was 68%, with 78% response in lower gut and skin GVHD, and 57% in those with liver GVHD16. A similar pattern of organ-specific response has also been reported by Park et al19. The higher rates of response in gut and skin GVHD are possibly because of the TNF-α driven pathophysiology of GVHD in these organs14.

The median OS of the patients who had standard risk GVHD at presentation as per Minnesota criteria was significantly better in our cohort of patients. This has been validated by MacMillan et al., who showed that standard risk aGVHD patients as per Minnesota criteria had lower TRM and better 6-month survival20.

The median OS of the patients who had a response to etanercept at Day 28 from the start of the drug was significantly better in our cohort of patients (median survival 6.8 months versus 2.8 months, p=0.0008). In a multicenter study by Ma et al., 58 patients with Grade II-IV aGVHD who received etanercept were analysed. Patients who responded to etanercept by Day 28 had a better median survival (52 months versus 2.8 months, p=0.002)21.

In our study, patients of SR-aGVHD in whom we were able to reduce the corticosteroid dose to ≥ 30% before Day 28 from the start of etanercept, had a significantly better median OS (9.5 months versus 3.5 months; p=0.0001). This factor was significant even in multivariate analysis. Infections were the major cause of morbidity and mortality in our study, with 51% of patients developing probable and/or definite fungal infections and 78% of patients developing bacterial infections. Infections were the cause of death in close to 90% of our patients. Most of these patients succumbed to bacterial and fungal infections. CMV reactivation was also seen in nearly 60% of our patients with SR-aGVHD.

In the study of children by Faraci et al., the incidence of infections requiring systemic treatment was 68%. They had a lower incidence of bacterial infections (36%) and fungal infections (20%) than our patients16. The reason for a higher incidence of bacterial and fungal infections in our cohort could be due to a higher incidence of multidrug-resistant organisms seen in our clinical practice22. In the above prospective study, steroids were tapered by 25% every 7 to 10 days to reach a daily dose of 0.5 mg/kg. In our study, the patients in whom corticosteroids were not tapered off quickly did significantly worse, and this could be a contributing factor for the higher infections seen. Early tapering of corticosteroids may be the key to improving outcomes in our patients with SR-aGVHD treated with etanercept.

Some important differences between ruxolitinib and etanercept deserve discussion. Both these drugs target different pathways23. From a clinical perspective, one major difference is in their toxicity profiles. Ruxolitinib causes significant haematological toxicity, whereas etanercept does not24. In patients with SR-aGVHD who already may have low counts and where ruxolitinib may not be feasible, etanercept may be an option. Additionally, a phenomenon of JAK-STAT rebound (known as the withdrawal syndrome) has been described with ruxolitinib. In one retrospective analysis of cGVHD, nearly 30% of patients experienced a flare after ruxolitinib tapering25. Events of cardiac arrest, shock, and death have been reported following discontinuation of ruxolitinib for aGVHD. In our cohort of patients, we did not see such a rebound phenomenon with the stoppage of etanercept.

There are other therapies that have been explored in SR-aGVHD apart from ruxolitinib and etanercept in the past. These include ATG and alemtuzumab, which show variable responses and a higher incidence of infections26,27. ECP has been used widely in SR-aGVHD patients, due to its ability to generate tolerogenic dendritic cells, inducing anti-inflammatory responses and expanding T regulatory cells. In a meta-analysis, ECP was demonstrated to show encouraging results in skin, liver and gut GVHD28. Gut dysbiosis has been a key contributing factor in the pathogenesis of GVHD, and faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) is being increasingly explored in this setting with promising results29. MSCs have an immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effect. There have been several trials looking at their safety and efficacy, and recently, promising data have emerged in the pediatric population30. In the period when we used etanercept for SR-aGVHD, there was limited access to therapies like ECP and MSC. The accessibility to these modalities has improved over the years, but most of the time, these are not feasible. FMT as a treatment for SR-aGVHD was not established during this time period. We are now able to do FMT for all our patients with SR-aGVHD.

Our study is retrospective in nature with its inherent limitations. Despite being a retrospective study, the medical records were complete, and there was no missing information about the patients. One of the main limitations is the variable steroid taper in our cohort of patients, which has impacted the outcomes. Implementing a stringent steroid taper policy is difficult (or perhaps even impossible) in clinical practice. Even in the REACH trial of ruxolitinib, a steroid taper schedule was advised, but it wasn't mandatory to follow the policy, and the taper plan was eventually left to the discretion of the treating clinicians. Following the analysis from this cohort, we now have a more stringent policy for steroid tapering in place. Another limitation of our study is the small number of patients, limiting further statistical analysis; however, our study still remains one of the largest single-center analyses on the use of etanercept.

To conclude, despite good response rates, the overall outcomes with etanercept remain modest to poor due to infections and TRM. The responses seem to be better in lower gut and skin GVHD. A rapid taper of steroids once etanercept is initiated for SR-GVHD may hold the promise for reduction in infections and TRM and thereby may potentially improve survival. In the current ruxolitinib era, etanercept may hold a place in patients where ruxolitinib has failed or is not feasible (due to cytopenia or any other reason). Prospective studies with etanercept with a defined rapid steroid taper plan are required to revisit the role of etanercept in SR-GVHD.
