Early norepinephrine in septic shock: are we treating physiology or system efficiency?
Authors: Jay Prakash, Shio Priye
Published: 2026

To the editor

The study conducted by Ko et al. examines the effect of early norepinephrine administration on septic shock patients with different haemodynamic phenotypes, defined by diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and pulse pressure (PP) measurements [1]. The study successfully introduces a new research idea through its innovative hypothesis-testing approach, which requires us to address specific study points.

A DBP cutoff of < 50 mmHg is conceptually reasonable as a definition of a “vasoplegic phenotype,” based upon previous physiological understandings of septic circulatory shock. Nonetheless, we urge a note of caution. The current study utilizes a single estimation of the non-invasive DBP at the emergency department (ED) triage, which may not be universally accurate in hypotensive, tachycardic, or arrhythmic states. Oscillometric devices are pragmatic but can underestimate or overestimate true arterial pressure in shock states. Although oscillometric devices are useful for obtaining blood pressure, they can frequently underestimate or overestimate true blood pressure levels, especially in shock states. Even minor measurements can change between groups for the analysis of the interaction between DBP and the time to norepinephrine [2].

The definition of “early norepinephrine” as administration within 25 min of triage may also measure system efficiency, rather than purely pharmacologic benefit. In many emergency departments, achieving vasopressor initiation within such a short timeframe depends on rapid triage, early physician recognition, timely vascular access, and streamlined pharmacy workflows. Thus, the observed mortality benefit may partly represent the advantage of high-performing care systems rather than the isolated effect of early norepinephrine. This interpretation is consistent with the existing literature on sepsis, which suggests that timely completion of the sepsis bundle is frequently an indirect measure of the quality of care provided [3].

As with all observational studies, the problem of confounding by indication remains, and it is possible that clinicians may have preferentially initiated norepinephrine earlier in patients perceived as more vasodilated or less tolerant of fluids. Such patients may have had earlier source control, better monitoring, and earlier ICU admission, all of which improve outcomes. We note that granular fluid data (volume, rate, and dynamic fluid responsiveness indices) were unavailable, making it difficult to fully separate the effect of early norepinephrine from broader resuscitation strategies [4].

The secondary stratification by pulse pressure is innovative and physiologically appealing. However, several subgroups had small sample sizes with wide confidence intervals, which limits the robustness of some inferences. The concept that low DBP with wide PP represents “vasoplegia with preserved stroke volume” is biologically plausible but remains speculative without concurrent echocardiographic or invasive hemodynamic validation. Integration of bedside ultrasound in future studies would greatly enhance clinical applicability [5].

We also postulate that in-hospital mortality is an incomplete surrogate for the clinical effect of early norepinephrine. Other endpoints, such as vasopressor-free days, resolution of organ dysfunction, duration of mechanical ventilation, and 90-day survival, may better describe benefit. Additionally, in-hospital mortality is susceptible to bias in institutional discharge practices and end-of-life decisions that differ between centers [6].

In the broader context, this study contributes meaningfully to the ongoing debate regarding early vasopressors versus fluid-first strategies in septic shock. While the CENSER trial suggested potential advantages of earlier norepinephrine, the CLOVERS trial did not demonstrate clear superiority of a restrictive fluid strategy with early pressors. Thus, Ko et al.’s findings should be viewed as complementary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, helping refine rather than resolve the question of which patients benefit most from early vasopressor therapy [7, 8].

We believe that the most valuable contribution of this work is to reinforce phenotype-guided resuscitation rather than adherence to a rigid time threshold. Future randomized trials stratified by DBP, PP, and echocardiographic profiles are required to determine whether such precision resuscitation improves outcomes.

In conclusion, Ko et al., by linking early norepinephrine use to readily available hemodynamic markers, refine current understanding of vasopressor timing and underscore the need for more individualized treatment strategies. These findings should encourage prospective, physiology-based trials incorporating detailed hemodynamic assessment to better define which patients derive the greatest benefit from early vasopressor therapy.
