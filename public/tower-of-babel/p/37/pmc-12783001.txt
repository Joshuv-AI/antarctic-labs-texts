A dual-lobe strategy in living-donor liver transplantation: overcoming small-for-size graft limitations in an obese patient: a case report
Authors: Bhabani Sankar Sahoo, Kausar Makki, Yogesh Yadav, Tathagata Karan, Piyush Srivastava, Vivek Vij
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The primary goal of living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) is to optimize outcomes for the recipient while ensuring donor safety. However, there is a risk of small-for-size syndrome, particularly in obese patients, where graft weight might be inadequate. In such scenarios, dual-lobe LDLT represents a more viable alternative. Although ethical concerns exist regarding potential risks posed to two healthy donors, this method may be most appropriate when deceased donor grafts are unavailable. Here, we describe a case involving an obese patient classified as Child-Pugh class C with chronic liver disease who underwent dual-lobe LDLT. The criteria for selecting the right lobe graft differed slightly from standard protocols, as did the implantation technique. The patient experienced low-output bile leakage, which resolved spontaneously. Priority in graft selection was given to the number of bile ducts, as the right lobes of both donors had nearly equal volumes. Despite simultaneous implantation, cold and warm ischemia times remained manageable due to effective coordination. Although the literature suggests a higher incidence of vascular and biliary complications in dual-lobe LDLT than in single-lobe procedures, these risks can be mitigated by careful planning and coordination.

INTRODUCTION

The liver graft volume is critical for survival in living donor liver transplantation (LDLT). Introduced in 1999, grafts with a graft-to-recipient weight ratio (GRWR) of <0.8 or a volume <40% of standard liver volume are considered small-for-size grafts (SFSGs). When SFSGs are insufficient to meet functional requirements, recipients may develop small-for-size syndrome (SFSS) [1,2]. The incidence of SFSS following LDLT is approximately 20% [3]. LDLT is the predominant method of liver transplantation in India, yet some patients remain unable to receive transplants due to unsuitable donors. One particularly affected group is obese patients [4]. Dual-lobe LDLT offers a promising alternative for obese patients with end-stage liver disease. This technique, which involves partial liver grafts from two donors, utilizes the regenerative capacity of the liver and can significantly enhance the recipient's quality of life, particularly in SFSS cases [5]. Dual-lobe transplantation was first introduced in 2001 by Lee et al. [6]. Ethical concerns persist due to the involvement of two healthy donors for the benefit of a single patient. Over 300 dual-lobe transplants have been documented, employing various combinations such as the right lobe (RL) and left lobe (LL), both LLs, the LL with left lateral segment, two left lateral segments, and the RL with left lateral segment [7]. Most centers employ sequential perfusion and various biliary reconstruction techniques [8]. There is no standardized dual-lobe implantation protocol in India, with variations existing across institutions. In this report, we describe a modified dual-graft implantation technique using the RL and LL.

CASE REPORT

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication. A 42-year-old male patient with hepatitis B and D-related chronic liver disease presented with decompensation characterized by hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice, minimal ascites, and no pedal edema. He was classified as Child-Pugh class C (score 10/15), had a Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score of 18, weighed 110 kg, and had a body mass index of 38 kg/m2. During evaluation for LDLT, the first donor, a healthy 37-year-old female, was found to have an RL volume of only 596 g. Our institution requires a liver remnant volume of at least 30% and a GRWR of at least 0.8. With a calculated GRWR of only 0.54, there was a significant risk of SFSS. Consequently, the family was approached to provide a second donor. A second potential donor, a 41-year-old female, had an RL volume of 577 g. After thorough counseling regarding the risks and benefits of single-lobe transplantation, the family consented to dual-lobe transplantation. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was conducted on both donors to evaluate bile duct anatomy, although this is not a standard component of our donor evaluation protocol. A donor with Huang type A anatomy was selected for the right-lobe donation (Figs. 1 and 2) [9].

Priority was given to the number of bile ducts (Table 1), as the RL volumes of both donors were very similar. Ethical concerns exist in LDLT due to the inherent risks involved in hepatectomy on healthy donors. This ethical dilemma is compounded further in dual-lobe transplantation, as two healthy individuals undergo risks for the benefit of a single recipient. Given the limited availability of deceased donors, and based on the living donors' availability, dual-lobe transplantation was determined to be the best life-saving option for this patient. At high-volume centers such as ours, donor complications are very minimal.

With appropriate informed consent from all parties involved, simultaneous surgical procedures commenced for both donors and the recipient. The recipient's hepatectomy followed our standard protocol, involving careful mobilization and looping of the suprahepatic inferior vena cava (IVC) in the suprarenal area to facilitate easier cross-clamping—this procedure is not typically routine for single-lobe transplants at our institute. Meticulous arterial dissection was performed to preserve as many branches as possible. The bile duct and portal vein (PV) were divided as high as feasible, leaving the cystic duct stump elongated. Both donor liver transections proceeded as planned, starting with the harvesting of the RL (RL-middle hepatic vein [MHV] graft). Both donors had consented to open hepatectomies. We typically do not use indocyanine green in open procedures, opting instead for intraoperative cholangiography for bile duct division. Reconstruction of segments V and VIII veins (V5 and V8) with polytetrafluoroethylene, creating a common orifice with the right hepatic vein (RHV), was completed on the back table, per our routine practice. The RL graft had two PVs (Nakamura type C) (Fig. 3), and a Y-shaped PV graft was used for reconstruction. The LL graft had a single PV (Fig. 4) [10]. As implantation of the RL graft began, the LL was harvested and perfusion was initiated. In the recipient, the IVC was cross-clamped, and the RL graft was orthotopically implanted. The common RHV and neo-MHV openings were anastomosed to the recipient's RHV. The graft PV was anastomosed to the recipient's right PV, ensuring sufficient growth factor (Fig. 5). Following this, the LL was implanted orthotopically, and the common opening of the left hepatic vein (LHV) and MHV was anastomosed to the recipient cava at the LHV and MHV sites using a triangular technique. The recipient’s left PV was shortened to prevent kinking and then anastomosed to the graft PV with a growth factor (Fig. 5). Upon releasing the IVC cross-clamp, both grafts were simultaneously perfused without adverse events. The cold and warm ischemia times (WITs) for both grafts are detailed in Table 2.

Following reperfusion, one artery from the RL graft and two arteries from the LL (left hepatic artery [LHA] and accessory LHA) graft were anastomosed to the recipient’s right hepatic artery, segment IV artery, and LHA, respectively (Figs. 6 and 7). Subsequently, bile duct anastomosis was performed: the right graft duct was connected to the recipient's right hepatic duct, and the left graft duct was connected to the left hepatic duct (Fig. 8). The posterior layers of both ducts were secured first, followed by the anterior layer for the left, and finally, the RL. A negative leak test was performed via the cystic duct. Intraoperative Doppler assessments revealed normal parameters. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit and successfully extubated the following morning. Immunosuppression was initiated per institutional protocol, and no major postoperative complications occurred. The patient was discharged on postoperative day (POD) 14. The work has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2023 criteria [11].

DISCUSSION

The dual-lobe LDLT procedure raises significant medical and ethical concerns, as it involves two living donors providing portions of their livers to a single recipient. This surgical technique requires meticulous hilar dissections, preserving the length of hepatic arteries, PV branches, and bile ducts. Additionally, complex vascular reconstructions and multiple arterial anastomoses are necessary. Balancing the surgical risks posed to both donors against the anticipated benefits to high-risk recipients is crucial [7,12].

Historically, most available data on dual graft LDLT comes from case reports, underscoring the procedure's complexity and rarity. Securing adequately sized grafts from living donors, especially for larger recipients, is challenging because a GRWR of at least 0.8 or a standard liver volume of at least 50% is required to minimize the risk of SFSS. Suitable donors must provide sufficient graft volume while retaining an adequate remaining liver volume after donation [1,13,14].

Both donors for our patient had nearly identical RL volumes, which individually proved inadequate for the recipient, posing a significant risk of SFSS. This created a dilemma regarding donor selection for the RL and LL. MRCP was performed on both donors to verify biliary anatomy. Our institutional preference is to perform multiple arterial and venous anastomoses rather than multiple biliary connections. This approach is based on the understanding that biliary anastomosis is critical in LDLT. Thus, the RL graft was implanted first, followed by the LL graft, with both perfused simultaneously. Although the WIT for the RL graft slightly increased to 23 minutes, the recipient experienced only a single reperfusion injury rather than two sequential injuries. Overall, the WIT remained low due to the creation of a common orifice for both the RHV and the neo-MHV of the graft, which was anastomosed to the recipient's RHV, corresponding to our routine practice [15,16].

Furthermore, the complexity of implantation was reduced by cross-clamping the IVC instead of using a side-biting clamp typically employed during sequential perfusion. From a technical perspective, combining the RL and LL for dual graft LDLT is often optimal, as the implantation technique is simpler than with other lobe combinations. Anatomically, positioning these grafts does not require additional supportive devices or significant technical modifications. Both sequential and simultaneous implantation techniques are described in the literature; however, most authors prefer simultaneous reperfusion for combined RL and LL grafts. Song et al. [18] reported higher morbidity and mortality rates in dual-lobe LDLT compared to single-lobe LDLT, with in-hospital mortality rates as high as 7%, significantly above rates observed in single-lobe procedures. Moreover, vascular and biliary complications occurred more frequently in dual-lobe LDLT. In our patient, bile leakage was noted on POD 7, gradually decreasing and resolving spontaneously by POD 21. The maximum drain aspirate recorded was 200 mL of bile-mixed peritoneal fluid on POD 7. We suspect this leakage originated from the caudate duct or cut-surface pedicle, resolving independently without further complications. Before discharge, a noncontrast computed tomography scan was performed to rule out any significant undrained collections. Both donors experienced uncomplicated postoperative recoveries. At the latest follow-up, 6 months postoperatively, both donors remain in excellent health, returning fully to their presurgical routines. The recipient is healthy and maintains stable graft function on a single immunosuppressive medication (tacrolimus) [17,18].

Dual-lobe LDLT has demonstrated effectiveness in achieving sufficient liver volumes in obese recipients without substantially increasing risks to individual donors. Nevertheless, meticulous planning and execution remain essential for successful outcomes. In cases involving grafts of nearly identical volumes, the number of bile ducts may be a decisive factor in selecting donor grafts. The success of dual-lobe LDLT procedures depends significantly on a highly skilled surgical team and effective coordination.
