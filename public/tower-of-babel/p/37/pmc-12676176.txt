Closing GDMT Gaps in PAD Patients During Supervised Exercise Therapy
Authors: S.M. Muir, A.R. Albers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral artery disease carries high cardiovascular risk. Although supervised exercise therapy (SET) improves function, uptake of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) is inconsistent in practice.Project RationaleWe observed frequent missed opportunities for GDMT among patients with peripheral artery disease who were referred to a high-touch SET program.Project SummaryIn phase 1 of a multiphase quality improvement initiative, we retrospectively analyzed 60 consecutive SET referrals (January 2023–March 2025); 41 patients had complete follow-up and were included in the final analysis. Embedding clinical decision support, feedback, and outreach is the next step to translate functional gains into durable vascular protection.

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects over 8 million adults in the United States, and it carries rates of cardiovascular morbidity and functional decline comparable to coronary artery disease.1,2 The 2024 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines reaffirm comprehensive guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT)—statins, antiplatelet agents, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin-2 receptor antagonists in hypertensive patients, and GLP-1 receptor agonists for those with diabetes or obesity—as essential in PAD management.1,3, 4, 5 Yet adherence remains inconsistent, particularly outside major vascular centers, making it a prime target for quality improvement (QI) efforts.Take-Home Messages•SET improves function, but half of the patients still leave without complete GDMT—our biggest missed opportunity.•Clinical decision support prompts, clinician feedback, and patient outreach are the practical levers to convert SET gains into long-term cardiovascular risk reduction.

Supervised exercise therapy (SET) is a proven intervention, covered by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, that improves walking capacity and quality of life, but its integration with proactive GDMT optimization has been underexplored. Landmark trials such as STRIDE demonstrate the functional benefits of SET, but not the additive effect of concurrent GDMT or practical strategies to improve adherence.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

This phase 1 report of a multiphase QI initiative examines GDMT adherence among patients with PAD in a tertiary SET clinic as well as the relationship of GDMT adherence with functional outcomes. Subsequent phases will address system-level gaps. All patients received structured education within SET sessions, allowing assessment of outcomes in a high-touch, multidisciplinary environment.3, 4, 5

Project Rationale

Without concurrent risk-factor management, the functional gains from SET may not translate into long-term cardiovascular benefit. We hypothesized that: 1) GDMT adherence at follow-up would be suboptimal even in a high-touch environment; and 2) physiologic improvement (assessed using metabolic equivalents of task [METs]) would be most consistent among patients maintained on GDMT. A baseline audit was necessary to target system-level fixes.

Project Description

This work represents phase 1 of a planned 3-phase quality QI initiative to improve GDMT adherence among patients with PAD who were referred for SET. Phase 1 focused on a retrospective baseline audit to quantify adherence rates, assess functional outcomes, and identify system-level gaps. Findings from this phase will inform targeted interventions in phase 2 and prospective monitoring in phase 3 (Figure 1).Figure 1Overview of the 3-Phase QI Initiative to Optimize GDMT Adherence in Patients With PAD Undergoing SETPhase 1: retrospective analysis of baseline adherence and functional outcomes. Design and setting

A retrospective observational review was conducted on 60 consecutively referred adults with PAD who were enrolled between January 2023 and March 2025 in a hospital-based, structured, outpatient SET program within the cardiovascular rehabilitation service of a tertiary health system. Referrals were accepted from any licensed clinician, most commonly from vascular surgery and cardiology, with additional referrals from primary care and inpatient services. In line with GDMT, SET was offered regardless of revascularization candidacy. Of the 60 patients who were enrolled, 41 completed baseline and postprogram assessments and comprised the analytic cohort; 19 were excluded owing to nonattendance at therapy sessions, with reasons including interim hospitalizations, financial barriers, and time constraints given the frequency of therapy sessions.

Data and measures

Demographics, comorbidities, ankle-brachial index (ABI), and GDMT (antiplatelet, statin with intensity, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin-2 receptor antagonists when hypertensive, and semaglutide when clinically indicated). Functional outcomes included symptom relief, walking improvement, stress test time, total test time, and change in metabolic equivalents (ΔMETs).

Grouping

Medication status was categorized post hoc at follow-up as “on GDMT,” “never started,” or “discontinued.” “Never started” indicated no documented GDMT at baseline, whereas “discontinued” indicated therapy was present at baseline but absent at follow-up. Prescribing was managed by the referring clinicians.

Analysis

Group comparisons used t test/ANOVA (analysis of variance) and chi-square test as appropriate; effect sizes (Cohen d) were estimated for exercise endpoints. An exploratory regression examined baseline ABI versus change in performance.

Embedded education

All patients received structured medication and lifestyle education during SET, an intentional “background intervention.”

Project Deliverables

Phase 1 deliverables included: 1) a baseline performance report quantifying GDMT status and functional outcomes at follow-up; 2) a candidate measurement plan for phase 2 (service-level GDMT adherence, prescription and refill capture, and patient-reported barriers); 3) draft clinical decision support (CDS) artifacts (prompts in the electronic medical record and a GDMT intake checklist); and 4) a provider feedback model (brief huddles plus quarterly dashboards) with a patient outreach script for post-SET medication reconciliation.

Planned phase 2 changes

Planned phase 2 interventions include: 1) prompts in the electronic medical record and a structured GDMT checklist at SET intake/discharge; 2) provider dashboards with missed-opportunity flags; 3) patient outreach for medication initiation, refills, and synchronization; and 4) refined outcome tracking privileging METs over duration alone. Success targets include a ≥20-point absolute increase in GDMT at follow-up and sustained improvement in METs at 90 days.

Discussion

Phase 1 of this multiphase QI initiative demonstrated sizable GDMT gaps despite a high-touch SET environment. Patients maintained on GDMT showed the clearest physiologic improvement (ΔMETs), even with greater comorbidity burden; although not statistically significant (P = 0.02), the small to moderate effect size (Cohen d = 0.42) is consistent with a clinically meaningful signal.

Conversely, non-GDMT patients exhibited longer treadmill durations despite declines in METs, underscoring the limitations of duration-only endpoints that are sensitive to pacing, pain tolerance, and claudication thresholds. METs better reflect workload and cardiovascular reserve. Exploratory ABI patterns were concordant—greater perfusion tracked with endurance—though variability limited interference. Together, these observations support prioritizing workload-based measures (eg, METs) and pairing them with patient-reported outcomes, particularly given the paradox of subjective improvement among patients who discontinued therapy despite physiologic decline.

Critically, most referrals originated from cardiology, yet underuse of GDMT at enrollment persisted. This mismatch points to implementation failure rather than awareness: Ambiguity over prescribing ownership at the point of referral, transition-of-care friction, therapeutic inertia, and medication complexity likely contribute, compounded by structural barriers to SET adherence (time intensity, hospitalizations, cost). The weak alignment between comorbidity burden and GDMT use further suggests inconsistent execution of guideline-directed care.

These modifiable gaps motivate phase 2: standardized “default” prescribing pathways embedded at referral, CDS prompts at intake and follow-up, rapid feedback loops on adherence, and routine capture of patient-reported outcomes alongside METs, with closed-loop communication to the referring clinician.

Limitations of this study include its single-center design, modest sample size (41 patients with complete follow-up), potential selection/indication bias, reliance on discrete rather than continuous adherence capture, and an embedded education component that may have confound observed effects. These constraints are typical of an initial QI phase, and they directly informed the phase 2 design.

Conclusions

Even within a structured SET program, GDMT adherence at follow-up was approximately 50%, representing a major, fixable gap. Patients maintained on GDMT exhibited the most consistent physiologic gains. Phase 2 of this project will deploy CDS, provider feedback, and patient outreach to improve initiation and persistence and to align functional recovery with durable vascular protection.
