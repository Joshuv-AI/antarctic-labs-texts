Long-Term Survival in Liver Transplant Patients With Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Christopher L Wray, Jennifer C Scovotti, Jonathan Tobis, Henry M Honda, Raymond Planinsic, Ann Walia, James Findlay, Gebhard Wagener, Jacek B Cywinski, Andrea Olmos, Daniela Markovic, Christopher Hughes, Abhinav Humar, Rigoberto Sierra-Anderson, Douglas G Farmer, Danny Q Le, Randolph H Steadman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe age of liver transplant (LT) recipients continues to increase, and as a result, the prevalence of coronary artery disease (CAD) has risen. Long-term post-transplant outcomes in LT patients with CAD are poorly understood.ObjectivesWe previously demonstrated in a multi-institutional LT cohort that early post-transplant survival was similar between recipients with angiographically-proven, treated CAD and those with no CAD and nonobstructive CAD. We now present a follow-up study in this same cohort. Our objective is to determine long-term post-transplant survival in LT recipients with known pretransplant CAD. All-cause mortality is the primary outcome.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis for post-transplant survival in 604 adult LT recipients who underwent angiography prior to LT at 7 institutions between 1998 and 2010.ResultsAngiographically-proven obstructive CAD (≥50% stenosis) was present in 145 of 604 patients (24%). No CAD or nonobstructive CAD was found in 459 patients (76%). Mortality was higher in patients with CAD (adjusted HR: 1.39; CI: 1.06-1.82; P = 0.0159) than in those without CAD. Patients with CAD were further stratified by CAD severity and by pretransplant coronary revascularization. Mortality was higher in patients with severe CAD as defined by >70% vessel stenosis (adjusted HR: 1.63; CI: 1.19-2.22; P = 0.002) and in patients that underwent revascularization (adjusted HR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.18-2.23, P = 0.003) than in those without CAD. Mean follow-up time was 87.6 ± 55.6 months (7.3 ± 4.6 years) for all patients.ConclusionsContrary to our previous report, this investigation suggests that long-term post-transplant survival is adversely affected by underlying obstructive CAD.Key words: coronary angiography, coronary artery disease, liver transplant, post-transplant survival

Cardiovascular assessment is a major pretransplant step for orthotopic liver transplant (LT) candidates in the current era. A prevalence of coronary artery disease (CAD) in LT candidates that matches the general population has been well established.1, 2, 3, 4 Shifting cirrhosis etiologies and an aging population have resulted in an increased prevalence of CAD risk factors in current LT candidates.1,5 Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is recognized as a discrete CAD risk factor and has emerged as a leading cirrhosis etiology on a worldwide level.6,7 Consequently, LT centers employ significant resources towards the detection and treatment of obstructive CAD in LT candidates. Cardiology assessment in candidates with CAD risk factors increasingly includes the use of coronary angiography; revascularization of obstructive CAD as a bridge to LT has become a common practice in many transplant centers.8 A recent update regarding the screening for CAD in LT candidates suggests paradigms that include coronary angiography, medical management of nonobstructive CAD, and revascularization in select candidates with obstructive CAD.9

Despite the concern for the impact of CAD on LT outcomes, there remains a limited pool of evidence to support current management practices. Therefore, there is wide variability of risk tolerance for centers to perform LT in patients with obstructive CAD. We previously demonstrated in a multi-institutional cohort of LT patients that early post-transplant survival (median follow-up or time to death 24.5 months) was similar between recipients with angiographically-proven CAD who were successfully treated and recipients with no CAD and nonobstructive CAD.10 Other single-center studies have since reported similar findings.11,12 However, a recent meta-analysis of outcome studies in LT recipients found that the presence of pretransplant CAD was a risk factor for both cardiac-related and overall mortality.5 Following LT, CAD risk may further increase secondary to the effects of long-term immunosuppression on CAD risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and renal dysfunction. Previous reports from national datasets have shown that cardiovascular events are common over extended periods following LT and contribute to long-term mortality.13,14 Despite the perceived risk of CAD progression in LT recipients, particularly in those with pretransplant CAD, long-term outcomes related to CAD in LT candidates are poorly understood. We now report the results of our analysis of post-transplant survival in 604 adult LT recipients who underwent angiography prior to transplantation at 7 institutions over a 12-year period. Based on observations of long-term survival in patients with obstructive CAD in the general population, we hypothesized that long-term post-transplant survival would be adversely affected by the presence of pretransplant CAD.15

Materials and methods

After obtaining investigational review board approval (IRB-19-0245) at all 7 institutions (University of California Los Angeles, University of California-San Francisco, University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, Vanderbilt University, Mayo Clinic, Columbia University, and Cleveland Clinic), we reviewed the medical records of previously identified adult patients who underwent coronary angiography followed by primary LT over a 12-year period ending in December 2010. Study methods were similar to our previously published manuscript.10 Briefly, patients were identified by a search of the databases at each institution and followed up to determine post-LT survival. All LT recipients at each center were cross-referenced with angiography data collected at each respective institution. All patients underwent a pre-LT cardiac assessment that included pretransplant coronary angiography. The decision to employ angiography was determined independently by each institution as were revascularization decisions. Pretransplant cardiac paradigms were determined by each center and were not included as a study variable. Catheterization results were categorized as CAD positive (≥50% stenosis of one or more vessels) or CAD negative (no stenoses or <50% stenosis). Coronary stenoses of any major epicardial coronary artery were recorded. Positive angiography results were further stratified based on the degree of stenosis as severe CAD (>70% stenosis) or moderate CAD (50%-70% stenosis). Positive angiography results were also stratified by the presence or absence of pretransplant revascularization. Patients with a history of previous remote coronary intervention were stratified as severe CAD.

Demographic characteristics and survival variables selected from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients risk-adjustment models were collected in all patients. Demographic characteristics included age, gender, and model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score calculated at the time of LT. MELD calculation from this era included serum creatine, serum bilirubin, and International Normalized Ratio. Recipient risk factors for survival included a history of renal failure (identified by pre-LT dialysis), life support (pre-LT mechanical ventilation), and retransplantation following primary LT. Patients that underwent simultaneous liver kidney transplantation and living related liver transplantation were included. End-stage liver disease diagnosis was categorized in each patient. Diagnoses included hepatitis C, alcoholic liver disease, primary biliary cirrhosis and primary sclerosing cholangitis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and other. The category of other included hepatitis B and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Deceased donor risk factors included in the analysis were donor age, race, height, cause of death, partial/split donor organ, donation after cardiac death, donor location (regional or national), and cold ischemia time. These donor factors were used to calculate a donor risk index (DRI) score for all recipients according to the formula by Feng et al.16

All patients underwent perioperative surgical and anesthetic management as per their respective institutions. Date of angiography, date of coronary intervention when applicable, and date of LT were recorded in all patients. Date of death or last date of follow-up were recorded in all patients. Post-transplant management was as per each institution’s practice. All-cause mortality was recorded up to the last date of follow-up and was considered the primary outcome. The method of CAD intervention was categorized by type. Invasive CAD treatments included percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, bare metal coronary stenting, drug-eluting coronary stenting, coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG), and/or a combination of techniques.

Statistical analyses

A statistical analysis was performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and was consistent with our previous publication.10 We compared demographic characteristics and risk factors across the negative CAD, moderate CAD, and severe CAD patients. All results are expressed as mean (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, median (IQR) for nonnormally distributed continuous variables, or n (%) for categorical variables. P-values for comparing the groups were computed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables or the chi-square test for categorical variables. Survival curves by CAD groups were computed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared across groups using the log-rank test. We used Cox regression to assess the impact of the CAD group on mortality for the previously described recipient and donor covariates, using negative CAD patients as the reference category. The following predictor variables had missing values ranging from 2.3% to 11.8%: donor cause of death (2.3%), donor race (3.1%), partial/split graft (10.1%), graft location (3%), donor age (2.5%), donor height (11.8%), and cold ischemia time (2.5%). All missing values were singly imputed using the Markov chain Monte Carlo method. The multivariable model was adjusted for prespecified covariates including recipient age, recipient gender, MELD score, simultaneous liver/kidney transplant, history of dialysis, life support, DRI, and center. We reported the adjusted hazard ratios with corresponding confidence intervals and P-values using the Cox model. Adjusted survival curves by CAD groups were computed using the direct method based on the Cox model. We accounted for potential variations between institutions by including transplant center as a fixed effect in the Cox proportional hazards model. In a post-hoc analysis, we entered CAD status as a three-level variable (CAD−; CAD+ with pretransplant intervention; CAD+ without intervention) in the same Cox model with the same negative CAD reference group and reported the corresponding hazard ratios for each subgroup. For our Cox models, the outcome was time to death. The unadjusted model included CAD status only (reference group was the negative CAD group). The multivariable model additionally adjusted for age (dichotomized ≤55 vs >55 years), sex (male vs female), MELD score and DRI (both as continuous variables), simultaneous liver-kidney transplant, history of dialysis, life support at transplant, and center (7 sites).

Results

We previously identified a total of 630 adult LT patients that underwent pretransplant angiography at the 7 participating centers.10 Of these 630 patients, 26 (4%) were lost to long-term follow-up and were excluded from the study. Of the 26 patients lost to follow-up, 20 (77%) had no CAD, and 6 (23%) had obstructive CAD. A total of 604 patients were therefore analyzed for survival and were considered the total patient cohort for our long-term survival analysis (see study flow diagram) (Figure 1). Angiographically-proven obstructive CAD (≥50% stenosis) was present in 145 patients (24% of the total cohort). No CAD or nonobstructive CAD was found in 459 patients (76% of the total cohort) and were categorized as the CAD negative control cohort. Patients with CAD were further stratified by severity of CAD. These groups were defined as such based on angiographic findings prior to any coronary intervention. Patients with severe CAD were defined by having >70% stenosis (n = 93, 64% of the CAD+ patients). Patients with moderate CAD patients were defined by having 50% to 70% stenosis (n = 52, 36% of the CAD+ patients). Of the 145 patients with obstructive CAD, there were 78 patients (54% of the CAD+ patients) that underwent coronary interventions prior to LT; nine patients with moderate CAD and 69 patients with severe CAD. 2 patients underwent percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty alone, 37 patients underwent stent placement (14 drug-eluting coronary stenting and 23 bare metal coronary stenting), 28 patients underwent CABG, and 5 patients underwent a combination of coronary interventions. Of the patients that underwent CABG, there were 5 patients that underwent combined CABG/LT surgery. There were 17 patients (12% of the CAD+ patients) with a history of remote coronary intervention that occurred prior to pretransplant angiography. These patients were included within the severe CAD patients. There were 67 patients (46% of the CAD+ patients), 24 with severe CAD and 43 with moderate CAD, that received no pre-LT coronary intervention. Demographic characteristics, liver disease etiology, and recipient and donor organ covariates were compared between the CAD negative, moderate CAD, and severe CAD patients (Table 1). For the entire group of subjects, the mean age (standard deviation) was 58.5 years (7.3), and the mean MELD score was 25.0 (10.1). The 3 groups differed significantly by MELD score (P < 0.001) and gender (P = 0.002). MELD score was highest in patients without CAD (25.9) and lowest in the patients with severe CAD (20.9). There were more male patients with obstructive CAD. The proportions of male patients with moderate CAD (89%) and with severe CAD (77%) were greater than those in patients without CAD (67%). None of the other factors differed significantly between the patient groups including liver disease diagnosis and DRI. Overall, 50 patients underwent simultaneous liver kidney transplant, and 28 patients underwent living donor LT. There were 34 patients that underwent retransplantation following the primary LT. Total follow-up time or time to death in all patients was 87.6 ± 55.6 months (7.3 ± 4.6 years). There were significant differences between centers in number of patients, distribution of CAD groups, recipient age, MELD, and donor characteristics. The adjustment for center did not alter the effect of the CAD status on mortality, consistent with our previous findings. Due to the relatively small sample sizes within individual centers, we were unable to investigate interactions between CAD status and centers.

There were 207 deaths in patients with no CAD (45% of the CAD− patients). There were 23 deaths in patients with moderate CAD (44% of moderate CAD patients), and 56 deaths in patients with severe CAD (60% of severe CAD patients). We compared adjusted survival in patients with moderate CAD and severe CAD patients to patients with no CAD (Figure 3). Survival was worse in the patients with severe CAD (adjusted HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.19-2.22; P = 0.002). We compared adjusted survival in these 2 subgroups to the patients with no CAD (Table 3). Survival was worse in patients that underwent coronary intervention (adjusted HR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.18-2.23; P = 0.003) (Figure 4). Discussion

We report the findings of our long-term investigation in a multicenter cohort of patients that underwent coronary angiography prior to LT. Our results suggest that LT patients with known pretransplant CAD have inferior outcomes compared to those with no CAD or nonobstructive CAD over a prolonged post-transplant period. This finding was observed after adjustment for donor and recipient risk factors. In addition, we found that subgroups of patients with CAD had even worse long-term survival, specifically, in those patients with documented severe CAD and in those that underwent revascularization prior to LT. These findings differ from our original investigation in the same multicenter cohort that demonstrated no significant differences in early post-transplant survival between patients with and without obstructive CAD. To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study that has reported long-term outcomes in LT patients that had universally undergone pretransplant coronary angiography. Our findings have implications on the expected long-term outcomes for LT patients with known CAD.

Cardiovascular disease has received significant attention as a major risk factor in current LT candidates. Aging of LT candidates and shifts in cirrhosis etiologies have impacted the prevalence of occult CAD in LT candidates.1,5 Advances in the diagnosis, medical management, and interventional treatment strategies for CAD over the past 2 decades have affected the pretransplant management of LT candidates with CAD. Based on published guidelines, many transplant centers employ significant resources for the detection, treatment, and risk stratification of LT candidates with known CAD.9,17 Single-center series in addition to our previous publication have provided evidence to support the justification for allocating organs to LT candidates with CAD that has been managed in accordance with current practices.10, 11, 12 Results of these studies suggest that LT candidates with CAD that are otherwise appropriate candidates do not have an excessive risk of transplant futility in the early post-transplant period. However, other previously published reports have demonstrated that cardiovascular events are common over long term in LT recipients and have an adverse impact on survival.13 Current immunosuppression regimens are known to lead to the development of conditions that increase the risk of CAD such as metabolic syndrome and hyperlipidemia.18,19

Despite this, there remains a knowledge gap regarding long-term outcomes in LT recipients particularly related to the impact of underlying CAD. Our study demonstrates that survival decreases within a few years after LT in patients with CAD. Given the cumulative impact of chronic immunosuppression on the development and progression of conditions that contribute to CAD, these results are not particularly surprising. The risk of mortality related to CAD progression over time in patients with pre-existing obstructive CAD would presumably be higher than that in those patients with nonobstructive CAD or no CAD. Observations of long-term survival in nonsurgical populations with obstructive CAD have been previously published. In a study of a large cohort of patients with stable CAD and documented myocardial ischemia, the cumulative rates of primary outcome events (cardiovascular mortality and major adverse cardiac events) occurred in approximately 16% to 18% of patients at 5 years.15 Long-term survival in kidney transplant patients with CAD has also been reported. In a subset of patients enrolled in the ISCHEMIA-CKD trial, the primary outcome of all-cause mortality or nonfatal myocardial infarction at 3 years was approximately 30%.20 Collectively, these studies provide evidence for the long-term cumulative risks of cardiovascular complications and mortality in patients with known obstructive CAD. The results of our study are consistent with these previous investigations.

Interestingly, 2 subgroups of patients in our study had outcomes that were unanticipated. We found that patients with moderate CAD (defined as 50%-70% obstruction of one or more vessels) and patients with CAD that did not undergo pretransplant coronary intervention had survival outcomes that essentially matched the survival of our no-CAD control patients. This was surprising given the presumptive likelihood that the risk of CAD progression due to the sequalae of chronic immunosuppression would be similar for all patients. However, our retrospective design limited our ability to detect factors that may have influenced post-transplant survival in these subgroups. There may have been differences between centers regarding long-term postoperative cardiac surveillance and treatment in the patients with known CAD. Patients with multiple CAD lesions may have had a higher risk of developing coronary events than those with single lesions due to a higher burden of vessels at risk of disease progression. Patients with previous revascularization may have developed in-stent stenoses or CABG graft stenoses that led to fatal cardiac events over the long-term post-transplant period at a higher rate than in patients with nonobstructive CAD that did not require pretransplant interventions. All these factors could have adversely impacted long-term outcomes in the obstructive CAD subgroups that we found had worse survival.

Our study suggests that LT recipients with known pretransplant obstructive CAD should be considered a high-risk group that would merit close long-term post-transplant cardiology follow-up. American Heart Association guideline documents for LT patients mainly address pretransplant assessment.9,17 There is no clear consensus regarding how transplant centers should manage this unique group of post-transplant patients, particularly over a prolonged period. A large prospective study in a nontransplant surgical population demonstrated an association between perioperative myocardial injury detected by elevated levels of troponin isoforms and postoperative mortality.21 Presumably, LT patients with severe CAD would likely have evidence of perioperative myocardial injury. Detection of perioperative myocardial injury in LT recipients may provide an opportunity for early interventions that could possibly improve long-term outcomes related to cardiovascular disease. Interventions such as early postoperative anticoagulation and long-term medical therapy have been investigated in surgical populations.22,23 Early anticoagulation may not be suitable for LT patients at an elevated risk of postoperative hemorrhage. However, long-term medical therapy with antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and statins may be feasible. There is evidence for a survival benefit of statin use in post-transplant LT patients.11 Perhaps a more aggressive approach to atherosclerosis in LT patients with CAD with the use of a PCSK9 inhibitor to drive the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level below 40 μg/dL might be considered. A dedicated post-transplant management paradigm that includes medical management and serial cardiology follow-up may be considered in LT recipients with known pretransplant obstructive CAD. Further research in this area is necessary.

As previously mentioned, our study cohort was older with a mean age of 58.5 years. Although we did not collect data on pretransplant comorbidities that are correlated with CAD risk, our cohort could be inherently considered a higher risk group due to their requirement for pretransplant angiography. It may be expected that this older cohort with a greater burden of chronic diseases and risk factors for CAD would be at risk of worse long-term survival compared to all LT recipients. Current United Network for Organ Sharing survival statistics report a 10-year overall survival rate of 65%.24 Our patients with CAD had a 46% gross survival rate, notably lower than the overall 10-year survival rate reported for all U.S. recipients. However, we found a 65% gross survival rate in our patients without CAD that closely matches the current UNOS 10-year survival rate.

Study Limitations

Our study has limitations. Consistent with our previous investigation, we relied on retrospective analyses of medical data from 7 transplant centers.10 Differences in candidate selection and patient care between centers may have biased our results. As previously mentioned, adjustment for center did not alter the effect of the CAD group on mortality, but we were unable to investigate interactions between CAD groups and centers due to small institutional sample sizes. In addition, there may have been subtle differences in the anatomical distribution of CAD between patients that did not receive pretransplant interventions vs those that did. Some patients may have had differences in pretransplant comorbidities that may have impacted the decision to perform revascularization. In addition, there may have been differences in post-transplant cardiology management between patients that may have influenced the long-term results of our study. We were unable to determine the effect of post-transplant management on the impact of outcomes. Likewise, we could not determine the cause of mortality or the prevalence of cardiac events in our cohort. We performed multivariable analyses to control for variables that have significant impacts on post-transplant survival based on Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients risk-adjustment models. Nevertheless, differences in unmeasured variables may have impacted survival in our subjects in ways that we could not detect. We excluded 26 patients from our initial cohort since these patients were lost to long-term follow-up. Some of these patients may have died in the interim time frame, and these deaths would have remained undetected if we included these patients in our final analysis. To control for this, we chose to exclude this small cohort (4% of the original total patients). Of this group of patients lost to follow-up, 26% had CAD. This roughly matches the proportion of patients in our total study cohort that had CAD. Therefore, patients with CAD were not overrepresented in the group lost to follow-up.

Conclusions

Cardiovascular disease has an outsized impact in LT candidates and affects both candidate selection and postoperative outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report long-term survival in a multicenter cohort of patients that underwent coronary angiography prior to LT. Contrary to our previous study that addressed early post-transplant survival, our results demonstrate that LT patients with pretransplant CAD have inferior long-term outcomes compared to those with no CAD or nonobstructive CAD over a prolonged period of post-transplant follow-up. In addition, we found that subgroups of LT patients with severe CAD and with a history of pretransplant revascularization also had inferior outcomes. Our findings have implications on the expected long-term survival of LT patients with known CAD. Our results suggest that LT recipients with a history of pretransplant CAD should undergo intensive post-transplant cardiology follow-up. Specific post-transplant cardiology-management strategies including frequency of follow-up, noninvasive and invasive CAD testing paradigms, and medical management are not defined. Further study regarding long-term post-transplant CAD management in LT recipients is necessary. This manuscript is dedicated to Dr. Jonathan Tobis.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Cardiovascular disease has a significant impact on LT populations and affects both candidate selection and post-transplant outcomes. Our investigation particularly highlights the importance of known pretransplant obstructive CAD for long-term post-transplant survival. Our findings call to attention the need for long-term post-transplant cardiovascular surveillance in this high-risk subpopulation.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Further investigation is necessary to elucidate clinical paradigms that may positively impact long-term outcomes in these patients. LT institutions have developed highly trained teams of practitioners including cardiology and anesthesiology specialists focused on the pretransplant cardiovascular assessment and risk stratification of LT candidates. LT institutions may further consider development of multidisciplinary teams, including cardiologists, that are focused on long-term post-transplant follow-up as well. Specific posttransplant cardiovascular management strategies in this LT population remain to be defined.
