Contralateral Balance Training Improves Postural Control and Functional Recovery After Arthroscopic Anterior Talofibular Ligament Repair: A Prospective, Randomized, Single-Blind Trial
Authors: Yanmin Zhao, Long Pang, Yanlei Ge, Wenxi Li, Qian Deng, Zhipeng Zhou, Qi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) affects approximately 40% of patients within 1 year after a first-time acute lateral ankle sprain. While arthroscopic anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) repair is preferred when conservative treatment fails, early postoperative balance training is limited by weight-bearing restrictions. Cross-education, which transfers training benefits from one limb to the contralateral untrained limb, has demonstrated efficacy in improving postural control under conservative care; however, its postoperative utility remains poorly understood. This study investigates whether integrating contralateral balance training into standard rehabilitation enhances functional recovery after ATFL repair.MethodsThis study was conducted at the Sports Medicine Center of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China) between November 2024 and November 2025. Fifty-eight participants with unilateral CAI undergoing arthroscopic ATFL repair were randomized to an assisted rehabilitation group (ARG, n = 29) or conventional rehabilitation group (CRG, n = 29). Both groups received 6-week standard rehabilitation. The ARG additionally performed progressive contralateral limb balance training three times per week for 6 weeks. Primary and secondary outcomes included static balance (sway velocity), dynamic postural control (Star Excursion Balance Test [SEBT]), sensory integration (Sensory Organization Test [SOT]), and patient-reported outcomes (American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society [AOFAS] Ankle-Hindfoot Scale, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure-Activities of Daily Living [FAAM-ADL] subscale, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure-Sports [FAAM-Sport] subscale, and Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia [TSK]).ResultsPost-intervention, the ARG demonstrated significantly lower eyes-closed sway velocity (P = 0.016) and greater SEBT reach distances in anterior (P = 0.005) and posterolateral (P = 0.018) directions than the CRG. The ARG also achieved superior equilibrium scores during sensory conflict (SOT-3: P < 0.001; SOT-4: P = 0.023). Furthermore, the ARG reported significantly better functional scores than the CRG, including AOFAS and FAAM-ADL, while FAAM-Sport showed relative preservation compared with a significant decline in the CRG.ConclusionCompared with conventional rehabilitation alone, supplemental contralateral balance training resulted in statistically superior postural control under eyes-closed conditions and in selected dynamic balance directions. Improvements in sensory integration (SOT3, SOT4) were statistically significant but did not reach the respective condition-specific MCIDs. This approach appears safe and feasible during early-stage rehabilitation, with the primary effect being attenuation of early postoperative decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01078-x.Keywords: Chronic ankle instability, ATFL repair, Cross-education, Balance training, Postural control

Background

Lateral ankle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries in sports and physical activities [1, 2]. Although frequently perceived as benign, a substantial proportion of patients experience persistent mechanical and functional deficits after the initial insult [3]. Evidence from prospective cohort tracking indicates that approximately 40% of patients sustaining a first-time acute lateral ankle sprain progress to chronic ankle instability (CAI) within1 year of the initial injury, a progression heavily driven by high recurrent re-injury rates [1, 4–6]. This transition is characteristically defined by recurrent episodes of "giving way", compromised postural control, and persistent functional limitations [7, 8]. CAI not only restricts daily activities and athletic performance but also significantly elevates the long-term risk of developing post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis [9].

The anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) is the most frequently injured ligament structure during lateral ankle sprains [3]. For patients with CAI who fail conservative management, arthroscopic ATFL repair has become an established and effective surgical intervention to restore mechanical stability to the ankle joint [10]. However, surgical success does not equate to complete functional recovery; postoperative rehabilitation is equally crucial [11, 12].

Exercise therapy has long been established as the cornerstone of conservative care for CAI. Indeed, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have robustly demonstrated that diverse exercise modalities, encompassing supervised clinical rehabilitation and home-based training programs, effectively optimize functional outcomes, dynamic balance, patient-reported function, and sensorimotor control in CAI populations [13, 14]. Nevertheless, in current clinical practice following ATFL repair, conventional rehabilitation protocols during the initial postoperative weeks remain predominantly restricted to protective passive mobilization and basic range-of-motion exercises, whereas active balance training and progressive weight-bearing are traditionally delayed until after 6 weeks postoperative [12]. Consequently, even after completing systematic postoperative rehabilitation, a significant proportion of patients consistently exhibit residual deficits in proprioception and neuromuscular balance control from 3 months up to 1 year postoperative, indicating incomplete functional restoration compared to the contralateral untrained limb [15–17].

Cross-education is a neurophysiological phenomenon whereby unilateral training of one limb induces strength and skill gains in the contralateral untrained limb [18, 19]. This phenomenon has been well documented in healthy individuals, and recent studies have demonstrated that unilateral balance training in patients with CAI can significantly improve balance performance of the contralateral untrained limb [20–22], with mechanisms involving central nervous system adaptations, including interhemispheric transfer via the corpus callosum and modulation of corticospinal pathways [23]. However, the application of cross-education has not been systematically investigated during the early stage following ATFL repair, when single-limb balance training on the affected side is not yet feasible. In typical clinical management, active balance training on the affected side is restricted during the first 6 weeks postoperation to safeguard ligament healing [12, 17]. Based on this rationale, we developed a modified rehabilitation protocol that integrates a progressive, remotely supervised contralateral balance training program specifically targeting this early 6-week non-weight-bearing window. This approach aims to address the early post-surgical rehabilitation interval by stimulating the contralateral untrained limb before weight-bearing on the affected side is clinically permissible.

Therefore, the primary aim of this prospective, randomized, single-blind trial is to compare the effects of adding contralateral balance training to standard postoperative rehabilitation versus conventional rehabilitation alone on postural control and functional recovery at 6 weeks after arthroscopic ATFL repair. We hypothesized that patients receiving contralateral balance training would demonstrate superior outcomes in postural control and functional recovery at 6 weeks postoperatively compared with those receiving conventional rehabilitation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective, randomized, single-blind, parallel-group controlled trial was conducted at the Sports Medicine Center of West China Hospital in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [24].

Participants

Participants scheduled for primary arthroscopic ATFL repair for unilateral CAI were recruited between November 2024 and November 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged 15–50 years undergoing standardized all-inside arthroscopic ATFL suture repair and footprint reconstruction using a single double-loaded suture anchor; (2) meeting the definitive diagnostic criteria for CAI established by the International Ankle Consortium (IAC), which required a history of at least one significant lateral ankle sprain sustained ≥ 12 months prior, recurrent episodes of the ankle 'giving way' or persistent instability within the past 6 months, and a baseline Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) score ≤ 24 [25]; (3) a positive anterior drawer test confirming ATFL laxity, while a negative varus stress test indicating no calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) laxity (verified by bilateral comparisons); (4) ATFL injury or avulsion insufficiency verified by preoperative musculoskeletal ultrasonography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (5) failed conservative management for ≥ 3–6 months; and (6) willingness to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) bilateral CAI or history of revision ankle surgery; (2) concomitant lower-limb musculoskeletal conditions (e.g., cartilage damage, fractures, multi-ligament injuries); (3) neurological disorders impairing balance or motor control; and (4) inability to comply with study protocols.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6, Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). The Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) was selected as the primary outcome measure for sample size estimation because it is a well-validated, functionally relevant measure of dynamic postural control that is highly responsive to balance training in CAI populations. Referring to Sefton et al. (2011) [26], who reported a partial η2 of 0.2 for the SEBT following balance training in individuals with chronic ankle instability, we adopted this effect size for our surgically treated population, anticipating potentially greater variability in the postoperative setting.

Based on partial η2 = 0.2 (corresponding to Cohen’s f = 0.5), with α = 0.05, power = 0.90, 2 groups, 2 measurements, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5, and nonsphericity correction ε = 1, G*Power calculated a minimum of 14 participants per group. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate and an additional 15% safety margin to ensure adequate power for secondary outcome analyses and to accommodate greater variability in this surgical population, the final sample size was set at 30 participants per group (N = 60 total).

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomized to the ARG or CRG (1:1 ratio) via a computer-generated random number sequence (Microsoft Excel). Group allocations were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes opened by a research assistant only after baseline assessments. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout the study, and participants were strictly instructed not to disclose their assignment.

Interventions

Conventional Rehabilitation Group (CRG)

Participants in the CRG performed a standardized 6-week home-based rehabilitation program for the surgical limb (3 sessions/week, approximately 40 min/session), delivered via instructional videos and remotely supervised through weekly video calls with a qualified physiotherapist. The treatment regimen included the following: (1) weight-bearing progression: crutch-assisted partial weight-bearing for the first 2 weeks postoperatively; full weight-bearing while wearing a rigid ankle brace from weeks 3 to 6; single-limb stance on the surgical limb was not initiated until after 6 weeks postoperatively. (2) Brace usage: cast immobilization for the first 2 weeks; transition to a rigid ankle brace worn during daily activities from weeks 3 to 6, removed only during prescribed exercises. (3) Range of motion training: active ankle dorsiflexion and static standing on a tilting board (15°) initiated at week 3; plantarflexion added at week 4 with tilting board angle increased to 30°; inversion and eversion added at week 5 with tilting board angle increased to 45°. All ROM exercises were performed without resistance and without restrictions beyond pain tolerance. (4) Strengthening timeline: straight leg raises, toe flexion/extension, towel curls (toe grasping), and great toe extension were performed during weeks 1–3; elastic band resistance for dorsiflexion (15 lbs) was introduced at week 4; at week 5, elastic band resistance for dorsiflexion was increased to 20 lbs, and isometric contractions for plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion were added; at week 6, elastic band resistance was upgraded to include all four directions (dorsiflexion: 25 lbs; plantarflexion, inversion, eversion: 15 lbs), while towel curls, great toe extension, and self-myofascial release of the plantar fascia were continued. Further details regarding sets, repetitions, and rest intervals are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Assisted Rehabilitation Group (ARG)

Participants in the ARG received the same standard postoperative rehabilitation program for the surgical ankle as the CRG (3 sessions/week, approximately 40 min/session). In addition, they completed a supervised, progressive balance training program for the contralateral limb. To avoid interference, contralateral training was scheduled on different days from standard rehabilitation, also performed 3 times per week for 6 weeks (18 sessions total, approximately 40 min/session).

The balance training program was designed according to progressive difficulty and integrated principles of motor learning and neuroplasticity [21, 22]. It progressed from single-limb stance on a firm surface (eyes open to eyes closed) to unstable surfaces, and finally to dynamic tasks with dual-task interference. Each session included a 5-min warm-up and a 5-min cool-down (ankle mobility and stretching exercises). Exercises were performed in 3 to 6 sets with 30-s rest intervals. Progression required successful completion of the current level with minimal postural sway before advancing. Instructional videos were provided, and each session was remotely supervised by a qualified physiotherapist via scheduled video calls to monitor performance, provide feedback, and ensure adherence. Detailed progression is presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Adherence Monitoring

The supervising physiotherapist conducted weekly video calls with each participant to review training logs and provide feedback. For the remaining sessions, participants performed exercises independently while recording their completion in the training logs. The physiotherapist cross-checked at least one randomly selected session per week via live video demonstration during the scheduled calls. Completion was defined as finishing all prescribed sets and repetitions with adequate form. Adherence data were extracted from these logs and video records. Missed or incomplete sessions were documented but not replaced.

Outcome Measures

All assessments were conducted by a single blinded assessor at two time points: preoperatively (within 1 week before surgery) and post-intervention (6 weeks after surgery, approximately 45 days postoperation). The assessment battery included measures of static and dynamic postural control, sensory integration, motor control, adaptation, gait parameters, and patient-reported outcomes.

Primary Outcomes

Unilateral Stance Test (UST): static balance on the surgical limb was assessed under eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions using the NeuroCom Smart EquiTest dynamic posturography system (Natus Medical Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA). Participants performed a 10-s single-limb stance with the contralateral limb flexed. The average center of pressure sway velocity (degrees/second) across three successful trials was analyzed [27].

Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT): dynamic postural control was evaluated in the anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions. Maximum reach distances were normalized to leg length (measured from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus) and expressed as a percentage, with the average of three trials used for analysis [28].

Sensory Organization Test (SOT): sensory integration was evaluated across six conditions (SOT1–SOT6) on the Smart EquiTest system. The equilibrium score (0–100) for each condition served as the primary outcome, with higher scores reflecting superior postural control [29, 30].

Secondary Outcomes

Motor Control Test (MCT): automatic postural responses were evaluated by measuring response latency (milliseconds) and amplitude scaling to sudden, unexpected forward and backward platform translations (small, medium, and large amplitudes). Data from the surgical limb during medium and large translations were averaged for analysis [31, 32].

Adaptation test (ADT): sensory-motor adaptation to repeated perturbations was assessed by measuring sway energy (Newton/second) during five consecutive toes-up and toes-down support-surface rotations (8 degree amplitude, 0.4 s duration) [33].

Gait analysis: spatial–temporal parameters were evaluated using the Walk Across Test (WAT: measuring step width, step length, and walking speed at a self-selected comfortable pace) [34] and the Tandem Walk Test (TWT: measuring step width, speed, and end-sway velocity during heel-to-toe walking along a 153-cm line) [35].

Patient-reported outcomes: subjective functional recovery was assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot Scale (0–100, higher scores indicate better function) [36]. The Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) evaluated daily activities (FAAM-ADL, 21 items) and sports performance (FAAM-Sport, 7 items), normalized to 0%–100% [37]. Kinesiophobia was measured via the 17-item Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK, scores > 37 indicate clinically significant fear of movement) [38].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and RStudio 2022.12.0, with significance set at P < 0.05. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline characteristics were compared using independent-samples t-tests or Chi-square tests. For normally distributed data, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) evaluated the main effects of group, time, and their interaction, with partial eta-squared (ηp2 ≥ 0.14) denoting a large effect size [39]. Bonferroni corrections were applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Scheirer–Ray–Hare test was used, with within-group and between-group post hoc analyses performed via Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann–Whitney U tests, respectively. Non-parametric data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges.

Results

Participant Flow and Baseline Characteristics

Of 70 patients initially screened, 60 met the inclusion criteria and were randomized to the ARG (n = 30) or CRG (n = 30). Based on training logs and video confirmations, adherence to the prescribed sessions was as follows: in the ARG, 29 of 30 participants (96.7%) completed all 36 prescribed sessions (18 standard + 18 contralateral training), with one participant missing two sessions due to temporary illness; in the CRG, all 30 participants (100%) completed all 18 prescribed standard sessions. Two participants (one per group) were excluded from the final analysis because they were out of town at the 6-week follow-up and could not return for in-person outcome assessments; their training data were complete up to the point of exclusion. The final analysis included 58 participants who completed the 6-week outcome assessment (complete-case analysis). Intention-to-treat analysis was not performed because the two excluded participants had no post-intervention outcome data available for imputation. No adverse events related to the interventions were reported in either group, supporting the preliminary safety and feasibility of this remotely supervised paradigm (Fig. 1). Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics, including sex, injury side, age, height, weight and baseline CAIT scores, were highly comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05; Table 1).

For patient-reported outcomes (Table 6), significant group-by-time interactions were confirmed for AOFAS (P = 0.010) and FAAM-Sport (P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.473) scores. Post hoc testing indicated that post-intervention improvements were exclusive to the ARG for AOFAS (P < 0.001), while for FAAM-Sport, the CRG suffered a severe longitudinal decline (P < 0.001) contrasted against a stable profile in the ARG (P > 0.05). Additionally, significant main group effects favored the ARG for both FAAM-ADL (P = 0.002) and TSK scores (P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.720), with the ARG reporting significantly superior overall daily function and lower post-intervention kinesiophobia (P < 0.001) compared with the CRG.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial investigated whether adding contralateral balance training to standard rehabilitation enhances functional recovery after arthroscopic ATFL repair for CAI. The ARG showed better static balance (eyes‑closed), greater dynamic control (anterior/posterolateral SEBT), improved sensory integration (SOT3/4), and better patient‑reported outcomes (AOFAS, FAAM-ADL, TSK) than the CRG, while FAAM-Sport showed relative preservation compared with a significant decline in the CRG. These findings suggest that cross‑education may play a contributing role during the early postoperative period when the surgical limb cannot engage in weight‑bearing balance training, although nonspecific factors such as increased training volume cannot be excluded. We acknowledge the absence of direct neurophysiological measures (e.g., EMG, TMS, EEG, fMRI) in this study. Thus, the following mechanistic interpretations are speculative and intended to generate hypotheses for future research.

Improved Static and Dynamic Postural Stability

The superior performance of the ARG in eyes‑closed single-limb stance is particularly noteworthy. Removing visual input forces greater reliance on somatosensory and vestibular information for balance maintenance [40]. CAI patients are known to exhibit increased visual dependence and impaired proprioceptive acuity [41]. The ARG's advantage under this challenging condition suggests that contralateral training may have enhanced central processing of somatosensory feedback, effectively reducing pathological visual dependence [42]. This interpretation aligns with McKeon et al., who demonstrated that balance training in CAI patients improved time‑to‑boundary measures during eyes‑closed but not eyes‑open stance, indicating specific gains in somatosensory contributions to postural control [43].

The SEBT findings further support this interpretation. The ARG demonstrated superior performance in the anterior and posterolateral directions, which place high demands on ankle neuromuscular control and are consistently impaired in CAI populations. The ability to dynamically stabilize the ankle during multiplanar movements is critical for preventing recurrent sprains during daily and sports activities [44]. Notably, the CRG showed a significant decline in posteromedial reach distance postoperatively, suggesting that standard rehabilitation alone may be insufficient to maintain dynamic stability during the early healing phase. The ARG's preservation of dynamic balance may reflect, at least in part, transfer of enhanced central motor commands from the trained to the untrained limb, alongside potential contributions from increased rehabilitation volume and therapist interaction.

These findings are consistent with prior cross‑education studies in CAI. Elsotohy et al. reported that unilateral balance training improved overall, anteroposterior, and mediolateral stability indices in the contralateral limb of CAI patients, with effects comparable to traditional bilateral training [22]. Similarly, Hale et al. demonstrated bilateral improvements in SEBT performance following 4 weeks of unilateral balance training [21]. The present study extends these observations by showing that contralateral training may confer benefits even when the target limb is surgically repaired and initially non‑weight‑bearing, although the specific contribution of cross-education versus nonspecific training effects cannot be determined from this design.

Enhanced Sensory Integration

The SOT findings provide insight into potential neural mechanisms underlying the observed behavioral improvements. The ARG demonstrated superior equilibrium scores under conditions of sensory conflict (SOT3: visual conflict with stable platform; SOT4: platform sway with stable visual surround). These conditions require the central nervous system to rapidly and appropriately re‑weight sensory inputs when one channel provides unreliable information [45]. Efficient sensory re‑weighting is essential for maintaining balance in daily environments characterized by changing surfaces and visual conditions [46, 47].

The significant improvement in the ARG's performance under SOT3 and SOT4 raises the possibility that contralateral training may enhance central sensory integration capacity, while acknowledging the potential confounding effects of increased training exposure and therapist engagement. Although we did not directly measure neural activity, previous neurophysiological studies have demonstrated that unilateral limb training activates bilateral sensorimotor cortices, premotor areas, and cerebellum [18, 48]. One hypothesis is that repeated activation of these networks during challenging balance tasks could potentially strengthen interhemispheric connections via the corpus callosum, which might facilitate transfer of motor learning to the untrained limb [49]. Recent neuroimaging studies have shown that balance training induces structural and functional changes in cortical regions involved in sensory integration, including increased cortical thickness in visual and vestibular areas [50]. However, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative, and the contribution of nonspecific factors such as increased training volume cannot be ruled out.

Regarding the clinical relevance of these SOT improvements, Li et al. (2025) established condition-specific MCIDs for the SOT in CAI population: 4.41 for SOT3 and 6.04 for SOT4 [51]. The ARG improvements in SOT3 (+ 1.1) and SOT4 (+ 4.76) did not reach these thresholds, suggesting that although statistically significant, these changes may not represent clinically meaningful benefits. For SOT5 and SOT6, while the ARG showed within-group improvements exceeding MCID (+ 6.4 and + 14.04, respectively), these reflected only significant main effects of time without group-by-time interactions; thus, they cannot be attributed specifically to the intervention.

The absence of significant group differences in automatic postural responses (MCT, ADT) is noteworthy and may reflect differential engagement of neural pathways. MCT and ADT assess relatively reflexive, spinally‑mediated responses to external perturbations, whereas SOT and SEBT require more volitional, cortically‑mediated postural strategies [52]. One hypothesis is that cross‑education may preferentially influence higher‑level central processing rather than spinal reflex pathways, which might require direct stimulation of the involved limb for optimal adaptation [53, 54]. This dissociation aligns with the concept of hierarchical motor control, where different rehabilitation strategies target different levels of the neuraxis [55, 56].

Improved Patient-Reported Outcomes and Reduced Kinesiophobia

The convergence of objective balance outcomes with superior patient‑reported outcomes in the ARG strengthens the clinical relevance of our findings. AOFAS scores, which integrate pain, function, and walking ability, showed greater improvement in the ARG compared with the CRG. The FAAM‑Sport subscale, a sensitive measure of higher‑level functional capacity relevant to athletic populations, showed a particularly striking difference: the ARG maintained baseline scores while the CRG declined substantially [37]. This suggests that contralateral training preserved the capacity for sports participation during a period when the surgical limb was least accessible to direct training [20].

The significant reduction in TSK scores in the ARG addresses an often‑overlooked but critical aspect of postoperative recovery [38]. Kinesiophobia is prevalent in CAI patients and predicts poorer functional outcomes [57]. Ayas et al. recently reported that 84% of patients remained kinesiophobic at long‑term follow‑up after ATFL repair, with persistent postural control deficits compared to healthy controls [17]. The mechanism linking contralateral training to reduced fear may hypothetically involve enhanced self‑efficacy through successful task completion on the contralateral limb, which positively influences beliefs about the surgical limb's capabilities [58]. This interpretation is consistent with the biopsychosocial model of CAI proposed by Hertel and Corbett, which emphasizes interactions among physiological, psychological, and behavioral factors [59].

Clinical Implications and Limitations

Clinically, we recommend incorporating a progressive and increasingly challenging contralateral balance training program into standard post-ATFL repair protocols as early as the first postoperative week, advancing from stable to unstable surfaces and from single- to dual-tasks. This adjunctive strategy requires minimal equipment, imposes zero mechanical stress on the healing ligament, and fills a critical early rehabilitation window during which weight-bearing balance training on the surgical limb is typically restricted.

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the 6-week follow-up limits insights into long-term functional sustainability. Whether the observed early benefits, primarily prevention of postoperative decline, translate into meaningful long-term outcomes such as faster return to sport or reduced reinjury risk remains unknown and requires extended follow-up. Second, the lack of neurophysiological measures such as transcranial magnetic stimulation leaves the exact mechanisms unconfirmed, rendering our mechanistic interpretations speculative. Third, our pragmatic design cannot fully isolate the effects of cross-education from the confounding influences of increased rehabilitation volume and therapist interaction time in the ARG. Although sham-training or attention-matched control groups were considered, they were excluded due to logistical difficulties in ensuring exercise standardization and safety in a remotely supervised home environment. Thus, the observed clinical benefits cannot be definitively attributed to cross-education alone. Fourth, baseline ceiling effects (SOT1/SOT2 > 93, FAAM-ADL > 90%) may have limited detectable improvement, particularly for the CRG. Fifth, the gait assessment tasks (Walk Across Test and Tandem Walk Test) may have been insufficiently challenging to detect improvements in this population, and the 6-week intervention period may be too short to capture gait changes. Future multi-center studies with extended follow-up, volume-matched control designs, more challenging gait assessments, and larger sample sizes are needed to address these limitations.

Conclusions

Incorporating a 6-week progressive contralateral balance training program into the early postoperative phase after arthroscopic ATFL repair yielded statistically superior outcomes in static and dynamic postural control compared with conventional rehabilitation alone, primarily by attenuating early postoperative decline. Improvements in sensory integration (SOT3, SOT4) were statistically significant but did not reach the respective condition-specific MCIDs. The intervention also mitigated the early decline in sports-specific functional capacity. These findings suggest that contralateral balance training is a safe and feasible adjunctive strategy during the non-weight-bearing window, with cross-education representing a plausible but unconfirmed mechanism. Longer-term follow-up is needed to determine whether these early benefits translate into clinically meaningful outcomes.
