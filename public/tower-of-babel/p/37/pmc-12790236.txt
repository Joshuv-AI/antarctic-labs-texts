Multimodal Imaging in the Diagnosis of Late-Stage Loeffler Endocarditis
Authors: Catherine Teng, Aneesh Dhore-Patil, Neal Bhachawat, Muneeb Ajaz, Miles Hill, Henry Ellison, John Buergler, Karla Kurrelmeyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoeffler endocarditis (LE) is a rare and progressive form of eosinophilic myocarditis characterized by endomyocardial fibrosis, mural thrombus, and restrictive cardiomyopathy.Case SummaryA 70-year-old woman presented with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and eosinophilia. Transthoracic echocardiography and computed tomography revealed subendocardial calcifications. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed subendocardial fibrosis and mural thrombus, consistent with fibrotic-stage LE. Stool testing identified parasitic infection as the cause of eosinophilia. Antimicrobial therapy normalized eosinophil level, but cardiac fibrosis persisted on follow-up.DiscussionLE is a rare cause of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, with irreversible cardiac remodeling in its late stages. This case emphasizes the importance of multimodality imaging in diagnosing LE and the potential to reverse systemic—but not always cardiac—manifestations with appropriate treatment.Take-Home MessageThis case highlights the features of late-stage LE and the importance of early recognition and treatment of potentially reversible causes.

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with worsening shortness of breath for the past several days and increasing lower extremity edema for a few weeks in addition to chronic diarrhea for approximately 3 months. On examination, she had increased work of breathing. She was tachycardic with irregular heart rhythm. Bilateral crackles and lower extremity nonpitting edema were also noted.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, central obstructive pulmonary disease, and hypertension. She had no known coronary artery disease, confirmed by prior angiogram.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis considered by the initial treating physician included pulmonary embolism, acute decompensated heart failure, and central obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation.

Investigations

The patient's initial laboratory data were notable for hyponatremia 134 mEq/L, marked eosinophilia accounting for 21% of total cell count, and elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide 1,807 pg/mL. Renal function and serum calcium levels were within normal limits. The electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular rate.

Given her symptoms of dyspnea, tachyarrhythmia, and elevated natriuretic peptide, a contrast-enhanced, nongated computed tomography (CT) scan was performed to evaluate for pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed the presence of a linear, echodense mass along the subendocardial left ventricular (LV) myocardium, particularly involving the distal inferolateral wall and apex (Figure 2). The LV ejection fraction was moderately preserved at 50% to 55%, though global hypokinesis was noted. Management

The patient was admitted for HFpEF exacerbation. She was started on intravenous furosemide for diuresis and apixaban for atrial fibrillation. Review of the CT and TTE findings narrowed the differential diagnosis to rare causes of dystrophic myocardial calcification. Chronic myocardial infarction was considered unlikely, given the absence of myocardial thinning and a recent normal coronary angiogram. Other potential causes—such as chronic kidney disease, extracardiac sarcoidosis, and disorders of calcium metabolism—were ruled out based on clinical and laboratory findings. Given the elevated eosinophil count and a review of laboratory tests over the past 20 years showing persistent eosinophilia (Figure 4), chronic eosinophilic myocarditis became the leading diagnosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was performed to assess for endomyocardial fibrosis and thrombus. Findings revealed prominent subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement of the lateral and apical LV walls, seen well on the bright-blood and dark-blood (flow independent) sequences (Figure 5). Further evaluation for the cause of eosinophilia identified Entamoeba histolytica on stool culture and positive Strongyloides IgG. The patient was treated with a 10-day course of metronidazole and nitazoxanide for amebiasis and was initiated on guideline-directed medical therapy for HFpEF.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After treatment, the patient noted improvement of her dyspnea on exertion and chronic diarrhea. She was continued on guideline-directed therapy for heart failure and apixaban for atrial fibrillation and LV thrombus. Her eosinophilia count was normalized. Repeat echocardiography performed 3 and 6 months later showed unchanged LV ejection fraction with global hypokinesis and endomyocardial calcification; no thrombus was noted on the repeat studies. The patient subsequently had further exacerbations of HFpEF but no recurrence of eosinophilia.

Discussion

Hypereosinophilic syndrome is a rare condition, with an annual incidence of 0.36 to 6.3 per 100,000 patients.1 Cardiac involvement, referred to as LE, occurs in up to 50% of cases and is even more uncommon in North America, as it is typically associated with tropical infections.2 The main manifestation of LE is hypereosinophilic syndrome with endomyocardial fibrosis and thrombus formation.2 LE results from prolonged eosinophilia and is characterized by progressive stages of myocardial damage, beginning with necrosis, followed by thrombus formation, and culminating in fibrosis and restrictive cardiomyopathy.3

This case highlights the importance of using multimodality imaging to guide in the diagnosis of LE and underscores how early clues, such as longstanding eosinophilia, can be overlooked, leading to irreversible stages of the disease. Contrast-enhanced CT, originally performed to rule out pulmonary embolism, was instrumental in detecting subendocardial calcification. This incidental yet critical finding prompted further evaluation and ultimately led to the diagnosis of LE. To the best of our knowledge, this is the only case reported in the literature of late-stage eosinophilic myocarditis manifesting as endomyocardial calcification.

LE progresses according to 3 overlapping phases: an acute necrotic phase, a thrombotic phase, and a fibrotic phase, where the inflammation is replaced by fibrosis, leading to restrictive cardiomyopathy.2 The acute necrotic phase involves eosinophilic infiltration and degranulation, which can cause myocardial injury with few initial cardiac symptoms. The findings on TTE can be nonspecific or normal. Endomyocardial biopsy can show eosinophil-mediated myocardial inflammation; however it is not often pursued at this early stage. The thrombotic phase follows, marked by endothelial damage and mural thrombus formation, often mediated by eosinophil-derived proteins such as peroxidase.4,5 The presence of a mural thrombus, confirmed on CMR, is a hallmark of the thrombotic phase and underscores the hypercoagulable state seen in eosinophilic cardiomyopathy. The fibrotic stage, however, involves replacement of damaged tissue with fibrous scar, leading to impaired ventricular compliance and restrictive physiology.2 This stage is often irreversible, and patients manifest with symptoms of HFpEF. TTE often shows effacement of the LV apex owing to subendocardial fibrosis, with calcification, impaired relaxation, and elevated filling pressures. A thrombus may coexist in this stage. CMR at this stage can further demonstrate extensive subendocardial hyperenhancement and scar formation. It is often helpful to confirm thrombus formation using CMR.6 In our patient, echocardiography revealed key features of late-stage LE: apical subendocardial thickening, calcification, impaired diastolic function, and elevated filling pressures. The severely enlarged left atrium and abnormal Doppler indices were consistent with HFpEF. CMR confirmed subendocardial fibrosis and mural thrombus, hallmarks of the fibrotic and thrombotic stages of LE. Advanced CMR techniques, such as flow-independent dark-blood delayed enhancement sequences, were particularly useful in highlighting subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement.6

LE diagnosis remains challenging because of its nonspecific clinical presentation. The key findings of LE on TTE include endomyocardial thickening of the LV apex with or without thrombus, in addition to typical signs of HFpEF, such as an enlarged left atrium, low mitral annulus tissue Doppler velocity, elevated E velocity and E/A ratio on mitral inflow pulsed-wave Doppler, elevated E/e′ ratio >14, low isovolumic relaxation time, and low E-wave deceleration time. With contrast-enhancing agents, a thrombus may also be detected.4 CMR is crucial in diagnosing LE, with higher sensitivity and specificity in detecting subtle subendocardial involvement and thrombus.4,7 A standard CMR protocol in patients with suspected LE should include long-axis views (2-, 3-, and 4-chamber) and short-axis views (basal, midventricular, and apical) with steady-state free precession, T1-and T2-weighted, and contrast-enhanced (first passage of gadolinium and late phase) dynamic sequences. Additional sequences should be acquired if a thrombus is suspected. This comprehensive approach allows accurate assessment of morphological and functional cardiac chamber changes, early detection of subendocardial tissue abnormalities, and detection of ventricular thrombi.7 CT offers high spatial resolution and provides more anatomical detail; however, echocardiography and CMR are preferred, as they allow hemodynamic assessment and cardiac tissue characterization.7

Although in the current case the eosinophilia was ultimately linked to parasitic infections, specifically E. histolytica and Strongyloides, the patient's laboratory history revealed 2 decades of persistent eosinophilia. Earlier recognition of this abnormality could have prompted investigation into possible cardiac involvement before irreversible fibrosis developed. This case illustrates the cost of missed opportunities for early diagnosis in chronic eosinophilic conditions.

Management of LE involves treating the underlying cause of eosinophilia and addressing cardiac complications. In this case, targeted antiparasitic therapy normalized the eosinophil count and improved systemic symptoms. However, the persistence of endomyocardial calcification and systolic dysfunction on follow-up imaging indicates that the cardiac damage had progressed to a late stage before intervention. This reinforces the importance of longitudinal monitoring and early work-up of unexplained eosinophilia.

Ultimately, this case emphasizes the importance of integrating imaging findings, clinical suspicion, and laboratory trends to reach a timely diagnosis. It underscores the potential value of reviewing imaging performed for unrelated concerns, in this instance CT for pulmonary embolism, as a diagnostic turning point in complex cardiac presentations.

Conclusions

This case shows an end-stage presentation of eosinophilic myocarditis and underscores the importance of using multimodality imaging as a tool to facilitate the diagnosis of this condition. Early recognition and targeted treatment are essential to prevent disease progression and improve outcomes.
