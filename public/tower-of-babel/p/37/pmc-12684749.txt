Cognitive and physical functions among Chinese community-dwelling older adults with motoric cognitive risk syndrome: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Junhong Wu, Xinyu Yao, Xing Wu, Yamei Bai, Yayi Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis prospective cohort study examined the change trajectories of cognitive and physical functions of individuals with motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome, as well as the longitudinal associations between MCR syndrome and changes in cognitive and physical functions, to provide a new perspective on preventing dementia.MethodsParticipants were selected from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Demographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle variables were assessed in 2011. MCR syndrome was defined as the presence of subjective cognitive complaints and objective slow gait, with preserved activities of daily living and absence of dementia, and assessed in 2011. Cognitive function, including orientation, attention and calculation, episodic memory, and visuospatial ability, was measured from 2011 to 2018. Physical function, including grip strength, balance ability, and repeated chair stand tests, was measured from 2011 to 2015. Generalized estimating equation was employed to analyze the longitudinal associations between MCR syndrome in 2011 and changes in cognitive functions over 7 years and physical functions over 4 years.ResultsAmong 4,217 participants, 475 had MCR syndrome in 2011. Both participants with MCR syndrome and those without exhibited a decline in both cognitive and physical function over 7 years and 4 years of follow-up, except for fluctuations in visuospatial ability. Non-MCR syndrome participants demonstrated significantly better overall cognitive function in 2018 compared to 2011 (Group × Time: B = 0.44, P = 0.035) than those in the MCR syndrome group. However, participants with non-MCR syndrome demonstrated significantly worse visuospatial ability in 2013 (Group × time: B = −0.44, P = 0.002) and 2018 (Group × time: B = −0.34, P = 0.016) compared to those in the MCR syndrome group. Non-MCR syndrome participants demonstrated significantly better performance in repeated chair stand tests in 2013 (Group × time: B = 0.31, P < 0.001) and 2015 (Group × time: B = 0.37, P < 0.001) compared to those in the MCR syndrome group in 2011.ConclusionsOlder adults with MCR syndrome experience worse overall cognitive and physical function performance, especially in repeated chair stand tests, than individuals without MCR syndrome over 7-year and 4-year follow-up periods. It is suggested that future interventional studies will target both physical and cognitive functions in MCR syndrome individuals, providing insights for the prevention of dementia.Keywords: Aged, Cognitive function, Cohort study, Community-dwelling, Motoric cognitive risk syndrome, Physical function

Introduction

Physical disability and cognitive impairment are detrimental to late-life health and quality of life. Both physical and cognitive function in older adults have been studied separately [[1], [2], [3]]. However, the bidirectional and inseparable associations between them are complex. The brain is the physical substrate of cognition, and physical health has a direct impact on cognition [4]. Meanwhile, cognition influences physical functions, as cognition is essential for executing and learning complex physical skills. Cognitive disorders with physical manifestations, and vice versa, such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, involve the physical degeneration of specific brain regions, leading to progressive cognitive decline (memory loss, impaired reasoning) and physical symptoms (tremors, gait problems) [5]. Thus, combining the physical and cognitive functions of older adults, such as in motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome, has shown enhanced performance in predicting a wide range of adverse outcomes, such as falls [6], disability [7,8], increased hospitalization [9], cognitive impairment [10,11], and mortality [12]. Thus, this topic has gained much attention in research.

MCR syndrome is defined as the presence of subjective cognitive complaints (SCCs) and objective slow gait, with preserved activities of daily living (ADLs) among people without dementia [13]. It is a newly developed pre-dementia syndrome that describes the state between normal aging and dementia [13,14]. Although SCCs and slow gait speed have been proven to be independent predictors of cognitive decline and dementia [15], older adults with MCR syndrome have a greater risk of developing dementia than those without [14,16], with a 31.9 % incidence of dementia from MCR syndrome over a 4-year follow-up in a United States population [11]. As a pre-dementia syndrome, MCR syndrome incorporates cognitive and physical functions and is easy to assess in a large population. Given the importance of MCR syndrome, understanding the association between MCR syndrome and various domains of cognitive and physical functions could help reveal altered pathways and provide targeted interventions during the long pre-dementia stage, thereby offering a new perspective for preventing dementia.

However, the associations between MCR syndrome and the domains of cognitive functions are inconsistent, and knowledge on the change trajectories of the cognitive and physical functions of individuals with MCR syndrome is lacking. Several cross-sectional studies revealed that MCR syndrome was associated with decreased global cognitive functioning [[16], [17], [18]], processing speed [19], attention and language domains [17], and executive functions [17,20]. However, Bortone et al. [19] found no significant association between MCR syndrome and decreased overall cognition in a population in Southern Italy. By comparison, the association between MCR syndrome and memory remains a topic of controversy [[19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, the associations between MCR syndrome and various domains of physical function have not been extensively studied [22]. Older adults with MCR syndrome are more likely to experience exhaustion and low muscle strength and physical activity than those without MCR syndrome [19], indicating that people with MCR syndrome may have multiple impairments in physical function. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prospective study has explored the trajectories of changes in cognitive and physical functions of individuals with MCR syndrome over time, or the longitudinal associations between MCR syndrome and changes in cognitive and physical functions. A prospective study could investigate how cognitive and physical functions evolve and identify causal pathways, thereby providing more robust evidence for future interventions aimed at preventing cognitive and physical decline among older adults.

Therefore, this study aimed to profile the cognitive and physical functions of participants with MCR syndrome at baseline, describe the trajectories of change in cognitive and physical functions among those with MCR syndrome, and analyze the longitudinal associations between MCR syndrome and changes in cognitive and physical functions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a prospective cohort survey design. The report of this study adheres to the STrengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [23]. The data used in this study originated from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and were collected in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018. CHARLS is a national observational cohort survey examining health and aging-related issues among middle-aged and older Chinese individuals (aged 45 and above) in 450 counties or villages. In the baseline survey in 2011, a total of 17,708 participants were interviewed face-to-face, and they were followed up every 2 years. The CHARLS sample was recruited through a multistage cluster probability sampling design. The detailed data collection process of CHARLS has been described elsewhere [24]. The CHARLS data are freely available on its website for nonprofit use. The inclusion criteria for this study were that participants aged 60 or above who completed the measures independently, could finish the gait speed test, had preserved ADLs, and did not have memory-related disease, including dementia. Participants with incomplete data that could not be determined for ADL, gait speed, and memory-related diseases were excluded.

Measures

Demographic and health characteristics

Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, and highest educational level achieved, were collected. Health status and lifestyle variables, including number of comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), depressive symptoms, self-reported health, the number of social activities, ADLs, and instrumental ADLs (IADLs), were assessed.

The number of comorbidities was measured by asking participants: “Have you been diagnosed with a chronic condition (including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer or malignant tumor, chronic lung diseases, liver disease, heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive diseases, emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems, memory-related disease, arthritis or rheumatism, asthma) by a doctor?” Height and body weight were measured by a vertical height bar and a weighing scale, respectively, with uniform standards. BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The 10-item Center for Epidemiological Survey Depression Scale measured depressive symptoms. The answers to each item were assigned values of 0, 1, 2, and 3, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 30. The total scores of 0–10, ≥10, and ≥15 were regarded as no depression, depression trend, and high risk of depression, respectively. Participants reported self-reported health through the question “Would you say your health is very good, good, fair, poor, or very poor?” Considering the limited answers of “very poor” and “very good” in this study, they were combined with “poor” and “good,” respectively. Social activities were measured by asking “Have you done any of these activities last month?” The activities included: 1) interacted with friends; 2) played Ma-jong, chess, cards, or went to a community club; 3) provided help to family, friends, or neighbors who do not live with you and who did not pay you for the help; 4) went to a sport, social, or other kinds of club; 5) took part in a community-related organization; 6) performed voluntary or charity work; 7) cared for a sick or disabled adult who does not live with you and who did not pay you for the help; 8) attended an educational or training course; 9) stock investment; 10) used the Internet; and 11) other. In this study, the number of participants’ self-reported activities was calculated. ADLs were assessed by asking participants if they had any difficulties dressing, bathing, eating, getting into or out of bed, using the toilet, or controlling urination and defecation. The answers to each item were “No, I dont have any difficulty,” “I have difficulty but can still do it,” “Yes, I have difficulty and need help,” and “I cannot do it.” Scores of 1, 2, 3, and 4 were given for each response above. Thus, the overall score for ADLs ranged from 6 to 24, with higher scores indicating worse ADLs. Disability was considered when the answers to one or more activities were “Yes, I have difficulty and need help” or “I cannot do it” [14]. IADLs were assessed by asking participants if they experienced difficulties with household chores, preparing hot meals, shopping for groceries, making phone calls, taking medications, or managing their finances. The answers and scores were the same as those for ADLs described above.

Motoric cognitive risk syndrome

MCR syndrome was defined as the presence of SCCs and objective slow gait, with preserved ADLs and absence of dementia [13]. SCCs were assessed by asking participants to rate their memory through the question, “How would you rate your memory at present? Would you say it is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” An answer of “fair” or “poor” was considered SCCs [25,26]. Gait speed was measured by asking participants to walk straight at their normal pace over a distance of 2.5 m in an open area twice. Gait speed was calculated as the average of two trials in meters per second. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of gait speed were calculated by sex and age. The cutoff speed for slow gait was defined as ≤ 1.0 SD above the age- and gender-appropriate mean values of gait speed established in the present cohort [25,26]. This cohort’s criteria for defining low gait speed were 0.41, 0.33, 0.41, and 0.29 m/s for men <75 years, men ≥75 years, women <75 years, and women ≥75 years, respectively. ADL were assessed as described above (2.2.1). Participants who reported a diagnosis of memory-related disease, including dementia, based on the question “Have you been diagnosed with memory-related disease by a doctor?” were disqualified from the diagnosis of MCR syndrome [27].

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed in the areas of orientation, attention and calculation, episodic memory, and visuospatial ability. Orientation was assessed by asking participants about their current year, month, day, day of the week, and season. Attention and calculation were evaluated five times with a test for serial subtraction of 7 from 100. Episodic memory was assessed using the word recall of 10 Chinese words, with the score representing the average number of words correctly recalled in both the immediate and delayed tests. Visuospatial ability was assessed by presenting participants with two overlapping pentagons and asking them to replicate the picture in their drawing. A correct answer for each test was given a score of 1; otherwise, it received a score of 0. The scores for orientation, attention and calculation, episodic memory, and visuospatial ability were 0–5, 0–5, 0–10, and 0–1, respectively, and the overall cognitive score ranged from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognitive ability [28].

Physical functions

Physical functions, including grip strength, balance ability, and repeated chair stand tests, were measured. Given that data on physical function, including grip strength, balance ability, and repeated chair stands, were not collected in 2018, those variables were analyzed up to 2015. Grip strength was tested using a mechanical dynamometer, and each hand was tested twice [24]. Grip strength was highest for the participants’ dominant hand (kg). Balance ability was measured through semi-tandem, full-tandem, and side-by-side stands. If the participant could complete the semi-tandem test for 10 s without stepping out of place or grabbing hold of anything, they were allowed to stand in complete tandem. A 30-s full tandem test was administered to participants aged 70 or older, whereas a 60-s full tandem test was administered to those younger than 70. Otherwise, a side-by-side stand was implemented. If the participant could complete the semi-tandem test for the full 10 s and stand for 0–2, 3–9, or ≥10 s, then scores of 2–4 were assigned, respectively. If the participant could not complete the semi-tandem for the full 10 s but could complete the side-by-side stand, a score of 1 was given; otherwise, a score of 0 was given [29]. In the repeated chair stand tests, participants were asked to stand up straight from the chair and then sit down at their usual pace five times without stopping in between and without using their arms to push off. The time in seconds was recorded. Participants were unable to finish or took ≥16.7, 13.7–16.7 (excluding 16.7), 11.1–13.7 (excluding 13.7), and <11.1 s, scores of 4, 3, 2, 1, and 0 were given, respectively [29]. The higher score means worse performance. Before administering all tests, the researcher demonstrated the measurement and ensured that the participants could perform the test and understood the instructions, agreeing to participate.

Data analysis

SPSS (version 25.0) for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze data. Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as the mean and SD, and if the normal distribution is not presented, the median and quartiles are used to describe them. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. Independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables with and without normal distribution, and chi-square tests for categorical variables were used to examine differences in participants’ characteristics and the cognitive and physical functions between participants with and without MCR syndrome at baseline. The generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was employed to assess longitudinal changes in cognitive function and four domains between participants with MCR syndrome and non-MCR syndrome in 2013, 2015, and 2018, compared to 2011. Additionally, physical functions were assessed in 2013 and 2015, compared to 2011. Participants’ characteristics, which were statistically different between MCR syndrome and non-MCR syndrome at baseline, were adjusted as covariates in the models. Multiple imputation was employed to address missing data. All P-values in this study were two-tailed, with a significance of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Ethical approval was not required in this study as the data were public.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Among the 17,708 participants surveyed in 2011, 11,979 were excluded because of incomplete gait speed tests. Then, another 1,512 participants were excluded due to memory-related disease (n = 129), impaired ADL (n = 329), age younger than 60 (n = 20), and incomplete data that could not be determined for ADL, gait speed, and memory-related diseases (n = 1,034). A total of 4,217 participants were included in this study. Among them, 475 had MCR syndrome, accounting for 11.3 % of the total in 2011. Participants with MCR syndrome were significantly more likely to be female, unmarried, illiterate, have poor self-rated health, engage in fewer social activities (doing two activities last month compared to one and zero), and have a worse ability in ADLs than those without MCR syndrome (P < 0.05) at baseline. However, their IADLs were slightly better than those without MCR syndrome (P = 0.002). No significant differences were detected in other characteristics between the two groups (Appendix A).

Cognitive and physical functions at baseline and changes over a 7- and 4-year follow-up

At baseline, the participants without MCR syndrome performed better than those with MCR syndrome in global cognitive function and the domains of orientation, attention and calculation, and episodic memory (P < 0.01, Appendix A). However, both groups had similar visuospatial ability performance (P = 0.690, Appendix A). Regarding physical function, the participants without MCR syndrome performed better on gait speed, balance ability, and repeated chair stand tests (P < 0.001, Appendix A). Overall, both groups exhibited a decline in both cognitive and physical function over a 7- and 4-year follow-up, except for fluctuations in visuospatial ability. The non-MCR syndrome group consistently performed better than the MCR syndrome group (Appendix B).

Longitudinal associations between motoric cognitive risk and cognitive and physical functions

Table 1 compares the cognitive and physical functions between groups over a 4-year and 7-year period using the GEE model, all adjusted for sex, marital status, highest educational level achieved, self-reported health, social activities, ADLs, and IADLs. For cognitive function, participants with non-MCR syndrome demonstrated significantly better global cognitive function in 2018 compared to 2011 (Group × time: B = 0.44, P = 0.035), when compared to participants in the MCR syndrome group. However, the non-MCR syndrome group demonstrated significantly worse visuospatial ability at both 2013 (Group × time: B = −0.44, P = 0.002) and 2018 (Group × time: B = −0.34, P = 0.016) compared to the 2011 MCR syndrome group. For physical function, the non-MCR syndrome group demonstrated significantly better performance in repeated chair stand tests in 2013 (Group × time: B = 0.31, P < 0.001) and 2015 (Group × time: B = 0.37, P < 0.001) compared to the 2011 baseline, when compared to participants in the MCR syndrome group. Other functions were not significantly different at all time points between groups (P > 0.05). The group effect of all cognitive functions and physical functions was significant (P < 0.05), except for orientation (P = 0.590). The time effect of all cognitive and physical functions was significant (P < 0.05), except for visuospatial ability in 2013 (P = 0.609) and 2018 (P = 0.078), and balance ability in 2013 (P = 0.636) compared to 2011.

Discussion

In baseline analysis, participants with MCR syndrome were significantly more likely to be female, unmarried, illiterate, have poor self-rated health, and engage in fewer social activities. Participants with MCR syndrome were substantially more likely to be female, which may be because women face a higher lifetime risk of cognitive decline due to a combination of biological factors and psychosocial stressors [30]. A recent study highlighted that social frailty, characterized by a depletion of social resources and activities, is a significant and independent risk factor for MCR syndrome [31]. This finding helps to contextualize several of the observed baseline disparities. The tendency for MCR syndrome participants to be unmarried, engage in fewer social activities, and have poor self-rated health can all be interpreted as manifestations of this underlying social frailty. Socially isolated individuals may have less cognitive stimulation and social support, which are known protective factors against cognitive decline [32], potentially accelerating the progression from subjective complaints to MCR syndrome. Fewer social activities among MCR syndrome participants further support the “social cognitive reserve” hypothesis [33]. Consistent social engagement (e.g., community participation, interpersonal interactions) maintains synaptic plasticity and reduces amyloid-beta accumulation—pathological hallmarks of cognitive impairment. In contrast, social withdrawal among MCR syndrome participants may create a vicious cycle: cognitive deficits limit participation in activities, which in turn accelerates further cognitive decline.

An intriguing finding is the slightly better IADLs among MCR syndrome participants compared to non-MCR syndrome counterparts, which appears to contradict the typical expectation that cognitive decline correlates with functional impairment. This discrepancy may be explained by the “compensation hypothesis” [34,35]. Individuals in the MCR syndrome stage (mild memory deficits without severe executive dysfunction) may develop adaptive strategies to maintain IADLs (e.g., using calendars for medication management, relying on routine for meal preparation). Additionally, non-MCR syndrome groups may include individuals with physical comorbidities that impair IADLs independently. Future analyses should adjust for physical health status to clarify this relationship.

In terms of cognitive function, participants with non-MCR syndrome demonstrated significantly better global cognitive function in 2018 compared to 2011, whereas those in the MCR syndrome group showed no improvement. Cross-sectional studies have also confirmed the association of MCR syndrome with decreased global cognitive function [[16], [17], [18]]. This suggests neuroplastic resilience in this subgroup. This aligns with the cognitive reserve hypothesis, which posits that preserved motor-cognitive integration may buffer against neurodegeneration [30,35]. SCC characterizes participants with MCR syndrome, while the relationship between SCCs and future decline in cognitive function is widely recognized [1], which provides strong evidence for the association between MCR syndrome and cognitive function. A systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies, which synthesized data from 15 cohorts, confirmed that individuals with MCR syndrome are at a substantially elevated risk of progressing to dementia and cognitive impairment [36]. Therefore, the steeper decline observed in the current study is not merely a statistical difference but likely reflects the progression toward these clinically defined endpoints. The meta-analysis further indicates that the prognostic value of MCR syndrome extends beyond cognition to include a higher risk of mortality and falls, underscoring the syndrome’s role as a marker of broad physiological decline.

From a clinical perspective, the consistent performance gap between the MCR syndrome and non-MCR syndrome groups highlights the critical importance of early identification of MCR syndrome. Simple, inexpensive, and non-invasive criteria define the syndrome. Integrating this assessment into routine evaluations for older adults with memory concerns could be an efficient strategy for identifying those at the highest risk for functional decline and dementia, especially in settings with limited access to advanced diagnostic tools. Early detection, combined with the cognitive resilience of the non-MCR syndrome group, suggests a window of opportunity for targeted early interventions. Traditionally, interventions for individuals with SCC or mild cognitive impairment have primarily focused on cognitive aspects. MCR syndrome considers both cognitive and physical functions, offering a new direction for future interventions in cognitive functions, such as multicomponent programs that integrate physical training (e.g., tai chi) and cognitive exercises, as supported by motor-cognitive interaction theories. This approach may enhance the effectiveness of cognitive interventions and help prevent the onset of dementia.

However, the association between cognitive domains and MCR syndrome yielded inconsistent results compared to previous studies. A non-significant difference in visuospatial ability was found between participants with and without MCR syndrome by Cheng et al. [37]. In contrast, the current study found that participants with non-MCR syndrome demonstrated significantly worse visuospatial abilities in both 2013 and 2018 compared to those in the MCR syndrome group. Visuospatial domains may be late vulnerability markers of global decline, or compensatory mechanisms in other cognitive domains may mask specific deficits. Attention domains [17], episodic memory [38], and executive functions [17,20,38] are associated with MCR syndrome in cross-sectional studies, whereas these associations disappear in the current longitudinal cohort. Among participants with SCCs, episodic memory impairment was frequently reported [39]. A survey by Zuroff and colleagues revealed that partner-SCCs and self-reported SCCs were negatively associated with delayed recall performance in participants with mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease, and higher self-reported SCCs in participants with normal cognition and higher partner-reported SCCs in participants with Alzheimer’s disease were associated with worse immediate recall and delayed recall performance [2]. Future studies are needed to confirm the associations between cognitive domains and MCR syndrome.

Regarding physical function, MCR syndrome participants demonstrated significantly worse performance in repeated chair stand tests in 2013 and 2015 compared to those in the non-MCR syndrome group in 2011. Studies have shown that MCR syndrome was related to increased exhaustion [19], low muscle strength [19], low physical activity [19,40], and sarcopenia [40]. These factors contribute to poor performance on repeated chair stand tests. The repeated chair stand tests assess lower limb muscle strength, dynamic balance, and motor control capacity. This finding may be explained by the impaired lower limb strength of the participants with MCR syndrome, as older adults with slow gait and impaired performance on repeated chair stand tests typically exhibit low limb strength. This finding was also supported by the results of the timed up and test, which were significantly lower in the MCR syndrome group than in the non-MCR syndrome group [37]. However, the balance ability did not show a significant longitudinal association with MCR syndrome, which may be attributed to the compensatory contributions of other functional domains, such as motor control capacity, for independent balance function during repeated chair stand tests [41]. This suggests that interventions targeting multidimensional physical functions (such as repeated chair stand tests) may yield greater benefits than those focused on a single function (such as balance ability), making them more effective in preventing the occurrence of MCR syndrome.

In this study, participants with MCR syndrome had worse grip strength than those without; however, this association was not confirmed in the longitudinal regression models. However, a previous study indicated that grip strength, weakness, and asymmetry were longitudinally associated with increased odds of incident MCR syndrome [3]. The inconsistency may be due to their research grouping, which only includes participants with a decline in grip strength. In contrast, our study treated grip strength as a continuous variable encompassing all strength levels. This suggests that the association with MCR syndrome may be specific to diminished grip strength. This is evidenced by sarcopenia and frailty, which are associated with a decline in grip strength and are also linked to MCR syndrome [38]. Thus, more evidence is needed to confirm the longitudinal relationships between the multidimensional characteristics of grip strength and the risk of incident MCR syndrome in future studies.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, participants who were unable to complete the gait speed measurements were excluded from this study. Thus, selection bias may exist, and the results may not be generalized to older adults with weaker limbs or those who are unable to walk. Second, the 7- or 4-year follow-up periods may not have been sufficiently long to observe the longitudinal associations between MCR syndrome and changes in cognitive and physical functions, as changes in cognitive function are a slow and individually varying process. The onset of dementia often has a preclinical phase that spans several decades, resulting in the detection of its effects taking longer.

Conclusions

Over the 7- and 4-year follow-ups, both participants in the MCR syndrome and non-MCR syndrome groups experienced a decrease in cognitive and physical function; however, the non-MCR syndrome group consistently performed better than the MCR syndrome group. After adjusting for participants’ characteristics, non-MCR syndrome participants demonstrated significantly better global cognitive function in 2018 and better performance in repeated chair stand tests in 2013 and 2015 than in 2011, compared to those in the MCR syndrome group. The results offer new insights into preventing cognitive decline by focusing on the simultaneous development of cognitive and physical strategies to prevent MCR syndrome. Meanwhile, regular MCR syndrome assessments for individuals aged 60 years or older may facilitate early detection of those with decreased cognitive function.
