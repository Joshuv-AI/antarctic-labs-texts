Rational antibiotic prescribing practices among dentists in Turkey: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Pelin Ozmen, Alime Okkesim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrrational antibiotic use is one of the most important reasons for the rise of antibiotic resistance worldwide. This study questioned how dentists comply with rational antibiotic use (RAU) principles when prescribing antibiotics, their inadequacy, and the need for training. The study included 324 dentists working with patients from public, private, and university settings in Turkey. An online survey was conducted to evaluate demographic information, approaches, and attitudes toward antibiotic prescribing. 64% of the participants were female dentists. 50.6% of all participants are dentists working in private clinics, and the rate of those with less than five years of professional experience is 48.5%. While the participants mainly prescribe antibiotics for infection treatment (89.2%), the rate of those who inform their patients about the maximum daily dose is 91.7%. Participants reported that they had a lack of knowledge about drug interactions (76.2%) and pharmacological properties (44.4%), and it was determined that these participants wanted to receive training on RAU. The rate of participants who wish to receive RAU training is 86.1%. It would be beneficial for dentists to update their training in rational antibiotic prescribing in order to monitor antimicrobial resistance patterns and to obtain the necessary information about contraindications and disinfection protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07013-y.Keywords: Rational antibiotic use, Dental prescribing practices, Pharmacological knowledge

Introduction

Although the introduction of antibiotics into our lives dates back to the 17th century, when research was conducted on molds producing substances that suppressed the growth of other microorganisms, their use gained momentum in the second half of the 19th century, the age of advancement in microbiology [1]. Antibiotics have revolutionized healthcare by saving lives and preventing serious health complications, thereby saving millions of lives and providing critical advances in many medical fields [2]. However, their excessive use has serious adverse effects on the health of people and communities. Antibiotics can cause adverse drug reactions, ranging from minor and reversible to severely debilitating or fatal. Antibiotics can disrupt a person’s healthy flora, causing opportunistic bacterial infections from Clostridioides difficile [3]. This condition can be life-threatening. Of most significant concern are antibiotic-resistant bacteria because they cause worse clinical outcomes and a higher risk of death in patients with such infections. Due to the overuse of antibiotics, resistant microbes have entered the public’s radar as a serious threat to public health. As a result, the World Health Organization approved a global action plan to combat this problem in May 2015, focusing specifically on antibiotic resistance [4].

According to current international guidelines (American Dental Association (ADA), American Association of Endodontists (AAE), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), European Society of Endodontology (ESE), and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP)), appropriate indications for antibiotic use include severe odontogenic infections with systemic involvement (such as cellulitis, diffuse swelling, fever, malaise), spreading infections, and specific prophylactic scenarios such as dental procedures in patients with a high risk of infective endocarditis or other serious systemic complications [1–4].

Antibiotics are the most commonly used drugs in daily dental practice. Antibiotics are pharmacological agents that can destroy pathogenic microorganisms or prevent their growth or proliferation without causing significant harm to the human body and the host organism [5]. Although the clinical use of antibiotics began as compounds isolated from natural microorganism products, the fight against infections has accelerated with the development of semi-synthetic and synthetic antibiotics [6]. Among these, lactams, macrolides, lincosamides, nitroimidazoles, and tetracyclines are the most widely used classes of antibiotics and most suitable for dental problems [7].

Antibiotics are widely prescribed in dentistry for the treatment of acute and chronic infections, both odontogenic and non-odontogenic, prophylactic in high-risk patients (e.g., those with endocarditis or congenital heart disease), or for the treatment of focal, systemic, and surgical site infections in patients requiring oral surgery.Antibiotic prescribing in dentistry, whether for prophylactic or therapeutic purposes, accounts for approximately 10% of primary care antibiotic prescriptions worldwide and is not always considered appropriate, leading to excessive or incorrect use of antibiotics in dental practice [8, 9]. In turn, dental patients may not correctly adhere to prescribed antimicrobial therapy, exacerbating the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in dentistry [10].

The inevitable effect of irrational antibiotic use is the development of microbiome imbalance, or in other words, intestinal and oral dysbiosis [11]. Longitudinal studies of antibiotic use have shown that antibiotics’ short and long-term effects on the diversity and composition of the gut microbiota are profound. Many recent studies on dysbiosis have shown that dysbiosis plays a severe role in the development of metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases. Accordingly, the unnecessary and incorrect use of antibiotics forms the basis for this situation [12, 13].

This study aims to evaluate dentists’ attitudes and behaviors regarding antibiotic prescribing and their need for RAU training in Turkey.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by the Nevsehir Hacı Bektas Veli University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Publication Ethics Board (2024.02/02) and followed the guidelines in the Declaration of Helsinki for human studies.

The survey questions prepared in our study were sent online (Google Docs™) to dentists working in private, public and university institutions between March 2024 and June 2024. Dentists who were actively caring for patients in private, public or dental faculties between the ages of 23–65 were included in the study; intern dentists and those who did not complete the survey form were excluded from the study. The survey was developed based on previously validated instruments used in studies with necessary modifications made to suit the national context and scope [14–16].

The participants indicated their reasons for antibiotic prescribing via a structured questionnaire. The term “appropriate indication” or “correct indication,” as used in the survey, referred specifically to scenarios supported by current internationally recognized guidelines (ADA, AAE, NICE). Thus, prescriptions were considered appropriate if they were associated with clinical situations explicitly recommended for antibiotic treatment, such as severe spreading infections, systemic involvement (fever, lymphadenopathy, malaise), or guideline-endorsed prophylaxis indications (high-risk cardiac conditions, compromised immune system).

Prior to full distribution, the questionnaire underwent a pilot application with 20 participants to assess its clarity and reliability. Based on the responses, minor revisions were made. Since the survey could not be completed without the dentists answering all the questions, the surveys reached us in complete form. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All questions were mandatory to ensure complete datasets. The online survey link was distributed via professional email lists and social media channels frequented by dentists. The survey was anonymous, and no personal data was collected from participants. The questions in the survey were adapted from previous studies to suit the purpose of our research. In the first part of the survey, information is collected on participants’ age, gender, current workplace, and work experience. In the second part, participants’ indications for prescribing antibiotics, their consultation status, their knowledge about prophylaxis, their understanding of the drug when prescribing antibiotics, which antibiotics they prescribe most, their level of knowledge about the antibiotics they prescribe, informing patients and how competent they feel about the rational use of antibiotics. Last part is the section where requests for education are evaluated. Participants answered the questions in the survey by selecting multiple options or by selecting one of the options rated on a Likert scale.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this descriptive cross-sectional survey was determined based on the objective of estimating the prevalence of specific antibiotic prescribing practices among dentists with a pre-defined level of precision. The calculation was performed using the standard formula for estimating a population proportion. Assuming a conservative estimated prevalence of 50% (p = 0.5) to yield the maximum required sample size, a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a desired margin of error of ± 5.5% (E = 0.055), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 318 participants. The study successfully recruited 324 dentists, thus satisfying the sample size requirement and ensuring that the study’s findings have a high degree of precision.

Discussion

Due to the excessive misuse of antibiotics, antibiotic resistance has become a global problem [17]– [18]. Our study aims to evaluate the antibiotic-prescribing behavior of dentists in Turkey and their knowledge and attitudes about RAU. The data of all participants were examined, and dentists’ tendency to prescribe antibiotics, factors affecting prescribing, and lack of knowledge were revealed.

Prescribing antibiotics as the first choice in cases of infection encountered in dentistry is common among dentists worldwide [2, 7, 8, 19]. A study evaluated dentists’ knowledge about antibiotic prophylaxis for infective endocarditis and concluded that the idea that antibiotics are not harmful is one reason for overprescription [20].

In 1985, the World Health Organization defined the rational use of medicines as “patients taking medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses appropriate to their individual needs, for a sufficient period, and at the lowest cost for both themselves and society” [21]. In dentistry education, the necessity of not prescribing antibiotics for all infections occurring in the oral area and the importance of focusing on treatment, especially in localized infections, are emphasized [14, 22, 23]. During dentistry education, students try to acquire these attitudes through both pharmacology courses and clinical practices.

In current study, the rate of participants prescribing antibiotics before surgical intervention was about half of the participants. This rate shows that dentists tend to prescribe antibiotics for infection prevention in surgical procedures. However, unless the patient has a systemic disease that requires prophylaxis, there are very few situations in which antibiotics are needed [24]. There was no significant difference between the rates of antibiotic prescribing before surgical intervention and the reasons for prescribing, such as “presence of correct indication” and “patient request” (p >0.05). The rate of participants prescribing antibiotics for endodontic reasons was very high. While there are very few cases in the literature where antibiotics are recommended for endodontic reasons, studies have shown that antibiotics for endodontic purposes that do not comply with the guidelines are prescribed [10, 19, 25]. This situation shows us that dentists need more evidence-based clinical indications for prescribing antibiotics and lack of knowledge.

Several factors influence dentists’ decision-making process when prescribing antibiotics. These are influenced by a combination of patient-related factors, clinical guidelines, time and resource constraints, and their beliefs and knowledge [8, 9, 26]. Although dentists make decisions based more on clinical findings when prescribing antibiotics in the presence of an appropriate indication, it cannot be ignored that they prescribe antibiotics upon patient request at significant rates or when there is no time for intervention. However, results from previous studies have reported that 30% to 50% of prescribed antibiotics are either unnecessary or not prescribed appropriately [25]. In the survey conducted by Koyuncuoğlu et al. in our country in 2017, it was stated that approximately 75% of the prescriptions written in the Turkish population were prescribed without a correct indication [27]. The results obtained from studies in which researchers evaluated physicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding antibiotic use have shown that patient demands and expectations, service providers’ perceptions of patient expectations, fear of litigation, and diagnostic uncertainty are essential factors that lead to overprescribing [10, 28]. This is consistent with our findings in the literature showing that patient expectations and time constraints contribute to inappropriate antibiotic use. Therefore, the number of dentists who select the options at the patients’ request or under clinical time pressure is considerable. According to a systematic review conducted in England, factors such as clinical time pressure, patient expectations, and patients’ inability or unwillingness to accept surgical treatments contributed to the inappropriate prescription of antibiotics [24]. Compared to the results of the previous study conducted in Turkey, although it is seen that there has been an improvement in antibiotic prescribing since the survey, this is not a sufficient improvement. It will be possible to turn the current situation in a positive direction by further improving the competence of pharmacology courses regarding rational drug use during dentistry education and reinforcing this situation with the clinic.

The studies have shown that prophylactic antibiotic use is more common in patients whose systemic health may be negatively affected, such as cardiac reasons, and more antibiotic prophylaxis is performed in invasive procedures [22, 29–31]. In this study, when examining the participants’ attitudes toward prescribing antibiotics for procedures in patients with systemic diseases requiring antibiotic prophylaxis, it was found that antibiotic prophylaxis was most commonly used during invasive procedures such as apical resection and tooth extraction. However, antibiotic use appears to be low during simple procedures such as superficial caries treatment and loss of primary teeth. These results indicate that dentists accurately assessed the need for prophylaxis by procedure type. When the attitudes of dentists are examined in the literature, it is seen that antibiotics are not prescribed for noninvasive procedures such as intraoral radiography, placement of bandless orthodontic appliances, deciduous tooth loss, and fluoride application [32–34]. Dentists have stated differences in their decisions regarding endodontic treatment, tooth preparation with oral impressions, and Class II restorations [32–34].

In this study, most of the participants stated that they always inform their patients when prescribing antibiotics. According to the results of other studies in the literature, it has been stated that it is vital to increase knowledge and awareness about the appropriate use of antibiotics and the risks of antibiotic resistance [10, 35]. The most frequently informed topics include daily dose and duration of treatment. On the other hand, it is noteworthy that the rate of information on critical issues, such as possible side effects of antibiotics and drug-food interactions is low. These deficiencies may pose significant risks to the safety of antibiotic use. It is essential to emphasize that dentists who prescribe antibiotics should provide more comprehensive information, particularly regarding potential side effects and drug interactions. Informing patients can increase compliance with prescriptions and help prevent antimicrobial resistance.

This study found that nearly half of dentists felt inadequate about pharmacological properties, drug interactions, the side effects of antibiotics, and contraindications. Additionally, it was found that dentists who perceived these deficiencies were more likely to desire further training on RAU (p < 0.01). Most of the participants stated they would like to receive more education on RAU. This shows the willingness of dentists to improve themselves in the use of antibiotics. In particular, a lack of pharmacological knowledge can negatively affect dentists’ antibiotic prescribing processes and contribute to the spread of antibiotic resistance. Pharmacology and antibiotic use training needs to be more comprehensive and continuous in this context. Due to the aging society, dentists may treat elderly patients who take an average of two to nine medications daily. This polypharmacy leads to a high risk of adverse drug reactions, especially drug interactions [36].

When asked what sources the participants use when prescribing antibiotics, they say they mostly get information by searching on the internet, colleagues, and diagnosis and treatment guides. They stated that they used it at least in promotional activities of pharmaceutical companies. According to a survey conducted by Pisarnturakit et al. on Thai dentists, dentists addressed their lack of knowledge through mobile applications in [21]. In their study, D’Ambrosio et al. found that very few people consulted the guidelines when prescribing antibiotics [37]. In the study by Al-Taani et al., more than half of the participants stated that they wanted to receive training on antibiotic use [33]. It emphasizes the central role of further proficiency in pharmacological education provided in dental faculties and its application in the clinic, particularly in rational antibiotic prescribing by dentists. However, the rates of receiving training on RAU among participants varied according to the study site (p < 0,05). While dentists working in the public sector are subject to more planned and periodic training with the coordination of in-service training units, a dentist running a private practice receives extra training only with his/her own consent and if there is a concern that it will contribute to his/her academic development. For this reason, it is inevitable that dentists with different work places will have different needs and knowledge levels on subjects open to education such as RAU. Although public sector dentists receive more structured training, the findings suggest that additional training on rational antibiotic use is needed across all practice groups.

The RAU is essential for providing effective and safe treatment, as well as preventing microbial resistance. When comparing antimicrobial prescription rates in the literature, it was reported that amoxicillin was preferred first in the USA [38], Germany [39], Canada [40], Australia [14], Scotland [41], and Kuwait [42]. These results are also consistent with our study. A study conducted in Japan found that Japanese dentists primarily prescribed antibiotics from the cephalosporin group [19]. Ono et al. stated in their research that this result was due to pharmaceutical companies’ excessive promotion of Cephalosporins [19].

A previous study conducted in Turkey showed that the combination of amoxicillin and beta-lactamase inhibitors was widely preferred [27]. In this study, dentists stated that they prescribe amoxicillin first and amoxicillin + beta-lactamase inhibitors, which should be preferred less because of their side effects in odontogenic infections, second. This finding is similar to the preferences of dentists in Belgium and Spain, in contrast to the lower prescribing rates of amoxicillin + enzyme inhibitors in Norway and Wales. Clindamycin is a preferred prophylactic antibiotic for patients with penicillin allergy [22, 43, 44]. However, dentists have also prescribed alternative antibiotics such as azithromycin, clarithromycin, and erythromycin for these patients [22, 43].

In our study, clindamycin was the third most frequently preferred antibiotic by dentists. although there is no systematic data on penicillin allergy in Turkey, a prevalence of only 8–12% has been reported depending on the population. The fact that clindamycin prescriptions are much higher than this rate suggests that physicians often choose this drug cautiously without a confirmed allergy. In fact, similar situations have been found in surveys conducted in the UK and the US where physicians turn to clindamycin due to legal concerns or insufficient allergy verification. However, clindamycin is not the first choice in routine orofacial infection treatment and its unnecessary use carries serious risks such as Clostridioides difficile infection [45]. Therefore, the fact that clindamycin use rates are high in our country compared to other countries (e.g., 0.5% in the UK and 9.5% in Sweden, 6,6% in Belgium) indicates that there may be a gap in rational antibiotic education [46, 47].

No significant difference was found when the time spent in the profession was compared with the status of receiving training on RAU. A study by Baskaradoss et al. showed that dentists with higher educational qualifications were more sensitive about prescribing antibiotics [48]. In a survey study by Teoh et al., it was observed that antibiotics were prescribed more frequently in accordance with guidelines in the first five years after graduation than in later periods [14]. In this study, the relationship between antibiotic prescription and the study year was not significant.

When considering the use of systemic antibiotics, the real question is not which antibiotic to choose, but whether any antibiotics should be prescribed. When prescribing systemic antibiotics, factors such as pharmacological properties of the drug, dose, duration of use, host-related properties, patient compliance (compliance with the dentist’s instructions regarding antibiotic use and oral hygiene practices), causative bacteria and side effects (side effects of antibiotics, antibacterial resistance and postoperative Many issues such as infection risk) should be taken into consideration. Encouraging RAU in dentistry will improve the quality of dental care and reduce bacterial resistance [45]. The lack of a robust evidence base to inform clinical guidelines for the selection of antibiotics in odontogenic infections affects appropriate prescribing practices and contributes to antimicrobial resistance [14].

Antibiotics should be prescribed with great care. Clinicians should consider the potential impact of antibiotic prescribing choices on both the community and individual patients, as there are risks to both. With each use of antibiotics, the risk of developing a subsequent antibiotic-resistant infection increases, both in the patient receiving the antibiotic and in those in contact with the patient in the community. We are moving into a period where antibiotics to be used on resistant bacteria are decreasing. Infections that can be quickly resolved with antibiotic treatment require many qualified therapies. This situation impacts the healthcare system in terms of economic and labor usage. Antibiotic use also disrupts the microbiome, potentially leading to other long-term consequences such as asthma and obesity [49]. It is a fact that unconscious and overuse of antibiotics brings antibiotic resistance. Using antibiotics instead of primary treatment (e.g. drainage or surgery) increases the risk of infection progression and complications. Sometimes antibiotics prescribed to calm a persistent patient lead to the development of superbugs that are resistant to all antibiotics [6, 8]. It is known that unnecessary antibiotic use causes many problems, from allergic reactions to negative effects on liver and kidney functions, and is especially responsible for the shift of healthy microbiota to dysbiosis. Disruption of the oral microbiome balance can lead to periodontal diseases and even neurodegenerative diseases in the longer term [50].

We recommend the implementation of structured continuing education programs and antibiotic stewardship initiatives for dentists, including regular workshops and updates focused on rational antibiotic use. Furthermore, multifaceted strategies—such as audit and feedback mechanisms, clinical decision support systems, and adherence-promoting guideline reminders—may be required to achieve meaningful improvements in prescribing behavior. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of accessible, practical, and case-based educational interventions tailored to the needs of dental professionals. Additionally, system-level reforms that alleviate clinical workload and facilitate evidence-based decision-making may contribute to reducing inappropriate antibiotic prescribing in dental practice.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have overestimated their adherence to rational prescribing practices or underestimated their knowledge deficiencies. For this reason, quantitative data at the education level are not available, making standardization difficult. The fact that the majority of participants in the sample were physicians in their first five years of their careers and that a lack of homogeneous age distribution also contributes to the study’s findings. Issues such as training needs, appropriate indication selection, and consultation may be evaluated differently in younger age groups.

Second, although the sample included dentists from private, public, and academic institutions, it may not fully represent all practicing dentists in Turkey, particularly those in rural or underserved regions. The convenience sampling approach and online survey distribution may have introduced selection bias, favoring more digitally engaged or academically inclined respondents.

Third, the cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of prescribing behaviors but does not allow for causal inferences or changes over time. Longitudinal studies and audit-based research could provide deeper insights into the effectiveness of interventions aiming to improve RAU. In this study, the lack of in-depth answers to some questions may have led to more superficial results. For example, expanding the heading of endodontic reasons into subheadings in the reasons for antibiotic prescribing could have revealed new results that reveal inappropriate prescribing.

Finally, the study did not collect data on actual prescription records, limiting the ability to compare self-reported behaviors with objective practice data. Future research could incorporate prescription databases or patient chart reviews to validate findings.

To ensure the correct use of antibiotics, slow down the development of resistance to antibiotics, and prevent unnecessary use of antibiotics, making limited antibiotic sensitivity notifications can shift the clinician’s antibiotic preferences from broad-spectrum antibiotics, where multiple resistance development is observed, to antibiotics with a narrower spectrum, less cost, and where the development of resistance is seen at a low level. With these notification reports, classification tables are created based on the antibiotic resistance profiles of each country, taking into account the patient’s condition, the severity of the infection, and its location. They are evaluated according to the threshold values ​​within the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) standards framework. Such practices, which are thought to limit antibiotic resistance, can somewhat mitigate the harms of irrational drug applications.

Dental curricula should focus on lifelong learning, such as investigating possible drug interactions through a reliable scientific database. In addition to dental school curricula, various approaches to continuing education, such as online learning, workshops, and symposia, can help clinicians update their knowledge and promote rational drug use.

Antibiotic resistance, a global problem, can be controlled by continuously monitoring the changing resistance profiles of microorganisms. To this end, it is essential that dentists receive up-to-date training on rational drug use and continue planning and activities to positively change their antibacterial prescribing habits.
