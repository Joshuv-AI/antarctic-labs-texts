Association between remnant cholesterol inflammation index and erectile dysfunction among U.S. adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Heyi Chen, Sujun Jia, Liangliang Zheng, Bo Wu, Linjin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundC-reactive protein (CRP) and remnant cholesterol (RC) are two indicators that are included in the remnant cholesterol inflammation index (RCII). The purpose of this study is to assess the relationship between RCII and the likelihood of erectile dysfunction (ED).MethodsA total of 1,806 participants from the 2001–2002 and 2003–2004 survey cycles of the United States (U.S.) National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were included in the analysis. The self-reported questionnaire data from NHANES participants served as the basis for the ED diagnosis in this investigation. To increase the normality of the data distribution and lessen the influence of extreme values on the study outcomes, the RCII was log-transformed before statistical modeling. RCII was natural log-transformed [ln (RCII)] to improve distributional normality for regression analyses. In order to thoroughly assess the relationship between RCII and ED, this study used subgroup analysis, smooth curve fitting, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve study, and logistic regression models.ResultsElevated ln (RCII) levels were substantially positively correlated with an increased risk of ED after multivariable adjustment [odds ratio (OR) =1.13; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–1.27]. The greatest tertile of ln (RCII) (T3) was substantially linked to a higher risk of ED when compared to the lowest tertile (T1) (OR =1.62; 95% CI: 1.12–2.35). Elevated levels of ln (RCII) were positively connected with an increased chance of developing ED, according to smooth curve fitting research. Subgroup analysis also showed that people over 50 years, people aged 30–39 years, those with higher body mass index (BMI), non-smokers, and those with higher levels of education had a stronger correlation between RCII and the risk of ED. The area under the curve (AUC) of RCII was significantly greater than that of the other indicators, according to ROC curve performance analysis.ConclusionsThe study’s findings show that higher RCII levels are strongly linked to a higher risk of ED and imply that they could be used as a stand-alone predictor of ED risk.Keywords: Cross-sectional study, erectile dysfunction (ED), C-reactive protein (CRP), remnant cholesterol (RC), National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Higher remnant cholesterol inflammation index (RCII) levels were independently associated with an increased risk of erectile dysfunction (ED) among U.S. adults.

• Participants in the highest tertile of ln (RCII) had a significantly higher prevalence of ED compared with those in the lowest tertile, even after full multivariable adjustment.

• Smooth curve fitting analysis demonstrated a positive and approximately linear relationship between ln (RCII) and ED risk.

• The association between RCII and ED was more pronounced in men aged ≥50 years, those with higher body mass index, non-smokers, and individuals with higher educational attainment.

What is known and what is new?

• ED is closely associated with dyslipidemia and chronic systemic inflammation, and both remnant cholesterol and C-reactive protein have been individually linked to cardiovascular and metabolic disorders related to ED.

• This study demonstrates for the first time that RCII, a composite marker integrating lipid metabolism and inflammation, is independently and positively associated with ED risk in U.S. adults.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• RCII may serve as a practical and integrative biomarker for identifying individuals at high risk of ED, enabling earlier risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies in clinical practice.

• Assessment of RCII could be incorporated into routine metabolic and inflammatory evaluations, highlighting the need to address both lipid abnormalities and systemic inflammation in the prevention and management of ED.

Introduction

Men’s quality of life and mental health are greatly impacted by erectile dysfunction (ED), a prevalent urogenital illness that affects men worldwide. It is characterized by a complicated etiology and multiple interactions (1,2). The pathophysiology of ED is complicated and usually involves the interaction of psychosocial, partner/relationship-related, and organic factors (3). According to a European study on male aging, prevalence rates of ED range from roughly 6% to 64%, and the risk varies greatly among various populations and age groups (4). Previous epidemiological research has shown that the prevalence of ED among adult males aged 40 to 70 years varies greatly by country, with rates of 42.1% in Brazil, 47.7% in China, and 46.1% in the United States (U.S.) (5). A number of risk factors, such as advanced age, hyperlipidemia, psychological conditions like anxiety and depression, and metabolic and cardiovascular disorders like diabetes and hypertension, are involved in the complicated etiology of ED (6,7). A person’s mental health (such as anxiety and depression), social functioning (including sexual intimacy and career development), and general quality of life can all be significantly and profoundly negatively impacted by ED, even though the disorder itself is not directly life-threatening (8). Therefore, it is crucial from a clinical and public health perspective to comprehensively investigate the pertinent factors driving ED and create prevention strategies.

The cholesterol found in triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, such as medium-density lipoprotein, very low-density lipoprotein, and chylomicron remnants, is referred to as remnant cholesterol (RC). It is computed by deducting low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) from total cholesterol (TC). One separate risk factor for atherosclerosis is RC (9). Moreover, RC has been identified as a significant cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factor (10). In addition to being a significant risk factor for CVD, RC has been linked to the development and course of a number of prevalent illnesses, such as diabetes, depression, and sarcopenia (11-13). It has been established that dyslipidemia is a separate risk factor for ED (14). Moreover, vascular endothelial dysfunction, which is crucial to the development and course of ED, is known to be largely caused by systemic inflammatory reactions (15). Academic circles have shown growing interest in composite biomarkers that can integrate these two biological pathways in recent years due to the interplay between anomalies in lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses in the onset and progression of ED (16). RC and C-reactive protein (CRP) are combined to create the remnant cholesterol inflammation index (RCII), a novel composite biomarker that gives a complete picture of a person’s lipid metabolism state and degree of inflammatory risk (17). RCII shows potential benefits over conventional single biomarkers in risk stratification and holds promise for offering deeper insights into the possible pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ED by concurrently reflecting abnormalities in lipid metabolism and inflammatory activity levels. Nevertheless, there is still very little actual study on RCII’s connection to ED, despite its strong theoretical biological foundation. Our thorough grasp of RCII’s potential clinical benefit and application prospects has been somewhat hampered by the absence of systematic research assessing the association between RCII and ED to date.

This study uses nationally representative population data from the U.S. to investigate the relationship between RCII and ED in order to fill this research gap. To comprehensively assess the relationship between RCII and ED, a cross-sectional analysis using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was carried out in order to clarify the possible involvement of this composite indicator in the occurrence of ED. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-1-0006/rc).

Methods

Survey description for the NHANES

The official NHANES website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) provides demographic information and detailed methodologies. The survey protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and all procedures complied with U.S. Department of Health and Human Services regulations on the protection of human research subjects. Before being included in the study, each participant gave written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Because NHANES uses both structured interviews and standardized physical examinations for data gathering, its study approach is different from surveys that only use questionnaires. NHANES uses a stratified, multistage probability sampling approach for subject recruitment in order to guarantee that the sample is representative of the overall U.S. population.

Study participants

Based on information from two consecutive NHANES survey cycles (2001–2004), this study is a cross-sectional analysis. A total of 1,806 persons aged ≥20 years were eventually included in the analysis thanks to the use of stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria. There were 21,161 participants in the first sample. Following the application of exclusion criteria, the following categories were gradually eliminated: (I) females (n=10,860); (II) people under the age of 20 years (n=5,347); (III) people with missing ED-related data (n=838); and (IV) people with missing RCII indicators (n=2,310). As a result, 1,806 people made up the final study cohort. Figure 1 describes the particular screening procedure and subject inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Assessment of RCII

The product of RC and CRP is known as RCII (17). Among these, RC is computed by deducting TC from HDL-C and LDL-C (18). The RCII was log-transformed before statistical analysis to improve the normality of the variable distribution and lessen the possible influence of extreme values on the study outcomes. To improve the robustness of the regression model and better meet the linearity condition, the natural logarithm ln (RCII) is calculated for each RCII value. The reliability and interpretability of the research findings were improved by this treatment, which also improved the linearity of the association between RCII and ED risk and brought the data distribution closer to normal.

Definition of ED

The erectile function questionnaire from NHANES (variable code: KIQ400) served as the basis for the evaluation of ED. Audio computer-assisted self-interviewing (ACASI) was used to gather data in separate, private rooms set up at the Massachusetts Education Center. The Massachusetts Male Aging Study’s standardized self-report question was used to measure ED, the study’s dependent variable (19): “How would you describe your ability to get and maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual intercourse?” “Never”, “Sometimes”, “Usually”, and “Almost always or always” were the response options available to respondents. Those who said they were “usually” or “almost always or always” able to get and keep an erection were categorized as having no ED, whereas those who said they were “sometimes” or “never” able to do so were classified as having ED.

Covariates

Age, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, and body mass index (BMI) are some of the potential factors that could affect the association between RCII and ED. Age was divided into two categories: under 50 years and over 50 years. Race/ethnicity is categorized as Hispanic/Latino Americans, non-Hispanic whites, non-Hispanic blacks, other Hispanics, and other races. “Married or living with a partner” and “living alone” are the two categories for marital status. Participants’ drinking status was classified according to whether they had drunk more than 12 alcoholic beverages in the previous 12 months. Participants’ current smoking status and whether they had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lives were used to determine their smoking status. A BMI of less than 25 kg/m2 was considered normal weight, and a BMI of more than 25 kg/m2 was considered overweight or obese. Diabetes and hypertension were two past medical history-related factors that were gathered using self-reported data. Information on prescription medication use was obtained from the NHANES prescription drug database collected during the in-home interview. Participants were asked to report all prescription medications used in the past 30 days and to present medication containers for verification. Based on standardized drug classification codes, three medication categories known to affect erectile function were included as covariates: antihypertensive agents (particularly beta-blockers), antidepressants, and phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE-5) inhibitors. Each medication category was defined as a binary variable (yes/no) indicating exposure. Please see the official NHANES website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/) for further information on covariate definitions and data collection.

Statistical analysis

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria for NHANES data analysis are closely followed in all statistical studies. The lowest tertile (T1) is used as the reference group when RCII is categorized by tertiles. Categorical data are shown as percentages (%), whilst continuous variables are provided as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Chi-squared and t-tests were used to compare baseline characteristic differences between RCII quartile groups. The linear connection between RCII and ED was evaluated using a multivariable logistic regression model, and three stepwise adjusted models were created. Use a trend test (P for trend) to further evaluate the trend in the linear relationship between ED and ln (RCII) (tertiles). Additionally, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were carried out using three models: Model 1 was the unadjusted model; Model 2 made adjustments for age, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment; and Model 3 included additional adjustments for all potential factors. Additionally, an exploratory examination of the possible nonlinear link between RCII and ED prevalence rates was carried out using a smoothing curve fitting method. Predefined effect modification variables are used to assess interaction effects. Further stratified analyses based on age, education level, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and BMI were carried out to investigate possible variations in association effects across other demographic factors. To enhance the clinical applicability of RCII, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate its discriminatory ability for ED. EmpowerStats (version 2.0) and the survey package in R software (version 4.4.1) were used for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was defined as P values less than 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The baseline characteristics of 1,806 research participants are summarized in Table 1, with an average age of 49.82±18.75 years; 534 of them reported having ED. Participants with ED showed considerably greater levels of RC and RCII than those without ED. In addition to being substantially older than participants without ED, patients with ED typically had worse socioeconomic position and educational attainment. In terms of lifestyle and comorbidities, men with ED are more likely to be married or live with a partner, smoke, consume less alcohol, and have higher rates of diabetes and hypertension. They also exhibit greater use of beta-blockers, antidepressants, and PDE5 inhibitors. Additionally, racial/ethnic distribution and laboratory markers like LDL-C and CRP showed significant variations between ED and non-ED participants (all P<0.05; see Table 1).

Association between RCII and ED

There is a strong link between ln (RCII) and ED, as Table 2 illustrates. The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that ln (RCII) was substantially positively correlated with the risk of ED in the unadjusted Model 1 [odds ratio (OR) =1.40; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.29–1.51]. This positive correlation was still statistically significant in Model 2 after additional adjustments for age, race/ethnicity, and educational achievement (OR =1.22; 95% CI: 1.11–1.35). Ln (RCII) continued to have an independent positive association with ED in Model 3, which completely controlled for all variables (OR =1.13; 95% CI: 1.01–1.27). In particular, there is a roughly 13% increased chance of ED incidence for every unit increase in ln (RCII). This favorable correlation continued when ln (RCII) was divided into tertiles for analysis; all trend tests produced statistically significant findings (all P for trend <0.05). The ln (RCII) level in the highest tertile (T3) in the fully adjusted Model 3 was substantially linked to a 62% higher risk of ED when compared to the lowest tertile (T1) (OR =1.62; 95% CI: 1.12–2.35).

Smooth curve fitting models for ln (RCII) and ED

This study used a smooth curve fitting model for analysis in order to look at the possible nonlinear link between ln (RCII) and ED. Smooth curve fitting results based on large-scale NHANES data show that ln (RCII) and ED risk have a substantial overall positive connection (P for overall <0.05). The nonlinearity test failed to meet standard statistical significance criteria (P for nonlinear =0.09), despite the curve shape suggesting a possible nonlinear trend. Therefore, it is not yet possible to confirm a substantial nonlinear association between ln (RCII) and ED based on the available data. A linear model can be used to analyze this association, which can be roughly described as linear (see Figure 2).

Subgroup analyses

This study also performed subgroup analyses to evaluate the consistency of the relationship between ln (RCII) and ED prevalence across various demographics (Table 3). Diabetes history, smoking status, hypertension status, age, alcohol intake, educational achievement, and BMI were used to stratify the study participants. Subgroup analysis showed that people over 50 years, people aged 30–39 years, those with higher BMIs, those who do not smoke, and those with higher levels of education had a stronger positive connection between ln (RCII) and the prevalence of ED. All of these stratified factors showed statistically significant interactions (P for interaction <0.05). However, there were no significant interaction effects between ln (RCII) and ED across other stratification criteria, such as alcohol use, hypertension status, and history of diabetes (P for interaction >0.05).

Predictive value of RCII, RC, CRP, and LDL-C for ED

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of RCII to discriminate ED. The optimal cut-off value of RCII was identified as 2.6450. Detailed ROC analysis results are presented in Table 4. To evaluate the incremental predictive performance of RCII, ROC curve analyses were conducted to compare RCII with its individual components (RC and CRP) and a conventional lipid parameter (LDL-C). As shown in Table 4 and Figure 3, RCII demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability for ED among the evaluated markers, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.6264 (95% CI: 0.5988–0.6540). In comparison, the AUCs for RC, CRP, and LDL-C were 0.5648 (95% CI: 0.5365–0.5931), 0.6210 (95% CI: 0.5932–0.6488), and 0.5409 (95% CI: 0.5117–0.5702), respectively. The optimal cut-off value of RCII was 2.6450, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.7584 and a specificity of 0.4222. These findings indicate that RCII provides improved discriminatory performance compared with its individual components and traditional lipid parameters.

Discussion

This study evaluated the relationship between RCII and the prevalence of ED using a cross-sectional analysis based on NHANES data. Elevated levels of ln (RCII) are strongly linked to an increased risk of developing ED, according to research findings. Those over 50 years, those with higher BMIs, non-smokers, and those with higher educational attainment showed the strongest correlation. A linear positive connection between ln (RCII) and ED incidence was further validated by smooth curve fitting analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to use the NHANES database to systematically assess the relationship between RCII and ED in a sizable population sample. This cross-sectional analysis cannot establish causality. Elevated RCII may represent a surrogate marker of systemic vascular dysfunction and chronic inflammatory burden rather than a direct etiological factor for ED. Importantly, the ROC analyses conducted in the present study demonstrated that RCII exhibited the strongest discriminatory performance for ED among the evaluated markers. Compared with its individual components (RC and CRP) and the conventional lipid parameter LDL-C, RCII consistently showed superior predictive ability. These findings provide direct empirical evidence from our study population that a composite index integrating lipid-related atherogenic burden and systemic inflammatory status may offer improved risk discrimination over single biomarkers. The ROC results, therefore, support the potential clinical relevance of RCII as a more informative indicator of vascular-related ED.

It is important to note that a number of prior studies have shown that RCII can function as a comprehensive risk indicator for a variety of diseases. In 2025, Chen and colleagues proposed RCII as a new composite index for stroke risk prediction (17). A large prospective cohort study indicates that elevated RCII levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of future diabetes development in middle-aged and older adults in China and the United Kingdom (20). Additionally, RCII was found to be strongly correlated with the prevalence of diabetic nephropathy in a cross-sectional study involving 5,943 individuals in the U.S. by Xie et al., indicating that RCII may function as a novel and potentially promising biomarker for diabetic nephropathy risk (21). In the meantime, a large longitudinal study found that RCII levels significantly positively correlated with the risk of dementia onset in multivariate regression analysis after controlling for potential confounding factors. This correlation was especially strong in the female population (22). Furthermore, a cross-sectional investigation found a strong correlation between raised RCII levels and a markedly higher risk of metabolic syndrome (23).

Numerous intricate physiological and pathological processes may mediate the relationship between RCII and ED, with the connection between chronic low-grade inflammation and lipid metabolism abnormalities thought to be crucial (24). Increased vascular endothelial permeability due to elevated RC plasma levels enables RC to pass through the endothelium and progressively build up in the artery intima. There, macrophages phagocytose it, increasing the process and encouraging the production of foam cells. In the end, these foam cells and their aggregates make up a significant portion of atherosclerotic plaques (25). Additionally, by improving the chemotaxis of vascular smooth muscle cells and increasing the permeability of vascular endothelial cells, RC may further contribute to the instability of atherosclerotic plaque fibrous caps (26). Remaining lipoprotein particles have been shown to activate immune-related and vascular endothelial signaling pathways in human studies and in vitro experimental models. This results in the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, which exacerbate vascular inflammatory responses (27). At the same time, prior research has shown a strong and intimate link between ED and atherosclerosis (28,29). In the meantime, the pathophysiological process of ED is significantly influenced by CRP, a traditional indicator of chronic low-grade inflammation. Generally considered a sign of systemic inflammation, elevated CRP levels may indicate the interaction between inflammatory processes and problems of lipid metabolism (30). According to earlier research, CRP can damage vascular endothelial function by either increasing the expression of E-selectin, P-selectin, chemokines, and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) or reducing the activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) (31). The aforementioned process may result in less nitric oxide (NO) being produced, which would lower local blood perfusion and vasodilation capability and negatively impact erectile performance (32). RC and CRP may have intricate synergistic relationships in which each influences the other. Through inflammatory reactions and vascular endothelial activation, elevated RC levels may encourage elevated CRP levels. Increased CRP levels may worsen lipid metabolism issues, creating a vicious cycle that eventually speeds up the development of ED and its associated problems (33,34). By incorporating data on lipid metabolism and inflammatory status, RCII efficiently captures the intricate relationships between RC and CRP, improving the usefulness and precision of this indicator in disease risk assessment. Lipid deposition and systemic inflammation are two pathogenic processes that may be involved in the onset and progression of ED, according to RCII, which shows the combined effects of anomalies in lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses on ED. Together, these elements accelerate the development of atherosclerosis and worsen problems with lipid metabolism (35,36). Interestingly, refined age-stratified analyses identified a significant association between ln (RCII) and ED in adults aged 30–39 years, with a larger effect size than that observed in the overall population. Although the association was not statistically significant when all individuals aged <50 years were analyzed together, the presence of a stronger association in this narrower age range suggests potential heterogeneity in age-specific risk patterns. In relatively younger individuals, ED is less likely to be driven by advanced structural vascular changes and may instead reflect early endothelial dysfunction and low-grade systemic inflammation (15,37). Because RCII integrates lipid metabolism abnormalities and inflammatory status, elevated RCII in this age group may indicate early vascular impairment preceding overt clinical disease. These findings suggest that RCII may have potential value as an early risk indicator in selected younger populations, although confirmation in prospective studies is warranted.

Study strengths and limitations

The robustness of the results was confirmed by various sensitivity analyses, and this study is the first systematic assessment of the relationship between RCII and ED. There are several benefits to this study. First, one of the biggest national epidemiological surveys in the U.S. is NHANES. Its data collection follows strict quality control guidelines, guaranteeing that research findings are very dependable and representative. Second, to reduce potential bias, we adjusted for multiple confounding factors in our analysis. Subgroup analysis was further adjusted for confounding variables, strengthening the validity and relevance of the research findings. Our results show a substantial positive correlation between RCII and ED in the U.S. adult population. Furthermore, subgroup studies indicate that people over 50 years, those with higher BMI, those who do not smoke, and those with higher levels of education may be more vulnerable to this link and should be given priority as important targets for clinical and public health interventions. There are still a number of shortcomings in this study, though. First, the NHANES cycles from 2001 to 2004 are the only ones covered by the data utilized in this study, which is an older dataset that might not accurately reflect the population situation now. Second, the results only show an observational link between RCII and ED; due to the cross-sectional design, causal inference cannot be made. RCII should be interpreted as an associated biomarker that may reflect underlying systemic vascular disease processes contributing to ED. Third, the diagnosis of ED depends on self-reported data from NHANES, of which its accuracy may be impacted by reporting biases and respondents’ subjective assessments. Additionally, the NHANES cycles used in this study (2001–2004) were conducted more than two decades ago, which may limit the applicability of the findings to contemporary populations with evolving lifestyle patterns and treatment strategies. Lastly, ED classification was based on a single self-reported question rather than a validated clinical instrument such as the International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5), which may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias, and potential misclassification.

Conclusions

In the adult population of the U.S., this study shows a substantial positive correlation between RCII and ED. When compared to several distinct inflammatory or lipid metabolism markers, RCII has been shown to be a strong and independent predictor of ED. This result emphasizes the potential clinical value of RCII in identifying people who are at high risk. To confirm these results and offer more solid proof, more prospective cohort studies or randomized controlled trials are required.
