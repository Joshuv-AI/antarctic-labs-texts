Impairment of the frontal lobe in late-onset fetal growth restriction: is it detectable in fetuses with a normal cerebroplacental ratio?
Authors: Shu-Jing Huang, Yan Lin, Huan-Zhong Su, Xian-Lan Liu, Yu-Qing Su, Yan-Ting Lin, Mei Huang, Long-Cheng Hong, Yan-Qing Wang, Jin-Rong Lin, Xiao-Dong Zhang, Ying Zhou, Yi-Ming Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn fetal growth restriction (FGR), the brain-sparing effect refers to a hemodynamic adaptation that prioritizes blood flow to the brain. The aim of this study was to assess whether fetuses with late-onset FGR have smaller brain structures and evaluate whether the structural impairments of the brain are present even when the cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) is still normal.MethodsIn this prospective cross-sectional study, ultrasonography was used to measure the frontal antero-posterior diameter (FAPD), occipito-frontal diameter (OFD), biparietal diameter (BPD), sylvian fissure depth (SFD), and insular lobe depth (ILD) in 30 control fetuses and 90 late-onset small fetuses: 30 small for gestational age (SGA) and 60 FGR. The FAPD/OFD, SFD/BPD and ILD/BPD ratios were calculated. The differences in FAPD, ILD, SFD, and their ratios among the study groups were calculated.ResultsCompared to controls or SGA, late-onset FGR fetuses had significantly smaller FAPD (both P < 0.01)and FAPD/OFD ratio ((both P < 0.05)), but there were no differences in FGR between normal and abnormal CPR (P > 0.05). Frontal lobe changes were present in 56.7% (34/60) of all FGR fetuses. In addition, FGR with abnormal CPR was associated with smaller head circumference and OFD relative to controls (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found in ILD, SFD, SF/BPD ratio, or IL/BPD ratio among the four groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionUltrasound may be a sensitive and convenient method of detecting changes in the brain in late-onset FGR. Frontal lobe impairment was observed in late-onset FGR fetuses regardless of their CPR status, suggesting that this structural change may be present even in those with a normal CPR.

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR), which is associated with increased perinatal morbidity and death, is among the most common complications of pregnancy. This condition may be associated with adverse perinatal and neurodevelopmental outcomes [1]. Of these, late-onset FGR (usually defined as that which is detected > 32 weeks) is defined by a mismatch between fetal needs and placental supplies and is linked to a greater risk of neurodevelopmental delay (NDD) [2]. Frisk et al. [3] showed that where the brain is impaired in utero due to FGR, little or no catch-up head growth is possible after birth. The effects of FGR have impacts beyond the neonatal period, into childhood and adulthood. Persistent and long-term adverse neurological outcomes are observed, including psychomotor and cognitive impairment, behavioral changes during childhood, and speech impairment, motor disorders, and schooling delays from childhood to adolescence [4, 5]. Consequently, early prenatal identification of fetuses at increased risk for NDD is clinically significant for perinatologists and neonatologists managing pregnancies complicated by FGR..

In FGR, the brain-sparing effect, referring to the redistribution of cerebro-placental blood flow, causes a change in oxygenation pattern in the fetus to allow the brain to obtain the necessary resources for its proper development and functioning [6, 7]. However, this adaptive phenomenon might not be entirely protective. In previous studies, when brain sparing has been identified, smaller head circumferences (HCs) [8] and smaller brain volumes [9] at birth have been the main findings reported, with implications for delayed neurocognitive development at later ages. Therefore, it is necessary to continue to explore whether fetuses with late FGR have intrauterine brain-structure impairment. The cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) is a useful tool for identifying FGR and at-risk fetuses [10]. However, Meher et al. [11] proposed that the development of abnormalities of the middle cerebral artery might be a late event in the overall redistribution of blood flow in the fetal brain, although this remains uncertain. Furthermore, it remains unknown whether changes in brain structures are present before the development of an abnormal CPR value.

It has previously been observed that there is a degree of axonopathy in the frontal lobe in late-onset small fetuses as viewed via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [12]. These MR changes seem to be associated with neurobehavioral manifestations after birth [13]. Although MRI is the most generally used technique for brain imaging in fetuses, ultrasonography also provides a superb picture of brain regions and structures, and is a cheaper and more readily available imaging approach. Using three-dimensional sonography with the virtual organ computer-aided analysis method, reduced volume has been demonstrated in many intracranial structures in small fetuses, most notable within the frontal region [6]. Some studies have also linked variation in fissure depth of the fetal brain to neurodevelopment [14]. However, this methodology is relatively complex, and the use of prenatal ultrasonography for assessing changes in neurodevelopment in high-risk FGR has received little attention.

The main aim of this study was to assess whether fetuses with FGR have a smaller brain structure. The secondary aim was to evaluate whether such structural changes in the brain are present in FGR fetuses regardless of their CPR status..

Materials and methods

Subjects

This is a prospective cross-sectional study carried out at our medical center on cases evaluated between January 2021 and January 2023. The control group consisted of normal pregnancies referred to our obstetric ultrasound services. The study group included cases of small fetuses identified prenatally, defined by an estimated fetal weight (EFW) below the 10th centile after 32 weeks of gestation, consistent with international guidelines [15]. All neonates in the study group had a confirmed birthweight below the 10th centile postnatally. Patients reported the date of their last menstrual period and confirmed their regular menstrual history. Pregnancy dating was verified according to crown-rump length (CRL), measured during a routine first trimester intake before 13 weeks GA. The exclusion criteria were multiple gestations, abnormal fetal chromosomes or structural abnormality, and poor image quality that limited accurate measurement.

We divided all fetuses into four different groups according to the table proposed by the ISUOG guidelines and Delphi consensus [15, 16]. The FGR group were those with EFW < third centile and/or abnormal uterine artery Doppler results and/or abnormal CPR, while fetuses with EFW between the third and the ninth centile and normal fetoplacental Doppler results were considered small for gestational age (SGA). Abnormal CPR values were considered to be those below the fifth centile for the sake of these studies. In addition, a control group of fetuses with sizes that were adequate for gestational age were selected among the low-risk pregnancies with confirmed birthweights between the tenth and ninetieth centiles. To compare intracranial parameters, we used stratified sampling to derive the random sample. Every group of fetuses was matched by GA at the time of ultrasound examination (± 1 week).

The local institutional Ethics Committees approved this study protocol. All participants in this study were informed and consented prior to enrollment.

Transabdominal ultrasound measurements

All ultrasound measurements for this study were performed at the time of enrollment, which occurred between 32 + 0 and 39 + 6 weeks of gestation, prior to delivery. Once a transthalamic plane view images were obtained in which the cavum septi pellucidi can be visualized and the thalami are visible symmetrically on either side of the midline, images were saved automatically for offline analysis. All of the measurements were performed offline by a single experienced examiner blinded to study groups. The frontal lobe, Sylvian fissure (SF), and the insular lobe (IL) values were analyzed, following previous studies [17, 18]. The US apparatus used was an EPIQ 7 (Philips Healthcare, Bothell, WA), Voluson E8 and E10 (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI).

Assessment of frontal lobe (Fig. 1) [18]

Trans-thalamic axial view of the fetal head. The shorter solid double arrow represents the frontal-antero-posterior-diameter (FAPD) and is drawn from the internal edge of the frontal bone to the posterior edge of the cavum septi pellucidi (identified by a dotted line). The longer dotted double arrow represents the occipito-frontal diameter (OFD)

The frontal antero-posterior diameter (FAPD) was quantified by placing the calipers on the internal edge of the frontal bone to the posterior edge of the cavum septi pellucidi (measurement IN-to-IN), assuming it to be representative of the frontal lobes’ area. The occipito-frontal diameter (OFD) was also measured IN-to-IN from the internal edge of the frontal bone to the internal edge of the occipital bone. The FAPD/OFD ratio (FAPD/OFD*100) that we utilized in this study was measured to reflect the growth of the fetal brain’s frontal component in relation to overall brain growth.

Assessment of sylvian fissure and insular lobe (Fig. 2) [17]

Ultrasound image (axial view) of the fetal head, solid double arrow represents insula lobe depth (ILD) and dotted double arrow represents SF depth (SFD)

IL depth (ILD) was measured from the cerebral falx to the point of maximum prominence of the insular cortex in a line perpendicular to the orientation of the cerebral falx. The prominent hyperechoic band at the external surface of the cortex was included in the measurement. The SF depth (SFD) was measured by drawing a line to continue the insular line, from the internal border of the insular cortex to the inner border of the parietal bone. The IL and SF were adjusted by BPD to SFD/BPD ratio = SFD/BPD *100 and ILD/BPD ratio = ILD/BPD *100.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 26.0 software package (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the normality of distributions. Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs), medians (interquartile ranges), or percentages (%), as appropriate. As some data deviated from a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test, a non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA, was employed to compare the four groups (controls, SGA, FGR with normal CPR, and FGR with abnormal CPR). When the Kruskal–Wallis test was statistically significant, Dunn's post-hoc procedure with a Bonferroni correction was used for multiple pairwise comparisons. All P values were two-tailed, and P values of 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on detecting a clinically meaningful difference in the primary outcome measure, the frontal antero-posterior diameter (FAPD). Based on pilot data from our center, we anticipated a mean difference of 3 mm in FAPD between the control and FGR groups, with a standard deviation of 3 mm. To achieve 80% power with a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, a sample size of 17 per group would be required. To account for multiple comparisons among the four groups and potential dropouts due to poor image quality, we aimed to recruit 30 patients per study group.

In addition, late-onset FGR with abnormal CPR presented shorter HC (mm) (P = 0.026) and OFD (mm) (P = 0.031). For HC, a statistically significant difference was only observed between FGR with abnormal CPR and the control group (P = 0.003). Using gestational age-adjusted reference values from our control group (FAPD < 10th centile for gestational age), frontal lobe reduction was observed in 60.0% (18/30) of FGR fetuses with normal CPR and in 53.3% (16/30) of FGR fetuses with abnormal CPR, with an overall prevalence of 56.7% (34/60) among all FGR fetuses.

Discussion

Main findings of the study

This study shows that ultrasonography may be a sufficiently sensitive and convenient approach for detecting differences in neurodevelopment in late-onset FGR. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate whether structural neurological changes can be identified prior to the development of an abnormal CPR value in the setting of FGR. Both FAPD and the FAPD/OFD ratio were significantly smaller in both FGR with normal and aberrant CPR compared to the controls group. Nevertheless, no significant reduction was found in HC, OFD, SFD, ILD, SFD/BPD ratio, or ILD/BPD ratio across the study groups.

Interpretation of results and comparison with existing literature

Frontal lobe growth is critical for achieving normal cognitive function and intelligence [19–21]. Our results suggest that FGR fetuses exhibit reduced FAPD and FAPD/OFD ratios compared to SGA and controls, which seems to be associated with frontal lobe neurodevelopment. This accords with the observations by Benavides-Serralde et al. [21] that in many intracranial structures, FGR fetuses show the largest differences in the volume of the frontal region compared to what is appropriate for gestational age fetuses. One possible explanation for the frontal lobe changes in FGR might be that it makes up almost one third of the cerebral hemisphere. Therefore, these values are sensitive and may indicate the vulnerability of the frontal lobes to FGR. However, the observation that SGA fetuses, despite having low birthweight, showed no significant differences in frontal lobe measurements compared to controls underscores that it is the underlying placental insufficiency, rather than low birthweight, that is associated with altered brain development in FGR. This supports the conceptual distinction between constitutionally small fetuses and those with true growth restriction due to placental dysfunction..

Whether these structural changes are reflected prior to the formation of an abnormal CPR value, as hypothesized by Meher et al. [11], remains unclear. The presence of an abnormal CPR value is associated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes [4]. In this study, however, we were unable to demonstrate any significant difference in FGR between normal and abnormal CPR, as the FAPD and FAPD/OFD ratio was lower in FGR with normal CPR compared to the SGA or controls. FAPD and FAPD/OFD ratio are probably better expressions of the frontal lobe development in appropriately grown fetuses and fetuses with FGR [18]. The finding that FAPD and the FAPD/OFD ratio were significantly reduced in FGR fetuses with a normal CPR, compared to controls and SGA, suggests that impairment of the frontal lobe can be detected prior to, or in the absence of, an abnormal CPR. This supports the hypothesis that cerebral redistribution, as indicated by an abnormal CPR, may be a later phenomenon in the cascade of hemodynamic changes affecting the fetal brain. It is worth noting that we also found that the reduction of HC and OFD were detected only when CPR was abnormal. Although HC remains an important independent predictor of neurodevelopmental outcomes [5], this finding, while preliminary, suggests that poor neurodevelopment may occur prior to HC change. Therefore, FAPD and the FAPD/OFD ratio may be new earlier ultrasound markers of altered brain growth in FGR. However, Further research is needed to correlate these biometric changes with long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

An important finding of our study is the high prevalence of frontal lobe changes in late-onset FGR. Using FAPD below the control 10th centile as the definition, frontal lobe reduction was observed in 60.0% of FGR fetuses with normal CPR and 53.3% of those with abnormal CPR, with an overall prevalence of 56.7%. This high prevalence (approximately 56.7% of cases) suggests that frontal lobe involvement is a common characteristic of late-onset FGR pathophysiology rather than a rare complication. The similar prevalence between FGR groups with normal and abnormal CPR further supports our hypothesis that frontal lobe changes occur regardless of CPR status.

In contrast to earlier findings, no significant differences were evident in SFD, ILD, SFD/BPD ratio, or ILD/BPD ratio among the four groups. Although several attempts have been made to evaluate brain development by measuring brain fissure depth by ultrasound [6, 14], this approach has limitations, such as the different positions of the fetus, which may result in a slight deformation of the skull, preventing us from observing differences in ILD and SFD among groups.

A primary feature of this study is that, unlike some previous research, we classified FGR fetuses according to their CPR status to investigate the association between CPR and changes in brain structures implicated in neurodevelopmental delay [18]. We found that abnormal CPR might be a later biometric change in the process of FGR. In addition, brain volume based on three-dimensional reconstruction is difficult to measure due to the bone shadow, particularly the increase in skull mineralization during the third trimester of pregnancy. The trans-thalamic axial view is the routine reference view recommended by national and international guidelines. It is a quite straightforward method to investigate differences in neurodevelopment in late-onset FGR on this view is much faster. Compared to fetal brain volume, this approach can be implemented quickly on stored images.

An important clinical question arising from our findings is whether frontal lobe measurements should influence the timing of delivery in late-onset FGR. Currently, CPR remains the primary hemodynamic parameter guiding delivery decisions in clinical practice [4]. Our finding that frontal lobe reduction is detectable even in FGR fetuses with a normal CPR raises the question of whether current management algorithms should consider this parameter. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine the natural history of frontal lobe changes and whether they predict adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes independent of CPR.

Limitations

This study had certain limitations. All fetal brain measurements were performed offline by a single experienced examiner who was blinded to group allocation and Doppler findings. This ensures that the observed differences in frontal lobe parameters are not attributable to measurement bias. However, The patients and clinicians may not have been sufficiently blinded to the results of the ultrasound and Doppler assessments, as in most clinical circumstances, clinical intervention will likely follow ultrasound in a small fetus to prevent a birthweight < tenth centile. Furthermore, for fetuses identified with growth limitations during the prenatal stage, there is a lack of tracking their neonatal and adolescent neurodevelopment. Despite the comparison between FGR at similar gestational ages with normal and abnormal CPR were performed in our study, the main weakness is that we overlooked difference in fetal weight in the comparison of SGA and FGR. In addition, the cross-sectional design of this study is a primary limitation. While our finding of frontal lobe reduction in FGR fetuses with a normal CPR suggests this change may be an early feature, we cannot definitively conclude that it precedes the development of an abnormal CPR. Longitudinal studies with serial measurements from diagnosis to delivery are required to establish the true temporal relationship between these biometric changes and the onset of cerebral redistribution.

Conclusion

Ultrasound image of trans-thalamic axial view may be a sufficiently sensitive and convenient approach for detecting changes in the brain in fetuses with late-onset growth restriction. This study demonstrates that impairment of the frontal lobe, as evidenced by reduced FAPD and FAPD/OFD ratio, can be identified in FGR fetuses even when the CPR is still normal. Whether these structural changes are predictive of long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes requires further longitudinal investigation.
