Hydrochemical and isotopic signatures revealing surface-groundwater dynamics: An empirical evidence from the Cherchen River Basin
Authors: Zhiyuan Xu, Bin Wu, Kun Liu, Fan Gao, Bin He, Waheed Abdul, Aishajiang Aili, Fanghong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWater scarcity directly constrains the development of arid inland river basins. Quantifying surface water-groundwater (SW-GW) transformation is critical for refined water resource management. To this end, we selected the Cherchen River Basin as a representative study area. By integrating hydrochemical analysis, isotope tracing, and end-member mixing analysis, we systematically quantified SW-GW interactions under varying spatiotemporal conditions. The results show that: (1) Hydrochemical facies exhibit distinct spatial zonation, transitioning from HCO3 dominated types upstream to Cl-Na types downstream; (2) Hydrogeochemical processes are controlled by the weathering and dissolution of silicate and evaporite minerals; (3) SW mainly recharges GW in the upstream and downstream reaches, while GW discharges into SW in the midstream, with notable seasonal reversals at certain sections. These findings advance the understanding of hydrogeochemical evolution and SW-GW dynamics in arid inland basins and provide a scientific basis for sustainable water management in similar regions.

Introduction

In arid inland river basins, the reciprocal recharge-discharge processes between surface water (SW) and groundwater (GW) jointly sustain the regional water-cycle balance and exert profound influences on hydrochemical evolution and ecosystem stability.1 In recent years, under the combined influence of the rigid growth in agricultural water demand, the continuous expansion of cultivated land, and global climate change, SW-GW transformation processes have been altered. The resulting mismatch between water supply and demand, driven by pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity, has become increasingly acute and poses substantial challenges to water-resources management in arid regions.2 Therefore, exploring the spatiotemporal evolution of basin water chemistry, clarifying the hydraulic connection between SW and GW, and quantitatively identifying the transformation processes among different water bodies are crucial for understanding water-cycle mechanisms in arid regions and achieving sustainable water-resources management at the basin scale.

In recent years, to investigate interactions among different water bodies, researchers have developed and applied a range of approaches, including direct measurement methods, water-balance methods,3 and numerical simulation methods.4 Among these approaches, direct measurement methods can capture SW-GW interactions in a straightforward manner. However, because direct measurement methods require the installation of specialized instruments, they can only quantify SW-GW exchange at fixed locations and are often constrained by issues such as complex instrument installation and maintenance as well as high operational costs. The water-balance method estimates SW-GW interactions by measuring flow at the upstream and downstream boundaries of a river reach. However, its practical application is often limited under complex hydrological conditions such as flood events.5 Modeling-based approaches, which are generally grounded in Darcy’s law and implemented through software such as HydroGeoSphere, GMS, and MIKE, are widely used to simulate SW-GW interactions in river basins. However, these approaches typically require extensive datasets for model calibration and validation.6 With advancements in analytical techniques, numerous studies have demonstrated that basin-scale hydrochemical characteristics provide critical insights into environmental change. When integrated with isotope tracing techniques, they allow for a more comprehensive elucidation of hydrological processes and functional attributes within the basin.7 Based on these characteristics, hydrochemical analyses and environmental isotope tracing methods have been widely applied in geological and hydrological investigations.8,9

Hydrochemical characteristics, derived from measurements of electrical conductivity, pH, and major anions (Cl−、SO42−、HCO3−、CO32−) and cations (Ca2+、 Na+、 K+、 and Mg2+) in GW and SW, are widely applied in studies of water-quality assessment (Liu et al.;10 Li et al.11), hydrochemical evolution,12 and GW pollution.13 Stable isotopes serve as ideal tracers for identifying recharge sources and elucidating water-cycle processes. They are widely applied in studies of GW recharge and discharge,14,15 pollutant traceability,16,17 and SW-GW interaction.18 At present, studies integrating these two methods have primarily focused on GW evolution,19 ecological water demand,20 seawater intrusion,21 nitrate traceability,22 and SW-GW conversion.23

The Tarim River, the largest inland river in China, traverses southern Xinjiang and serves as a vital backbone for sustaining regional ecological integrity and socioeconomic development. Specifically, as a major source stream of the Tarim River Basin, the Cherchen River represents a typical arid inland river system (Figure 1). In recent decades, the expansion of artificial oases and irrigated agriculture in the Cherchen River Basin has significantly intensified the exploitation of SW and GW, leading to increasingly severe water-resource constraints across the basin.24 Under these circumstances, the hydrological characteristics and evolutionary patterns of different river reaches remain unclear, and issues such as the difficulty in quantitatively assessing water consumption have increasingly emerged. These challenges impede the sustainable management of local water resources and constrain socioeconomic and ecological development. However, prior research on the Cherchen River Basin has predominantly centered on the basic physicochemical characteristics of the water bodies25 and GW recharge sources,26 leaving a gap in the quantitative assessment of SW-GW exchange rates across the basin under varying spatiotemporal conditions. Furthermore, current isotopic analyses are largely restricted to a macro-basin scale without fine-grained, segment-specific explorations, and a systematic evaluation of the evolutionary patterns of hydrogeochemical processes remains absent. Therefore, there is an urgent need to strengthen research on the hydrochemical characteristics and SW-GW interaction mechanisms in the Cherchen River Basin, to provide a solid theoretical foundation for basin-scale water-resources management.Figure 1Study area(A) The location of the Cherchen River Basin.(B) The sample sites in the Cherchen River.(C) The detailed sample sites in the middle reaches.

To address this gap, this study takes the Cherchen River Basin as a representative case to investigate SW-GW exchange processes in arid inland river systems. Drawing on multi-period field-measured hydrochemical data and stable isotope (δ18O and δ2H) analyses, and integrating PHREEQC-based hydrogeochemical simulations with end-member mixing analysis (EMMAs), we systematically analyzed the hydrogeochemical genetic mechanisms, hydrochemical evolution, and interactions among different water bodies within the basin. The specific research objectives include: (1) identifying the hydrochemical and isotopic characteristics, as well as water-rock interactions within the basin; (2) elucidating the hydrochemical genetic mechanisms of SW and GW; (3) revealing SW-GW transformation patterns across different wet/dry periods and river reaches, and quantitatively characterizing their mutual transformation processes. This study aims to deepen the understanding of water transformation mechanisms in arid inland river basins and provide a scientific basis for the sustainable management of water resources in these regions.

Results

General characteristics of surface water and groundwater

Hydrochemical analysis of SW and GW in the Cherchen River Basin (Figure S1) shows that both water bodies are weakly alkaline, with pH values generally ranging from 7.25 to 9.28 and exhibiting low variability (CV = 0.01–0.07). Seasonal differences in pH are minor, indicating overall chemical stability. TDS values of SW and GW range from 497 to 8,789 mg/L and from 513 to 140,266 mg/L, respectively, with consistently higher values in GW, reflecting pronounced GW salinization. Major cations follow the order Na+ >Mg2+ >Ca2+ >K+, while anions follow Cl− >SO42− >HCO3−+CO32− in both seasons. Na+ and Cl− dominate the ionic composition, accounting for 67.09%–82.45% of total cations and 42.69%–70.03% of total anions, respectively. Most solute concentrations exhibit higher values in the dry season than in the wet season and are consistently higher in GW than in SW. CV analysis further indicates that Na+ (1.42–2.97) and Cl− (1.43–3.19) show the strongest variability, suggesting high sensitivity to environmental and hydrological processes.

The Piper diagram analysis shows that in the cation triangle during the dry season (Figure 2), most samples are clustered near the Na-K apex, where the milliequivalent percentages of Na+ and K+ exceed 50%, indicating that alkali metal ions dominated by Na+ are predominant in both SW and GW in the basin. In the anion triangle, the samples are mainly distributed in zones B and G, indicating that Cl− is the dominant anion, while some samples exhibit mixed characteristics. In the diamond field, most samples are concentrated in the right-side region, corresponding to the Cl-Na hydrochemical type, while a few GW samples exhibit the Cl·SO4-Ca·Mg type. Mineral saturation index (SI)

The mineral saturation index (SI) was used to evaluate mineral dissolution and precipitation processes in the Cherchen River Basin, where SI values between −0.2 and 0.2 indicate equilibrium, values < −0.2 indicate dissolution, and values >0.2 indicate precipitation (Figure S2). PHREEQC simulations were conducted for eight mineral phases, including anhydrite, aragonite, calcite, CO2(g), dolomite, gypsum, halite, and sylvite. During the dry season, GW is undersaturated with respect to CO2(g), halite, and sylvite, and most samples also show undersaturation with respect to anhydrite and gypsum, with only a few samples approaching equilibrium. In contrast, aragonite, calcite, and dolomite are generally supersaturated, indicating carbonate precipitation. SW exhibits similar behavior, with sulfate and chloride minerals remaining undersaturated and carbonate minerals showing supersaturation. SI patterns during the wet season are broadly consistent with those of the dry season, although calcite and dolomite at some GW sites shift from supersaturation toward equilibrium or dissolution, reflecting seasonal variations in water-rock interactions. From the perspective of spatial distribution, SI generally shows a gradual increasing trend along the flow direction, indicating that as the water migrates downstream, the degree of mineral saturation continuously increases and gradually evolves toward saturation or even supersaturation.

Stable hydrogen and oxygen isotopes are widely used to trace water recharge sources and hydrological evolution. In the Cherchen River Basin, δ18O and δ2H values of SW show minor differences between the upper and middle reaches but become markedly enriched in the lower reaches, whereas GW isotopes exhibit pronounced depletion in the middle reaches relative to the upper and lower reaches (Table 1). Discussion

Sources of major ions and mechanisms of hydrochemical evolution

Spearman correlation analysis reveals distinct ion-association patterns between GW and SW in the Cherchen River Basin (Figure S3). Strong positive correlations among Na+, Cl−, SO42−, and TDS indicate that dissolved salts dominate water chemistry. During dry season, the extremely high correlation between Na+ and Cl− (r > 0.98), together with their strong relationships with TDS, suggests that halite dissolution is the primary contributor to salinity. Positive correlations between Na+ and SO42−, as well as between TDS and SO42−, reflect contributions from evaporite dissolution and anthropogenic inputs. In GW, the negative correlation between Ca2+ and CO32− indicates calcite precipitation, whereas the stronger Ca2+ and SO42− correlation relative to SW suggests more active gypsum dissolution. Overall, water chemistry is mainly controlled by mineral dissolution and precipitation, with additional influences from human activities.

During the wet season, significant positive correlations among Na+, Cl−, SO42−, and TDS indicate that dissolved salts remain an important factor controlling water chemistry. In SW, Na+ shows an extremely strong positive correlation with Cl− (r = 0.96), and both ions are strongly positively correlated with TDS (r = 0.96 and 0.95, respectively). In addition, SO42− also exhibits a strong positive correlation with TDS (r = 0.91), indicating that the dissolution of evaporite minerals such as halite remains an important source of solutes in SW. In GW, the major cations are all strongly positively correlated with TDS (r > 0.8), suggesting that GW salinity is mainly controlled by major cations. However, the correlation between Na+ and Cl− is relatively weaker (r = 0.64), implying that, compared with SW, the control of halite dissolution on GW salinity is reduced. Moreover, HCO3− in GW is significantly negatively correlated with Na+, Cl−, and TDS, reflecting that GW in the wet season is more strongly affected by mixing with other water sources than in the dry season.

The Gibbs diagram27 has been widely used to identify the dominant sources of major ions in aquatic systems. For all SW and GW samples sites in the Cherchen River Basin, the ratios ρ(Na+)/ρ(Na++Ca2+) and ρ(Cl−)/ρ(Cl−+HCO3−) are mostly greater than 0.5 (Figure 3), indicating that Na+ and Cl− are the dominant ions in the study area and that the water bodies are mainly influenced by evaporation concentration. The TDS values range from 490 to 141,000 mg/L, and most samples are distributed in the evaporation-dominance field, while a smaller number of samples plot close to the rock-weathering field, indicating that the hydrochemical composition in the study area is jointly controlled by evaporation concentration and water-rock interaction (Figures 3A and 3B). Dry-season samples are relatively closer to the evaporation-dominance field, whereas wet-season samples are relatively closer to the rock-weathering field, although their overall distribution patterns are similar. In addition, GW generally exhibits higher TDS values than SW, suggesting that it has undergone longer water-rock interaction and stronger evaporation concentration. Although evaporation is an important driving factor of water composition, rock weathering also contributes significantly to the ionic composition of the water. The Gaillardet ternary diagram28 is commonly used to identify the types of rock weathering sources that influence hydrochemical composition and is composed of three end-members: carbonate rocks, silicate rocks, and evaporite rocks. As shown in Figure 4, the water samples collected during the wet and dry seasons in the study area are distributed between the silicate and evaporite end-members, with most samples being closer to the silicate end-member. The distribution ranges of the two sets of samples are generally similar, without obvious seasonal differentiation, indicating that the control of rock weathering sources on hydrochemical composition is generally consistent between seasons. The source of NO3− can be further determined by analyzing the relationship between the NO3−/Cl− ratio and Cl− concentration. A high NO3−/Cl− ratio accompanied by low Cl− concentrations indicates that NO3− mainly originates from fertilizer application, whereas a low NO3−/Cl− ratio combined with high Cl− concentrations suggests the contamination of the basin’s water by domestic sewage or livestock wastewater.34 As shown in Figure 5G, the water samples in the study area cluster below the 1:1 line on the right side, indicating that the source of NO3− in the water is influenced by domestic sewage and livestock wastewater. In addition, some samples exhibit very low NO3−/Cl− ratios along with high Cl− concentrations, suggesting that these samples are affected by denitrification processes.

The major cation exchange process in water involves the exchange of Ca2+ and Mg2+ on aquifer material surfaces with Na+ and K+. This results in an increase in Na+ and K+ and a decrease in Ca2+ and Mg2+, and the molar ratio Na+/(Na++Cl−) is expected to exceed 0.5. In Figure 5H, most water samples cluster near 0.5, with some points located at higher values, indicating the presence of cation exchange in the study area. To further evaluate the extent of cation exchange, the chloro-alkaline indices (CAI-I and CAI-II) are used to analyze forward and reverse cation exchange processes in GW ((Equation 8), (Equation 9); Figure 5I). When Na+ and K+ in the water are exchanged with Ca2+ and Mg2+ on the surfaces of aquifer materials, forward cation exchange occurs, and the indices show positive values. Conversely, when Na+ and K+ on aquifer material surfaces are exchanged with Ca2+ and Mg2+ in the water, the indices exhibit negative values, indicating the occurrence of reverse cation exchange. Hydrogeochemical inverse modeling

Inverse hydrogeochemical modeling was performed using PHREEQC 3.7.3 to quantify the evolutionary processes of SW and GW in the Cherchen River Basin. Given the single main channel of the Cherchen River, inverse modeling paths were defined as S2→S7 and S7→S12. Based on basin lithology, hydrogeological conditions, dolomite, calcite, gypsum, halite, sylvite, and CO2 were selected as potential mineral phases involved in water and rock interactions, with NaX and CaX2 included to represent cation exchange. During the dry season, along the S2→S7 pathway for GW (Table 2), GW chemistry is mainly controlled by halite and gypsum dissolution, calcite precipitation, and cation exchange, accompanied by minor CO2 degassing. These processes promote Na+ enrichment and drive the hydrochemical facies from the HCO3-Ca type toward the SO4/Cl-Na type. Along the S7→S12 pathway, intensive dissolution of halite, dolomite, and gypsum, together with calcite precipitation and cation exchange, further increases mineralization. Additional CO2 input enhances solution acidity and mineral dissolution, resulting in the evolution of highly mineralized Cl-Na type water dominated by halite dissolution. Along the S2→S7 pathway for SW, carbonate minerals exert only a minor control on hydrochemistry, as indicated by slight calcite precipitation and weak dolomite dissolution. Gypsum, halite, and sylvite dissolution provide the main sources of Ca2+, SO42−, Na+, Cl−, and K+, while cation exchange further promotes Na+ enrichment. As a result, the hydrochemical type evolves from the HCO3 type toward the SO4-Na type. Along the S7→S12 pathway, continued evaporite dissolution and enhanced cation exchange dominate the system, leading to further Na+ enrichment and driving the water chemistry from the HCO3 type to the Cl-Na type.

During the wet season, the overall hydrochemical evolution pattern is broadly consistent with that in the dry season, with GW and SW still mainly controlled by evaporite dissolution, carbonate reactions, and cation exchange, although some differences exist in both reaction intensity and local transformation direction. Along the S2→S7 pathway for GW, halite and gypsum dissolution remain the dominant controlling processes, accompanied by dolomite dissolution, calcite precipitation, and minor CO2 input. Along the S7→S12 pathway, halite dissolution remains dominant, whereas CO2 changes from input during the dry season to weak degassing during the wet season, and CaX2 also changes from precipitation to dissolution. Along the S2→S7 pathway for SW, the controlling mechanisms are generally similar to those in the dry season, with halite and gypsum dissolution still dominating, although the overall reaction intensity is slightly lower than that in the dry season. At the same time, sylvite changes from dissolution in the dry season to weak precipitation in the wet season. Along the S7→S12 pathway, halite dissolution, gypsum and dolomite dissolution, and cation exchange are all enhanced, while CO2 changes from degassing in the dry season to weak input in the wet season.

Inverse hydrogeochemical modeling indicates that the dominant processes controlling SW and GW evolution along the flow direction include calcite precipitation, dissolution of evaporite minerals (gypsum, halite, and sylvite), and forward cation exchange. These results are consistent with the hydrochemical analyses discussed above. From a seasonal perspective, the overall hydrochemical evolution pattern during the wet season is broadly consistent with that during the dry season, although the intensity of individual reaction processes and the local transformation directions differ to some extent. In both the upper and lower reaches, SW and GW exhibit similar evolutionary trends, shifting from the HCO3 type toward the SO4/Cl-Na type. Compared with SW, GW experiences stronger evaporite dissolution and carbonate precipitation, resulting in higher mineralization, whereas SW is moderated by runoff dilution and thus shows weaker chemical transformation. Overall, despite differences in reaction intensity, SW and GW maintain a relatively close hydraulic connection.

Transformation between surface water and groundwater

Long-term agricultural irrigation has intensified GW overexploitation in arid plains, altering flow paths and SW-GW interactions and thereby influencing hydrochemical evolution.1 Because Cl− behaves conservatively and TDS reflects transport pathways and residence time, their spatiotemporal variations provide effective indicators of SW-GW transformation (Figures 6A and 6B). The hydrochemical characteristics along the flow path during the dry season exhibited clear segmental differences. In the upstream reaches (S1–S3), TDS and Cl− in SW showed little variation, while Cl− in GW decreased slightly, indicating slow recharge of GW by SW in this region. In the middle reaches (S4–S9), Cl− and TDS in SW increased gradually along the flow path. Given that Cl− and TDS in GW were generally higher than those in SW, this suggests that the reach was overall characterized by GW recharging SW at a relatively slow rate. However, at sections S4–S6, Cl− and TDS in GW decreased markedly, indicating that a certain degree of mixing occurred between GW and SW within this local reach. In the downstream reaches (S10–S12), at sections S10 and S11, Cl− and TDS in GW decreased synchronously with those in SW. Considering that GW concentrations were significantly higher than those in SW, this indicates that SW in this reach was significantly recharged by GW. Quantification of surface water-groundwater interaction

In the 1960s, Craig first investigated hydrogen and oxygen isotopes in global freshwater and found a pronounced linear relationship between hydrogen and oxygen isotopes in global atmospheric precipitation, with the fitted equation δ2H = 8δ18O + 10. This equation was later defined as the global meteoric water line (GMWL), which serves as an important reference for studying variations in hydrogen and oxygen isotopes in regional water cycle processes.36 In addition, based on the precipitation data from the study area, this study fitted the local meteoric water line (LMWL) as δ2H = 8.38δ18O + 11.42. From the δ2H–δ18O relationship diagram of SW and GW in the basin (Figures 7A and 7B), it can be observed that the LMWL lies entirely below the GMWL, indicating that regional precipitation is strongly affected by evaporation. The evaporation lines of both types of water lie below the LMWL, indicating that regional SW and GW have undergone pronounced evaporative isotopic fractionation. Based on the qualitative analysis of SW-GW interactions (Figures 6C and 6D), the upstream region of the Cherchen River Basin during the dry season is mainly characterized by SW recharging GW, with SW contribution rates ranging from 59.2% to 68.4%. In the midstream region, GW contribution rates range from 24.7% to 51.0%, whereas at section S7, SW recharges GW, with a contribution rate of 60.2%. In the downstream region, SW contribution rates range from 63.9% to 82.4%. During the wet season, the upstream region of the Cherchen River Basin continues to be characterized by SW recharging GW, with SW contribution rates ranging from 75.0% to 81.4%. From the midstream to downstream region, section S10 is characterized by GW discharging to SW, with GW contribution rates ranging from 17.1% to 63.4%. In the downstream S11–S12 sections, SW recharges GW, with SW contribution rates ranging from 41.6% to 73.1%.

The recharge proportions within the basin exhibit pronounced spatiotemporal variability, with the SW contribution rate in the upstream region during the dry season (average 64.0%) being lower than that during the wet season (77.2%). In the midstream region, the SW contribution rate during the dry season (average 38.0%) is lower than that during the wet season (44.3%), partly because increased channel runoff in the wet season and agricultural irrigation exert a certain influence on GW levels. In the downstream region, the average SW contribution rate during the dry season (74.9%) is higher than that during the wet season (66.1%), partly because GW experiences stronger evaporation and increased SW recharge during the summer wet season.

Hydrological and hydrochemical patterns of Cherchen river basin

Based on the previous findings, a conceptual model of the spatiotemporal and seasonal patterns of hydrochemical evolution and SW-GW interactions in the Cherchen River Basin is proposed (Figure S4). The transformation patterns between SW and GW in the study area exhibit pronounced seasonal variations. During the dry season, SW is affected by strong evaporation and GW discharge, while GW is influenced by surface runoff and irrigation infiltration. During the wet season, the recharge from SW to GW becomes stronger. In addition, pronounced spatial variations are observed, with GW in the upper and lower reaches being mainly recharged by SW, while in the middle reaches GW primarily discharges to SW. Under this hydrological pattern, the hydrochemistry of the basin’s GW and SW exhibits high consistency along the flow path, and the hydrochemical characteristics are primarily influenced by evaporation, human activities, and water-rock interactions. Owing to strong evaporation, TDS increases along the flow path, with GW generally exhibiting higher TDS than SW.

On this basis, we propose the following recommendations for water resource protection in the Cherchen River Basin and other inland river basins in arid regions: (1) Implement zoned management of water resources across different river sections, reduce water diversion and interception in the upper and lower reaches to prevent further declines in GW levels, and strengthen the management of GW overexploitation in the middle reaches by limiting GW extraction; (2) Monitor whether the reverse input of GW recharging river water during the dry season leads to changes in river water salinity; (3) Develop seasonal water resource management strategies by appropriately increasing the proportion of SW used for agricultural irrigation and ecological purposes during the wet season, and by controlling GW extraction during the dry season to reduce pressure on GW recharge; (4) Strengthen cross-regional coordinated management. Given the close hydraulic connection between the Cherchen River Basin and the Tarim River Basin, establish a unified regional water resource allocation model to rationally distribute water from the Tarim River and the Cherchen River, thereby improving water-use efficiency while maintaining the ecological environment of Lake Taitema and its surrounding areas.

Despite its unique regional characteristics, the hydrological structure of the Cherchen River Basin—characterized by runoff generation in mountainous areas, water consumption in oases, and GW regulation and storage—represents a typical water cycle pattern in inland river basins of Northwest China and arid endorheic regions of Central Asia.37,38,39 In such basins, precipitation and snow/ice meltwater from upstream mountainous regions constitute the primary sources of river runoff, while GW recharge in downstream oasis areas heavily depends on river channel infiltration, canal system leakage, and irrigation return flow, all of which are strongly influenced by intense evaporation and agricultural water extraction. Consequently, the seasonal variations in SW-GW exchange directions, the differential contributions of various water sources to GW recharge, and the enhanced water-salt migration processes during the irrigation period identified in this study can provide valuable references for understanding GW recharge mechanisms and salinization risk formation in similar basins.

From a methodological perspective, the integrated research framework combining hydrochemical analysis, stable isotope tracing, and EMMA is highly applicable to arid basins with limited long-term monitoring data. By synchronously collecting river water, GW, and irrigation water samples across different seasons, this approach enables the identification of major recharge sources, quantification of the contributions from different end-members to GW, and assessment of seasonal differences in SW-GW interactions and water-salt migration processes. Therefore, this framework can be extended to the Shule River, Heihe River, Tarim River, and similar inland river basins in Central Asia for GW recharge source identification, salinization risk assessment, and irrigation water management. For these basins, which are facing climate warming, changes in snow and ice melt, and increasing agricultural water demand, this study further emphasizes the need to implement zoned and seasonally differentiated water allocation strategies based on upstream-downstream hydrological connectivity, irrigation seasons, and GW recharge conditions.

Limitations and future perspectives

The EMMA model adopted in this study is subject to some uncertainty because end-member compositions vary across time and space, the isotopic signatures of different end-members may overlap, and isotopic fractionation under strong evaporative conditions may interfere with mixing relationships. In addition, the three-end-member model is inherently a simplification of complex hydrological processes and cannot fully reflect local heterogeneity or short-term hydrological fluctuations. Future research should further strengthen sampling and monitoring across different periods and river reaches so as to further improve the reliability of the model results.

The inverse hydrogeochemical modeling in this study included carbonate minerals, evaporite minerals, and cation-exchange processes, but did not incorporate silicate minerals. Although hydrochemical analysis suggests that silicate weathering contributes to the ionic composition, key constraints such as dissolved Si and Al were not measured. Because PHREEQC inverse modeling relies on elemental mass balance, the lack of these data makes it difficult to reliably constrain silicate dissolution-precipitation processes. Therefore, silicate weathering in this study was interpreted mainly from hydrochemical characteristics rather than quantitatively resolved in the inverse model. Future work should include dissolved Si and Al measurements to better constrain silicate weathering and its role in SW-GW interactions.

Method details

Study area

The Cherchen River is one of the headwaters of the Tarim River, located in the southern part of the Tarim Basin (36°30′–39°15′ N, 83°30′–85°15′ E), and originates from Muztag Peak on the northern slope of the Kunlun Mountains. It flows through Qiemo County and Ruoqiang County, and enters Lake Taitema in Ruoqiang County, where it connects with the Tarim River Basin, with a multi-year average runoff of 8 × 108 m3.40 The Cherchen River has a total length of 813 km and a drainage area of 4.74 × 104km2. The basin exhibits complex geomorphological characteristics, with elevations decreasing from south to north, and consists of four geomorphic units from south to north: mountainous areas, alluvial–proluvial fans, alluvial plains, and desert. The region has a warm temperate, extremely arid continental climate, with a mean annual temperature of 10.9°C, a mean annual precipitation of 25.7 mm, and a mean annual evaporation of 1526.2 mm, which is mainly concentrated from May to August and accounts for approximately 62% of the annual evaporation.

The river system of the Cherchen River Basin is mainly composed of the Cherchen River along with other small rivers and intermittent mountain-flood gullies, and the Cherchen River is the largest river within the basin. The geological structure of the study area is mainly composed of Quaternary alluvial–proluvial deposits and aeolian deposits. The alluvial–proluvial deposits are primarily distributed within the basin, with grain size decreasing from coarse to fine along the river flow direction, and are composed of complex mixtures of gravel, sand, and clay. The aeolian deposits are extensively distributed across the basin except along the riverbanks and the county town area, and are mainly composed of quartz silt. The groundwater occurrence types in the Cherchen River Basin mainly include two categories: fractured bedrock water in mountainous areas and pore water in Quaternary loose rock formations. Among them, fractured bedrock water is unevenly distributed, while pore water in Quaternary loose rock formations is distributed in the piedmont plain area. This study did not involve human participants, animal models, or cell lines.

Sample collection and measurement

Two field sampling campaigns were conducted in this study in April 2025 (dry season) and August 2025 (wet season) (Figure 1). A total of 57 groundwater samples and 29 surface water samples were collected. Specifically, 31 groundwater samples and 17 surface water samples were collected in April, while 26 groundwater samples and 12 surface water samples were collected in August. The surface water sampling sites in August were selected based on those in April, with some duplicate or closely spaced sites removed. The groundwater sampling sites were intended to remain consistent with those in April; however, some wells were blocked or had been shut down in August, resulting in a reduced number of groundwater samples during the dry season. To ensure data quality, duplicate field samples were additionally collected for each sampling site for quality control. The precision of duplicate samples was evaluated using the relative percent difference (RPD). The differences between paired duplicate measurements were small, and the RPD values were generally less than 5%, indicating good consistency. The sampling sites were arranged along the main course of the Cherchen River from upstream to downstream. The spacing between surface water sampling sites was greater than 5 km to ensure the representativeness of different river reaches, while groundwater sampling sites were distributed within 2 km on both sides of the river channel, with a spacing of approximately 500 m, to reflect the hydraulic connection and spatial gradient characteristics between surface water and groundwater. Among them, surface water samples were collected from the main flow zone in the center of the river channel to avoid interference from stagnant water along the banks; prior to groundwater sampling, a water pump was used to continuously pump water to discharge the stagnant water retained in the well, and samples were taken after the outflow stabilized to ensure sample representativeness. All samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane and sealed in brown polyethylene bottles pre-rinsed with deionized water to prevent external contamination. During sampling, residual air bubbles in the sampling bottles were avoided to reduce oxidation. On-site measurements of water temperature, pH, and electrical conductivity (EC) were carried out using a portable multi-parameter water quality meter. The samples were refrigerated at 4°C and delivered to the laboratory for analysis within one week. The analytical items included pH, Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, HCO3−, SO42−, Cl−, CO32−, total hardness, total alkalinity, total dissolved solids (TDS), and the stable isotopes δ2H and δ18O. The concentrations of dissolved cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) were determined using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Optima 2100DV, USA), while anions (Cl− and SO42−) were analyzed using an ion chromatograph (ICS-900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentrations of HCO3− and CO32− were determined using acid–base titration. The hydrogen–oxygen stable isotopes were analyzed using a Picarro L2140-i water isotope analyzer (USA) with the WS-CRDS method. Each sample was measured with six injections, and the average of the last three injections was used as the analytical result. The analytical precision was ±0.4‰ for δ2H and ±0.1‰ for δ18O. The charge-balance error calculated using PHREEQC was used to verify the reliability of the hydrochemical data. For most samples, the charge-balance error was less than ±5%.

Deuterium excess (d-excess) and line-conditioned (lc-excess)

Deuterium excess was first proposed by Dansgaard41 and reflects the extent of stable hydrogen–oxygen isotope fractionation in precipitation within the study area relative to Global Meteoric Water Line (GMWL).42 The calculation method is as follows:(Equation 10)d−excess=δ2H−8·δ18O

The lc-excess index was proposed by Landwehr43 and can characterize the evaporation degree of precipitation in the study area relative to local precipitation. It has been widely used to determine the evaporation degree of different water bodies relative to precipitation input. Typically, variations in lc-excess in local precipitation are mainly influenced by different moisture sources, with an annual average value of 0‰. Because the stable isotopes in other water bodies are enriched by evaporation, their average lc-excess is usually below 0. When a positive lc-excess value appears in a water sample, it indicates that the sample may be influenced by water sources other than precipitation. The smaller the lc-excess value of the sample, the stronger the evaporative fractionation. The calculation method is as follows:(Equation 11)lc−excess=δ2H−(a×δ18O+b)In the equation, a and b represent the slope and intercept of the local meteoric water line, respectively, and δ2H and δ18O are the stable isotope values in the water sample.

Mineral saturation index

The mineral saturation index (SI) represents the equilibrium state between minerals and water. In this study, the mineral saturation indices (SI) of 86 samples were calculated using the PHREEQC 3.7.3 software. The calculation formula is as follows:(Equation 12)SI=lg(IAPK)

In the equation, IAP represents the ionic activity product, and K is the equilibrium constant of the mineral dissolution reaction. When SI = 0, it indicates that the mineral and water are in equilibrium; when SI > 0, it indicates that the system is supersaturated; and when SI < 0, it indicates that the system is undersaturated.10

End-Member Mixing Analysis

End-Member Mixing Analysis (EMMA) is widely used to investigate the potential recharge sources of different water bodies by utilizing their chemical and isotopic compositions.44 Various tracers, such as Cl−, stable isotopes, and integrated hydrochemical indicators, can be used as end-members in EMMA. Because δD and δ18O in water are sensitive to environmental changes and mixing processes among different water bodies, and generally exhibit relatively good conservativeness and stability, they have strong tracer identification capability and are considered ideal tracers for studying water circulation processes, and have been widely applied in hydrological process investigations and water source identification studies.45 Therefore, δD and δ18O were selected in this study as tracers to construct the mixing model. The model assumes that variations in end-member contributions are mainly controlled by water mixing processes rather than runoff processes.46 In this study, this assumption is considered applicable to some extent in the plain area of the Cherchen River basin. Within the study area, no tributaries flow into the river, and external runoff inputs are relatively limited. In addition, the hydraulic connection between SW and GW was strong, and variations in isotopic compositions were generally jointly controlled by upstream inflow, precipitation infiltration, and SW–GW exchange processes. Therefore, in this study, the three end-members were defined as precipitation, surface water from the upstream river channel, and groundwater. A three-end-member mass-balance mixing model was then used to quantify their contributions to river runoff, with all calculations implemented in R. The calculation equations are as follows:(Equation 13)Xs=F1X1+......+FnXn

In the equation, X represents the tracer concentration used to estimate the contribution of each water source. The subscript s refers to the sampled river water at the study site, while n refers to each potential end-member source. F denotes the proportional contribution of each end-member to the river water sample.47

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph preparation were performed using Microsoft Excel, OriginPro 2021, R, ArcGIS 10.8, and PHREEQC 3.7.3. In this study, n represents the number of independent field water samples. A total of 86 samples were analyzed, including 57 groundwater samples and 29 surface-water samples. Specifically, 31 groundwater and 17 surface-water samples were collected in the dry season, and 26 groundwater and 12 surface-water samples were collected in the wet season. The hydrochemical parameters of the collected water samples are summarized in Figure S1 of the Supplemental Information. Detailed statistical results are provided in the corresponding figure legends and results section.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize hydrochemical and isotopic characteristics. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess relationships among major ions and TDS, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Mineral saturation indices and charge-balance errors were calculated using PHREEQC 3.7.3, and inverse hydrogeochemical modeling was conducted to quantify water–rock interaction processes. End-Member Mixing Analysis was performed in R using δ2H and δ18O as tracers to estimate the proportional contributions of precipitation, surface water, and groundwater. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No randomization or blinding was applied because this was a field-based hydrochemical and isotopic study.
