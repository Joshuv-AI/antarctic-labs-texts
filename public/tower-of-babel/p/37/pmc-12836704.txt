DNA methylation markers for sensitive detection of circulating tumor DNA in patients with gastroesophageal cancers
Authors: N. Øgaard, C.R. Iden, S.Ø. Jensen, S.M. Mustafa, E. Aagaard, J.B. Bramsen, L.B. Ahlborn, J.P. Hasselby, K.S. Rohrberg, M.P. Achiam, C.L. Andersen, M. Mau-Sørensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with gastric and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinomas (G-GEJ ACs) face poor outcomes. Thus sensitive biomarkers for improved clinical management are highly warranted. Detection of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) using DNA methylation biomarkers is a highly sensitive approach for cancer detection and management. Here, we explored the potential of a tumor-agnostic test targeting DNA methylation to detect ctDNA in patients with resectable and advanced G-GEJ ACs.Material and methodsA tumor-agnostic digital PCR test—TriMeth—targeting the gastrointestinal cancer-specific methylated genes C9orf50, KCNQ5, and CLIP4 was carried out on a total of 131 study patients. DNA from surgical tumor specimens of 29 patients with G-GEJ ACs and plasma cell-free DNA from 52 patients with advanced and resectable G-GEJ ACs, and from 50 healthy controls, were analyzed.ResultsMethylated tumor DNA was detected by TriMeth in all of the surgical tumor specimens (29/29, 100%). Furthermore, TriMeth detected ctDNA in plasma from 31/52 (60%) patients with G-GEJ AC, including in 13/17 (76%) advanced cases, and 18/35 (51%) resectable cases. ctDNA was not detected in healthy controls (0/50, 0%).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that TriMeth may hold potential as a biomarker for identifying ctDNA in patients with G-GEJ ACs. The study sets the scene for ongoing larger clinical studies investigating the performance of TriMeth in different clinical settings.

Introduction

In 2020, gastric and gastroesophageal junction (G-GEJ) cancers were responsible for >1.3 million deaths worldwide, making it the third leading cause of cancer-related death globally.1,2 Up to 90% of gastric and GEJ cancers are adenocarcinomas (ACs). Gastric and GEJ ACs (G-GEJ ACs) share many common clinicopathological traits, with GEJ ACs being the most prevalent. Patients with G-GEJ ACs carry a dismal prognosis and are often diagnosed at an advanced stage.3 This limits the curative potential of available therapeutic interventions as reflected by the poor 5-year survival rate of 20% across stages.4 Even patients with resectable disease have a 5-year survival of <50%.5 Hence there is an urgent need for sensitive biomarkers for the surveillance of recurrence and monitoring of treatment benefits in patients with G-GEJ ACs.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a surrogate for tumor burden. ctDNA is released to the bloodstream from dying tumor cells and can be quantified from routinely collected peripheral blood, allowing minimally invasive assessment.6,7 In recent years, the detection of ctDNA through DNA methylation alterations has broken new grounds for cancer diagnostics and surveillance.8, 9, 10 In contrast to tumor-informed strategies, ctDNA methylation detection has the major advantage of being tumor-agnostic, that is, not requiring prior tumor tissue analysis.

We have previously shown that the detection of ctDNA using a multiplex digital PCR test targeting the gastrointestinal cancer-specific DNA methylation markers C9orf50, KCNQ5, and CLIP4 (the TriMeth test) is a highly sensitive approach for the early detection of colorectal cancer (CRC) and CRC recurrence.9,11,12 If this method can also provide sensitive detection of G-GEJ ACs, it could potentially improve the clinical management of patients with G-GEJ ACs.

Here, we investigated the performance of the TriMeth test in tissue from patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs, and furthermore, explored the potential of TriMeth to detect ctDNA in plasma from patients with resectable and advanced G-GEJ ACs.

Material and methods

Study design

This is a retrospective study exploring the performance of the TriMeth test in tumor tissue and plasma from patients with G-GEJ ACs. Because of the explorative design, no sample size estimation was carried out. A brief study overview is illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 131 study patients were included; tumor tissue was collected from 29 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs, while plasma was collected from 17 patients with advanced disease, 35 patients with resectable disease, and from 50 healthy individuals (Figure 1). Patients and samples

Freshly frozen surgical tumor specimens were collected from 29 patients undergoing surgery for G-GEJ ACs between 2014 and 2016 at the Department of Surgery and Transplantation at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. All 29 patients received curatively intended, standard-of-care treatment (i.e. perioperative chemotherapy and surgical resection).

Plasma samples were collected from a total of 102 individuals, including 17 patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs, 35 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs, and 50 healthy individuals.

The 17 patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs were referred to The Phase 1 Unit at The Department of Oncology at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. These patients were included in the Copenhagen Prospective Personalized Oncology (CoPPO) study between 2018 and 2019, with the aim to allocate patients with exhausted treatment options to early clinical trials of targeted therapies based on genomic profiling.13,14 Plasma was collected at the time of referral to The Phase 1 Unit.

The 35 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs were included in the prospective study Clinical Utility of Circulating tumor DNA (CURE)15 at The Department of Oncology at Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. These patients received curatively intended treatment with perioperative chemotherapy with eight cycles of docetaxel, oxaliplatin, and fluorouracil/leucovorin (FLOT) and surgical resection according to national guidelines.16,17 Plasma was collected before oncological treatment.

As negative controls, 50 noncancer individuals from the Danish National CRC Screening Program were included.18 These all had a plasma sample collected in relation to a positive fecal immunochemical test. All individuals showed a clean colon on the subsequent colonoscopy, and none of them had any comorbidities or previous diagnoses of cancer.

The Ethics Committee of the Capital Region Denmark approved the collection and use of biological samples (j. nos. H-16046103; H-4-2013-050; H-2-2013-101; H-19076846; H-20033168) and the capture of clinical data from patient journals (j. nos. H-21052982; H-1300530; H-16046103). Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients. The Danish Data Protection Agency (j. nos. HVH-2013-022/2007-58-0015; P-2019-701) approved the study.

Methylation array data

DNA methylation data generated by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project including esophagus cancer (ESCA), stomach AC (STAD), and CRC (COAD + READ) samples were acquired from the GDC data portal (v1.30.0; Data Release 34.0, on 20 March 2023) using the following filters: Data Type = ‘Methylation Beta Value’, Platform = ‘illumina human methylation 450’, Workflow Type = ‘SeSAMe Methylation Beta Estimation’, ‘Data Format’, Samples Is FFPE = ‘FALSE’.19 Only primary tumor and normal samples were included. TCGA File IDs are available in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104. The histological type of the tumors was previously defined by Liu et al.20 Methylation array data from 556 blood cell samples were downloaded and processed as previously published by Jensen et al.11 Beta-values (the ratio of the methylated probe intensity and the overall intensity) for each sample were evaluated for each of the TriMeth marker candidate CpG sites (C9orf50 probe: cg14015706, CLIP4 probe: cg08808128, and KCNQ5 probe: cg24687051), which were identified by Jensen et al.11

DNA methylation analysis (TriMeth)

The three TriMeth markers were identified and selected in a previous study using DNA methylation array data from >5000 samples for bioinformatics marker discovery, described in detail by Jensen et al.11 Here, the TriMeth marker assays were developed, tested, and validated in tissue and plasma from patients with CRC. In the present study, TriMeth analysis was carried out according to previous procedures11,12 using methylation-specific droplet digital PCR assays targeting the promoter regions of C9orf50, CLIP4, and KCNQ5 and a quantification assay (cytosine-free assay). All TriMeth assays are listed in Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104. dMIQE checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104. Details on sodium bisulfite conversion and droplet digital PCR can be found in Supplementary Materials and Methods S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104. Each analysis included a positive control (5 ng of fully methylated DNA; Nordic Biosite, Täby, Sweden), a negative control (66 ng of fully unmethylated DNA; Nordic Biosite), and a nontemplate control. For each plate, a threshold was determined by evaluation of the positive and negative controls as previously described.11,12

Data analysis

A TriMeth score was assigned to each sample using a predefined cut-off classifying a sample as ‘positive’ if more than one positive droplet was recorded for two out of three TriMeth markers.11,12

For the tissue samples (Figure 2), the input DNA [in genome equivalents (GEs)] for the TriMeth analysis was assessed for each sample by a cytosine-free DNA quantification assay which provides a measure of the total number of DNA copies in the analysis quantification digital PCR assay (Supplementary Material and Methods S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104). For each marker, we calculated the methylated allele frequency (AF) as ‘the number of methylated DNA copies/the input DNA’. The mean methylation AF (mean meth AF) was calculated as the mean of the three marker’s methylated AFs. This measure illustrates how much of the analyzed DNA on average was methylated in the given sample (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104). The relative methylation levels (calculated as the methylation signal for an individual marker/sum of methylation signal for all three markers; Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104) provide a measure of the relative abundance of methylation at the three markers. Spearman analysis was carried out to estimate the correlation between histologically evaluated tumor cell density and methylated AF assessed by TriMeth. Results

In this study, we investigated the performance of three DNA methylation markers—the TriMeth test—in tissue and plasma from patients with G-GEJ AC. An overview of the study workflow is presented in Figure 1. We included 29 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs for tumor tissue analysis. All 29 patients were treated with standard-of-care perioperative chemotherapy and resection. Their median age was 71 years, 82.8% were male, and in 93.1% of the patients, the tumor was localized in the lower esophagus or GEJ. For plasma analysis, we included 35 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs. All 35 patients received curatively intended perioperative chemotherapy and resection. Their median age was 66 years, 85.7% were male, and in 91.4% of the patients, the tumor was localized in the lower esophagus or GEJ. For plasma analysis, 17 patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs were also included. Their median age was 60 years, 82.4% were male, and 58.8% of tumors were located in the lower esophagus or the GEJ. Finally, we included plasma from 50 noncancer control individuals. The median age of the healthy controls was 62 years, and 76.0% were male. Methylation levels of TriMeth markers across gastrointestinal cancers

To gain insights into the overall methylation levels of the TriMeth markers across gastrointestinal cancers, we carried out an in silico assessment of the marker methylation levels using publicly available DNA methylation array data (Figure 4). All three markers were negligibly methylated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and minimally methylated in most normal epithelial samples from esophageal, gastric, and colorectal tissues (Figure 4). All three markers were highly methylated in CRC samples (medians of 0.61 for C9orf50, 0.68 for KCNQ5, and 0.69 for CLIP4). The esophageal cancer samples were divided into the histological subtypes AC (ESAC) and squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). The methylation levels of the TriMeth markers varied greatly between the two subtypes. In ESCC samples, the markers showed no or minimal methylation, whereas all three markers were highly methylated in the majority of ESAC samples. In gastric AC (GAC) samples, all three markers were moderately methylated (medians of 0.51 for C9orf50, 0.57 for KCNQ5, and 0.45 for CLIP4, respectively). For the CLIP4 marker, ∼25% of the ESAC and GAC samples showed no methylation (Figure 4). TriMeth methylation in gastric and GEJ tumor tissue

We analyzed the performance of the TriMeth markers in tumor DNA from 29 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs. Detailed TriMeth data of the 29 tumor samples are presented in Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104.

Methylated DNA was detected by TriMeth in all (29/29, 100%) of the tumor samples (Figure 2A). The relative methylation level of the individual TriMeth markers was plotted to investigate the relative abundance of methylation of the three markers in the tissues. The KCNQ5 and C9orf50 markers showed the most persistent contribution to the TriMeth methylation signal (KCNQ5 median 0.44, interquartile range 0.33-0.52, and C9orf50 median 0.28, interquartile range 0.21-0.40), whereas the CLIP4 relative methylation level varied greatly between the samples (Figure 2A). In 10/29 samples, CLIP4 showed the highest methylation level of the three markers, and in 5/29 samples no CLIP4 methylation was detected. The mean methylated AF assessed by TriMeth ranged from 0.4% to 83.6% (Figure 2A). The mean methylated AF was moderately correlated with the histologically estimated tumor cell density (tumor%) (R = 0.56, P = 0.0016; Figure 2B). In summary, all investigated tumor specimens were TriMeth positive.

TriMeth ctDNA detection in plasma from patients with G-GEJ ACs

Next, we aimed to assess the performance of TriMeth for ctDNA detection in plasma from patients with G-GEJ ACs. For this analysis, we included 17 patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs, 35 patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs, and 50 healthy controls. ctDNA was detected in 31 out of 52 (60%) patients (Figure 3A and B and Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104). For the patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs, 13 out of 17 (76.5%) plasma samples were scored as TriMeth positive. For the patients with resectable G-GEJ ACs, 18 out of 35 (51%) samples were TriMeth positive. The mean methylated AF was significantly higher in patients with node-positive compared with node-negative disease (6.1 versus 0.7; P = 0.009) as shown in Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104. Seven TriMeth-negative patients were positive for one of the three markers. In four of these, the methylation levels were low, but for two samples (GAS8 and GAS9) >500 methylated ctDNA copies were detected by KCNQ5 (Figure 3A and Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2024.100104). The CLIP4 marker was blank in all but one plasma sample from patients with gastric cancer. Four out of fifty plasma samples from the healthy individuals were positive for one out of three TriMeth markers, accordingly, all healthy controls were scored as TriMeth negative (Figure 3A and B).

The performance of the markers evaluated by the area under the curve (AUC) was higher for C9orf50 (AUCs 0.72-0.86) and KCNQ5 (AUCs 0.78-0.92) than for CLIP4 (AUCs 0.59-0.66) across all patients regardless of disease type and stage (Figure 3C). The AUC of TriMeth for all cases was 0.80.

In summary, TriMeth detected ctDNA in plasma from patients with G-GEJ ACs with high sensitivity, whereas all plasma samples from the healthy controls were negative.

Discussion

In the past decades, multiple tissue-based biomarkers enabling personalized management of solid cancers have been developed. Recently, blood-based biomarkers, such as ctDNA, have shown promising potential to take personalized cancer management to the next level. ctDNA-based biomarkers have particularly promising potential in clinical settings such as detection of recurrence or minimal residual disease and selection and monitoring of targeted therapy.21 So far, no ctDNA-based biomarkers have been identified and validated for routine clinical use in patients with G-GEJ AC.22 Here, we investigated the performance of a previously developed tumor-agnostic test, TriMeth, targeting gastrointestinal DNA hypermethylation in tissue and plasma from patients with G-GEJ ACs and demonstrate that TriMeth may hold potential for robust identification of ctDNA in patients with G-GEJ AC.

To investigate the TriMeth marker methylation levels across gastrointestinal cancers, we analyzed 450K methylation array data and showed that the TriMeth markers were highly methylated in CRC, ESAC, and GAC, whereas the ESCC samples were unmethylated. Therefore only patients with G-GEJ ACs were included in TriMeth analyses of tissue and plasma, and it should be noted that TriMeth may not be suitable for ctDNA detection in patients with ESCC.

When we investigated TriMeth in G-GEJ AC tumor tissue, all samples (29/29, 100%) were classified as TriMeth positive. While C9orf50 and KCNQ5 were positive in all samples (29/29) and these markers had the strongest contribution to the overall TriMeth methylation signal, the CLIP4 marker was more infrequently methylated (Figure 2). This correlates well with the 450 K array data showing that ∼25% of the GAC samples had no CLIP4 methylation (Figure 4). For future studies, it may be relevant to adjust the TriMeth scoring algorithm or to replace the CLIP4 marker with another marker that is more sensitive in G-GEJ ACs.

The mean methylated AFs measured by TriMeth in the G-GEJ AC tissue samples were generally lower compared with the levels previously reported in CRC tissue.11 This observation was corroborated by the methylation levels from the array data (Figure 4), indicating slightly lower methylation of all three markers in G-GEJ ACs compared with CRC. A potential consequence of the lower methylation levels in G-GEJ AC tissues could be a lower release of methylated ctDNA fragments into the circulation which could potentially challenge the TriMeth detection of plasma ctDNA. However, in plasma, TriMeth detected ctDNA in 60% of all patients, hereof in 76% of patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs, demonstrating a high detection rate. Interestingly, two of the TriMeth-negative patients (GAS8 and GAS9) were positive with >200 copies for KCNQ5. In previous published and unpublished work, a false-positive KCNQ5 signal >5 copies has never been observed (in >1000 analyzed plasma samples from healthy controls).11,12 Hence considering these two samples as positive, the sensitivity of TriMeth in patients with advanced G-GEJ ACs would be 88% (15/17).

KCNQ5 and C9orf50 contributed consistently to the TriMeth positivity in plasma (AUCs of 0.83 and 0.77 for all cases), whereas the combined AUC of the TriMeth test was 0.80. These results confirmed the findings of a recent study by Li et al.23 who tested the CLIP4, C9orf50, and KCNQ5 markers in gastric cancer plasma cohorts by quantitative PCR and reported AUCs of 0.78-0.85.

This research sets the scene for studies unraveling the clinical utility of the TriMeth test within different clinical settings of G-GEJ cancer management, such as detection of minimal residual disease detection after surgery, recurrence surveillance, guidance in treatment selection, and monitoring of treatment effects, which may all contribute to improved patient outcomes.

Although having intriguing potential, a few limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the reported performance of the TriMeth assay applies to the conditions of the present study. The estimated sensitivity is dependent on several study-specific pre- and postanalytic variables such as the applied patient cohort, limited sample size, plasma sampling, and the exact ctDNA quantity in the samples and its recovery. Accordingly, the findings need to be validated in larger independent cohorts to confirm the potential of TriMeth in G-GEJ AC. Second, tissue and plasma were collected from different cohorts, and therefore no matched samples were available, limiting the ability to directly correlate methylation of tumor DNA from tissue and plasma of the same individual. In addition, as tissue from patients with advanced disease was not available, a more comprehensive assessment of the TriMeth assay across the course of disease progression was not feasible. Third, the healthy controls had endoscopic examination of the colon and rectum and not of the upper gastrointestinal tract. Finally, it should be noted that the TriMeth markers and the TriMeth 2/3 scoring algorithm were developed to be sensitive toward early detection of CRC. Therefore it is likely that the TriMeth test needs adjustment for enhanced performance toward G-GEJ AC.

In conclusion, the TriMeth assay detected ctDNA in patients with G-GEJ AC with high sensitivity, elucidating the potential of TriMeth for early detection and detection of recurrence in the clinical management of patients with G-GEJ ACs. Larger clinical studies are needed to demonstrate the utility of TriMeth in different clinical settings during the disease course of patients with G-GEJ ACs.
