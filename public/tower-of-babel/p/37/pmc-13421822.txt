Comparing reported and observed use of insecticide-treated nets in Tanganyika, Democratic Republic of the Congo: a post-campaign longitudinal household study
Authors: Nono Mvuama, Josue Zanga, Osée Y Sanogo, Baudouin Matela, Tiffany Clark, Aklilu Seyoum, Kristin Banek, Emery Metelo, Victoire Nsabatien, Eric Mafuta, Aimée Lulebo, Yung-Ting Bonnenfant, Michael Humes, Ferdinand Ntoya, Sheila Ogoma Barasa, Paul Mansiangi, Romain Jouffroy, Fiacre Agossa, Seth Irish

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate measurement of insecticide-treated net (ITN) use is essential for evaluating malaria control programs. Most routine indicators rely on self-reported ITN use, which may be affected by recall and social desirability biases. This study aimed to compare reported and directly observed ITN use, assess the validity of self-reported indicators, and examine behavioral patterns and net durability following a mass distribution campaign in Tanganyika Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo.MethodsA longitudinal observational study was conducted across four survey rounds between April 2021 and August 2022 in Tanganyika province. Household members were directly observed over three consecutive nights to document ITN use and nighttime behaviors, followed by household interviews. Self-reported ITN use was compared with direct observation at the individual level. Diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and Cohen’s Kappa, were calculated using observation as the reference standard. ITN access, reported use, and physical integrity were also assessed over time.ResultsA total of 72 households (456 individuals at baseline) were included. Self-reported ITN use remained high across rounds (average approximately 91%), whereas observed use ranged from 72.9% to 76.5% when expressed relative to the total surveyed population. Sensitivity of self-reported ITN use was consistently high (97.8–99.4%), while specificity was low (18.7–47.7%), indicating frequent misclassification of non-users as users. Overall accuracy ranged from 79.1% to 85.6%, but agreement between reported and observed use was limited (Cohen’s Kappa: 0.25–0.56). Direct observation revealed that ITN use was dynamic, with incomplete or inconsistent use occurring throughout the night. ITN access declined over time, and physical integrity deteriorated substantially, although most nets remained serviceable. Reported ITN use among children under five years remained consistently high (97–100%), suggesting prioritization within households.ConclusionsSelf-reported ITN use is a highly sensitive but poorly specific indicator, leading to systematic overestimation of effective coverage. ITN use is dynamic and influenced by both access and net condition, which are not fully captured by standard survey indicators. Malaria control programs should interpret self-reported ITN use with caution and consider complementary approaches to better assess actual protection and sustain effective coverage over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05962-5.Keywords: Insecticide treated nets, Malaria, Observed behavior, Reported use, Diagnostic performance metrics, Tanganyika, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Vector control

Background

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) remains one of the countries that is most heavily burdened by malaria, ranking second globally after Nigeria, and accounting for approximately 13% of malaria cases reported worldwide [1]. Malaria remains the leading cause of outpatient consultations in health facilities across the country [2]. While the period between 2000 and 2015 experienced a steady decline in malaria incidence globally, the trend reversed after 2016 [3, 4]. Despite declining malaria-related mortality, the number of cases has surged again in endemic regions across sub-Saharan Africa [2, 5].

In response, national malaria control programs, including that of the DRC, have increased control efforts, most notably the mass distribution of insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs). Over the past two decades, ITNs have become a key intervention in malaria prevention strategies. Large-scale ITN distribution campaigns have contributed significantly to reducing the burden of malaria, by improving household ownership and coverage. It is estimated that ITNs accounted for two-thirds of the averted cases between 2000 and 2015 [3]. However, the ownership of ITNs alone is insufficient. The protective impact of ITNs depends on three inter-dependent components: (i) appropriate insecticide activity, particularly in the context of increasing resistance; (ii) adequate physical integrity to provide mechanical protection; and (iii) correct and consistent use by household members [6–8]. Measuring ITN use reliably remains a fundamental challenge for malaria surveillance systems, because it occurs at night and within the privacy of bedrooms. Consequently, all malaria indicator surveys rely on self-reported data, typically responses to whether a household member slept under a net the previous night (questions such as "Was this net used last night?" and, if so, "which household members used this net last night" are used to determine level of ITN use) [9]. Although widely used, these indicators are subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and normative response bias, especially in settings where ITN use is strongly promoted as a recommended practice [10, 11]. To improve the validity of ITN use estimates, some studies have introduced direct observation methods, wherein trained observers document net usage behaviors in real-time [10]. Net use reported “objectively” included observation of ITN use and hanging nets (as a proxy for regular use) [12, 13]. These objective approaches can help overcome the limitations of self-reporting, such as recall bias and social desirability bias.

Net integrity is another key determinant of ITN use and effectiveness. Damaged nets reduce perceived protection, potentially discouraging users, and increase exposure to mosquito bites. Studies conducted in multiple countries consistently show rapid deterioration of net integrity within 12–24 months post-distribution, particularly in high-use or high-density households [8, 14, 15]. Understanding user behavior therefore requires accounting for net condition, access dynamics, and household sleeping patterns.

In the DRC, indicators of ITN ownership, access, and use have shown substantial variability over time, partly due to the non-synchronized schedule of distribution campaigns across provinces, regional inequities, and supply chain constraints. According to the national Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and Multiple indicator cluster survey (MICS) data, national ITN ownership declined from 69.5% in 2014 to 63.0% in 2018, while population-level access decreased from 47.0% to 44.1%, and reported use remained relatively stable but low (49.8% to 48.4%). In Tanganyika Province, these declines were even more pronounced, with ownership decreasing from 65.9% in 2014 to 43.0% in 2018, and reported use from 47.5% to 36.0% with a population-level access to ITN of 28.3% over the same period [16, 17], which is well below the 80% threshold required for universal coverage [18, 19]. This is despite the fact that, in general, the ratio of use to access to ITNs in the DRC is often greater than one [20]. These trends underscore the importance of understanding how ITN use evolves following distribution campaigns and how accurately it is captured by routine indicators.

This study aimed to assess human behavior related to net use and compare this observed net use with the net use reported in questionnaires in Tanganyika Province, following the 2020 ITN mass distribution campaign. This study focused on the timing, frequency, and seasonality of net use, alongside ownership and access, in two health zones: Kalemie and Manono. The goal was to evaluate the validity and the reliability of self-reported data by quantifying any difference between the reported and observed net use (sensitivity, specificity and accuracy) and identify potential barriers to effective net use.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in the peri-urban areas of Kalemie and Manono (Fig. 1), two health zones (HZs) located in Tanganyika Province, DRC. A mass ITN distribution campaign took place in December 2020, during which two types of ITNs were provided: Safenet® (alpha-cypermethrin) in Kalemie and Veeralin® (alpha-cypermethrin combined with piperonyl butoxide (PBO), a synergist) in Manono. Tanganyika is a province with more than three million inhabitants and is situated along the western shores of Lake Tanganyika, one of Africa's largest lakes. It has a tropical climate, with a rainy season extending from September to May and a dry season from June to August. Malaria transmission remains high throughout the year, with peak rainfall typically occurring from March–April and November-January. In 2020, the malaria incidence was 54%, ranking it among the regions with the highest burden in the DRC [2]. Kalemie and Manono was selected due their operational importance, accessibility for longitudinal follow-up, relative demographic stability and ongoing ITN durability monitoring and entomological surveillance activities coordinated by the U.S. President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI) through the VectorLink project. In each health zone, three villages were purposively selected based on their population density, housing structure, and behavioral patterns in these peri-urban areas which made them particularly relevant for evaluating nighttime ITN behaviors. Within each village, 12 households were randomly sampled, resulting in a total of 72 households across both Kalemie and Manono.

Study design, timeline and sample size considerations

A longitudinal observational study was conducted across four rounds of data collection following the 2020 mass ITN distribution campaign: April 2021 (4 months post-distribution), November 2021 (11 months post-distribution), February 2022 (14 months post-distribution), and August 2022 (20 months post-distribution). The April, November, and February surveys were conducted during the rainy season, whereas the August survey took place during the dry season. Each round consisted of three consecutive nights of behavioral observation, followed by a household interview conducted on day after the third night.

The sample size was initially powered to detect an absolute difference of 15 percentage points between reported (proportion of population with access to nets that report having used nets for any period through the night) and the observed (proportion of population observed that use nets for any period through the night) net use, using the Fleiss formula for comparing two proportions. This approach was adopted as a pragmatic planning tool within the operational framework of the longitudinal household survey, where logistical feasibility and field implementation constraints were key considerations. However, the analytical structure of the final dataset differs from this initial simplifying assumption. Reported and directly observed ITN use were measured for the same individuals, resulting in paired binary observations, and reported ITN use for all household members was provided by a single respondent per household, introducing intra-household correlation and a clustered data structure. Consequently, the assumptions underlying sample size formulas for independent proportions are not fully applicable to the present analysis. Considering this, the validity analyses presented in this study (including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and agreement (Cohen’s Kappa)) were conducted on the full set of available paired observations collected across the four survey rounds. This pragmatic analytical approach was considered appropriate for addressing the primary objective of the study, namely, to assess the field validity of self-reported ITN-use indicators against direct observation under real-world post-campaign conditions, while acknowledging the paired and clustered nature of the data.

Eligibility criteria

Households were eligible for inclusion if they had at least an ITN within their household at the baseline, were permanent residents of the selected villages, agreed to participate in repeated follow-up across survey rounds, and had household members regularly sleeping in the dwelling. Households were excluded if they declined participation, were temporarily absent during the survey period, or could not reasonably be followed across rounds. When a selected household was unavailable or refused participation, a replacement household was selected from the same village.

Behavioral observations

In each consenting household, an adult member, usually the head of household or a primary caregiver, was recruited and trained to serve as the observer. These individuals were literate and compensated for their participation. The observers recorded the activities of the family members (not including the observer) every 30 min from 7:00 PM to 7:00 AM, via a standardized observation worksheet. To ensure consistency, the observers used timers that signal every half hour.

Activities were classified by location and type:

Outdoors: talking, cooking, eating, resting, or working.

Outdoors: talking, cooking, eating, resting, or working.

Sitting room: watching TV, sleeping under an ITN, or engaging in similar activities.

Sitting room: watching TV, sleeping under an ITN, or engaging in similar activities.

Bedroom: sleeping under an ITN or performing other tasks.

Bedroom: sleeping under an ITN or performing other tasks.

Correct ITN use was defined as complete coverage of the sleeper’s body under the net for the full duration of sleep. Local supervisors were available during the observation period to ensure protocol adherence and provide technical support to the observers, particularly during the early evening hours. This approach allowed for detailed characterization of nighttime behavior, but also introduces potential limitations, including observer fatigue and reactivity (Hawthorne effect), which are addressed in the discussion.

Household questionnaire

On day four of each round, the morning after the third night of observation, a structured questionnaire adapted from the Malaria Indicator Surveys (MIS) toolkit [https://malariasurveys.org/toolkit.cfm] was administered to a household respondent other than the observer. Self-reported ITN use was assessed using the standard question referring to the previous night only, ensuring temporal alignment with observed behavior. Socioeconomic characteristics; perceptions of malaria risk; and behavioral and attitudinal factors related to ITN use were included as well.

Assessment of ITN physical integrity

Physical integrity is an important factor in the efficacy of ITN and can be a barrier to its use by household members. All ITNs present in the household at the time of the survey were physically inspected to assess their condition. Holes were categorized by diameter according to WHO Guidelines [21]. The number of holes of each size category was summarized in the questionnaire separately for the sides and the roof. The presence and number of repaired holes were noted but not included in the hole count. Data management and statistical analysis

Data were collected using SurveyCTO® (SurveyCTO Server v2.80.8 Copyright © Dobility, Inc. 2012–23, Cambridge, MA, USA) on Android tablets and exported in CSV format. Data management and statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software (v4.2.2; R Core Team 2022) [22]. An ITN was defined as any insecticide-treated bed net, or any conventional bed net treated with insecticides within the last twelve months.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize household characteristics, ITN ownership, access, and use. Household-level access was defined per national guidelines as one ITN for every two people in the household, and the population-level access as the proportion of individuals with access to an ITN within their household.

Comparisons of ITN-use across survey rounds and between HZ were primarily descriptive. Because reported and observed ITN-use were measured for the same individuals, analyses were conducted within a paired-data framework. In addition, results were interpreted with consideration of within-household clustering arising from the use of a single respondent to report ITN-use for all household members.

To evaluate the validity of reported ITN-use, direct observation was used as the operational reference standard under field conditions. Sensitivity, specificity and accuracy were calculated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Agreement between reported and observed ITN-use beyond chance was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient.

Given the modest sample size, the repeated follow-up structure, and the within-household clustering of reported responses, formal hypothesis testing for changes across survey rounds was minimized. Results were therefore interpreted primarily descriptively, with emphasis placed on diagnostic validity measures rather than inferential comparisons across rounds. Where inferential comparisons were explored, findings were interpreted cautiously in light of the paired and clustered structure of the data. A two-sided significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used for any statistical tests performed.

Ethical clearance

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Kinshasa School of Public Health, (approval number ESP/CE/39/2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating households prior to data collection. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw at any time.

The mean household size was 6.3 ± 2.1 individuals and was slightly higher in Manono than in Kalemie. Most survey respondents were women (91.67%) and were primarily the spouse (84.7%) or child (11.1%) of the household head. The mean age of the respondents was 32 years. Primary education was the most common educational level (40.3%), followed by secondary education (25%).

Household structure differed modestly between the two sites. Although the median number of total rooms was similar, households in Manono had more sleeping spaces used the night before the survey compared to Kalemie. Sleeping arrangements differed modestly between sites, with predominantly fixed sleeping spaces in Manono, and relatively more mobile sleeping spaces in Kalemie.

Directly observed ITN use and nighttime behavioral patterns

Direct observation revealed a clear temporal pattern in ITN use and nighttime behavior across both study sites (Fig. 2). ITN use increased progressively during the evening hours, with most individuals transitioning under nets between approximately 21:00 and 23:00.

During the night (23:00–04:00), ITN use remained consistently high, with the majority of individuals observed sleeping under nets. However, a non-negligible proportion of individuals remained indoors without ITN protection throughout the night. In Kalemie, at least 10% of individuals were consistently observed sleeping indoors without using an ITN between 22:00 and 05:00 (supplementary material).

Patterns of incomplete ITN use were also observed. The proportion of individuals partially covered or not fully protected by ITNs peaked at approximately 02:00. In Manono for example, this proportion reached around 30% during the dry season compared with approximately 20% during the rainy season (supplementary material). These patterns were particularly noticeable among young children sharing nets or individuals frequently leaving and re-entering the nets during the night. These observations suggest that correct ITN use (defined as being fully covered under the net) was not universal, even during peak sleeping hours.

In the early morning hours (after 05:00), ITN use declined sharply as individuals began leaving their sleeping spaces. This period was characterized by increased indoor activity without ITN use and a rise in the proportion of individuals outside the household.

These temporal patterns highlight that ITN use is dynamic rather than static, with transitions into and out of net use occurring throughout the night. Such variability may contribute to discrepancies between reported and observed ITN use, particularly when reported indicators rely on a single question referring to net use during previous night.

ITN ownership, access and reported ITN use

All sampled households owned at least one ITN at the time of the survey. Overall, 82.7% of ITNs found in households originated from the most recent mass distribution campaign. The mean number of ITNs received during the campaign was 2.6 in Kalemie and 3.2 in Manono (Table 1). Over time, ITN availability remained relatively stable in Kalemie but declined in Manono, decreasing from a mean of 3.7 nets per household in the first round to 2.8 in the fourth round. Using the national benchmark of one ITN per two persons, household-level access was suboptimal in both sites across all survey rounds. In Kalemie, between 36 and 42% of households did not meet the minimum coverage threshold. In Manono, this proportion varied more widely, increasing from 22% in the second round to 58% in the fourth round, reflecting a marked deterioration in access over time. At the population level, access to ITNs also declined, particularly in Manono, where it decreased from 92% in the first round to 78.8% in the fourth round (Fig. 3). In Kalemie, access remained comparatively more stable, although a gradual decline was also observed. Despite these variations in access, reported ITN use remained consistently higher than both household-level and population-level access across all rounds and in both sites (Fig. 3), indicating a discrepancy between reported use and actual availability of nets.

ITN access and ITN use in Tanganyika according to the national ITN standard of one ITN per two people in each household

Reported ITN use for children under five years of age was consistently high, ranging from 97 to 100% across sites and rounds (Fig. 4). Among all household members, reported use in Kalemie decreased slightly from 94.5% in the first round to 90.2% in the fourth round, whereas Manono experienced a more significant decrease from 98% to 83.8%. These findings suggest that, in contexts of limited ITN availability, priority is given to younger children, while other household members may experience reduced access and use. This pattern further highlights the gap between reported ITN use and effective coverage at the population level.

Comparison of reported vs observed ITN use

Across all survey rounds, self-reported ITN use remained consistently high, averaging approximately 91%, whereas observed ITN use, when expressed relative to the total surveyed population, ranged from 72.9% to 76.5% (Table 2). Sensitivity of self-reported ITN use was consistently very high across all rounds (97.8–99.4%), indicating that individuals who used ITNs were almost always correctly identified as users. In contrast, specificity was substantially lower (18.7–47.7%), reflecting a considerable proportion of individuals who did not use ITNs but reported that they did.

Overall accuracy of reported ITN use compared to the observed use ranged from 79.1% to 85.6% across survey rounds, suggesting a relatively high proportion of correctly classified observations. However, this apparent performance should be interpreted with caution, as it is largely driven by the high prevalence of ITN use and the very high sensitivity of self-reported measures, while failing to adequately capture non-use, as reflected by the consistently low specificity. As a result, overall agreement between reported and observed ITN use remained limited, with Cohen’s Kappa values ranging from 0.25 to 0.56, indicating poor to moderate agreement across rounds. These findings suggest that self-reported ITN use tends to overestimate actual use, particularly by misclassifying non-users as users.

In some instances, observed ITN use slightly exceeded reported use. For example, in Manono, observed correct use reached 85.7% and 87.8% in the third and fourth rounds, respectively, compared with reported values of 84.7% and 83.8%. Similar patterns were observed in Kalemie during the fourth round (supplementary material). However, these differences remained marginal and did not alter the overall pattern of high sensitivity and low specificity.

Taken together, these results indicate that while self-reported ITN use is effective in identifying users, it does not reliably capture non-use, thereby limiting its validity as a measure of effective ITN coverage.

Physical integrity of campaign nets

The physical condition of ITNs deteriorated markedly over time in both sites (Table 3). In Kalemie, the proportion of SafeNet ITNs with at least one hole increased from 21.0% in the first round to 69.0% in the fourth round, approximately 20 months after distribution. A similar trend was observed in Manono, where 78.2% of Veeralin nets had developed holes by the final survey round. Despite this deterioration, most ITNs remained classified as serviceable according to WHO criteria. In the fourth round, 85% of SafeNets in Kalemie and 79.2% of Veeralin nets in Manono were still considered serviceable. However, the proportion of nets classified as being in “Good” condition (pHI < 64) declined substantially over time, reaching 63% in Kalemie and 44.6% in Manono. At the same time, the proportion of severely damaged nets (pHI > 642) increased, particularly in Manono, where more than 20% of Veeralin ITNs were classified as “Torn” by the fourth round. These findings underscore the need to consider both physical durability and user behavior when evaluating ITN effectiveness over time.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of ITN use dynamics and the validity of self-reported ITN-use indicators in a post-distribution context in Tanganyika Province, DRC. Three main findings emerged. First, ITN access declined over time, particularly in Manono, despite high initial coverage following the mass campaign. Second, ITN-use followed dynamic nighttime patterns, with incomplete or inconsistent protection occurring throughout the night. Third, and most importantly, self-reported ITN use demonstrated very high sensitivity but low specificity, resulting in systematic overestimation of actual ITN use.

Reported vs observed ITN use: validity of self-reports

The diagnostic performance analysis shows that self-reported ITN use is highly sensitive (97.8–99.4%) but poorly specific (18.7–47.7%). This indicates that true ITN users were almost always correctly identified, whereas a substantial proportion of non-users were incorrectly classified as users. Consequently, agreement between reported and observed use remained limited, with Cohen’s Kappa values indicating only poor to moderate agreement across survey rounds.

These findings align with previous evidence suggesting that self-reported ITN use frequently overestimates actual use when assessed against more objective methods, including observation and sensor-based monitoring [23]. Such overestimation appears to be particularly marked in contexts where ITN use is strongly promoted as normative behavior, thereby increasing the likelihood of socially desirable responses [10, 11].

The relatively high overall accuracy observed in this study (79.1–85.6%) may appear reassuring at first glance, but it is largely driven by the high prevalence of ITN use and the near-perfect sensitivity of reported measures. As such, accuracy masks the poor ability of self-reported data to identify non-users. This limitation is critical in high-coverage settings, where misclassification of non-use can substantially bias estimates of effective protection.

ITN-use as a dynamic behavior

Direct observation revealed that ITN use is not a static, binary behavior but rather a dynamic process occurring over the course of the night. Individuals progressively move under nets during the evening and exit in the early morning, with intermittent periods of partial or no protection. Even during peak sleeping hours, a non-negligible proportion of individuals were either not using ITNs or were only partially covered.

These findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that ITN use can fluctuate substantially within a single night and may not be adequately captured by a single retrospective question [23]. This has important implications for routine malaria indicators, which typically rely on self-reported use “the previous night” and implicitly assume continuous protection throughout the night. In practice, however, actual exposure may vary according to sleeping arrangements, nighttime mobility, and patterns of partial or interrupted ITN use.

Role of ITN access and household dynamics

One of the major objectives of the cyclical and quasi-systematic ITN distribution is to achieve universal coverage and promote consistent use [18, 24–26]. Recent modelling evidence suggests that maintaining universal ITN coverage requires not only initial distribution but also continuous replenishment strategies to account for household growth, net attrition, and evolving sleeping arrangements [27].

This study also highlights the central role of ITN access in shaping use patterns. While reported ITN use remained high across rounds, both household-level and population-level access declined over time, particularly in Manono. Importantly, reported use consistently exceeded access, suggesting that self-reported indicators may not accurately reflect effective coverage. These findings further support the need for complementary distribution strategies, as continuous distribution mechanisms have been shown to be effective in sustaining ITN coverage between mass campaigns [28].

High rates of ITN use were reported and observed among children under five, a promising finding. The prioritization of children under five years of age further illustrates intra-household allocation dynamics. Consistently higher reported use among young children suggests that households allocate limited ITN resources preferentially to vulnerable groups. Similar patterns have been observed across sub-Saharan Africa, where ITN use is preferentially allocated to vulnerable groups such as young children and women [29]. This also raises concerns about possible overexposure of older children to mosquito bites and therefore to malaria [30, 31], as well as net overcrowding, which may reduce the protective efficacy of ITNs and accelerate wear and tear [32]. This could explain why data from the country generally show a ratio of ITN use by ITN access greater than one [20]. This pattern is consistent with findings from DHS analyses and reinforces the need to interpret ITN-use indicators in relation to access and household structure [6, 17].

Integrity and durability of ITNs

The progressive deterioration of ITN physical integrity observed in this study adds an important dimension to the interpretation of ITN use. Although most nets remained classified as serviceable, the proportion of nets in “good” condition declined substantially, and the proportion of severely damaged nets increased over time, particularly in Manono.

These findings are consistent with previous durability monitoring studies conducted in the DRC and elsewhere, which have documented substantial attrition within the first 12–24 months post-distribution [7, 8, 32]. The rapid deterioration observed here may be attributed to several factors: overcrowded use (more than two users per net), frequent repositioning, child-related wear, and environmental damage (e.g., burns, abrasions). Deterioration may reduce perceived protection and discourage consistent use, even when nets remain technically serviceable. Therefore, both physical integrity and behavioral factors must be considered when evaluating ITN effectiveness.

To mitigate this, national programs should reinforce communication on net care and consider strategies to extend the ITN lifespan, particularly in high-burden zones such as Tanganyika.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its longitudinal design, repeated nighttime observations, and the use of diagnostic validity measures (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and Kappa). The combination of behavioral observation and household survey data provides a comprehensive assessment of ITN use dynamics.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of household members as observers may have influenced behavior (Hawthorne effect). While these individuals were trained and supported, their proximity to the observed individuals may have affected the accuracy of the data. The repeated visits to the same households across survey rounds could have influenced participant behavior or responses, a form of the Hawthorne effect. Second, reported ITN use was provided by a single respondent per household, introducing potential intra-household correlation. Third, the study was conducted in peri-urban settings and may not be generalizable to all contexts. Fourth, no electronic or sensor-based validation tools were used.

Finally, the study did not directly assess insecticidal efficacy; instead, it focused on physical integrity. As such, nets categorized as "serviceable" may have diminished vector control effectiveness if insecticide levels declined.

Conclusions

This study compared reported and observed ITN use across four survey rounds in Tanganyika Province following a mass distribution campaign. The findings showed that self-reported ITN use is a highly sensitive but poorly specific indicator of actual use, leading to systematic overestimation of effective coverage. Although self-reported measures may serve as a pragmatic proxy under field conditions, they should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in high-coverage settings.

ITN use was shown to be dynamic and influenced by both access and net condition. Declines in ITN availability and physical integrity over time highlight the challenges of sustaining universal coverage and effective protection beyond the immediate post-distribution period. Despite these constraints, ITN use among children under five years remained consistently high, suggesting intra-household prioritization of vulnerable groups. However, such prioritization may also contribute to increased wear and accelerated degradation of nets due to shared use.

Direct observation provided valuable insights into nighttime behaviors and patterns of incomplete or inconsistent net use, which are not captured by standard survey indicators. These findings emphasize the added value of combining behavioral observation with routine survey approaches to better understand actual protection levels.

From a programmatic perspective, malaria control programs should consider four priorities. First, distribution strategies should better match household composition and sleeping arrangements to improve effective access. Second, behavior change communication should place greater emphasis on correct and consistent net use, as well as net care and maintenance. Third, routine monitoring systems should integrate indicators of ITN physical integrity alongside standard coverage and use metrics. Finally, supplemental or continuous distribution strategies may be needed to offset net attrition and maintain adequate ITN access over time.

Overall, improving both the measurement and the sustained effectiveness of ITN use remains essential for optimizing malaria prevention strategies in high-burden settings such as Tanganyika.
