Identification of a novel and high-affinity cyclic peptide targeting Keap1 for inflammation treatment by a combined virtual screening strategy
Authors: Shenglin Gao, Xiaokai Shi, Shudan Yang, Yuting Wang, Qian Wang, Minyan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInhibition of the Keap1-Nrf2 protein-protein interaction with cyclic peptides represents an attractive strategy to treat inflammation. However, the cyclic peptides for this inhibition are constrained by their low affinity. In this study, the peptides 1-6 were computationally screened using Keap1-pharmacophore modelling, toxicity screening, molecular docking, and interaction analysis. Subsequently, affinity experiments showed that peptide-4 exhibited potent binding affinity for Keap1 (Kd = 7.1 ± 0.2 nM). MD simulations further demonstrated that peptide-4 stably bound to Keap1. Additionally, MTT assays confirmed that peptides 1-6 induced negligible cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 macrophages. In vitro anti-inflammatory experiments indicated that peptide-4 significantly inhibited the mRNA and protein expression of IL-6 and TNFα in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells. More importantly, experiments in Keap1-knockout RAW264.7 macrophages confirmed that the anti-inflammatory activity of peptide-4 was highly Keap1-dependent. In conclusion, the data demonstrated that the peptide-4 may be a promising candidate cyclic peptide to treat inflammatory disease.

Introduction

Oxidative stress represents a state of redox imbalance in the body, manifested by the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)1. ROS can activate a cascade of inflammation-related pathways, thereby inducing upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like tumour necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interleukin-6 (IL-6)2. Excessive expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines may induce a spectrum of inflammatory disorders, including prostatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, osteoarthritis, and pneumonia3,4. The nuclear factor erythroid 2-associated factor 2 (Nrf2), together with its endogenous inhibitor Kelch-like epichlorohydrin-associated protein 1 (Keap1), constitutes a crucial cellular mechanism to counteract oxidative stress and maintain ROS homoeostasis5. Elevated ROS levels induce Keap1 inactivation, thereby enhancing Nrf2 stability and promoting its nuclear translocation6. Upon activation, Nrf2 augments antioxidant enzymes expression, a mechanism that exhibits significant therapeutic potential for inflammatory diseases7. At present, Keap1 inhibitors activating Nrf2 have been acknowledged as effective therapeutics for various inflammatory diseases8. The curcumin analogue ACS-JM17 activating Nrf2, showed promise treating spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (SBMA). Notably, it has already obtained approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)9. However, Keap1 inhibitors might engage in non-specific binding with other protein targets harbouring cysteine (Cys) residues, resulting in diminished target specificity10. Developing inhibitors that specifically target the Keap1-Nrf2 protein-protein interaction (PPI) is a promising strategy treating inflammatory diseases11.

The Keap1-Nrf2-ARE (antioxidant response element) signalling pathway critically governs the redox balance within the body12. Under normal physiological circumstances, Keap1 associates with Nrf2 and recruits E3 ubiquitin ligases, thereby promoting Nrf2 polyubiquitination13. The ubiquitinated Nrf2 is subsequently degraded via ubiquitin-proteasome system14. In patients with inflammatory diseases, ROS induce modifications to the Cys residues of Keap1, thereby triggering a conformational change in Keap115. This alteration prevents Keap1 from binding to Nrf2, thereby activating Nrf216. Activated Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus and binds to the promoter regions of ARE17. The aforementioned interaction triggers the expression of antioxidant enzymes including haem oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1). These enzymes neutralise excessive ROS, thereby alleviating inflammatory diseases18.

Nrf2 is a transcription factor with inherent anti-inflammatory properties19. It comprises 605 amino acids and harbours seven conserved Nrf2-ECH homology domains (named Nch 1-7)20. Nch-1 is responsible for DNA binding and interaction with small Maf proteins (sMafs). Nch-2 contains the DLG and ETGE motifs, which mediate interaction with Keap121. Keap1, the endogenous inhibitor of Nrf2, is a dimeric protein constituted by 624 amino acids22. The DGR domain of Keap1 is the binding site for the Nch-2 domain of Nrf223. The interaction interface between Keap1 and Nrf2 is not only extensive but also highly charged24. Therefore, the development of small-molecule Keap1-Nrf2 PPI inhibitors poses a particularly formidable challenge. The peptidomimetics derived from the DLG or ETGE motifs present a highly promising approach for disrupting the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI25. However, studies have shown that peptidomimetics based on the ETGE motif exhibit low affinity26. Therefore, developing ETGE-based peptidomimetics to disrupt the Keap1-Nrf2 PPI emerges as a potentially efficacious therapeutic approach treating inflammation.

The 7-mer peptide (Ac-DPETGEL-OH) demonstrated a Kd (dissociation constant) value of 2.1 μM for Keap111, 27. However, the development of linear peptides is limited by poor stability and rapid clearance28. Cyclic peptides are regarded as optimal alternatives to enhance stability, exhibiting key advantages such as potent binding affinity and compact structure29. The 11-mer cyclic peptide (c[GQLDPETGEFL]) exhibited the affinity (Kd = 18.12 nM) for Keap1 and significant inhibited pro-inflammatory factors release in RAW264.7 macrophages30. ZC9 (c[KLDEETGEQ]) bound to Keap1 with a Kd value of 51 nM and possessed excellent permeability, stability, water solubility, and anti-inflammatory effects31. The cyclic 7-mer (c[Dha-DPEFTG]) conducted the affinity assay towards Keap1 (Kd = 20 ± 2 nM)32. The native 9-mer peptide (Ac-LDEETGEFL-NH2) had high affinity for Keap1 (In this study, Kd = 170.4 ± 9.1 nM) and was selected as the positive control31. However, the affinity of cyclic peptides still needs to be improved. Therefore, developing the shortest cyclic peptides with high-affinity ETGE motifs is urgently needed.

Pharmacophore modelling predicated on structural information represents a ubiquitously employed strategy within the realm of drug discovery33. It expedites and augments the precision of drug screening, concomitantly curtailing costs34,35. Previously, we successfully discovered active compounds using pharmacophore modelling, molecular docking, interaction analysis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and activity evaluation35–40. In this study, we successfully identified a cyclic peptide (peptide-4) targeting Keap1 for treating inflammatory diseases through pharmacophore modelling, molecular docking, interaction analysis, MD simulations, and anti-inflammatory evaluation.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents and cell culture

The line-peptide and cyclic peptides 1–6 were synthesised by Nanjing peptide Biotech Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The recombinant human Keap1 protein was purchased from Abcam (ab132384, Cambridge, UK). Human serum was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). The foetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin sulphate, and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). The RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (TIB-17, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The DMEM was supplemented with 10% inactivated FBS, 100 units/mL of penicillin, and 100 µg/mL of streptomycin sulphate. The cells were cultured in the incubator that was humidified with 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 °C.

Construction of the pharmacophore model

The co-crystal structure of the previously reported peptide with the Kelch domain of human Keap1 (PDB ID: 7K2S), with a resolution of 1.91 Å (distance unit), was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)32. The complex was imported and optimised under the Amber10:EHT force field in the molecular operating environment (MOE, Chemical Computing Group Inc., Montreal, Quebec, Canada)41. The QuickPrep tool was used to automatically pre-process the complex, which included deleting water molecules father than 4.5 Å from the ligand or receptor, calculating charges, adding hydrogen atoms, and performing energy minimisation. The System tool was employed to colour the receptor protein atoms teal and ligand atoms green, facilitating differentiation between ligand and receptor atomic sites. The Site View tool visualised the docking active site, colouring key amino acids white to facilitate clear observation of ligand-protein interactions. The Ligand Interaction tool was utilised to analyse interactions between the peptide and the Kelch domain of human Keap1. Given these interactions, a pharmacophore model for Keap1 composed of pharmacophore features was constructed using the pharmacophore editor. The pharmacophore features mainly considered hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrogen bond donors, and aromatic centres. The constructed pharmacophore model of Keap1 was used in the subsequent pharmacophore validation process.

Pharmacophore model evaluation

A test database for pharmacophore model validation was established, comprising 500 peptides, of which 13 were active cyclic peptides30,31,42. The database screening was conducted using the pharmacophore search protocol implemented in MOE. The Güner–Henry (GH) metric evaluated the constructed pharmacophore model’s ability to discriminate active from inactive compounds. The GH score spanned 0 to 1, with 0 denoting a null model (statistically random) and 1 an ideal model (theoretical optimum)43.

Construction of peptide library

Methods for the construction of peptide library draw references from prior research studies40,44. A combinatorial peptide database was generated via the QuaSAR-CombiGen algorithm, leveraging a collection of short peptide fragments as building blocks. Every peptide fragment library contains 39 peptide sequences. Consequently, two dipeptide databases and one tripeptide database were systematically enumerated within the MOE environment using the QuaSAR-CombiGen algorithm. The database of 59,319 cyclic heptapeptides was constructed via integration of three previously described fragments for virtual screening of Keap1 inhibitors. Attachment ports were defined as terminal atoms designated “An”, where n represents a positive integer. In one dipeptide library, C-terminal oxygen atoms of fragments were labelled as “A0” port. In another, N-terminal nitrogen atoms and oxygen atoms were labelled as “A1” and “A2” ports, respectively. The tripeptide library had N-terminal nitrogen atoms labelled as “A0” port. The “A0” attachment port of the dipeptide fragment was linked to the “A1” port of another dipeptide fragment, whereas the “A0” port of the tripeptide fragment was connected to the “A2” port of the dipeptide fragment. A cyclic heptapeptide database was constructed via fragment terminal ligation to form cyclic structures. The virtual library was output to the database containing 59,319 cyclic peptides. Finally, the two-dimensional (2D) structures of these peptides were converted to three-dimensional (3D) structures via energy minimisation in MOE. The 59,319-peptide database was used for subsequent virtual screening.

Virtual screening

The virtual screening approach was performed following the previously described protocols45–47. The database of 59,319 peptides was selected to identify potential inhibitors of Keap1-Nrf2 PPI. Based on the constructed pharmacophore model of Keap1, preliminary screening of the database was conducted. Next, toxicity screening of the selected peptides was performed with ADMETlab 3.0. Subsequently, the selected peptides were docked to the binding site of the human Kelch domain Keap1. Peptide docking scores were calculated using the London dG and GBVI/WSA dG scoring functions. Using docking scores, the top six peptides (peptides 1–6) with optimal conformations were selected. The binding modes of the peptides with the Kelch domain of human Keap1 were analysed to identify interactions. Additionally, in vitro evaluation experiments were performed, including microscale thermophoresis (MST), serum stability assays, MD simulation, cytotoxicity assays and anti-inflammatory experiments. Using the virtual screening workflow, the most promising peptide for inflammation therapy was identified.

MST assay

The MST experiment was conducted following relevant literature and established protocols48. The linear peptide was used as the positive control. The recombinant human protein of Keap1 was fluorescently labelled using the MonolithTM RED-NHS second-generation protein labelling kit (Nano Temper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). The tube with labelled Keap1 was wrapped in aluminium foil and subsequently stored on ice. The cyclic peptide was fully dissolved in PBST (Phosphate Buffered Saline with Tween), followed by serial two - fold dilutions to generate 16 distinct concentration gradients. Subsequently, 2 μM Keap1 and 2 mM DLG/ETGE reagent were incubated with the cyclic peptide at various concentrations of in the dark for 5 min. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was measured on the Monolith NT.115 instrument (Nano Temper Technologies GmbH) using capillary loading. The binding curves were generated using the MO Affinity Analysis software. The Kd values were calculated based on the dose-response curves.

To validate affinity consistency, binding assays of peptide-4 to Keap1 were performed across PBST buffers and temperatures. Three PBST buffers at pH 6.0, 7.4, 8.0 were prepared. And all other conditions stayed constant. The Kd values for the interaction between peptide-4 and Keap1 were determined across PBST buffer systems. Sixteen diluted samples were dark-incubated for 5 min at 4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C, with all other conditions unchanged. Kd values for peptide-4 binding to Keap1 were quantified at 4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C.

Serum stability test

The method was implemented following prior established protocols40. Human serum was diluted to 25% (v/v) in assay buffer, then centrifuged at 12,500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was immediately harvested for subsequent analyses. The line-peptide and peptide-4 were spiked into the supernatant to a final concentration of 5 nM and incubated at 37 °C. At 30, 60, 120, and 240 min, 200 μL aliquots were withdrawn and mixed with equal-volume acetonitrile for protein precipitation. The mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.25 μm membrane filter, and the filtrate was collected as the sample for High - Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) analysis. Peptide remaining (%) was determined by integrating the peak area.

MD simulation

Method was performed as previously described37,44. The peptide-4 exhibited the best performance, outshining other candidates in the virtual screening. The docking-derived binding mode of Peptide-4 with the Kelch domain of human Keap1 was imported into MOE. The complex was topologized using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field49. This force field demonstrates superior performance in reproducing protein conformational dynamics with widespread application in MD simulations50,51. The complex was confined within a cubic cube with an edge length of 1.0 nm, and solvent molecules (SPC/E water model) were added. Counterions (sodium and chloride) were added to neutralise the system charge. Energy minimisation was performed using 5000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm. Pre-equilibration was performed under NVT (300 K) and NPT (1.0 bar) conditions. Finally, the complex was subjected 100 ns of MD simulation on the Beikun Cloud Computing Platform. The stability of the complex was evaluated using metrics such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), secondary structure, radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond analysis, and residue contribution analysis.

MTT assay

Cytotoxicity of the peptides 1–6 towards RAW 264.7 cells was evaluated using the colorimetric 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) dye reduction assay40. Cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 50 μM of the designed peptides were added, and cells were incubated for an additional 72 h. Next, 20 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well, followed by a 4-h incubation. Finally, 150 μL of DMSO was added to solubilise the formazan precipitate, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability = Atreated/Acontrol × 100%.

In vitro anti-inflammatory assays

The reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) experiment was performed according to previous study52,53. The RAW264.7 macrophages (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. The cells were stimulated with LPS for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, or 12 h. The treated RAW264.7 macrophages were collected in centrifuge tubes. The TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was added to lyse the cells. The concentration and purity of total RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesised using the FastQuant RT Kit with gDNase (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers and TB Green PCR Master Mix were used for PCR amplification. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was then detected using the LightCycler 96 real-time PCR system. Reactions were initiated by incubation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s. The gene expression levels of IL-6 and TNFα were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The expression data were presented as fold-change for clear visualisation. 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA) was chosen as the reference gene because of its high abundance in cells and stable expression levels, which make it suitable for normalising gene expression data54. The primer sequences for IL-6, TNF-α, and 18S rRNA were presented in Table S1.

Keap1-knockout (KO) RAW264.7 macrophages were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 lentivirus (sgRNA: sc-400190-KO-2) with puromycin selection. Construct was from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA)55. The Wild-type (WT) and KO RAW264.7 macrophages (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight at 37 °C prior to treatment. After treatment with peptides 1–6 for 1 h, LPS was added and incubated for 6 h. The procedures for determining IL-6 and TNFα gene expression levels were essentially identical to the aforementioned protocols.

The cell culture supernatants of the peptide-4 treatment group were harvested for quantitative analysis of IL-6 and TNFα protein levels. Commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were procured, and experiments were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols56.

Statistics analysis

Data analysis and graphing were conducted with the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), version 9.5. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for significance testing of mean differences among groups. The difference was manifested as the mean values ± SD (standard deviation). Each experiment was repeated at least three times.

Results

The pharmacophore model of Keap1

The co-crystal structure of the peptide bound to the Kelch domain of human Keap1 (PDB ID: 7K2S) was downloaded for pharmacophore model construction. Interactions were subsequently analysed using the Ligand Interaction tool. The amino group of Arg415 in the Kelch domain of human Keap1 formed a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of the residue Thr80 in the line-peptide. In the model, the hydroxyl group of Thr80 served as the hydrogen-bond acceptor (F1:Acc). The amino group of Gln530 in Keap1 established a hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of Glu79 in the line-peptide. The hydrogen-bond acceptor (F2:Acc) was constructed using the pharmacophore editor. The residues Arg483, Ser508, and Arg415 of Keap1 interacted with residue Glu78 of the line-peptide through hydrogen bonds. The hydroxyl group of Glu78 functioned as the hydrogen-bond acceptor (F3:Acc). The amino groups of Keap1 residues Arg380 and Asn382 hydrogen bonded with the carbonyl oxygen of Glu82 in the line-peptide. The hydroxyl group of Glu82 was considered as the hydrogen bond acceptor (F4: Acc). Additionally, the key Keap1 Kelch domain residues Ser508, Ser363, and Ser602 also interacted with the line-peptide through hydrogen bonds. The Keap1 residues Tyr334, Gly603, and Tyr525 engaged in hydrophobic interactions with the line-peptide. Figure 1 depicted the successfully constructed Keap1 pharmacophore model, comprising four acceptor features (F1–F4) essential for ligand binding.

The pharmacophore model of Keap1. The pharmacophore characterizations-hydrogen bonding receptors (F1-F4: Acc) were displayed as orange spheres. The hydrogen bonding was indicated by the black dotted line.

Virtual screening

The virtual screening workflow for high-affinity of cyclic peptides was illustrated in Figure 2. Based on the interactions between the line-peptide and Keap1, the pharmacophore model of Keap1 was successfully constructed. Subsequently, the validated pharmacophore model achieved the GH score of 0.87, indicating its good predictive power. The database containing 59,319 peptides was then constructed using the QuaSAR-CombiGen algorithm in MOE. Leveraging the validated pharmacophore model, 233 peptides were screened from the 59,319-peptide database. Additionally, 230 peptides were shortlisted via toxicity screening using ADMETlab 3.0. Following docking to the human Keap1 Kelch domain active site, peptides were ranked by their docking scores. The top six peptides (peptides 1–6) with green-flagged toxicity predictions were selected for further evaluation (Table S2). Molecular interaction analysis was performed to characterise the binding modes of peptides 1–6 to the human Kelch domain Keap1. Subsequently, the affinity experiments between peptides 1–6 and Keap1 were conducted. The serum stability of peptide-4 was assessed. Thereafter, peptide-4 with optimal performance underwent the 100-ns MD simulation. The cytotoxicity assays of peptides 1–6 against RAW264.7 macrophages were performed. The mRNA and protein levels of IL-6 and TNFα were evaluated in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells treated with peptides 1–6. Finally, Keap1-knockout experiments further demonstrated that peptide-4 exerted its anti-inflammatory activity through this target. In summary, peptide-4 was identified as the most promising candidate cyclic peptide treating inflammatory diseases via the virtual screening process.

MST analysis

Given the low calculated binding free energies and strong interactions between peptides 1–6 and the Kelch domain of Keap1, MST experiments were conducted to evaluate the affinity. The smaller the value of Kd, the stronger the affinity59. The Kd values of the line-peptide and peptides 1-6 were presented in Table 2. The Kd value of the linear peptide was 170.4 ± 9.1 nM. All Kd values of peptides 1-6 were lower than that of the line-peptide. Among peptides 1-6, peptide-4 had the lowest Kd value (Kd = 7.1 ± 0.2 nM). The dose-response curve of peptide-4 to Keap1 was presented in Figure S1. The single Kd value indicated a 1:1 binding interaction between peptide-4 and Keap160,61. Therefore, peptide-4 exhibited strong affinity for Keap1.

To investigate the effect of pH on binding affinity, we determined the affinity between peptide-4 and Keap1 in three PBST buffers with different pH values. The Kd values for peptide-4 binding to Keap1 remained stable at ∼7 nM across PBST buffers of pH 6.0, 7.4, and 8.0. Additionally, we investigated the effect of temperature on binding affinity. The Kd values for peptide-4 binding to Keap1 were quantified at 5 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C. Kd invariance at ∼7 nM across temperatures confirmed thermally invariant binding. The results were presented in Figure S2. In summary, the potent binding affinity of peptide-4 (the optimised compound) to Keap1 demonstrated consistency across PBST buffers (pH 6-8) and temperatures (5-37 °C).

Serum stability test analysis

Human serum contains proteolytic enzymes (e.g., esterases, peptidases) that hydrolyse therapeutic peptides62. Moreover, serum stability is critical for peptide drugs development, underpinning their translational potential63. Thus, the serum stability assays for peptide-4 and the line-peptide were performed. The results were presented in Figure S3. The line-peptide underwent ∼60% degradation at the 30 min time point, with almost complete degradation observed by 240 min. The peptide-4 preserved approximately 84.95% structural integrity following a 240-min incubation period. Compared to the line-peptide, peptide-4 demonstrated significantly enhanced serum stability and a reduced enzymatic degradation rate. The results demonstrated that peptide-4 exhibited stability in serum and resistance to enzymatic degradation.

MD simulation

Among the peptides 1-6, the peptide-4 exhibited the lowest binding free energy and the strongest affinity for Keap1. Based on this, peptide-4 was selected for 100 ns MD simulation. RMSD is a quantitative indicator used to assess the stability of proteins and ligands64. The RMSD value of the Keap1-peptide-4 complex stabilise at approximately 0.17 nm after 5 ns, as evidenced in the curve (Figure 6(A)). The RMSD values of Keap1 were also calculated. The results indicated that the binding of peptide-4 to the Keap1 active pocket exerted no effect on the Keap1’s stability. RMSF is a physical indicator utilised for evaluating the fluctuations of amino acids in proteins65. The lower RMSF values indicated smaller fluctuations66. The RMSF values of residues in the active pocket of Keap1, including Arg380, Ser363, Gly603, Asn382, Ser602, Tyr334, Tyr525, Arg415, Ser508, Arg483, and Gln530, were all below 0.08 nm (Figure 6(B)). In contrast, the RMSF values of the N/C terminal residues of Keap1 were above 0.13 nm. The hydrogen bond formation between the proteins and ligands played a significant role in molecular recognition67. The number of hydrogen bonds formed between Keap1 and peptide-4 was calculated during the 100 ns simulation. The peptide-4 formed up to 16 hydrogen bonds (Figure 6(C)). The results demonstrated that the hydrogen bond interactions between Keap1 and peptide-4 remained stable throughout the simulation.

The conformational stability analysis of the Keap1-peptide-4 complex.

Rg is a physical quantity used to evaluate the compactness of protein structures64. The Rg values of Keap1 maintained stable at 1.77-1.79 nm (Figure 6(D)). The smaller Rg values were closely associated with more compact protein structures68. The results indicated that peptide-4 could bind tightly to Keap1. The secondary structure of Keap1 mainly consisted of Structure, β-sheet, and Coil structures (Figure 6(E)). The MM/PBSA method was used to calculate the binding free energy of the Keap1-peptide-4 complex during 100 ns simulation69. To validate the contributions of critical residues, residue contribution analysis was conducted on the active pocket of Keap1. If the binding free energy of a residue was below −1 kcal/mol, it was considered the hot residue exerting the critical role in ligand recognition67. The residues Arg380, Ser363, Asn382, Ser602, Tyr334, Tyr525, Arg415, Ser508, Arg483, and Gln530 in the active pocket of Keap1 were identified as key amino acids for binding to peptide-4 (Figure 6(F)). Among these, residue Arg483 exhibited an interaction energy of −8.62 ± 0.79 kcal/mol, contributing the most to the Keap1-peptide-4 complex. In summary, peptide-4 stably bound to Keap1 during the 100 ns MD simulation.

MTT analysis

The cytotoxicity of peptides 1-6 towards RAW264.7 cells was evaluated via the MTT assay, with results are presented in Figure S4. Compared with the control group, no significant difference in cell viability was observed in the peptides 1-6 treatment groups. These data indicated that peptides 1-6 exhibited negligible cytotoxicity towards RAW264.7 cells, thereby suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effects of peptides 1-6 were dependent on their targeting of Keap1.

In vitro anti-inflammatory evaluation

The LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages are used as the anti-inflammatory model70. LPS stimulates the high expression of IL-6 and TNFα in RAW264.7 macrophages71. The mRNA expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in RAW264.7 cells stimulated with LPS at varying co-incubation durations were depicted in Figure S5. The mRNA expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells were significantly upregulated, far surpassing those in the control group. Notably, the mRNA expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in RAW264.7 cells reached its peak at the 6-h time point. Thus, 6-h LPS stimulation of RAW264.7 cells was chosen for the subsequent in vitro anti-inflammatory assessment.

The anti-inflammatory experiments of the line-peptide and peptides 1-6 were evaluated by RT-qPCR. The mRNA expression levels of IL-6 and TNFα in WT RAW264.7 macrophages treated with the line-peptide and peptides 1-6 were shown in Figure 7. Compared with the control group, LPS significantly increased the mRNA expression of IL-6 and TNFα in WT RAW264.7 macrophages. In contrast, the line-peptide suppressed the expression of these inflammatory factors. Moreover, peptides 1-6 exhibited more prominent inhibitory effects on IL-6 and TNFα expression than the line-peptide did. Notably, peptides 1-6 failed to suppress anti-inflammatory cytokines expression (IL-6 and TNFα) in Keap1-KO RAW264.7 macrophages (Figure S6). Cyclic peptides inhibited Keap1 exclusively in WT cells, with no activity in Keap1-KO cells, confirming their Keap1-dependent action. Among these peptides, peptide-4 demonstrated the most pronounced mRNA suppression of IL-6 and TNFα expression.

Next, the effect of peptide-4 on the protein expression levels of IL-6 and TNFα was further investigated. The results were shown in Figure S7. Compared with the control group, LPS significantly stimulated the protein expression levels of IL-6 and TNFα. Moreover, the data demonstrated that peptide-4 significantly inhibited the protein expression levels of IL-6 and TNFα. In summary, peptide-4 was the candidate cyclic peptide exerting in vitro anti-inflammatory effects.

Discussion

Chemical structure of peptides is intrinsically correlated with their biological activity72. The peptides 2–4 possessed the hydroxyl modifications on Pro78, which formed hydrogen bonds with the key residue Arg483 in the active pocket of Keap1. Thus, peptides 2-4 exhibited lower Kd values. Peptide-3 had an isopropyl group replacing the Pro78 ethyl group in peptide-2. The isopropyl group is more hydrophobic than the ethyl group73. Therefore, the peptide-3 formed stronger hydrophobic interactions with the Tyr525 residue of Keap1. Compared to the peptide-2, peptide-3 exhibited higher affinity (Kd = 13.5 ± 0.4 nM). Compared to peptide-3, the structure of peptide-4 replaced the ethyl group connected to the methylene group with a three-membered ring. The three-membered ring has higher hydrophobicity than the ethyl group74. Therefore, peptide-4 could deeply penetrate the hydrophobic cavity formed by the residues Ser603 and Tyr334 of Keap1. Compared to peptides 1–6, peptide-4 demonstrated stronger affinity for Keap1 (Kd = 7.1 ± 0.2 nM).

Currently, peptide inhibitors targeting Keap1 were limited by low binding affinity31. (1) The peptide Ac-DPETGEL-OH showed the Kd of 2.1 μM11; (2) The cyclic peptide c[GQLDPETGETL] had the Kd of 18.12 nM30; (3) The cyclic peptide c[KLDEETGEQ] exhibited the Kd of 51 nM31; (4) The cyclic peptide c[Dha-DPEFTG] displayed the Kd of 20 ± 2 nM32. Moreover, the peptide cyclisation strategy can enhance its binding affinity31. In this study, peptide-4 exhibited the Kd of 7.1 ± 0.2 nM. Compared to current Keap1-targeting peptide inhibitors, it showed superior binding affinity, as validated by MST assay. In summary, peptide-4 was the high-affinity cyclic peptide targeting Keap1 for inflammation treatment by a combined virtual screening strategy.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully constructed and validated the pharmacophore model for Keap1. the cyclic peptides 1–6 were screened from the constructed library of 59,319 peptides via a combined approach involving Keap1 pharmacophore modelling, toxicity screening and molecular docking. Interaction analysis revealed that peptides 1–6 could interact with the key residues in the active pocket of Keap1 through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. Subsequently, affinity experiments indicated that peptide-4 exhibited the highest binding affinity for Keap1 (Kd = 7.1 ± 0.2 nM) between the line-peptide and peptides 1–6. Moreover, peptide-4 demonstrated excellent stability in human serum. 100-ns MD simulation demonstrated that peptide-4 stably bound to Keap1. Additionally, MTT assays confirmed that peptides 1–6 induced negligible cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 macrophages at 50 μM. In vitro anti-inflammatory experiments exhibited that peptide-4 significantly inhibited the mRNA expression and protein expression of IL-6 and TNFα in LPS-induced RAW264.7 macrophages. In conclusion, data demonstrate that peptide-4 may be a promising candidate cyclic peptide to treat inflammatory disease.
