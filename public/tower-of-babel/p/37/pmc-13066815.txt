Differential Pathogenicity and Secreted in Xylem Gene Patterns in Fusarium spp. Causing Onion Basal Rot
Authors: Sieun Kim, Ha-Kyoung Lee, Da-Ran Kim, You-Kyoung Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusarium basal rot (FBR) poses a serious threat to onion (Allium cepa L.) production worldwide. In South Korea, FBR is primarily associated with Fusarium oxysporum, F. commune, and F. proliferatum. To investigate the relationship between effector gene profiles and virulence, we screened 34 isolates collected from FBR-affected fields for 14 Secreted in Xylem (SIX) genes and three additional effector candidates (CRX1, CRX2, and C5). F. oxysporum isolates carrying the effector suite SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, SIX14, together with CRX1, CRX2 and C5, exhibited significantly higher aggressiveness on onion seedlings and bulbs than effector-negative strains. Among F. commune isolates lacking SIX genes, those carrying both CRX1 and CRX2 tended to show greater pathogenicity than CRX-negative strains. Nevertheless, SIX-negative strains still caused substantial seedling loss and bulb-rot, indicating the involvement of SIX-independent virulence factors. All F. proliferatum isolates were comparably pathogenic to SIX-negative F. oxysporum and F. commune strains, and uniformly carried SIX2-1 and CRX2, with a subset also harboring the SIX2-2 homologue. Across all isolates, SIX9 was the most frequently detected SIX gene and was markedly enriched in strains exhibiting strong pathogenicity. We developed and validated a SIX9-targeted quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay that specifically detects SIX9-positive Fusarium isolates (mainly F. oxysporum and F. commune), with detection limits of 1 pg of DNA or 104 conidia/g soil. These findings enhance our understanding of effector repertoires contributing to Fusarium pathogenicity on onion and provide a molecular tool to support FBR diagnosis.Keywords: Fusarium basal rot, onion, qPCR, SIX genes

Fusarium basal rot (FBR) is a major soil-borne disease that poses a serious threat to onion (Allium cepa L.) production worldwide (Le et al., 2021). The disease causes substantial yield losses both in the field and during postharvest storage (Latvala et al., 2020). FBR affects onion plants at all developmental stages, leading to seedling damping-off and progressive basal rot in mature bulbs. While Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cepae (Foc) is considered the primary causal agent of FBR, other Fusarium species, including F. proliferatum, F. redolens, and F. avenaceum have also been implicated in the disease (Galván et al., 2008; Ghanbarzadeh et al., 2014; Haapalainen et al., 2016). In South Korea, F. oxysporum, F. proliferatum and F. commune have been identified as major causal agents of FBR in onion (Shin et al., 2023).

F. oxysporum is a genetically diverse species complex comprising numerous strains with distinct host ranges. These strains are classified into different formae speciales (f. sp.) based on their host specificity (Michielse and Rep, 2009). A key mechanism underlying host specificity involves the secretion of effector proteins that modulate host defense responses and facilitate colonization (Ayukawa et al., 2021). In F. oxysporum, a well-characterized group of effectors known as Secreted in Xylem (SIX) genes plays a crucial role in pathogenicity (Ma et al., 2010). To date, fourteen SIX genes (SIX1–SIX14) have been identified in F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici (Fol), among which SIX1 (AVR3), SIX3 (AVR2), SIX5, and SIX6 are required for full virulence on tomato (Houterman et al., 2007; Laurence et al., 2015; Sakane et al., 2023a; Schmidt et al., 2013). These genes are typically located on lineage-specific chromosomes associated with virulence (Ma et al., 2010). Homologs of SIX genes have also been identified in several other formae speciales, supporting the hypothesis that horizontal transfer of pathogenicity chromosomes contributes to the evolution of host-specific virulence in the F. oxysporum species complex (Williams et al., 2016).

Previous studies have identified seven SIX gene homologs—SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14—in pathogenic Foc isolates, many of which are located on a 4-Mb pathogenicity chromosome (Sakane et al., 2024b; Sasaki et al., 2015; Taylor et al., 2016). In addition to these effectors, other virulence-associated genes such as C5, CRX1, and CRX2 have shown varying degrees of correlation with pathogenicity (Haapalainen et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2016). Notably, FocSIX5 has been shown to function as an avirulence factor in shallot, where it triggers host immune responses (Sakane et al., 2023a). Furthermore, SIX2 and SIX9 were recently identified on a distinct 3-Mb pathogenicity chromosome in Welsh onion-infecting Foc strains (Sakane et al., 2023b, 2024a, 2024b).

In F. proliferatum, a putative effector gene (CRX2) and two paralogous homologs of SIX2, designated SIX2-1 and SIX2-2, located on distinct contigs of the F. proliferatum genome, have been reported (Armitage et al., 2019; Haapalainen et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2016). Among these, the SIX2-1 variant was consistently detected across all isolates. While no statistically significant differences in virulence were observed on mature onion bulbs among isolates carrying SIX2-2, CRX2, or both genes, seedling assays revealed that isolates harboring CRX2 exhibited a marginally significant increase in virulence compared to those possessing only SIX2-2 (Haapalainen et al., 2023).

The aims of this study were (1) to investigate the profiles of virulence-related genes in F. oxysporum, F. commune, and F. proliferatum isolates collected from onion fields in South Korea; and (2) to examine the correlation between the presence of these genes and the pathogenicity of the isolates on onion seedlings and mature bulbs. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive survey of virulence gene profiles in onion-associated Fusarium species in South Korea and provides the first molecular characterization of SIX genes in F. commune isolates pathogenic to onion.

Materials and Methods

Fungal isolates and plant materials

Fusarium species were isolated from onion bulbs, roots, stems, and rhizosphere soils collected between 2016 and 2024 from fields in Jeolla and Gyeongsang Provinces, South Korea, where symptoms of FBR were observed (Supplementary Table 1). The isolates analyzed in this study included strains previously reported by Shin et al. (2023), as well as newly selected isolates to enhance the diversity of SIX gene genotypes. To ensure consistency, all isolates—including those previously characterized—were re-identified in the present study. The onion cultivar ‘Hwangryongball’ (Nongwoo Bio Co., Ltd., Suwon, Korea) was used for pathogenicity assays.

For fungal isolation from plant tissues, samples were cut into approximately 10 mm segments, surface-sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite for 1 min, rinsed with sterile distilled water, blotted dry with sterile tissue paper, and plated onto potato dextrose agar (PDA). Soil samples were serially diluted and spread onto PDA plates for fungal isolation. All isolates were purified and stored in 20% glycerol at −80°C.

DNA isolation, PCR, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from fungal isolates using the HiGene™ Genomic DNA Prep Kits (BIOFACT, Daejeon, Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For molecular identification of Fusarium species, the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1) gene was amplified using the primers EF-1 and EF-2 (O’Donnell et al., 1998), and the RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2) gene was amplified using the primers 5F2 and 7cR (Liu et al., 1999; Reeb et al., 2004). The resulting PCR products were sequenced by BIOFACT, and the obtained sequences were deposited in GenBank (Supplementary Table 1).

To investigate the presence of virulence-associated genes, genomic DNA from Fusarium isolates was screened for SIX genes (SIX1–SIX14), C5, CRX1, and CRX2 with gene-specific primer pairs (Lievens et al., 2009; Meldrum et al., 2012; Sakane et al., 2024a; Taylor et al., 2016) (Table 1). For F. proliferatum, homologs of the SIX2 gene (FprSIX2-1 and FprSIX2-2), as well as a CRX2 variant specific to F. proliferatum, were additionally amplified using gene-specific primer (Haapalainen et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2016). All oligonucleotides used in this study were synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea).

Phylogenetic analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, tef1 and rpb2 gene sequences from reference Fusarium spp. isolates were retrieved from NCBI GenBank database (Supplementary Table 2). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT v.7 (Katoh and Standley, 2013). A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on a concatenated tef1 and rpb2 sequence dataset using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates under the GTR+GAMMA model, implemented in raxmlGUI v.2.0 (Edler et al., 2021).

Onion seedling assay

The pathogenicity of Fusarium strains toward onion seedlings was assessed with minor modifications to previously described methods (Haapalainen et al., 2022; Sakane et al., 2024a). Each strain was cultured in potato dextrose broth (PDB) at 25°C for 3 days on a rotary shaker at 150 rpm. The resulting cultures were filtered through three layers of sterile gauze to harvest conidia, and the suspension was adjusted to 8 × 105 conidia/mL. Onion seeds were surface-sterilized in 0.8% sodium hypochlorite for 1 min and rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. The disinfected seeds were soaked for 1 h in a conidial suspension of each test strain or in sterile water (control) and then sown in 20 × 20 cm plastic pots (50 seeds per pot; three replicate pots per strain). All pots were incubated in a growth chamber at 25°C under a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod. Two weeks after sowing, disease severity was assessed using a 0–2 scale: 0 = symptomless; 1 = mild symptoms, including growth retardation, poor root development, or root yellowing; 2 = severe symptoms such as root darkening, wilting, collapse, or non-emergence. For each pot, individual seedling scores were averaged to obtain a mean disease severity value per pot, which was used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Onion bulb assay

The pathogenicity of Fusarium strains on onion bulbs was evaluated with slight modifications to a previously described method (Sakane et al., 2024a). Each strain was cultured on PDA plates and incubated at 25°C for five days. Onion bulbs were surface-sterilized by immersion in 0.8% sodium hypochlorite for 1 min, followed by three rinses with sterile distilled water. An 8-mm-diameter cavity was made into the basal plate of each bulb using a cork borer. Mycelial plugs of the same size, excised from the actively growing margins of fungal colonies, were inserted into the cavities. PDA plugs without fungal mycelium served as negative controls. The inoculated bulbs were placed in sealed humid boxes containing moistened paper towels and incubated at 25°C. After three weeks, bulbs were longitudinally sectioned through the inoculation site and photographed. The symptomatic area, including brown discoloration and/or visible mycelial growth, was quantified using ImageJ software. Lesion measurements were performed under a blinded protocol, in which the analyst was unaware of the isolates’ effector gene profiles. Each treatment included four replicate bulbs.

Primer and probe design

Genomic DNA from F. oxysporum isolates 19–151 and 19–188, and F. commune isolates 19–183 and 22–098, was PCR-amplified using primers SIX9-F and SIX9-R. The resulting amplicons were sequenced by BIOFACT and deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PV930264 (F. oxysporum 19–151), PV930265 (F. oxysporum 19–188), PV930266 (F. commune 19–183) and PV930267 (F. commune 22–098). Published SIX9 sequences from F. oxysporum formae speciales—f. sp. pisi (MT710731, MT710730), f. sp. lycopersici (KR855731, KR855730), f. sp. niveum (KR855729, KX435041), f. sp. cubense (KX435016, KX435017), f. sp. passiflorae (KR855724, KX435047) and f. sp. cepae (MW939406, MW939420, MW939414)—were retrieved from GenBank and aligned using MAFFT v.7 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Based on conserved regions identified from the alignment of F. oxysporum f. sp. cepae sequences and our newly obtained amplicons, a quantitative PCR primer pair (SIX9-Q-F and SIX9-Q-R) targeting a 113-bp fragment was designed (Table 1). Primers and a hydrolysis (TaqMan) probe were designed using the PrimerQuest tool (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA), and their specificity was confirmed by BLASTn searches against the NCBI nucleotide database. All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Bioneer. The probe was labeled with 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM) at the 5′ end and Black Hole Quencher 1 (BHQ-1) at the 3′ end.

Real-time qPCR assay

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System with QuantStudio Design and Analysis Software v1.5.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.5 μM each of forward and reverse primers, 0.2 μM TaqMan probe, 7 μL of nuclease-free water, and 1 μL of DNA template. For soil-derived samples, 5 μL of template DNA was used. All reactions were performed in technical triplicates, with nuclease-free water included as a no-template control (NTC). Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial activation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Samples with cycle threshold (Ct) values less than 36 were considered positive.

Standard curves were generated using 10-fold serial dilutions of genomic DNA extracted from F. oxysporum 19–188 and F. commune 22–098, ranging from 10 ng/μL to 1 pg/μL. For each dilution, the Ct value was plotted against the logarithm of DNA concentration, and linear regression analysis was conducted. Amplification efficiency (E) was calculated from the slope (S) of the standard curve using the formula: E = (10(-1/S) − 1) × 100.

To evaluate detection sensitivity in soil, 2 g of potting soil was inoculated with 2 mL of 10-fold serially diluted conidial suspensions (107 to 101 conidia/mL) of F. oxysporum 19–188 and F. commune 22–098, resulting in final concentrations of 107 to 101 conidia/g of soil. Control samples consisted of soil inoculated with sterile distilled water. Total DNA was extracted from 250 mg of each soil sample using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and subjected to qPCR as described above.

Assay specificity was assessed using genomic DNA from 25 fungal isolates, including seven target and 15 non-target Fusarium species, as well as three additional onion pathogens: Sclerotium cepivorum, Stemphylium vesicarium, and Botrytis aclada (Table 2). Each reaction contained 5 ng of genomic DNA and was performed under the same qPCR conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the effects of the presence or type of SIX and other effector genes (including CRX1, CRX2, and C5) on pathogenicity, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted with isolate as a random effect, accounting for heteroscedasticity. Pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were performed with Tukey adjustment. The F. oxysporum group carrying only SIX9 (n = 1) was excluded from statistical analyses due to insufficient sample size. The correlation between seedling disease severity and bulb rot severity was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis.

Results

Identification of Fusarium spp. causing onion basal rot

To identify the fungal pathogens associated with FBR in onions, Fusarium isolates were obtained from diseased onion tissues and rhizosphere soils collected from FBR-affected fields across South Korea between 2016 and 2024.

A total of 34 isolates were subjected to phylogenetic analysis based on a concatenated sequence dataset of the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1) and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2) gene sequences using the ML method (Fig. 1). The resulting phylogenetic tree resolved the isolates into three major clades corresponding to F. oxysporum (16 isolates), F. commune (11 isolates), and F. proliferatum (7 isolates), each clustering with their respective reference strains.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of Fusarium spp. based on combined translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1) and RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (rpb2) gene sequences. The tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Fusarium dimerum was used as the outgroup. Bootstrap values greater than 50% are shown at the corresponding nodes.

Within the F. oxysporum clade, several isolates (e.g., 22–194, 23–147, 23–148, and 23–152) formed a well-supported subclade with high bootstrap support (≥ 98). Notably, these isolates possessed a common set of virulence-associated SIX genes (SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14), indicating a shared pathogenicity-related genetic background among these F. oxysporum isolates (Supplementary Table 3). Isolates of F. proliferatum and F. commune were clearly separated into species-specific clades, clustering with reference strains such as CBS 480.77 and NRRL 25028 (F. proliferatum), and CBS 110090 and LC18583 (F. commune), with strong bootstrap support.

SIX genes and other virulence-related genes in Fusarium isolates

The presence of all 14 known SIX genes, along with three additional effector candidates (C5, CRX1, and CRX2), was assessed by PCR in 16 isolates of F. oxysporum, 11 isolates of F. commune, and 7 isolates of F. proliferatum (Supplementary Table 3). For F. proliferatum, species-specific primers targeting FprSIX2-1, FprSIX2-2, and a F. proliferatum-specific variant of CRX2 were additionally used (Supplementary Table 4).

Among the F. oxysporum isolates, four strains harbored a distinct combination of SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14, along with C5, CRX1, and CRX2. Notably, C5 and CRX1 were exclusively detected in this group. Another six isolates carried SIX2 and SIX9, two of which also harbored CRX2. One isolate possessed only SIX9 and CRX2. The remaining five isolates lacked all detectable SIX genes, although four of them carried CRX2.

In F. commune, C5 was not detected in any isolate. Three isolates carried both SIX2 and SIX9 but lacked CRX1 and CRX2. Another three harbored SIX9 only, two of which also carried CRX2. Among the five isolates lacking all SIX genes, two harbored both CRX1 and CRX2.

In F. proliferatum, none of the canonical SIX1 and SIX3–SIX14, C5, CRX1, or CRX2 genes were amplified using primer sets originally designed for F. oxysporum. However, all seven F. proliferatum isolates carried the SIX2-1 homolog and a species-specific CRX2 variant. Additionally, three isolates also harbored a second SIX2 homolog (SIX2-2).

Virulence of different Fusarium isolates to onion seedlings

Pathogenicity assays on onion seedlings revealed significant differences in virulence among isolates with different SIX gene profiles (Fig. 2A–2D, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Among F. oxysporum isolates, those harboring a combination of SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14, together with C5, CRX1, and CRX2, exhibited significantly higher virulence on seedlings compared to other groups (Fig. 2B). Among the remaining F. oxysporum isolates, the presence of CRX2 was not significantly associated with differences in virulence (P = 0.6153).

In F. commune, isolates lacking all SIX genes generally showed low virulence. Notably, among the SIX-negative isolates, those carrying both CRX1 and CRX2 tended to exhibit higher virulence than SIX-negative isolates lacking these genes (P = 0.0486) (Fig. 2C), though further validation with a larger number of isolates is needed to confirm this trend. Within the group of SIX-positive F. commune isolates, the presence of CRX2 did not significantly affect virulence (P = 0.2036).

In F. proliferatum, no significant difference in seedling virulence was observed between isolates carrying SIX2-1 alone and those carrying both SIX2-1 and SIX2-2 (P = 0.4295) (Fig. 2D).

Virulence of different Fusarium isolates to onion bulbs

Pathogenicity assays on onion bulbs also revealed significant differences in virulence according to SIX gene profiles, with effects more pronounced than those observed in seedling assays (Fig. 3A–3D, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). In F. oxysporum, isolates harboring the effector suite SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14 consistently exhibited high virulence (Fig. 3B). Among the remaining SIX profile groups, the presence of CRX2 was not significantly associated with differences in bulb rot severity (P = 0.0583).

Development and validation of a SIX9-targeted qPCR assay

SIX9 was frequently detected in Fusarium isolates exhibiting high pathogenicity on onion seedlings and bulbs. To evaluate its prevalence in the field, conventional PCR screening was performed on 94 Fusarium isolates collected from FBR-affected onion fields across South Korea between 2016 and 2024 (Supplementary Table 5). Among these, 52.1% were SIX9-positive, and most fields contained mixed populations of SIX9-positive and SIX9-negative isolates. Given its correlation with pathogenicity and widespread occurrence among field isolates, SIX9 was selected as a molecular target to develop a quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay that specifically detects SIX9-positive Fusarium isolates—mainly F. oxysporum and F. commune—which are enriched for higher virulence on onion. Primers and a TaqMan probe were designed to specifically amplify the SIX9 allele associated with onion-pathogenic Fusarium isolates. Standard curves generated from 10-fold serial dilutions of genomic DNA from F. oxysporum 19–188 and F. commune 22–098 showed strong linear relationships between Ct values and log10-transformed DNA concentrations, with a detection limit of 1 pg under laboratory conditions (Fig. 5A and 5B).

Standard curves for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). (A, B) Standard curves generated from 10-fold serial dilutions (10 ng to 1 pg) of genomic DNA from F. oxysporum 19–188 (A) and F. commune 22–098 (B). (C, D) Standard curves generated from soil samples spiked with 107 to 104 conidia/g soil of F. oxysporum 19–188 (C) and F. commune 22–098 (D). Each genomic DNA dilution was tested in triplicate. For soil-based assays, four biological replicates were analyzed per concentration, each with three technical replicates. Amplification efficiency (E) and the coefficient of determination (R2) were calculated from the linear regression of each curve.

To assess assay performance in soil, soil samples were spiked with conidial suspensions ranging from 107 to 101 conidia/g soil. The qPCR assay consistently detected target DNA at 104 conidia/g soil for both F. oxysporum and F. commune, with all four biological replicates yielding Ct values below 36 (95% CI: 0.51–1.00; Fig. 5C and 5D). In contrast, amplification at ≤103 conidia/g soil was inconsistent or undetectable. Accordingly, 104 conidia/g soil was defined as the lowest concentration showing consistent detection under soil conditions.

Assay specificity was tested using genomic DNA from 25 fungal isolates, including seven SIX9-positive Fusarium isolates from onion, 15 non-target Fusarium isolates, and three additional onion pathogens: Sclerotium cepivorum, Stemphylium vesicarium, and Botrytis aclada (Table 2). The presence or absence of SIX9 in each isolate was confirmed by conventional PCR prior to qPCR analysis. Specific amplification occurred only in SIX9-positive Fusarium isolates from onion. No cross-reactivity was observed with non-target Fusarium species or other onion pathogens. Notably, F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici, which harbors a distinct SIX9 homologue, was not amplified, confirming the high specificity of the assay for the onion-pathogenic SIX9 variant.

Discussion

Effector proteins play a critical role in the infection process of plant-pathogenic fungi by facilitating host colonization and suppressing host immune responses. In Fusarium species, the Secreted in Xylem (SIX) proteins represent a well-characterized group of effectors that contribute to virulence (Rep et al., 2004). In addition to the canonical SIX genes, recent studies have identified additional candidate effectors—CRX1, CRX2, and C5— in Fusarium strains pathogenic to onion (Haapalainen et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2016).

In this study, we profiled 14 SIX genes along with CRX1, CRX2, and C5 across 34 Fusarium isolates collected from FBR-affected onion fields in South Korea. F. oxysporum isolates carrying a complete effector suite—SIX3, SIX5, SIX7, SIX9, SIX10, SIX12, and SIX14—together with CRX1, CRX2, and C5, consistently exhibited high virulence in both seedling and bulb inoculation assays (Figs. 2 and 3). Similar effector combinations have previously been associated with strong pathogenicity in onion (Haapalainen et al., 2023; Sakane et al., 2024a; Sasaki et al., 2015; Taylor et al., 2016).

In contrast, F. commune isolates exhibited more limited SIX gene profiles, typically carrying SIX9 alone, both SIX2 and SIX9, or lacking SIX genes entirely. Notably, SIX-negative isolates harboring both CRX1 and CRX2 exhibited strong virulence, whereas isolates lacking both SIX and CRX genes were weakly pathogenic (Figs. 2 and 3). Although based on a limited number of isolates, these data suggest a possible compensatory role for CRX effectors in the pathogenicity of F. commune. Expanding the isolate collection and experimentally characterizing these candidate effectors will be important to substantiate this hypothesis.

All F. proliferatum isolates in this study carried a homolog of the SIX2 gene (SIX2-1) and exhibited moderate virulence in onion pathogenicity assays (Figs. 2 and 3). Although a previous report reported a marginal increase in virulence among CRX2-positive F. proliferatum strains (Haapalainen et al., 2023), all isolates in our collection carried CRX2, precluding direct comparison.

All pathogenicity assays in this study were conducted using a single onion cultivar, ‘Hwangryongball’. Given the potential for host genotype × effector interactions in Fusarium–Allium pathosystems, the disease responses observed here may not fully represent those of other onion genotypes. Future validation using multiple cultivars will be valuable for assessing the generality of these virulence patterns. Among all genes analyzed, SIX9 showed broad association with pathogenicity in F. oxysporum and F. commune isolates. Previous studies have reported that SIX9 expression is upregulated during onion and Welsh onion infection (Armitage et al., 2018; Haapalainen et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2016), supporting its potential role in host colonization. However, the presence of SIX9 alone does not necessarily indicate its expression or functional contribution to virulence. SIX9 is also conserved across multiple formae speciales of F. oxysporum, including f. sp. lycopersici, pisi, cubense, radicis-cucumerinum, and sesami (Carvalhais et al., 2019; Duan et al., 2020; Jenkins et al., 2021; Thatcher et al., 2012; van Dam et al., 2017), and is frequently detected in endophytic strains (Constantin et al., 2021). These findings suggest that SIX9 may play a role in general colonization processes rather than being limited to strict host specificity. Further functional and expression studies will be necessary to clarify the mechanistic role of SIX9 in the Fusarium–onion interaction.

Given its prevalence among F. oxysporum and F. commune isolates from onion, we developed a TaqMan qPCR assay targeting the SIX9 gene (Fig. 5). The assay exhibited high specificity, effectively distinguishing onion-associated SIX9 variants from homologs present in other formae speciales, such as F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici. While previous detection methods for onion-pathogenic Fusarium have targeted SIX3 (Latvala et al., 2020), our results indicate that SIX3-positive isolates are relatively rare among Korean field populations. In contrast, SIX9 was detected more frequently, supporting its value as a practical diagnostic target in this region. Importantly, the SIX9-targeted assay does not detect F. proliferatum, which lacks the canonical SIX9 gene, and should therefore be regarded as a tool for identifying SIX9-positive Fusarium spp.—primarily F. oxysporum and F. commune—that are associated with higher virulence potential.

The developed qPCR assay reliably detected Fusarium DNA in soil at concentrations as low as 104 conidia/g soil. This threshold corresponds to the level at which significant reductions in onion seedling emergence and the onset of disease symptoms have been observed (Kim et al., 2024). indicating the assay’s utility for early detection and risk assessment in FBR management.

In summary, this study highlights the diversity of effector repertoires in Fusarium spp. infecting onion and underscores the diagnostic value of SIX9 as a widely distributed gene enriched among aggressive isolates in our dataset. The qPCR assay developed herein provides a practical tool for the early detection of SIX9-positive F. oxysporum and F. commune populations in soil and may facilitate the implementation of integrated disease management strategies for Fusarium basal rot.
