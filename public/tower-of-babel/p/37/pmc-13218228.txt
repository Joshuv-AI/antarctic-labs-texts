Calcium peroxide loaded liposomes reprogram hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment to overcome lenvatinib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yaowei Bai, Xiaoye Li, Yu Lei, Wei Yao, Xiatong Bai, Yang Su, Yao Lu, Chuansheng Zheng, Xuefeng Kan, Yingliang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLenvatinib resistance severely limits the therapeutic efficacy in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma and is increasingly recognized as a systemic adaptive state driven by the hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment. However, effective strategies targeting these upstream constraints remain lacking.MethodsCalcium peroxide–loaded liposomes (CaO2@Lipo) were synthesized to relieve hypoxia, neutralize acidity, and disrupt calcium homeostasis. Physicochemical properties and oxygen/pH-regulating capacity were characterized. Lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma models were established in vitro and in orthotopic tumors. The therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms of CaO2@Lipo, alone or combined with lenvatinib, were evaluated through cellular functional assays, histological analysis, immune profiling, and transcriptomic sequencing.ResultsCaO2@Lipo induced pronounced calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to marked cytotoxicity in resistant cells. In orthotopic resistant models, CaO2@Lipo significantly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of lenvatinib, resulting in robust tumor suppression and prolonged survival without evident systemic toxicity. Mechanistically, combined treatment markedly downregulated the hypoxia associated resistance signaling axis involving HIF-1α, VEGF, CA9 and ABCB1. Moreover, CaO2@Lipo triggered immunogenic cell death, enhanced dendritic cell infiltration, increased CD8+ T cell recruitment and shifted systemic cytokine profiles toward a proinflammatory phenotype.ConclusionCaO2@Lipo reprograms the hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment, dismantles resistance sustaining networks and restores lenvatinib responsiveness in hepatocellular carcinoma.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Lenvatinib resistance, Hypoxia, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma is the third-leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1,2]. Although there have been advancements in molecular targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the outcomes for advanced HCC remain suboptimal [3,4]. Lenvatinib, a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) targeting VEGFR and FGFR pathways, is a standard first-line therapy based on the Phase III REFLECT trial showing non-inferior overall survival (OS) compared with sorafenib (13.6 vs. 12.3 months) and improved progression-free survival (PFS) (7.3 vs. 3.6 months) in unresectable HCC [5,6]. However, real-world studies consistently show that most patients develop acquired resistance within 6–8 months, with >70% experiencing disease progression within one year [7,8]. Thus, lenvatinib resistance represents a major barrier to durable clinical benefit.

While traditional studies focus on tumor-intrinsic mechanisms [[9], [10], [11]], increasing evidence indicates that the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a decisive role in lenvatinib resistance [[12], [13], [14]]. Lenvatinib-induced vascular dysfunction aggravates intratumoral hypoxia and lactate accumulation, fostering aggressive phenotypes and therapeutic tolerance [8]. Hypoxia and acidosis act synergistically to establish a microenvironmental adaptive state that sustains resistant phenotypes even when target pathways are pharmacologically inhibited [15]. Sustained hypoxia activates HIF-1α signaling, driving metabolic reprogramming, extracellular acidification, and drug efflux, thereby reinforcing this adaptive state and explaining the limited durability of single-target interventions [16].

Accordingly, recent efforts have shifted toward remodeling the tumor microenvironment as a strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance [[17], [18], [19]]. Accumulating evidence indicates that direct alleviation of adverse microenvironmental stresses, particularly hypoxia and acidity, can enhance tumor responsiveness to tyrosine kinase inhibitors by weakening microenvironment-driven adaptive programs [17]. However, most existing approaches focus on single microenvironmental components, and studies aiming to comprehensively target the interconnected hypoxia–acidosis network remain limited, leaving effective and durable microenvironment reprogramming an unresolved challenge.

Calcium peroxide (CaO2) decomposes to release oxygen and alkaline ions, enabling simultaneous hypoxia relief and acidity neutralization, while Ca2+ perturbs calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial metabolism [20]. However, free CaO2 suffers from poor stability and low bioavailability. Specifically, naked CaO2 undergoes rapid, uncontrolled hydrolysis in physiological aqueous environments, leading to premature drug leakage and potential systemic toxicity [21]. To address this, liposomal encapsulation provides a robust hydrophobic barrier that prevents premature water penetration during systemic circulation [21]. This lipid shielding significantly enhances the colloidal stability and circulation half-life of CaO2, ensuring effective tumor-targeted delivery [22]. Notably, our previous study demonstrated that nanoscale CaO2 materials could modulate the tumor microenvironment and enhance therapeutic efficacy in liver cancer treatment, providing a rationale for further development of CaO2-based strategies [23]. Here, we developed a CaO2-loaded liposomal system (CaO2@Lipo) to achieve sustained microenvironment modulation. Using in vitro and in vivo lenvatinib-resistant models, we demonstrate that CaO2@Lipo disrupts the hypoxia–acidosis adaptive network, reverses resistant phenotypes, and restores therapeutic sensitivity. This study provides evidence for a microenvironment-based strategy to overcome lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

Methods

Materials and reagents

All chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade or cell-culture grade unless otherwise stated. Calcium peroxide (CaO2), dioctyl phosphate (DOPA), 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC), cholesterol, and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol 2000 (DSPE-PEG2000) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). Triton X-100, cyclohexane, n-hexanol, calcium chloride (CaCl2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), ammonium hydroxide (25–28%), 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), Alizarin Red S, JC-1, and coumarin-6 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Lenvatinib was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kits were obtained from Dojindo Laboratories (Kumamoto, Japan). ELISA kits for TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10 were purchased from MultiSciences (Hangzhou, China). High-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin–streptomycin solution were obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Paraformaldehyde (4%), DAPI, methanol, crystal violet, absolute ethanol, and chloroform were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of CaO2@Lipo

CaO2 nanoparticles were synthesized using a reverse microemulsion method and subsequently coated with a lipid bilayer. Briefly, a microemulsion system (cyclohexane:Triton X-100:n-hexanol = 75:15:10, v/v/v) was prepared. Eight milliliters of this system was mixed with 50 μL ammonium hydroxide and 120 μL DOPA/chloroform solution (25 mg/mL) and magnetically stirred for 10 min to form the aqueous-phase microemulsion. Another equal volume of microemulsion containing 50 μL H2O2 and 50 μL CaCl2 (2 M) was prepared and stirred for 10 min to form the oil-phase microemulsion. The two microemulsions were then mixed and stirred for 30 min at room temperature, allowing Ca2+ and H2O2 to react under alkaline catalysis to form CaO2 nanocrystals.

15 mL absolute ethanol was then added to break the emulsion, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. The precipitate was washed three times with absolute ethanol and finally dispersed in 3 mL chloroform to obtain DOPA-modified CaO2 nanoparticles (CaO2@DOPA). DMPC, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG2000 were added to the CaO2@DOPA chloroform dispersion at a molar ratio of 4:4:1. The mixture was rotary-evaporated at 37 °C and 60 rpm for 30 min to remove chloroform and form a uniform lipid film. The lipid film was hydrated with 3 mL ultrapure water at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by probe sonication (200 W, 3 s on/5 s off, total 10 min) to obtain CaO2@Lipo. The suspension was stored at 4 °C protected from light until further use.

Physicochemical characterization of CaO2@Lipo

A small volume of CaO2@Lipo suspension was dropped onto copper grids and air-dried, and the morphology and dispersity of the nanoparticles were observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The CaO2@Lipo suspension was diluted with ultrapure water to an appropriate concentration, and the hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential were measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Each sample was measured in triplicate. For crystal structure and elemental composition analysis, CaO2@Lipo suspensions were lyophilized and analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany), with comparison to the standard diffraction pattern of pure CaO2. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to determine elemental composition and chemical states. For ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption analysis, CaO2@Lipo formulations prepared with different lipid component ratios were scanned from 200 to 800 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), and the absorption spectra were recorded to evaluate formulation stability.

Evaluation of oxygen-generating and pH-regulating capacity of CaO2@Lipo

To evaluate oxygen-generating capacity, 1 mL of CaO2@Lipo suspension (final CaO2 concentration: 1 mM) was added to 8 mL of ultrapure water preheated to 37 °C and incubated in a thermostatic shaker at 37 °C and 100 rpm. The dissolved oxygen concentration was continuously monitored using a dissolved oxygen meter, with data recorded every 10 min for 24 h. Ultrapure water served as the blank control. To evaluate pH-regulating capacity, 1 mL of CaO2@Lipo suspension (final CaO2 concentration: 1 mM) was added to 8 mL of buffer solutions with different pH values (pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C. The pH of each solution was measured at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h using a pH meter. Each group was tested in triplicate. In addition, redox-related properties of CaO2@Lipo were evaluated using colorimetric assays (Supplementary Appendix 1).

Establishment of lenvatinib-resistant cell lines

The resistant cell lines were established as previously described [24,25]. HuH-7 and Hepa1-6 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates. After cell attachment, lenvatinib was added at a final concentration of 0.5 μM for induction. The lenvatinib concentration was gradually increased every 1–2 weeks according to cell growth status (increment of 0.5 μM each time) over 4–6 months. Stably surviving cells were selected and designated as HuH-7-LR and Hepa1-6-LR, and were routinely maintained in medium containing a maintenance concentration of lenvatinib.

Parental cells (HuH-7 and Hepa1-6) and resistant cells (HuH-7-LR and Hepa1-6-LR) were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were treated with lenvatinib at different concentrations (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 μM), with three replicate wells per group. After 48 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated. Dose–response curves were fitted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 to calculate IC50 values. A resistance index (IC50 of resistant cells/IC50 of parental cells) ≥ 5 was defined as successful establishment of lenvatinib-resistant cell lines.

Cellular uptake study

Hepa1-6-LR cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into confocal culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing Coumarin-6–labeled CaO2@Lipo (final Coumarin-6 concentration: 5 μg/mL), and cells were incubated for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 24 h. After incubation, the medium was removed and cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed again with PBS, and stained with DAPI for 5 min in the dark. After three additional PBS washes, intracellular fluorescence distribution and intensity of Coumarin-6 were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Coumarin-6: excitation 458 nm, emission 500–550 nm; DAPI: excitation 350 nm, emission 460 nm).

In vitro evaluation of antitumor effects

The effects of different treatments on lenvatinib-resistant HuH-7-LR and Hepa1-6-LR cells were evaluated using CCK-8 cell viability assays, colony formation assays, wound-healing migration assays, and Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry for apoptosis analysis. These assays were performed to assess cellular proliferation, long-term clonogenic potential, migratory capacity, and apoptosis induction, respectively. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 2.

Intracellular calcium deposition assay

Hepa1-6-LR cells were seeded into confocal culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h, followed by incubation with CaO2@Lipo for an additional 24 h. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and washed again with PBS. Cells were stained with Alizarin Red S solution (pH 4.2) at room temperature for 30 min. After washing thoroughly with running water to remove excess dye and air-drying, intracellular calcium deposition was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Red fluorescence was considered positive for calcium deposition. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

Hepa1-6-LR cells were seeded into confocal culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h, followed by incubation with CaO2@Lipo for another 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Cells were then washed twice with PBS, fresh medium was added, and fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. JC-1 aggregates (red fluorescence; excitation 549 nm, emission 590 nm) indicated normal mitochondrial membrane potential, whereas JC-1 monomers (green fluorescence; excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm) indicated decreased mitochondrial membrane potential. The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was calculated to evaluate changes in mitochondrial membrane potential.

Limiting dilution tumorigenicity assay

To evaluate the tumor-initiating capacity of resistant cells, a limiting dilution transplantation assay was performed. HuH-7 and HuH-7-LR cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, prepared as single-cell suspensions, and serially diluted to concentrations of 1 × 105, 1 × 104, 1 × 103, and 1 × 102 cells per 100 μL. Each dilution was injected subcutaneously into the axilla of NOD/SCID mice (n = 5 per dose). Mice were maintained under standard conditions and monitored for 6 weeks. Tumor incidence was recorded weekly. At the experimental endpoint, tumor formation rates at each dose were calculated, and tumor-initiating cell frequency was determined using ELDA (Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis) software. Differences in tumorigenic capacity between parental and resistant cells were compared.

In vivo biodistribution and fluorescence imaging

To investigate the in vivo distribution of CaO2@Lipo, liposomes were labeled with the near-infrared fluorescent dye DiR (DiR/CaO2@Lipo). The preparation method was identical to that of CaO2@Lipo, except that DiR was added during the lipid film formation step at a lipid molar ratio of 0.5%. After establishing the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model, DiR/CaO2@Lipo was administered via tail vein injection. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed at 1, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection using a small-animal in vivo imaging system, with excitation at 748 nm and emission at 780 nm.

In vitro tumor cell–dendritic cell coculture and immune-associated assays

Hepa1-6-LR cells were treated with CaO2@Lipo at the indicated concentration and time points and subsequently cocultured with dendritic cells using a transwell system. Tumor cell–derived danger-associated molecular patterns, including HMGB1 and extracellular ATP, were quantified from culture supernatants. Dendritic cell activation–associated changes were evaluated by flow cytometric analysis of MHC class II expression. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 3.

Establishment of orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma models

For the HuH-7-LR orthotopic model, HuH-7-LR cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL. Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). A left abdominal incision (∼0.5 cm) was made to expose the left liver lobe, and 50 μL of the cell suspension (containing 5 × 105 cells) was slowly injected into the parenchyma of the left hepatic lobe using a microsyringe. The incision was then sutured, and mice were maintained under standard conditions. Tumor formation was confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) two weeks after implantation.

For the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic model, Hepa1-6-LR cells were prepared in the same manner (1 × 107 cells/mL). Six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized, and 50 μL of the cell suspension (5 × 105 cells) was injected into the left hepatic lobe. Mice were maintained postoperatively, and tumor formation was confirmed by MRI after two weeks.

Animal grouping and treatment protocol

For the HuH-7-LR model, tumor-bearing nude mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Vehicle group (tail vein injection of equal-volume saline); (2) Lenvatinib group (oral administration, 30 mg/kg, once daily); (3) CaO2@Lipo group (tail vein injection, CaO2 dose 5 mg/kg, once every 3 days); (4) Combination group (CaO2@Lipo + Lenvatinib, administered at the same doses and schedules as the monotherapy groups). For the Hepa1-6-LR model, C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group) using the same grouping strategy and dosing regimen. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the ARRIVE [26] and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval number: HUST-IACUC-2026-0023)

In vivo therapeutic evaluation

Treatment was continued for 2 weeks. Body weight and tumor progression were monitored every 3 days. At the end of treatment, mice were anesthetized, blood samples were collected, and mice were then euthanized. Tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were excised, and tumor weights were recorded. Portions of tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while others were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis.

In parallel experiments using the same grouping and treatment regimens, mice were continuously monitored for survival. Survival time was recorded, and survival curves were generated.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

All immunohistochemical staining analyses were performed using tumor tissues derived from the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model established in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed to evaluate tissue injury, tumor necrosis, hypoxia-related signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis. Tumor and major organs were subjected to H&E staining to assess histopathological changes and quantify tumor necrosis. Immunohistochemistry was conducted to examine the expression of hypoxia- and resistance-associated markers, including HIF-1α, VEGF, CA9, and ABCB1. In addition, immunofluorescence staining for Ki67 and TUNEL was used to assess tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis, respectively. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 4.

Measurement of intratumoral pH and hypoxia

Tumor extracellular pH (pHe) and oxygen partial pressure (pO2) were measured using microelectrodes in tumor-bearing mice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane during measurements. For pH determination, a pH microelectrode (MI-408B) and reference electrode (MI-401F) connected to a pH meter (CyberScan pH 11, Thermo Scientific) were used. The reference electrode was placed subcutaneously near the tumor, and the pH electrode was inserted into the tumor core (∼1.0–1.3 cm). For pO2 measurement, a needle-type oxygen microelectrode (OX-100, Unisense) was pre-polarized and calibrated prior to use, then inserted into the tumor and advanced stepwise (0.5 mm) from the surface to the core. Values were recorded after stabilization at each position. Measurements from multiple locations (≥3 for pH, ≥5 for pO2) were averaged. For the direct visualization of tumor hypoxia, the hypoxia probe RDPP ([Ru(dpp)3]Cl2) was utilized. Tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with RDPP (10 mg/kg) 4 h prior to sacrifice. The excised tumor tissues were embedded in OCT compound, cryosectioned, and counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. The fluorescence signals indicating severe hypoxia were then observed and captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (excitation: 488 nm, emission: 610–620 nm). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software to reflect the level of intratumoral hypoxia.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues of the CaO2@Lipo + lenvatinib group and the vehicle group. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using a standard protocol and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were quality-controlled and aligned to the reference genome for gene expression quantification. Differentially expressed genes were identified using predefined statistical criteria. KEGG pathway and functional enrichment analyses were performed to characterize affected biological pathways. Genes related to the HIF-1 signaling pathway were further analyzed for expression changes. Differentially expressed genes were defined as those with |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05 (Supplementary Appendix 5).

Measurement of intracellular and extracellular ATP levels

Fresh tumor tissues were rinsed three times with prechilled PBS to remove blood and blotted dry with filter paper. Tissues were divided into two portions for extracellular and intracellular ATP determination, respectively.

For extracellular ATP measurement, approximately 50 mg of tumor tissue was added to 1 mL of prechilled serum-free DMEM, gently homogenized on ice for 30 s, and centrifuged at 4 °C and 8000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected. ATP levels were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of working solution was added to each well of a white 96-well luminescence plate and incubated for 5 min, followed by addition of 20 μL of standards or samples. After incubation at room temperature in the dark for 10 min, luminescence was recorded, and extracellular ATP concentration was calculated using a standard curve.

For intracellular ATP measurement, approximately 50 mg of tumor tissue was added to 1 mL of prechilled lysis buffer, homogenized and lysed on ice for 15 min, and centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected as the intracellular ATP extract, and ATP content was determined as described above.

Flow cytometry analysis

All flow cytometric analyses were performed using tumor tissues derived from the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma model established in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Fresh tumor tissues were collected, necrotic areas were removed, and tissues were cut into ∼1 mm3 fragments. Samples were digested in DMEM containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and hyaluronidase (0.5 mg/mL) at 37 °C with shaking for 30 min. An equal volume of DMEM containing 10% FBS was added to terminate digestion. The suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions, centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS.

Cells were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (CD8a-PE and MHC II–FITC, BD Biosciences, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing twice with PBS, cells were resuspended and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the proportions of CD8+ T cells (CD8a+) and dendritic cells (MHC II+).

Serum cytokine analysis

Mouse serum was collected and centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Supernatants were harvested, and concentrations of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10 were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves.

Biosafety evaluation

First, the biological safety of CaO2@Lipo was evaluated via the hemolysis assay (Supplementary Appendix 6). In addition, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from each treatment group were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining. Histopathological changes, including inflammatory infiltration, cellular necrosis, and structural damage, were examined under a light microscope. Serum biochemical parameters were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer, including liver function indicators (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total bilirubin [TBIL], and albumin [ALB]) and renal function indicators (creatinine [CRE] and blood urea nitrogen [BUN]), to assess potential hepatic and renal toxicity.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and in vivo experiments included at least five mice per group. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Construction and physicochemical characterization of CaO2@Lipo

To develop a functional nanosystem for tumor microenvironment modulation, CaO2 nanocrystals were first synthesized using a reverse microemulsion method and subsequently encapsulated into liposomes to construct CaO2@Lipo (Supplementary Fig. 1). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that CaO2@Lipo exhibited a uniform quasi-spherical morphology with good dispersibility (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the bare CaO2 possessed an amorphous and irregular morphology, characterized by disordered flakes, massive chunks, or random aggregates without uniform geometric outlines. Dynamic light scattering analysis showed a narrow hydrodynamic size distribution, low polydispersity index, and stable ζ-potential, indicating good colloidal stability of the formulation (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 2). XRD patterns of both bare CaO2 and CaO2@Lipo matched the standard PDF card, confirming the successful incorporation of calcium peroxide into the liposomal structure (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, TEM elemental mapping analysis confirmed the uniform co-distribution of Ca, O, C, N, and P elements within the spherical CaO2@Lipo nanoparticle (Fig. 1D), whereas bare CaO2 nanoparticles exhibited only Ca and O signals (Supplementary Fig. 3). This direct elemental contrast provides clear proof for the intact encapsulation of the CaO2 core within the outer phospholipid bilayer. Further kinetic analysis demonstrated that CaO2@Lipo exhibited sustained decomposition behavior (Fig. 1E), with a markedly accelerated release rate under mildly acidic conditions (Fig. 1F), suggesting tumor microenvironment–responsive properties. Meanwhile, starting from a deoxygenated state, CaO2@Lipo continuously generated oxygen (Fig. 1G) and significantly increased the pH of the system (Fig. 1H), indicating its dual capability to alleviate hypoxia and neutralize acidity.

In addition to oxygen generation and pH regulation, the redox-modulating capability of CaO2@Lipo was assessed using a DTNB-based glutathione (GSH) depletion assay. UV–Vis absorption spectra showed that increasing concentrations of CaO2@Lipo led to a progressive decrease in the absorbance of the TNB product peak in the 400–450 nm range (Fig. 1I), indicating concentration-dependent GSH depletion. Mechanistically, in an acidic environment, CaO2 reacts with protons to generate H2O2, which can be further converted into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via Fenton-like reactions. These abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) then directly oxidize reduced GSH into its oxidized form (GSSG), leading to the observed GSH depletion. To further characterize the reactive behavior of CaO2@Lipo, a dual-colorimetric reporting system was employed, involving hydroxyl radical–mediated oxidation of TMB and H2O2–Ti(SO4)2 complexation (Fig. 1J). Time-dependent UV–Vis spectra revealed progressive TMB oxidation following CaO2@Lipo decomposition (Fig. 1K). In parallel, H2O2 production was further assessed using the Ti(SO4)2 assay, which revealed pH-dependent formation of the H2O2–Ti(SO4)2 complex, with stronger signals at pH 5.5 than at pH 6.5 and 7.4 (Fig. 1L and Supplementary Fig. 4), further supporting the tumor microenvironment–responsive reactivity of CaO2@Lipo.

Establishment and biological characterization of acquired lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma models

By long-term exposure to gradually increasing concentrations of lenvatinib, Hepa1-6-LR and HuH-7-LR acquired-resistant cell lines were successfully established (Fig. 2A). Compared with their parental counterparts, both resistant cell lines exhibited markedly reduced sensitivity to lenvatinib, with significantly increased IC50 values (Fig. 2B and C), indicating the formation of a stable drug-resistant phenotype. At the in vivo level, xenograft tumors subjected to continuous lenvatinib treatment showed a substantially weakened inhibitory response, with significantly reduced tumor growth inhibition rates (Fig. 2D and E), further confirming the stability of the resistant phenotype in vivo.

Beyond drug resistance, resistant cells also displayed pronounced alterations in malignant biological behaviors. Functional assays demonstrated that under lenvatinib pressure, resistant cells retained strong clonogenic capacity (Fig. 2F and G) and migratory ability (Fig. 2H and I), and significantly escaped drug-induced apoptosis (Fig. 2J and K). Furthermore, limiting dilution transplantation assays revealed that HuH-7-LR cells exhibited significantly enhanced tumorigenic capacity and tumor-initiating frequency compared with parental cells (Fig. 2L and M).

CaO2@Lipo induces calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cytotoxicity in lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma cells

To investigate the intracellular behavior and biological effects of CaO2@Lipo in lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma cells, we first examined its cellular uptake. Confocal microscopy demonstrated that Coumarin-6–labeled CaO2@Lipo was efficiently internalized by Hepa1-6-LR cells and showed a time-dependent accumulation pattern (Fig. 3A), indicating effective intracellular delivery in resistant cells. Functional assays revealed that CaO2@Lipo significantly suppressed the viability of resistant cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3B) and markedly impaired their migratory capacity (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting direct disruption of survival and invasive potential. Further mechanistic investigations showed that CaO2@Lipo significantly inhibited clonogenic ability (Fig. 3E and F) and induced apoptosis (Fig. 3G and H) in resistant cells. Alizarin Red S staining demonstrated a pronounced increase in intracellular calcium deposition after treatment (Fig. 3I), indicating severe disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis. Meanwhile, JC-1 staining revealed a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 3J and K), suggesting the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction. To further confirm whether the calcium overload mechanistically drives the observed cytotoxicity, rescue experiments were performed using the intracellular calcium chelator BAPTA-AM in both Hepa1-6-LR and HuH-7-LR cells. Notably, the colony formation assay revealed that the co-treatment of CaO2@Lipo and BAPTA-AM largely rescued the clonogenic capacity of the resistant cells compared to CaO2@Lipo monotherapy, effectively mitigating the severe long-term cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 5). These findings confirm that Ca2+ overload serves as a primary driver of the tumor-killing effect.

In addition to its direct cytotoxic effects, CaO2@Lipo elicited immune-associated responses at the tumor cell level. Under coculture conditions, CaO2@Lipo–treated Hepa1-6-LR cells exhibited increased release of the damage-associated molecular pattern HMGB1, along with elevated extracellular ATP levels in culture supernatants (Fig. 3L and M). Furthermore, coculture with CaO2@Lipo–treated tumor cells resulted in a significantly increased proportion of MHC class II–positive dendritic cells, as determined by flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3N and O), indicating enhanced antigen-presenting DC–associated responses in vitro. In addition, in vivo fluorescence imaging showed that CaO2@Lipo effectively accumulated in orthotopic liver tumors (Fig. 3P), confirming its tumor-targeting and in vivo delivery capability. Furthermore, ex vivo fluorescence imaging at 48 h post-injection demonstrated that, compared to free DiR, CaO2@Lipo specifically accumulated in the orthotopic liver tumor, with negligible signals in other major organs (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To further evaluate whether CaO2@Lipo could reverse lenvatinib resistance, colony formation and apoptosis assays were performed under four treatment conditions: vehicle, lenvatinib alone, CaO2@Lipo alone, and the combination treatment (Fig. 3Q–T). Lenvatinib monotherapy showed limited efficacy in resistant cells, whereas CaO2@Lipo alone inhibited clonogenic growth and induced apoptosis. Notably, the combination treatment resulted in the most pronounced suppression of colony formation and the highest apoptotic rate, indicating that CaO2@Lipo effectively restores lenvatinib sensitivity in resistant hepatocellular carcinoma cells.

CaO2@Lipo enhances the antitumor efficacy of lenvatinib in orthotopic lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma models

The experimental design and treatment schedule for the HuH-7-LR orthotopic model are shown in Fig. 4A. Under this regimen, lenvatinib monotherapy exhibited minimal inhibitory effects on tumor progression (Fig. 4B–E). In contrast, the combination of CaO2@Lipo with lenvatinib resulted in a pronounced reduction in tumor burden (Fig. 4B–E) without inducing significant body weight loss (Fig. 4F). Survival analysis further demonstrated that combination therapy significantly prolonged mouse survival compared with monotherapy or control groups (Fig. 4G). Consistent results were obtained in the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic model, in which the combination treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth and significantly extended survival time (Fig. 4H–N).

CaO2@Lipo reverses lenvatinib resistance by suppressing the hypoxia-driven HIF-1α/VEGF/CA9/ABCB1 signaling axis

Histological analysis showed that combination treatment markedly expanded tumor necrotic areas (Fig. 5A and B) and simultaneously inhibited tumor proliferation while promoting apoptosis (Fig. 5C and D), indicating a pronounced shift in the balance between tumor proliferation and apoptosis in resistant tumors. Besides, compared with the Vehicle and Lenvatinib groups, CaO2@Lipo markedly increased tumor pH and remarkably ameliorated tumor hypoxia in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 7). In addition, intratumoral reactive oxygen species levels were evaluated by immunofluorescence staining. Compared with the vehicle and lenvatinib monotherapy groups, CaO2@Lipo treatment markedly increased ROS signals in tumor tissues, which were further enhanced in the combination group (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 8). Using RDPP hypoxia probe imaging, strong fluorescent signals indicating severe hypoxia were observed in tumors from the Vehicle and lenvatinib-treated groups, while hypoxia signals were significantly reduced in the CaO2@Lipo group (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 9). On this basis, we further investigated the upstream regulatory networks underlying resistance. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that combination treatment significantly downregulated HIF-1α expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 5F), suggesting suppression of hypoxia-associated signaling. Consistent with HIF-1α inhibition, the downstream angiogenic factor VEGF was markedly reduced (Fig. 5G and H), indicating disruption of aberrant angiogenic regulation. Meanwhile, the key mediator of acidic microenvironmental adaptation, CA9, was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5I), suggesting impaired adaptation to an acidic tumor microenvironment. Notably, concomitant with inhibition of hypoxia–acidity–associated signaling, expression of the multidrug efflux transporter ABCB1 was also significantly decreased (Fig. 5J), indicating suppression of a resistance-related drug efflux mechanism.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed a distinct gene expression profile between the CaO2@Lipo + lenvatinib group and the vehicle group (Fig. 5K and L). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in hypoxia-related pathways, particularly the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. 5M). Consistently, genes involved in HIF-1 signaling were broadly downregulated following combination treatment (Fig. 5N). Functional enrichment analysis further revealed that differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to cell proliferation, development, and kinase activity (Fig. 5O and Supplementary Fig. 10), several of which are functionally associated with hypoxia-responsive signaling. Together with histological, immunohistochemical, and transcriptomic evidence, these findings support a working model in which CaO2@Lipo alleviates tumor hypoxia and suppresses the HIF-1α/VEGF/CA9/ABCB1 signaling axis, thereby contributing to the reversal of lenvatinib resistance (Fig. 5P).

CaO2@Lipo remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and enhances antitumor immune responses

To assess immune remodeling under an intact immune system, the following analyses were performed in the Hepa1-6-LR orthotopic model established in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Chronic hypoxia and acidosis not only drive targeted therapy resistance but also represent major sources of tumor immunosuppression. Therefore, after weakening the resistance network, we further evaluated whether CaO2@Lipo could remodel the immune landscape of resistant tumors. A schematic illustration of the mechanism of immunogenic cell death (ICD) is shown in Fig. 6A. Combination treatment significantly induced calreticulin (CRT) exposure and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release (Fig. 6B–D), accompanied by intracellular ATP depletion and extracellular ATP accumulation (Fig. 6E and F), indicating effective induction of immunogenic cell death in resistant tumors. These findings suggest that CaO2@Lipo not only directly kills tumor cells but also converts them into a source of tumor antigens.

At the level of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, combination treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6G) and markedly promoted dendritic cell accumulation (Fig. 6H), indicating coordinated activation of antigen presentation and effector T-cell responses. Consistently, serum levels of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2 were significantly elevated, whereas Th2-type and immunosuppressive cytokines IL-10 and IL-4 were markedly reduced (Fig. 6I–M), demonstrating a systemic immune shift from a tolerogenic to an effector phenotype.

Biosafety evaluation

The hemolysis assay demonstrated that CaO2@Lipo exhibited no significant hemolytic activity and favorable biological safety (Supplementary Fig. 11). H&E staining showed no obvious histopathological damage in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) across all treatment groups (Fig. 7A). Serum biochemical analysis revealed that levels of ALT, AST, TBIL, ALB, CRE, and BUN remained within normal ranges, with no significant differences among groups (Fig. 7B–G).

Discussion

Lenvatinib resistance remains one of the most formidable challenges in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma [5]. Traditional efforts have largely focused on tumor cell–intrinsic mechanisms, attempting to overcome resistance by targeting compensatory signaling, metabolic reprogramming, or autophagy [[9], [10], [11]]. However, increasing evidence suggests that lenvatinib resistance rarely arises from abnormalities in a single pathway. Instead, it reflects a systemic adaptive state established under sustained therapeutic pressure, shaped by chronic hypoxia, acidic metabolism, and vascular dysfunction [13,14]. This microenvironment-dependent adaptation explains why interventions aimed at individual molecular nodes often achieve only transient benefit.

It is also important to note that while the long-term induced resistant cells exhibit a profound loss of direct cytotoxicity in vitro, lenvatinib monotherapy still exerts a weak, partial tumor-inhibitory effect in vivo. This may be attributed to the fact that lenvatinib continuously targets non-resistant host endothelial cells via the VEGFR/FGFR pathways. In HCC, lenvatinib-induced vascular suppression further aggravates perfusion deficiency and chronic hypoxia, which is now recognized as a central driver of resistance evolution [12]. Persistent HIF-1α activation promotes metabolic reprogramming, angiogenic dysregulation, and invasive phenotypes, while concomitant lactate accumulation maintains an acidic milieu that impairs drug efficacy and enhances drug efflux [16]. These coordinated alterations progressively stabilize a hypoxia–acidosis adaptive network, which acts not merely as a byproduct of resistance, but as a structural foundation sustaining its persistence.

Several prior studies have attempted to modulate components of the tumor microenvironment to improve responses to targeted therapies. For example, a hollow MnO2-based TME-responsive nanoplatform was shown to alleviate hypoxia and reverse sorafenib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma models, underscoring the potential of oxygen-generating strategies to sensitize resistant tumors to treatment [27]. Additionally, reviews of TKI effects on the HCC microenvironment highlight that tyrosine kinase inhibitors can both enhance antitumor responses and inadvertently contribute to resistance through microenvironmental remodeling, providing a mechanistic rationale for integrated TME-targeted strategies [19]. Mechanistic insights have also linked sustained hypoxia and HIF activation to sorafenib resistance, further supporting the role of microenvironmental stress in therapeutic failure [28]. While multifunctional nanoplatforms have been developed to target multiple microenvironmental features simultaneously, comprehensive strategies that disrupt interdependent adaptive networks such as hypoxia–acidosis remain underexplored [29].

Rather than introducing another resistance-associated molecular target, this study explored whether weakening this adaptive foundation could globally destabilize resistance. Based on this rationale, CaO2@Lipo was designed to simultaneously generate oxygen, neutralize acidity, and perturb calcium homeostasis. This intervention operates at the level of physical and metabolic conditions rather than isolated signaling pathways. Notably, CaO2@Lipo exerted a coordinated regulatory effect across hypoxia signaling, angiogenic balance, acidic homeostasis, and drug efflux pathways, suggesting that microenvironment modulation can overcome the compensatory resilience inherent to single-target inhibition.

These findings support a growing conceptual shift in oncology, in which therapeutic failure often reflects the tumor's ability to reprogram its microenvironmental context rather than incomplete blockade of specific pathways [15]. Hypoxia, acidosis, and metabolic stress are increasingly recognized as resistance determinants comparable to genetic alterations. Importantly, because microenvironmental status also governs immunosuppression and therapeutic tolerance, strategies that restore oxygen and metabolic homeostasis may function as central hubs linking targeted therapy, metabolic intervention, and immunotherapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The long-term biosafety, metabolic fate, and controllability of CaO2@Lipo require further investigation. Moreover, the relative contributions of hypoxia relief and calcium modulation across different tumor and immune contexts remain to be clarified. Future studies integrating multi-omics profiling and clinically relevant combination regimens will be essential to identify responsive patient subgroups and fully define the translational potential of microenvironment-oriented strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, our core findings demonstrate that CaO2@Lipo relieves tumor hypoxia and acidosis while inducing calcium overload. This completely dismantles the HIF-1α-driven resistance network and triggers robust anti-tumor immunity, effectively restoring lenvatinib sensitivity. Significantly, this establishes a paradigm of overcoming resistance by destroying its microenvironmental foundation rather than targeting isolated pathways. Given its excellent biosafety and in vivo efficacy, CaO2@Lipo holds promising clinical application value as an adjuvant strategy for lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma.
