Differential Effects of T-Cell Inhibitors and Antimetabolites on Graft Survival in High-Risk Penetrating Keratoplasty: A Tertiary Center Experience
Authors: Susama Chokesuwattanaskul, Thanapong Somkijrungroj, Fanxiu Xiong, John A Gonzales, Vannarut Satitpitakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the comparative effectiveness of T-cell inhibitors versus antimetabolites on graft survival in high-risk penetrating keratoplasty (PKP), with a particular focus on cases of repeat grafting.DesignRetrospective cohort study.ParticipantsAll high-risk eyes undergoing optical PKP between 2015 and 2022 at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand.MethodsThe medical records of 293 high-risk eyes undergoing optical PKP were reviewed. The use of any systemic steroid-sparing immunosuppressive (IS) agents was identified. In practice, all patients received either T-cell inhibitors or antimetabolites. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were applied to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the effect of systemic IS therapies on time to graft failure in high-risk PKP, adjusting for demographics, indications for PKP, and possible risk factors of graft failure.Main Outcome MeasuresTime to graft failure in high-risk PKP.ResultsAmong 281 high-risk eyes undergoing PKP, 217 received no IS medications, whereas 64 received IS medications (31 T-cell inhibitors, 49 antimetabolites). Notably, all eyes received topical corticosteroid immediately postoperation. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for influential risk factors for graft failure, using any systemic IS was associated with a reduced risk of graft failure (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.42–1.18), though not statistically significant. In subgroup analysis, T-cell inhibitors statistically significantly reduced the risk of graft failure (HR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.24–0.95), whereas antimetabolites did not (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.58–1.63). Among eyes with 2 or more previous grafts, IS use demonstrated a protective effect on graft failure (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.28–0.94), but no benefit was observed for first-time grafts.ConclusionsAmong IS medications for high-risk PKP, T-cell inhibitors, in addition to topical corticosteroid, significantly improved graft survival, whereas antimetabolites showed no benefit. The protective effect was most pronounced in repeat transplantation, suggesting T-cell inhibitors should be preferentially considered for eyes undergoing multiple grafts.Financial Disclosure(s)The authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Antimetabolites, Graft survival, High-risk corneal transplantation, Immunosuppressive, T-cell inhibitors

Due to the inherent immune privilege of the cornea, the survival rate for corneal allografts, particularly penetrating keratoplasty (PKP), has been reported to be as high as 74% at 5 years and 62% at 10 years,1 without the necessity of major histocompatibility complex matching and systemic immunosuppressive (IS) medications, unlike other solid organ transplantations.2

However, in a substantial proportion of corneal transplantation with specific risk factors (termed high-risk corneal transplantation), there can be significantly higher rates of rejection and/or graft failure. In 1992, the Collaborative Corneal Transplantation Study3 defined high-risk as having 2 or more quadrants of vascularization or a graft with a previous episode of rejection. The characteristics of corneal vascularization, particularly deep stromal vessels, have consistently been reported as a significant high-risk feature.4 Additionally, several other characteristics of a high-risk cornea have been defined, including prior transplantation, large donor graft (≥8.75 mm), eccentric graft, anterior iris synechiae, ocular surface disorders [e.g., limbal stem cell deficiency, severe dry eye diseases, persistent epithelial defect, and chronic inflammation including ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), and graft versus host disease].5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Other high-risk characteristics of patients themselves have included an age ≤14 years, systemic inflammatory conditions [e.g., human immunodeficiency virus infection, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and SJS], and diabetes mellitus. Eyes with glaucoma, a history of glaucoma surgeries, associated intraocular inflammatory diseases, and chronic intraocular viral infection are also at high risk of graft rejection and/or failure.3,5, 6, 7

Among eyes undergoing corneal transplantation, the rejection episodes of the high-risk graft were increased up to 30% to 60% of cases, and the rate of graft failure was reported to be up to 70% within 10 years.10, 11, 12 Various protocols have been studied to improve the outcome of high-risk corneal transplantation. The standard practices are the use of either local or systemic IS treatment, including corticosteroids, T-cell inhibitors, and antimetabolites13, 14, 15, 16 (Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci. 58:5666, 2017).

Despite the potential benefits of systemic IS in high-risk corneal transplantation, previous studies have been limited by small sample sizes and have shown conflicting results regarding optimal IS strategies.10 From a previous systematic review on cyclosporine in high-risk PKP in 2016, only 817 eyes (from 16 studies with sample size range of 6–131 eyes) were included in the analysis.17 In particular, the comparative effectiveness of different IS agents, especially in cases of repeat grafting, remains poorly understood. Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate the impact of different systemic IS medications, specifically comparing T-cell inhibitors with antimetabolites, on graft survival in high-risk PKP, with particular attention to outcomes in repeated corneal transplantation.

Methods

Data Collection

Data from 293 eyes with optical PKP between January 2015 and December 2022 were retrieved from the Excellence Center of Cornea and Limbal Stem Cell Transplantation database, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital. The retrospective medical record review study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee (IRB No.741/66 COA No.0226/2024), Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, and King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, and the clinical data were anonymized. All eyes included had ≥1 high-risk characteristic of graft failure, including deep stromal vessels, repeat transplantation, large donor graft (≥8.75 mm), eccentric graft, anterior iris synechiae, ocular surface disorders (e.g., limbal stem cell deficiency, severe dry eye diseases, chronic inflammation including ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, SJS, graft versus host disease, or persistent epithelial defect), patient age younger than 14 years at time of PKP, systemic inflammatory conditions (e.g., human immunodeficiency virus, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and SJS), diabetes mellitus, glaucoma, glaucoma surgeries and associated inflammatory diseases and chronic viral infection (e.g., herpes simplex virus, varicella-zoster virus, and cytomegalovirus). Glaucoma surgeries at any time before the graft failure were counted. We excluded therapeutic PKP for infection from our analysis for 3 key reasons: First, in Thailand, the urgent need for transplantation combined with donor shortages often compromises corneal graft quality. Second, due to the high prevalence of severe fungal corneal ulcers, topical corticosteroid treatment is typically delayed. Third, IS medications are usually avoided in the early postoperative period to reduce the risk of recurrent new infections. Additionally, we excluded 5 patients who had <3 months of follow-up to eliminate cases of primary graft failure, which typically occurs within the first 3 months after transplantation. To account for potential correlation between eyes from the same patient due to systemic and unknown factors, only 1 eye per patient was included in the analysis. When patients underwent bilateral keratoplasty during the study period, only the first transplanted eye was analyzed. Seven patients received bilateral keratoplasty; thus, 7 fellow eyes were excluded. The remaining 281 eyes (1 per patient) were included in our analysis.

Medical records of high-risk patients, including demographics, diagnosis for this operation, follow-up periods, the use of IS medications, and possible risk factors relating to graft failure, were further reviewed to analyze the detailed clinical courses and graft survival. All eyes received topical corticosteroid treatment at immediate postoperation and adjusted the doses according to the postoperation time and clinical graft status. The IS medication used at our center, including corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone), antimetabolites (e.g., mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, and methotrexate), and T-cell inhibitors (e.g., cyclosporine and tacrolimus), along with the start and end dates of use, were recorded. Due to the heterogeneous dose and duration of corticosteroid use, systemic corticosteroids were not analyzed as a separate treatment group. However, we recognized that corticosteroid use might contribute to the action of IS and graft survival; systemic corticosteroid use, therefore, was included as 1 covariate in the models.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the high-risk patients were described in 2 groups: the eyes of the patients who received IS medications and those who did not receive any IS medications. In our study, the use of systemic IS consisted of either T-cell inhibitors or antimetabolites. Patients were followed from the date of the corneal transplantation until either graft failure or the last follow-up, whichever came first. Incidence rates for graft failure in each IS medication group were derived from the number of graft failures divided by person-months of follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves were fit to compare survival for patients with IS medications to those without IS medications. The difference in survival between the IS groups was compared using the univariate log-rank test.

Cox proportional hazard models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for graft failure incidence with length of follow-up as the primary time scale. The first multivariable model was adjusted for demographic risk factors, including age and sex. We additionally adjusted the multivariable model for ocular pathologies, including the number of graft rejections, diagnosis at operation, deep stromal vessels, preexisting inflammatory ophthalmic condition, herpetic keratitis, large donor graft, eccentric graft, anterior iris, ocular surface disorders, glaucoma, and preglaucoma surgery, and the diagnosis at this graft, as well as the use of systemic corticosteroids. Candidate covariates were specified a priori based on literature and clinical relevance. Because unstable estimates, inflated variance, and overfitting were concerns given the number of outcome events, we applied bidirectional stepwise selection using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to identify the model with optimal fit and parsimony for clinical interpretability.18 Variables were iteratively added or removed based on whether their inclusion improved (lowered) the AIC value, balancing model fit against complexity. Only variables retained by this procedure were included in the final Cox model. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using the Schoenfeld residual test and did not find evidence of a violation.

Furthermore, considering the potential mediation effect of the number of grafts (once, twice or more times), any rejections (no, yes), deep stromal vessels (no, yes), anterior iris synechiae (no, yes), and glaucoma (no, yes) on the association between systemic IS treatment and graft failure, we conducted subgroup analyses by the aforementioned factors. The interaction between these subgroup variables and IS therapy use was evaluated by entering the interaction term of IS medication use and covariates into the models. Due to the small number of eyes in each subgroup, we did not perform subgroup analyses for IS categories. Given the high proportion of systemic corticosteroid use among any IS group, we performed 2 sensitivity analyses to assess the association between systemic IS treatment and graft failure: (1) among the systemic IS users and those receiving systemic corticosteroids only; and (2) among the systemic IS users and those receiving no systemic IS or corticosteroids.

The P values provided are 2-sided, with a significance level of 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software, version 4.0.2.

Results

After a total follow-up time of 11 619.9 months, 281 high-risk eyes undergoing PKP with ≥3 months of follow-up were included in the study. Of the 281 eyes, 217 received no IS medications, and 64 received ≥1 IS medication (31 eyes for T-cell inhibitors and 49 eyes for antimetabolites). The demographics and clinical characteristics of high-risk eyes with and without IS medications are described in Table 1. Overall, the most common high-risk corneal characteristics were deep stromal vessels (108 eyes, 38.4%), followed by ocular surface disorder (96 eyes, 34.2%) and anterior iris synechiae (86 eyes, 30.6%) (Table 1). Glaucoma was present in more than half of the eyes (158, 56.2%). Among high-risk classified eyes not receiving IS medications, 75 of 217 eyes (34.2%) received systemic corticosteroids at some point between 7 days preoperatively and either final follow-up or graft failure. By comparison, a much higher proportion (60 of 64 eyes, 96.8%) in the high-risk group treated with IS medication received oral prednisolone during the same period (Table 1).

In the initial multivariate Cox regression analysis adjusting for age and sex, IS medication use showed no statistically significant association with graft failure (HR: 1.34, 95% CI: 0.86–2.09) (Table 2). However, after incorporating additional risk factors identified through AIC as influential for graft failure, including previous graft failure, any graft rejections, anterior iris synechiae, ocular surface disorders, and glaucoma in the model, IS medication use was negatively associated with the risk of graft failure (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.42–1.18). Moreover, graft rejection, glaucoma, and ocular surface disorder were the most significant factors affecting graft survival (Fig. 2, Table 3). Further subgroup analysis by IS type revealed that T-cell inhibitors statistically significantly reduced graft failure risk (HR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.24–0.95), whereas antimetabolites showed no protective effect (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.58–1.63) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis revealed differential effects of IS medications depending on prior graft history (Fig. 3). In first-time graft recipients, IS use was not associated with a protective effect (HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 0.45–2.92). Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we restricted the comparison to patients receiving either systemic steroid-sparing IS agents versus systemic corticosteroids alone (excluding those with no systemic therapy). In this analysis, steroid-sparing systemic IS was associated with a lower, though not statistically significant, risk of graft failure (HR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.47–1.29). Second, we compared systemic steroid-sparing IS users to patients receiving neither IS agents nor systemic corticosteroids (excluding the corticosteroid-only group). This analysis showed no significant difference (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 0.58–2.16). The lack of statistical significance in both sensitivity analyses, despite different comparison groups, supports the robustness of our primary findings that T-cell inhibitors, rather than IS use alone, were the key determinant of graft survival.

Discussion

This retrospective study of 281 high-risk eyes undergoing optical PKP at a tertiary referral center examined the impact of systemic IS therapy on graft survival. Although IS overall showed a protective trend against graft failure, subgroup analysis by IS type revealed that T-cell inhibitors specifically provided a statistically significant benefit, whereas antimetabolites did not. Notably, IS was most effective in eyes with multiple previous grafts, where it reduced graft failure by more than 50%. These findings suggest that targeted IS with T-cell inhibitors may be particularly beneficial in repeat corneal transplantation.

The demographics and clinical characteristics of high-risk patients with and without IS medications were comparable in this study, except those with associated inflammatory eye diseases, e.g., peripheral ulcerative keratitis, were more likely to receive IS medications. In all cases, topical steroids were prescribed postoperation immediately and maintained if the grafts survived. The doses were adjusted in accordance with the degree of inflammation and the postoperative duration. Additionally, as all eyes received topical corticosteroid treatment at postoperation, the use of IS medications was considered as the additive treatments in the proportion of the high-risk eyes.

At our center, it is standard practice for oral corticosteroid (usually oral prednisolone) to be prescribed during either the preoperative period (while awaiting corticosteroid-sparing IS medication to reach full therapeutic efficacy) or the perioperative period (typically 1 month). Given the substantial difference in systemic corticosteroid use between IS users (96.8%) and non-IS users (34.2%), it is possible that part of the observed benefit of systemic IS may be attributable to corticosteroid use. To address this potential confounding, we adjusted for systemic corticosteroid use in multivariable Cox regression models. We further conducted 2 sensitivity analyses to isolate the effect of steroid-sparing IS agents: (1) comparing systemic IS users to patients receiving systemic corticosteroids alone; and (2) comparing systemic IS users to patients receiving neither systemic IS nor systemic corticosteroids (excluding the corticosteroid-only group). Both sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with our primary findings, confirming that the protective effect was driven by the specific IS agent (T-cell inhibitors) rather than by corticosteroid coadministration or general immunosuppression. Because corticosteroid dosing and duration varied substantially across patients based on clinical response and disease severity, we did not include these parameters as covariates in the analysis. This variability may have biased our results toward the null, as patients with more severe disease were more likely to receive more intensive and prolonged therapy, potentially masking beneficial treatment effects. Our center has not yet widely adopted newer biological response modifiers for high-risk corneal transplantation.

Previous studies have reported high failure rates in high-risk corneal transplants, with 34% failing by 3 years and up to 70% at 10 years, mainly due to irreversible graft rejection.10, 11, 12,19 In our study, although eyes receiving IS medications showed a higher incidence of graft failure compared with those not receiving IS medications, this difference was not statistically significant. Such a counterintuitive finding likely reflects reverse causality, as IS medications were preferentially prescribed to eyes with multiple high-risk factors or more severe pathology. Moreover, IS medications were often reduced from maximal doses due to side effects or adjusted based on clinical response, potentially limiting their therapeutic effects.

Several IS medications, with different mechanisms of action, have been reported to reduce graft rejection and failure rates after high-risk PKP.11 Among the IS medications evaluated in our study, T-cell inhibitors demonstrated significant effectiveness, reducing graft failure risk by 52% compared with no IS, regardless of corticosteroid use. In contrast, antimetabolites showed no significant protective effect. Although some eyes received combinations of IS medications, the number was too small to analyze these combinations separately. The superior effectiveness of T-cell inhibitors may be explained by their targeted mechanisms of action. Corneal graft rejection and failure are primarily mediated through T-cells. T-cell allo-sensitization and activation occur in the lymph node and migrate into the cornea, where they can subsequently induce inflammation and tissue destruction.20 T-cell inhibitors prevent calcineurin and interleukin-2 synthesis, reducing T-cell migration to the graft. Additional effects of T-cell inhibitors include suppression of corneal neovascularization, and promoting regulatory T-cells that maintain corneal immune privilege.21, 22, 23, 24 In contrast, antimetabolites work broadly by inhibiting nucleic acid synthesis in both T and B cells. Such a less targeted approach may explain their limited effectiveness, as demonstrated in a randomized multicenter trial, where mycophenolate mofetil reduced immune-mediated reactions, but failed to improve graft survival compared with the control group receiving only early postoperative corticosteroids for 5 months.24

Deep stromal vessels were the most common high-risk characteristic in our study (38.4%), followed by ocular surface disorders (34.2%) and anterior iris synechiae (30.6%), with similar distributions between IS and non-IS groups. Whereas previous studies have identified deep stromal vessels as a critical risk factor for graft failure, sometimes even more significant that prior graft history, our interaction analysis revealed a different pattern.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 The use of IS medications were most effective in preventing graft failure in eyes with multiple previous grafts but showed less benefit in eyes with deep stromal vessels alone.31 This finding is particularly noteworthy as repeat graft cases often involve vascularized corneal scars and likely harbor multiple risk factors—both known and potentially unknown (e.g., genetic predisposition, subtle immune responses)—that may increase vulnerability to subsequent graft failure.32

This finding adds to the growing evidence that the interaction between risk factors and treatment response is complex. Previous studies have shown that very high-risk PKP cases (those with multiple high-risk characteristics) can achieve good outcomes with combined IS medications (such as prednisolone, azathioprine, and cyclosporine) for ≥3 years. However, the complexity of these multiple risk factors and their varying interactions with different treatments poses significant challenges for conducting definitive randomized controlled clinical trials.33,34

The primary limitation of this study was the retrospective design. Treatment decisions, including the choice of IS agent, were influenced by the considerable heterogeneity in patient characteristics and individual tolerance to specific medications. Moreover, the dosing, frequency, and durations of systemic IS were tailored to the patients’ clinical conditions and the graft status. This variability precluded a systematic assessment of dose–response relationships. Future randomized controlled trials and prospective studies are warranted to more rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of different IS regimens and to inform treatment guidelines for high-risk PKP.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates systemic IS medication, in addition to the topical corticosteroid treatment, may have a beneficial effect in high-risk corneal transplantation. T-cell inhibitors were particularly effective, reducing graft failure risk by 52%, whereas antimetabolites did not show an association with graft failure in high-risk PKP. This differential effect likely reflects the central role of T-cells in graft rejection and the multi-targeted mechanisms of T-cell inhibitors. Importantly, IS use showed the greatest benefit in repeat transplantation, reducing graft failure risk by more than 50% in eyes with multiple previous grafts. These findings suggest that T-cell inhibitors may be associated with lower graft failure rates than antimetabolites in high-risk PKPs, particularly in repeat corneal transplantation, where a relative reduction in graft failure of more than 50% was observed. The lack of benefit in first-time grafts and the differential effectiveness between IS agent classes warrant validation in prospective randomized trials.
