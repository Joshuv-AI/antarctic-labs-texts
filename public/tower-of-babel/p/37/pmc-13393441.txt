Letter to the Editor on Comparative Effect of Non-surgical Versus Surgical Therapy for Stage III/IV Periodontitis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Li Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article titled “Comparative Effect of Non-surgical Versus Surgical Therapy for Stage III/IV Periodontitis: A Retrospective Cohort Study” by Cao et al., recently published in the International Dental Journal.1 We commend their work with a substantial sample size of 3194 patients is particularly enlightening.

The finding that surgical periodontal therapy (SPT) offers superior short-term clinical improvements in probing depth (PD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) aligns with traditional biological rationale. However, the most clinically significant revelation is the diminishing therapeutic advantage of SPT over time. The observation that both treatment modalities converge in efficacy after three years, provided regular supportive periodontal care is maintained, challenges the long-held dogma that surgery is indispensable for all deep pockets. This conclusion—that “long-term outcomes depend more on maintenance adherence than initial therapy”—implies that for surgery-hesitant patients or in resource-limited settings, a rigorous non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) protocol combined with strict maintenance can achieve comparable long-term stability, a reassuring message for general practitioners.

To further enhance the robustness of this conclusion and address inherent limitations of retrospective designs, I would like to propose several considerations for future validation studies.

Firstly, despite rigorous adjustment for demographic and clinical confounders, non-randomised allocation inevitably introduces selection bias. As the authors noted, fewer than 10% of eligible patients opted for SPT, primarily due to fear, cost, or perceived discomfort. These unmeasured psychosocial factors—including dental anxiety, health literacy, and socioeconomic status—may independently influence both treatment choice and long-term compliance. Patients declining SPT might represent a subgroup with inherently lower adherence to supportive care, potentially biasing the observed equivalence of long-term outcomes. Future prospective studies employing propensity score matching or instrumental variable analysis are warranted to isolate the true treatment effect of SPT from patient-level behavioural determinants.

Secondly, the standardisation of both surgical and non-surgical protocols needs further refinement. The study period (2017–2022) witnessed evolving adjunctive modalities,2 including laser-assisted debridement,3 local antibiotic delivery,4 and minimally invasive flap designs. While the authors commendably restricted SPT to open flap debridement (modified Widman flap), the absence of detailed documentation on adjunctive therapies introduces potential heterogeneity. Moreover, the definition of “repeated NSPT” in the control group would benefit from stricter protocolisation (eg, specifying the number of sessions, use of magnification, or ultrasonic versus hand instrumentation) to ensure that comparisons genuinely contrast “surgery plus maintenance” versus “intensive NSPT plus maintenance”, rather than heterogeneous retreatment regimens.

In summary, Cao et al. have contributed a valuable dataset validating those non-surgical approaches, when coupled with rigorous supportive care, achieve long-term stability comparable to surgical intervention for Stage III/IV periodontitis. This study serves as an important reminder that while SPT is a powerful tool for initial debridement, the long-term prognosis rests firmly on the patient’s commitment to ongoing maintenance, regardless of the initial therapeutic approach.
