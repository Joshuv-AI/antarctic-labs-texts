Effect of depression screening on future depressive symptoms in a persistent pain population: mediation analysis
Authors: Brendan Mouatt, Tasha R. Stanton, G. Lorimer Moseley, Aidan G. Cashin, Laura E. Simons, Hayley B. Leake, Felicity A. Braithwaite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

1. Introduction

Persistent pain is the leading cause of burden and disability globally,26 affecting 20% of the population.34 Persistent pain and depression often co-occur12; depression can result from, and contribute to, pain.12,17 When depressive symptoms accompany persistent pain, quality of life, treatment response, pain intensity, and function are negatively affected.12,17 Accordingly, many guidelines for persistent pain support the identification of psychosocial factors, including depressive symptoms, and referring to psychological care when indicated.23

In clinical practice, identification of depressive symptoms commonly involves structured self-report questionnaires. These questionnaires are often embedded within clinical interactions and are generally assumed to function as inert assessment tools for guiding referral and management. Beyond guiding care and treatment, such questionnaires may themselves constitute clinical exposures that influence patient sense-making, symptom interpretation, and future behaviour, a phenomenon described as the mere-measurement or assessment reactivity effect.5 In a recent systematic review, Long and colleagues24 found that patient exposure to patient-reported outcome measures was associated with changes in both perceptual and behavioural outcomes. Whether such assessment effects extend to structured depression questionnaires in pain care, and whether they influence subsequent mood trajectories independent of referral decisions, remains unknown.

Although screening is widely conducted in clinical practice, evidence that its implementation, including the tool, its interpretation, communication of results, and the clinical context, improves long-term mood-related outcomes in people with persistent pain remains limited. To our knowledge, no study has isolated the downstream effects of assessment exposure itself, independent of subsequent treatment decisions, using direct screened vs unscreened comparisons in people with persistent pain.

Despite the widespread use of depression screening questionnaires, the rationale for their routine use reflects acceptance of a biopsychosocial model37 and the recognition that depression and persistent pain frequently co-occur, rather than on direct evidence that screening itself improves outcomes. Improvements in depressive symptoms are associated with larger reductions in pain intensity, interference, disability, and sick leave,29 with evidence supporting psychotherapy benefitting those with pain,12 yet this does not establish that the act of screening contributes to those outcomes. Understanding whether structured symptom questionnaires function solely as measurement tools or also as active elements within clinical encounters is, therefore, important.

Beyond identifying risk, screening may also shape symptom experience by influencing how sensations, thoughts, and emotions are interpreted. Within a predictive processing framework, depression and pain are not direct reflections of sensory input,9,13 but expectation-driven inferences. Exposure to structured screening may alter the salience or precision of those expectations, potentially influencing symptom perception and trajectories over time.

Here, we examined whether exposure to a structured depression symptom questionnaire (PHQ-9), compared with a pain-knowledge questionnaire that does not assess affect, was associated with depressive symptom trajectories 6 months later in a real-world pain care context, independent of referral to, or engagement with, psychological care.

2. Methods

Our analysis plan was preregistered on Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/45jkg) before data analysis. As the current study was a secondary analysis of data collected as part of a clinical audit, we did not register this work as a clinical trial. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of South Australia (protocol: 0000031670). This study was reported following STROBE guidelines for prospective cohorts36 and A Guideline for Reporting Mediation Analyses (AGReMA).18

2.1. Study design and setting

This was a secondary causal mediation analysis of fully deidentified clinical audit data collected from 7 clinical sites in Australia, the United States, and the United Kingdom (4 physiotherapy clinics, 1 multidisciplinary pain unit physiotherapy department, and 2 private hospital physiotherapy departments) between 2001 and 2010. Data from some of this cohort have been used to investigate mediators of the effect of improved pain-related knowledge on pain and disability outcomes.20

2.2. Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 years, fluent in English, had chronic primary or secondary pain (≥3 months), and were referred from a medical professional or self-referred. Exclusions included recent surgery (≤3 months), cancer treatment, phantom limb pain, specific psychiatric disorders other than anxiety or depression, and neurological conditions (eg, dementia, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, spinal cord injury, or stroke). All participants provided informed consent for their data to be used in research.

2.3. Study procedures

At baseline, consenting participants completed demographic information and were then quasi-randomly assigned to complete the PHQ-9 or the alternative questionnaire (NPQ or rNPQ) via coin-flip, in accordance with a standardised assessment protocol implemented across all sites.4 All participants completed the mood Numerical Rating Scale (mNRS),28 the Catastrophic Thoughts about Pain Scale (CATS),32 the Patient Specific Functional Scale (PSFS),31 and pain intensity (100 mm visual analogue scale).

All participants then received treatment grounded in pain-related education and graded functional retraining for people with persisting pain.27 Treatments were not recorded and were individualised at the discretion of the treating practitioners, who were unaware of the current study hypothesis. Clinicians were also able to consider referral for psychological support if a participant scored ≥15 on the PHQ-9 (ie, moderate to moderately severe depression),16 or using clinical judgment (in both groups). All participants completed the mNRS at 6-month follow-up. Figure 1 provides the study overview.

Across all sites, clinicians followed a standardised assessment protocol, which involved flipping a coin to determine which baseline tools were administered (ie, PHQ-9 or the alternative questionnaire [NPQ or rNPQ]). There was no intention to evaluate screening effects at cohort outset. This constitutes a pragmatic, quasi-random allocation process rather than true individual randomisation. No formal random sequence generation or allocation concealment procedures were used. As such, the original study was not a prospectively designed randomised controlled trial; allocation was not informed by any a priori hypothesis relating to mood outcomes, but a pragmatic step to reduce patient burden.

2.4. Groups

2.4.1. Group 1: patient health questionnaire 9-item (exposure)

We considered completing the PHQ-9 as the Exposure. Participants in the Exposure group were screened for depressive symptoms using the PHQ-9. This nine-item self-administered tool reflects depressive symptom frequency over the past 2 weeks.15 Items are rated 0 to 3, with higher scores reflecting more frequent symptoms (nearly every day). Scores range from 0 to 27, with 5, 10, 15, and 20 indicating mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, respectively.16 The PHQ-9 is a valid surrogate of The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV Axis I Disorders (SCID-I; accuracy = 67.7%, using a cut-off of >15)6 and has good psychometric properties, excellent internal reliability (Cronbach alpha = 0.89), and test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.84). A PHQ-9 score >10 has 88% sensitivity and specificity, with an area under curve of 0.95 in ROC analysis for major depression.16 A 21% reduction in the PHQ-9 score represents the minimum clinically important difference (MCID).14

2.4.2. Group 2: neurophysiology of pain questionnaire or revised version (comparator questionnaire)

Participants in the comparator group were not screened for symptoms of depression but instead received a questionnaire that was similar to the PHQ-9 in length and time to complete. Participants completed the Neurophysiology of Pain Questionnaire (NPQ) or its revised version (rNPQ),4 which assesses an individual's conceptualisation of the biological processes underpinning pain. The NPQ includes 19 true/false/undecided items with good internal consistency (Person Separation Index = 0.84) and construct validity.4 The rNPQ includes 12 items and has good internal consistency (Person Separation Index = 0.82) with high test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.97).4 The rNPQ replaced the NPQ after Rasch analysis identified poorly fit or redundant items in the original version.4

2.5. Outcome measure

2.5.1. Mood numerical rating scale

All participants were asked an 11-point mood question, “How would you rate your general mood this past week?” (0 = “very bad, as depressed as I could be,” 10 = “excellent”), assessed at baseline and at 6-month follow-up. The score was reverse-scored to align with the PHQ-9 (ie, higher scores = greater depression). The mNRS strongly correlates with the PHQ-9 (r = 0.83); when using a cut-off of 5/10, it adequately discriminates between those with and without “moderately severe” and “severe” depression as determined by the established PHQ-9 cut-off of >15 (sensitivity = 95.5%, specificity = 70.5%; area under curve = 0.93).28 This threshold was used to identify more clinically significant depressive symptomatology, recognising that lower cut points (eg, the accepted cut point for probable depression of ≥10) maximise sensitivity for identifying probable depression, whereas higher thresholds increase specificity for more severe depressive symptomatology.25 Sensitivity analyses based on age, gender, pain intensity, geographical location, diagnosis, and function verified the robustness of the mNRS across these factors.28 Given its strong correlation with the PHQ-9,28 we refer to mNRS changes as changes in depressive symptoms.

2.5.2. Mediators

We investigated the influence of referral for, and engagement with psychological care, as potential mediators in the relationship between screening (exposure) and depressive symptoms at 6-month follow-up (outcome). Both variables were treated as separate mediators in a causal mediation analysis. We used participant responses at 6-month follow-up to capture prior referral for and engagement with “psychology or counselling,” with this serving as a proxy for psychological care receipt. Engagement with psychological care at follow-up reflects attendance after referral by the study clinician. Details on the specific care and dosage were not captured. Baseline engagement with existing counselling, psychological, or psychiatric care was not captured, nor was independent access to this type of care during participants' involvement in the study.

2.5.3. Confounding and causal assumptions

We used a Directed Acyclic Graph to identify a minimum sufficient adjustment set to control for potential confounding of the mediator–outcome effect. We assumed the same confounders applied across both mediator–outcome relationships. Given the pragmatic, quasi-random allocation of screening type, we assumed no unmeasured confounding of the exposure–mediator and exposure–outcome pathways. The Directed Acyclic Graph identified baseline catastrophising, pain intensity, and functional capacity as measured covariates to include in all models. We also assumed the mediators were independent. 2.6. Analysis

A researcher (B.M.), blinded to the group allocation, completed the primary analysis using Jamovi (Version 2.3) and R Studio (Version 2022) using the mediation package for the causal mediation analysis.33 P-values, with P < 0.05, or 95% CIs where values did not cross zero, were considered statistically significant. Normality was confirmed using Q–Q plots and skewness (−1.5 and +1.5).

2.6.1. Primary analysis

A linear regression model was used to determine whether group (PHQ-9 vs NPQ/rNPQ) influenced depressive symptoms (mNRS score) at 6 months.

2.6.2. Secondary analysis

Causal mediation analysis was used to estimate the direct effect of the exposure on the outcome at 6-month follow-up, independent of referral or engagement with a psychologist or counsellor (mediators of interest). We estimated the intervention–mediator and mediator–outcome effects and corresponding uncertainty estimates for each of the potential mediators using 2 regression models: the mediator model (probit regression) and the outcome model (linear regression), including identified confounders. The average direct effect, average causal mediation effect, and total effect were estimated using a simulation-based approach, with 95% confidence intervals generated through bootstrapping.

2.6.3. Post-hoc analyses

A post-hoc subanalysis assessed group effects across low (0–2), moderate (3–7), and high (8–10) mNRS categories, determined using k-means clustering.22

To explore the effect of screening on pain and function outcomes, we conducted off-protocol repeated-measures ANOVAs with time (baseline, 6 months) as the within-subject factor and screening group (PHQ-9 vs NPQ/rNPQ) as the between-subject factor, and compared groups at 6 months using estimated marginal means. These analyses were not specified in the preregistered protocol and were conducted post hoc in response to reviewer request.

A post-hoc sample size calculation using G*Power8 indicated that this analysis was well powered (>0.99) to detect a small effect size (f2 = 0.1) with 1770 participants, 6 predictors (2 conditions, 2 mediators, and 3 confounders), with alpha set at 0.05.

2.6.4. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the robustness of our results to bias from residual confounding using the E-value method.35 The E-value quantifies the minimum risk ratio that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and outcome to fully account for the observed effect, with a higher E-value signifying that the observed association is less susceptible to potential unmeasured confounding. To assess potential provider-level clustering, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted including clinician as a random intercept in mediator and outcome models. Given minimal missing data (0.5%), no further sensitivity analysis was performed.

3.1. Primary objective

On average, depressive symptoms improved in both groups from baseline to 6 months with greater improvement in the NPQ/rNPQ group on the mNRS (PHQ-9 group = −1.3 points [SD 1.3]; NPQ/rNPQ group = −2.0 points [SD 1.8]). When engagement with and referral for care were treated as mediators of the relationship between baseline screening and 6-month depressive symptoms, the direct effect was attenuated but remained significant at 0.41 points (95% CI = 0.23–0.57) and 0.45 points (95% CI = 0.32–0.60), respectively. Engagement with psychological care partially mediated the relationship between depression screening and smaller improvements in mNRS at 6-month follow-up (indirect effect = 0.07, 95% CI = 0.02–0.30, P = 0.024). Referral for psychological care did not mediate the relationship between screening and mNRS (indirect effect = 0.10, 95% CI = −0.08 to 0.30, P = 0.2). 3.3. Sensitivity analysis

Findings from the sensitivity analyses suggest that the direct effects remain robust against potential unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding. For example, the minimum strength of the association of an unmeasured confounder with both the mediator and the outcome conditional on the measured confounders would need to be greater than 1.73 (engagement with psychological care) and 1.81 (referral for psychological care) on the risk ratio scale to reduce the direct effect to zero.

In models including clinician as a random intercept, the clinician-level variance was estimated at 0.00 (singular fit), and inclusion of this term did not materially alter the total, direct, or indirect effect estimates.

3.4. Post-hoc analysis

Post-hoc subanalysis revealed statistically significant depressive symptom differences at 6 months between the PHQ-9 and NPQ/rNPQ groups across different baseline mNRS thresholds. For low baseline scores (0–2), the PHQ-9 group showed a small mean improvement (−0.46 points, 95% CI = −0.61 to −0.32), whereas the NPQ/rNPQ group did not meet significance (0.09 points, 95% CI = −0.06 to 0.23). For moderate baseline scores (3–7), the PHQ-9 group had a smaller mean improvement (−1.87 points, 95% CI = −1.99 to −1.75) than the NPQ/rNPQ group (−2.96 points, 95% CI = −3.04 to −2.88). For high baseline scores (8–10), the PHQ-9 group had a smaller mean improvement (−1.74 points, 95% CI = −1.86 to −1.61) than the NPQ/rNPQ group (−3.32 points, 95% CI = −3.4 to −3.23; Fig. 3B).

Exploratory repeated-measures ANOVAs showed no between-group differences at 6-month follow-up for pain (PHQ-9 vs NPQ/rNPQ, MD = −1.53, SE = 0.86, t(1777) = −1.78, P = 0.284) or function (MD = 0.15, SE = 0.12, t(1777) = 1.34, P = 0.540).

Given the higher proportion of fibromyalgia/CWP/CFS in the PHQ group, and the higher proportion of CRPS in the NPQ/rNPQ group (Table 1), post-hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted using linear regressions to assess whether diagnostic differences were driving the group effect. The group difference in depressive symptoms remained after removing those with fibromyalgia/CWP/CFS (MD = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.55–0.76; P < 0.001; n = 1715), CRPS (MD = 0.64, 95% CI = 0.51–0.76; P < 0.001; n = 1373), and both diagnoses (MD = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.53–0.78; P < 0.001; n = 1309).

4. Discussion

In this real-world pain care context, participants with persistent pain who completed a validated screening tool for depressive symptoms (PHQ-9) had significantly less improvement in depressive symptoms at 6-month follow-up than those who completed a questionnaire assessing pain knowledge (NPQ/rNPQ). This effect remained after accounting for referral to, or engagement with, psychological care. The main effect appeared to be driven by participants with high and moderate depressive symptoms at baseline.

Our findings suggest that structured symptom assessment with the PHQ-9 within clinical encounters may function as more than passive measurement and may itself be associated with subsequent mood trajectories. Further, our findings build on previous literature showing that screening alone has little to no effect on depression-related outcomes in general, high-risk, and perinatal care populations.2,10 Although symptom improvement occurred in both groups, we identified a small attenuation of symptom improvement among those with moderate or high baseline symptoms screened with the PHQ-9. That is, structured assessment may not be neutral within pain care encounters—perhaps here it reduced engagement or improvement. Although our analysis cannot identify the exact mechanism, contextual factors such as symptom identification, feedback, or care pathways may contribute. This suggests that the assessment process itself should be considered an important component of the influence of clinical interactions.

How might structured depression screening influence subsequent mood trajectories? Current theories of depression21 suggest that the act of screening may increase the salience of negative mood–related thoughts and behaviours, resulting in greater rumination or awareness. Such awareness has been described as attention bias7 and has been implicated as central to depressive disorders. The screening process may initiate a shift in self-concept,1 a construct influenced by experiences, context, and environmental factors. Negative self-concepts are associated with risk for depression38 and maintenance of depressive symptoms.7

It is also possible that the observed effect relates to clinician behaviour around screening, which we did not assess beyond whether the patient was referred to psychological care. How clinicians handle, interpret, and provide feedback about the results of depression questionnaires is presumably important. A recent qualitative evaluation of women's experiences when screened for perinatal depression found that participants felt uncared for when clinicians failed to report their results.11 Thus, the difference between groups observed here may have been influenced by clinician handling of the responses to the PHQ-9. Another possible explanation for the observed effect is that clinicians relied on a questionnaire rather than clinical judgment, resulting in suboptimal patient validation and identification of the true need for psychological care. A final consideration is that the observed effect is driven by completing the NPQ/rNPQ. Although the NPQ/rNPQ is a knowledge-based questionnaire specific to the understanding of pain, and we can see no theoretical basis for it contributing to these results, the current design cannot rule out that completing the NPQ/rNPQ was the causal agent.

That the process of structured depression symptom assessment may influence depressive symptom trajectories in people with persistent pain, is consistent with the broader literature on assessment reactivity, suggesting that structured measurement may function as more than a passive interaction.5,24 An MCID of approximately 21% (−1.7 points; within-group) has been proposed for the PHQ-9 in primary care, with larger percentage reductions required for mild symptoms.14 Although an MCID for the single-item mNRS has not been established, our previous work in a similar population reported a strong correlation with the PHQ-9 (r = 0.83).28 Thus, with caution, we used a 21% symptom reduction on the mNRS as a pragmatic threshold to aid interpretation of group-level differences. Between-group differences in symptom change favoured the PHQ-9 group for low baseline symptoms (∼38%), and the NPQ/rNPQ group for moderate (∼22%) and high (∼19%) symptoms, with only the moderate group exceeding the 21% threshold.

All PHQ-9 screened groups exceeded this threshold, whereas only the moderate and high baseline NPQ/rNPQ groups did. Although this suggests symptom improvement across groups, a significant between-group difference was seen. Among those with moderate baseline symptoms, NPQ/rNPQ participants improved by 1.1 points more than PHQ-9 participants; in the high group, by 1.6 points. These differences were statistically significant, but their clinical relevance remains uncertain. These findings suggest that although both groups likely achieved clinically meaningful improvement, those who were screened with the PHQ-9 improved less overall (see supplemental digital content, Table 1.4, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A413).

These findings should not be interpreted as evidence against identifying psychological symptoms in pain care. Rather, they highlight the importance of how assessment tools are introduced, interpreted, and integrated within clinical conversations. The mNRS, as a brief and well-correlated alternative,28 may offer a lower-burden first-pass screening option. However, ensuring that such a strategy does not leave particularly vulnerable patients at risk of not receiving support is important. Certain questions might be necessary inclusions, such as item 9 from the PHQ-9 (Thoughts that you would be better off dead or hurting yourself in some way), a strong predictor of those who would later attempt suicide.30 Another strategy may be to ensure that clinicians have skills to assess mental health distress and context through conversation.

Given the relationship between pain and depressed mood, it may be important that clinical interactions validate and normalise such symptoms and use an approach that considers depression in the context of pain rather than screening and treating it as an isolated condition. For example, of those referred, 76% in the NPQ/rNPQ group engaged with a psychologist vs 68% in the PHQ-9 group. This suggests that questionnaire exposure alone does not necessarily enhance engagement, and that how results are discussed and contextualised may be influential. Indeed, how practitioners communicate depression screening outcomes with the patient, and how the patient makes sense of the clinical encounter, may be important factors influencing subsequent behaviour and mood outcomes. Future work should examine how different assessment formats, feedback processes, and clinician communication styles influence patient sense-making and behavioural engagement.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

We followed contemporary open science practices as recommended in the pain field,19 by prospectively registering our analysis plan before data analysis on Open Science Framework and reporting deviations. We adhered to STROBE reporting guidelines36 and the AGReMA best practice statement for mediation analysis.18 Further, because of our large sample, we were adequately powered for our primary outcome. Given the real-world context of this study, our findings have ecological validity that may not be present in a general survey or clinical research setting.3

These data were collected as part of a clinical audit, not as part of a study prospectively designed to test our hypotheses. We did not have access to treatment characteristics beyond what we have reported (eg, psychological treatment received). Although we identified and controlled for potential mediator–outcome confounders in our analyses, there may be other unmeasured confounders of this relationship introducing bias, for example social determinants and genetic predisposition, an existing depression diagnosis at baseline, prior engagement with psychological care, and current psychiatric medication use, which may influence both pain and depressive symptom trajectories. Future research could explore these possibilities. Notably, our sensitivity analyses suggested that our findings remain robust to unmeasured confounding and would require a strong unknown confounder to explain away the effect. Our comparator group still completed the mNRS, which may itself have influenced results. The scope of our data is constrained to pain-related clinics across 3 Western countries, limiting wider generalisability. We used available audit data providing a 6-month follow-up, possibly obscuring larger depressive symptom changes that may emerge over extended treatment and follow-up periods. Participants were quasi-randomly assigned to the 2 questionnaires to reduce burden rather than to test the present hypothesis, which may limit the study's causal interpretability. However, this approach also eliminated the possibility that bias in our question would affect the outcome. Unmeasured site-level factors may have influenced results—we could not examine site-specific cultures, service setting types, or their potential influence on provider behaviour around screening. Future work should use a prospective experimental design with a true control to confirm effects. We acknowledge that the mNRS and PHQ-9 may measure different constructs and that the comparison is not one-to-one. However, the strong correlation and diagnostic properties of the mNRS in this population support its use as a proxy.28 As such, the results should be interpreted with caution.

5. Conclusion

Our findings suggest that exposure to a structured depression symptom questionnaire (PHQ-9), compared with a pain-knowledge questionnaire, was associated with smaller improvements in depressive symptoms for people with persistent pain, regardless of referral for, or engagement with, psychological care, and particularly in those with moderate to high baseline. There seems a need to better understand how structured assessments influence clinical encounters and clinical trajectories. Future research with a priori hypotheses is needed to replicate these findings and clarify underlying mechanisms.
