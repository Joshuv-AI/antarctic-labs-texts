ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntensive care units (ICUs) represent high-risk environments for adverse events (AEs). Preventing these events requires a solid understanding of their epidemiology based on standardized and validated methodologies. The aim of this study was to quantify AEs using the established Harvard Medical Practice Study (HMPS) methodology, identify associated factors, and analyze their prognostic impact on critically ill patients.Materials and methodsA multicenter observational study with a cross-sectional design was conducted in 26 hospitals across the Community of Madrid on a single, specified day in May 2019. Following the HMPS methodology and using its standardized instruments, clinical records were reviewed in two phases: (1) an AE screening phase and (2) a confirmation and characterization phase for cases with positive screenings. A multivariate logistic regression model was developed to identify variables associated with the presence of AEs, and the association between AEs and in-hospital mortality was assessed using an explanatory model adjusted for potential confounders.ResultsA total of 458 patients were included, of whom 125 (27.3%; 95% CI: 23.4 to 31.6) had ≥1 AE. The following antecedents were associated with the presence of AEs: pressure ulcer (PU), hypoalbuminemia, mechanical ventilation, central venous catheter, surgical intervention and sensory deficit. Among the AEs found, 44.6% were health care-associated infections (HAIs). In-hospital mortality among patients with AEs was twice that observed in patients without AEs (OR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.10–4.25).ConclusionsThe presence of AEs was associated with a two-fold higher risk of in-hospital mortality. Among the identified AEs, HAIs were the most frequent and had the highest mortality. Designing and implementing bundles of measures adapted to the individual risk of presenting AEs in the ICU emerges as the next step in the development of strategies to reduce the prevalence of AEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05917-x.

Introduction

Adverse events (AEs) related to health care are among the leading challenges to patient safety, as they involve unintended harm resulting from the care provided. AEs refer to injuries attributable to medical management and may occur with or without error; in contrast, medical errors represent failures in the process of care that may or may not result in patient harm [1]. Several studies have estimated that the prevalence of AEs in hospitalized patients ranges from 3.2% to 23.6% [2, 3], with these figures varying according to patient comorbidities and the care setting. AEs rarely arise from a single cause; instead, they result from a multifactorial interplay between latent system failures, patient factors and individual errors, shaped by organizational, environmental, and human factors.

In this context, intensive care units (ICUs) represent an environment of particular vulnerability to AEs. The complexity of critically ill patients—combined with the use of invasive procedures, polypharmacy, and advanced technologies—creates conditions prone to errors in care [4]. Consequently, the ICU is the hospital setting with the highest prevalence of AEs, estimated to range between 15% and 51% [5–7], with medication-related AEs occurring twice as frequently as in other units [8, 9]. To factors such as organ failure, the need for higher levels of care, advanced age, the number of invasive procedures performed (including intubation, catheterization, and drainage), admission to a surgical service, elevated APACHE scores, and ICU readmission [8–10]. Moreover, experiencing AEs in the ICU has negative consequences for both patients and the health system, including increased mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and higher economic costs [11–13].

Further epidemiological characterization of adverse events in ICUs is essential to identify priority areas and to develop proactive strategies that anticipate errors before they occur. To achieve this, the use of AE measurement tools with a high degree of validity and reliability is crucial. Among the different strategies employed in patient safety, the methodology providing the strongest level of evidence is that developed by Brennan et al. in the Harvard Medical Practice Study (HMPS) [14], which is based on a two-phase retrospective medical record review: an initial screening phase with high sensitivity (84%) and negative predictive value (92%) for AE detection, followed by a second confirmation phase with acceptable inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.61) [15]. This methodology has been used in large-scale studies measuring AEs in various regions, such as the United Kingdom, Australia, Spain, Ireland and Latin America [16–19]. The results have been used by the WHO to estimate the burden associated with AEs and for the preparation of their current recommendations [20].

However, most studies on AEs in the ICU do not use the HMPS methodology and are based on questionnaires developed from an expert consensus (the Delphi method) [21, 22] or use the Global Trigger Tool [12], thus yielding greatly variable results, making reliability and comparability difficult. In this context, the aim of the present study was to analyze the frequency and distribution of AEs in all ICUs in the same administrative region using the standardized and validated HMPS methodology [15]. The specific objectives of the study are 1) to identify the variables associated with the appearance of AEs in critically ill patients, 2) to determine the main types of AEs that affect patients admitted to the ICU, and 3) to analyze the clinical impact of AEs and evaluate their association with in-hospital mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design

Multicenter cross-sectional observational study. All patients admitted to ICUs of various care profiles in 26 public hospitals in the Community of Madrid, Spain, were included (16 medical ICUs, 6 surgical critical care units, 4 neonatal ICUs, and 6 pediatric or mixed units). The cross-sectional assessment was conducted on a single, specified day in May 2019.

Phases of the study

The study was developed within the framework of the Safety Study in Hospitals of the Community of Madrid (ESHMAD) [23], following the HMPS methodology, which involved two phases of medical record review:

Screening phase: An adaptation of the Screening Review Form was used [24] to detect possible adverse events (AEs) and collect basic epidemiological variables. Records that met at least one screening criterion were considered positive screenings, indicating a potential AE that requires further review. Records that did not meet any criteria were considered negative screenings and were excluded from the second phase.Confirmation and classification phase: Patients with positive screening results were individually assessed using the Modular Review Form 2 (MRF2) [24] at hospital discharge (or at 30 days if the patient was still hospitalized) to confirm, classify and characterize the AEs.

Prior to the execution of the ESHMAD, all participating hospitals received training in patient safety and in the application of the assessment tools to ensure high-quality data collection. This training consisted of several sessions, beginning with a comprehensive theoretical overview of patient safety principles and detailed instructions on the use of the assessment tools followed by practical exercises to standardize reviewer evaluations. All the sessions were conducted by experienced patient safety professionals. Full details of the study design, training procedures, and data collection process are described in Valencia-Martín et al. [25]

Study variables

The main variables were the presence AEs and in-hospital mortality. AEs were defined according to the World Health Organization framework as any incident occurring during medical care that results in harm to the patient, including injury, suffering, disability, or death. Accordingly, AEs were not limited to events arising from negligence or error [18, 26]. To assess the likelihood that the event was attributable to healthcare, the MRF2 Likert-type scale was used, which assigns scores from 1 to 6, with 1 indicating “minimal evidence that medical management caused the harm” and 6 indicating “definite evidence that medical management was the cause of the harm”. According to the HMPS methodology and the previous validation of the MRF2, those with a score ≥4 were considered to have a confirmed AE [15, 24, 27].

Variables related to AEs, such as type (following the classification provided by the MRF2), were collected [27]. Categories were mutually exclusive, and severity was assessed following the definitions of the WHO Taxonomy [1], the Point Prevalence Survey of the European Center for Disease Control [28], and previous studies based on the HMPS [15, 29]. Severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on their impact on patient care and outcomes. Mild AEs were those that caused minimal or no additional intervention or monitoring. Moderate AEs required additional treatment, tests, observation, or temporary prolongation of hospitalization but did not result in permanent harm. Severe AEs involved significant consequences, such as permanent harm, life-threatening events, major surgical intervention, or the need for life support. The time of onset of each AE during hospitalization was documented, as well as its impact on health care or additional days of hospitalization. The impact on healthcare was evaluated individually for each AE using the MRF2 questionnaire. For each event, reviewers assessed whether it affected healthcare and, if so, classified the impact as: healthcare not affected; required observation or monitoring; required an additional test; required medical treatment or rehabilitation; required an additional surgical intervention; or required life support. This approach allowed multiple AEs and their respective impacts to be recorded independently for the same patient.

In addition, the following demographic and clinical variables of the patients were recorded: sex, age, total hospital stay, surgical history, type of ICU and level of complexity of the hospital. A history of relevant comorbidities (cardiovascular diseases, neoplasms, COPD, immunodeficiencies, liver cirrhosis, hypoalbuminemia, pressure ulcers [PUs], mobility limitations, sensory deficits, renal failure, neutropenia, obesity and active smoking [etc.]) was included [29, 30], as well as factors related to invasive procedures in clinical practice (the presence of peripheral or central venous catheters, mechanical ventilation and bladder catheterization at the time of screening).

The complete definitions of the variables, criteria and categories can be found in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1 and S2).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed according to the presence or absence of AEs. For quantitative variables, the mean and standard deviation were reported when the data were approximately normally distributed, while the median and interquartile range were used for variables with non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were summarized using proportions. AE characteristics are described according to the outcome of the patient (discharge vs. death). For bivariate analysis, the Mann‒Whitney U test and the chi-square test were used. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Variables associated with the presence of AEs in the ICU were identified using a multivariate unconditional logistic regression model, with AEs as the dependent variable. All candidate variables were initially considered for inclusion. A stepwise selection procedure was then applied, in which variables were sequentially entered or removed based on a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05. Variables not meeting this criterion were excluded, resulting in a parsimonious final model that retained only those factors significantly associated with AEs.

Finally, the association between the presence of AEs and in-hospital mortality was evaluated using an explanatory logistic regression model, adjusting for confounding variables (those that modified the crude association by more than 10%). The results of the analysis of confounding factors can be found in the Supplementary Material (Table S3).

The mean age of patients who experienced ≥1 AE was higher than that of patients who did not experience an AE (59.7 years vs. 49.3; p<0.001). In addition, there was a higher incidence of AEs in patients with a previous surgical intervention (34.3% vs. 19.8%; p<0.001), in patients with a greater number of comorbidities (36.9% in patients with ≥ 3 comorbidities vs. 8.5% in patients without comorbidities; p<0.001) and in patients with a greater number of clinical instrumentation factors (34.8% in those with ≥ three vs. 8.5% in those with one; p<0.001). Among the patients who died, 58.5% had ≥1 AE, whereas this figure was 23.2% for patients who did not die (p<0.001).

Among HAIs, the most frequent type was pneumonia (31.1% of HAIs), followed by surgical site infection (20.3%). Among the total number of patients who died (53), 15.1% had pneumonia, and 5.7% had surgical site infections. Among the procedure-related AEs, hemorrhages or hematomas accounted for 34% of the total and 12.5% of the total number of patients who died. Among the care-related AEs, the most frequent AE was PUs (85.7% of the total), whereas heart failure and other cardiovascular diseases (acute myocardial infarction (AMI), pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), cardiovascular accident (CVA) or deep vein thrombosis (DVT)) accounted for 18.7% of the medication-related AEs (Table 3).

76.5% of AEs were classified as moderate or severe, and 96.4% required some type of additional care, including observation or monitoring, additional tests or procedures, medical treatment, rehabilitation, or life support. Among patients who died, 36% experienced at least one severe AE, and in 63.2% of cases, the AE resulted in an increased hospital stay of five to nine days (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of AEs in critically ill patients and to evaluate their association with in-hospital mortality. Our findings show that AEs are highly frequent in the ICU setting and are strongly associated with patient outcomes. Among the 458 patients admitted to 50 ICUs with different care profiles, nearly three out of ten experienced at least one AE. In-hospital mortality was 11.6%, higher than that reported in conventional hospital wards [31] and the presence of ≥1 AE was associated with a twofold increase in the risk of death after adjusting for confounding factors (OR [95% CI] 2.15 [1.10–4.25]). Using the HMPS methodology, three out of five patients who died had ≥1 AE, a proportion higher than that reported in ICU populations by Nilsson et al. [32] or by Zegers et al [33], who reported prevalence rates of 19.5% and 10.7%, respectively. The higher prevalence of AEs observed in our study likely reflects a combination of methodological and sample-related factors. Methodologically, the use of a highly sensitive detection tool, rigorous training and calibration of reviewers, and the inclusion of all identified AEs—regardless of severity—enhanced detection rates. In terms of the sample, our study included a large and diverse group of hospitals with varying levels of care and patient case-mix, increasing the likelihood of identifying AEs compared with studies with smaller or more homogeneous populations. Although this may limit direct comparability with studies using less sensitive tools or narrower samples, these factors together provide a comprehensive and systematic assessment of AEs in the ICU setting.

Additionally, 4 out of 10 AEs detected were classified as severe, increasing to 5 out of 10 among patients who died. This frequency is higher than that reported in previous studies, which estimated the prevalence of severe AEs between 12.8% and 22.8% [33, 34], and aligns with other studies indicating that the rate of severe AEs is up to seven times greater in patients who die during hospitalization [34]. Given that previous evidence considers 50% of AEs preventable [35, 36], these findings highlight the potential impact of AE prevention strategies on improving patient safety in the ICU, reinforcing its role as a strategic element in reducing mortality.

The most frequent type of AE detected were HAIs, followed by procedure-related AEs, care-related AEs, and medication-related AEs. This differs from the findings of longitudinal studies, which revealed that AEs related to medication were the most frequent AEs [4, 22]. The cross-sectional design may have led to an overestimation of HAIs and procedure related AEs because since both are associated with a prolonged hospital stay. Similarly, the impact of HAIs highlights the importance of their prevention [11, 16]. Among the patients who died, approximately 40% had HAIs, and 1 in 7 had procedure related AEs.

The high prevalence and impact of HAIs in ICUs can be explained by the clinical complexity of these patients, who often require the use of invasive devices that predispose them to complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) or catheter-associated bloodstream infections [8, 12]. In addition, in our sample, HAIs were the AEs that were most strongly associated with in-hospital mortality. Among them, pneumonia stood out for its high mortality, a finding that is consistent with the results of a study by Johnstone et al., who reported that VAP was associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality of between 30% and 80%, depending on the definition used [37]. Similarly, another study revealed that the risk of death doubled in patients with nosocomial pneumonia in the ICU (OR: 2.1 to 2.7), adjusting for severity and comorbidities [38].

The magnitude of HAIs in the ICU is known and has motivated various initiatives to mitigate their impact. In the Spanish context, the National Health System, in collaboration with the Spanish Society of Intensive Medicine and the Spanish Society of Intensive Nursing, has promoted the development of Project Zero, which is part of the country’s patient safety strategy [1]. This project includes Bacteremia Zero, Pneumonia Zero, Resistance Zero and UTI Zero. Its implementation, which is based on numerous preventive measures, has reduced the frequency of HAIs by up to 50%, has decreased mortality and has improved safety culture [39].

Since AEs arise from a multifactorial interaction between latent system failures—such as organisational culture, communication processes, workflow design, or lack of resources—and patient-related factors—including comorbidity, severity of illness, and exposure to high-risk interventions—along with active human errors, it is essential to understand how these patient-related factors interact with the care system to shape vulnerability to harm [40]. Preventive strategies should therefore combine system-level interventions with targeted measures adapted to the epidemiological risk profile of patients, alongside the implementation of evidence-based safety bundles that integrate multiple coordinated actions. Thus, our multivariate model identified older patients undergoing a previous surgical intervention and with a history of hypoalbuminemia, PUs, sensory deficit, mechanical ventilation and central venous catheter as risk profiles. These findings offer a comprehensive perspective of the epidemiology of AEs in the ICU and coincide with the results of previous studies. In a 2020 study in Canada, Sauro et al. reported an association between the presence of AEs and surgical intervention, mechanical ventilation, a high APACHE score and the presence of two or more comorbidities [8]. In other studies, multiorgan failure, previous comorbidities, longer ICU stays and increased NEMS scores were identified as factors associated with the presence of AEs [9, 10, 13]. Among clinical instrumentation factors, one study revealed that 10% of AEs were associated with the presence of vascular ports and catheters (including accidental removal) [7].

In this regard, our results provide a detailed view of the epidemiology of AEs by using a model with 23 epidemiological variables, allowing for more precise identification of at-risk patients. These findings support the development of targeted bundles of interventions tailored to the risk profiles of patients, as a strategy to reduce the prevalence of AEs in ICU. The evidence shows that the application of sets of structured measures is effective, as indicated by improvements in adherence to the guidelines for the prevention of surgical wound infections or with the implementation of protocols for the safe use of catheters and probes [41]. Similarly, organizational and cultural factors—team coordination, effective communication and continuous training—increase the effectiveness of these interventions [9]. A recent study from 2023 revealed that a higher proportion of certified nurses with specific training in critical care at the time of admission was associated with a lower probability of AEs [42]. It is also important to emphasize the need for a proactive approach to address the underlying causes of errors that can lead to AEs [43]. Preventive measures must therefore be tailored to the specific context in which they are applied, taking into account patient characteristics, types of procedures performed and centre-specific factors such as workload, staff turnover, and safety culture. This approach enhances the relevance and feasibility of preventive measures and increases the likelihood of achieving meaningful reductions in AE incidence.

Our study has certain limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow the establishment of causal relationships; thus, the findings should be interpreted as an association. However, this design offers significant advantages, as it not only allows for more efficient case review but also demonstrates high sensitivity for detecting serious AEs or those that prolong hospital stay—precisely the outcomes we aimed to analyze in detail to inform the implementation of prevention strategies [16]. Another possible limitation of our study lies in the fact that we analyzed all the participating ICUs together, despite the fact that they have different characteristics. However, this also provides relevant advantages, since it provides results that are more generalizable and applicable to a larger population of critically ill patients. Besides, although all hospitals were from a single administrative region, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems or regions with different organizational structures, the inclusion of 50 ICUs from 26 hospitals increases the representativeness of the sample and enhances both the internal and external validity of the findings. Furthermore, the study relied on retrospective review of medical records, which may be subject to missing or incomplete information. In particular, some AEs may not have been documented, potentially leading to underestimation of their true incidence. Although electronic health records provided structured and consistently organized data, supported by automated laboratory, diagnostic, and discharge reports, and represent the most feasible and accessible method, we acknowledge that this approach has inherent limitations that may affect the completeness and accuracy of the information collected. Finally, while the two-stage HMPS methodology may potentially undercount AEs by not reviewing negative screenings from the first stage, we maintained this approach to ensure comparability with other studies using this well-established method. Moreover, the Screening Review Form (SRF), with its high sensitivity and negative predictive value, minimizes the impact of this limitation [44]. One of the greatest strengths of our study is the use of the standardized and validated HMPS methodology, complemented with WHO definitions for AEs and ECDC for HAIs, thus providing an additional degree of quality in the results. Finally, the consideration of 23 risk factors in the analyses ensured a comprehensive approach and may facilitate the design of preventive bundles adapted to the individual risk of each patient as a strategy to reduce the prevalence of AEs in the ICU. Future research with longitudinal designs should evaluate the effectiveness of interventions based on this approach.

Conclusion

ICUs represent a critical environment in patient safety because of the high prevalence of AEs, which are associated with a significant increase in in-hospital mortality, even doubling it.

Among the identified AEs, HAIs were the most frequent and those associated with higher mortality; procedure-related AEs were the second most frequent. Almost all of the AEs required some type of assistance or additional intervention, providing evidence of their high clinical and care impacts.

The risk profile identified corresponds to older patients and patients with a history of surgery, hypoalbuminemia, PUs, mechanical ventilation, sensory deficit or a central venous catheter. These findings support the need to design and implement preventive measures adapted to patients according to the presence of these risk factors to optimize the prevention of AEs and reduce their impact on in-hospital mortality and on the care burden.

Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of this approach in longitudinal studies, integrating strategies that increase the culture of safety and training of professionals to maximize patient safety in the ICU.
