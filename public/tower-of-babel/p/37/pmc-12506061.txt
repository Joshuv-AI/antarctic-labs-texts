Shared decision-making using a decision aid for patients with breast cancer considering breast reconstruction: study protocol for a cluster-randomised controlled trial in Japan
Authors: Yoshihiro Sowa, Wakako Osaka, Itaru Tsuge, Takako Komiya, Miho Saiga, Mifue Taminato, Naomi Nagura, Tomohiro Shiraishi, Hirohito Seki, Yuki Otsuki, Yuki Matsuoka, Shoichi Tomita, Shinsuke Akita, Hiroshi Fujimoto, Kazuhiro Otani, Kotaro Yoshimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionShared decision-making (SDM) requires that individuals are correctly and smoothly supported to make decisions. However, in Japan, development of decision aids (DAs) to support implementation of SDM is lagging behind Western countries, and there are few reports focused on breast reconstruction. Thus, it is unclear if SDM using a DA in the context of the unique national character and medical culture in Japan is useful in decision-making for breast reconstruction, including whether or not to undergo reconstruction. The aim of this multicentre collaborative study is to investigate the clinical effectiveness of SDM using a DA for patients with breast cancer considering reconstruction, from the perspectives of decisional conflict and postoperative quality of life.Methods and analysisA multisite trial will be conducted at 12 facilities certified by the Japanese Society of Breast Oncoplastic Surgery. A cluster-randomised controlled trial is planned at centres that have implemented SDM with DAs and those that have not implemented SDM, but use a conventional surgical explanation and informed consent to make decisions about reconstruction methods. The study participants will be female patients aged ≥20 years with newly diagnosed stage 0–III breast cancer who are interested in breast reconstruction. Data collection includes baseline and follow-up patient surveys and medical record review. The effectiveness of the DA at reducing conflict and regret in decision-making (primary outcome) will be evaluated using the decision conflict scale.Ethics and disseminationThis protocol has been approved by the Kyoto University Central Institutional Review Board, and permission for performance of the study has been obtained from the Ethics Review Board at each participating centre. We plan to disseminate the findings through journal publications and national meetings, including a presentation of the research results at the Japanese Society of Breast Oncoplastic Surgery. Our findings will advance the science of medical decision-making and have the potential to reduce socioeconomic health disparities.Trial registration numberUMIN000052161.Keywords: Patients, Breast tumours, Patient Care Management, Patient-Centered Care

Introduction

Background and rationale

There has been significant progress in transformation and diversification of breast reconstruction in Japan. Personalised medical approaches are becoming increasingly common, with combined use of autologous tissue and implants, including fat grafting, in approaches tailored to each patient’s preferences, values and body shape.1,3 However, these advances suggest that treatment options in breast reconstruction will become increasingly complex, and optimising reconstruction methods to suit each individual is the next challenge. For instance, in early-stage breast cancer, there is no difference in survival rates between breast-conserving surgery (BCS) combined with radiation therapy and mastectomy. However, decision-making regarding how to choose and incorporate breast reconstruction can be challenging for women in their 40s and 50s who juggle multiple roles as professionals, mothers, wives, daughters and daughters-in-law, especially considering the potential changes to body image caused by breast cancer surgery.

In such circumstances, a new approach to decision-making, referred to as shared decision making (SDM), is gaining attention in Japan in various medical fields.4,6 SDM supports patients in active participation in treatment decisions and uses collaboration between patients and healthcare providers to determine the best treatment plan. This approach is gradually becoming important in breast cancer treatment, including breast reconstruction.7 8 By using decision aids (DAs) that provide neutral and accurate information and present various options, SDM can be facilitated smoothly and effectively to encourage patients to engage in the decision-making process.9 10

In this context, a DA has been developed in Japan to assist with selection of breast cancer surgical options of BCS, mastectomy or mastectomy with reconstruction. Patients who used this DA had a significant decrease in postsurgical decisional conflict scores 1 month after surgery compared with those who received normal care.6 However, there is still a lack of information regarding breast reconstruction options associated with breast cancer, and a suitable DA is required to allow full implementation of SDM. We have developed the first DA in Japan specific to breast reconstruction that meets the qualifying and certification criteria of the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) framework.11 In a small field test, we showed that our DA can aid in the smooth and effective implementation of SDM in decision-making for selecting surgical options for post-mastectomy breast reconstruction.

There are still a few high-quality studies focused on breast reconstruction, and it remains unclear whether use of a DA for SDM in the context of the unique national characteristics and medical culture in Japan is beneficial for making decisions about breast reconstruction methods, including whether to undergo breast reconstruction at all. In the future, we hope that patients with breast cancer in Japan who are considering breast reconstruction will be able to use a DA to facilitate effective SDM.

Objectives

We plan to perform a cluster-randomised controlled trial in Japan. Types of trial include: parallel testing of two groups with equal allocation ratios, superiority testing. The purpose of this multicentre collaborative study is to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of implementation of SDM using a DA in breast reconstruction for patients with breast cancer, from the perspectives of decision conflict, effectiveness of SDM, and postoperative quality of life (QOL) and satisfaction. The study also aims to show the usefulness of a DA in supporting decision-making and improving postoperative QOL for patients with breast cancer based on consideration of breast reconstruction options, and to provide a resource for establishing guidelines to ensure decisions are made through the correct processes.

Methods and analysis

Patient and public involvement

Patients will be invited to comment on and guide various aspects of the research process, such as research materials to improve clarity and readability. Furthermore, patients will be interviewed about the intervention, how it works for them and how it can be adapted to their needs. Reporting of patient involvement in this study will be done according to the ‘Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public checklist’.

Trial design and setting

A clustered-randomised controlled trial will be performed to assess the effectiveness of SDM using a DA developed in Japan (intervention) compared with normal informed consent (control/usual decision-making) at a 1:1 ratio for two groups or trial arms. A cluster corresponds to a university hospital or a civil hospital, including Kyoto University Hospital, with plastic surgeons with experience of at least 20 breast reconstructions per year. Based on our sample size calculation, a total of 12 clusters, each with an average of two eligible plastic surgeons, will be formed at three geographical sites located in different cities and regions of Japan.

The trial design and chronological implementation steps at each site are shown in figure 1. The group in which physicians will receive education for SDM will be determined at random before the start of the recruitment period at each site. Thus, cluster randomisation will be stratified at the study site. Patients will be assigned chronologically to one of the two groups according to their time of recruitment. Data for primary and secondary outcomes will be assessed at each time point. The educational programme for SDM will run from March to May 2024 and end after all patients in the cluster at the last facility to start the trial have been followed for 12 months.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: The potential subjects are patients scheduled for breast cancer surgery at an outpatient plastic surgery clinic at Kyoto University Hospital and participating centres. A determination of whether each patient meets the trial criteria will be made at their clinic visit to discuss breast reconstruction. To be eligible, patients must meet all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) pathological diagnosis of breast cancer including Ductal Carcinoma In Situ between the date of approval for implementation by the head of the research centre and 31 March 2025, and visiting an outpatient plastic surgery clinic to be considered for breast reconstruction surgery; (2) age ≥20 years old at the time of diagnosis; (3) female and (4) breast cancer stages 0–III. Patients meeting any of the following criteria will be excluded: (1) secondary breast reconstruction; (2) diagnosed with recurrent or distant metastasis of breast cancer; (3) undergoing expander insertion; (4) difficulty answering the questionnaire in Japanese; (5) participants who discontinue or deviate from intervention protocols and (6) serious complications such as mastectomy flap necrosis in breast surgery or breast reconstruction, or implant infection requiring prolonged hospitalisation and revision surgery.

Secondary reconstruction was excluded because many patients who wish to undergo breast reconstruction have already been exposed to some form of information or counselling at the time of their initial breast cancer surgery, and this influence is considered to be non-negligible. Furthermore, patients with tissue expanders in place were also excluded, as most of them had likely already made definitive decisions regarding their reconstructive plans and were therefore no longer in the active phase of decision-making.

Intervention and comparator

Control group: General pamphlets that expand knowledge and understanding of breast reconstruction may be used, but use of these pamphlets is not mandatory. The practitioners will be free to use the same decision-making methods as those used in normal practice.

Intervention group: Physicians participating in the study will attend an SDM educational programme and will meet the study acceptance criteria. The Japanese DA for breast reconstruction, ‘Guide to Deciding on Your Own Breast Reconstruction’ (healthliteracy.jp/decisionaid/decision/post-7.html) will be used to implement SDM in the decision-making process (figure 2).

Japanese version of the DA for breast reconstruction: ‘Guide to Deciding on Your Own Breast Reconstruction’. DA, decision aid.

DA: The Japanese DA for breast reconstruction that will serve as a support tool for implementing SDM was systematically developed based on the IPDAS collaboration (http://ipdas.ohri.ca/; accessed on 1 November 2012). The DA meets all six qualification criteria and fulfils the 10 certification criteria and 28 quality criteria to the greatest extent possible. Cycles of iterative feedback have been obtained from steering group members and from additional patients, clinicians and design experts. The developed protocol can be implemented in various clinical settings and has undergone a preliminary fieldwork test at a single facility.

Outcomes

Decision Conflict Scale (DCS): The DCS was developed in 1995 to measure uncertainty in choosing healthcare options, modifiable factors contributing to this uncertainty and the quality of the decision-making process.12,15 The DCS has 16 items assessing 5 dimensions of decision-making: feeling uncertain (3 items), feeling uninformed (3 items), feeling unclear about personal values (3 items), feeling unsupported (3 items) and ineffective decision-making (4 items).16 This scale is available in Japanese and is useful for evaluating the impact of decision support interventions (DESI) and for determining how to tailor DESI to meet the specific needs of patients.6

SDM-Q-9: The SDM-Q-9 is a validated 9-item questionnaire to assess the level of SDM perceived by patients.17 Each item describes a separate step in the SDM process and is rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 0 (‘completely disagree’) to 5 (‘completely agree’). Higher scores are indicative of more SDM. Summed scores per patient will be calculated (range: 0–45) and multiplied by 100/45 to obtain a percentage of the maximum score.18

Breast Reconstruction Education and Support Tool Questionnaire (BREAST-Q): The BREAST-Q is a self-administered assessment tool composed of 15 subscales and 121 items designed to measure the impact of breast surgery on patient satisfaction and health-related QOL (HRQOL).19 Each subscale is scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction and QOL. In this study, the following four subscales will be used: satisfaction with breasts, psychosocial well-being, physical well-being of the chest and upper body, and sexual well-being. An official Japanese version of the BREAST-Q has been developed20 and validation of the reconstruction module has been completed (Cronbach’s α>0.7, inter-rater reliability (intracluster correlation coefficient, ICC)>0.8).

Data collection methods

Data for the following items will be collected: (1) age, gender, height, weight and medical history and (2) information related to breast cancer or breast reconstruction, including clinical stage; date of surgery; type of breast reconstruction (a. prosthesis (one submental, two subcutaneous), b. abdominal flap, c. vastus lateralis flap, d. other); mastectomy (nipple-sparing mastectomy, skin-sparing mastectomy, areolar-sparing (if yes: NAC reconstruction (nipple: graft, local flap or tattoo; areola: healthy nipple graft, inguinal thigh graft or tattoo); breast size (mastectomy volume (g), implant size (cc) (only for implant reconstruction)); axillary technique (a. sentinel lymph node biopsy, b. axillary lymph node dissection, c. unknown); radiotherapy (a. yes, b. no); chemotherapy (a. yes, b. no); endocrine therapy (a. yes, b. no); molecular targeted drugs (a. yes, b. no); surgical complications (postoperative haematoma, effusion, infection, wound necrosis, nipple-nipple blood flow failure); pathological results (tumour invasive and non-invasive diameter, histological evaluation of postoperative pathology specimen) and cancer biomarkers (ER, PgR, HER2, Ki67).

Harms

No serious harms are expected, given the observational nature of the study, but some patients may feel psychological distress when asked about their sexual well-being.21 The estimated time to complete the survey is about 10 min, and this may be a burden.

Participant timeline

The primary outcome measure of the study is the DCS, for which summary statistics will be calculated for each surgical method at the following time points: before the surgical decision (time 1), immediately after the surgical decision (time 2), 1 month postsurgery (time 3) and 1 year postsurgery (time 4). Data will be analysed for comparisons between groups and for changes over time. The secondary outcome measures include the SDM-Q-9 score, which will be used to assess the effectiveness of SDM immediately after the surgical decision (time 2) and the BREAST-Q to evaluate satisfaction and HRQOL after breast reconstruction at 1 month (time 3) and 1 year (time 4) postoperatively. These scores will also be compared between groups.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint, the DCS, using results from a previous study6 that provided strong evidence of the effectiveness of a DA in breast cancer care in Japan. In this study,6 use of the DCS showed a treatment effect at 1 month postsurgery with mean scores of 26.5 in the intervention group and 32.7 in the control group, with an SD of 12. Assuming a significance level (alpha) of 10% (two-sided), a power of 80%, and an ICC of 0.02, the required sample size was calculated to be 117.1 participants (assuming use of analysis of variance for group comparisons). Given that there are 10 participating research facilities, the required number of cases per cluster is 117.1 divided by 10; that is, 11.7. Anticipating a dropout rate of approximately 3%, we determined that a total of 120 participants (12 per facility) would be needed. Given the frequency of breast reconstruction cases at each participating facility, there were no issues with feasibility or practicality. Based on this rationale, we set the total number of cases at 120 (60 in the intervention group and 60 in the control group).

Recruitment

Recruitment, consent and registration: Each week, research teams at each facility will prescreen the consultation schedules of registered breast reconstruction surgeons to identify eligible patients. Members of the research team will obtain written informed consent from eligible patients who are interested in breast reconstruction and secure their agreement to participate in the study. Strategies for achieving adequate participant enrolment to reach target sample size are not taken.

Randomisation: sequence generation, allocation concealment mechanism and implementation

Allocation of participants

A total of 12 participating facilities will be randomised by an independent organisation, the Institute for Advancement of Clinical and Translational Science of Kyoto University Hospital. Six facilities will be assigned to the intervention group, and six facilities to the control group using computer-generated block random numbers.

Educational programmes for implementing SDM

All physicians in the intervention group will undergo training that is specifically developed for this study. The training has two primary objectives: to provide a foundational understanding of SDM and the DA, and to instruct on the practical application of SDM using the DA for patients with breast cancer considering breast reconstruction. At the start of the training, the physicians will be given a URL and password for a website where they can access training materials, including information on the breast reconstruction DA and instructional videos. The physicians will access the training website to view the following online learning modules: (1) an overview video of the training (3 min) and four lecture videos on the basic concepts of SDM and the DA (each about 6–14 min in length); (2) a video clip (5 min) illustrating an example of SDM using a DA; (3) communication with a patient regarding breast reconstruction options (for one case, using the Japanese version of the ‘Questionnaire on Shared Decision Making for Doctors (SDM-Q)’ to evaluate both successful and unsuccessful aspects of implementation)22 and (4) a knowledge test on SDM and the DA (10 questions, to be retaken until all answers are correct).

The video illustrating an example of SDM using a DA in module 2 above is an educational video on SDM for use of antibiotics for upper respiratory tract infections, developed by Laval University as part of Décision+ - Projet Terminé: décision partagée.23,26 Although this video does not cover SDM for breast reconstruction, it was selected because it provides a clear and concise example of SDM using a DA. With permission from the creators, the video was translated into Japanese and subtitles were added for use in this training.

Next, physicians in the intervention group will participate in real-time online sessions of about 90 min in small groups. These sessions will involve Q&A and discussions about the training and intervention implementation. Through these interactions, the physicians will have the opportunity to deepen their understanding of the fundamental concepts of SDM and use of a DA, and to gain insights into how to implement SDM effectively. The website containing the training materials will remain accessible throughout the study period.

Blinding

Due to cluster randomisation at the facility level and the nature of the intervention, blinding of the clinicians and patients is not feasible. To reduce the risk of bias, a co-researcher who is independent of this research team will carry out the data collection. During the inclusion process, the independent co-researcher will be blinded to the allocation of the patient.

Statistical methods

Scores for the primary endpoint (DCS) and secondary endpoints (SDM perception: SDM-Q-9, postoperative QOL or satisfaction: BREAST-Q) at each assessment time point (before and immediately after decision-making, and 1 month and 1 year postoperatively) will be summarised by centre and compared between the SDM and control groups by Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test according to the distribution of variables. For comparison of data from different patients, scores or changes at each time point will be compared between groups using an unpaired t-test. Subanalyses will also be performed to evaluate the relationship between the primary and secondary endpoints and patient and treatment backgrounds, including age, body mass index and radiotherapy, postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy and cancer stage. Logistic regression analysis will be used to identify risk factors for low scores (poor performance). For each result, the goodness of fit will be evaluated and the validity of the assumptions underlying the model will be checked. All analyses will be performed using JMP V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Data monitoring committee

A data monitoring committee or audit is not planned. After the study concludes, raw data will be stored in a physically secure location and will be appropriately deleted after 10 years.

Trial monitoring

There will be no data monitoring committee.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

All investigators will conduct this study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Ethical Guidelines proposed by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare. This study protocol and the informed consent form have been approved by the IRBs at all participating sites (approval number: C1608). The protocol version is 1.2, September 2023. The study has been registered with the UMIN Clinical Trial Registry (UMIN000052161).

Dissemination policy

All results will be analysed and reported after data anonymisation. The findings will be presented at conferences and published in peer-reviewed medical journals domestically and internationally.

Protocol amendments

There is no plan for communicating important protocol modifications (eg, changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes, analyses) to relevant parties.

Consent or assent

Before enrolment, patients will be given an informed consent form and the details of the study will be explained according to the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. All participants will be informed that they have the right to withdraw consent at any time and without any disadvantages.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality has been issued to protect the privacy of subjects by limiting the disclosure of identifiable, sensitive information. Only clinical data managers at the central data centre will have access to integrated raw data collected from each facility after the study. Site investigators will have access to case data at their sites. The data manager will transfer the final dataset to the principal investigator and the data will be stored in electronic format.

Ancillary and post-trial care

If any positive effects are confirmed in the intervention group after the study is completed, the same care will be applied to the control group.

Discussion

SDM is a process of identifying priorities for patients among options that involve trade-offs between conflicting values, especially regarding lifestyle and postoperative QOL. This is a new decision-making approach that aims to present choices in a way that reflects these considerations. The process is structured in a sequence that aligns with decision-making theories, while maintaining a neutral stance. Each option for breast reconstruction, like any medical choice, has its own advantages and disadvantages, and because patients’ values significantly influence their choices, SDM is particularly applicable. To implement SDM smoothly within the unique medical culture of Japan, a high-quality, Japan-specific DA for breast reconstruction is required. Therefore, in 2021, our breast reconstruction team created a high-quality prototype DA, the first in Japan, based on the IPDAS qualification and certification criteria and following the systematic method described by Coulter et al.27 In this study, we define the target users of the DA as patients who are interested in breast reconstruction, to establish its scope and purpose. It is evident that Japan is lagging in understanding and practice in this field and historically favors a paternalistic approach to decision-making.

Internationally, promotion of SDM and DA development was mentioned in the ‘Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010’ established in the USA, and there has been a gradual increase in interest in SDM. By 2014, the number of papers including SDM in the title surpassed those with ‘Informed Consent’, highlighting this growing interest, and several DAs specifically for breast reconstruction are now publicly available.28 A notable example is the (BREAST Choice) developed by Politi et al at Washington University.29 This support tool provides accurate information for clinical decisions and personalised risk information, and aids in eliciting patients’ preferences, values and priorities. Implementation of SDM using BREAST Choice for 120 patients desiring breast reconstruction resulted in a significant improvement in correct answers (84.6% vs 58.2%, p<0.001) on a test about breast reconstruction, demonstrating high knowledge acquisition. For example, it was noted that understanding of necessary information such as ‘reconstruction typically requires more than one surgery’, ‘delayed reconstruction reduces risk’ and ‘implants need to be replaced over time’ improved significantly, as did patients’ confidence in having chosen the correct reconstruction method (all significant with p<0.002). Additionally, the required consultation time did not differ significantly from the usual approach.

Several prospective randomised controlled trials have also evaluated SDM using a DA. Luan et al found that SDM significantly reduced patients’ preoperative decision conflict, which was corroborated by preoperative BREAST-Q scores.30 Platt and Zhong at the University Health Network in Toronto also found that preoperative patient education using a DA enhances the quality of SDM.31 32 Thus, a DA can help improve patient knowledge and provide individualised risk estimates, enhancing the quality of decision-making in breast reconstruction and reducing decision conflict and regret.

Despite these successes, further research is needed to evaluate the long-term impact of use of a DA on patient decision-making. As information about breast reconstruction spreads through mass media such as magazines and television, as well as blogs and social media, the ability of patients to gather and use accurate information has become crucial to their health. This implies that patients with low health literacy may struggle to obtain and understand necessary information, leading to incorrect decisions and less active participation in treatment decisions. Thus, SDM can be a powerful tool that may particularly help patients with low health literacy, low socioeconomic status or with limited information to receive treatments they understand, agree with and are satisfied with.33 SDM can also encourage disadvantaged patients to participate in decision-making and achieve satisfactory decisions through communication with healthcare providers, which may address health inequalities.

In clinical practice, SDM requires time and healthcare providers need more understanding of its necessity and applicability.34 Additionally, the perspectives of organisational leaders, medical policies and guidelines, incentives, the culture of healthcare systems and education of healthcare providers significantly influence the implementation of SDM.35 Addressing these organisational and system-level factors will be essential for integrating SDM into routine care.

Limitation and future perspectives

There are a few limitations to this study protocol. First, we plan to use only self-reported questionnaires to measure patient outcomes, which may be subject to bias. Second, due to the randomised design, participants will not be adequately blinded, potentially affecting responses to the questionnaires. Third, we will not evaluate clinicians’ support techniques related to medical decision-making. Fourth, it is possible that some clinicians in the control group will already have been exposed to information about SDM and will understand its content and importance. The DA used in the study is available on the internet, and so the risk of ‘intergroup contamination’ cannot be ruled out. Fifth, some clinicians in the intervention group might find the research tasks burdensome and difficult to integrate into their routine clinical practice. Sixth, we do not plan to assess patients’ levels of autonomy or their attitudes toward decision-making; some patients in the control group might already be accustomed to participating in medical decision-making. Seventh, the sample size will be relatively small due to financial constraints, which might affect the generalisability of the results. However, a strength of the study is the use of a clustered-randomised controlled trial design. Also, to our knowledge, this will be the first study to apply an SDM training programme for clinicians using a DA developed based on the IPDAS criteria, and the first to investigate this approach for enhancement of awareness of participation in the decision-making process for patients with breast cancer who are interested in breast reconstruction.

Due to the lack of sufficient evidence on the detailed options for breast reconstruction, future efforts should focus on developing a DA that includes detailed options for breast reconstruction, as well as a DA for other treatment options and screenings. Furthermore, strategies for implementing support using a DA within medical teams and training for healthcare providers remain critical issues. This study will be significant for future development of a high-quality DA in Japan.
