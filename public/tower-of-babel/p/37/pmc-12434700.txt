GLP‐1RA comparative effectiveness against dementia onset relative to other antidiabetic medications in a large, multi‐site cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Nerissa Nance, Paola Gilsanz, Andrew J. Karter, Holly Finertie, Julie A. Schmittdiel, JaeJin An, Alyce S. Adams, Caryn Oshiro, Andrea E. Cassidy‐Bushrow, Sarah Krahe‐Dombrowski, Maher Yassin, Sharon Lin, Keanu Izadian, Patrick J. O'Connor, Romain Neugebauer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONTo address gaps in current research, this study aims to compare the impact of exposure to glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) versus sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i), and sulfonylureas (SU) on reducing the risk of dementia, using a rigorous targeted learning causal inference approach.METHODSUsing clinical and claims data from four diverse US health‐care systems, we emulated three two‐arm trials contrasting sustained treatment with GLP‐1RA versus SGLT2i, DPP4i, and SU on dementia diagnosis. We included diabetes patients aged ≥ 60 years who initiated medication between 2014 and 2022. We estimated cumulative risk differences at 2.5 years.RESULTSIn Cohort 1, there was no evidence of differential dementia risk between sustained exposure to GLP1‐RA versus SGLT2i (adjusted risk difference [aRD] −0.001, 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.004, 0.001). In Cohorts 2 and 3, GLP‐1RA was associated with reduced risk of dementia diagnosis compared to DPP4i and SU, respectively (aRD −0.013, 95% CI −0.017, −0.009; aRD −0.016, 95% CI −0.018, −0.015).DISCUSSIONRigorous causal inference analysis suggests that sustained exposure to GLP‐1RA may modestly reduce risk of dementia, compared to DPP4i or SU exposure—but not compared to SGLT2i.Highlights We researched the comparative effects of diabetes medications on dementia.We studied a large, diverse observational cohort of patients with diabetes in the United States.Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) may modestly reduce risk of dementia compared to dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitor or sulfonylurea exposure.GLP‐1RAs do not show evidence of dementia risk reduction compared to sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors.Physicians may consider this when making prescription decisions with patients.

BACKGROUND

Diabetes is a key risk factor for vascular dementia and Alzheimer's disease. 1 A substantial and growing body of evidence suggests that some antidiabetic therapies may have a protective effect against cognitive decline in patients with diabetes. There is active research particularly regarding the neuroprotective effects of the drug class glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA). 2 , 3 , 4 Mechanisms of action include increasing insulin sensitivity, inducing weight reduction through increased feelings of satiety, as well as reducing neuroinflammation, among others. 5 , 6 A secondary analysis of randomized, placebo‐controlled trial data of patients with diabetes showed a significant reduction in cognitive decline among those in the GLP‐1RA treatment arm compared to a placebo control arm over 24 months. 7 Several observational studies have shown reduced dementia risk in GLP‐1RA users, either compared to placebo or for every unit increase in GLP‐1RA dosage. 8 , 9 , 10 These studies notably did not assess comparative effectiveness relative to other medications. Two target trial emulation 11 studies comparing GLP‐1RA use to active comparators (sulfonylureas [SU], dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors [DPP4i], metformin), adjusting for only baseline confounding, found similar protective effects of GLP‐1RAs. 12 , 13

Other diabetes medications, such as sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), have also shown some protection in observational studies against cognitive decline in patients with diabetes, compared to no SGLT2i use 9 , 14 and compared to DPP4i use. 15 Randomized trials show mixed results. 16 Likewise, DPP4i have been shown to slow some measures of cognitive decline in trial data 17 in observational studies compared to no treatment 18 or to SU. 19 , 20 , 21 Consistent with these findings, in other observational analyses, SU have null effects or comparatively harmful effects on cognitive outcomes compared to other medications (DPP4i, metformin, insulin). 22 , 23 Notably, these studies did not use causal inference methodology to adjust for time‐varying confounding affected by prior treatment.

There are, to date, no head‐to‐head randomized trials assessing the comparative effectiveness of different diabetes treatments on dementia onset. While evidence is urgently needed, randomized trials with rare outcomes requiring longer term follow‐up would imply recruitment of an unrealistically large number of subjects, require tens of millions of dollars, and take ≥ 10 years to complete—by which time results may be irrelevant as diabetes treatment guidelines evolve. Due to these challenges, randomized trials have either enrolled dementia patients and studied disease progression, 24 , 25 or studied markers of cognitive decline rather than dementia onset. 7 , 26 These studies therefore do not easily generalize to the broader diabetes population, or to dementia onset or diagnosis.

Observational studies that leverage electronic medical records or claims may circumvent these shortcomings by allowing access to expansive datasets with sufficient power to look at less‐common exposures (e.g., newer medications) and longer term and rare outcomes (e.g., dementia). Such studies can suffer from confounding bias and can be particularly challenging in longitudinal settings, in which time‐dependent confounders (e.g., mild cognitive impairment) can be affected by prior exposure and thus be on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome. 27 In such cases, standard analytic methods such as matching or outcome regressions are inadequate to adjust for biases from these time‐varying confounders. 28 Modern causal inference methods based on the G‐computation formula address these shortcomings by allowing researchers to adjust estimates for time‐dependent confounders affected by prior exposure. 28 To our knowledge, only one analysis has applied such an estimator to this question; however, that analysis suffered some data limitations, including a limited covariate set and a homogeneous study population. 29

This study aims to address these clinically relevant gaps in current knowledge by examining the comparative causal effect of sustained exposure to GLP‐1RA versus comparator medications on dementia onset in a large, diverse, multi‐site cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes. We contrast the effect of exposure to GLP‐1RA to other established therapies (SGLT2i, DPP4i, and SU) using causal inference methodology to properly adjust for time‐dependent confounding.

METHODS

Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study aims to emulate three conceptual target trials. 11 Each target trial was emulated by first constructing a separate cohort (hereafter Cohorts 1, 2, and 3) using the same eligibility criteria as the ideal trial of interest; we defined a patient's index date(s) as the patient's cohort entry date(s) when all eligibility criteria were met. Longitudinal data on each cohort were then used to compare sustained exposure to GLP‐1RA versus one of three comparator medications: SGLT2i (Cohort 1), DPP4i (Cohort 2), and SU (Cohort 3).

The study sites included: Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC), Kaiser Permanente Hawaii (KPHI), Geisinger, HealthPartners Institute, and Henry Ford Health. Electronic health records and administrative claims data were extracted at all sites, including information on enrollment, demographics, diagnoses, procedures, use, vital signs, lab results, and prescription fills.

Cohort eligibility

We constructed three cohorts, one for each of the drug comparisons of interest. Patients were potentially eligible for inclusion in each cohort if they met the following criteria: a pharmacy dispensing record of the medication of interest (GLP‐1RA, SGLT2i, DPP4i, or SU) between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2022; continuous health and pharmacy plan coverage in 2 years prior to index (washout period); no prior dispensing of the medications of interest in the washout period; evidence of type 2 diabetes (as defined in a three‐step algorithm that relies on diagnosis code and medication fills; see Appendix A in supporting information); at least one A1c test in the washout period; no evidence of shortened life expectancy in 2 years prior to or on index date (i.e., metastatic cancer, hospice or palliative care); no evidence of cystic fibrosis prior to index; no evidence of dementia diagnosis in 2 years prior or on index; no evidence of current pregnancy; at least 60 years of age; and no contraindications to the two drug classes compared, as defined below.

Cohort 1 (GLP‐1RA vs. SGLT2i) contraindications included patients with prior evidence (looking back through 2009) of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 or pancreatitis prior to index date (GLP‐1RA contraindications), or patients with pyelonephritis or necrotizing fasciitis or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73m2 (SGLT2i contraindications) in 2 years prior to or on index.

Cohort 2 (GLP‐1RA vs. DPP4i) contraindications included patients with prior evidence (looking back through 2009) of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (GLP‐1RA contraindication) or pancreatitis prior to the index date (GLP‐1RA & DPP4i contraindication).

Cohort 3 (GLP‐1RA vs. SU) contraindications included patients with prior evidence of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 or pancreatitis prior (looking back through 2009) to index date (GLP‐1RA contraindication), or alanine transaminase ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal (56 × 3 = 168) in the prior 2 years or on index (SU contraindication).

Some patients had multiple index dates and thus contributed repeated longitudinal observations to the analyses. 30 For a detailed description of cohort eligibility criteria, see Appendix A.

Follow‐up

Longitudinal data were collected starting at each patient's index date and continuing until the earliest of (1) administrative end of study (December 31, 2022), (2) disenrollment from the health plan or pharmacy coverage, (3) death, or (4) dementia diagnosis (outcome). The LtAtStructuR R package 31 was used to structure these longitudinal data into analytic datasets in which measurements on exposure, outcome, censoring, and covariates were updated every 30 days during follow‐up. The temporal ordering of events was preserved such that, for example, only past covariate information was used to predict exposure status at each 30‐day interval in the analyses.

Exposure

We used pharmacy dispensing data to determine exposure to the diabetes medications of interest for each cohort. At the index date, members were considered exposed to the medication of the drug dispensing that qualified them for cohort entry. During follow‐up, a patient was deemed exposed to a specific drug class starting on the date of a drug dispensing event and for the duration of the prescription coverage period, which was defined as twice the days of supply (to allow for some non‐adherence). We bridged gaps between exposure intervals ≤ 90 days (i.e., we assumed exposure was uninterrupted during these gaps) and ignored stockpiling (i.e., a new drug dispensing event that occurred before the end of the coverage period of the prior prescription did not extend the coverage period of the new prescription).

Outcome

We defined the outcome as the time from the index date to the first dementia diagnosis. Dementia diagnosis was defined using any inpatient, outpatient, or emergency department code of: Alzheimer's disease (International Classification of Diseases Ninth Revision [ICD‐9]: 331.0; ICD‐10: G30.x), vascular dementia (ICD‐9: 290.4x; ICD‐10: F01.5x), or non‐specific dementia (ICD‐9: 290.0, 290.1x, 290.2x, 290.3, 294.2x, 294.8; ICD‐10: F03.9x). If a dementia diagnosis was from the emergency department, we required two diagnosis codes due to the risk of misclassification of delirium as dementia (event defined at the first of the two diagnoses). This definition has been used previously in the Kaiser population 32 , 33 and validated to be highly sensitive and specific in a similar population. 34

Covariates

We considered a comprehensive list of the > 350 candidate covariates in the analyses, as listed in Appendix B in supporting information. The covariates capture time‐independent and time‐varying information on demographic characteristics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and vital signs. Exposures to antidiabetic medications that were not part of the pairwise comparison of interest were considered candidate covariates.

Analysis

We estimated marginal counterfactual cumulative risks of dementia diagnosis through 2.5 years of follow‐up under sustained exposure to GLP‐1RA and no censoring versus sustained exposure to a comparator medication (SGLT2i, DPP4i, or SU) and no censoring. Effect measures were risk differences (RD) at 2.5 years and average RD (ARD; equivalent to area under the curve [AUC]) through 2.5 years. The ARD corresponds to the difference between the areas under the two survival curves associated with each of the two arms of the emulated trial. For further details, see Appendix C in supporting information. We used both an unadjusted estimator of the effects (i.e., discrete‐time Kaplan–Meier curves, assuming no confounding), as well as an adjusted estimator of effects (i.e., accounting for baseline and time‐varying confounding). To adequately adjust for time‐independent and time‐varying confounding and sources of attrition bias (i.e., right censoring), we used targeted learning, which combines targeted minimum loss‐based estimation (TMLE) and super learning (SL). Specifically, TMLE is an efficient doubly robust estimation approach 35 that can provide more precise effect estimates than the commonly used inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator for trial emulation. 11 SL is a machine learning algorithm that is used to avoid arbitrary modeling assumptions when estimating the propensity scores and outcome regressions on which correct TMLE inference relies. 36 , 37 For a detailed description of the SL approach used, see Appendix C. We note that separate propensity scores were derived for treatment initiation, continuation, and the occurrence of each right‐censoring event type. IPW estimation 38 was also implemented as a secondary analytic approach. Following the per‐protocol principle of analysis, 11 person‐time observations collected after discontinuation of the treatment initiated on the index date did not contribute to the IPW and TMLE estimates of the survival curves (i.e., inverse probability [IP] weights were set to 0 for these observations). Inferences were derived analytically based on the estimators’ influence curve and the delta method. 38 This approach accounts for the dependency between repeated longitudinal observations from the same patients (i.e., observations resulting from patients with multiple index dates).

Missing data were handled with the missingness indicator approach using the mean or mode values for unobserved baseline values. 39 To examine the sensitivity of results to extreme IP weights, we implemented analyses with truncated weights. Two weight truncation methods were used: truncating weights > 99th percentile, and truncating weights above the absolute value of 200 (TMLE) or 20 (IPW). We conducted sensitivity analyses 40 , 41 that aim to assess how unobserved confounders with effects on exposure and outcome that are similar to a major observed confounder used in the analyses—mild cognitive impairment—would influence inferences from our analyses had we been able to account for such unobserved confounders if they exist (for further detail, see Appendix D in supporting information). Last, we ran sensitivity analyses examining the vascular dementia endpoint, and an analysis excluding all dementia cases that developed in the first year to address concerns over undocumented dementia at baseline. All analyses were implemented using R version 4.1.2 and the stremr R package. 42

Human subjects

The institutional review board of Kaiser Permanente Northern California Division of Research approved this study.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics and end of follow‐up information

In Cohort 1 (GLP‐1RA vs. SGLT2i), a total of 62,156 patient‐fills (61,187 unique individuals) met eligibility requirements (Figure 1). Among them, 14,085 (23%) initiated GLP‐1RA, and 48,071 (77%) initiated SGLT2i (Table 1). GLP‐1RA initiators were more likely to be of White race and less likely to have a cardiovascular disease diagnosis at baseline than SGLT2i initiators. Among the SGLT2i group, < 1% had a dementia diagnosis at the end of follow‐up, compared to 1.5% in the GLP‐1 RA group (Figure 1). End of follow‐up by death was higher in the GLP‐1 RA group (3.8% vs. 3.0% in the SGLT2i group).

Cohort 2 (GLP‐1RA vs. DPP4i) included 34,972 patient‐fills (33,917 individuals) meeting inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Of those meeting criteria, 16,373 (47%) initiated a GLP‐1RA and 18,599 (53%) initiated a DPP4i (Table 1). Those initiating GLP‐1RA were more likely to be White, to initiate treatment in a later index year, and to live in a census tract with slightly less poverty compared to those initiating DPP4i. Those initiating GLP‐1RA were less likely to receive a dementia diagnosis, die, or disenroll from the health plan by the end of follow‐up (Figure 1).

In Cohort 3 (GLP‐1RA vs. SU), 117,971 patient‐fills (113,027 individuals) were eligible for inclusion (Figure 1). Of these, 9095 (8%) initiated GLP‐1RA, and 108,876 (92%) initiated SU (Table 1). Those initiating GLP‐1RA were more likely to initiate in later years, to be of White race and of non‐Hispanic ethnicity, to live in a census tract with slightly less poverty, and to have an end‐stage renal disease diagnosis or a hypertension diagnosis at baseline. They were also less likely to die, receive a dementia diagnosis, or disenroll from the health plan by the end of follow‐up (Figure 1).

Extensive tables of all candidate covariate values for each cohort can be found in Appendix E in supporting information. Of note, few dementia events occurred in patients with a baseline age of 60 to 64 years (Appendix F in supporting information).

Survival curves

Figure 2 displays survival curve estimates for each of the three study cohorts. Tables describing the number of patient fills following the regimes of interest over time for each of the three comparisons of interest can be found in Appendix G in supporting information. After adjusting for covariates, in Cohort 1 (GLP‐1RA vs. SGLT2i), there were no statistically significant differences in AUC (i.e., difference in the area under the survival curves) between the two exposure groups (Figure 2; AUC P value 0.43). In Cohorts 2 (GLP‐1RA vs. DPP4i) and 3 (GLP‐1RA vs. SU), we found a significantly lower average risk of dementia over time in the GLP‐1RA group relative to the comparator group (Figure 2; AUC P values < 0.01). Risk differences

Comparing GLP‐1RA versus SGLT2i in Cohort 1, we found no statistical evidence of a difference in the cumulative 2.5‐year risk of dementia onset between groups (crude risk difference [cRD]: −0.002; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.006, 0.003; adjusted risk difference [aRD]: −0.001; 95% CI: −0.004, 0.001; Table 2). We found a statistically significant protective effect of GLP‐1RA compared to DPP4i in Cohort 2, both crudely and after adjustment (cRD: −0.012; cRD 95% CI: −0.017, −0.008, number needed to treat [NNT]: 82; aRD: –0.013; aRD 95% CI: −0.017, −0.009, NNT: 78; Table 2). We also found a statistically significant protective effect of GLP‐1RA compared to SU in Cohort 3 in crude and adjusted estimates (cRD: –0.011; cRD 95% CI: –0.016, –0.006, NNT: 89; aRD: −0.016; aRD 95% CI: −0.018, −0.015, NNT: 62; Table 2). All results were robust to truncation level (Appendix F). The IPW estimator yielded similar results (Appendix H).

Sensitivity analyses estimating bias from potential unmeasured confounders showed that unobserved confounding with the same direction and strength of effects on exposure and outcome as mild cognitive impairment would not change the direction or significance of effect in any of the analyses presented, had these analyses been able to adjust for such unmeasured confounding (Appendix D). Specifically, comparing GLP1‐RA to SGLT2i, we found that after correcting for the estimated unmeasured confounding effect of 0.00003 (using mild cognitive impairment as a proxy), the updated causal risk difference was −0.00143 (95% CI: −0.00363, 0.00087). Comparing GLP1‐RA to DPP4, after correcting for the estimated unmeasured confounding effect of –0.0007, the updated causal effect was –0.0121 (95% CI: −0.0161, −0.0082). Comparing GLP1‐RA to SU, after correcting for the estimated unmeasured confounding effect of −0.0003, the updated causal effect was –0.0159 (95% CI: −0.0172, −0.0145). Vascular dementia subtype results were consistent with the main analysis, except for the DPP4i cohort (Appendix K). Results were robust to excluding year 1 dementia cases (Appendix L).

DISCUSSION

Our analyses of three diverse cohorts of adults with type 2 diabetes suggest a modest protective effect of sustained GLP‐1RA use compared to sustained DPP4i or sustained SU use on dementia onset during the 2.5 year follow‐up period. We observed no significant difference in dementia onset in those with sustained use of GLP‐1RA versus SGLT2i at 2.5 years in unadjusted and adjusted analyses.

Overall, our results are consistent with prior observational analyses comparing GLP‐1RA to DPP4i or SU in diabetic populations 9 , 29 and reinforce previous findings that GLP‐1RA may be protective against dementia, 10 , 24 but not to a greater extent than SGLT2i. 43 Our results uniquely compare these medications head to head and robustly estimate effects using modern causal inference methods. Unlike prior studies, we provide absolute effect measures (i.e., risk differences) as opposed to commonly reported relative effect measures (e.g., hazard ratios) that rely on arbitrary parametric modeling assumptions (e.g., proportionality assumption). The validity of the adjusted inferences presented relies on the correct estimation of the propensity scores. To avoid arbitrary modeling assumptions, we used SL 36 , 37 for flexible adjustment for confounding and sources of attrition bias.

We interpreted risk differences as the NNT, an effect measure that may be more easily understood by many patients, clinicians, and policy makers. We found in adjusted analyses that 78 patients would need to initiate and be continuously treated with GLP1‐RA versus DPP4i to avert one case of dementia within 2.5 years; similarly, 62 patients would need to be treated continuously with GLP1‐RA versus SU to avert one case of dementia.

Limitations of our analyses include: the non‐randomized study design, limited follow‐up time of 2.5 years, and possible imprecision in the ascertainment of cognitive status based on diagnosis codes. Notably, we would not be able to examine longer‐term follow‐up in the data due to poor data support past 2.5 years (see Appendix G). We also note that subtype endpoint results should be interpreted with caution (Appendix K), as the sensitivity/specificity of dementia subgroup diagnoses has not been verified. The analysis relies on two key assumptions: positivity (i.e., no baseline or time‐varying covariate deterministically predicts exposure over time) and no unmeasured confounding. 44 The distributions of propensity scores (Appendix G) do not raise concern over practical violation of the positivity assumption. Although we included a robust set of possible covariate values (> 350 candidate covariates), it is possible that there is still some unmeasured (and therefore, uncontrolled) confounding in this analysis. This is critical because of the observed variation in characteristics of those using different medications. Some potential residual confounders might include, for example, behavioral factors like diet or exercise, or individual socioeconomic factors not captured by the census tract variables. Results from this analysis must be interpreted relative to the plausibility of these assumptions. To evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounders, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounders that could have similar confounding effects as observed mild cognitive impairment in our analyses (Appendix D). These sensitivity analyses suggested that results would remain unchanged in the presence of unobserved confounding with the same direction and strength of effects on outcome and exposures as mild cognitive impairment.

Study limitations should be considered in the context of significant study strengths. Unlike most prior observational studies on this topic, this study implements rigorous longitudinal causal inference methodology (including a robust estimator) in a large and diverse real‐world cohort with a comprehensive covariate adjustment set—making our findings more generalizable. Additionally, the use of machine learning for propensity score estimation allows for flexible confounding adjustment. Finally, compared to many health plans across the United States, we had relatively high retention in the study over the 2.5 years of follow‐up; on average, only ≈ 15% of censoring was due to disenrollment from the health plan, which may alleviate bias concerns from informative attrition. We also address censoring over time explicitly in our estimand and estimation strategy (Appendix C).

This study adds to the growing body of evidence about the cognitive benefits of certain diabetes medications compared head‐to‐head. Guidelines for prevention and management of cognitive decline exist, particularly for high‐risk populations, but our understanding of the comparative impact of different diabetes drugs on cognitive decline is incomplete. Although there is a substantial and expanding body of evidence on the neuroprotective effects of diabetic medications, there are no randomized trials studying the head‐to‐head impact of different diabetes medications on dementia onset. Few observational studies have used a rigorous target trial approach, and those that did have been limited by comparison group, covariate set, or population. 10 , 29 Thus, the current study adds to the research in the field of comparative effectiveness of diabetes treatment and underscores the need for further randomized and observational analyses of the impact of these widely used medications on cognitive decline.
