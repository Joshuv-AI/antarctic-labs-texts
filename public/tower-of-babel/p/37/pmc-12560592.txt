Convenience sampling of primary school students in Tianjin to evaluate the effectiveness of mobile-based virtual reality experimental learning in oral health education
Authors: Shuai Nie, Dasen Yuan, Cuicui Li, Yanmei Dai, Yingming Yang, Tao Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health education provides schoolchildren with correct information about health and oral health care techniques. Traditional oral health education has poor knowledge transmission effect. Virtual reality (VR) currently has extensive applications and excellent results in medical education and technical learning. However, its application in oral health education for primary school students has not yet been known.MethodsA convenience sampling approach was employed to recruit 1,416 children in grades 3–5 from 16 districts of Tianjin City who had previously received traditional oral health education. One week prior to the intervention, parents received a quick response code to access the VR experience and were instructed to guide their children through the smartphone-based learning module. Then, medical staff administered questionnaires to the students in the classroom. VR efficacy was assessed by comparing oral health knowledge, attitudes, and technology acceptance between students exposed to VR and controls. Data were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis H-tests and Mann-Whitney U-tests, and multiple linear regression analysis was employed to elucidate the pivotal factors influencing the efficacy of the oral VR experience.ResultChildren in grades 3–5 in Tianjin exhibited high receptivity to mobile-based VR technology, with a perceived ease of use of 9.03 ± 1.36 and a perceived usefulness of 9.40 ± 0.98. Among the students who experienced oral VR, 398 individuals (75%) reported a significant improvement in their attitudes towards oral health care, while 120 (22.6%) perceived a more substantial improvement. Students who experienced oral VR demonstrates a significant improvement in oral health knowledge (experimental group: 13.00(12.00,13.00) vs. control group: 10.00(8.00,12.00), p < 0.001). Schoolchildren in grade 4, with high father educational level, good brushing habits, and prior VR experience, achieved higher oral knowledge scores after the VR experience, with statistically significant differences observed.ConclusionWe present the first application of mobile virtual reality for oral health education in primary schools. Our study demonstrates high student acceptance of VR technology in Tianjin, China, alongside significant improvements in oral health knowledge and attitudes. Given its efficacy, accessibility, and visualization capabilities, mobile VR represents a promising tool for scalable oral health education.Trial registrationInternational Standard Registered Clinical/Social Study Number Registry ISRCTN13464089(17/07/2025) “Retrospectively registered”.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07078-9.

Introduction

The Fourth National Oral Health Survey in China revealed that the prevalence of dental caries among 12-year-old children was 35.55%, with only 14.77% of children having received fissure sealants. These findings highlight a significant challenge for the oral health of school-age children [1]. Good oral health is an important component of maintaining and improving overall health and quality of life [2]. Children with poor oral health will experience pain and tooth loss, which can interfere with normal eating and have a negative impact on their nutrition, self-esteem, speech, socialization, quality of life, and school attendance [3, 4]. Various methods for preventing dental diseases exist, such as daily care, dietary modifications, and regular oral examinations, with the efficacy of oral health education has been robustly substantiated [5]. Schoolchildren are in the process of developing oral hygiene habits and behaviors. Through oral health education, they can be provided with accurate information about health and oral care techniques, enabling them to adopt effective behaviors to protect their oral health [6, 7]. Because habits and behaviors formed in the school environment tend to be more durable and sustainable, schools represent an important setting for early oral health education interventions among school-aged children [8, 9]. However, collective action is often taken in the traditional oral health education model, including the use of different types of resources in the form of lectures, pamphlets, showing videos, etc. Students passively receive knowledge, lack of interaction, and the results are usually poor and resource-consuming [6]. How to go beyond traditional methods to enhance the effectiveness of children’s oral health education represents an important challenge currently faced by dental practitioners.

Some studies have found that learning through virtual reality (VR) courses is more effective than through traditional courses in knowledge interest and concentration enhancement [10–12]. Mobile VR is a form of non-immersive VR that interacts with simulations displayed on screens such as computers and mobile phones, using input devices like a mouse, keyboard, or touchscreen [13]. Previous research has shown that VR can improve scores and experiences on learning tests [14–16]. A recent study indicates that VR has potential in online learning [17]. VR currently has extensive applications and excellent results in medical education and technical learning. However, the high cost of traditional VR equipment (e.g. headsets) has limited its application and universality [18]. The enhanced processing and screen display capabilities of modern mobile devices have created a new mobile environment for VR applications. The portability and universality of mobile VR provide more application possibilities [19].

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is used to explain the behavioral intentions of potential users to utilize a technological innovation, which involving two main predictors including perceived ease of use (EU) and perceived usefulness (U). The level of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness usually implies the likelihood that the experiencer will use the technology [20, 21]. The Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) survey serves as a valuable diagnostic tool for understanding a group’s comprehension, attitude, and behaviors on specific topics, particularly within health literacy. This type of survey is typically conducted using interviews or self-administered questionnaires and involves collecting and analysing non-numerical data [22].In the context of oral health, greater knowledge promotes awareness of its importance and ultimately leads to improved oral health [23–25]. Therefore, we designed a questionnaire based on the Technology Acceptance Model and the KAP model to assess students’ acceptance of the technology, as well as their knowledge of oral health and attitudes towards oral health care. Additionally, research has shown that parents’ oral health-related behaviors influence their children’s oral health status. Therefore, we asked parents to bring their children to experience oral VR on their cell phones, with the expectation of drawing parents’ attention to their children’s oral health [26]. Despite the rapid universality of mobile VR technology in education, its application in oral health education for primary school children has not yet been explored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on the application of mobile VR in primary school oral education.

Methods

VR design content

Our oral VR is developed based on mobile virtual reality technology. Based on over ten years of practical experience in oral health education for primary school students, the content strictly followed the Guidelines for Oral Health Behavior of Chinese Residents (2009 edition), the 1 st edition of Experiment Tutorial of Preventive Dentistry and the 7th edition of Preventive Dentistry [27–29]. At the same time, the material was adapted to the cognitive level of primary school students. In addition, several preventive dentistry experts, in conjunction with specialists from pediatric dentistry and emergency departments, conducted a review of the content design. Subsequently, external expert review was employed to further refine the content.Then, based on the pilot survey, the content was finally adjusted. This VR content includes four parts, namely exploring teeth, understanding dental caries, fun question and answers, tooth decay great battle game (The presentation of VR content can be found in the appendix 1).

The Exploring Teeth section shows and explains the structure of teeth. The understanding dental caries section introduces the most caries-prone permanent teeth, the development of endodontics, the introduction of oral hygiene measures and simulation exercises, and the operation process of pit and fissure sealing. The fun question section includes five questions and analysis of the answers (Do decayed teeth always have painful sensations? How many times a day do you brush your teeth, What’s the most caries-prone permanent teeth. Which toothpaste prevents caries, and methods of preventing caries besides brushing). And the tooth decay great battle game section allows students to distinguish between foods which are good or bad for dental health by interactive game. The exposure duration for these four activities—exploring teeth, understanding dental caries, fun quiz, and tooth decay battle game—is 2, 2, 1, and 1 min respectively, totaling 6 min.

Participant

The subjects are students in grades 3–5 and their parents in Tianjin, who participated in the Comprehensive Intervention Program for Children’s Oral Diseases. The inclusion criteria for students were with no learning disabilities and informed consent from their parents or legal guardians. A total of 1,472 students from 24 primary schools in 16 districts of Tianjin completed the questionnaire. A quick response code for the VR experience was sent to the parents by a doctor from the department of preventive dentistry, Tianjin stomatological hospital, via the teachers in school. The parents were asked to bring their children to engage in the learning process together on their smartphones. One week later, medical staff from the department of preventive dentistry, Tianjin stomatological hospital conducted a centralized questionnaire filling session in the classroom and collected the forms on-site. The students who experienced the dental VR technology were assigned to the experimental group, while those who did not were classified as the control group, through convenient sampling.

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin Hospital of Stomatology (PH2024-Q-017).

Data collection

Data were collected using a Student Questionnaire(see Appendix 2 for the full instrument), which was obtained by adding VR-related questions to the survey questionnaire that we had been using for over ten years. The content of the questionnaire was reviewed by several specialists, and a pre-survey was conducted at West China Hospital of Stomatology and Tianjin stomatological hospital [30]. An internal pilot survey was conducted, the initial stage of the entire investigation was regarded as a pilot study, and the results of this stage were also incorporated into the final statistical data [31]. The correspondence between the questionnaire analysis structure and the questionnaire questions can be found in the appendix 3.The purpose of this questionnaire was to assess the effectiveness of mobile VR technology in oral health education for primary school students. A total of 1472 students in grades 3 to 5 participated in the survey, with 586 students in the experimental group and 886 students in the control group. The paradoxical performance of extremely high subjective scores but very poor objective performance may reflect low-quality questionnaire completion, which could have reduced the ability to detect the effects of the educational intervention [32].Therefore, 56 students in the study were excluded due to the significant difference between the VR experience evaluation and oral knowledge scores. These questionnaires were regarded as invalid.

The questionnaire comprised scales, single-choice, and multiple-choice questions covering demographics, acceptance of mobile oral VR, and oral health literacy. The psychometric properties of the TAM scale are shown in Table 1. Data entry was performed in a double-entry format by two doctors from the department of preventive dentistry, Tianjin stomatological hospital. The results show that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.777, indicating good internal consistency of the questionnaire. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin(KMO) value is 0.764, confirming that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The results of chi-squared fit statistic (χ2/df), root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA), GOF index (GFI), adjusted goodness of fit index(AGFI) and comparative fit index (CFI) are all be close to or better than the recommended levels, which verify the content validity of this questionnaire is acceptable [33].

Based on the TAM theory, we assessed students’ acceptance of oral VR and changes in their attitudes toward oral health care through scale questions 3–7 [34]. These questions were rated on a five-point Likert scale, with responses coded as follows: 5 (totally agree), 4 (agree), 3 (neutral), 2 (disagree), and 1 (totally disagree).

Scale questions 3, “I do not think there are problems or difficulties in using this VR interactive program,” and 4, “I think the oral science content in this VR interactive program is well understood,” are categorized as perceived ease of use, with scores ranging from 0 to 10; question 5 “I think that using this VR interactive program has improved my oral health knowledge” and Question 6 “I think that the interactive format of the VR has increased my interest in learning about oral health” were categorized as perceived usefulness, with scores ranging from 0 to 10; Question 8 “I think the presentation of fossa sealing in this VR made me more aware of its treatment process and protective effects” scored in the range of 0–5; Scale question 1 “Have you ever been exposed to virtual simulation (VR) interaction before?” Summarized as “VR experience.” Scale question 4 “Have you had previous knowledge about oral health?” Summarized as “prior knowledge” answers were coded as “2, yes, 1, no.”

Single-choice questions 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 14 and multiple-choice questions 2 and 3 were closely related to the content of the oral VR experience, and the sum of their scores was categorized as the oral knowledge score. Single-choice questions were scored 1 point for a correct answer and 0 points for an incorrect answer, multiple-choice questions were scored 1 point for choosing the correct option and 0 points for choosing the incorrect option, and the oral knowledge score ranged from 0 to 14 points. Multiple choice question 6 “How many times a day do you brush your teeth?” and Question 8 “What method do you use to brush your teeth” were categorized as brushing habits. Answers of “not every day”, “1 time”, “2 times” and “more than 2 times” were scored as 0, 1, 2 and 2 points respectively. Single-choice questions8 were scored 1 point for correct answers and 0 points for incorrect. Scores for tooth brushing habits range from 0 to 3, where 3 is excellent, 2 is good, and 1 and 0 are poor. Higher scores on the above questions indicate a more positive stance in the respective areas. The subjective experience score for VR is calculated from the sum of the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use scores, which range from 0 to 20.

Data analysis

In this study, all students in the convenience sample were analyzed as a whole and differences in oral knowledge scores between the experimental and control groups were compared by Mann-Whitney U-test. Kruskal-Wallis H-test and Mann-Whitney U-test were used in the experimental group to compare the differences in oral knowledge scores in terms of gender, grade, parental education, brushing habits and VR experience. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the factors influencing the effectiveness of oral VR adoption, with the dependent variable being the oral knowledge score and the explanatory variables being those with significant differences in the analysis of difference. Which further verified that the model assumptions were met. All statistical analyses were completed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and the significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate the effectiveness of mobile virtual reality (VR) technology in oral health education for school-age children. The effectiveness of the application was assessed in terms of students’ oral health knowledge, attitudes towards oral health care, and acceptance of the technology. Our results showed that mobile oral VR significantly improves oral health knowledge and oral health care attitudes among school-age children. This is consistent with the effects of VR technology in medical education [35]. The results of the TAM assessment indicate that school-age children have a higher acceptance of mobile virtual reality technology, which also represents their higher willingness and behavior to use it [21, 34, 36].

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are two key factors in the TAM. Studies based on this model have found that users are more likely to adopt a technology when they perceive it to be useful. Additionally, perceived ease of use influences users’ attitudes and intentions toward using the technology [20]. In our study, school-age children perceived that the technology enhanced their knowledge and interest in learning, suggesting that they viewed the technology as valuable and meeting their oral health needs. Additionally, during use, school-age children found the experience to be free of problems and difficulties and the content easy to understand. The high perceived usefulness and lack of perceived difficulty indicate a high acceptance of the technology by school-age children, providing key support for its application.

Research indicates that parents with higher education levels are more likely to pay attention to their children’s oral health, and their children tend to develop better oral hygiene habits [37].This is consistent with the finding in the present study that students with more educated fathers scored higher on oral health knowledge. It is worth noting that, in this study, differences in mothers’ education level did not significantly affect experimental outcomes. This may be related to the fact that oral VR is a novel digital technology. Father tends to solve problems using technological solutions and engage more with technology-related content in the home. A study on digital literacy among school-age children showed that the positive effect of fathers’ education level on children’s digital literacy was stronger than that of mothers’ [38, 39]. Based on this, we encourage a home-school approach to involve families, especially fathers, in oral VR health education, which can help improve children’s oral health [40]. Regarding grade, fourth-grade students’ cognitive abilities are well-suited to VR content, whereas fifth-grade students may experience diminished interest due to increased academic pressure. Regarding brushing habit, students with good brushing habits exhibited higher oral knowledge scores. This group typically demonstrated greater daily awareness of their oral health and a stronger sense of oral health care [41]. The synergistic effect of good brushing habits and oral VR makes them have better application results. Therefore, oral VR may be more effective in school children with good oral hygiene habits. Students with VR experience tend to have higher oral knowledge scores after the experience, which is consistent with the effect of “technology proficiency” on users in the TAM [42].

Mobile VR technology offers new possibilities for its universality in oral health education, given its portability, low cost, low threshold and wide audience. Mobile devices such as smartphones and tablets are more portable compared to Personal Computer-based VR(PC-based VR) devices, making them flexible for use in schools, homes, and other scenarios. The use of smartphones allows for easy access to knowledge, enhancing convenience and autonomy for students [18, 43]. In recent years, the significant reduction in the cost of equipment and technology has fueled the rapid growth of VR applications in medicine, science and other educational fields, and has shown positive implications [44, 45]. The low threshold of oral VR operation also creates conditions for the universality of the technology. In this study, the high perceived ease of use scores indicate that students perceived oral VR as being low in difficulty to use and not requiring additional technical guidance [46]. Prior to participating in this study, more than 90% of the students had prior VR experience, and “VR experience” had a significant positive predictive effect on the oral knowledge score. This further validates the potential of mobile VR in terms of its universality. It is worth mentioning that the relationship between prior knowledge and dental knowledge scores was not significant. This indicates that the effect of students’ experience of dental VR was not affected or weakly affected by their prior knowledge. It also reflects that the technology is suitable for students with different knowledge backgrounds and is suitable for universal education. Moreover, mobile VR does not require complex hardware support, which dramatically reduces the threshold and cost of use. With the help of low-cost equipment, it can quickly reach a wider student population which can promotes educational equity [47].

Since 2012, the Tianjin region has been implementing a major public health program for pit and fissure sealing. Pit and fissure sealing is an effective method for preventing dental caries in children and adolescents. Enabling students and parents to recognize and understand pit and fissure sealing is crucial for their ultimate decision to undergo the procedure [48, 49]. Oral VR enhances school-age children’s understanding of pit and fissure sealants by visualizing their therapeutic and protective effects., and the application of this technology may be beneficial to the universality of pit and fissure sealing. Overall, mobile VR, with its features such as universality, visualization and interactivity, has a desirable application in oral health education.

While mobile VR has the advantages of being universal and portable, its sense of immersion is lower than that of head-mounted VR. Immersive functions can bring better learning outcomes. However, studies have shown that the strongest sense of immersion occurs when the interaction device is simple and interesting [19]. In this study, school-age children found the technology easy to use and it increased their interest. Furthermore, according to the theory of game-based learning, educational games designed for learning purposes have a clear potential to engage students in an immersive and interactive environment [45, 50]. To sometime, these compensated for the deficiency of insufficient immersion. Some studies have shown that there is not always a difference in the knowledge acquired from mobile VR compared to immersive VR, such as PC-based VR. Both have similar effects in delivering knowledge [15]. Notably, the health impacts brought about by VR warrant attention. Some researches indicate that the use of VR increases visual and cognitive loads. Excessive load may have potential adverse effects, such as causing dizziness, vision deterioration, kinesia and other disorders [51–53].In contrast, Mobile VR is more likely to allow users to leave the experience environment independently, achieving relaxation and relief than head-mounted VR, which is beneficial for alleviating dizziness, kinesia, and other physical discomforts [54].Thus, mobile VR has increasingly emerged as a cost-effective alternative that offers educational benefits similar to those of immersive VR, while requiring fewer resources and imposing less physical burden [55].

There are several limitations to this study. The low R2 value indicates that the model has limited explanatory power. This result can be attributed to human behavior and cognition are influenced by a multitude of factors, which may not have been captured by the model or included in this study [56, 57]. Although R2 is relatively low, the P value is significant, so the findings of this study still very meaningful [58, 59]. The significance of grade, VR experience, father’s education level, and brushing habits indicates these variables are very likely play important roles in explaining oral knowledge scores, which could hold important implications for the improvement of educational and intervention strategies. However, future research is needed to explore other potential influencing factors, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of the effects. Participation in the oral VR experience was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias. Future studies could employ more scientific grouping methods to mitigate this issue. Additionally, this study was conducted in the Tianjin area and may not fully reflect the application of this technology in different regions. Future research could be expanded to multiple regions and incorporate a more rigorous grouping method to better evaluate the effectiveness of the dental VR experience. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the technology was only assessed in the short term, one week after a single experience. No studies have been conducted on the long-term effects or the impact of repeated exposure over time. These areas could be investigated in future research, such as by tracking behavioral changes.

Conclusion

We present the first application of mobile virtual reality for oral health education in primary schools. Our study demonstrates high student acceptance of VR technology in Tianjin, China, alongside significant improvements in oral health knowledge and attitudes. Given its efficacy, accessibility, and visualization capabilities, mobile VR represents a promising tool for scalable oral health education.
