Targeting druggable kinases in neuropathic pain
Authors: Harrison Benson, Komalpreet Kaur, Mahmoud Salama Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the prevalence of neuropathic pain and the opioid crisis, there has been little progress in the development of non-opioid alternative therapies. Protein kinases orchestrate multiple key pathophysiological events in the progression of pain signaling pathways. This review highlights druggable kinases and the associated scaffolds as well as molecular modalities to reverse mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia in neuropathy models whether preclinically or clinically.

Neuropathic pain (NP)

was defined by the international association for the study of pain (IASP) as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage”. Where pain is a net result of consequences of activation of primary nociceptive stimulating events due to potential tissue damage associated with inflammatory pain. NP is classified into two categories: Central Neuropathic Pain (CNP) and Peripheral neuropathic Pain (PNP) based on the anatomical and histopathological location of the disease (Fig. 1). If the lesions or the pain initiates inside the brain and/or spinal cord, it is termed CNP1,2. Meanwhile, CNP is subdivided into four subcategories-associated with spinal cord injury3, brain injury3, post-stroke pain3, and multiple sclerosis pain4. Most CNP iterations share the anatomical location within the thalamus and cortex of the brain, causing alteration in the pain signaling associated with unpleasant abnormal sensations, burning pain, stabbing, or electric-shock-like-pain4. PNP is defined by neuralgia and painful polyneuropathy with significant distribution to the innervation territories of peripheral nerves, branches of the brachial or lumbar plexus, or spinal segments2. PNP’s subcategorizations involve trigeminal neuralgia, chronic neuropathic pain after peripheral injury, painful neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, and painful radiculopathy. PNP is one of the commonly associated manifestations of a series of diseases, such as autoimmune diseases, diabetes, different types of cancers, neurofibromatosis, viral infections, and exposure to toxins, among others5–11, that results in significant morbidity and disability12–14. For instance, diabetic neuropathic pain occurs in Type-I and Type-II diabetic patients, but not all patients develop diabetic neuropathic pain. However, there are multiple risk factors that can lead to development of neuropathy, including poor glycemic control, cardiovascular disorder (hypertension, higher triglycerides, obesity and smoking), and genetic factors. PNP is characterized by pain in hands and feet due to the damage in sensory neurons15. Also, Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN) is identified under the painful polyneuropathy category and is caused by administration of chemotherapeutic agents such as taxanes (paclitaxel and docetaxel), platinum-based drugs (cisplatin and oxaliplatin), vinca alkaloids (vincristine), thalidomide, and proteosome inhibitors (bortezomib) to cancer survivors. CIPN is characterized by pain or electric sensation in hands, feet, face and stocking distribution occur in lower arms and lower legs. Additionally, pre-existing neuropathy can be another cause. Thus, it affects 60% of patients within the initial three months of chemotherapy and 30% observed passing ≥6 months of treatment16. The current therapeutic regimens for treatment of NP are limited to opioids and opioid-related therapeutics. They are only partially efficacious for short-term pain management, and the response to their long-term use is widely variable17.Fig. 1Classification of neuropathic pain.Illustrations were created by Biorender.com.

Despite the magnitude of the pain epidemic and the opioid crisis, there has been little progress in the development of non-opioid alternative therapies. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel non-opioid and non-addicting therapies that are effective in chronic pain management18. Current therapies for the treatment of PNP include over-the-counter (OTC) drugs such as ibuprofen and acetaminophen, in addition to prescription pain medication, including opioids, anticonvulsants, and antidepressants19,20. However, these therapies are seldom effective. Opioids in particular, which as outlined above, are often inadequate for long-term chronic pain treatment and require dose escalation. These further contribute to the ongoing opioid crisis. Hence, we will focus on protein kinases as non-opioid molecular targets that can be recruited towards treatment of PNP.

Involvement of protein kinases in the progression of pathophysiological Iterations associated with PNP

Neuropathy involves major pathological events that can result in peripheral neuropathy including demyelination, distal axonal degeneration and neuronopathy21. These events eventually can lead to inflammatory signaling activation22, altered synaptic signaling23, and astrocyte / microglia activation24,25. Multiple pathological iterations are associated with the progression of diabetic neuropathy related to oxidative stress and dysregulation of key cell-cycle protiens26, hypoxia in tubular cells and axonal degeneration26,27, and increased cytokine signaling, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and neuroinflammation26,28. Additionally, different pathological iterations can lead to the progression of CIPN including mitochondrial disruption from oxidative stress, altered neurotransmitter activity, neuronal hyperexcitability from decreased signaling thresholds, growth factor inhibition, axonal degeneration, astrocyte activation, neuroinflammation from immune processes, and altered calcium homeostasis29–31. GABAA receptors and TRPV1 channels had been linked to the upregulation of different kinases that can lead to the progression of pathophysiological iterations of neuropathic pain32,33. However, in the review we will establish the connection between kinases overactivation and associated pathophysiological iterations supported with the latest preclinical and clinical advancements for introducing scaffolds targeting NP. Dual leucine zipper kinase (DLK), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK), and ephrin B (EphB) receptor tyrosine kinase have been linked to axonal degeneration34–39. MAPK c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), and EphB can lead to astrocyte and microglial activation36–41. p38 MAPK, PI3K, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt), mTOR, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and EphB have been linked to neuroinflammation36–39,42–47. Finally, ERK, p38 MAPK, and PI3K/Akt have all been linked to altered synaptic signaling39,46–49.

Druggable kinases in peripheral neuropathic pain

Dual Leucine Zipper Kinase (DLK)

Dual Leucine Zipper Kinase (DLK) was initially identified as a potential target for peripheral pain treatment due to its role in axonal degeneration in cisplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy. While there are no FDA approved drugs for DLK inhibition yet, research into development of small molecule inhibitors for DLK was promising (Fig. 3). IACS-8287 was identified as an effective and selective DLK inhibitor with Kd of 14 nM in vitro associated with in vivo studies to suggest successful mitigation for the progression of cisplatin induced mechanical allodynia50. Later, Le et al. further optimized IACS-52825, with better binding affinity at Kd of 1.3 nM and an IC50 value of 107 ± 22 μM51. However, IACS-52825 inevitably caused reversible swelling of the optic nerve in monkey models. No therapeutic window was found to overcome that associated adverse effect while also maintaining the efficacy in treating CIPN51. Additionally, GDC-0134 was first of the DLK inhibitors with promising binding affinity that were recruited for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) studies52. However, phase I clinical trials for GDC-0134 were halted due to serious complications leading optic ischemic neuropathy and permanent blindness53. This echoes the optic nerve issues seen with IACS-52825 in the monkey models51. However, previous studies have found that DLK plays a role in the regeneration of the optic nerve54. In our view, this suggests that DLK may not be the most appropriate therapeutic target for inhibiting neuropathic pain due to the above-mentioned adverse effects.Fig. 3Structures of DLK inhibitors.

p38 mitogen activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK)

p38 MAPK signaling has major role in the axonal degeneration, neuroinflammation and alteration of the synaptic signaling in the spinal cord microglia. Its activation can lead to the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators via NF-κB, which can lead to a cytokine loop. Furthermore, p38 MAPK is responsible for regulating ion channels such as NaV1.7, NaV1.8, and TRPV4; dysregulation can lead to abnormal neuronal firing and persistent sensations of pain55–57. Different therapeutics showed potential to reverse allodynia and hyperalgesia in different pain models (Fig. 4). Duloxetine is a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) which has been traditionally used for treating depression and nerve pain35. Recent clinical trials and case reports for duloxetine show efficacy in treating paclitaxel- and oxaliplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy, however evidence for the pathways involved was limited58–62. For these reasons, duloxetine is the only treatment for CIPN recommended by the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) for cancer survivors who have completed neurotoxic chemotherapy35,60. In 2019, Meng et al. discovered that duloxetine was able to significantly reduce the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK in vitro. For in vivo studies, they also found that duloxetine reversed both mechanical allodynia and cold allodynia in CIPN models. Additionally, studies on the dorsal root ganglia proved that duloxetine treatment diminished p38 MAPK phosphorylation. Inhibition of p38 MAPK via duloxetine diminished pro-inflammatory cytokines to alleviate CIPN35. Currently, duloxetine is being investigated in phase II clinical trials for treating CIPN60,63. Ongoing clinical trials involving duloxetine for treating CIPN have been ongoing, showing promising results60. Treatment consisting of 30-60 mg per day orally over the course of 5 weeks was able to reduce CIPN in 59% of the treatment group. Using the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF), an 11-point questionnaire for describing pain, the treatment group reported an average pain change of -1.06 points. Using Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy/Gynecologic Oncology Group – Neurotoxicity (FACT/GOG-NTX), a questionnaire for assessing levels of CIPN, respondents reported an average of a 1.58-point decrease on the 44-point scale60,62. However, one of the major issues with duloxetine is that it is not universally effective for unknown causes64. The full pharmacological and mechanistic profiles for duloxetine are still understudied. p38 MAPK pathway was studied in the context of L5 spinal nerve ligation leading to mechanical allodynia associated with activation of p28 and increased p38 signaling65. Administration of p38 MAPK inhibitor SB203580 prior to ligation showed a preventive effect and partially reverse the neuropathic pain associated with nerve damage65,66. Unfortunately, SB203580 preclinical trials have shown poor results, due to off-target effects with other kinases such as RIPK2, GAK, and CSNK1D, and failure to inhibit the gamma and delta isoforms of p3867,68. Similarly, p38 inhibitors such as FR167653 and CNI-1493 have shown efficacy in reversing pain in the sciatic nerve ligation model and inflammatory neuropathy models65,66,69. While FR167653 is still preclinical, CNI-1493 has undergone phase I clinical trials for renal cancer and melanoma treatment66,70. In cases of nerve damage caused by sciatic nerve chronic constriction injury (CCI) induced neuropathic pain and formalin-induced inflammatory pain, the p38 inhibitor Skepinone-L was able to inhibit mechanical allodynia. However, when administered intrathecally, this effect was only seen in male mice, suggesting a sex-dependent microglial signaling pathway may be involved71,72. Despite this, these sex differences were not present in intraperitoneal or perineural administration. Furthermore, FGA-19 was also found to target the p38 MAPK signaling to inhibit carrageenan-induced inflammatory hyperalgesia in mice, as well as diminish post inflammatory pain71,73. Similarly, compound 12 has also been found to be effective in inhibiting complete Freund adjuvant (CFA)-induced inflammatory pain by inhibiting p38 MAPK71,74. Resolvins, a family of compounds derived from omega-3 fatty acids, have recently come under consideration as potential treatments for inflammatory and neuropathic pain75,76. Resolvins have gained notoriety for their ability to regulate cytokines and neuroinflamation75. Specifically, Resolvin D1 (RvD1) has shown an ability to treat neuropathic pain, making use of p38. Rather than direct inhibition, RvD1 was able to suppress p38 MAPK in the microglia. This suppression can help alleviate pain. Compounded with the fact that Resolvins have also been associated with cytokine expression and anti-inflammatory effects, RvD1 warrants further investigation into its pathways and potential for further drug discovery77,78. Finally, we present Dilmapimod as a reversible and selective p38α MAPK inhibitor that reduced neuronal sensitization to enroll clinical trials for reducing pain in patients suffering from nerve trauma, radiculopathy, or carpal tunnel syndrome79. Dilmapimod was well tolerated with no adverse effects and was able to enroll phase II clinical trials79. In trials for treating nerve injury pain, 7.5 mg doses of dilmapimod twice daily over the course of two weeks were able to decrease scores on the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), an 11-point metric for reporting pain, by 0.80 points in two weeks. 27% of patients reported a decrease in pain of 50% or greater. However, there is a delayed effect; treatment did not significantly reduce pain during the first week of administration79.Fig. 4Structures of p38 MAPK inhibitors.

c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)

Activation of the phosphorylation signaling of JNK is highly expressed in the injured DRG associated with the progression of axonal sprouting that eventually led to hypersensitivity, mechanical allodynia, and peripheral neuropathy80,81. Activation of JNK isoforms is associated with activated signals contributing to cellular-stress, proinflammatory cytokines and growth factors82. Additionally, astrocyte JNK activation increases the neuroinflammation due to the production of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NO, PGE₂) which induces pain signaling83. SP600125 is a JNK inhibitor was found to mitigate CIPN in iPSC-derived sensory neurons in vitro84 and to treat mechanical allodynia in cancer induced bone pain85 (Fig. 5). Another peptide-based JNK inhibitor was administered intrathecally to reverse mechanical allodynia after spinal nerve ligation86.Fig. 5Structure of a JNK inhibitor.

Structure of a JNK inhibitor.

PI3K γ-Akt signaling pathway

Phosphoinositide 3-kinase gamma (PI3Kγ) is highly expressed in immune cells and the microglia. Its activation leads to the downstream signaling of protein kinase B (Akt), which is helps regulate neuronal signaling and apoptosis signalling87. The PI3K γ/AKT signaling cascade plays a pivotal role in the neuroinflammation and nerve injury pathways in the progression of PNP and spinal cord injury88, where different therapeutics showed potential to reverse allodynia and hyperalgesia in preclinical pain models (Fig. 6). Modulation of PI3Kγ-Akt signaling pathway was validated for analgesic purposes in the DRG via the µ-opioid receptor, however this approach still needs further investigation89.Fig. 6Structures of PI3K inhibitors.

Structures of PI3K inhibitors.

AS-605240 is a selective PI3Kγ inhibitor that blocked the morphine-induced nociceptive activation of the PI3Kγ/AKT/nNOS to suggest that PI3Kγ/AKT is potential therapeutic target for peripheral neuropathy90. Later, AS-605240 showed potential inhibitory profiles for CXCL1 chemokine and its receptor CXCR2 to reverse mechanical allodynia in paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy91. In CCI models, the covalent pan PI3K inhibitor wortmannin was found to significantly inhibit mechanical allodynia92. However, wortmannin lacks specificity, is reactive, has a short life-life, and causes side effects including liver dysfunction and hyperglycemia in animal models. Therefore, wortmannin can’t be considered as a translational option for treatment of pain93. Later, GSK690693 was developed as a pan-Akt inhibitor with IC50 values of 2 nM for Akt1, 13 nM for Akt2, and 9 nM for Akt394,95. GSK690693 showed promising relieving effects in bone cancer pain preclinical models associated with significant downregulation for WNK1 expression in the spinal cord lysates and reduced levels of inflammatory cytokines96. GSK690693 failed clinical trials for cancer treatment due to off-target effects97.

Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)

Targeting the mTOR pathway is linked to elevated expression of mTOR signaling associated with increased neuropeptide Y (NPY) signaling leading to enhanced neuronal excitability, glial activation, and synaptic plasticity in injury-induced neuropathic pain model in rats98. Also, activation of mTORC1 can lead to the downstream activation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 (S6K1) that can result in hyperexcitability, mechanical hypersensitivity in dorsal root ganglia in CIPN38,99,100. Rapamycin is a known mTOR inhibitor that alleviated neuropathic pain-induced synaptic plasticity associated with significant reduction in mechanical allodynia101. Mechanistically, rapamycin and everolimus (rapamycin analog) do not directly bind to mTOR, however they bind to FK506-binding protein12 (FKBP12) (Fig. 7). This FKBP12-rapamycin or FKBP12-everolimus complex acts as an allosteric inhibitor to mTORC1 but not mTORC2102. Interestingly, mTORC1 expression is highly correlated with the progression of CIPN with no contribution of mTORC238,103. Based on that, rapamycin and everolimus alleviated the mechanical allodynia in bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy38, where everolimus showed a slightly reduced efficacy at lower does and duration of effect38. Targeting mTOR-S6K1 signaling is potential therapeutic approach for treatment of PNP including CIPN, confirm the translational readiness for both of rapamycin and everolimus for further clinical trial testing.Fig. 7Structures of mTORC inhibitors.

Structures of mTORC inhibitors.

Tropomyosin receptor kinases (Trk)

TrkA is the receptor nerve growth factor (NGF) is able to interact with the TrkA receptor, which leads to the downstream activation of ERK and PLC/PKC104,105. Activation of TrkA/NGF signaling can lead to increased peripheral hypersensitivity, hyperalgesia106,, and alterations in pain related gated-ion channel signaling107. Additionally, neuron-specific deletion of TrkA alleviated hyperalgesia in osteoarthritis pain mouse model via suppression of the NGF/TrkA singaling pathway108. There are different small molecules (Fig. 8) featured the potential of potent and peripherally restricted pan-Trk inhibitors by Pfizer such as PF-06273340- related analogs supported with SAR campaign to alleviate hyperalgesia in UV irradiation-induced thermal hyperalgesia inflammatory pain rat model109,110. PF-06273340 showed significant reduced hyperalgesia upon exposure to the UVB heat via pain detection threshold (PDT) assessment clinically111. Additionally, 3-pyrazolyl-indazole based analogs were featured as potent and selective TrkA inhibitors to alleviate CFA-induced thermal hypersensitivity rat model112. ARRY-470 or Larotrectinib is FDA approved pan-Trk inhibitor for treatment of metastatic cancer, where administration of Larotrectinib showed significant reduction in bone cancer pain behaviors associated with reduced sarcoma-induced nerve sprouting and neuroma formation113.Fig. 8Structures of Trk inhibitors.

Structures of Trk inhibitors.

Extracellular regulated kinase (ERK1/2)

ERK is an isoform of the larger MAPK family and is responsible for nociceptor excitability and synaptic plasticity in the CNS in glial cells and neurons114. The activation ERK signaling pathway in dorsal root ganglion, neurons, microglia, and astrocytes contributes to peripheral inflammation post-nerve injury and sciatic nerve ligation115. This involves multiple pathological iterations related to axonal degeneration and abnormal myelinetlation115,116. This suggests that ERK/MAPK pathway can be a plausible molecular target for the progression of peripheral neuropathic48 and diabetic neuropathic pain117. Magnolin is a naturally occurring compound isolated from Magnolia denudata Desrousseaux, that has been used in Korean folk medicine to treat pain43,118,119. In mouse models, Magnolin reversed paclitaxel-induced cold allodynia and downregulated the phosphorylation signaling of ERK1/2 in a dose dependent manner43. While Magnolin showed potential therapeutic modality, it lacks specificity with multi-kinase inhibitory profiles against MAPK, JNK/Sp1/MMP15, and CXCL10/p38 signaling pathways118,120–122. The protein activin C has previously been noted for its ability to treat pain in SNL models of neuropathic pain in vivo via modulating the TRPV1 channel32. Additionally, activin C has also been found to suppress thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia in vivo via regulation of ERK1/2123. The fact that activin C can modulate neuropathic pain via multiple pathways makes it a promising lead for further studies into pain. Another possible strategy for regulating ERK is by modulating the activities of the upstream MEK1/2, where MEK1/2 inhibitors such as PD98059 and U0126 were able to inhibit the downstream phosphorylation of ERK1/2, thus diminishing pain hypersensitivity in nerve-injury models48,71,124,125 (Fig. 9).Fig. 9Structures of ERK inhibitors.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)

The activation of AMPK pathway showed potential contribution to improvement of mitochondrial energy production, reduction of reactive oxidative species (ROS) production, and reduction of inflammatory nociception126–128. AMPK pathway signaling is often impaired in peripheral neuropathy, injury induced plasticity, and diabetic neuropathy129,130. Based on that, different therapeutics were recruited to activate AMPK signaling in the progression of PNP whether directly or indirectly (Fig. 10). Metformin is one of the most commonly prescribed drugs that has been used for controlling hyperglycemia in diabetic patients and showed a preventive effect to reverse diabetic mechanical allodynia129 and cisplatin-induced mechanical allodynia by activating AMPK signaling indirectly131–133. Metformin is currently undergoing clinical trials for treating CIPN134. In previous clinical trials, 500 mg metformin taken orally three times a day by chemotherapy patients was able to decrease average Ntx-12 (12-items version of the previously mentioned FACT/GOG-Ntx metric) scores from 34.5 to 37.8 after 6 cycles of treatment (a 3.3-point difference), and from 24.0 to 19.2 (a 4.8-point difference) after 12 cycles when compared to the control group. Furthermore, BPI-SF scores decreased from 7.3 to 6.7 after 12 rounds between the groups134. Narciclasine is a naturally occurring alkaloid isolated from Amaryllidaceae which reduced oxidative stress to activate AMPK signaling indirectly through the regulation of upstream liver kinase B1 (LKB1) to prevent and reverse mechanical hypersensitivity and hyperalgesia priming in mouse models treated with paclitaxel135–138. This suggests that Narciclasine, as indirect AMPK activator, could be an attractive hit for further development targeting CIPN136.Fig. 10Structures of AMPK activators.

Structures of AMPK activators.

MK8722 is one of the most potent, direct, and specific AMPK activators. It had managed to reduce incision-induced mechanical hypersensitivity and hyperalgesia priming in male and female mice139. Meanwhile, MK8722 did not show any reversal nor preventive effect on mechanical hypersensitivity caused by paclitaxel136. However, MK8722 did attenuate hyperalgesia priming in male and female mice.

5-Aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleoside, (AICAR) is a prodrug that is phosphorylated into AICAR monophosphate, which mimics AMP and binds to AMPK, activating it and protecting it from dephosphorylation140,141. Administration of AICAR prevented and reversed diabetic neuropathy in T1D and T2D mouse models and increased the phosphorylation of AMPK signaling in DRG neurons142.

Studies into the structure-function relationship between neuropathic pain and AMPK signaling are just beginning. The important takeaway is that while direct activation of AMPK is effective, indirect activation has shown to be more promising42,131,136,143.

Erythropoietin-producing hepatocellular carcinoma (EphB) tyrosine kinase

EphB receptor tyrosine kinases are highly expressed in the excitatory CNS synapse due to the direct interaction of EphB with NMDA-type glutamate receptors144. Following peripheral nerve damage pre-synaptic ephrin-B2 ligand protein is upregulated in nociceptive peripheral nerve fibers and post-synaptic EphB1 receptor protein becomes upregulated on dorsal horn neurons in the spinal cord. EphB activation can increase NMDA phosphorylation, leading to spinal sensitization36,145. EphB2 specifically has been validated to further activate NMDA receptors via PKC, leading to pain maintenance36. Additionally, EphB1 and EphB2 have been linked to calpain-1 and caspase-3, which have been linked to neuronal apoptosis, with EphB1 also being linked to the upregulation of spinal cytokines36. This model of EphB1 and EphB2 in neuropathic pain, and the ephrin-B:EphB:NMDAR protein interactions has been validated by numerous groups146–160. Furthermore, EphB signaling has been linked to the activation of other kinases including ERK1/2 p38 MAPK, JNK, SFK, and PI3K signaling, all of which are mentioned in this paper as further contributors to pain36,150. EphB1 and EphB2 receptors are highly relevant therapeutic targets for neuropathic pain, where EphB1 and EphB2 knockout mice showed reversal and preventive phenotypes for thermal hyperalgesia in CCI of the sciatic nerve models. Additionally, p-EphB1/p-EphB2/p-EphB3 signals were highly expressed in myofascial trigger point located in a taut band in patients with myofascial pain syndrome161. Interestingly, intramuscular administration of the EphB1 inhibitor EphB1-Fr significantly suppressed mechanical hyperalgesia developed in myofascial pain syndrome preclinical rat models161.

Later, a structure-based drug repurposing strategy was recruited to study the effect of FDA approved tetracyclines on EphB1/2 tyrosine kinase signaling cascade. This suggested that an equimolar combination of the antibiotics minocycline, chlortetracycline, and demeclocycline (MCD) reversed thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia id different inflammatory pain models induced by CFA, capsaicin, or formalin in vivo (Fig. 11)162. As a reminder, these three compounds are antibiotics; overuse of them for pain management will lead to antibiotic resistance. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that EphB1/2 tyrosine kinase signaling could be promising molecular target integrated in the progression of thermal and mechanical nociception of neuropathic pain.Fig. 11Structures of EphB1/2 tyrosine kinase inhibitors.

Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3)

FLT3 discovered as a specific therapeutic target for PNP as it involves nerve-injury induced hyperalgesia and pain sensitization but not inflammatory pain. Cytokine FL activates FLT3 which induces molecular modification and generation of FLT3 signals. This further leads to hypersensitivity in injury-induced PNP163 and sensory neuronal hyperexcitability in the DRG neurons164. In-vivo studies reported that single injection of FL in mice determined pain-related behaviors, rapid upregulation and potentiation of TRP family transducers in the DRG leading to hyperexcitability. Mechanistically, FLT3 activation enhanced the function of TRP ion channels (TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPM8), contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability. Hence, reduction in FLT3 levels either by genetic deletion, siRNA, or neuron-targeted viral knockdown; reversed established neuropathic pain and suppressed molecular markers of both peripheral and central sensitization163. FLT3-defcienct mice exhibited strong resistance to pain hypersensitivity in injury-based PNP models. Therefore, FLT3 can be a promising molecular target for PNP. Structure-based virtual screening introduced a novel FLT3 negative allosteric inhibitor, BDT001 (Fig. 12), which selectively blocks FL-FLT3 interaction and effectively reversing neuropathic pain in mice without impairing normal pain sensation or motor function163. However, other inhibitors targeting intracellular ATP-binding site of the FLT3 kinase domain are under clinical evaluation for the treatment FLT3- mutated acute myeloid leukemia164.Fig. 12Structure of an FLT3 inhibitor.

Structure of an FLT3 inhibitor.

Adaptor-Associated Kinase 1 (AAK1)

Adaptor-Associated Kinase 1 (AAK1) is a serine/threonine kinase belonging to the Ark1/Prk1 family that was established as validated molecular target for neuropathic pain, a chronic condition caused by lesions or diseases of the somatosensory system165. Genetic deletion and pharmacological inhibition of AAK1 significantly reduced pain behaviors in multiple preclinical models of neuropathic pain, including spinal nerve ligation and chemotherapy-induced neuropathy166. LP-935509 was identified as AAK1 inhibitor to reverse pain behavioral indices in injury-based and diabetic peripheral neuropathy rodent models165,166.

BMS-986176 (LX9211) was identified as a novel biaryl alkyl ethers-based analog as an AAK1 inhibitor with stable oral bioavailability that showed good efficacy to reverse thermal hyperalgesia in CCI and mechanical allodynia in diabetic peripheral neuropathy models in rats167. Based on its preclinical efficacy and drug-like properties, BMS-986176 (LX9211) was evaluated in phase II clinical trials for diabetic neuropathy and postherpetic neuralgia168. BMS-986176 (LX9211) was advanced into double-blinded, multicenter, and randomized diabetic peripheral neuropathic pain human trial to support further investigation in larger trials169 (Fig. 13). AAK1 is considered as promising target for neuropathic pain but it is mainly associated with clathrin-mediated endocytosis and other signaling pathways such as WNT, Notch and NF-kB and neuronal dendritic regulation. Additionally, AAK1 inhibitors target other protein such as GAK and BIKE which are homologous to AAK1 protein, this leads to induction of off-target toxicity.Fig. 13Structures of AAK1 inhibitors.

Src-family kinases (SFK)

Activation of Src-family kinases (SFKs) induced in spinal cord can lead to mechanical hypersensitivity in nerve injury-induced models that can lead to peripheral neuropathic pain170–172. SFK activation is associated with molecular iteration whether upstream (nerve injury inputs) and downstream effectors (ion channels); leading to neuropathic pain173. Therefore, SFKs specific inhibitors are needed to reverse different pain behaviors in inflammatory and peripheral pain models174 (Fig. 14). Intrathecal administration of SFKs inhibitors reversed mechanical allodynia in chronic pain models including CFA and PNI models174. Dasatinib is an orally bioavailable Src-kinase inhibitor that tackles pain-related behavior in cancer-induced bone pain175. multitargeted kinase inhibitor, showed nanomolar potencies against SFKs and has potential to treat pain associated with increased osteoclast activity176. Bafetinib is a dual tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets both Bcr-Abl and SFK contributes to alleviating of neuropathic pain established by CCI via inhibiting Lyn-mediated glycolysis in the microglia177 and Protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2)-induced activation of TRPV4 channels178.Fig. 14Structures of SFK inhibitors.

Opportunities and limitations for kinase druggability in peripheral neuropathy

In this review, we explained that targeting kinase signaling can offer plausible pathways to offer non-opioid based therapeutics that can modulate pain signaling pathways without addiction risks, potentially effective for chronic PNP where current options (e.g., opioids, anticonvulsants, antidepressants) are partially efficacious or require dose escalation. Also, we covered the pathophysiological iterations associated with peripheral neuropathy associated with the expression levels of different kinase signaling pathways. Also, we highlighted promising druggable kinases as alternatives for non-opioid based therapeutics to reverse mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia stimuli towards treatment of PNP, diabetic PNP, and CIPN whether preclinically or clinically (Table 1). However, the main challenge associated with kinase targeting will be off-target effects, selectivity, and specificity due to the high homology among the ATP binding domains among the kinome. For example, p38 MAPK and c-Jun kinases share high degree of structural similarity in the hinge region, DFG motif, and activation loop. Our review suggested that p38 MAPK inhibitors and AMPK activators could have potential promising clinical therapeutic modalities for peripheral neuropathy associated with diabetes and/or chemotherapeutic administration. Duloxetine, Dilmapimod, and Metformin showed translational readiness after improvements in phase 2 clinical trials whether in CIPN and/or pain. However, it is challenging to enable cross-target comparison among them due to the variability in the recruited quantitative metrics and difference in the clinical settings. Additionally, the preclinical testing whether thermal hyperalgesia or mechanical allodynia among different kinase inhibitors was not conclusive enough to account for improvement in the pain threshold. In this review article, we featured understudied kinases such as EphB1/2, AAK1, and FLT3 that showed a limited number of inhibitors. In summary, kinase targeting represents a high-opportunity area for delivery of molecular modalities towards PNP via non-opioid based therapies, with several candidates advancing clinically and addressing unmet needs in chronic pain management. However, overcoming limitations like toxicity, specificity, selectivity, and translational gaps will require further optimization and recruitment of structure-activity relationship campaigns.
