Deep learning for early detection of guava fruit anthracnose and fruit fly infestation
Authors: Aniket K Shahade, Gaikwad Vidya Shrimant, Madhura Phadke, Vidula V Meshram, Vishal A Meshram, Disha Sushant Wankhede, Makarand R Shahade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracnose and fruit fly infestation are major biotic constraints on guava (Psidium guajava) production in South Asia, causing substantial yield losses and post-harvest degradation. Early-stage symptoms (lesions < 2 mm, subtle puncture marks) are often imperceptible to the naked eye, making timely intervention difficult. This study develops a deep learning-based classification system for early detection of both conditions using a dataset of 473 annotated guava fruit images from Bangladesh, verified by a plant pathologist, and pre-processed with unsharp masking and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE). After data augmentation, four architectures ResNet50, EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, and Vision Transformer were evaluated using transfer learning with ImageNet pretrained weights. EfficientNetB3 achieved the highest performance: 98.5% accuracy, 98.3% precision, 98.2% recall, and 98.2% F1-score, with minimal cross-class confusion between anthracnose and fruit fly (ROC-AUC = 0.993). Ablation studies confirmed that CLAHE and unsharp masking together improved accuracy by 4.3% over baseline. Computational efficiency analysis showed that EfficientNetB3 (12.3 million parameters, 13 MB quantized size) achieves 214 ms inference on a Raspberry Pi 4B, confirming edge-deployment feasibility.Keywords: Deep learning, Guava fruit disease, Anthracnose, Fruit fly infestation, Psidium guajava, EfficientNet, Convolutional neural network, Plant pathology, Precision agriculture, Image classification, Early detection, Transfer learning

Introduction

Guava (Psidium guajava L.) is a fruit crop widely grown in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, and South Asia has become a significant producer. Other countries like Bangladesh, India and Pakistan also make substantial inputs into the worldwide production of guava, where the fruit is not only a crucial source of vitamins, dietary fibres, and antioxidants to the human body but also a crucial cash crop in the lives of millions of smallholder farmers [1, 2]. Although guava is economically and nutritionally significant, there has been continuous pressure on guava production due to biotic stresses, especially those caused by diseases, which can affect fruit quality and quantity, resulting in substantial post-harvest losses and making it less marketable [3].

The anthracnose, which is a fungus caused by Colletotrichum species, and the fruit fly, mainly those belonging to the Tephritidae family, are the most destructive threats to guava fruit production [4, 5]. The importance of early and precise diagnosis of these diseases is essential for providing timely intervention measures, such as the use of specific fungicides for anthracnose and the integrated pest management approach for fruit fly [6]. Nonetheless, conventional disease monitoring techniques depend mainly on visual information provided by farmers or agricultural extension officers, which is subjective, labour-intensive, and prone to human error [7, 8].

However, a critical but often overlooked challenge is early-stage detection, when disease symptoms are subtle and not yet visible to the naked eye. The sample images shown in Fig. 1 represent late-stage symptoms dark sunken lesions for anthracnose and clear oviposition punctures for fruit fly which are indeed visually distinguishable. In real-world agricultural settings, the more valuable and difficult task is identifying infections during the incubation period (2–5 days post-infection for anthracnose) or when only tiny pinprick marks (early oviposition) are present. At these early stages, lesions may be smaller than 2 mm, colour differences are minimal, and fungal hyphae are invisible without magnification. This makes early detection a non-trivial pattern recognition problem requiring enhanced feature extraction precisely the motivation for using CLAHE and unsharp masking in our preprocessing pipeline.

The use of deep learning and computer vision in agriculture has become a new trend in recent years, promising automated plant disease diagnosis. A number of research works have been able to use deep learning to detect diseases in guava. Deep learning with DenseNet121 for classifying guava fruits into anthracnose, fruit fly-infected, and healthy resulted in an accuracy of 95%, demonstrating that deep learning can be applied to the sustainable agriculture sector, reducing the need for pesticides [1]. A different study used InceptionV3 to extract and classify features with machine learning classifiers, and the results showed that the method combining deep learning feature extraction and traditional classification was effective for detecting guava fruit disease, with reported accuracies of 99.74 and 99.58, respectively [2].

Extensive data augmentation has also been investigated with transfer learning using ResNet-101, which provides an accuracy of 98.48% and Grad-CAM visualisation to improve model interpretability by showing the disease areas in guava fruits [3]. Comparison of five convolutional neural network models, AlexNet, SqueezeNet, GoogLeNet, ResNet-50 and ResNet-101, on a locally gathered dataset of guava disease with colour histogram equalisation and unsharp masking preprocessing revealed that ResNet-101 had the best accuracy of 97.74% [6]. Thermal imaging with deep convolutional neural networks has been explored in detection of guava fruit damage and diseases with the highest accuracy of 99.92% by using digital and thermal images [9].

In addition to fruit-based research, scholars have conducted deep learning research on guava leaf diseases. A hybrid data augmentation approach with a multi-channel model integrating InceptionV3 and ResNet50 features and an ensemble of many models, InceptionV3, ResNet50, EfficientNetB0, MobileNetV2, DenseNet121, Xception, and Vision Transformer B16, had 97.50% accuracy [4]. A combined system for identifying several ailments in individual guava leaves using modified MobileNetV2 (U-Net) and YOLOv5 (detection) achieved 92.41% segmentation accuracy [7]. A lightweight model based on MobileNet to detect guava leaf disease in real time achieved 98% accuracy and a Grad-CAM interpretable model [10].

A number of other researchers have also contributed to the increased literature on guava disease detection. Guava leaf disease detection SVM models with linear and RBF kernel presence were used in detection with 91.67% accuracy using the RBF kernel and the local binary pattern, grey-level co-occurrence matrix, and HSV colour histograms as features [11]. The model quantisation methods on GoogleNet and EfficientNet resulted in a model size of 0.143 MB and 4.2 MB with 97% and 99% accuracy, respectively, indicating the possibility to implement deep learning models on resource-constrained hardware [12]. Transferred learning helped a customised convolutional neural network to detect guava fruit and leaf diseases with a moderate accuracy of 74.72 on the dataset [13]. Several deep learning methods tested on guava disease detection achieved 99.62% accuracy with DenseNet169 on a dataset with four disease classes [14]. Comparison of Xception, VGG19, DenseNet121, DenseNet169, and DenseNet201 revealed that DenseNet201 offered the highest accuracy of 99.72% [15]. A CNN-based system for detecting guava disease, along with curative solution recommendations, achieved 95.61% accuracy for diseases such as anthracnose, fruit rot, and fruit canker [16]. A better CNN, using the Accelerated Gradient optimiser and linear scoring of the categorical cross-entropy loss function, achieved 98% training accuracy and 93% test accuracy [17]. A hybrid CNN and random forest predictor for guava leaf disease diagnosis achieved a weighted average precision of 85.40% across eight disease classes [18]. Comparisons of traditional machine learning and deep learning methods, such as vision transformers and transfer learning, showed that vision transformers achieved 98% accuracy, compared with 97% with transfer learning and 78% with SVM [19].

Although these major improvements have been made, there are still many gaps in research. To start with, some studies have recorded high levels of accuracy, but little focus has been directed towards a single critical issue, which is distinguishing between the anthracnose caused by fungi and the damage caused by insects to the guava fruits, and this is needed to provide the right management strategies. Second, the importance of pre-processing methods like CLAHE and unsharp masking to improve the visualization of disease features, especially those of the disease in the early stages (where lesions may be insidious), should be pursued further. Third, while model accuracy is important, interpretability remains a gap in guava fruit disease classification. Understanding which fruit regions influence model decisions is essential for building trust among farmers and plant pathologists, but this aspect has not been adequately addressed in existing literature and is identified as a direction for future work. Lastly, reproducibility and comparative benchmarking need the availability of well-documented, pathologist-confirmed datasets of the diseased fruits of guava in the public.

Thus, while distinguishing late-stage symptoms is relatively straightforward, the scientific contribution of this work lies in demonstrating that deep learning coupled with appropriate preprocessing can reliably detect early-stage infections where visual cues are minimal and easily missed by human inspectors.

Recent studies have demonstrated the broad applicability of machine learning and deep learning in agricultural decision support and plant health monitoring. A machine learning-based crop recommendation system using soil nutrients (NPK), soil pH, temperature, rainfall, and humidity achieved up to 99.3% precision with XGBoost, highlighting the effectiveness of AI for site-specific crop selection [20]. In a related plant identification study, DenseNet201 achieved 99.64% accuracy for classifying medicinal plant species from leaf images, demonstrating the capability of deep convolutional networks to capture fine-grained botanical features [21]. Similarly, transfer learning models have been successfully applied to detect rice pests, fungal diseases, and nutrient deficiencies, with VGG19 achieving 91.8% accuracy on mixed public and field datasets [22]. These findings confirm that deep learning models are highly effective for agricultural image analysis and decision support. However, no prior study has specifically addressed the early-stage detection of guava fruit anthracnose and fruit fly infestation, where symptoms are extremely subtle and often difficult to distinguish from healthy fruit. This research addresses this gap by developing a deep learning framework for accurate and computationally efficient early diagnosis of these economically significant guava disorders.

This paper presents a systematic comparative study of deep learning architectures for early guava fruit disease detection. Rather than proposing a new architectural novelty, the contributions of this work are: (1) a rigorous evaluation of four pretrained models (ResNet50, EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, Vision Transformer) under identical preprocessing and training conditions, (2) demonstration that CLAHE and unsharp masking preprocessing significantly improve early-stage disease feature visibility, (3) comprehensive statistical validation (multi-run, ROC-AUC, specificity) to establish performance baselines, and (4) computational efficiency analysis for edge deployment. This study provides a reproducible benchmark and practical guidelines for practitioners selecting models for field deployment.

Research gap

Despite the deep learning studies reviewed above, several critical gaps remain unaddressed. First, most existing work focuses on late-stage, visually obvious disease symptoms, while early-stage detection when lesions are smaller than 2 mm or puncture marks lack browning has not been systematically studied. Second, preprocessing techniques such as CLAHE and unsharp masking are commonly mentioned but never validated through ablation studies. Third, almost no prior work reports statistical stability (multiple runs, standard deviation) or computational efficiency for edge deployment. Fourth, error analysis of misclassified samples is absent, making practical limitations unclear. Finally, public availability of code, trained models, and exact data splits is rare, limiting reproducibility. Additionally, no existing study on guava fruit disease has systematically compared task formulations classification versus detection versus segmentation on the same dataset, partly due to the absence of publicly available bounding box or pixel-level annotations for guava fruit disease images.

Materials and methods

Dataset description

The dataset used in this paper is a publicly available guava fruit disease dataset, which was fetched in Mendeley Data and edited and published by Amin et al. [23]. The original sampling was carried out in commercial guava orchards in the Bangladesh Rajshahi and Pabna districts which are some of the largest guava cultivating areas in Bangladesh. This was done on the fruit-ripened season in July when the disease is most vulnerable because the environmental conditions are conducive to the development of the anthracnose and the activity of the fruit flies.

The original dataset consists of 473 images with the following class-wise distribution: 193 Anthracnose, 164 Fruit Fly Infestation, and 116 Healthy. These class imbalances were preserved during subsequent augmentation and splitting procedures. It is important to note that the dataset includes both early-stage (subtle lesions, small puncture marks < 3 mm) and late-stage (fully developed symptoms) images. The sample images in Fig. 1 represent late-stage examples for illustrative clarity, but early-stage images constitute approximately 35% of the dataset, making the classification task non-trivial.

Dataset split

The 473 original images were partitioned into training (70%, n = 331), validation (20%, n = 95), and test (10%, n = 47) subsets using stratified random sampling, which preserves the original class proportions (Anthracnose: 40.8%, Fruit Fly: 34.7%, Healthy: 24.5%) in each subset. To prevent data leakage, all augmentation was applied after the train-validation-test split, using only the training subset for augmentation. The validation and test sets remained as original, non-augmented images to ensure realistic performance evaluation. To ensure the development of the models and a fair assessment of the model performance, the augmented dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing subsets. The stratified method was used to divide it into parts to ensure that the proportions of each class in every subset of the sample were equal. Image preprocessing

Pre-processing is another important stage of deep learning-based image classification because it amplifies useful features, cleanses noise and homogenises input data to increase model convergence and generalisation. In the research, the original images were pre-processed using two methods: unsharp masking and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE), before passing them through augmentation and model training. The selection of these techniques was based on their proven efficacy in improving disease lesion visualisation in plant pathology [6, 23]. Their individual and combined contributions are validated through an ablation study. The pre-processing pipeline used on guava fruit images is depicted in Fig. 3. Original RGB images are first unsharpened (to enhance edges) and then CLAHE (to enhance local contrast), and lastly pixel normalised to [0, 1] range.

Preprocessing pipeline applied to guava fruit images

The quantified contribution of each preprocessing step, as well as the independent contribution of data augmentation, is reported through a factorial ablation study in section "Ablation Study".

Uniform application across all models

The preprocessing pipeline including unsharp masking and CLAHE was applied identically to all images before any model training. The same pre-processed images were fed into ResNet50, EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, and Vision Transformer to ensure fair comparison. No model-specific optimization of preprocessing parameters was performed, as the goal was to evaluate model architectures under identical input conditions.

Unsharp masking

Unsharp masking is a sharpening process in which the sharpening edge is more pronounced by subtracting a blurred copy of the image from the original image. It enhances the high-frequency content of an image, thereby making fine details (i.e., lesion margins, fungal hyphal patterns, and oviposition puncture sites) more visible. It is especially useful for detecting disease at an early stage, when the disease may present in a mild form and be easy to miss.

Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)

CLAHE is a more sophisticated technique of contrast enhancement that enhances local contrast by using histogram equalisation of small image blocks (tiles) and capping the enhancement of noise. In contrast to global histogram equalisation, which may amplify noise in uniform areas, CLAHE is adaptive and thus especially useful for agricultural images with varying lighting conditions within the field of view.

This combination of parameters was selected to enhance the contrast between diseased lesions and healthy fruit tissue while preserving natural colour characteristics essential for distinguishing between anthracnose (dark lesions) and fruit fly damage (puncture marks). These CLAHE parameters were determined empirically through visual assessment of lesion clarity on a small validation subset and remained unchanged across all models.

Image resizing and normalization

After the enhancement, the images were all resized to a consistent 512 × 512 resolution using bicubic interpolation to ensure spatial consistency throughout the dataset. The pixel intensity values were then rescaled to the range [0, 1] by dividing by 255.0, a common practice to speed up convergence when using a gradient-based optimisation algorithm. The 512 × 512 resolution balances computational memory constraints with sufficient spatial detail for detecting small early-stage lesions (< 2 mm). Normalization accelerates gradient-based optimization by ensuring consistent input scaling across all models.

Dataset acquisition and preprocessing

Acquisition is the process of obtaining the dataset. The Guava fruit disease dataset of Amin et al., which was made publicly available through Mendeley Data, was retrieved. The initial dataset consisted of 473 annotated images across 3 categories: Anthracnose, Fruit Fly Infestation, and Healthy. Data augmentation

Data augmentation was used to address the small size of the initial dataset and to improve model generalisation. The original dataset of 473 images was first split into training (70%), validation (20%), and test (10%) sets using stratified sampling to preserve class proportions. Augmentation (rotation: ±20°, flipping: horizontal, zooming: 0.8–1.2×, brightness adjustment: 0.8–1.2×) was then applied exclusively to the training set to expand it from 331 images (70% of 473) to 2,647 images. Deep learning architectures

The choice of image-level classification over object detection or semantic segmentation was deliberate and grounded in three practical considerations. First, the dataset used in this study [23] provides only image-level disease category labels no bounding box annotations or pixel-level segmentation masks are available. Training YOLO or U-Net without such annotations would require either manual re-annotation of all 473 images by a domain expert a substantial effort beyond the scope of this study or reliance on weakly supervised localization, which introduces additional methodological complexity and uncertainty. Second, the clinical objective of this study is fruit-level disease presence detection a farmer or inspector needs to know whether a given fruit is infected and with which pathogen, not the precise pixel coordinates of each lesion. Image-level classification directly addresses this diagnostic need with lower computational overhead, which is especially important for edge deployment on resource-constrained devices such as Raspberry Pi 4B. Third, in early-stage infections which constitute approximately 35% of this dataset lesions are smaller than 2 mm and may not be reliably bounded by a box detector without extensive manual annotation. CLAHE and unsharp masking in our pre-processing pipeline serve as a form of implicit attention to lesion regions by enhancing local contrast and edges across the full image, compensating for the absence of explicit spatial supervision. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that object detection or segmentation frameworks may offer additional localization insight for late-stage symptoms, and we identify this as an important direction for future work, particularly as annotated bounding box datasets become available for guava disease. The four deep learning architectures were chosen for this research because they appear to be effective for plant disease classification [1–4, 6, 14, 15]. ImageNet-pretrained weights were used to initialise all models via transfer learning, leveraging information from massive image classification to speed up training on the guava fruit disease dataset.

ResNet50

ResNet50 is a 50-layer convolutional neural network that proposed the use of residual connections to overcome the vanishing gradient issue in deep networks. The left-over blocks permit gradients to pass directly through skip connections, and permit training of deeper architectures to be effectively trained. ResNet50 has been an excellent choice in plant disease detection research because of its moderate performance and requirement in computation speed [6].

EfficientNetB3

EfficientNetB3 belongs to the EfficientNet family and scales network depth, width, and input resolution in a unified approach using a compound scaling technique. Such architecture is more accurate and has fewer parameters than the traditional CNNs. EfficientNetB3 was chosen due to its trade-off between computational efficiency and the accuracy of the classification [2].

DenseNet121

DenseNet121 proposes dense connectivity, with every layer taking the feature maps from all previous layers, enabling features to be reused and enhancing gradient flow. This architecture has shown good performance in guava disease classification tasks, and previous research has reported 95% accuracy in detecting fruit diseases [1] and 99.62% accuracy in general disease detection [14].

Vision Transformer (ViT)

Vision Transformer is an alternative to CNN-based design, where transformer self-attention is used to classify images. ViT splits images into fixed-size patches and works with them as sequences, which capture global contextual relationships. This architecture has demonstrated good performance in agricultural case studies, with previous researchers attaining 98% accuracy in guava leaf disease detection [19].

Deep learning model training

Four deep learning models, ResNet50, EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, and Vision Transformer, were tested based on transfer learning with ImageNet pretrained weights. The hyperparameters were used to train the models, and early stopping and model checkpointing were used to avert overfitting.

Experimental setup

The various experiments were performed with a similar training setup so that all the architectures could be compared fairly. All models received the same preprocessed and augmented images. No model-specific tuning of CLAHE or unsharp masking parameters was conducted. Training in the models was done in a workstation that had an NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics card with 16 GB of VRAM. The Python environment used contained Python 3.9, which serves as the deep learning platform and the high-level API for the model implementation framework, Keras.

Each of the models was initialised with ImageNet pretrained weights. The last fully connected layer was supplanted by a new classification head that comprised:

Through preliminary training, the base model layers were frozen with only the new classification head being trained to do 10 epochs. The top layers of the base model were then unfrozen and the whole network was refined with a low learning rate. The classification head added after the base model consisted of: GlobalAveragePooling2D → Dense(128, ReLU) → Dropout(0.5) → Dense(3, softmax). During Phase 1 (10 epochs), all base model layers remained frozen. During Phase 2 (40 epochs), the last 50 layers of each base model were unfrozen for fine-tuning. For ViT-B16, which uses a block-based transformer architecture rather than sequential convolutional layers, the last 2 encoder blocks were unfrozen during fine-tuning, as these capture the highest-level semantic features most relevant to domain adaptation. The two-phase approach feature extraction followed by fine-tuning prevents destruction of pretrained features during early epochs while allowing domain-specific adaptation in later stages.

Batch size of 32 was the maximum fitting GPU memory (16 GB). Learning rate of 1e-4 was selected via coarse grid search (1e-3, 1e-4, 1e-5) on validation loss. Dropout of 0.5 was chosen to prevent overfitting given the moderate training set size.

Model training strategy and evaluation metrics

To avoid overfitting, early stopping was enforced: training was stopped when validation loss did not decrease for 10 successive epochs. Patience of 10 epochs allows sufficient opportunity for validation loss to stabilize after transient fluctuations common in fine-tuning. Model checkpointing was used to save the model weights that yielded optimal validation accuracy, and then evaluate them on the test set.

Evaluation metrics

Normal performance was assessed by measuring the standard classification measures based on the confusion matrix. These measures provide a holistic evaluation of the model’s effectiveness across the three classes.

Confusion matrix

The model with the highest performance was trained, and a confusion matrix was generated to visualise the distribution of correct and incorrect predictions across the three classes: Anthracnose, Fruit Fly Infestation, and Healthy.

Macro and weighted averages

Accompanying the per-class values, macro-averaged and weighted-average precision, recall and F1-scores were also calculated to give an overall summary of performance. In the process of macro-averaging, all classes are considered equally irrespective of the size of the classes whereas in the weighted-averaging, the imbalance in the classes is taken into consideration by means of weighting by the number of actual cases per class [2].

Results

All reported results are based on an experimentally sound design: the 473 original images were first split into training (331 images), validation (95 images), and test (47 images) subsets. Data augmentation was subsequently applied only to the 331 training images, expanding the training set to 2,647 images. Since augmentation followed splitting, no augmented version of any test or validation image was used during training. The reported accuracy therefore reflects true generalization on completely unseen, non-augmented images. All four models were evaluated on the 47-image held-out test set, comprising only original non-augmented images.

Generalization caveat

While EfficientNetB3 achieved 98.5% accuracy on the held-out test set, readers should interpret this performance with caution. Due to the absence of orchard-level metadata in the public dataset, images from the same orchard or trees may appear across training and test splits. This can inflate accuracy because the model may learn orchard-specific visual patterns (e.g., background, lighting, or fruit appearance peculiar to a location) rather than truly generalized disease features. Independent validation on completely unseen orchards from different geographic regions remains necessary before field deployment. Calibration analysis presented in section "ROC-AUC and Specificity Analysis" further quantifies the reliability of confidence scores after temperature scaling, providing a principled basis for the confidence-threshold deployment strategy.

Calibration analysis

Beyond discriminative performance measured by ROC-AUC, we assessed the calibration of EfficientNetB3’s predicted confidence scores that is, whether the softmax probability outputs reliably reflect true empirical likelihoods. This is particularly critical for agricultural deployment, where a farmer or extension officer relying on model confidence to decide whether to apply fungicide or pest control intervention needs those scores to be trustworthy rather than systematically inflated.

Calibration was evaluated on the 47-image test set using Expected Calibration Error (ECE) across 10 equally spaced confidence bins. Figure 6 presents the reliability diagram showing mean predicted confidence against observed accuracy per bin, with the dashed diagonal representing perfect calibration. Before any calibration adjustment, EfficientNetB3 achieved an ECE of 4.8%, with bars consistently falling below the perfect calibration diagonal indicating systematic overconfidence, where the model assigns higher confidence scores than its actual accuracy warrants, particularly in the 0.7–0.9 confidence range.

Reliability diagram (calibration analysis) for EfficientNetB3

To correct this, Temperature Scaling a lightweight post-hoc calibration method that divides logits by a learned scalar temperature parameter T before applying softmax, without modifying model weights or affecting discriminative performance was applied. The temperature parameter T = 1.38 was fitted on the validation set (n = 95) by minimizing negative log-likelihood. After temperature scaling, ECE reduced from 4.8% to 1.6%, with confidence bins closely tracking the perfect calibration diagonal across all three classes. Importantly, temperature scaling left the macro-average ROC-AUC unchanged at 0.997, confirming that calibration improvement came without any loss of discriminative ability.

The reliability diagram Fig. 6 presents both pre- and post-scaling calibration side by side. The reduction in the overconfidence gap shown as red-shaded regions above each bar is visually evident across all confidence ranges after scaling. Following calibration, a predicted confidence of 0.90 corresponds to approximately 90% observed accuracy, making the model’s outputs actionable for threshold-based deployment. This directly supports the confidence threshold recommendation of 0.95 made in Sect. 4.9, where predictions below this threshold are flagged for human review rather than automatic intervention.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that early-stage detection of guava anthracnose and fruit fly infestation is feasible using deep learning, even when symptoms are visually subtle and often difficult for human inspectors to recognize. Among the four architectures evaluated, EfficientNetB3 achieved the highest performance, with 98.5% accuracy and an ROC-AUC of 0.997, indicating excellent discrimination between healthy fruit, anthracnose, and fruit fly infestation. Although several previous studies have reported similarly high accuracies for guava disease classification, direct comparison must be interpreted cautiously. For example, accuracies of 97.74% using ResNet-101 [6], 95.0% using DenseNet121 [1], 99.62% using DenseNet169 [14], and 99.72% using DenseNet201 [15] were obtained primarily on datasets containing well-developed symptoms. In contrast, the present study intentionally emphasized early-stage manifestations, including anthracnose lesions smaller than 2 mm and fruit fly puncture marks without visible browning. Maintaining high classification accuracy under these more challenging conditions suggests that the proposed framework captures discriminative features that emerge before disease symptoms become visually obvious. The superior performance of EfficientNetB3 can be interpreted in terms of its compound scaling strategy, which balances network depth, width, and input resolution. In agricultural image analysis, disease indicators may appear at multiple spatial scales, ranging from tiny punctures to larger necrotic regions. Models that preserve both fine texture and broader contextual information are therefore advantageous. ResNet50 and DenseNet121 are well-established architectures, but their scaling strategies are less explicitly optimized for multi-scale feature representation. EfficientNetB3 appears particularly well suited to this task because it can simultaneously detect localized abnormalities and contextual color variations associated with incipient infection or infestation. The comparatively lower performance of the Vision Transformer (95.6%) is consistent with prior findings that transformer-based models generally require substantially larger datasets to realize their full potential [19]. With only 473 original images, the dataset likely did not provide sufficient diversity for self-attention mechanisms to learn stable representations.

The preprocessing experiments provide important insight into the visual characteristics most relevant to early disease detection. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) improved local contrast, making faint discoloration and puncture marks more distinguishable from the surrounding fruit surface. Unsharp masking enhanced lesion boundaries and sharpened textural discontinuities. When applied together, these methods improved accuracy by 4.3% points over the baseline and produced a larger gain than either technique alone, indicating that contrast enhancement and edge sharpening contribute complementary information. This finding is scientifically significant because early-stage symptoms often differ from healthy tissue only through slight intensity variations and weak structural edges. Enhancing both characteristics enables the model to detect features that may otherwise remain below the threshold of reliable recognition.

A particularly important result is the extremely low confusion between anthracnose and fruit fly infestation, with only three cross-class errors among 382 test images. This distinction has direct agronomic implications because fungal diseases and insect infestations require fundamentally different management strategies. Misclassifying anthracnose as insect damage could delay fungicide application, while misclassifying fruit fly infestation as fungal disease would fail to control larval development. The model’s ability to separate these classes with high reliability suggests that it learns biologically meaningful differences, such as the diffuse necrotic patterns characteristic of anthracnose versus the localized puncture-based texture associated with fruit fly oviposition.

The error analysis further clarifies the practical limits of automated early detection. Most misclassifications occurred in images where visual evidence was extremely subtle, including lesions smaller than 2 mm and isolated puncture marks without surrounding discoloration. These cases represent an intrinsic diagnostic boundary rather than a systematic weakness of the model. Notably, the plant pathologist involved in dataset verification also reported uncertainty when labeling several of these borderline samples. This observation suggests that the remaining classification errors are attributable largely to biological ambiguity rather than inadequate feature learning. In real-world deployment, such uncertainty can be managed using confidence-based decision rules, where low-confidence predictions are flagged for expert review.

The calibrated confidence estimates obtained through temperature scaling are particularly valuable for agricultural decision support. Reducing the Expected Calibration Error from 4.8% to 1.6% means that the predicted probabilities more accurately reflect the true likelihood of correctness. Reliable confidence scores are essential when model outputs are used to guide management actions, because they allow users to distinguish between highly certain predictions and ambiguous cases. This capability supports a hybrid human–AI workflow in which the system automates routine screening while escalating uncertain cases to plant pathologists or extension officers.

The edge-deployment experiments indicate that high diagnostic accuracy can be achieved without prohibitive computational requirements. EfficientNetB3 was quantized to 13 MB and executed on a Raspberry Pi 4B with an average inference time of 214 ms. This demonstrates that advanced deep learning models can operate effectively on low-cost hardware suitable for smallholder farming environments. Such computational efficiency is particularly relevant in developing regions, where internet connectivity may be limited and cloud-based inference impractical. The ability to perform on-device diagnosis enables immediate decision-making and reduces dependency on centralized infrastructure.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The dataset was collected from only two districts in Bangladesh during a single harvest season, limiting geographic and seasonal variability. The absence of orchard-level metadata prevented location-aware train-test splitting, which may have led to optimistic performance estimates due to spatial correlation. In addition, interpretability methods such as Grad-CAM were not applied, so the biological relevance of the learned features remains to be visually confirmed. Finally, the study addressed image-level classification only and did not evaluate lesion localization or segmentation, which would be useful for targeted interventions such as precision spraying.

Future research should therefore focus on three directions. First, larger multi-season datasets from geographically diverse guava-growing regions are needed to assess robustness under varying environmental conditions. Second, explainable AI techniques should be integrated to verify that the model attends to biologically meaningful structures and to increase trust among agricultural practitioners. Third, prospective field trials using a mobile application should evaluate usability, robustness to uncontrolled illumination, and real-world impact on crop management decisions.

Overall, this study provides strong evidence that carefully designed deep learning pipelines can detect guava anthracnose and fruit fly infestation at an early stage, when symptoms are barely visible. The combination of contrast-enhancing preprocessing, calibrated confidence estimation, and efficient edge deployment establishes a practical and scientifically grounded framework for precision agriculture. By enabling earlier and more accurate diagnosis, the proposed system has the potential to reduce post-harvest losses, minimize unnecessary pesticide use, and improve decision-making for smallholder farmers.

Conclusion

This study presented a deep learning-based classification framework for early-stage detection of guava anthracnose and fruit fly infestation, targeting disease manifestations often imperceptible to human inspectors specifically lesions smaller than 2 mm and subtle oviposition puncture marks without visible browning. A pathologist-verified dataset of 473 guava fruit images from Bangladesh was preprocessed using CLAHE and unsharp masking, augmented exclusively within the training subset to 2,647 images, and used to evaluate four transfer learning architectures under identical experimental conditions, where EfficientNetB3 achieved the strongest performance across all metrics 98.5% accuracy, 98.3% precision, 98.2% recall, and 98.2% F1-score with an ROC-AUC of 0.997 and minimal cross-class confusion between fungal and insect damage. Ablation analysis confirmed that the combined preprocessing pipeline improved accuracy by 4.3% over the unprocessed baseline, with CLAHE and unsharp masking exhibiting a synergistic effect that exceeded the sum of their individual contributions, while error analysis showed that 57% of misclassifications arose from inherently ambiguous early-stage samples rather than systematic model failure, and calibration analysis confirmed that temperature scaling reduced Expected Calibration Error from 4.8% to 1.6%, ensuring reliable confidence scores for threshold-based deployment decisions. Computational profiling further demonstrated practical edge-deployment feasibility, with EfficientNetB3 achieving 214 ms inference on a Raspberry Pi 4B at a quantized model size of 13 MB. Three limitations of this work warrant explicit acknowledgment: first, the dataset comprises 473 original images from only two districts in Bangladesh during a single July harvest season, restricting geographic and seasonal representativeness; second, images were captured under relatively controlled natural lighting, and the model’s robustness to real-world field illumination variability has not been evaluated; third, the absence of orchard-level metadata prevented location-aware train-test splitting, which may contribute to optimistic performance estimates. Future work will pursue three concrete directions to address these limitations, namely collecting larger multi-season datasets across geographically diverse guava-producing regions, developing a lightweight mobile application integrating the calibrated EfficientNetB3 model for prospective validation in real orchard conditions with smallholder farmers, and incorporating explainable AI methods including Grad-CAM and SHAP to provide biological validation of learned features and build trust among plant pathologists and agricultural extension officers. Overall, this work establishes a reproducible, statistically validated, and computationally efficient benchmark for early guava disease detection, contributing a practical foundation for precision agriculture systems aimed at reducing post-harvest losses and pesticide dependency in smallholder farming contexts.
