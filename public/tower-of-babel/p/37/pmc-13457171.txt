Energy flood: The overlooked surplus extreme in global wind-solar power systems
Authors: Qian Cheng, Jianxiao Wang, Pan Liu, Lei Cheng, Jiabo Yin, Maoyuan Feng, Bo Ming, Xiaodong Li, Zongliang Zhang, Zenghai Zhao, Jie Gao, Jun Xia
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The ambitious goal of net-zero carbon emissions has driven an unprecedented expansion of global wind and solar power.1 Increasing reliance on these weather-dependent resources amplifies the variability in energy supply, exacerbating the supply-demand mismatches within power systems.2 To date, scientific and policy attention has focused disproportionately on the extreme energy deficit events, in which demand far exceeds supply, commonly termed “energy droughts.”3 However, the opposing extreme energy surplus events remain critically overlooked: periods of massive oversupply—energy floods.

In reality, the critical deficit in energy systems is often not in total electricity generation but in flexibility. Aggressive capacity expansion of wind and solar power aimed at mitigating energy droughts may paradoxically exacerbate the severity of energy floods, which pose systemic risks across three key dimensions. At the operational level, energy floods challenge electricity grid stability by overwhelming transmission lines and storage capacities, resulting in large-scale curtailment and resource waste.4 In 2024, for instance, wind and solar curtailment rates reached 10% in Chile and 8.5% in the United Kingdom,5 while in Tibet, the utilization rate for solar power was limited to merely 68.6%.6 At the market level, such massive oversupply frequently drives negative electricity prices, transforming generation from an asset into a liability.7 For example, the proportion of hours with negative electricity prices rose sharply across multiple regions from 2022 to 2024, including increases from 19% to 26% in South Australia, 1%–13% in Southern California, and <1%–8% in Finland.8 At the planning level, such revenue uncertainty creates a severe barrier to future investment in renewable projects,9 perversely stalling the capacity expansion required for carbon neutrality. While regional studies increasingly highlight the escalating risks posed by renewable energy surplus, a systematic global assessment of energy floods in wind-solar power systems remains lacking, particularly regarding their spatiotemporal regimes, dominant inputs, and mitigation strategies.

Here, we bridge this gap by developing a standardized framework to map global energy floods, utilizing the bias-adjusted ERA5 reanalysis (WFDE5, 1979–2019).10 We characterize distinct energy flood regimes across diverse regions and identify their dominant inputs. Furthermore, while spatial interconnection is widely recognized as an effective approach for managing wind and solar variability, we demonstrate its limitations in mitigating energy floods and outline region-specific planning implications. By shifting the analytical focus from scarcity to abundance, this study offers critical insights for reliable yet efficient decarbonized power systems.

Defining and mapping global energy floods

We develop a standardized framework to systematically identify global energy floods. Within each grid (1° × 1°), we assume a local energy system with a specific dependence on wind and solar power. This dependence is quantified as the proportion (here 50%) of days in which their aggregate supply meets or exceeds local demand. On the demand side, daily load is modeled as the sum of a constant baseline and temperature-sensitive components (i.e., heating/cooling loads during cold/hot days).2 On the supply side, daily wind and solar power output is derived from the capacity factor (CF) and installed capacity (IC). Specifically, CFs are calculated from surface wind speed, downwelling shortwave irradiance, and near-surface air temperature; ICs are then optimized to minimize surpluses while meeting the specified dependence level. Finally, the daily residual load (RL) series is calculated as the difference between energy demand and the combined wind and solar supply, where positive values indicate supply deficits while negative values imply surpluses (Figure 1A). Applying the proposed framework to the daily WFDE5 data, we reveal the global spatial patterns of energy flood frequency, duration, and intensity (Figures 1C–1E). These patterns remain robust across alternative thresholds (1.0/2.0 standard deviations) and dependence levels (25%/75%). High-latitude regions (e.g., Northern Europe and Russia) are characterized by a “high-frequency, high-intensity, short-duration” pattern, in which annual frequencies peak at ∼14.5 events and average intensities can exceed 4 times the mean daily load yet typically persist for only 1–2 days. In contrast, low- to mid-latitude regions (e.g., the Sahara and the Middle East) exhibit a “low-frequency, moderate-intensity, long-duration” regime, where the average event duration peaks at ∼4.2 days. Temporally, the peak occurrence of these events clusters around the onset of the local warm season (Figure 1F). This timing likely reflects a seasonal supply-demand mismatch that fuels energy floods: in high latitudes, the rapid decline in heating demand may coincide with sustained wind speeds, while in lower latitudes, intensifying solar insolation likely surpasses the yet-to-peak cooling demand.

To identify the dominant input of energy floods, we quantify the relative importance of wind speed, solar radiation, and temperature using extreme value theory (Figure 1G). This contribution analysis suggests three distinct flood regimes. In high-latitude regions, wind speed emerges as the dominant input, which likely reflects the influence of the westerlies and polar circulation, in which strong and transient winds drive the observed high-frequency, high-intensity, short-duration pattern. In certain arid and semi-arid regions (e.g., the Sahara and the Middle East), temperature is identified as the dominant input. Given the consistently strong solar radiation there, energy floods are largely governed by moderate temperatures that keep cooling loads relatively low. Unlike transient winds, the gradual variation of temperature (often associated with persistent high-pressure systems) likely sustains energy floods for consecutive days, explaining the observed long-duration events. Conversely, in many tropical regions (e.g., the Amazon and Southeast Asia [SEA]), solar radiation dominates. In contrast to arid regions, these areas experience consistently high temperatures and resulting high cooling demands. Consequently, the relative contribution of solar radiation is highlighted.

Limitations of spatial interconnection and strategic implications

To evaluate the effectiveness of spatial interconnection in mitigating energy floods, we propose a metric termed “neighboring absorption probability” (Figure 1B). This metric quantifies the likelihood that surrounding regions can accommodate excess power from the central grid during flood events. It is calculated as the average proportion of neighboring grids (within a specified radius) that are in supply deficit (i.e., positive RL) when the central grid experiences flood events. Higher probabilities indicate a greater theoretical potential for such extreme surpluses to be exported to and absorbed by adjacent deficit areas. Figure 1H illustrates the global distribution of neighboring absorption probability at a radius of 5° (∼550 km at the equator, selected as a representative scale for typical cross-regional transmission distances). The results reveal a challenging spatial pattern: regions with frequent, intense (e.g., Northern Europe) or persistent (e.g., the Sahara) energy floods tend to exhibit the lowest absorption probabilities (<10%). This implies a pronounced spatial synchronization in supply-demand mismatch patterns: local energy floods likely coincide with widespread regional power surpluses, severely limiting the possibility of neighboring absorption. Although higher values are observed in limited regions (e.g., parts of the tropics), the prevalence of low probabilities in flood-prone areas underscores the limitations of interconnection at this specific radius, motivating a systematic assessment of how this absorption potential varies across interconnection scales.

Figure 1I illustrates how the global median neighboring absorption probability and its marginal gain vary with transmission radius. The probability increases monotonically from less than 0.1% to approximately 26% as the radius expands from 1° to 30°. Initially, the marginal gain rises rapidly and peaks at radii of 5°–7° as decreasing weather similarity increases the percentage of neighboring deficit grids. However, even within this most efficient range, the absorption probability remains modest (<8%). Beyond this peak, the deficit percentage of newly added grids plateaus as distant weather conditions become nearly uncorrelated with the central grid, causing a sharp decline in the marginal gain. Eventually, for radii exceeding 23°, the marginal gain drops to its minimum (<0.5%) as expansion reaches geographical boundaries (e.g., oceans or Antarctica). It is informative to note that neighboring absorption probability represents the theoretical potential for mitigating energy floods through spatial interconnections, based solely on the surplus/deficit status of each grid. In reality, this potential may be further limited by physical and technical constraints, such as geographic restrictions on renewable deployment, transmission bottlenecks, competition for absorption capacity during concurrent regional floods, and magnitude mismatches where surpluses of flood grids vastly exceed neighboring deficits. Consequently, the actual effectiveness of spatial interconnection in mitigating energy floods is likely more limited than the derived probabilities suggest.

To derive region-specific strategic insights for mitigating energy floods, we map neighboring absorption probability and flood characteristics against regional gross domestic product (GDP) (Figure 1J). This analysis reveals four distinct regional regimes requiring tailored strategies. The first regime (bottom right) is characterized by high GDP but limited spatial absorption potential, notably eastern North America (ENA) and Western and Central Europe (WCE) (requiring 8° radii to achieve a 10% probability; high-frequency, high-intensity floods). Consequently, the strategic focus should shift from long-distance transmission toward internal flexibility, such as long-duration energy storage and demand response. In sharp contrast, a critical resource-capital mismatch exists in regions with lower GDP but higher spatial absorption potential, exemplified by Southeastern Africa (SEAF) and Northeastern Africa (NEAF) (probabilities >13% at a radius of 4°). While these areas exhibit greater mitigation potential through spatial interconnection, they lack the economic means for implementation, serving as a critical barrier to adaptation and underscoring the vital role of international cooperation. Meanwhile, for regions with both substantial economic scale and relatively higher spatial absorption potential, such as SEA and South Asia (SAS), regional interconnection may offer a possible pathway for flood mitigation. Finally, less-developed regions with limited spatial absorption potential are typified by geographically peripheral areas, such as the Russian Arctic (RAR) and southern South America (SSA). These regions face severe constraints where high-intensity floods coincide with negligible gains from spatial interconnection, suggesting that efforts should focus on enhancing localized absorption capabilities instead (e.g., green hydrogen and data centers).

Conclusion

Our global analysis reveals that energy floods in wind-solar systems are not random anomalies but structured extremes driven by distinct meteorological regimes. While spatial interconnection is commonly relied upon to manage renewable variability, we find its effectiveness in mitigating energy floods is severely constrained by strong regional synchronization of surplus/deficit status. Specifically, the global median probability of neighboring grids absorbing these extreme surpluses is less than 5% at a radius of 5°. Consequently, mitigation strategies must be tailored to distinct regional profiles rather than relying solely on spatial interconnection. While the daily scale was adopted to focus on prolonged events that are more likely to exceed local flexibility and require spatial interconnection, it fails to capture sub-daily extremes (e.g., midday solar surpluses). Additionally, other meteorological or circulation-related factors (e.g., the El Niño-Southern Oscillation) that might influence energy floods and their underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. Nevertheless, by providing a systematic global assessment, this study underscores the urgent necessity of actively and adaptively managing energy floods within the complex meteorology-energy-economy nexus to advance global decarbonization.
