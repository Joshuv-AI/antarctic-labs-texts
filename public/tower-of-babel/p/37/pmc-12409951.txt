Association between maternal circulating total triiodothyronine and subsequent risk of preeclampsia
Authors: Jiang-Nan Wu, Faustino R. Pérez-López, Ignacio Rodríguez, Li Yao, Lu-Yi Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo clarify the uncertain association between maternal total triiodothyronine (TT3) levels and preeclampsia risk.MethodsIn a hospital-based cohort of pregnant women with universal thyroid testing, we assessed the association between TT3 and preeclampsia using directed acyclic graphs (DAG) to control confounders.ResultsMaternal TT3 levels were associated with preeclampsia risk, with an adjusted odd ratio (OR) of 2.32 (95% confidence interval (CI) 2.02–2.68) in women with the highest TT3 quartile comparing to those in the lowest quartile. A J-shaped association was identified for early onset preeclampsia, with particularly strong association when thyroid-stimulating hormone exceeded 2.5 μIU/mL (OR, 17.22, 95% CI 4.52–65.60). Gestational age > 18 weeks at measurement significantly modified the effect of TT3 on preeclampsia risk, with an interaction OR of 3.22 (95% CI 1.56–6.62).ConclusionIncreased maternal TT3 levels were associated with preeclampsia. TT3 may contribute to preeclampsia pathogenesis and serve as a clinical biomarker, especially for early onset cases with thyroid dysfunction.

Introduction

Preeclampsia, affecting 2–4% of pregnancies globally, is a major cause of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. It impacts multiple organ systems in both mother and fetus, with lifelong negative consequences for both [1–5]. This imposes a significant disease burden, particularly on women in low- and middle-income countries or those otherwise disadvantaged [1].

The etiology of preeclampsia is complex, involving uteroplacental insufficiency, syncytiotrophoblast dysfunction, and angiogenic imbalance (e.g., soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1/placental growth factor ratio) [1, 6–8]. Maternal factors like abnormal uterine artery Doppler pulsatility index are also associated with risk [7, 8]. Furthermore, thyroid dysfunction in hypertensive pregnancies is linked to adverse neonatal outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight [9], and increased risks of miscarriage and preeclampsia [10, 11].

Although thyroid hormones are vital for fetal development and healthy pregnancy, a few studies specifically examine the link between thyroid hormone concentrations and preeclampsia risk. Maternal free thyroxine (FT4) and free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels are associated with adverse outcomes like preterm birth, congenital heart defects, and gestational diabetes mellitus [7, 12–14]. Triiodothyronine may be a superior biomarker to thyroxine [15], however, its specific role in preeclampsia pathogenesis remains underexplored. We previously found total triiodothyronine (TT3) to be the best thyroid hormone predictor of gestational diabetes mellitus, [16] suggesting its unique metabolic influence. Emerging evidence implicates that TT3 contributes to preeclampsia by regulating endothelial function and placental hypoxic responses [17, 18].

Preeclampsia is heterogeneous, with differing hemodynamics, origins, and clinical presentations related to gestational age at onset (e.g., < 34 vs. ≥ 34 weeks) [19]. The impact of maternal thyroid hormone on pregnancy outcomes may also be time-dependent. Before approximately 18 weeks of gestation, maternal thyroid hormone transferred via the placenta is the fetus's sole source, as the fetal thyroid gland is not yet functionally mature [20]. Our prior work showed that gestational age at thyroid testing (e.g., ≤ 18 vs. > 18 weeks) modified the association between FT4 and adverse fetal outcomes [7, 12].

Therefore, we analyzed a large cohort of pregnant women who underwent universal thyroid function testing to assess the association between TT3 and preeclampsia risk. We further investigated whether this relationship differs by preeclampsia subtype or gestational age, and selected covariates for adjusted models using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs).

Methods

Study design and data collection

All singleton pregnant women receiving routine prenatal care at Obstetrics and Gynecology Fudan University Hospital between 2014 and 2016 were recruited. Thyroid function tests were routinely performed at the first visit. Participants were included at their first obstetric consultation if they accepted thyroid function evaluation [16]. Clinical examinations during subsequent trimesters, delivery, and postpartum (6 weeks) were performed. General characteristics, thyroid function results, gestational complications, and pregnancy outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records. We excluded women lost to follow-up, those with thyroid dysfunctions and without TT3 measurement, and women tested after preeclampsia diagnosis. The Ethics Committee of Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University approved the study (No. 2017-35, 2017-35-C1) and waived informed consent due to its retrospective nature.

Gestational thyroid function measurements

Four milliliters of venous blood were collected in red-top coagulation tubes. After centrifugation at 2500–3000 rpm for 15 min, the serum supernatant was separated for measurement. Thyroid hormones were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (Roche Elecsys, Germany) [11], including thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), FT3, TT3, FT4, total thyroxine (TT4), thyroperoxidase antibody, TSH receptor antibody, and thyroglobulin antibody. All participants were tested at least once. For women tested multiple times (24.1%), only first-visit results were analyzed, as subsequent visits measured only FT4, TSH, and FT3 [11, 16]. Mean gestational age at testing was 13.6 weeks (SD: 3.5), with 13,618, 13,855, and 314 tests in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively.

TT3 showed the strongest association with preeclampsia (Table S1). Euthyroid women were defined as FT4 and TSH concentrations within gestational age-specific 2.5th–97.5th percentiles [16]. In the current study, antibody positivity thresholds were: thyroperoxidase antibody > 34 IU/mL, thyroglobulin antibody > 115 IU/mL, TSH receptor antibody > 1.75 IU/L [16]. Reference ranges were: TT3 0.8–2.0 ng/mL; TT4 5.1–14.1 µg/dL. TSH ranges: 1–12 weeks: 0.05–5.17 µIU/mL; 13–28 weeks: 0.39–5.22 µIU/mL; > 29 weeks: 0.60–6.84 µIU/mL. FT3 ranges: 1–12 weeks: 4.00–6.01 pmol/L; 13–28 weeks: 3.23–5.28 pmol/L; > 29 weeks: 3.14–5.04 pmol/L. FT4 ranges: 1–12 weeks: 12.91–22.35 pmol/L; 13–28 weeks: 9.81–17.26 pmol/L; > 29 weeks: 9.12–15.71 pmol/L.

Preeclampsia and eclampsia definitions

Pregnant women received routine prenatal care, including blood pressure, urine protein screening, and preeclampsia-related exams. Women at suspected risk underwent ambulatory blood pressure monitoring and 24 h urine protein quantification. Hypertensive disorders were diagnosed by Chinese Medical Association guidelines [21]: hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) and/or proteinuria ≥ 0.3 g/24 h, or a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3, or random urine protein positive after 20 weeks in previously normotensive women. In the present study, eclampsia (an advanced form of preeclampsia characterized by convulsions from unexplained causes), gestational hypertension (hypertension after 20 weeks without proteinuria), and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia (new onset proteinuria in preexisting hypertension) were classified as preeclampsia [22, 23]. Preeclampsia was subclassified as early- (< 34 weeks) or late-onset (≥ 34 weeks) based on diagnosis timing.

Covariates and DAG assessment

The covariates included maternal age (years), residence (Shanghai or other), parity (nulliparous or multiparous), natural conception (yes or no), gestational diabetes mellitus (yes or no), gestational diseases (yes or no), and fetal gender (male or female). General information was self-reported. Gestational diseases referred to anemia, chorioamnionitis, or cholestasis. A DAG was designed to identify confounders for adjustment, taking into account endogenous thyroid hormones as exposure and preeclampsia as outcome [24, 25].

Gestational diabetes mellitus was diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria, with fasting glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h ≥ 10 mmol/L, or 2-h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [26]. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 110.0 g/L at any antenatal visit. Chorioamnionitis and cholestasis were diagnosed per standard criteria [27, 28].

Data analyses

Thyroid hormone concentration outliers (> mean ± 4 SD) were excluded. General characteristics of women with and without TT3 measurements were compared to assess sample representativeness. TT3 concentrations were categorized into quartiles using gestational week-specific P 25, P 50, and P 75 (table S2). Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), categorical variables as frequencies (%). Differences between women with and without preeclampsia were assessed using Mann–Whitney U, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests.

Logistic regression models were conducted to estimate association between TT3 (continuous or quartiles) and preeclampsia, expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Multivariable models adjusted for maternal age and parity, which were identified by DAG as confounders. To mitigate potential bias from missing key variables (e.g., BMI), we also adjusted for all covariates to test robustness. Multinomial logistic regression was run to assess the association of TT3 with early- and late-onset preeclampsia. Sensitivity analyses restricted to euthyroid women or specific thyroid dysfunction subgroups (e.g., hypothyroidism). Given the potential for treatment bias in women with abnormal TSH levels and documented U-shaped associations between TSH and preeclampsia [29], we performed stratified analyses based on TSH levels (e.g., below the institutional reference range or > 2.5 mU/L). [30] Observed heterogeneity in the association between TT3 and early onset preeclampsia across TSH strata prompted formal interaction analysis.

Stratification analyses were also conducted by the thyroid antibody status and gestational age at thyroid testing. The stratified analysis excluded women with an unknown antibody status. We performed interaction analyses to examine the modification effect of TT3 and gestational age at measurement. Gestational ages were categorized into three groups by (e.g., < 12, 12–18, or > 18 weeks) based on previous studies [7, 12]. Restricted cubic spline regression models (with knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) were constructed to visually explore potential nonlinear relation between continuous TT3 levels and preeclampsia. All analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp) and R version 3.6.1 (R Foundation). A two-sided P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Most baseline variables were unbalanced between the included women and the excluded women or those without TT3 measurements (Table S3). Association betweenTT3 and preeclampsia

Logistic regression indicated an association between elevated TT3 levels and preeclampsia, with an adjusted OR of 3.05 (95% CI 2.60–3.57) for each 1 ng/mL increase in TT3. Women in the highest TT3 quantile (Q4) exhibited 2.44-fold (95%CI 2.12–2.81) risk compared to those in the Q1 (P for trend < 0.001) (Table 2). The association between TT3 and late-onset preeclampsia remained. For early onset preeclampsia, continuous TT3 exhibited suggestive association (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.33–4.81), though quartile-based analysis was non-significant (Table 3). The results did not change after full adjustment for all covariates. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses in euthyroid women showed no significant difference (Table S4). However, in pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction, the association between TT3 and late-onset preeclampsia was inconsistent. Null associations were found among women with isolated hyperthyroxinemia, subclinical or overt hypothyroidism (Table S5).

Stratification analysis according to TSH levels and thyroid antibody status

Stratification by TSH levels yielded unchanged results (Table S6). However, a modification effect of TT3 and TSH levels was identified on early onset preeclampsia (Table S7). TSH receptor antibody positivity was associated with TT3 (Table S8). The association between TT3 and late-onset preeclampsia remained among women negative for thyroid antibodies but was largely non-significant in antibody-positive individuals (Tables S9-S11).

Interaction between TT3 and gestational age at measurement

Interaction analyses revealed a significant effect modification between TT3 and gestational age at measurement on preeclampsia risk. Compared with measurements taken < 12 weeks, the adjusted ORs were 1.62 (95% CI 1.10–2.38) for 12–18 weeks and 3.22 (95% CI 1.56–6.62) for > 18 weeks. Results from the fully adjusted model remained unchanged (Table 4). The modifying effect persisted for late-onset preeclampsia but was not observed for early onset cases (Table S12). Nonlinear association between TT3 levels and preeclampsia

Restricted cubic spline revealed a nonlinear relationship between elevated TT3 levels and preeclampsia risk (Fig. 3A), with distinct pattern by subtype. Early onset preeclampsia exhibited a J-shaped association, with elevated risk at both low and high TT3 levels relative to median levels (Fig. 3B). Discussion

Main findings

In this large cohort, we demonstrated that elevated maternal TT3 levels were independently associated with preeclampsia risk after DAG-directed adjustment. The association revealed nonlinear dynamics with distinct early vs. late-onset heterogeneity. Early onset risk was modified by TSH levels, while gestational age at measurement modified late-onset risk. These findings may implicate TT3 in preeclampsia pathogenesis and support its potential as a clinical biomarker.

Context within previous literatures

Maternal thyroid function is critical for placental development, endothelial homeostasis, and blood pressure regulation during pregnancy, suggesting a link between thyroid dysfunction and preeclampsia [20, 31–33]. However, inconsistencies persist across studies due to variable definitions of thyroid abnormalities and gestational timing of assessment [11, 34–36]. While meta-analyses associate subclinical hypothyroidism with preeclampsia [29], findings on TT3 are conflicting [38–40]. One meta-analysis reported null association with preeclampsia (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.95–1.24) [37], while another meta-analysis found significantly higher TT3 levels in preeclamptic women (63.55 vs. 62.22, standardized mean difference 0.88, 95% CI 0.41–1.36) [38]. In the individual-participant data meta-analysis, methodological variations across cohorts, including heterogeneous populations, different preeclampsia definitions (incidence: 1.8%), and inconsistent TT3 handling protocols may explain the observed discrepancies [37]. However, the positive association between TT3 z-scores and gestation hypertension (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–0.33) aligns with our findings.

As the main active thyroid ligand, TT3 more strongly predicts gestational diabetes mellitus than other thyroid hormones [16]. However, its association with preeclampsia remains underexplored due to limited clinical measurement. Our study findings position TT3 as a potential mediator subclinical hypothyroidism/TSH abnormalities and preeclampsia risk, possibly through dysregulated placental deiodinase activity impacting extravillous trophoblast invasiveness and decidual angiogenesis [29].

Potential mechanisms underlying the association

Critical to normal placentation, spiral artery remodeling establishes high flow, low resistance vessels essential for maternal–fetal exchange is critical for normal placentation [41]. This process—regulated through fetal–maternal crosstalk—involves trophoblast differentiation, vascular remodeling, and angiogenesis, with dysregulation predisposing to preeclampsia [42]. Our findings mechanistically align with TT3’s multifaceted roles. TT3 synergizes with epidermal growth factor to drive trophoblast proliferation and hCG-mediated syncytialization [43], while enhances extravillous trophoblast invasiveness through upregulated expression of oncofetal fibronectin, integrin α5β1, and MMP2/MMP3 [44–46]. Furthermore, TT3 promotes decidual angiogenesis by stimulating vascular endothelial growth factor-A, angiopoietin-2, and interleukin-10 [17], and suppresses trophoblast apoptosis via Fas and Fas ligand downregulation [18]. Collectively, these pathways substantiate TT3’s association with preeclampsia observed in our study.

Maternal TT3 levels exhibited divergent dose–response relationships with preeclampsia subtypes. This disparity peaked at TT3 > 2.35 ng/mL, with substantially higher adjusted ORs for early onset versus late-onset preeclampsia (6.0 vs. 3.0). The differential risk patterns likely reflect pathophysiological heterogeneity between subtypes, partly attributed to a greater cardiovascular morbidity burden characterizing early onset preeclampsia [47], and further amplified by subtype-specific TT3 modulation of key angiogenic growth factors and cytokines [17].

In the present study, the strongest association between elevated TT3 and preeclampsia risk emerged when measured after 18 weeks, a pattern consistent with known trimester-specific TT3 variations [17]. This temporal effect likely reflects fetal thyroid maturation, as functional competency is attained at 18–20 weeks [43]. Our prior findings on gestational age-dependent FT4 effects on preterm birth and congenital heart defects [7, 12] support broader temporal specificity in thyroid hormone actions. Before fetal thyroid maturation, maternal thyroid abnormalities predominantly impact fetal development. Post-maturation, as fetal thyroid autonomy reduces placental transfer demand, maternal thyroid effects become increasingly prominent. Further studies are warranted to clarify the modifying effects of gestational age at measurement and TT3 levels on maternal health.

Clinical implications of the findings

Our findings have clinical importance. First, the heterogeneity in the association between TT3 levels and preeclampsia subtypes, along with the modification effects of gestational age at measurement and TSH levels, provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying this disorder. Second, given that universal thyroid screening remains non routine [30] and TT3 is infrequently assessed, our data suggest targeted TT3 measurement after 18 weeks could enhance late-onset preeclampsia prediction. Integration with established risk factors may improve perinatal risk stratification. Furthermore, the pronounced association between TT3 and early onset preeclampsia in women with TSH > 2.5 µIU/mL warrants prioritized TT3 monitoring in this high-risk subgroup for early intervention.

Limitations and strengths

The present study had some limitations. First, due to constraints in data availability, important confounders, such as maternal BMI, smoking status, family history of preeclampsia, and socioeconomic status, were not collected. Maternal BMI is particularly critical as it may regulate deiodinase activity and influence triiodothyronine T3 level [48]. In addition, information on treatment (e.g., levothyroxine) due to thyroid dysfunction was not available. Although we acknowledge that omitting these variables might introduce bias, further analyses adjusting for all available covariates showed that the core associations remained. Differences in the association between TT3 and preeclampsia among pregnant women with various forms of thyroid dysfunction may reflect the influence of specific thyroid disorder and/or its treatment. Further studies are required to address these issues.

Second, although maternal thyroid function assessment is not routinely recommended for pregnancy screening, our findings of its strong association with preeclampsia after 18 weeks suggest clinical potential for identifying high-risk women. Third, concerns regarding the representativeness of the single-center sample and limited statistical power in certain subgroup analyses due to small sample size should be acknowledged. Finally, thyroid hormones were measured using immunoassay rather than the gold standard of mass spectrometry [49]. This could potentially impact the precision of results for samples with low concentrations, which may affect reliability of the results. In addition, although thyroid function was tested before the diagnosis of preeclampsia, results should be cautiously interpreted, since this was an observational study.

Despite limitations, this study offers substantial strengths. First: it represents a large cohort from a major Chinese hospital with systematically measured TT3 levels and preeclampsia outcomes rigorously diagnosed per Chinese Medical Association Guideline. This design ensures robust statistical power and credible effect estimates. Our findings reveal the key role of TT3 in maternal complications and underscore its clinical relevance. Critically, while both thyroxine and triiodothyronine are bound to carrier proteins, only free fractions exhibit tissue bioavailability [50], yet circulating TT3 remains a valid proxy for tissue-level activity in preeclampsia contexts. Finally, despite potential mediator adjustment, evaluation using DAGs reduces residual spatial confounding [51].

Conclusion

In this large Chinese cohort, we revealed a nonlinear association between increased maternal TT3 levels and preeclampsia risk, with effect heterogeneity across disease subtypes and gestational ages. Moreover, TSH might modify the association of TT3 on early onset preeclampsia. These findings elucidate novel pathophysiological pathways and provide evidence for clinical decisions relating to thyroid function tests and preeclampsia screening.
