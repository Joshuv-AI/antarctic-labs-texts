FOXP genes regulate Purkinje cell diversity and cerebellar morphogenesis
Authors: Nagham Khouri-Farah, Qiuxia Guo, Thomas A. Perry, Ryan Dussault, James Y. H. Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cerebellar Purkinje cells (PCs), the sole output neurons of the cerebellar cortex, are essential for motor coordination, learning and circuit formation. While functionally diverse, the extent of PC heterogeneity and the molecular drivers of this diversity remain unclear. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we identified at least 11 molecularly distinct PC subtypes in the embryonic mouse cerebellum. Spatial reconstruction revealed that these subtypes are organized in embryonic patterns that predict key features of adult cerebellar architecture, including longitudinal stripes and lobular specificities. PC-subtype identity is defined by the combinatorial expression of Foxp1, Foxp2 and Foxp4. Genetic deletion of Foxp1 and Foxp2 disrupts PC diversification and cerebellar patterning, including the loss of a Foxp1+ subtype and the failure of cerebellar hemisphere formation. Foxp1+ PCs are enriched in the fetal human cerebellum but are rare in chick, suggesting a role in cerebellar evolution. These findings uncover early PC diversification and identify Foxp1+ PCs as critical regulators of cerebellar hemispheric development.

Main

Despite its uniform cytoarchitecture, the human cerebellar cortex is partitioned into discrete functional domains as shown by neuroimaging. Notably, the cerebellar hemisphere, which is a unique feature in mammals and is greatly enlarged in primates1, participates in cognitive and affective processes2. Alterations in activity originating from the cerebellar hemisphere result in autism spectrum disorder-like behaviors in both mice and humans3,4. However, the developmental parcellation of the cerebellar cortex and formation of the cerebellar hemisphere remain poorly understood.

Anatomical, molecular and physiological evidence suggests that the mammalian cerebellum is intricately patterned and organized as a series of transverse zones and longitudinal stripes5,6. Transverse fissures divide the cerebellar cortex into lobes and lobules along the anteroposterior (AP) axis, while variations in lobulation delineate the following three domains from medial to lateral: the medial vermis, paravermis and hemisphere1. The tripartite division is further subdivided by alternating longitudinal stripes of Purkinje cells (PCs) that express Aldoc or Plcb4 (refs. 5,6). These parasagittal PC stripes align with afferent terminal fields and efferent projections, forming the basis of functional partitioning of the cerebellar cortex5,6. Expression studies, using a limited number of markers, have revealed molecularly distinct PC subtypes in the developing mouse cerebellum7–11. Heterogeneous PC subtypes can provide a framework for organizing cerebellar afferent topographies, as they are the earliest-born and sole output neurons of the cerebellar cortex5,6. Although single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has the potential to systematically characterize the molecular heterogeneity of PCs, a crucial obstacle is the loss of spatial context inherent to the technology. This difficulty is exacerbated by complex morphological transformations of the cerebellum during the perinatal stages. Furthermore, it is challenging to correlate embryonic PC subtypes with their adult counterparts due to the dynamic expression of markers in PC subpopulations. This creates a barrier to specifically perturbing PC diversification to assess its impact on cerebellar development.

Here we integrated spatial protein expression analysis and scRNA-seq to identify 11 molecularly distinct PC subtypes, map them in three-dimensional (3D) space and align them with alternating Aldoc-positive and negative (Aldoc+/−) stripes in the adult mouse cerebellum. These subtypes are defined by the combinatorial expression of three Foxp genes (Foxp1, Foxp2 and Foxp4). Mouse conditional knockout (cKO) experiments demonstrated that Foxp1 and Foxp2 are essential for PC diversification. Their removal resulted in progressive reduction and eventual loss of the cerebellar hemispheres, coinciding with disruption of a specific PC subtype. Remarkably, this subtype is substantially expanded in the human cerebellum and largely absent in the chick cerebellum. Together, these findings demonstrate that PC diversification controls pattern formation in the cerebellar cortex and contributes to the evolution of the cerebellum.

Results

Identifying 11 PC subtypes in the embryonic mouse cerebellum

PCs are produced between embryonic days (E)10 and E13 in mice12. We have previously described at least five molecularly distinct PC subtypes at E13.5, indicating that PC heterogeneity is established soon after PCs are born13. To characterize PC heterogeneity at the later embryonic stage, we performed additional scRNA-seq of the mouse cerebella at E16.5 and E18.5 (Extended Data Fig. 1a). We assigned identity to each cell cluster from the combined dataset according to known cell-type-specific markers, recovering all major cerebellar cell types (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). We reiterated the cell clustering of PCs recognized by the pan-PC marker Foxp2 (refs. 13,14) and identified 11 PC subtypes, provisionally named PC1–PC11, based on their relative abundance (Fig. 1b–d, Extended Data Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 2). The salient molecular features distinguishing the PC subtypes were cell surface adhesion molecules, including eight members of the cadherin superfamily (Fig. 1e, Extended Data Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 2). To evaluate the robustness of the analysis, we re-analyzed two published scRNA-seq datasets of the E16–E18 mouse cerebella15,16 and identified similar heterogeneity and abundance of PC subtypes (Extended Data Fig. 1e–g).

On the basis of the Allen Developing Mouse Brain Atlas and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), we approximated the endogenous positions of seven PC subtypes in the embryonic cerebellum (Fig. 1f–h), revealing that these subtypes form discrete clusters. For instance, PC1 cells, characterized by Foxp1 expression, were confined to the lateral-most region of the cerebellum, whereas PC11 cells, largely lacking Foxp2 transcripts, were restricted to the flocculonodular lobe (Fig. 1f,g and Supplementary Fig. 1). Our findings indicate the presence of at least 11 molecularly distinct PC subtypes with regional specificity in the mouse cerebellum before birth.

Unsupervised mapping of PC subtypes to native space

Assigning all 11 PC subtypes to their original spatial contexts using conventional expression analysis was challenging because each subtype was mostly defined by combinatorial markers (Fig. 1d,f,h). We attempted to develop an objective and autonomous method to map cell clusters identified by scRNA-seq to their 3D spatial contexts within tissue. Through single-cell analysis of the transcriptome and chromatin landscape, we previously identified the gene regulatory network and its driver genes in various cerebellar cell lineages13,17. We hypothesized that, once spatially resolved, these driver genes could act as anchors to pinpoint their regulated gene networks within their native spatial context. This approach would enable the assignment of cell clusters identified by scRNA-seq to their original locations in the cerebellum. To accomplish this, we developed a method by using cyclic immunofluorescence (CycIF) to profile the expression of 30–50 proteins in serial cerebellar sections at single-cell resolution (Fig. 2a). By integrating single-cell spatial protein profiling with scRNA-seq data, we inferred the identity of individual cells within the sections (Fig. 2a). The consecutive sections were then aligned based on shared cellular features18,19, resulting in a reconstructed 3D structure (Fig. 2b). We applied scANKRS and determined the topographic distribution of the 11 PC subtypes (Fig. 2b). While most PC-subtype clusters appeared near the cerebellar surface, PC3, PC4 and PC5 were positioned beneath the surface, closer to the ventricular zone at E17.5, potentially representing PCs in transit (Fig. 2c,c′). We confirmed the reconstructed spatial configuration of the PC subtypes through volume imaging using light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM), following immunolabeling and tissue clearing (Fig. 2d).

To test the robustness and reproducibility of scANKRS, we transferred the transcriptome to individual cells within sections. As expected, the imputed expression patterns of the mRNAs closely matched those of their corresponding protein products (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). We validated the imputed spatial expression of a few selected molecules using FISH20 (Extended Data Fig. 2c). By relying only on imputed genes (excluding those initially analyzed by CycIF), we successfully identified all major cerebellar cell groups, observing remarkably consistent abundance across samples (Extended Data Fig. 2d,e). Thus, by integrating scANKRS with 3D reconstruction and volume imaging, we reliably determined the 3D positioning of the PC subtypes within the embryonic mouse cerebellum.

Embryonic PCs diversity predicts adult PC regional specificity

The mammalian cerebellum exhibits an evolutionarily conserved pattern of alternating longitudinal stripes of Aldoc+ or Aldoc− PCs5. Single-nucleus transcriptome profiling has not only confirmed these two main PC groups in the adult mouse cerebellum21,22, but also revealed regional specificities among the PCs22. By aligning the embryonic and adult scRNA-seq datasets, we found that most of the 11 PC subtypes corresponded to either Aldoc+ or Aldoc− PCs (Fig. 3a,b). When the inferred Aldoc identity was projected in the 3D reconstructed cerebellum according to the corresponding PC subtypes, the Aldoc+ and Aldoc− precursors formed alternating longitudinal stripes, mirroring the adult pattern (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, aligning adult regional PC identities with embryonic PCs showed that different PC subtypes were associated with specific lobules (Fig. 3d). To confirm the relationship between PC-subtype clusters and longitudinal stripes, we performed 3D imaging of immunolabeled cerebella from E14.5 to P6. We also found that many PC-subtype clusters and stripes were separated by PC-sparse gaps—regions with a reduced density of PC soma and processes—visible by immunostaining of nuclear (Foxp proteins) or cytoplasmic (Pchd10 and Plcb4) proteins (Fig. 3e–i, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplementary Video 2). Four major PC-sparse gaps extended along the entire AP axis, persisting from E14.5 to P6, while several minor gaps emerged between E17.5 and P6 (Fig. 3e–i, Extended Data Fig. 3 and Supplemental Video 2). Among the major gaps, the lateral-most gap separated PC1 from the other subtypes, while the medial-most gap was constituted by the boundaries of several PC subtypes (Fig. 3e–g). We propose that these two PC-sparse gaps delineate the junctions between the hemisphere and paravermis, and between the paravermis and vermis (see below). The other two major gaps were along the border between PC7 and PC2 in the paravermis, revealing that PC7 contributes to the 5+ Aldoc stripe in lobule VII, the Pcdh10+ domain in CrI and the posterior paraflocculus (Fig. 3e–i and Extended Data Fig. 3). These PC-sparse gaps, potentially representing lineage-restriction boundaries, serve as reliable landmarks for linking PC subtypes from the embryonic to postnatal cerebellum. In summary, by correlating molecular features with cellular landmarks, we relate embryonic PC-subtype-specific clusters to longitudinal PC stripes in the adult cerebellum.

Foxp1 and Foxp2 control cerebellar development and function

Different PC subtypes exhibited distinct combinatorial expression patterns and varying levels of Foxp1, Foxp2 and Foxp4, which belong to a subfamily of forkhead-box transcription factors (Fig. 4a,b). Analyses using scRNA-seq data17,23 and immunofluorescence (IF) indicated that all three Foxp genes were predominantly expressed in the postmitotic PCs (Extended Data Fig. 4). Unlike global knockouts of Foxp1 and Foxp2, which cause embryonic or early postnatal lethality, En1-lineage-specific deletion allowed survival. Global Foxp2 knockout abolishes ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) in pups separated from their mothers25,26,28. Remarkably, En1cre-mediated deletion of either a single allele or both alleles of Foxp2, or deletion of Foxp1, substantially reduced USVs in mice (Fig. 4c,d)29. Although Foxp1 is expressed in glutamatergic neurons within the cerebellar nuclei13, its removal from these neurons using Atoh1-Cre30 did not affect USVs (Fig. 4e). These data suggest that Foxp1 and Foxp2 are crucial for cerebellar function, probably acting through PCs, and contribute to the regulation of USVs. However, we cannot rule out contributions from noncerebellar cell types affected by En1cre-mediated deletion, such as the periaqueductal gray, a region implicated in USV control31.

We next examined cerebellar morphology and histology in Foxp1/Foxp2 mutant mice between P21 and P23, when cerebellar morphogenesis is mostly complete32. Although overall cerebellar foliation was preserved in Foxp1-cKO mice, the paraflocculus exhibited an abnormal branch, displacing parts of the CrI, CrII and Prm lobules (Fig. 4f–g′,j,k). The Foxp1-cKO phenotype was apparent at P6 (Fig. 4l–m′ and Extended Data Fig. 5a), highlighting the critical role of Foxp1 in the formation of the cerebellar hemisphere.

In Foxp2-cKO mice, although the cerebellar vermis was relatively intact, the central lobe was markedly reduced (Extended Data Fig. 5b). By contrast, the cerebellar hemisphere, along with the paraflocculus, was diminished in Foxp2-cKO mice and nearly absent in Foxp1/Foxp2 double cKO (Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO) mice (Fig. 4h,i,n,o and Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). In Foxp2-deficient mice, the flocculonodular lobe was notably enlarged, forming a continuous folium rather than a distinct lobe X in the vermis and flocculus laterally, as observed in wild-type (WT) mice (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Volumetric measurements confirmed that the flocculus was substantially larger in Foxp2-cKO mice than that in littermate controls at E17.5 (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Differential changes in the paraflocculus and flocculus due to the loss of Foxp1 and Foxp2 were further validated by IF for Hspb1 to mark PCs and Tlx3 to mark granule cells in the paraflocculus (Extended Data Fig. 5e). Despite these extensive morphological defects, cortical lamination and cytoarchitecture were remarkably intact in the cerebella lacking Foxp1 and Foxp2 (Fig. 4j–o and Extended Data Fig. 5b–c). Thus, Foxp1 and Foxp2 are essential regulators of regional and lobular identity in the developing mouse cerebellum.

Cerebellar hemisphere deleted without Foxp1 and Foxp2

As the cerebellar hemisphere is defined by a unique set of gyri and fissures, we asked whether Foxp2 deletion alters its regional identity rather than merely its morphology. PCs in the cerebellar hemisphere are primarily innervated by climbing fibers from the principal olive (PO) and dorsal accessory olive (DAO), two subnuclei of the inferior olive in the medulla oblongata5,6. As neuronal survival typically relies on proper connectivity33, we hypothesized that the loss of hemispheric identity would lead to a loss of PO and DAO neurons. Although Foxp2-cKO mice showed normal inferior olive histology and Foxp2 expression at P2, consistent with the absence of En1cre activity in this region (Supplementary Fig. 2), we observed increased apoptosis (marked by activated Casp3) in PO and DAO at P2, followed by substantial neuronal loss by P4 (Fig. 5a–c). By P25, PO and DAO were markedly reduced, while other subnuclei remained largely intact (Fig. 5d–e′). To further corroborate the loss of the hemisphere in Foxp2-deficient cerebella, we analyzed ML patterning of the cerebellum using IF for Aldoc and Plcb4. The vermis shows a characteristic arrangement of four Aldoc+/Plcb4− stripes (1+, 2+, 3+ and 4+) interspersed with Aldoc−/Plcb4+ stripes (1−, 2− and 3−) in lobules VII and VIII on either side of the midline (Fig. 5f). Flanking the vermis, the paravermis and hemisphere display six alternating Aldoc+/Plcb4− stripes (4+, 4b+, 5a+, 5+, 6+ and 7+) and Aldoc−/Plcb4+ stripes in CrII and Prm lobes (Fig. 5f). In Foxp2-deficient mice, although the alternating Aldoc+/Plcb4− and Aldoc−/Plcb4+ stripes persisted in lobules VII and VIII, the 3− stripe was either reduced or shifted laterally, creating a broader Aldoc+/Plcb4− domain in the cerebellar vermis (Fig. 5g,h). Notably, the alternating Aldoc+ and Aldoc− stripes lateral to the vermis were replaced by a broad Aldoc−/Plcb4+ domain in both Foxp2-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO cerebella (Fig. 5g,h). An Aldoc+ region, likely representing the residual hemisphere, was observed in the lateral-most area of Foxp2-cKO cerebella but was absent in Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO cerebella, supporting the stepwise reduction of the cerebellar hemisphere in these mutants (Fig. 5g,h).

Foxp1-cKO mice exhibited subtle defects in the lateral cerebellum. Stripe 5+ was substantially widened and became broader than stripe 6+, reversing the pattern observed in littermate controls (Fig. 5i,j). When normalized to the total area, the region lateral to stripe 5− in lobules CrII and Prm was substantially reduced in the Foxp1-cKO cerebella at P25 (Fig. 5i,k). These findings indicate that Foxp1 deletion selectively impairs the development of the lateral-most region of the cerebellum, consistent with its restricted expression in the cerebellar anlage.

In summary, our data demonstrate that Foxp2 and Foxp1 are essential for the development of the cerebellar hemisphere.

Foxp1 and Foxp2 concertedly regulate PC diversification

To test our hypothesis that Foxp1 and Foxp2 regulate cerebellar development by controlling PC diversification, we quantified the abundance of various cerebellar cell types in Foxp1-cKO, Foxp2-cKO, Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO and their littermate controls at E16.5 with scANKRS. The relative abundance of the major cell types did not differ substantially among genotypes (Extended Data Fig. 6a). The total number and proportion of PCs in individual coronal sections at the corresponding AP positions were similar between the WT and Foxp2-deficient cerebella, with only a slight, nonsubstantial reduction at the two most anterior positions (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). In contrast, PC-subtype composition was substantially altered by Foxp2 deletion, with deduced PC1 and PC7, and increased PC9 and PC11 (Fig. 6a–b′). PC7 was similarly affected in Foxp2-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO mice, while PC1 exhibited a stepwise reduction, paralleling the gradual loss of the cerebellar hemisphere in these mutant mice. To confirm the alterations in PC subtypes caused by the loss of Foxp1 and Foxp2, we performed RNAscope FISH to examine additional markers that were associated with specific PC subtypes—Zfhx4 for PC1, Sulf1 for PC2, Lmo4 for PC7 and Sfrp1 for PC11. As expected, Foxp1 transcripts were mostly absent from the Foxp1-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO cerebella (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Zfhx4 marked PC1 cells and dentate nuclear neurons in Foxp1-cKO cerebella and revealed selective reduction and complete loss of PC1 in Foxp2-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO cerebella, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 6a). The expression of Sulf1 and Sfrp1 distinguished PC2 and PC11, respectively, and demonstrated an increase in PC11 cells in the Foxp2-deficient cerebella (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Lmo4 transcripts, which mark PC7, were absent in Foxp2-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Notably, the combinatorial expression of Lmo4, Ndst4 and Nrp2 revealed at least three PC1 subgroups, two of which were notably altered by the loss of Foxp1, suggesting that PC1 could be potentially subdivided (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Our data show that interactions between Foxp1 and Foxp2 are crucial for PC diversification.

Using 3D reconstruction from serial sections and volume imaging, we corroborated the reduction in PC1 and PC7, and the expansion of PC9 and PC11 in the Foxp2-deficient cerebella (Fig. 6d,e). In the absence of Foxp2, the two PC9 clusters in the vermis and paravermis expanded and became closer to each other, owing to the reduction in intervening PC clusters (Fig. 6b,d,e). We applied scANKRS to transfer Aldoc+ and Aldoc− adult PC identities to the reconstructed 3D space in WT and mutant cerebella at E16.5 (Fig. 6d′). The resulting model accurately predicted the altered longitudinal striped pattern observed in adult Foxp2-cKO and Foxp1/Foxp2-cKO cerebella (Figs. 5f–h and 6d′). These findings further support the notion that the embryonic spatial distribution of PC subtypes foreshadows adult Aldoc+/− stripes organization.

At E17.5, several PC-sparse gaps in the prospective paravermis of the Foxp2-deficient cerebella disappeared (Fig. 6e), suggesting the loss of PC compartment boundaries. To assess the impact on cerebellar growth and morphogenesis, we measured ML and AP lengths using 3D reconstruction. While AP length was unchanged in the vermis, it was substantially increased in the paravermis of the Foxp2-cKO cerebella; ML length was substantially reduced (Fig. 6f). These findings indicate that Foxp1 and Foxp2 cooperate to define PC-subtype identities and underscore the importance of PC diversification in cerebellar compartmentalization, growth and morphogenesis.

To investigate PC heterogeneity in the chick cerebellum, we conducted scRNA-seq on embryonic cerebella daily between E6 and E9, after the peak period of PC genesis (E4–E5.5)36. This yielded 13,064 cells, with a median of 2,338 cells per stage (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). Integrating scRNA-seq with spatial protein profiling, we identified six molecularly distinct PC subtypes organized as longitudinal stripes at E9 and E11 (Fig. 7d–f and Extended Data Fig. 8c). The rarest subtype expressed FOXP1 and ZFHX4, and resembled PC1 in the mouse and human (Fig. 7g and Extended Data Fig. 8a). Therefore, there is a stepwise increase of PC1 from chick to mouse to human, correlating cerebellar hemisphere enlargement (Fig. 7h). Notably, PC1-like cells resided beneath the main PC layer (Fig. 7f,g and Supplementary Video 3). Pulse-chase labeling with 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) revealed that PC1-like cells were born between E3.5 and E5.0, before the final PC production wave, suggesting that their unique position reflects intrinsic migration properties rather than birth timing (Extended Data Fig. 8d–g).

Collectively, our data show that PC1 cells are enriched in the human cerebellum and scarce in the chick. This FOXP1+ PC subtype likely has a pivotal role in the evolutionary expansion of the cerebellar hemisphere.

Discussion

In this study, we used scRNA-seq to characterize PC heterogeneity and identified at least 11 molecularly distinct PC subtypes in the mouse cerebellum during late gestation (E16.5–E18.5). By integrating embryonic and adult scRNA-seq with spatial proteomics, we demonstrate that individual PC subtypes are confined to specific areas of the cerebellar cortex, predicting the regional specificity of PCs in adults. Additionally, we show that Foxp1 and Foxp2 regulate PC-subtype differentiation, with disruptions in these genes leading to altered cerebellar patterning. Notably, a Foxp1-expressing PC subtype is essential for cerebellar hemisphere development, offering new insights into cerebellar patterning and evolution.

The cerebellar cortex is organized into parallel circuit modules, each comprising distinct assemblies of PCs, inferior olivary neurons and cerebellar nuclear neurons5,6. A long-standing hypothesis suggests that parcellation of the cerebellar cortex is based on scaffolding provided by diverse PC subtypes5,6. Ref. 37 identified 54 PC clusters in the prenatal mouse cerebellum, which were characterized by their position, shape and expression of a few molecular markers. However, applying this classification in comparative analyses is challenging due to the variability in sectioning planes between studies, especially when comparing cerebella with different morphologies across developmental stages and genetic backgrounds. By combining scRNA-seq and spatial proteomics, we established a workflow to map the topographic distribution of the PC subtypes identified by scRNA-seq. Notably, this approach enables precise and efficient alignment of PC subtypes across different developmental stages, genetic conditions and species. While the current classification of the 11 PC subtypes offers sufficient granularity to facilitate robust comparative studies, further subdivisions of certain PC subtypes may be possible. Notably, our data suggest that PC1 may contain subgroups, with the borders of these subgroups coinciding with the emergence of cerebellar fissures. Future studies are needed to determine whether these PC1 subgroups contribute to the lobulation of the hemispheres, encompassing Sim, CrI/II and Prm lobules.

The longitudinal organization of distinct PC stripes is evolutionarily conserved and functionally important5,6. However, linking embryonic PC clusters to adult PC stripes has been challenging due to the gradual and protracted nature of longitudinal PC stripe development38. Furthermore, the PC stripe pattern appeared refractory to experimental manipulations39–43, and the mechanism driving stripe development has remained elusive. Here we demonstrate that integrative scRNA-seq analyses can accurately predict adult PC phenotypes from embryonic subtypes (Fig. 3a–d). Perturbations in PC diversification caused by the removal of Foxp1 and Foxp2 altered PC stripe patterns as predicted (Fig. 6d,d′), shedding light on the molecular mechanisms underlying stripe formation. Prior studies have shown that excitatory cerebellar nuclear neurons regulate PC numbers44. We found that the loss of Foxp1 and Foxp2 did not result in obvious defects in excitatory cerebellar nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating that Foxp1 and Foxp2 regulate cerebellar development primarily through the control of PC diversification. Remarkably, we show that PC subtypes exhibit regional specificity across cerebellar lobules (Fig. 3d). For instance, PC11 is associated with flocculus, and its increase corresponds to an enlarged flocculus in the Foxp2-deficient cerebella. These findings suggest that PC-subtype heterogeneity contributes not only to ML stripe patterning but also to AP patterning, potentially influencing the stereotyped foliation of the cerebellum.

Mutations in FOXP1 and FOXP2 are linked to overlapping developmental brain disorders, including autism spectrum disorders and speech impairments in humans45. However, the mechanisms underlying these mutations and their primary target cell types in the brain remain unclear. Our findings show that removing Foxp1 and Foxp2 from the cerebellum results in vocalization defects and other behavioral changes in mice (Fig. 4c–e)29. Notably, our findings suggest that FOXP1-expressing cells, specifically PC1, have a crucial role in the development and evolution of the cerebellar hemisphere, which is involved in cognitive and affective processes2. This raises the possibility that alterations in cerebellar modularity, particularly in the cerebellar hemisphere, may contribute to syndromes caused by FOXP1 and FOXP2 mutations in humans.

FOXP proteins belong to a subfamily of forkhead-box transcription factors capable of forming homodimers and heterodimers46, suggesting that FOXP dimerization may establish a combinatorial ‘FOXP code’ to control cell identity and connectivity of individual PC subtypes. Our preliminary data revealed that, although Foxp4 deletion alone did not result in major cerebellar developmental defects, the removal of Foxp4 in the absence of Foxp1 and Foxp2 exacerbated the disruption of PC diversification. These findings support a model in which a combinatorial FOXP code drives the diversification of PC subtypes, ensuring proper cerebellar modularity and connectivity.

In summary, our study provides new insights into the molecular control of PC diversification, which contributes to compartmentalization, growth and morphogenesis of the cerebellar cortex. We propose that adult cerebellar compartments with similar molecular profiles, axonal projections and functional localizations likely originate from a common early PC compartment. Further research is needed to clarify the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving cerebellar compartmentalization and to explore the relationship between PC-subtype heterogeneity and their specific connectivity patterns.

Methods

Animals and tissue preparation

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Connecticut School of Medicine (protocol number AP-201154-0127) and were conducted in accordance with institutional and National Institutes of Health guidelines. Mice were housed on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Genetically modified mouse strains, including Atoh1-Cre30, En1-Cre47, Foxp1-flxoed48 and Foxp2-floxed24, were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were performed using male and female mice on a CD1 outbred background, aged E12.5 to P30. Embryonic day (E) 0.5 was designated as the day a vaginal plug was detected. Primer sequences and PCR genotyping protocols are available on the JAX website. Fertilized chicken eggs were obtained from the University of Connecticut poultry farm and incubated at 38 °C until the desired embryonic stages; day 1 of incubation was marked as E0. Both sexes were pooled for histological, scRNA-seq and USV studies. Animals were assigned to experimental groups based on genotype. No additional randomization or blocking was used during data collection or analysis. Experimental conditions and sample processing were conducted in parallel, where possible, to minimize batch effects.

Mouse brains were dissected in ice-cold PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) before P2. After P2, transcardial perfusion with PBS and PFA was performed before submersion fixation. The brains were cryoprotected, frozen in optimal cutting temperature freezing medium (Sakura Finetek) and sectioned to 12–20 µm using a cryostat microtome (Leica, CM3050S). The sections were mounted on ColorFrost Plus microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

USV tests

For the USV test, the mouse pup was separated from its mother and littermates and placed in a sound-attenuating chamber with an ultrasonic frequency detector set to 60 kHz. After a 60-s habituation period, the analysis commenced. Ultrasonic calls emitted for more than 6 min were recorded and analyzed for both number and duration using UltraVox XT 3.2 (Noldus).

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization

Hybridization chain reaction was performed as previously described20. Briefly, tissue sections were washed with PBS, equilibrated in hybridization buffer and incubated overnight at 37 °C with the probes. After hybridization, the slides were washed and equilibrated with amplification buffer. For HCR initiation, the hairpins (h1 and h2) were snap-cooled, mixed and applied to the slides. Following the HCR process, the slides were washed, mounted with SlowFade Gold Antifade mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged. All probes and hairpin oligonucleotides were purchased from Molecular Instruments.

RNAScope HiPlex assay

RNAScope HiPlex assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, slides were baked at 60 °C for 30 min, fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min on ice, and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (50%, 70% and 100%). A 5-min target retrieval step was conducted before protease treatment, which involved incubating the sections with protease III at 40 °C for 15 min. Hybridization was carried out with 12 specific probes (485491-T1 (Lmo4), 866311-T2 (Rreb1), 495411-T3 (Sulf1), 500661-T4 (Nrp2), 473421-T5 (Sorcs3), 480601-T6 (St6galnac5), 313631-T7 (Ndst4), 531081-T8 (Zfhx4), 404981-T9 (Sfrp1), 586761-T10 (Foxp1), 428791-T11 (Foxp2) and 442831-T12 (Gpc5)) at 40 °C for 2 h, followed by three rounds of signal amplification. The nuclei were stained with DAPI. The detection of the 12 targets was accomplished in three rounds of imaging, with four targets visualized per round. Between each round, the fluorophores were cleaved and washed away to prepare for the next round of signal development.

CycIF

Cryosections (10–12 µm) were thawed at room temperature for 1–2 h, air-dried and optionally postfixed in 4% PFA for 15 min on ice. Permeabilization was performed using 0.4% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min at 37 °C. Antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) preheated in a steamer, with slides incubated for 1–20 min, depending on the primary antibody, followed by cooling at room temperature for 20 min. Blocking was performed with 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 for 15 min at room temperature.

Slides were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 4% NDS in PBS with 0.05% Tween-20 for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C, washed five times with PBS, and incubated with secondary antibodies (1:1,000 in 4% NDS in PBS with 0.05% Tween-20) for 30 min at room temperature. After three washes in PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:10,000 in PBS) for 1 min. Slides were washed again, mounted in SlowFade Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S36938) and imaged using a Zeiss Observer 7 microscope.

For subsequent rounds of staining, coverslips were removed by immersing slides in PBS until detachment, and antibodies were eluted using glycine–urea–guanidine hydrochloride elution buffer (pH 2.5) containing 0.04% Tween-20 and 70 mM TCEP. Elution was performed for 10 min at room temperature, followed by five washes in PBS. The staining cycle, including blocking, primary and secondary antibody incubation, counterstaining and imaging, was repeated as needed.

Antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Generation of multiplexed images

Raw images with multiple scenes were captured and stitched using Zeiss ZEN (v3.5) and then split into individual scenes in the CZI format. Images from different staining rounds were organized into subfolders corresponding to individual scenes. A custom ImageJ macro was developed to streamline high-throughput image processing. This macro supported recursive folder processing, customizable parameters (for example, number of channels, magnification and segmentation options), and automated output generation. Specifically, after background subtraction, multichannel images from different CycIF rounds were registered using the DAPI channel as a reference. Registration was performed through the ImageJ_Linear Stack Alignment with SIFT MultiChannel_plugin. DAPI images were projected to a single 2D image and processed for nuclear segmentation using StarDist49. The resulting nuclear masks were converted into ROIs and their spatial coordinates were recorded to map the cell positions. These ROIs were used to quantify the fluorescence intensities across the channels. The generated expression matrix, along with the spatial coordinates, was processed and analyzed in R.

CycIF data analysis and integration with scRNA-seq

The CycIF-generated expression matrix was imported into Seurat (v.5.1.0)50 for cell clustering. Cells were filtered based on the nuclear size, shape and intensity sum. Standard Seurat workflows were used for data normalization, clustering and dimensional reduction. Seurat objects from different samples and genotypes were merged and jointly processed. Batch-effect correction was applied using Harmony51 when needed.

scRNA-seq data were integrated with single-cell spatial proteomic data to identify spatially resolved cell identities. Integration was performed by identifying features common to both datasets and computing a shared embedding space using reciprocal PCA reduction with the FindTransferAnchors function in Seurat. RNA-seq cluster IDs were transferred to the spatial dataset using the TransferData function, enabling the assignment of RNA-defined clusters to the spatial proteomic data. Additionally, the scRNA-seq expression matrix was transferred to the spatial dataset to impute transcriptomic profiles at the level of spatially resolved individual cells. Visualization of spatially resolved clusters and cell types was achieved using a customized 3D spatial plotting function. This approach systematically characterized the spatial distribution of RNA-defined clusters and cell types across the cerebellum.

To reconstruct the 3D configuration of PC subtypes, a custom Python function was used to convert Seurat objects into an AnnData object. The Scube alignment algorithm in the spatial architecture characterization by deep learning18 was applied to align clusters across slices. This involved neighbor-based cluster alignment, exclusion of outliers, and generation of aligned spatial coordinates, which were saved as.csv files. Aligned spatial coordinates were imported into R and added as a new dimensional reduction object in the original Seurat object. To ensure precise alignment of slices along the anterior–posterior axis, a manual fine-tuning procedure was applied to spatial transcriptomics data using a custom R function. To generate 3D visualizations of PC subtypes, a spatial dataset was analyzed using the rgl package in R.

Quantitative analysis of inferior olivary nuclei

Nuclear segmentation was performed using Fiji Stardist49,52 based on DAPI staining. Manual corrections and marker identification for Foxp2 and Casp3 were performed using the Fiji Annotator plugin. The nomenclature and delineation of the inferior olivary subnuclei followed a previous report14. Counts from the right and left sides of the inferior olive were averaged.

scRNA-seq analysis

Single-cell suspensions were prepared as described previously17. Sequencing libraries from E16.5 and E18.5 cerebella were generated using Chromium v.2 (10x Genomics), while an additional E16.5 cerebellar library was prepared using Chromium v.3. Reverse transcription and library generation were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing reads were demultiplexed and aligned to the mm10 mouse genome reference using CellRanger v.3.1, and expression count matrices were generated with the count function in CellRanger.

Count matrices were processed using the Seurat R package50. Data from E16.5 (v.2 and v.3) and E18.5 were merged, and quality control filtering was applied to remove low-quality cells based on thresholds for RNA features, mitochondrial gene content and total RNA counts. After quality control, the number of cells retained from each dataset was as follows: 5,076 from E16.5 (Chromium v.2), 5,174 from E18.5 (Chromium v.2) and 8,449 from E16.5 (Chromium v.3). Integration of datasets was performed using the FastMNN algorithm (bachelor v.1.18.1) in Seurat to correct for batch effects. The top 30 dimensions of the mnn embedding calculated by FastMNN were used for downstream analysis, including clustering and visualization.

A presumptive PC cluster containing 1,477 cells marked by Foxp2 was extracted, comprising 468 cells from E16.5 (Chromium v.2), 898 cells from E16.5 (Chromium v.3) and 111 cells from E18.5 (Chromium v.2). Similar batch correction and clustering were performed on these cells, resulting in the identification of 11 PC subclusters. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the wilcoxauc function in the presto package53. Cell-type-specific genes were identified using the COSG package54.

Sequencing libraries of the chick cerebellum were generated using the Evercode WT Mini Kit (Parse Bioscience). Sequencing reads were demultiplexed using split-pipe v.1.0.6p and aligned to the chicken genome GRCg7b and Ensembl Release 110 annotations. The ‘split-pipe’ pipeline was used to generate a cell-by-gene count matrix, which was processed with Seurat50.

BAM files were obtained from the European Nucleotide Archive (PRJNA637987) and NCBI GEO (GSE118068), and then converted to FASTQ using bamtofastq v.1.3.2. Expression count matrices were generated using the cellranger count function.

Integration of scRNA-seq data

Putative PCs were extracted from two published scRNA-seq datasets15,16. To integrate scRNA-seq datasets and transfer PC-subtype identities, we used Seurat v.4.3 (ref. 50). Transfer anchors were identified between the in-house E16.5/E18.5 dataset and query datasets based on shared features using PCA and the top 30 dimensions. Cell identity labels from the reference dataset were transferred to the query dataset using the TransferData function, and predictions were incorporated into the query metadata. The query dataset was subsequently mapped to the UMAP space of the reference using the MapQuery function, enabling visualization of predicted cell identities in the in-house E16.5/E18.5 UMAP space.

Immunostaining and volume imaging of whole-mount mouse cerebella

The detailed protocol for the procedure is available on protocols.io. Briefly, the cerebellum was dissected, meninges were removed and the tissue was fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by three washes in PBS. The samples were permeabilized with 0.05% Tween-20 and 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS (TTP) at 37 °C for 45 min, and then treated with citric acid at 95 °C in a steamer for 30 min. After blocking with 10% donkey serum in TTP, the samples were stained with primary and secondary antibodies for 2 days at 4 °C. Clearing was performed using the modified RTF method55. Samples were incubated at 4 °C in the following solutions: 30% triethanolamine (TEA), 40% formamide and 30% H2O for 1 h; 60% TEA, 25% formamide and 15% H2O for at least 4 h; and 70% TEA, 15% formamide and 15% H2O for 2 h. For imaging and storage, the samples were immersed in 80% glycerol with a refractive index adjusted to 1.45, which is compatible with the Zeiss light-sheet Z1 microscope (×5 objective). Furthermore, 3D reconstruction images and videos were generated using Imaris 9.0 (Bitplane).

Volumetric analysis of the embryonic mouse cerebellum

To measure the volume of flocculus, intensity-based surface objects were generated by manually outlining the structure using the surface creation module in Imaris 9.0 (Bitplane). The measurement points were placed at the desired anatomical landmarks to assess the distance between the two points in the 3D space. The circumference of the structures was calculated by summing the distances between the sequential measurement points, effectively creating a polyline that represented the perimeter of the cerebellar cortical surface.

Statistical analysis

Data processing, statistical analysis and plotting were performed using R v.4.3.2. For comparisons between two groups, a two-sided unpaired t test with Welch’s correction was applied, whereas analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. Sample sizes reflect those commonly used in the field and our previous studies13,56. Data distribution was assumed to be normal; however, this assumption was not formally tested. No data or animals were excluded from this study. The results were reproducible for three or more samples, with quantitative data expressed as the mean ± s.e.m. Data collection and analysis were not performed blindly to experimental conditions. However, quantification was conducted in an unbiased manner, using either automated image analysis or an operator blinded to the genotype.
