Spore siege: diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in fungal sternal osteomyelitis—a case series
Authors: Syeda Sakina Mehdi, Hina Inam, Syed Faisal Mehmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep sternal wound infections (DSWI) are rare but potentially fatal complications of cardiac surgery. While bacterial pathogens predominate, fungal involvement is a rare phenomenon coupled with high morbidity and mortality.AimThis study aimed to describe the clinical spectrum, management strategies, and outcomes of culture-proven fungal deep sternal wound infections following cardiac surgery at a tertiary care center, with particular emphasis on pathogen distribution, diagnostic challenges, therapeutic approaches, and patient outcomes, in order to enhance awareness of this rare but high-mortality entity and inform optimized, context-appropriate multidisciplinary care.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of seven patients with culture-proven fungal DSWI managed at Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi, between 2019 and 2024. Clinical features, imaging, microbiology, treatment modalities, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsThe cohort included four adults and three pediatric patients (2 females and 5 males). Candida species were isolated in four cases and Aspergillus species in three, contrasting with global trends where Aspergillus predominates. All patients underwent surgical debridement combined with systemic antifungal therapy and adjunctive measures such as vacuum-assisted closure dressings with amphotericin-soaked gauze. Azoles were primarily used for both Aspergillus and Candida Species. Liposomal amphotericin B was reserved for severe or refractory cases due to nephrotoxicity. Five patients recovered with ongoing follow-up, while two mortalities were recorded, both in patients with multiple comorbidities and complicated postoperative courses. ConclusionFungal sternal osteomyelitis is a rare but severe form of DSWI requiring early recognition, multimodal management, and multidisciplinary care. Our series highlights Candida as the predominant pathogen in this cohort, underscoring the importance of considering both yeast and mold infections in postoperative mediastinal complications. Individualized treatment strategies, timely surgical intervention, and judicious antifungal use are essential to improving outcomes, particularly in resource-limited settings.Clinical trialN/A.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13403-6.Keywords: Deep sternal wound infection, Fungal sternal osteomyelitis, Candida, Aspergillus, Post–cardiac surgery infection, Mediastinitis, Antifungal therapy, Surgical debridement, Vacuum-assisted closure

Introduction

Deep sternal wound infections (DSWI) are uncommon but emphasize the delicate balance between successful surgery and vigilant postoperative care. The infection can extend into deeper tissues, resulting in osteomyelitis, costochondritis, and mediastinitis [1]. These serious complications after sternotomy have an incidence of 1–5% and a mortality rate of 10–47% [1] Sternal osteomyelitis, a rare condition with an incidence of just 0.47%, involves the infectious destruction of the sternum [2] Individual patient factors like obesity, diabetes, and respiratory insufficiency contribute to an increased risk of deep sternal wound infections, which may or may not involve the bone [1]. Various microorganisms, from the common Staphylococcus aureus to both typical and atypical Mycobacteria, have been implicated in this disease [3].

Most of the reported cases of sternal wound infections report polymicrobial cultures [4] hence, antimicrobial agents are tailored according to the sensitivity of the isolated pathogens. Though common skin commensals are more commonly reported in wound cultures, isolation of fungal offenders is a rare but occasionally reported phenomenon [4]. Fungal involvement is often overlooked when compiling differential diagnoses and is considered after multiple cultures report findings incompatible with the clinical scenario [5]. Cases worldwide have tested positive for pathogens like Aspergillus fumigatus, Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida albicans, Coccidioides, Histoplasma, Mucormycosis etc [5–9]. Radiologic evidence of infection on Computed Tomography (CT) scans and Fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography (FDG-PET), a sensitive modality to rule in infectious pathology necessitating workup for etiology [2, 9]. Diagnosis involves species-specific tests as well as various fungal cultures, such as Sabouraud’s dextrose agar (SDA) are employed. The use of Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) and organism-specific stains is also a common practice. Less specific tests, such as beta-D-glucan and galactomannan antigen, have been shown to aid in the diagnosis [5, 8].

The existing literature on fungal infections emphasizes the risk factors for symptomatic cases and reinforces the widely accepted belief that invasive infections primarily affect immunocompromised individuals [5]. However, reported cases are not limited to immunocompromised states. Cases of primary fungal sternal osteomyelitis in immunocompetent patients have been published [8]. Additive risks due to uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, gender, and repeated surgical explorations have been associated with osteomyelitis with different fungi [5].

The management involves long-term antifungal therapy, and the infection frequently requires surgical debridement. Fungal antimicrobial therapy is organism-specific, and combination therapies are widely used to cover resistant strains and concomitant bacterial agents. Azoles, echinocandins, amphotericin, and flucytosine are some to mention [5]. A median duration of 4 months of pharmacological therapy shows adequate control of the infection, and repeat cultures are routinely performed to confirm remission. Aspergillus species are frequently isolated and are also associated with higher mortality. Concurrent comorbidities such as renal insufficiency significantly add to the risk of morbidity and mortality as most antifungals are highly nephrotoxic [10]. Individualized vigilant care is necessary to ensure favorable outcomes in such patients. In view of the limited existing literature, this study aims to characterize the clinical presentation, management strategies, and outcomes of seven patients with culture-confirmed fungal deep sternal wound infections treated at our center.

Therefore, this study aims to describe the clinical characteristics, diagnostic challenges, microbiological spectrum, treatment strategies, and outcomes of culture-confirmed fungal deep sternal wound infections in a single-center case series of seven patients.

Methods

We present a case series of seven patients with fungal DSWI managed at Aga Khan University (AKU) Hospital, a leading tertiary care facility in Karachi, Pakistan. Over the past decade, the hospital has been at the forefront of patient-centered care, adhering to international healthcare standards. These patients had a recent history of undergoing cardiac or thoracic surgery and presented with wound pain, with or without active discharge. All cases had strong clinical evidence of DSWIs, supported by radiological and pathological findings.

A retrospective review of hospital records identified seven cases of fungal DSWIs over the past ten years. Data were collected in a case report format, including 2 females and 5 male patients, two of whom were pediatric cases.

A multidisciplinary team comprising surgeons, infectious disease specialists, physiotherapists, and nutritionists ensured comprehensive, individualized patient care. In accordance with institutional policy, patients with post-operative complications from surgeries performed at other hospitals were admitted only upon submission of a referral or refusal-of-treatment letter from their primary care team. Each patient and their family were counseled about potential complications, and informed consent was obtained after discussing all possible outcomes.

Fungal culture and identification

Five culture plates were used for each specimen. These included five blood agar plates, two Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) plates supplemented with blood, one potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate, and one mycological (mycelial) agar plate. Of the SDA with blood plates, one was incubated at 37 °C and the other at 25 °C to support the growth of both thermotolerant and environmental fungi. PDA and mycological agar plates were incubated at 25 °C. All cultures were maintained under sub-ambient air conditions and observed for up to four weeks.

Specimen processing and inoculation

Tissue specimens were gently and roughly chopped to avoid damage to fungal hyphae. The cut tissue fragments were then lightly tapped onto the surface of each culture medium to facilitate fungal transfer without excessive disruption.

Pus specimens were inoculated by placing a single drop onto the agar surface, followed by streaking using the four-quadrant technique to allow for colony isolation [11].

Identification of fungal isolates

Mold isolates were identified based on phenotypic characteristics, including growth rate, colony morphology (gross appearance), and microscopic features. Identification was further supported by temperature and media preferences. Microscopic examination was performed using standard staining techniques, and identification was confirmed using established mycological atlases as reference standards.

Yeast isolates were identified based on colony morphology, microscopic appearance, and biochemical characteristics. Biochemical identification was performed using the VITEK® [12] 2 automated system, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bacterial cultures

Tissue and pus specimens were processed using standard bacteriological techniques and inoculated onto appropriate aerobic culture media, including blood and MacConkey agar. Cultures were incubated at 35–37 °C and examined for growth. Bacterial isolates were identified based on Gram staining, colony morphology, and biochemical characteristics, with confirmation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing performed using automated systems in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, as practiced in the Aga Khan University microbiology laboratory [13].

Case presentation: Case 1

A 59-year-old female patient, with diabetes, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease, presented with the complaint of lower sternal wound infection with purulent discharge and multiple fever spikes for a week, post-Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG). Imaging revealed an infection of the anterior chest wall extending into the anterior mediastinum with the involvement of the anterior pericardium. Intraoperatively, the entire necrotic sternal bone and multiple loculated collections of pus and sinuses were removed. Cultures identified Aspergillus versicolor and Acinetobacter species. The patient was treated postoperatively with amphotericin-soaked vacuum dressings, intravenous meropenem, along with oral voriconazole and linezolid for adequate polymicrobial coverage. Extensive wound care with amphotericin-soaked vacuum dressings was ensured, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 11 with an open wound. Outpatient follow-ups with cardiothoracic surgery and infectious disease were scheduled 2 and 4 days after discharge, respectively. She was readmitted with severe hemodynamic instability, complicated by acute kidney injury and thrombocytopenia on the 10th day of discharge. During her 43-day stay, she underwent multiple operations on her chest and thorax for the exploration of bleeding. Intravenous Colistimethate sodium, Cefoperazone–sulbactam were continued as per her initial cultures, and amphotericin B was initiated in accordance with antimicrobial susceptibility testing. All agents were dosed based on patient body weight, with appropriate renal dose adjustments made according to renal function. Her chest was closed on the 20th day, which went unremarkably. However, the patient’s kidney function worsened, and sepsis worsened. Inotropic support was given, but the patient succumbed to cardiopulmonary arrest.

Case 2

A 74-year-old male with diabetes, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease came in with complaints of lethargy and purulent discharge from the sternal and tracheostomy wound. The patient underwent CABG at another hospital, complicated with atrial fibrillation, warranting cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and had been on a home ventilator. Our radiological investigation revealed widely displaced sternal halves associated with surrounding inflammatory changes and edema. Dissection of the adhered ventricle to the sternum led to ventricular rupture intraoperatively. The post-operative course was complicated with cardiogenic shock requiring inotropic support in the cardiac Intensive Care Unit (ICU). Tracheal secretions demonstrated heavy growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella species, and Acinetobacter species, while cultures from the debrided tissue revealed Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia, Klebsiella species, Proteus mirabilis, and Candida glabrata. In response, antimicrobial therapy was optimized with a one-week course of caspofungin, along with tigecycline, meropenem, and vancomycin. However, the patient subsequently developed severe metabolic acidosis and acute renal dysfunction. Despite intensive management, he experienced a sudden cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated but was ultimately withdrawn per the family’s request. The patient was pronounced deceased.

Case 3

A 72-year-old male with diabetes, hypertension, and cervical spondylitis presented with neck swelling extending to the chest wall. Imaging confirmed necrotizing fasciitis extending into the mediastinum. He underwent urgent multidisciplinary debridement with the removal of necrotic tissue, and cultures revealed Staphylococcus aureus, for which he received cefazolin and clindamycin. Regular wound dressing with EUSOL was done, and chest physiotherapy was continued. A second wound exploration was conducted, removing more necrotic slough from the wound. The patient was encouraged to ambulate, and incentive spirometry was advised. He was discharged with a chest tube and followed up in clinics. Five months later, he returned with purulent neck and chest discharge, and imaging showed sternal osteomyelitis with mediastinal collection. A second debridement removed necrotic bone, and C. albicans was identified, leading to antifungal therapy with Fluconazole for 3 weeks. He completed six weeks of antimicrobial therapy and continues to heal well with no recurrence.

Case 4

A 65-year-old female with diabetes and hypertension presented with wound discharge and pain six months after CABG. She underwent sternal wire excision and bone debridement; however, bone excision was not performed as there were no signs of bone involvement during surgical exploration. She was managed empirically with antibiotics, and the excised tissue culture tested positive for C. albicans, leading to adjustments in her antimicrobial regimen. Inpatient treatment included meropenem and fluconazole. She was discharged after four days on fluconazole, meropenem, and linezolid, which had been initiated empirically and were continued at standard dosing. Patients were then regularly followed in surgical and infectious disease clinics.

Case 5

A 65-year-old male with a history of diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented to our emergency department with purulent drainage from his chest wound, six months after undergoing CABG. He was hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Imaging revealed osteomyelitis of the clavicles and a non-healing sternotomy wound with associated soft tissue thickening. The patient was taken to the operating room for surgical debridement, during which sternal wires were removed. However, the underlying bone appeared healthy and was not excised. Postoperatively, standard management was implemented, including the use of chest tubes and a vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) device. He made an uneventful recovery and was discharged on postoperative day four. As per protocol, intraoperative tissue cultures were sent, which later grew A. Flavus. The patient was empirically started on Amphotericin but was later switched to fluconazole for three weeks based on sensitivity results. The patient was followed up weekly in the cardiothoracic outpatient clinic for the first month after discharge. Thereafter, the frequency of visits was gradually reduced to bi-monthly and subsequently monthly until complete wound healing was achieved. Concurrently, outpatient follow-up with infectious disease specialists was scheduled every two weeks and continued until full resolution of symptoms.

Case 6

A 1-year-old male presented with a painful sternal wound discharge one month after complete atrioventricular defect repair. The child had previously presented with similar symptoms two weeks earlier and underwent incision and drainage of the purulent wound, initially covered with meropenem, later shifted to oral linezolid. During this admission, the patient underwent sternal wire excision. Tissue cultures revealed A. terreus, prompting the addition of fluconazole to the treatment regimen. The patient was discharged on oral antibiotics after a two-day inpatient stay. Outpatient follow-up with the primary cardiothoracic surgeon was scheduled one week after discharge. Monthly follow-ups with the pediatric cardiologist were conducted to ensure adequate cardiac function post-intervention until complete clinical recovery.

Case 7

A 6-month-old male with trisomy 21 was admitted for ventricular septal defect (VSD) repair and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) ligation. The intraoperative course was unremarkable, but postoperatively, he developed complete heart block requiring an extended CICU stay. Afterward, he required wound exploration due to purulent discharge, and his chest was kept open until hemodynamic stability was achieved. Empirical treatment included meropenem, later adding clindamycin and amikacin. Wound cultures showed methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), which later progressed to sepsis. A CT scan revealed air along the sternum extending into the mediastinum, necessitating a second wound exploration. This time, cultures were positive for C. parapsilosis and Burkholderia, so treatment was adjusted to amphotericin, later switched to fluconazole and clindamycin, which were to be taken for 42 days. After a 38-day hospital stay, the patient was discharged in stable condition and continued to do well.

Discussion

We present data of patients treated for fungal sternal osteomyelitis at our hospital from 2019 to 2024. We identified seven patients, out of whom five were successfully treated and are currently undergoing recovery, while two patients have passed away. All patients were thoroughly counseled about possible complications, as well as the potential favorable and unfavorable outcomes, before admission.

Fungal sternal osteomyelitis represents a rare but serious complication following sternotomy, with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly in patients with underlying comorbidities. In our series, five of the seven patients had diabetes mellitus, a well-established risk factor for impaired wound healing, increased susceptibility to secondary infections, and progression to osteomyelitis. Diabetes likely contributed to both the development and severity of infection in these patients, consistent with prior reports highlighting hyperglycemia as a key predisposing factor for post-sternotomy infections. Other risk factors observed included prior cardiac surgery (some performed outside our institution), repeated surgical interventions, immunosuppression, and prolonged antibiotic exposure, all of which may compromise host defenses and facilitate fungal colonization or invasion. Prolonged antibiotic exposures and diabetes mellitus have been the most prominent in our patients, while other factors were seldom significant [2, 14–16].

The microbial spectrum in our cohort demonstrated a predominance of Candida species (4/7) with three cases of Aspergillus, which contrasts with global data where Aspergillus is more frequently implicated in invasive sternal osteomyelitis [17, 18]. This variation may reflect regional epidemiology, patient comorbidities, or referral patterns. Our five recoveries and two fatalities are broadly consistent with literature-reported survival rates, emphasizing that early diagnosis, timely surgical intervention, and targeted antifungal therapy significantly improve prognosis [1, 5, 19–22].

Management was multimodal, combining surgical debridement, reconstructive procedures, and systemic antifungal therapy; thereby, a multidisciplinary team of surgeons and physicians was actively involved. Literature emphasizes that surgical debridement significantly improves outcomes in Candida mediastinitis compared to antifungal therapy alone, given the limited tissue penetration of many antifungals [19, 23]. The two fatalities in our series were associated with advanced disease, septic shock, renal insufficiency, and cardiovascular instability, highlighting the impact of systemic comorbidities on survival [10]. Alongside systemic antifungals, surgical excision of infected tissue was complemented by adjuncts such as vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) dressings with amphotericin-soaked gauze. This combination has been shown to enhance cure rates [23].

Regular wound care was essential to the healing process and was consistently ensured during inpatient management and post-discharge follow-up. In addition to medical therapy, supportive care measures such as a nutritionist-approved diet and early ambulation contributed to faster recovery. Hygiene was rigorously maintained by specially trained staff, and patients received regular incentive spirometry and physiotherapy to prevent further complications [2, 24] Since sternal osteomyelitis is a rare form of complicated deep surgical wound infection, accurate statistics on its incidence are still unavailable. Consequently, patients worldwide are managed according to standardized guidelines for wound care [2, 24].

While culture-based methods remain the diagnostic standard, their sensitivity may be limited by slow-growing fungi, prior antimicrobial exposure, or low organism burden, potentially delaying definitive diagnosis. Consequently, serum galactomannan and beta-D-glucan levels were used to guide empiric therapy for suspected yeast and mold infections while awaiting culture results. Tissue specimens were processed using careful mechanical homogenization to preserve fungal structures, followed by incubation on selective and differential media. Identification relied on phenotypic, microscopic, and automated biochemical methods, including VITEK®2 for yeast species [11–13]. Candida species in our patients were managed primarily with azole antifungals, consistent with comparative studies on antifungal drug outcomes [19]. For patients with Aspergillus-positive cultures, treatment also involved azoles; specifically, voriconazole (and, in selected cases, fluconazole) in conjunction with surgical debridement. Voriconazole, an azole antifungal, is highlighted here to emphasize organism-directed therapy based on microbial susceptibility testing. This approach ensured adequate treatment while minimizing unnecessary antifungal exposure. Azoles have been widely used for mold eradication, and a multimodal strategy has been associated with higher cure rates and lower relapse rates [5, 6].

All fungal pathogens in our study were amenable to treatment with liposomal amphotericin B, which we used empirically in selective cases with aggressive infection. However, its use is limited by significant nephrotoxicity, an adverse effect that is further exacerbated in patients with severe comorbidities, who are already predisposed to invasive fungal infections. Hence, amphotericin was reserved for topical application or use in severe cases [10, 25].

The two fatalities in our series underscore the severe prognosis associated with advanced fungal DSWI in patients with multiple comorbidities. These cases were complicated by septic shock, renal failure, and cardiovascular instability, which warranted longer hospital stays. Tailored care that prioritizes reducing hospital stay remains crucial in minimizing morbidity and the risk of nosocomial infections [26, 27]. Extended hospitalizations not only increase infection risk but also impose a significant financial burden on patients in LMICs due to out-of-pocket healthcare payment systems [13, 28, 29]. Relevant amendments to the management plan were made to minimize unnecessary hospital stay without compromising patient care. One of our patients was managed primarily through outpatient follow-ups due to financial constraints warranting limited inpatient care.

Treatment outcomes in our cohort align with the literature, demonstrating that multimodal strategies, combined surgical and pharmacological interventions, yield higher cure rates and reduced relapses, while conservative management alone is often insufficient. Our experience also underscores the importance of addressing modifiable risk factors, including glycemic control, nutritional optimization, and early mobilization, to enhance recovery [14].

Conclusion

In summary, fungal sternal osteomyelitis is a rare but severe post-sternotomy complication, most commonly affecting patients with significant comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus. Early recognition, a high index of suspicion, and a multimodal treatment approach combining surgical debridement with targeted antifungal therapy are critical for favorable outcomes. Multidisciplinary care and individualized management remain essential, particularly in resource-limited settings.
