Effects of hydrogen-rich hypochlorous acid water on broiler drinking water bacterial level, growth performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal environment
Authors: Jian Li, Chang Liu, Chonglian Peng, Yang Wang, Baoming Li, Li Yang, Han Peng, Weichao Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic microbial contamination in broiler drinking water constitutes a critical factor in disease occurrence and transmission within large-scale farming operations. Effective drinking water quality management is essential for optimizing broiler health and production performance. Functional additives, such as hypochlorous acid water (HAW) and hydrogen-rich water (HRW), are often added to farm drinking systems. HAW inhibits bacterial growth, and HRW enhances antioxidant capacity in broilers. This study aimed to investigate the combined effects of these two functional additives and evaluate their impacts on bacteria and biofilm in the drinking water system, broiler production performance, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal environment. A total of 480 broilers were randomly divided into four groups (n = 120 each) and raised for 42 days with distinct aqueous solutions: Group A (tap water), Group B (HAW, 0.3 mg/L), Group C (HRW, 1200 ppb), and Group D (combined HAW and HRW, hydrogen-rich hypochlorous acid water [HRHAW], 0.3 mg/L + 1200 ppb). The results indicated that the sterilization rate of planktonic bacteria in drinking water exceeded 99.90 % after the HRHAW intervention, and the biofilm biomass decreased by 62.85 %. Compared with controls, HRHAW showed no significant impact on the feed–gain ratio but significantly improved breast meat tenderness (39.15 %) and antioxidant capacity (SOD: 6.52 %–27.54 %; GSH-PX: 8.54 %–50.97 %). Intestinal health was enhanced through oxidative stress mitigation and antibacterial effects. In summary, HRHAW successfully integrates the antioxidant benefits of HRW with the antimicrobial efficacy of HAW, significantly reducing the bacterial content in drinking water and enhancing the antioxidant capacity of broilers, thereby positively influencing broiler health.Keywords: Drinking water, Hydrogen-rich hypochlorous acid water, Sterilization, Antioxidant capacity, Intestine

Introduction

Water comprises animal tissues and cells and regulates essential physiological processes. The microbiological safety of drinking water directly influences poultry growth and health, as water serves as a primary vector for pathogen transmission. For instance, Campylobacter hepaticus isolated from farm water can cause spotty liver disease, which can reduce egg production by 25 % and increase mortality by 10 % (Battersby et al., 2017; Petrovska et al., 2017; Phung et al., 2019). Virulent avian pathogenic Escherichia coli strains induce primary respiratory infections that may progress to systemic disease (Dziva et al., 2008), imposing substantial economic burdens through reduced hatchability, diminished egg production, impaired growth performance, and elevated mortality rates (Mol et al., 2019). Poultry bacterial infections present a dual burden: they compromise animal health and productivity, causing substantial economic losses, and simultaneously enable the transmission of zoonotic pathogens to humans through the food chain. This transmission dynamic may drive the evolution of human-adaptable strains, thereby threatening both poultry production systems and public health (Grace et al., 2024; Kuehn, 2018; Song et al., 2022). Empirical evidence indicates that a 10- to 1000-fold reduction in Campylobacter levels on poultry carcasses significantly lowers the incidence of human campylobacteriosis (Hermans et al., 2011), highlighting the critical importance of minimizing enteric pathogen loads in poultry to mitigate human bacterial infections (Iwata et al., 2023). Consequently, vitamins, polysaccharides, and other supplements are routinely administered via drinking water in modern intensive farming systems to enhance production performance. This high organic input regimen provides abundant carbon sources for microbial proliferation, thereby accelerating biofilm accumulation on the inner walls of waterlines.

During water distribution through pipeline networks in poultry farms, secondary contamination frequently compromises drinking water safety owing to disinfectant decay and biofilm shedding (Wan et al., 2021). Regional investigations of poultry farms reveal widespread bacterial contamination of drinking water systems. A Beijing study found that 60.7 % of sampled farms practiced irregular water disinfection, with only 10.7 % of water samples meeting total bacterial count standards. Bacterial loads increased progressively along waterlines from proximal to distal points. Total coliform compliance was 50 %, and thermotolerant coliforms and E. coli met the standards in 82.1 % of samples (Wu et al., 2013). Assessments in 20 Irish broiler houses further detected persistent Campylobacter contamination in drinking water (Battersby et al., 2017), indicating that microbial water safety remains a critical yet frequently overlooked biosecurity component in intensive poultry farming.

Effective drinking water disinfection is essential for interrupting pathogen transmission in poultry farming. Conventional water treatment primarily relies on physical flushing and chlorine-based chemical disinfectants. Although physical disinfection provides operational convenience, its limited efficacy and substantial wastewater generation constitute significant drawbacks. Chemical agents, such as chlorine dioxide, have potent antimicrobial activity but produce carcinogenic and teratogenic disinfection byproducts (Han et al., 2021). Consequently, identifying safe and effective alternatives to traditional disinfectants represents a critical challenge in poultry water management. In recent years, functional additive technologies have garnered attention as sustainable and ecofriendly chemical disinfectant substitutes. Among these, hypochlorous acid water (HAW) and hydrogen-rich water (HRW) show considerable promise owing to their unique biological functionalities.

HAW demonstrates remarkable efficacy in eradicating bacteria from water systems. For example, HAW achieves over 99.90 % inactivation of E. coli due to its sustained-release hypochlorous acid properties; furthermore, this method is considered safe and produces no harmful residues. Research indicates that compared with tap water, 0.3 mg/L HAW in layer drinking water achieves over 99.90 % inactivation of total bacteria and E. coli (Li et al., 2024), whereas adding 3 % HAW to swine water systems eliminates E. coli and significantly reduces total bacterial counts (Hao et al., 2023).

Studies have shown that molecular hydrogen (H2) alleviates oxidative damage, repairs intestinal barriers, and modulates microbial metabolism in poultry, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced growth inhibition. For instance, consumption of 0.6 mmol/L HRW enhances the quality of broiler breast meat (Zhu et al., 2025). Regarding intestinal health, HRW supplementation increases the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in cecal contents and improves the jejunal villus height-to-crypt depth ratio under heat stress, indicating its role in maintaining gut microbiota homeostasis (Ji et al., 2019). Biochemically, HRW positively regulates antioxidant enzyme activities in poultry (Shin et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2022).

Numerous studies have proven the advantages of HRW in antioxidant regulation and HAW in microbial control. However, most of these studies are limited to assessing the effects of a single functional additive and lack observational data under combined application conditions. The potential synergistic or antagonistic effects arising from their coadministration remain unknown, constituting a bottleneck that constrains the systematic implementation of functional additive technologies.

Given the uncertain synergistic or antagonistic interactions arising from the combined application of HAW and HRW, broilers were subjected to a 42-day drinking water intervention trial. The objectives of this study were as follows: (1) to explore the interaction between active components in HRHAW, thereby establishing practical guidelines for its application and storage; (2) to evaluate the inhibitory efficacy of HRHAW against microorganisms in broiler drinking water, elucidating the impact of HRW–HAW coadministration on antibacterial performance; and (3) to explore the effects of HRHAW on the antioxidant capacity and intestinal indicators of broilers, elucidating the impact of the combined use of HRW and HAW on antioxidant performance.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and consent to participat

All animal studies were conducted in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by both the Animal Care Committee and the Institutional Biosafety Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No. AW80605202-6-02).

Experimental animals and management

A total of 480 one-day-old male Daheng yellow-feathered broilers, with an average initial body weight of 38.78 ± 0.49 g, were randomly divided into four treatment groups, each consisting of six replicates of 20 broilers. The trial was conducted in two feeding phases (days 1–21 and 22–42), during which all control and experimental groups were fed identical mash-form diets. The composition and nutritional levels of the basal diet are presented in Table 1 & 2. Broilers were housed in cages with ad libitum access to feed and water. During the first 7 days, the house temperature was maintained at 33°C–35°C and then gradually reduced by 3°C per week until it reached 22°C–25°C. Relative humidity was controlled at 50 %–70 %, with a photoperiod of 23-h light and 1-h dark. The nipple drinker’s height was adjusted weekly based on visual assessment to ensure unrestricted water access. Over the 42-day experimental period, daily records were maintained for mortality, culled broilers, and feed intake per replicate. The primary performance metric was the feed–gain (F/G) ratio. Body weights were recorded after an 8-h fast on days 1 and 42. On day 42, one broiler closest to the average body weight of the replicate was selected per replicate for sampling. The production performance, meat quality, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal environment of the animals were all tested.

Experimental design

Group A received tap water (available chlorine concentration [ACC]: 0 mg/L) supplied from the municipal waterworks through the water delivery system. Groups B and C were provided with different functional water additives: 0.3 mg/L HAW (Group B), generated using the WaterGod HD-240L-V2 hypochlorous acid generator (Shanghai Fuqiangwang Hygiene Products Co., Ltd.), and 1200 ppb HRW (Group C), produced by the NB-X71A NanoBuber hydrogen inhaler (Shanghai NanoBubble Nanotechnology Co., Ltd.). Group D was supplied with a mixture of HAW and HRW (ACC: 0.3 mg/L; dissolved hydrogen: 1200 ppb; Hunan Van Gogh Technology Co., Ltd.).

All waterlines were newly installed and disinfected before use (preuse treatment: 24 h soaking in a 150 mg/L HAW, followed by thorough rinsing with tap water). Nipple drinkers were installed on PVC pipelines (diameter: 25 mm; length: 12 m per line). Water samples were collected every 4 days to quantify culturable planktonic bacterial concentrations. On experimental day 30, sectioned pipe segments were obtained for biofilm biomass assessment.

Collection and detection of water samples

Samples were collected from nipple drinkers at distances of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 m from the water tank. The nipple drinker and the water pipeline within 30 cm of it were disinfected with 75 % alcohol to prevent contamination of the sampling water. A 50-mL water sample was collected from a disinfected sampling bottle (the first three tubes of water should be discarded to avoid stagnant water in the sample). Microbial analysis was started immediately upon returning the samples to the laboratory. ACC was measured using a portable chlorine meter (ZNSK-100S1, Changchun Matai Instruments Co., Ltd., China; measurement range: 0–3 mg/L, accuracy: ±0.1 mg/L; RC-3F, Saitamihara Chemical Instruments, Japan; measurement range: 0–320 mg/L, accuracy: ±1 mg/L), and dissolved hydrogen levels were quantified with a portable hydrogen detector (ENH-2000, TRUSTLEX Hydrogen Water Tester, Japan; measurement range: 0–3000 ppb; accuracy: ±1 ppb). The experiment was conducted in a sterile environment in the laboratory. Each water sample (1 mL) was transferred to a sterile Petri dish (gradient dilution is required for nondisinfected water samples). Approximately 15 mL of sterilized and cooled nutrient agar or MacConkey agar (Beijing Aoboxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was poured into the dish, followed by thorough mixing. Plates were incubated in a constant-temperature foster box at 37°C for 48 h. Bacterial colonies within 30–300 counts per plate were counted to determine the concentration of total culturable planktonic bacteria in the samples.

Collection and detection of biofilm on the waterline

On day 30 of the trial, pipe sections were excised to collect and analyze biofilms. Before sectioning, the external surfaces of the pipes were rinsed with tap water, sanitized with high-concentration HAW (150 mg/L), and rinsed again with tap water. On-site cutting was performed using a cutting torch; both ends of each pipe segment were wrapped in plastic wrap to maintain moisture and prevent contamination during transport to the laboratory. Biofilms and pipe deposits (>5 cm2) were scraped from at least 10 cm away from the cut edges using sterilized brushes and silicone scrapers, with three replicates per section collected into phosphate buffer solution. The samples were stored in sterile sampling bottles refrigerated at 4°C to determine biofilm biomass and extracellular polymers (Liu et al., 2017). Biofilm biomass was characterized using heterotrophic bacteria cultured on R2A medium (Beijing Auboxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Biofilm biomass was quantified through heterotrophic plate counts. The samples were plated on sterilized R2A medium (Beijing Auboxin), incubated at 25°C for 7 days, and enumerated (Li et al., 2020). E. coli density was determined via MacConkey agar plating with incubation at 37°C for 48 h. Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) components were analyzed using anthrone–sulfuric acid (polysaccharides) and Folin–Lowry assays (proteins) (Cheng et al., 2018).

Measurement of production performance

On experimental days 1 and 42, the body weight of broilers was measured after an 8-h fast. The number of deaths and feed consumption were recorded every day, and the ADG, average daily feed intake, and F/G ratio were calculated.

Measurement of meat quality

In the tests, after the experiment was completed, the broilers were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. pH values (measured 45 min after death) were determined using a pH meter (pHSTAR, Meters, Germany, 0–9.9 pH, measurement range: 0–14, accuracy: pH ± 0.1). Breast meat samples from the broilers were individually packaged in plastic bags and stored at 4°C for 24 h. The difference between the initial and final meat weights was used to calculate the percentage of drip loss. Approximately 30 g of the breast muscle sample was taken and placed in a sealed plastic bag before being cooked in a water bath at 75°C for 30 min. After cooking, the samples were cooled, and surface moisture was removed by drying before weighing to determine the cooking loss rate. The cooked pectoral muscle samples were equilibrated at 25°C for 15 min. Thereafter, small cubes measuring approximately 4–6 cm in length with a cross-sectional area of approximately 1 × 1 cm2 were cut parallel to the direction of muscle fibers. The shear force (N) was measured using a tenderness meter (Sosnówka-Czajka et al., 2023).

Assessment of antioxidant capacity

At the conclusion of the trial, one broiler per replicate was randomly selected for blood collection via the brachial vein. Approximately 5 mL of blood was drawn into anticoagulant-free tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 1–2 mL of supernatant serum was aspirated and stored at −20°C for analysis of blood antioxidant parameters. Liver tissue samples were aseptically excised using sterilized scissors, placed in prechilled sterile centrifuge tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −20°C for hepatic antioxidant profiling, following established protocols (Jiang et al., 2009).

Intestinal analysis

At the end of the experiment, six healthy broilers with average weight were selected from each of the four groups and were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. Approximately 2 cm of the same part of the intestinal segments of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were removed by aseptic surgery. Subsequently, the segments were immersed in a universal tissue fixative solution containing 4 % paraformaldehyde and stored at room temperature for paraffin section preparation. The intestinal tissues were dehydrated, soaked in wax, embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin before observation and imaging under a microscope. The villus height and crypt depth in the small intestine were measured using Image-Pro-Plus software. Then, the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio was calculated. Meanwhile, an appropriate amount of cecal chyme was collected in a previously weighed 2-mL sterilized centrifuge tube, stored in liquid nitrogen, and placed in a − 80°C freezer for the analysis of intestinal microorganisms using 16S rRNA (Zou et al., 2019).

Statistical analysis

Parallel samples were prepared for all specimens, and values were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. To standardize the data, the bacterial concentration was expressed as a logarithm of the culturable bacterial concentration. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA), and significant differences were determined using the F-test. Different lowercase letters (a, b) indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).

Results and discussions

Storage of HRHAW and decay of its active components

The World Health Organization recommends 0.2–5.0 mg/L of residual chlorine in drinking water (Onyutha and Kwio-Tamale, 2022). Existing studies have demonstrated that HAW with an ACC of 0.3 mg/L reduces bacterial counts in drinking water to meet safety standards (Fan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Obaid et al., 2025). For HRW, dissolved hydrogen concentrations at three-quarter saturation (0.6 mM or 1.2 ppm) are sufficient to induce biological effects. Thus, in this study, the ACC in HAW was set to 0.3 mg/L, and the dissolved hydrogen content in HRW was maintained at 1200 ppb to ensure microbial safety and enhance animal performance. Before testing the HRHAW solution, it was essential to confirm that there was no mutual interference between the active components of the two functional additives. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the decay test was conducted at 25°C in a light-avoiding and sealed environment. The ACC levels in both the HAW and HRHAW solutions showed identical decay kinetics, decreasing from 0.3 mg/L to undetectable levels within 10 h. Similarly, the dissolved hydrogen concentrations in both HRW and HRHAW exhibited a decrease from 1200 ppb back to baseline following the same temporal trajectory. Notably, separate investigations have reported complete hydrogen depletion in HRW within 7 h (from 1330 ppb to undetectable levels) (Fan et al., 2021). This accelerated decay may be attributed to environmental variables and preparation methodologies. Smaller hydrogen bubble diameters significantly prolong molecular hydrogen retention in aqueous solutions, whereas elevated temperatures reduce hydrogen solubility (Fan et al., 2021). Regarding HAW, a 120-h study at 30°C demonstrated chlorine reduction from 0.38 to 0.09 mg/L, which is markedly slower than the decay rates observed in our investigation. This divergence primarily stems from chlorine dissipation along distribution pipelines, with decay kinetics governed by the initial chlorine concentration, temperature, exposure duration, and pipeline materials (Obaid et al., 2025). However, these parallel decay patterns substantiate the chemical independence of chlorine and hydrogen components within the blended solution, thereby validating their combined application for subsequent trials.

Variation patterns of active ingredients in HRHAW and pure solutions (HRW and HAW) over time.

To ensure the effective use of HRHAW in practical poultry drinking systems, the decay kinetics of its active components—ACC and dissolved hydrogen—were evaluated under varying storage conditions (sealed vs. open and light vs. light-avoiding), container materials (plastic, glass, and aluminum), and temperatures (5°C–45°C). The results are shown in Fig. 2. Under closed, light-avoiding conditions, ACC decreased from 0.3 to 0 mg/L within 9–10 h, whereas dissolved H2 decreased from 1200 to 0 ppb over the same period. In open, light-exposed environments, ACC decayed to 0 within 3–4 h, and H2 diminished to 0 after 6–7 h. When stored in plastic, glass, or aluminum containers, both ACC and H2 decayed to 0 within 9–10 h. At 45°C, ACC and H2 concentrations reached 0 after 3–4 and 9–10 h, respectively. At 5°C, ACC decayed to 0 within 9–10 h, and H₂ declined to 0 after 11–12 h. These findings demonstrate that HRHAW retains its stability longest under closed, light-avoiding, low-temperature (5°C) storage conditions, which is consistent with the results of previous studies on chlorine stability (He et al., 2013). Hydrogen concentration steadily decreased over time, regardless of the container material, consistent with the findings of earlier studies. However, this study showed little relationship with the storage temperature (Zuo et al., 2025). In summary, to maximize efficacy, HRHAW storage in poultry farms should be prioritized under sealed, dark, and low-temperature conditions.

Efficacy of the combined application: sterilization property of HRHAW

Influence of HRHAW on planktonic bacteria. Fig. 3 shows the effects of tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the concentration of total culturable planktonic bacteria in drinking water within poultry houses. Notably, HRW alone had no significant inhibitory effect on culturable total bacterial counts, whereas both HAW and HRHAW demonstrated potent bactericidal activity. Treatment with HAW and HRHAW reduced bacterial concentrations from 3.5 to 1.7 and 1.8 log CFU/mL, respectively, achieving bactericidal rates of >99.90 %. These results confirm that the antimicrobial action of HAW is primarily driven by its active component, hypochlorous acid (HClO). HClO disrupts bacterial cell membranes, resulting in leakage of critical ions (e.g., K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) that maintain membrane stability. This ion loss increases K+ permeability, denatures transmembrane channel proteins, reduces dehydrogenase activity, and disrupts intracellular enzymatic systems, ultimately causing cellular collapse and death. This result is consistent with the findings of previous studies (Rahman et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). Overall, the results demonstrate that HRHAW exerts a remarkable bactericidal effect on water planktonic bacteria.

Effects of tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the concentration of total culturable planktonic bacteria in drinking water from poultry houses.

Influence of HRHAW on biofilms along the waterline. From the initiation of system operation, microorganisms rapidly colonize the inner surfaces of waterlines and develop into resilient biofilms (Hahne et al., 2022), with biofilm-associated bacteria accounting for more than 90 % of the total bacterial load in drinking water systems (Hall-Stoodley et al., 2004). As shown in Fig. 4, the biofilm biomass levels measured for tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW were recorded at 4.15 ± 0.16, 3.70 ± 0.13, 4.04 ± 0.13, and 3.72 ± 0.13 log CFU/cm, respectively. Compared with tap water, HRW exhibited no significant reduction in biofilm biomass, which is likely due to its lack of direct antimicrobial activity and its inability to degrade EPS, operating solely through antioxidant properties. Conversely, HAW and HRHAW resulted in a significant decrease in biofilm biomass relative to tap water. This effect can be attributed to the available chlorine in HAW, which effectively eliminates free-floating bacteria and partially disrupts EPS matrices (ElKatcha et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022). Although the combined treatment of HRHAW was marginally less effective than HAW alone, it demonstrated superior performance compared with HRW, thereby confirming the main antibacterial role of HAW in the hybrid system. Further analysis revealed that HRW had a negligible impact on biofilm control because of its inherent nonbactericidal properties and the protective effect exerted by EPS barriers. Although HRHAW reduced biofilm biomass via the oxidative capacity of chlorine, complete eradication was unattainable, reflecting intrinsic biofilm tolerance toward disinfectants. This resistance may stem from their stratified architecture and synergistic metabolic adaptations present within bacterial communities (Zhao et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021).

Effects of HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on (a) biofilm biomass, (b) E. coil density, and (c) EPS content.

Effects of HRHAW on Broiler Performance

Production performance. A 42-day trial was conducted to evaluate the production performance of broilers treated with tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW. As shown in Fig. 5, no significant differences in mortality rates were observed among the groups. The F/G ratios for tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW were 1.81, 1.81, 1.75, and 1.75, respectively, with HRHAW showing no statistically significant impact on the F/G ratio. These findings are consistent with those of prior studies, indicating that HRW mitigates heat stress-induced reductions in body weight and feed intake but exerts no notable effects on growth performance under normal conditions. Similarly, HAW demonstrated no adverse or beneficial influence on production metrics (Fanxianzi et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2021).

Effects of HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on broiler production performance.

Meat quality. The evaluation of broiler meat quality from broilers that consumed tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW focused on pH, drip loss, cooking loss, and shear force as key indicators. As shown in Fig. 6, no significant differences were observed in pH, drip loss, or cooking loss across the groups. However, the average shear force values for breast meat in Group A, Group B, Group C, and Group D were 39.41, 33.66, 28.36, and 23.98 N, respectively. Notably, Group C and Group D exhibited significantly lower shear force than the tap water group, indicating that HRW markedly improved breast meat tenderness. These results are consistent with those of previous studies (Zhu et al., 2025), where reduced shear force was associated with HRW’s antioxidant properties, potentially mitigating oxidative stress in muscle fibers or enhancing moisture retention. Although HAW demonstrated lower shear force than tap water, its effect was less pronounced than that of HRW and HRHAW, with no statistically significant impact on breast meat tenderness, likely reflecting its primary antimicrobial rather than meat quality improvement.

Antioxidant capacity. The effects of tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the liver and blood antioxidant profiles of broilers were evaluated (Fig. 7). For liver tissues, superoxide dismutase (SOD)values were 12.62 ± 0.43, 13.48 ± 0.90, 14.56 ± 10.53, and 16.10 ± 1.96; glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX) levels were 23.07 ± 2.34, 25.80 ± 1.52, 27.03 ± 1.93, and 28.87 ± 1.22; total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) values were 1.28 ± 0.15, 1.26 ± 0.13, 1.26 ± 0.05, and 1.25 ± 0.17; and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were 0.48 ± 0.03, 0.38 ± 0.01, 0.30 ± 0.02, and 0.26 ± 0.01 U/mg for the tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW groups, respectively. Compared with tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW increased liver SOD by 6.81 %, 15.34 %, and 27.54 % and GSH-PX by 11.83 %, 17.17 %, and 25.14 %, respectively. In blood, SOD values were 67.04 ± 7.59, 71.41 ± 10.11, 80.37 ± 10.53, and 85.15 ± 2.18; GSH-PX levels were 134.72 ± 18.09, 146.22 ± 20.99, 155.98 ± 38.95, and 203.38 ± 60.21; T-AOC values were 10.97 ± 0.59, 9.89 ± 0.62, 10.98 ± 0.91, and 10.86 ± 0.72; and MDA levels were 3.98 ± 0.82, 3.03 ± 0.37, 2.68 ± 0.18, and 2.18 ± 0.27 nmol/mL for the respective groups. HAW, HRW, and HRHAW increased blood SOD by 6.52 %, 19.89 %, and 27.02 % and GSH-PX by 8.54 %, 15.79 %, and 50.97 %, respectively. T-AOC showed minor fluctuations (−9.84 %, +0.07 %, and −1.01 %) with no significant intergroup differences, likely because the comprehensive nature of T-AOC does not fully reflect individual enzyme changes. HAW, HRW, and HRHAW decreased MDA levels in the blood by 23.89 %, 32.61 %, and 45.14 %, respectively. Treatment with HRHAW significantly enhanced the activity of SOD and GSH-PX while markedly reducing MDA levels, demonstrating an improvement in antioxidant capacity primarily attributed to the antioxidative properties of HRW (Lee et al., 2021). In addition, the combined HRW–HAW treatment further amplified these antioxidant effects, as evidenced by synergistic increases in SOD and GSH-PX activities alongside reductions in MDA levels. This suggests an enhanced ability to mitigate oxidative stress and maintain physiological health. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, indicating that HRW can improve the antioxidant status in poultry (Zhu et al., 2025). The elevated activities of SOD and GSH-PX—key enzymes involved in scavenging free radicals—indicate a strengthened antioxidant defense system (Borgstahl et al., 2018; Surai et al., 2019). In contrast, a reduction in MDA signifies a decrease in lipid peroxidation damage (Bi et al., 2018). Notably, the increase in GSH-PX was particularly pronounced in the HRHAW group, likely due to the synergistic antimicrobial effect of HAW combined with the antioxidant effects of HRW. In conclusion, the combination of HRW and HAW has shown significant potential for enhancing antioxidant properties and promoting organismal health.

Effects of HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on (a–d) liver and (e–h) blood antioxidant capacity.

Intestinal morphology. The effects of drinking tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the intestinal morphology of broilers were examined (Fig. 8). The duodenal, jejunal, and ileal mucosa of broilers in all groups exhibited intact surfaces with visible mucosal folds, normal epithelial and glandular structures, and typical goblet cell distribution. Crypts, villi, and mucosal layers appeared physiologically normal without exhibiting any signs of hyperplasia, edema, or hemorrhage. Notably, in the duodenum and cecum of the broilers drinking tap water, there were some point-like damages to the epithelium and glands. In other words, the overall intestinal morphology of the broilers drinking HRHAW was better than that of the broilers drinking tap water. This is similar to the findings of previous research on piglets (Ji et al., 2019), reinforcing the protective role of HAHRW in preserving intestinal integrity.

Effects of HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the intestinal morphology of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum in the (a–c) tap water, (d–f) HAW, (h–j) HRW, and (k–m) HRHAW groups.

Fig. 9 shows the effects of tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on the positive area ratio of goblet cells in broilers. The positive areas of goblet cells were 7.06 ± 1.28, 8.87 ± 1.72, 11.29 ± 0.42, and 11.72 ± 1.54 for the tap water, HAW, HRW, and HRHAW groups, respectively. HRHAW significantly increased the number of goblet cells by approximately 66 % compared with that in the tap water group. This may be due to the antioxidant effect of HRW (Lee et al., 2021; Sheng et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2017), which exerted a synergistic effect with the antimicrobial properties of HAW. In addition, mucus secreted by goblet cells is an important component of the intestinal barrier, and its increase may enhance intestinal protection by decreasing oxidative stress and pathogenic bacterial loads (Xie et al., 2022). The similar goblet cell area in the HRW and HRHAW groups suggests that the hydrogen-rich component is the main driver, while HAW further optimizes this effect.

Impact of HRHAW on broiler intestinal microbiota. The cecal microbial abundance at the phylum and genus levels in broilers drinking tap water (A), HAW (B), HRW (C), and HRHAW (D) was analyzed (Fig. 10). Excluding unclassified bacteria, the dominant phyla in Group A included Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, Synergistota, Campylobacterota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota. Group B exhibited dominance of Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, Synergistota, Campylobacterota, and Proteobacteria. Group C was characterized by Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, Synergistota, Campylobacterota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Fusobacteriota. Group D showed dominant phyla of Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, Synergistota, Campylobacterota, and Proteobacteria. All four groups displayed absolute dominance of Bacteroidota and Firmicutes, accounting for 83.48 %, 80.00 %, 83.57 %, and 86.21 % in Groups A–D, respectively. Analysis revealed no significant differences in phylum-level bacterial abundance across groups, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies (Dong et al., 2017; Lv et al., 2021). Further examination of the top shared genera across groups indicated no significant differences in relative abundance at the genus level. These results suggest that water treatments have a limited structural impact on cecal microbiota at both the phylum and genus levels. The high prevalence of Bacteroidota and Firmicutes reflects stability in cecal fermentative function, with HAW’s antimicrobial properties and HRW’s antioxidant effects failing to significantly alter core microbial abundance. Substantial overlap in dominant genera across groups implies conserved community functionality.

Effects of HAW, HRW, and HRHAW on relative abundance of cecal microbiota at the phylum and genus levels.

As shown in Fig. 11, comparative analysis of cecal microbiota between broilers drinking tap water (A), HAW (B), HRW (C), and HRHAW (D) revealed significant shifts in microbial ecological structures. Group A exhibited marked enrichment of Coriobacteriaceae, Campylobacter, Campylobacteraceae, Nordella, and Rhizobiales Incertae Sedis. In Group B, Prevotella, Peptococcaceae, and Oxalobacter were notably abundant. Group C showed significant increases in Enterobacter and Vicinamibacteria, whereas Group D displayed enrichment of Helicobacter, Helicobacteraceae, Faecalitalea, Erysipelotrichales, Lactonifactor, Sellimonas, Gallibacterium, and Pasteurellaceae. Notably, the significant enrichment of Campylobacter in Group A may be associated with opportunistic infections that could trigger intestinal inflammation. As a typical pathogenic bacterium, Campylobacter is the most relevant genus for human infections. For Group D, the synergistic effect of HRHAW’s components, HAW and HRW, may have triggered the enrichment of Lactobacillus spp. and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, such as Faecalitalea and Lactonifactor. Faecalitalea and Lactonifactor may influence SCFA production or enhance intestinal barrier function (Zhou et al., 2021), which is consistent with previous results indicating that HRHAW improved intestinal morphology and intestinal barrier consistency. Overall, different water quality treatments significantly shaped the cecum flora pattern (Hao et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2019) through antimicrobial and antioxidant effects influencing intestinal barrier function.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that HRHAW is a safe and effective disinfectant for broiler drinking systems, showing no adverse effects on feed efficiency or mortality. HRHAW eliminated >99.90 % of waterborne pathogens, reduced pipeline biofilm biomass, and enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity, which corresponded with improved meat tenderness likely through mitigation of myofiber oxidative stress. The combined action of HRW and HAW improved intestinal morphology and barrier integrity while reshaping the cecal microbiota via their antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. The administration of HRHAW induced no toxicological or pathological alterations, confirming its safety for agricultural use. For optimal efficacy, light should be avoided, and storage at 5°C in sealed containers is recommended. These findings support the utility of HRHAW in enhancing broiler health and product quality. However, future research should assess the potential long-term effects of microbiota modulation on intestinal homeostasis.
