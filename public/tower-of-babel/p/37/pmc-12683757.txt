Impact of Antibiotic Exposure on Pediatric Drug Eruption: A Nationwide Cohort Study in Korea
Authors: Soo Min Jeon, Junwoo Jo, Juhee Ryu, Jin-Won Kwon, Jiung Jeong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAntibiotic-related drug eruption is a common cutaneous adverse reaction in pediatric populations. However, its incidence and risk factor remain unclear. This study investigated the incidence of drug eruptions in pediatrics and its association with antibiotic exposure to identify potential risk factors.MethodsUsing the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service-Pediatric Patient Sample database (2009–2019), we conducted a retrospective nationwide cohort study involving 6,699,010 pediatric patients. Drug eruptions were identified based on administrative claims data using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes L27.0 and L27.1. The incidence was compared between antibiotic-exposed and unexposed groups.ResultsWe analyzed 4,107,522 patients in the antibiotic-exposed group and 2,591,488 patients in the antibiotic-unexposed group. The overall incidence of drug eruptions was 10.84 per 10,000 person-years, with higher rates in the antibiotic-exposed group than in the antibiotic-unexposed group (14.59 vs. 4.89 per 10,000 person-years, Log rank P < 0.001). Any exposure to antibiotics significantly increased drug eruption risk (adjusted hazard ratio, 2.86; 95% confidence interval, 2.27–3.60). The risk of drug eruption was higher among pediatric patients who used multiple antibiotics than in those with single antibiotic use. The association remained consistent across age groups and was robust in sensitivity analyses, including extended follow-ups and inpatient-only outcomes.ConclusionsAntibiotic exposure increases the risk of drug eruption in pediatric populations, particularly with multiple antibiotic use. Careful consideration is needed when prescribing antibiotics to children, especially in combination therapy.Keywords: Anti-bacterial agents, drug eruptions, adverse drug reaction, incidence, exposure, pediatrics, cohort studies, risk

INTRODUCTION

Drug eruptions are adverse cutaneous reactions to medications, encompassing a spectrum from mild maculopapular rashes to severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN).1 In general, drug eruptions affect approximately 2%–3% of hospitalized patients and account for 45% of all adverse drug reactions, resulting in significant healthcare costs and prolonged hospital stays.2

Antibiotics are a significant cause of drug eruption in children. In Korea, antibiotics are the leading cause of pediatric drug eruption and also account for the largest proportion of pediatric SCARs.3,4 Pediatric drug eruptions, especially those induced by antibiotics, are less extensively studied and characterized than adult drug eruptions, resulting in critical gaps in our understanding. Previous pediatric studies have primarily focused on identifying the most common causative antibiotics rather than exploring broader risk factors.5,6 Given the frequent need for combination antibiotic therapy in pediatric patients, data on the effects of multi- antibiotic exposure on drug eruption risk are still lacking. Therefore, it is necessary to quantify this risk using population-based evidence in pediatric patients.

Although a few studies have examined associations between antibiotic exposure and drug eruptions in children,7,8,9 they have largely been limited to severe reactions such as SCARs, while a comprehensive analyses of broader drug eruptions is still lacking. Moreover, much of the current evidence is based on adult populations, despite recognized epidemiological differences between children and adults. Furthermore, there is limited evidence regarding relationships between the number of antibiotic classes and the risk of drug eruption in children. The lack of population-based data on the incidence of pediatric drug eruptions and their association with antibiotic exposure patterns represents a significant evidence gap that limits clinical decision-making.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the incidence of drug eruptions in children and to examine the association between antibiotic exposure and the risk of drug eruption using a large-scale, population-based claims database.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

This study used the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) Pediatric Patient Sample (PPS) database from 2009 to 2019. The HIRA is an independent government institution that reviews and assesses medical services and fees to ensure medical appropriateness. South Korea operates a national single-payer health insurance system, which covers about 97% of the population (with the remaining 3% covered by the medical aid program). The HIRA-PPS is an annual sample of all patients under 20 years of age, obtained through sex- and age-stratified random sampling. This sample represents 10% of all patients under the age of 20 years. Because the HIRA-PPS is extracted annually, patients from each year may differ, with no duplication across years. This dataset includes comprehensive medical claims records, including demographic information (sex, age, and health insurance type), diagnostic, procedural treatment, and prescribed drug records. The diagnostic records were presented using the International Classification of Disease 10th Revision (ICD-10). The research was exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyungpook University, as it utilized deidentified data made available to researchers through HIRA’s review process (IRB number: 2023-0446).

Study period and population

This retrospective nationwide cohort study was conducted using annually organized data from the HIRA-PPS database. In this study period for each year, the study period spanned from April 1st to December 31th. This period included a 6-month enrollment period (April 1st to September 30th) to allow for at least a 3-month baseline assessment and a minimum 3-month follow-up period (from index date to December 31th). We provided a visualization of each phase within the study period (Supplementary Fig. S1).

We identified all patients who had at least one medical service record during the enrollment period and categorized the patients into 2 groups: the antibiotic-exposed group (J01 according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes) and the antibiotic-unexposed group. The index date for the antibiotic-exposed group was defined as the first date of antibiotic use. For patients in the antibiotic-unexposed group, the index date was defined as the first date of the medical service record. We then excluded patients who had any record of antibiotic prescription or hospitalizations before the index date. Patients diagnosed with any comorbidities during the enrollment period were also excluded. To identify incident cases of drug eruption, we further excluded patients with a prior diagnosis record of severe cutaneous adverse reactions, including drug eruption, SJS, and TEN, before the index date. We present codes used in the cohort selection (Supplementary Table S1).

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of drug eruptions in pediatric patients. Owing to the inherent limitations of claims data in confirming the clinical diagnosis of drug eruption, we used a broader operational definition of drug eruption as a proxy outcome. Drug eruption was identified using the ICD-10 codes L27.0 and L27.1, as recorded in all the medical claims during the follow-up period. All study participants were followed from the index date until the first occurrence of drug eruption, 30 days after the index date, or the end of the study period, whichever came first.

The secondary outcome was to assess the impact of antibiotic use on the development of pediatric drug eruptions by comparing its incidence between children with and without antibiotic use. The 30-day follow-up period was chosen based on previous reports indicating a mean latency of approximately 9 days for antibiotic-related eruptions.

Antibiotic exposure

For patients in the antibiotic-exposed group, we extracted all records of oral or injectable antibiotic prescriptions at the index date. To assess the association between antibiotic exposure and the occurrence of drug eruptions, antibiotic exposure was categorized in 3 ways: 1) any exposure (exposed vs. unexposed), 2) the number of antibiotic classes (1 vs. 2 or more), and 3) individual antibiotic classes. The individual antibiotic classes included penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, glycopeptides, lacosamides, monobactams, nitroimidazoles, amphenicols, and other antibacterials (Supplementary Table S2).

Covariates

We measured potential covariates for the association between antibiotic exposure and drug eruptions, including sex, age group (0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–19 years), health insurance type (health insurance vs. medical aid), infection-related diagnoses (bacterial infection and viral infection), and comorbidity (asthma and allergic rhinitis). Information on sex, age group, health insurance type, and infection-related diagnosis was assessed at the index date, whereas comorbidities were evaluated during the baseline period before the index date. In addition, the use of medications known to be associated with drug eruption, including antiepileptics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), acetaminophen, tramadol, and anti-tuberculosis drugs, was included as potential covariates. Exposure to these medications was assessed within 30 days after the index antibiotic prescription. Detailed diagnostic and medication codes are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of children exposed and unexposed to antibiotics were summarized using descriptive statistics such as frequencies and proportions. Group comparisons were conducted using 2-sided χ2 tests, with the statistical significance set at P value < 0.05. The Kaplan–Meier method with a log-rank test was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of drug eruption based on antibiotic exposure. Crude incidence rates were calculated as the number of new events per 10,000 person-years during the follow-up period. To estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between antibiotic exposure (any exposure, number of antibiotics, and individual antibiotic classes) and the risk of drug eruption, we employed Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models. The models were adjusted for sex, age group, health insurance type, infection-related diagnosis, comorbidities (allergic rhinitis and asthma), and the use of other medications known to cause drug eruption.

To evaluate the robustness of our main analysis, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age group (0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–19 years). Sensitivity analyses were also performed using different follow-up periods (60- and 90-day follow-up) and restricting the outcome to inpatient-diagnosed status. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide 6.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R 3.6.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

A total of 6,699,010 pediatric patients were included in the cohort, comprising 4,107,522 in the antibiotic-exposed group and 2,591,488 in the antibiotic-unexposed group (Fig. 1). The proportion of male patients was similar between groups (51.03% in the antibiotic-exposed group vs. 50.74% in the antibiotic-unexposed group, P = 0.029). The antibiotic-exposed group showed a younger age distribution, with a higher proportion of children aged 0–4 years (20.45% vs. 11.74%, P < 0.001), whereas the antibiotic-unexposed group showed a higher proportions in the older age groups (10–14 years, 32.74%; 15–19 years, 36.33%). Most patients in both groups were covered by health insurance (96.96% in the antibiotic-exposed group vs. 97.13% in the antibiotic-unexposed group).

The antibiotic-exposed group exhibited higher rates of common bacterial infections (39.91% vs. 8.32%, P < 0.001), with pharyngitis (24.05%) and sinusitis (15.77%) being the most common. Similarly, viral infections were more prevalent in the antibiotic-exposed group (28.57% vs. 15.53%, P < 0.001), particularly viral upper respiratory tract infections (20.69%). Allergic rhinitis (44.86% vs. 19.30%, P < 0.001) and asthma (7.92% vs. 2.04%, P < 0.001) were also more common in the antibiotic-exposed group. Regarding concomitant medication use, NSAIDs (53.18% vs. 23.56%) and acetaminophen (43.40% vs. 17.15%) were the most frequently prescribed medications, with substantially higher use in the antibiotic-exposed group (Table 1).

In the antibiotic-exposed group, the majority of patients (94.67%) received a single class of antibiotics. Penicillins were the most frequently prescribed (44.16%), followed by cephalosporins (33.81%) and macrolides (12.68%). Among patients who received two or more antibiotic classes, the most common combinations were cephalosporin plus aminoglycoside (2.15%) and penicillin plus cephalosporin (1.07%) (Supplementary Table S4).

During the 30-day follow-up period, a total of 596 cases of drug eruption were identified in the overall cohort. The overall incidence was 10.84 per 10,000 person-years, with a higher rate observed in the antibiotic-exposed group compared to the antibiotic-unexposed group (14.59 vs. 4.89 per 10,000 person-years) (Supplementary Table S5). Fig. 2 shows the estimated incidence of drug eruption within 30 days after the index date. In both groups most events occurred within the first 10 days, followed by a gradual decline over time. The cumulative incidence of drug eruption was consistently higher in the antibiotic-exposed group than in the antibiotic-unexposed group (log-rank P < 0.001).

Cox PH regression analysis demonstrated that antibiotic exposure was significantly associated with an increased risk of drug eruption (unadjusted HR, 2.99; 95% CI, 2.42–3.69; adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 2.86; 95% CI, 2.27–3.60). In adjusted model, age showed a lower risk in patients aged 5–9 years (aHR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.53–0.90) than in patients aged 0–4 years. Furthermore, the use of antiepileptic drugs significantly increased the risk of drug eruption (aHR, 7.85; 95% CI, 4.52–13.63) (Table 2).

The risk of drug eruption showed a dose-dependent increase with the number of antibiotic classes. Patients exposed to 2 or more antibiotic classes had a significantly higher risk (aHR, 5.72; 95% CI, 4.11–7.96) compared to those exposed to a single class (aHR, 2.62; 95% CI, 2.08–3.31). When antibiotic regimens were analyzed in detail, most regimens were associated with a significantly increased risk of drug eruption, with combination therapies showing a stronger association than monotherapies. Among monotherapies, macrolide was associated with the highest risk (aHR, 5.94; 95% CI, 2.88–12.23), while aminoglycoside had a relatively lower risk (aHR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.41–2.95). The combination of cephalosporin plus macrolide showed the strongest association with drug eruption (aHR, 13.98; 95% CI, 5.09–38.42) (Fig. 3). Even when focusing solely on penicillin or cephalosporin monotherapy, combinations of two or more antibiotics demonstrated significantly higher risks than monotherapy. For instance, cephalosporin plus other antibiotics were associated with a 7.55-fold higher risk compared to cephalosporin monotherapy (aHR, 7.55; 95% CI, 2.38–23.97) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

We observed a consistent trend in the risk of developing drug eruption associated with antibiotics across all age groups (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Fig. S3). Furthermore, this association remained robust in sensitivity analyses, including extended follow-up periods of 60 and 90 days and analyses limited to inpatient-diagnosed outcomes (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Fig. S4).

DISCUSSION

This nationwide cohort study provided the first population-based evidence of a dose-dependent relationship between antibiotic polypharmacy and pediatric drug eruption risk. Our key finding—that concurrent use of multiple antibiotic classes increases drug eruption risk by 5.72-fold compared to no antibiotic exposure, and 2.62-fold compared to single antibiotic use—represents a previously unrecognized and clinically significant safety concern. These findings underscore the importance of exercising caution in the use of multiple antibiotics and avoiding unnecessary antibiotic prescribing in pediatric populations.

The overall incidence of drug eruption in our pediatric cohort was 10.84 per 10,000 person-years. While the incidence of drug eruption in adults has been well documented, approximately 10 cases per 1,000 new drug exposure,10 its incidence in pediatric populations remains poorly understood. Previous studies conducted in pediatrics have focused on identifying clinical features and causative agents among a limited number of confirmed drug eruption cases, making it more difficult to estimate population-level conclusion.5,11 To address this gap, we analyzed large-scale data from the HIRA-PPS, comprising 4,107,522 antibiotic-exposed and 2,591,488 antibiotic-unexposed pediatric patients. Almost the entire Korean population is covered by the National Health Insurance Service, with comprehensive medical records maintained in the HIRA database.12 The HIRA-PPS allows for detailed analysis of pediatric patient data while preserving patient anonymity. This dataset provides detailed demographics and clinical information such as age, prescribed medications, and comorbidities. Although our study did not specifically identify clinically confirmed drug eruption, applying a broader definition of drug eruption within a nationwide claims database enabled a more comprehensive understanding of its potential burden in pediatric populations.

Among antibiotic-exposed patients, the incidence of drug eruption increased to 14.59 per 10,000 person-years, compared to 4.89 per 10,000 person-years in the unexposed group. Through comprehensive analysis stratified by antibiotic class, we revealed that antibiotic exposure was associated with an increased risk of drug eruption, with combination antibiotic therapy further elevating this risk. Previous studies have identified various antibiotics as suspected drugs for drug eruption in pediatric patients.5,13,14 Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that exposure to antibiotics was significantly associated with drug eruption, which is recognized as a potential trigger of T cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. Although we could not demonstrate a significant difference due to the limited sample size, our findings highlight the need for awareness and caution regarding the risk of drug eruption across all antibiotic classes. In addition, we provided evidence that concomitant use of multiple antibiotics was associated with an increased risk of drug eruption. Treatment with a single antibiotic class was associated with a 2.62-fold increased risk of drug eruption, whereas combination antibiotic therapy increased the risk by 5.72-fold compared to antibiotic-unexposed group. These findings suggest that minimizing unnecessary combination antibiotic therapy may help reduce the risk of drug eruption in pediatric populations.

The association between multiple antibiotics and drug eruption might be related to changes in the gut microbiota due to antibiotic use. Several studies have suggested that the overactive T cell immune response was suggested to be due to an antibiotic-mediated reduction in regulatory T cells that control inflammatory responses.15,16,17 Furthermore, multi-antibiotic exposure has been reported as a contributing factor that aggravates various inflammatory conditions by disrupting gut microbiota.15,17 In particular, one study found that mice younger than 6 months had poorer diversity of their gut microbiota after antibiotic exposure compared to adults.17 In line with these findings, we observed that younger children aged 0–4 who exposed to multiple antibiotics tended to have a higher risk of drug eruption, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. These findings underscore the need for careful antibiotic use during early childhood.

In this study, we observed that a high proportion of children with viral upper respiratory infections (70.42%), bronchitis (83.62%), bronchiolitis (90.20%), and nonsuppurative otitis media (93.35%) received unnecessary antibiotic treatment. Common viral infections in pediatric patients include upper respiratory infections caused by rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza, adenovirus, and influenza; lower respiratory tract infections such as bronchitis and bronchiolitis, often attributed to rhinovirus, metapneumovirus, coronavirus, and influenza; and nonsuppurative otitis media, which is frequently associated with adenovirus, rhinovirus, enterovirus, and coronavirus.18,19,20 Antibiotics are generally not indicated for viral infections, except in instances of bacterial superinfection, such as pneumonia or suppurative otitis media, or in high-risk patients, such as those with cystic fibrosis or immunodeficiency.21 Inappropriate antibiotic use can cause adverse drug events, antibiotic resistance, and Clostridioides difficile-induced diarrhea, which ultimately increases healthcare costs.22 Therefore, efforts to ensure appropriate antibiotic use could be a valuable strategy for reducing the risk of drug eruption. To reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, several strategies can be considered: utilization of biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, Myxovirus resistance protein A, and procalcitonin; outpatient finger-stick blood tests such as FebriDx that distinguish between bacterial and viral infections; stewardship programs that assess antibiotic adequacy; and a change in awareness to reduce antibiotic use.23,24,25 In Finland, antibiotic prescriptions for children under 18 years diagnosed with upper respiratory tract infections decreased significantly from 18.0% in 2014 to 8.8% in 2020. This was due to the use of stewardship programs to reduce antibiotic prescriptions and improve consumer awareness following the disclosure of antibiotic prescription rates.26 We expect that a reduction in inappropriate antibiotic exposure will contribute to a decrease in the incidence of drug eruption.

This study presents several limitations. First, the use of ICD-10 codes in administrative claims data might be insufficient for accurately identifying drug eruptions. In the Korean healthcare system, diagnostic codes are often entered for reimbursement or administrative purposes, potentially leading to misclassification bias. Secondly, although we adjusted several confounding factors in the analysis, residual confounding may remain due to unmeasured variables, such as infection severity, polypharmacy, or concurrent medication use. Nevertheless, when we restricted the analysis to inpatient cases—who are more likely to represent patients with more severe infections and a higher burden of concurrent medications, the association between antibiotic use and drug eruption remained consistent with the main analysis. Thirdly, the retrospective design of the study precludes the establishment of a direct causal relationship between antibiotic use and the occurrence of drug eruption. Finally, there is a paucity of analysis regarding other potential risk factors, such as genetic predisposition and viral reactivation. To address these limitations, future research should focus on analyzing the incidence and severity of drug eruption in relation to antibiotic use within a cohort of drug eruption patients confirmed by drug allergy specialists. This approach would enhance the validity and clinical applicability of the findings.

In summary, the incidence of pediatric drug eruption was 10.84 per 10,000 person-years. The use of antibiotics was associated with a 2.86-fold increased risk, and the administration of two or more antibiotics was associated with a 5.72-fold higher risk. Therefore, we propose that unnecessary antibiotic use in children should be reduced, and caution should be taken when prescribing multiple antibiotics.
