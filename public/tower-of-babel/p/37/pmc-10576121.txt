Dynamic extracellular vestibule of human SERT: Unveiling druggable potential with high-affinity allosteric inhibitors
Authors: Kristine Salomon, Ara M. Abramyan, Per Plenge, Lingle Wang, Christoffer Bundgaard, Benny Bang-Andersen, Claus J. Loland, Lei Shi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe human serotonin transporter (hSERT) has been targeted to treat depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorders. An allosteric modulator binds to a protein at a site different from the protein’s primary active (orthosteric) site, and allosteric modulation is important for therapeutic targets of which orthosteric inhibition may either be ineffective or result in severe side effects. Here, we report the identification of a series of high-affinity allosteric inhibitors of hSERT with the lead compound having low nanomolar allosteric potency. The predicted and then validated binding location and pose of the lead compound at hSERT can explain much of the structure–activity relationship of these inhibitors. Our findings provide a promising lead compound and the structural basis for developing allosteric drugs targeting hSERT.

The human serotonin transporter (hSERT) is one of the most therapeutically targeted proteins in the human body. In particular, it is the target of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), which are the most successful and widely used treatment for common mood disorders, including depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorders. However, while the annual prevalence of a major depressive episode among US adults is 7.1%, more than 30% of the medication-treated patients with major depressive disorder suffer from treatment-resistant depression (1). Thus, there is a significant demand for alternative medications, targeting either hSERT or other targets (2).

SERT is located in the vicinity of the presynaptic neuron membrane, where it terminates the serotonergic signals by a rapid reuptake of the released serotonin (5-HT). This process hinders chemical cross-talk among neighboring synapses, thus making SERT a tight regulator of 5-HT signaling tone. In addition, the reuptake ensures the replenishment of 5-HT, which reduces the cost of its de novo synthesis (3). SERT belongs to the neurotransmitter:sodium symporter (NSS) family, the members of which are all secondary active transporters that transport cognate substrates against their concentration gradients by using the energy stored in the down-hill transport of other solute(s). For SERT, its uptake of 5-HT has been found to couple to a cotransport of Na+ (4) and an antiport of K+ (5) down their gradients.

The experimentally determined hSERT structures have revealed atomistic details of substrate and inhibitor bindings at the binding pockets located both centrally in the transmembrane domain and in the extracellular vestibule, i.e., the S1 and S2 sites, respectively (6–10). Together with the conformational transitions and dynamics in outward-open, occluded, and inward-open states characterized by structure determinations, mutagenesis, as well as computational simulations, critical insights on the transport, as well as the inhibition mechanisms of hSERT, begin to emerge (8, 11–17). Specifically, the translocations of substrate and ions are characterized by the alternating-access model in which the substrate binding site opens and closes to the intracellular and extracellular gates alternately, which ensures that accessibility to the binding site is only available from one side at a time (8, 10, 18).

It has been known for the past four decades that the function of hSERT can be allosterically modulated by both inhibitors and 5-HT (19). Allosteric modulation opens promising venues for therapeutic research, especially for physiologically important targets of which orthosteric inhibition may either be ineffective or result in severe side effects. The SSRI S-citalopram (S-CIT) has long been used as the model drug for studying allosteric inhibition of hSERT (15, 20). Unfortunately, its allosteric potency is very low (~5 µM), approximately 1,000-fold lower than its binding at the S1 site, and minor modifications on its scaffold have not significantly improved its affinity (21). We recently reported that vilazodone binds allosterically at hSERT (14 nM) in the presence of the S1-bound imipramine (IMI); however, its clinical profile is not much different from the SSRIs (6). The most promising allosteric hSERT inhibitor is, to the best of our knowledge, Lu AF60097, which has high allosteric potency (30 nM) and is based on the S-CIT scaffold (22). Moreover, we found that Lu AF60097 can synergistically inhibit hSERT with imipramine at their subthreshold concentrations both in vitro and in vivo (22). However, while Lu AF60097 is an excellent tool for in vivo studies if administered directly into the brain (22), it displays very limited brain availability if given systemically. Thus, we set out to search for a brain penetrant hSERT inhibitor with high affinity for the allosteric site. Inspired by the structural motifs of Lu AF60097 and vilazodone, we selected Lu 35-138 as a parent compound to develop allosteric inhibitors of hSERT. Lu 35-138 is an in vivo active and brain penetrant hSERT inhibitor, showing inhibition of [3H]5-HT uptake into rat whole brain synaptosomes (23).

Here, we pharmacologically characterize Lu 35-138 and its analogs and show that the lead compound Lu AF88273 has higher allosteric potencies than S-CIT, vilazodone, and Lu AF60097. Based on our extensive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, the binding free energy calculations with both absolute and relative protein–ligand binding free energy perturbation (AB-FEP and RB-FEP, respectively) algorithms, we reveal the binding pose of Lu AF88273, as well as the structure–activity relationship (SAR) of the analogs at the S2 site. The binding mechanism of Lu AF88273 is then substantiated with mutagenesis of key interacting residues.

Results

A Congeneric Series of Compounds Has Allosteric Inhibition Potency at hSERT.

With the overarching goal of identifying high-affinity hSERT allosteric inhibitors with promising pharmacological properties, we identified that Lu 35-138 ((S)-1a), which was a potential antipsychotic drug in clinical development by H. Lundbeck A/S, is a potent hSERT S2 inhibitor. Lu 35-138 incorporates a tetrahydropyridine moiety and has a chiral center at the 3-position of the indoline ring. In order to probe whether the chemical labile tetrahydropyridine moiety could be replaced with the corresponding piperidine moiety and whether the chiral center could be avoided, a series of analogs were selected from the Lundbeck compound collection or synthesized (Table 1).

We first investigated the allosteric potencies of Lu 35-138 and these analogs with a radioligand dissociation assay. As previously shown by us and others (6, 15, 21, 22, 24), this assay measures the capability of an allosterically bound hSERT ligand in different concentrations to inhibit the dissociation of a S1-bound radioligand. The dose dependence of the inhibition is then used to indirectly assess the affinity of the allosteric ligand, which is expressed as an IC50 value and referred to as allosteric potency below. Thus, after first incubating the membranes prepared from COS7 cells transiently transfected with hSERT wild type (WT) with a radioactive high-affinity SERT inhibitor ([3H]IMI or [3H]S-CIT), we added a compound in various concentrations and traced the dissociation of radioligand over time (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2A). All tested compounds showed a dose-dependent decrease in the dissociation rates of both [3H]IMI (Fig. 1) and [3H]S-CIT (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Notably, all the IC50 values of these compounds in inhibiting [3H]S-CIT dissociation (SI Appendix, Table S1) were several-fold higher compared to those in inhibiting [3H]IMI dissociation (Table 1). This finding is similar to our previous study (22), in which we also found a lower allosteric potency of Lu AF60097 in inhibiting the dissociation of prebound [3H]S-CIT compared to prebound [3H]IMI.

In addition, while the IC50 values of these compounds inhibiting [3H]S-CIT dissociation distribute in a relatively small range (395 to 1,857 nM, SI Appendix, Table S1), we found that their allosteric potencies in inhibiting [3H]IMI dissociation varied widely (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The parent compound in this study, Lu 35-138, has an allosteric potency of 43.8 [24.7; 78.5] nM inhibiting [3H]IMI dissociation. Interestingly, the epimer of Lu 35-138, (R)-1a, has only less than twofold larger IC50 value, suggesting that the chiral center at the 3-position of the indoline ring does not play a critical role in binding to hSERT. While most of the other analogs have lower potencies than that of Lu 35-138, ranging from 169 to 3,173 nM, two analogs have higher potencies. In particular, the most potent Lu AF88273 (4b) has an allosteric potency of 2.1 [1.5; 3.0] nM, while compound 4a is ~12-fold less potent than Lu AF88273 (25.5 [15.8; 40.8] nM). Notably, compared to Lu 35-138, both of these two analogs have the attachment of an indoline ring via its chiral center replaced by an attachment of an unmodified and achiral indole ring.

For Lu AF88273, which has markedly higher allosteric potency than the other analogs, we assessed its blood–brain barrier transport in vivo and found it to rapidly, and to a high extent, enter the brain after systemic administration (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results and Fig. S7). Thus, we chose Lu AF88273 as the lead compound for further characterizations.

Lu AF88273 Inhibits 5-HT Transport and Inhibitor Binding in a Noncompetitive Manner.

To further characterize the pharmacological properties of the Lu AF88273 at SERT, we assessed its capability to inhibit serotonin (5-HT) transport and to displace the bound orthosteric inhibitors. To determine the potency of Lu AF88273 in inhibiting 5-HT transport, COS7 cells transiently transfected with hSERT WT were preincubated with Lu AF88273 in increasing concentrations, followed by the addition of [3H]5-HT (11.6 nM). We observed that Lu AF88273 inhibits [3H]5-HT uptake with a Ki of 53 [38; 74] nM (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S7). Similarly, we investigated whether Lu AF88273 was able to displace the S1-bound inhibitors [3H]S-CIT and [3H]IMI (3 nM). The results revealed potencies of 40 [36; 44] nM and 73 [42; 127] nM, respectively, in these displacements (SI Appendix, Table S7).

We then determined whether the inhibition of transport and binding is competitive or noncompetitive relative to the orthosteric ligands, by assessing the influence of Lu AF88273 binding on Km and Vmax of [3H]5-HT transport as well as Kd and Bmax of [3H]IMI or [3H]S-CIT binding. For the uptake experiment, COS7 cells transfected with hSERT WT were preincubated with various Lu AF88273 concentrations before applying increasing [3H]5-HT concentrations until reaching Vmax. We found that the Vmax of [3H]5-HT transport was decreased with the increasing concentrations of Lu AF88273. However, the decrease in Vmax reached a plateau of around 55% at 400 nM of Lu AF88273 and could not be further decreased (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S2). Importantly, no significant difference in the Km values was observed. In the homologous competition binding assays using either IMI or S-CIT, the application of either 100 or 250 nM of Lu AF88273 led to notable reductions in Bmax without any significant influence on the Kd values for either S-CIT or IMI (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Table S8). The lack of impact on Km or Kd is indicative of noncompetitive inhibition.

Together, the results demonstrate that Lu AF88273 inhibited [3H]5-HT transport as well as [3H]IMI and [3H]S-CIT binding with comparable potencies in a noncompetitive manner. These findings support the hypothesis that Lu AF88273 binds allosterically to hSERT.

Lu AF88273 and Imipramine Synergistically Inhibit [3H]5-HT Transport.

Due to the higher allosteric potency of Lu AF88273 in the presence of [3H]IMI compared to [3H]S-CIT, we sought to investigate whether Lu AF88273 and IMI could synergistically inhibit [3H]5-HT transport. Accordingly, we measured the inhibition of [3H]5-HT transport in the presence of IMI (4 nM), Lu AF88273 (70 nM), or a combination of both. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9, IMI and Lu AF88273 individually inhibited [3H]5-HT transport by 19.6 ± 6.2% and 23.4 ± 3.9%, respectively. Remarkably, when the two ligands were combined, we observed an inhibition of 52.4 ± 3.9%. This increase in inhibition is significantly (P < 0.05) larger than the combined individual effects of IMI and Lu AF88273, suggesting a synergistic mechanism where the binding of one ligand promotes the binding of the other.

MD Simulations Revealed a Stable Lu AF88273 Binding Pose in the S2 Site.

To investigate the potential binding pose of Lu AF88273 and the mechanism of its allosteric inhibition at hSERT, we first carried out a combined docking and mutagenesis investigation to confirm that the high-affinity binding of Lu AF88273 is at the S2 but not the S1 site (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results). Our following molecular modeling and simulation study was then focused on exploring the poses of Lu AF88273 within the S2 site. Based on our previous mechanistic findings (15, 22), we docked Lu AF88273 in the S2 site of our established hSERT models (22), in the presence of IMI or S-CIT bound in the S1 site (Materials and Methods).

Lu AF88273 has three ring moieties, indole (“I”), piperidine (“P”), and 6-Cl-indole (“C”) rings, connected sequentially in a linear shape (Table 1). The centrally located protonated nitrogen on the piperidine ring and two terminal indole rings render the molecule some symmetry, which, however, is disrupted by the 6-Cl modification. In our docking results, while multiple possible Lu AF88273 poses were found in each condition, the largest clusters of poses can be categorized in two orientations, with either the 6-Cl-indole (“C”) or the unmodified indole ring (“I”) facing the S1 site, which we termed the “CPI” and “IPC” orientations, respectively, with the first letter indicating the ring (“C” or “I”) closer to the S1 site. By selecting the centroid poses for each orientation in each condition, we immersed the corresponding hSERT models into an explicit lipid bilayer–water environment (Materials and Methods, Fig. 3A), carried out extensive and prolonged MD simulations, and collected multiple trajectories for each condition (SI Appendix, Table S3).

We first examined the simulation results of two S1:IMI conditions, namely S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-CPI and S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-IPC. We found that Lu AF88273 in both CPI and IPC orientations were retained in the S2 site in all the MD trajectories, which were as long as 3.6 microseconds in simulation length. However, Lu AF88273 in the IPC orientation was quickly relaxed and converged to a defined pose, which was clearly more stable than when it is in the CPI orientation (Fig. 3 B and C). Correspondingly, from the contact frequency calculations of MD simulation results of both conditions (Materials and Methods), the identified contact residues for S2:Lu AF88273-IPC all had nearly 100% contact frequencies, while some of those of S2:Lu AF88273-CPI were not in persistent interactions (SI Appendix, Table S4). The stableness of the IPC pose is also reflected in its smaller ligand pairwise rmsd (3.3 ± 1.2 and 0.8 ± 0.3Å, for CPI and IPC poses, respectively).

Specifically in the IPC pose, the unmodified indole ring of Lu AF88273 stacks with the guanidino group of R104 in TM1, and they form a cation–π interaction (Fig. 4B) similar to that between R104 and the benzofuran moiety of S2:S-CIT or S2:Lu AF60097, observed in the crystal structure (PDB: 5I73) (10) and our previous MD simulations (22), respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In coordination with this interaction, the nitrogen of this unmodified indole ring also established a hydrogen bond (H-bond) with the sidechain carboxy group of E493 in TM10, while R104 and E493 were persistently in an ionic lock interaction in our simulations. Compared to the IPC pose, the CPI pose of Lu AF88273 could similarly form a H-bond to E493 with the nitrogen on the 6-Cl-indole ring, but this ring could not stack with the guanidino group of R104. Instead, it pointed toward T497, resulting in a sidechain rotation of the gating residue F335 to close the S1 site (the χ1 rotamer of F335 was in trans). In both the S1:IMI-bound hSERT structure (PDB: 7LWD) (6) and our previous MD simulations (22), the S1:IMI appears to prefer F335 to be in an open orientation with its χ1 rotamer in gauche-. Thus, this incompatibility of F335 orientation with S1:IMI likely contributes to the instability of the S2-bound Lu AF88273 in the CPI orientation.

Black dotted circles indicate the locations of the cavities at the TM1 and TM6 interface (formed by residues R104, Q332, F335, and S336) and at the TM11 and TM12 interface (dynamically formed after the rotation of F556). Black dashed lines indicate the interactions uniquely formed in the IPC pose.

This divergence between the IPC and CPI poses near R104 suggests that an aryl chloride is not favored in the subpocket formed by R104, Q332, S336, and F335, which is in the vicinity of the bound Na+ and Cl− ions. On the other hand, the subpocket may favor some moieties possessing specific partial charge distributions (Fig. 4). For example, the cyano group of S2:S-CIT, which is highly stable in this subpocket, has a partial negative charge on the terminal nitrogen and a partial positive charge on the carbon. In comparison, while 6-Cl of Lu AF88273 overall has a partial negative charge, its σ-hole results in a terminal positively charged region (25). The varied interactions to R104 affect the positioning of the protonated piperidine nitrogen of Lu AF88273, leading to a higher probability in forming a water-mediated interaction with the negatively charged E494 in the IPC than CPI pose.

On the solvent-exposed end of the S2 site away from the S1 site, both the IPC and CPI poses are in contact with F556 in TM11. When S2:Lu AF88273 is in the IPC pose, the sidechain of F556 rotates to open a small cavity at the interface between TM11 and TM12, in which the hydroxyl of Y579 in TM12 interacts with the 6-Cl group of Lu AF88273. Consequently, F556 is tightly packed against both the 6-Cl-indole and piperidine rings of Lu AF88273 in its IPC pose (Fig. 4B). Note that this reconfiguration of F556 is also observed in the hSERT structure bound with S2:vilazodone (PDB: 7LWD) compared to the one bound with S2:S-CIT (PDB: 5I73), resulting in forming a small cavity not present in the presence of S2:S-CIT, for S2:vilazodone to potentially interact with Y579 as well.

AB-FEP Calculations Indicated that Lu AF88273 in the IPC Pose Has Higher Binding Affinity.

To further characterize the CPI and IPC poses of S2:Lu AF88273 in the presence of S1:IMI, we applied the AB-FEP approach (Materials and Methods). AB-FEP is a theoretically rigorous description of protein–ligand binding thermodynamics (26, 27), and it has been applied with high accuracy (root mean square errors of 0.8 to 1.4 kcal/mol) toward a wide variety of diverse drug-like molecules as well as in resolving ambiguity between binding modes (26, 28). We first selected a representative frame from the MD simulations of the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-IPC condition (i.e., the IPC pose-stabilized hSERT conformation, referred to as “hSERT-IPC”) and carried out the AB-FEP calculations for both IPC and CPI poses—in the latter case, the original S2:Lu AF88273 was removed (Materials and Methods), and a new Lu AF88273 molecule was docked into the S2 site, and a CPI pose similar to that observed in the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-CPI simulations was selected. Our results showed that the Lu AF88273 bound in the IPC pose has a binding free energy (ΔG) of −20.7 ± 0.1 kcal/mol, while the ΔG of its CPI pose is −13.2 ± 0.1 kcal/mol. Thus, the >7 kcal/mol difference strongly suggests that the IPC pose is the preferred pose.

Using a representative frame from S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-CPI (“hSERT-CPI”), similar AB-FEP calculations were performed. In this case, the IPC pose was established by docking, while the CPI pose was from the original frame. The results showed that the difference between the two poses is significantly less (−13.4 ± 0.2 kcal/mol for IPC versus −12.8 ± 0.1 kcal/mol for the CPI pose), though the IPC pose is still slightly preferred. Our examination of the AB-FEP trajectory of the IPC pose showed that the critical cation–π interaction between R104 and the indole ring of Lu AF88273 described above (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4) cannot form in hSERT-CPI, which may account for the drastically higher ΔG compared to that in hSERT-IPC. However, the still decent ΔG values and the minor differences between the ΔGs of the CPI and IPC poses in hSERT-CPI (~0.6 kcal/mol) suggest that in this conformation both pose orientations appear to be feasible, though the IPC pose is not as optimized in hSERT-CPI as in hSERT-IPC.

Experimental Validations of the Computationally Predicted Binding Pose.

We then sought to validate the key interactions between Lu AF88273 and hSERT, as revealed by the computational modeling and simulations, with site-directed mutagenesis. In particular, we identified and focused on the residues that are more important for the IPC but not CPI pose, i.e., R104, E494, and F556 (see above), to explore both the binding site location and the orientation of the bound Lu AF88273 in the extracellular vestibule of hSERT.

By mutating R104 to Glu or Ala, to introduce the opposite charge or to remove the charge and bulkiness of the sidechain, respectively, we found that the potency of Lu AF88273 in inhibiting the [3H]IMI dissociation was reduced 231-fold and 643-fold, respectively, compared to that of the WT. While the impact of R104K mutation was much less severe, it still resulted in a 31.4-fold lower potency (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Table S5). Thus, although a lysine residue can form a cation–π interaction to the indole ring of Lu AF88273 as well, the interaction is much weaker than that of an arginine residue, likely due to the lack of the additional stacking between the guanidino group of the arginine and the indole ring (29). Note that this stacking only happens in the IPC but not CPI pose (Fig. 3).

All mutations decrease the allosteric potency of Lu AF88273 relative to hSERT WT (dotted line). The replacement of F556 with Ala also drastically lowered the inhibitory potency of Lu AF88273 by 280-fold, consistent with the tight packing between the phenyl ring of F556 and Lu AF88273 in the IPC but not CPI pose (see above). When F556 was replaced with Val, which has a bulky hydrophobic sidechain, the inhibitor potency was only reduced ~twofold, suggesting that Val can partially substitute the role of aromatic Phe at this position. Interestingly, however, Lu AF88273 could not fully inhibit the [3H]IMI dissociation in the presence of the F556V mutation, suggesting a distorted binding pose of Lu AF88273 that cannot completely block the exit pathway of S1:IMI.

The E494Q mutation retained the capability of forming polar interactions but not an ionic interaction. Nevertheless, the more than 240-fold reduction of the inhibitory potency indicates that the presence of a negative charge at this position is crucial in stabilizing the binding of Lu AF88273 and supports the IPC pose that forms the water-mediated interaction between the positively charged N in the piperidine ring and the negatively charged E494.

In addition to the S1:IMI conditions, we also investigated the binding pose of Lu AF88273 in the presence of S1:S-CIT in hSERT. We found that the protrusion of S1:S-CIT between T497 and F335 at the interface between the S1 and S2 sites affected nearby residues and significantly influenced the binding of Lu AF88273 in the S2 site, causing it to adopt a more extracellular pose (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results and Figs. S13–S15). Experimental results further support the divergent binding modes of Lu AF88273 in the presence of S1:S-CIT versus S1:IMI, highlighting the distinct roles of R104 (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results and SI Appendix, Fig. S12).

The Analogs with the Modified Indoline Ring Are Likely Bound in the CPI Pose.

To understand the structural basis of the structure–activity relationship (SAR) of the congeneric analogs of Lu AF88273, we applied the RB-FEP approach, which is robust in estimating the relative binding free energy differences (ΔΔG) among a series of congeneric analogs (30). With the improvements in the force field (31), sampling algorithms (32), and in the user-friendliness of the simulation setup and analysis (33), the FEP+ implementation of RB-FEP (30, 34) has grown to play a significant role in modern drug discovery projects across a wide variety of small-molecule alchemical perturbations (35). Thus, using the “hSERT-IPC” (see above), we carried out RB-FEP calculations on all the analogs tested in this study, except for compound 3, which differs by more than 10 heavy atoms from the other analogs (making it not suitable for RB-FEP calculations).

After converting the ΔΔG values from the RB-FEP runs to ΔG values (Materials and Methods), our results showed that Lu AF88273 indeed has the lowest ΔG, i.e., the highest binding affinity. Compound 4a, which is the second most potent analog and has a tetrahydropyridine instead of a piperidine ring compared to Lu AF88273, has 2.5 kcal/mol higher ΔG than Lu AF88273. When the unmodified indole ring of 4a is replaced by the saturated indoline ring, the modification results in 3.2 kcal/mol higher ΔG for compound (S)-1b. All the other analogs have higher ΔG than these three compounds (Table 2). In particular, the affinity predictions of the analogs with modifications at the indoline nitrogen have relatively low correlations with experimentally measured potencies. Our inspection of the RB-FEP trajectories showed that they could not be stable in the IPC pose during the perturbations, due to the loss of both the critical cation–π interaction to R104 and the interaction between the unmodified indoline nitrogen and E493, and an intolerance of acetyl moiety facing the negatively charged E493 in a tightly packed space. As an example, when examining the trajectory of the (R)-1a to 4b perturbation, we observed that 4b was very stable in the binding pocket while (R)-1a showed high rmsd values (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Thus, Spearman’s rank-order correlation between the computationally predicted binding affinities and experimentally measured inhibitory potencies of these analogs is relatively modest (R2 = 0.64) (Table 2).

We then performed similar RB-FEP calculations of the same set of compounds in their CPI pose using the “hSERT-CPI” (see above) to further validate the orientation preference of the inhibitor binding. Our results show a noticeably better correlation (R2 = 0.90) between the experimentally measured inhibitory potencies and computationally predicted ΔG, compared to those in the IPC pose (Table 2). The correlation of the affinity predictions to the inhibitor potencies in the CPI pose is likely due to the symmetry of the scaffold, which allows the analogs that are not able to stay in the IPC pose to adopt the CPI pose, such as the aforementioned analogs with modifications at the indoline nitrogen.

To substantiate the finding that the analogs with a modified indoline ring prefer the CPI pose due to the loss of the critical cation–π interaction to R104 in their IPC pose, we measured the potency of Lu 35-138 to inhibit the dissociation of [3H]IMI in three R104 mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). We specifically chose Lu 35-138 because it demonstrated the highest potency among the analogs with an acetyl decoration at the indoline nitrogen, a feature shared by four other analogs (Table 1). Our results showed that the allosteric potency of Lu 35-138 was reduced by 8.4-fold, 36-fold, and 5.5-fold for R104A, R104E, and R104K mutations, respectively, compared to SERT WT (SI Appendix, Table S9). Notably, these potency reductions are considerably smaller than those of Lu AF88273 at these three mutants, which are 231-fold, 643-fold, and 31.4-fold, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S5), indicating that the interaction with R104 is significantly less critical for the binding of Lu 35-138 than for Lu AF88273. Together with the RB-FEP results, our findings suggest that the analogs with a modified indoline ring, such as Lu 35-138, prefer the CPI pose.

Discussion

The insights into the allosteric ligand binding at the less conserved S2 site of NSSs are not only essential for our full understanding of the transport mechanism (7, 36, 37) but may also allow the development of therapeutics with the potential of less side effects (15, 22). Indeed, the unique therapeutic profile of S-citalopram (S-CIT), including its relatively better patient acceptability (38), may be partially attributed to the additional allosteric binding of S-CIT at the S2 site (39). Previously, based on the S-CIT-bound crystal structures of hSERT (10), we built the hSERT models in complex with both competitive inhibitors in the S1 site and allosteric modulators in the S2 site, in various combinations and characterized their allosteric communications (22). In addition, we identified a high-affinity hSERT allosteric modulator, Lu AF60097, and found that its S-CIT scaffold binds in a similar pose as the S2 bound S-CIT (S2:S-CIT).

While Lu AF60097 is the first allosteric high-affinity binder of hSERT (31 nM), its limited brain availability calls for further optimization. Here, by combining pharmacological screening, molecular modeling and simulations, mutagenesis, and rigorous binding free energy calculations, we identified a lead compound, Lu AF88273, for hSERT allosteric inhibition, that showed even higher allosteric potency at hSERT (2.1 nM) than Lu AF60097. However, as the potency in inhibiting the S1:IMI is an indirect estimation of the binding affinity at the S2 site and is likely a combination of both the strength of the binding at S2 and the capability in blocking the exit of S1:IMI, we cannot exclude the possibility that the IC50 ratio of Lu AF60097 and Lu AF88273 is not exactly their binding affinity ratio. Indeed, the inability of Lu AF88273 to fully inhibit the [3H]IMI dissociation at the F556V mutant suggests that the position of the S2:bound ligand, relative to the S1 ligand dissociation pathway, may be an element affecting the inhibitory potency as well. Nevertheless, if one of the therapeutic potentials of SERT allosteric inhibitors is to lower the dosage of tricyclic antidepressants (including IMI), which are effective but may have severe side effects (40), high allosteric inhibitory potency would obviously serve the purpose, regardless of the exact binding affinity or nature of the inhibition.

Our previous simulations have demonstrated that the extended quinolinone moiety of Lu AF60097 protrudes into a dynamically formed binding pocket (so-called cryptic binding pocket) under the extracellular loop 4 (EL4). Interestingly, this accommodation only happened in the presence of S1:IMI but not S1:S-CIT, which is consistent with the higher inhibitory potency of Lu AF60097 on the dissociation of S1:IMI than S1:S-CIT (22). In the current study, a cryptic binding pocket was similarly revealed in the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-IPC simulations at the interface between TM11 and TM12 after the rotation of the sidechain of F556. Note that the recently published hSERT structures in complex with S2:vilazodone or S2:5-HT revealed a similar cavity occupied by the ligand (6, 7), compared to the hSERT structure bound with S2:S-CIT, in which the cavity is not formed (PDB 5I73) (10). Thus, the dynamic nature of the extracellular vestibule of hSERT and those of NSSs in general provide both the opportunities for ligand discovery and the challenge to be fully characterized.

Our combined pharmacological, computational, and mutagenesis results of the tested series of compounds suggest a SAR of the scaffold in the S2 site of hSERT: An unmodified indole ring two alkyl carbons away from the positively charged nitrogen in a saturated piperidine ring has the highest potency. Thus, the cation–π interaction, in particular, the stacking between the unmodified indole ring and the guanidino group of R104, would position the nitrogen to properly interact with E494, as in the IPC pose of Lu AF88273. The analogs with the indole modified to indoline ((S)-1b) or methoxybenzene (compound 3) all have reduced potencies. The acetyl decoration at the indoline nitrogen of (S)-1b, resulting in the parent compound (Lu 35-138), improves the potency ~fivefold but still >20-fold weaker than Lu AF88273. In contrast, the carbamoyl decoration at this position of (S)-1b, resulting in compound (S)-1c, has no effect on the potency. However, from our modeling and free energy calculations, these decorations cannot be accommodated in the small cavity with a limited volume above the S1 site. Thus, we propose that only the analogs with the unmodified indole ring can be in the IPC pose, while the analogs with indoline rings, either decorated or not, may prefer the CPI pose in which the indoline ring points to a more open space.

The divergence of the S2 site among NSSs, compared to the more conserved S1 site (41), provides opportunities to rationally develop inhibitors specifically targeting one transporter but not others, which has significant therapeutic implications (3). Indeed, we found that Lu AF88273 is highly selective for hSERT over hDAT and hNET in its potency to inhibit the dissociation of the orthosteric inhibitor (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results and Fig. S3). Among the most critical residues in the Lu AF88273 binding that we have identified and validated with mutagenesis, only R104 is conserved among these three NSSs, while E494 and F556 in hSERT are divergent in hDAT (H477 and S539, respectively) and hNET (T474 and S536, respectively) as well as in other NSSs (41). However, upon testing Lu AF88273 against the SafetyScreen44 panel, we found that it displayed interactions with several off-targets (SI Appendix, Supplemental Results and Table S10). Although this is not unexpected for a lead compound targeting a protein activated by biogenic amine, additional optimization would be necessary for advancing Lu AF88273 further in drug discovery. In addition, future investigations should incorporate behavioral paradigms to comprehensively understand the neuropsychological impacts arising from allosteric modulation of SERT, in relation to its functional roles.

Taken together, our finding of high-affinity hSERT allosteric inhibitors, in particular, the lead compound Lu AF88273, and the characterizations of their hSERT structure-based SAR, as well as the dynamically formed small cavities in the S2 site, clear the way to rationally develop effective allosteric therapeutics targeting hSERT. Such therapeutics potentially have less side effects, not only due to the allosteric nature of their modulations, which may have less disruption of the normal protein functions, but also for divergence of the S2 site, that can easily render higher selectivity for a specific transporter target.

Materials and Methods

Chemistry.

The synthesis and characterization of (S)- and (R)-1a, (S)-1b were specifically disclosed in patent application WO1998/028293 (42). Compounds (S)-1c, (S)-1d, (R)-1d, (S)-1e, and 3 were made in a similar manner. The synthesis and characterization of compound 2 were disclosed in patent application WO2007/019867 (43). Compounds 4a and 4b were made as exemplified for compound 4b in the following.

Synthesis of 4b (3-(1-(2-(1H-indol-3-yl)ethyl)piperidin-4-yl)-6-chloro-1H-indole): 1H-indol-3-ylacetic acid (0.82 g, 4.69 mmol) was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF) (25 mL), and 1,1′-carbonyldiimidazole (0.79 g, 4.90 mmol) was added. The mixture was boiled under reflux for 1 h, and then 6-chloro-3-(piperidin-4-yl)-1H-indole (1.00 g, 4.26 mmol) was added. The mixture was boiled under reflux for an additional 1 h and stirred at room temperature overnight. Lithium aluminum hydride (0.50 g, 13.17 mmol) was added to the mixture that was boiled under reflux for 30 min. The mixture was cooled on ice and 2 mL of water (caution: strong evolution of gas) was carefully added, followed by 1 mL sodium hydroxide-solution (4 M) and 5 mL water. The mixture was stirred for 10 min, and a generous amount of magnesium sulfate was added. The resulting mixture was filtered and concentrated in vacuo. The oily residue was purified using column flash chromatography (eluent ethyl acetate/ethanol/triethylamine 18:1:1) to afford the crude title compound as a pale-yellow foam (1.18 g). The 1.18 g pale-yellow foam was dissolved in 10 mL ethyl acetate and stirred at room temperature. After approximately 1 h, a dense, white precipitate formed. The mixture was cooled on ice and stirred for 30 min. The solid was collected by filtration and dried in vacuo, yielding a dense, white powder (0.49 g, 30%).

The UV purity of each compound and the liquid chromatography (LC) methods used in determining them are specified in SI Appendix, Table S6.

Brain and Plasma Exposure Assessment.

The extent of brain penetration of Lu AF88273 was investigated in male Sprague Dawley rats following an intraperitoneal (IP) dose of 3 mg/kg dissolved in 10% hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (pH 7) (5 mL/kg). Brain and plasma samples were obtained 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 h after drug administration (n = 3/timepoint). At the time of sampling, rats were anesthetized by isoflurane followed by blood collection by heart puncture. Subsequently, animals were killed; the brains were rapidly removed and rinsed on filter paper. All blood samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 5,000 g at 4 °C, and the harvested plasma and brain samples were stored at −80 °C until bioanalysis.

Samples were prepared by homogenizing the brain tissue using isothermal-focused acoustic ultrasonication as described earlier (44). Quantitative analyses were performed using ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) followed by tandem mass spectrometry detection using a positive-ion electrospray ionization mode. The lower limit of detection was 1.0 ng/mL in plasma and 5 ng/g in brain tissue. Plasma protein and brain tissue binding of Lu AF88273 were determined in vitro using equilibrium dialysis in respective matrices diluted 10-fold due to high binding. In plasma, the free fraction was 0.6% (fu,pl), whereas the free fraction in brain tissue (fu,br) was <0.02%.

Molecular Docking.

We used our previously equilibrated hSERT models in S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF60097 and S1:S-CIT/S2:Lu AF60097 conditions (22) as the starting points to build S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273 and S1:S-CIT/S2:Lu AF88273 conditions, respectively. As noted before, we had added the binding site ions missing in the original hSERT crystal structure (PDB ID 5I73) and reverted three thermostabilizing mutations introduced in the ts3 construct back to the WT residues (22).

In each model, Lu AF88273 was docked into the S2 site using the induced-fit docking (IFD) protocol (45) implemented in the Schrodinger suite (release 2017-1). The rmsd values of the ensemble of IFD poses were calculated and clustered using the centroid linkage method, as implemented in the Conformer Cluster module of Maestro (release 2017-1, Schrodinger Inc.). Large clusters of Lu AF88273 poses in opposite orientations in the S2 site were found in each condition, with either its 6-Cl-indole (C) or the unmodified indole ring (I) of Lu AF88273 facing the S1 site. We termed them as the “CPI” and “IPC” orientations, respectively, with the first letter indicating the ring (“C” or “I”) closer to the S1 site and “P” standing for the piperidine ring in the middle of Lu AF88273. The cluster centroids from each orientation in each condition were selected as initial starting points for the subsequent MD simulations.

We also conducted an additional docking analysis to explore all the potential binding pockets of Lu AF88273 at hSERT. First, we removed the S1-bound ligand from the hSERT model and generated a grid that covered the entire protein. Subsequently, we employed Glide (46) to dock Lu AF88273 into this model. We filtered the generated poses based on their docking scores, excluding those with scores higher than −6.5 kcal/mol (~60 µM). Intriguingly, all the resulting poses were found to be located either in the S2 or the S1 site (for further details, refer to SI Appendix, Supplemental Results).

MD Simulations.

The hSERT models in complex with the selected S2:Lu AF88273 poses were placed back into their original equilibrated MD simulation systems, using the original orientation in the explicit 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine lipid bilayer (POPC) (22). The system was solvated with simple point charge (SPC) waters (47) with 0.15 M NaCl. The total system size was ∼121,000 atoms. Desmond MD systems (D. E. Shaw Research, New York, NY) as implemented within Schrodinger suite (release 2018-1) with OPLS3 force field (48) were used for the MD simulations. Force Field Builder (Schrodinger release 2017-1) (48) was used to optimize the missing torsions for Lu AF88273. The system was initially minimized and equilibrated with restraints on the protein backbone atoms and the ligand heavy atoms, followed by production runs with all atoms unrestrained. The isothermal–isobaric ensemble was used with the constant temperature (310 K) maintained with Langevin dynamics, and the constant pressure (1 atm) achieved with the hybrid Nose–Hoover Langevin piston method (49) on a semi-isotropic flexible periodic cell.

AB-FEP Calculations.

We used an AB-FEP approach as implemented within Schrodinger suite (release 2022-1) (26), which is based on the originally proposed double decoupling scheme (27). In this method, starting from the ligand in the water, the van der Waals and electrostatic interactions within the ligand, and between the ligand and water, were first slowly turned off, wherein the ligand became a dummy ligand. Then, the dummy ligand was restrained to the protein binding site through a set of cross-link restraints. Finally, the van der Waals and electrostatic interactions within the ligand, and between the ligand and protein, were slowly turned on, while the cross-linked restraints were relaxed.

The starting points for the AB-FEP runs were representative frames from the MD simulations of the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-IPC and S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-CPI conditions (as well as the S1:S-CIT/S2:Lu AF88273-X condition, see SI). Lu AF88273 was docked with Glide (46) (Schrodinger release 2021-3) in the hSERT S2 site in the CPI pose for the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-IPC frame and in the IPC pose for the S1:IMI/S2:Lu AF88273-CPI frame. The optimized potentials for liquid simulations 4 (OPLS4) force field (31) were used, and the default Schrodinger AB-FEP protocol was executed, wherein the complexes were solvated in a SPC water box (with a buffer width of 8 Å for the charged ligand, Lu AF88273), counterions and salt ions of 0.15 M were added, an MD relaxation protocol was first run, followed by a 1 ns of Grand Canonical Monte Carlo μVT simulations at 300 K, with the protein backbone heavy atoms restrained. This MD step was necessary to identify the set of atoms for the cross-link restraints described above. After the MD step, 5-ns AB-FEP simulations were run with 108 lambda windows for the charged ligand, Lu AF88273. This protocol is automated within the FEP+ module of Schrodinger suite.

RB-FEP Calculations.

The congeneric series of the ligands studied here were docked onto the IPC and CPI representative frames used in the AB-FEP above, using Glide (46) with the shape-based constraints (as implemented in Schrodinger suite 2022-1) using the bound Lu AF88273 as a reference. Force Field Builder (31) (Schrodinger release 2022-1) was used to parametrize any missing torsions found in the congeneric series studied here. The default protocol of RB-FEP as implemented in FEP+ module (30) was used, wherein 24 lambda windows, as well as 8 Å SPC water buffer, were used for the charged ligands studied here. After a relaxation stage [as described previously (30)], 30-ns production RB-FEP simulations were performed. To assess the consistency of the FEP+ predictions, cycle closure correction (34) was applied, and the ΔΔGpred were converted to ΔGpred using an offset obtained from the experimental ΔG data, as outlined previously (30). Finally, the perturbations labeled as “bad” by the FEP+ module [specifically those with high hysteresis and large Bennett errors (34)] were removed from the analysis.

Transfection and Membrane Preparation.

For transfection of 1 million COS7 cells, 0.4 µg plasmid and 1.2 µL Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) were each mixed with 250 µL Opti-Mem™(1X) (Gibco) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. After incubation, the plasmid and Lipofectamine 2000 were mixed and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to form a complex. For transfection in 24-well plates, 50,000 cells (uptake experiments), 25,000 cells ([3H]S-CIT binding), or 10,000 cells ([3H]IMI binding) were added to each well together with plasmid, Lipofectamine 2000, and 300 µL DMEM1885+NaHCO3+L-Glu media (in house). After incubation for 5 h at 37 °C and 10% CO2, 600 µL DMEM1885+NaHCO3+L-Glu media with antibiotics (in house) was added to each well, and cells were further incubated for 48 h with the same conditions. For membrane preparation, 7.2 million COS7 cells, corresponding to 10 mL cell mixture (including plasmid and Lipofectamine 2000), were seeded in a surface-adherent flask. After 5 h of incubation, 20 mL DMEM1885+NaHCO3+L-Glu media with antibiotics (in house) was added to the flask. Cells were further incubated for 72 h before harvesting. The cells were harvested with 10 mL PBS + 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, washed twice with a sucrose buffer (0.3 M sucrose, 120 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 1.2 mM CaCl2, and 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.4) and then lysed with one ultrasound burst. The membranes were then spun down at 3,500 rpm and resuspended in 1 mL sucrose buffer, followed by storage at −20 °C until use.

Equilibrium Binding and Uptake Assays.

For all binding and uptake assays a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, 130 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 1.2 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM L ascorbic acid, and 5 mM D glucose, pH 7.4 was used. After removal of media, cells were washed with buffer. The binding experiments were initiated by addition of Lu AF88273, S-CIT, or IMI of various concentrations, and 50 µL 3 nM [3H]IMI or [3H]S-CIT (81 Ci/mmol, Novandi Chemistry AB) to all wells. For Lu AF88273 and IMI, the addition was followed by 60 min of incubation with gentle shaking at room temperature. For S-CIT, the incubation was at 4 °C in 90 min. After incubation, buffer was removed from the cells by aspiration, and cells were washed twice with 500 µL ice-cold buffer.

For uptake experiments, a similar procedure was used. Lu AF88273, IMI, or S-CIT was preincubated for 60 min at room temperature, except for the synergistic inhibition where IMI and Lu AF88273 were preincubated for 30 min at room temperature. The incubation was then followed by the addition of 50 µL of 11.6 nM [3H]5-HT (43.1 Ci/mmol, Perkin Elmer) to all wells. After 3 min of uptake, buffer was rapidly removed by suction, and cells were washed twice with 500 µL ice-cold uptake buffer.

At this point, for both binding and uptake experiments, cells were lysed with 250 µL 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate at 37 °C for 60 min. The samples were moved to 24-well counting plates (Perkin Elmer), and 500 µL Opti-phase Hi Safe 3 scintillation fluid (Perkin Elmer) was added to each well.

Dissociation Rate Assay.

One aliquot of transfected membranes (1 mL) was thawed and dounce-homogenized together with 3 mL dissociation buffer (120 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 1.2 mM CaCl2, and 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.4). Radioligands were added to the membrane suspension with concentrations of 12.3 nM [3H]IMI (81 Ci/mmol, Novandi Chemistry AB), 36.9 nM [3H]S-CIT (81 Ci/mmol, Novandi Chemistry AB), 200 nM tritiated carbomethoxy fluorophenyl tropane ([3H]CFT) (80.0 Ci/mmol, Perkin Elmer) or 20 nM tritiated nisoxetine ([3H]NIX) (81 Ci/mmol, Novandi Chemistry AB). In addition, 50 µL of membrane suspension was added to all wells and incubated for 30 min. The experiment was initiated by addition of 600 µL Lu AF88273 to the membrane suspension and was repeated at seven times points (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50, and 70 min). The dissociation was performed at fixed temperatures ([3H]IMI at 6 °C, [3H]S-CIT at 18 °C, [3H]CFT at 4 °C, and [3H]NIX at 20 °C). At time 0, the dissociation was stopped by rapid filtration of the samples onto a filtermat soaked in 50% polyethyleneimine. Filtration was performed using a Tomtec® automatic cell harvester. The samples were then washed for 20 s with ice-cold 20 mM HEPES and 0.2 M NaCl, pH 7.4, through the cell harvester. The filtermat was dried on a heating plate, followed by the addition of the scintillant MeltiLex®.

Pharmacology Data Analysis.

To evaluate allosteric potency, we first calculated the rate constants (k[allosteric ligand]) for the dissociation of bound orthosteric radioligand from hSERT at the indicated concentrations of allosterically bound ligand and computed the ratios between k[allosteric ligand] and the dissociation rate constant without the allosteric ligand (kbuffer). The allosteric potency (IC50) was defined as the concentration of the allosteric ligand that impairs the 50% of kbuffer and was calculated from the dose–response curve of the dissociation ratio (k[allosteric ligand]/kbuffer) versus log[allosteric ligand]. These curves were derived by fitting the data with an inhibitory dose–response model (variable slope), i.e., the “four-parameter logistic equation,” using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.).

For competition binding analyses, the readout for nonspecific binding was subtracted before fitting the data. We obtained IC50 from logIC50 and derived the affinity (Kd or Ki) with the Cheng–Prusoff equation.

For statistical analysis of synergistic inhibition between IMI and Lu AF88273, a one-way ANOVA with Dunnett multiple comparisons test was used to evaluate whether the combined effect of the ligands was significantly different from the addition of either IMI or Lu AF88273 alone. The mean of each inhibitory effect was calculated and the sum including the variances of the effect of IMI and Lu AF88273 alone was calculated. An unpaired t test (α = 0.05) was used to test whether there was a significant difference between the ligands added together and the combined single effect of each ligand.

All presented results are given as mean IC50 [SEM interval], derived from pIC50 and the SEM interval from the pIC50 ± SEM.
