DeepPANB: integrating protein language model with PaiNN equivariant graph neural networks for prediction of protein–nucleic acid binding sites
Authors: Jilong Zhang, Zhixiang Wu, Jingjie Su, Xinyu Zhang, Yue Li, Zihan Li, Chunhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein–nucleic acid interactions are fundamental to various biological processes, and accurately identifying nucleic acid binding sites on proteins is essential for understanding gene regulation mechanisms and advancing drug design. Here, we present DeepPANB, an effective deep learning model for the issue. DeepPANB is built upon the Polarizable Atom Interaction Neural Network (PaiNN) architecture, an E(3)-equivariant graph neural network, which explicitly couples scalar and vector channels through lightweight message passing, thereby enabling effective modeling of the direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions. DeepPANB integrates multiple feature types including sequence embeddings from the Ankh pretrained model, residue–nucleotide pairwise propensity extracted by us from the large-scale datasets, as well as residue intrinsic disorder, physicochemical properties, and structural descriptors. To our best knowledge, PaiNN and Ankh are first introduced here for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction. On the protein–DNA test set, DeepPANB achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming the existing methods. On protein–RNA test set, DeepPANB, fine-tuned with the feature types unchanged, shows competitive performance. Overall, DeepPANB demonstrates robust and generalizable performance, providing an effective tool for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction. Introduction

Protein–nucleic acid interactions are fundamental to diverse cellular processes, such as gene expression and regulation [1, 2]. Correctly identifying nucleic acid binding sites on proteins is of great significance, as it provides crucial insights into the mechanisms for gene transcription and translation, and also facilitates the discovery of potential drug targets [3, 4]. Traditional experimental techniques for characterizing protein–nucleic acid interactions, such as X-ray crystallography and nuclear magnetic resonance, are often labor-intensive and time-consuming. Consequently, it is urgently needed to develop accurate and robust computational methods for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction.

Currently, the computational approaches for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction can be broadly categorized into sequence- and structure-based methods. The sequence-based ones, such as SVMnuc [5], NCBRPred [6], DNAPred [7], DNAgenie [8], RNABindRPlus [9], TargetDNA [10], and TargetS [11], leverage the accessibility and abundance of protein sequence data. However, the absence of structural information often limits their predictive accuracy. To address the issue, the structure-based methods, such as COACH-D [12], NucBind [5], GLMSite [13], aaRNA [14], NucleicNet [15], GraphBind [16], GraphPBSP [17], and GraphSite [18], incorporate structural information to enhance the binding site identification. Given their superior performances, we aim to develop a structure-based framework for predicting protein–nucleic acid binding sites.

In recent years, deep learning has significantly advanced the protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction. In particular, the graph neural network (GNN) [19] has emerged as a powerful tool for modeling biomolecular structures by representing residues as nodes and their spatial relationships as edges. The GNN can effectively capture both topological and physicochemical properties, suitable for the binding site inference. However, the conventional GNN lacks explicit strategies to account for the rotational and translational invariance or equivariance of molecular systems, which are critical for the robust prediction. To address the issue, the E(3)-equivariant architectures, such as the E(n)‑Equivariant Graph Neural Network (EGNN) [20] and the Polarizable Atom Interaction Neural Network (PaiNN) [21], have been introduced to maintain equivariance to geometric transformations. Currently, the EGNN has been used for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction, and the developed methods are EquiPNAS [22] and EGPDI [23]. The former integrates a protein language model (PLM) with EGNN, while the latter fuses EGNN with the graph convolutional networks (GCN) [24] within a multi-view architecture. As for PaiNN, it represents 3D molecular geometries by coupling scalar and vector features with a lightweight message-passing mechanism, and by capturing distance radial dependencies with continuous filters, which explicitly maintains equivariance while modeling the direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions. The PaiNN achieves lower prediction errors than common invariant models on molecular properties and atomic forces for molecular dynamics simulations [21]. Thus, we hope to employ the PaiNN as the framework for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction.

As for features, PLMs have emerged as powerful tools for encoding residue evolutionary, contextual, and functional information. The widely used PLMs such as ESM-1v (ESM1) [25], ESM-2 (ESM2) [26], and ProtT5_XL_U50 (ProtT5) [27] have demonstrated strong performances across diverse protein prediction tasks, including secondary structure [28] and functional annotation [29]. Notably, the Ankh model employs fewer parameters and lower embedding dimensionality, yet maintains competitive modeling capability and computational efficiency [30]. The superior performance is mainly attributed to Ankh’s bidirectional residual attention with cross-layer information integration, which enhances the modeling of long-range dependencies and contextual signals. Recently, in multiple protein-related prediction tasks, Ankh has shown competitive performance [31, 32], which may imply its potential for binding site prediction. In addition, for protein–nucleic acid complexes, our previous work extracted residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensity, which has proven high effectiveness for predicting binding hotspots in protein–RNA interactions [33] and protein–DNA binding affinities [34]. Therefore, we want to incorporate both the Ankh pretrained PLM and residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensity into our framework for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction.

In this work, we present DeepPANB, a novel framework for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction. DeepPANB employs a PaiNN-based equivariant architecture, where scalar and vector channels are coupled and distance-dependent modulation is introduced through radial filters, enabling effective modeling of direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions. Multiple categories of residue features are incorporated, including PLM Ankh and residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensities, as well as physicochemical descriptors, structural attributes, and intrinsic disorder scores. To validate its effectiveness, we perform extensive evaluations on multiple test sets, where DeepPANB consistently outperforms existing methods.

Materials and methods

Benchmark datasets

For protein–DNA binding site prediction, we utilize the training set DNA_573_Train and test set DNA_129_Test from the published work of GraphBind [16], comprising 573 and 129 proteins, respectively. An additional test set DNA_181_Test is obtained from the published work of GraphSite [18], containing 181 proteins. The DNA_573_Train includes the proteins published before January 6, 2016, while DNA_129_Test contains the proteins from 6 January 2016 to 5 December 2018, and DNA_181_Test includes more recent proteins published between 6 December 2018 and August 2021. For protein–RNA binding site prediction, the training set RNA_495_Train and test set RNA_117_Test are adopted from the published work of GraphBind [16], consisting of 495 and 117 proteins, respectively.

These datasets were originally constructed from the BioLiP database [35], which provides the binding site information from the known protein–nucleic acid complexes. To ensure non-redundancy, CD-HIT [36] was applied to exclude the sequences with >30% sequence identity within and between the protein–DNA/RNA binding site datasets. Feature representation

Here, a protein structure is modeled as a residue-centric graph, where residues are represented as nodes and edges are constructed between residues with their Cα–Cα distance within a cutoff of 15 Å. The detailed descriptions of node and edge features are provided below.

Node features

For each node, we incorporate six categories of residue features, including type (one-hot), physicochemical properties, structural features, intrinsic disorder score, pairwise propensities, and evolutionary characteristics (Ankh embeddings).

The physicochemical feature set is derived from the Amino Acid index database [37], where 10 descriptors are selected, including solvent accessibility, hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity, residue volume, isoelectric point, structural preference scores (α-helix, β-strand, coil), charge, and atom count. For a protein with N residues, this yields an N × 10 matrix.

The structural features are derived using DSSP [38], from which 13 descriptors are extracted, including trigonometric encodings of backbone torsion angles, relative solvent accessible surface area, and one-hot encodings of secondary structure states [α-helix (H), β-strand (E), π-helix (I), bridge (B), turn (T), 310-helix (G), bend (S), uncertain structure (C)]. This results in an N × 13 representation for a protein with N residues.

The intrinsic disorder score is considered, which quantifies the tendency of a residue to remain unstructured and flexible. The score is predicted using IUpred2A [39], which provides three complementary scores for each residue: a long disorder score (assessing the long stretches of disorder covering the query residue), a short disorder score (assessing the short stretches of disorder covering the query residue), and the ANCHOR score (denoting the probability of the query residue to be part of a disorder binding region). For a protein of length N, this yields an N × 3 matrix.

To obtain the residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensity feature, we first collected 9084/6744 protein–DNA/RNA complexes from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [40]. With the complexes with nucleic acid analogs/the ribosomes excluded from the protein–DNA/RNA complexes [34, 41], 6495/2889 protein–DNA/RNA complexes remained. Redundancy was removed based on a 70% sequence similarity threshold, yielding final datasets of 4494/2071 protein–DNA/RNA complexes. The pretrained PLM embeddings are derived from the Ankh model. It employs bidirectional residual attention with cross-layer integration, which helps capture long-range dependencies and contextual signals more effectively. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents the first application of Ankh in predicting protein–nucleic acid binding sites. Each residue in a protein of N residues is encoded into a 1536D vector, resulting in an N × 1536 embedding matrix for the protein.

Edge features

For edge features, three types of positional descriptors are incorporated, two of which are the Euclidean distance between residues and their directional vector. In addition, a sequence–space relation feature is introduced, which normalizes the logarithmic sequence distance by the spatial Euclidean distance. Network architecture

The protein–nucleic acid binding site predictor is built based on the PaiNN.

Input representations

For each node i, its initial scalar features are derived by projecting its high-dimensional descriptors (physicochemical properties, structural features, disorder scores, propensities, and Ankh embeddings) into a hidden dimension via a linear layer Win. All the extracted residue-level node descriptors are incorporated into the invariant scalar channel and modulate message passing through scalar weights without changing the E(3)-equivariant update of the vector channel. Crucially, the equivariant vector features are initialized to zero. This zero-initialization strategy ensures that geometric information is not assumed a priori but is gradually incorporated through subsequent message-passing layers. For each edge (i, j), we define its relative geometry , and its unit direction . Its sequence–space relation descriptor fij is embedded into an edge embedding .

Message passing

The message-passing phase propagates information via explicitly coupled scalar and vector channels. The scalar channel weights the residue interactions using distance-dependent modulation, while the vector channel captures the geometric structure by aggregating neighbor orientations and inter-residue directional vectors, which are explicitly modulated by scalar features. This design ensures that both intrinsic orientation and relative positional information are effectively aggregated (see Supplementary Section S2 for details).

Update block

Following aggregation, node states are refined via an update block that facilitates cross-channel information exchange. A critical innovation here is the vector-to-scalar projection mechanism, implemented via the inner product of equivariant vector features. This operation effectively compresses angular information and directional correlations into the rotationally invariant scalar features, allowing the scalar channel to perceive the local anisotropic environment. The vector features are simultaneously updated via a self-interaction term modulated by a scalar gating mechanism, which preserves equivariance while refining directional signals. Finally, residual connections are applied to update both channels. The details on the message passing and update blocks are provided in Supplementary Section S2.

Framework of DeepPANB model

DeepPANB is a deep learning framework consisting of two main components: feature extraction and model construction. In feature extraction, a protein is represented as a residue-level graph, where nodes correspond to residues and edges capture their geometric and sequential relationships. Node features integrate multiple categories, including physicochemical descriptors, structural characteristics, intrinsic disorder scores, residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensity, and pretrained embeddings derived from the Ankh model. Edge features encode geometric information, including Euclidean distance, unit directional vector, and a sequential-space relation. The model construction is based on the PaiNN architecture, an E(3)-equivariant GNN that explicitly couples scalar and vector channels. The final scalar representations are processed through an MLP to output residue-level binding probabilities. The above framework is used to construct a protein–DNA binding site prediction model, and subsequently the model is fine-tuned with the feature types unchanged for protein–RNA binding site prediction. Results and discussion

Comparison of pretrained model Ankh with the others

In this work, we employed the pretrained model Ankh to extract sequence embeddings for our model DeepPANB. To assess the effect of Ankh on binding site prediction, we compared DeepPANB with the three baseline models ProtT5_M, ESM1_M, and ESM2_M trained with the features from widely used PLMs (ProtT5, ESM1, and ESM2) following the same embedding procedure, respectively. The results of these models on the training sets DNA_573_Train and RNA_495_Train are presented in Fig. 2, while those on the test sets DNA_129_Test, DNA_181_Test and RNA _Test_117 are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. On both training sets, overall DeepPANB achieves the highest performances, surpassing the others. The results on the three test sets show a similar trend, confirming the robustness of DeepPANB. Thus, the results underscore the superiority of Ankh in protein–DNA/RNA binding site prediction, which may be attributed to Ankh’s enhanced modeling of long-range dependencies and contextual signals.

Comparison of PaiNN with graph convolutional network and graph attention network models

To further evaluate the effectiveness of the PaiNN-based architecture, we constructed two baseline models GAT_M and GCN_M based on graph attention network (GAT) and GCN following the same procedure as DeepPANB, respectively. Their performances on the training sets DNA_573_Train and RNA_495_Train are displayed in Fig. 3. From Fig. 3A–D, the ROC curves and precision-recall (PR) curves consistently indicate that DeepPANB outperforms both baselines, with higher AUC and AUPRC values across both datasets. On DNA_573_Train, DeepPANB achieves 0.941/0.633 (AUC/AUPRC), higher than 0.909/0.580 for GCN_M and 0.895/0.502 for GAT_M; similarly, on RNA_495_Train, DeepPANB attains 0.908/0.608, surpassing GCN_M (0.872/0.556) and GAT_M (0.850/0.519). In terms of MCC and F1 scores (Fig. 3E and F), DeepPANB improves MCC/F1 by 14.6%/12.9% on DNA_573_Train and by 21.2%/21.4% on RNA_495_Train relative to GCN_M (the second-best model), respectively. Moreover, from Supplementary Table S3 (detailed metric values), its higher REC and PRE underscore its balanced predictive capability. Thus, PaiNN has more powerful ability than GCN and GAT in protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction, which, we think, may be attributed to its explicit modeling of direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions, as well as its equivariant message-passing mechanism. Ablation study on the modules of PaiNN

To further elucidate the contributions of various modules in PaiNN, we performed ablation experiments and evaluated the three ablated models’ performance on the DNA_573_Train and RNA_495_Train datasets. The removed modules, one at a time, are the sinusoidal radial basis functions and cosine cutoff in edge embeddings, the coupling between scalar and vector features and the scalar–vector inner product, and all the vector features (see Supplementary Section S2), respectively, yielding three ablated models denoted as M_Radial, M_Coupling, and M_Vector.

The results (Table 3) show that M_Vector exhibits the most significant performance decline, with F1/MCC scores dropping by 18.2%/16.4% on DNA_573_Train and 12.1%/9.6% on RNA_495_Train. It is followed by M_Coupling (F1/MCC decreasing by 10.0%/9.4% and 5.5%/4.6%, respectively) and M_Radial (by 7.9%/6.8% and 4.1%/3.4%). The results underscore the important roles of the modules. The substantial performance drop of M_Vector affirms the necessity of equivariant vector representations, as their removal degrades the model to an invariant architecture incapable of capturing the direction-dependent geometric features. The performance decline of M_Coupling highlights the importance of scalar–vector coupling for encoding angular information and anisotropic geometric contexts into the invariant scalar features. The performance loss in M_Radial can be attributed to the lack of continuous distance modulation, which remains essential for weighting residue interactions based on proximity. Beyond the quantitative evaluations, the feature distribution visualizations from Supplementary Fig. S3 demonstrate that PaiNN can progressively learn more discriminative embeddings during training, with the final feature space exhibiting clearer separation compared to the initial representation.

Importance analysis of different features

In order to examine the contributions of different feature categories to model performance, we constructed four models denoted as PS_M, RPP_M, DSSP_M, and Ankh_M, which were trained with sequence properties (AA_onehot, physicochemical features, and intrinsic disorder), interface pairwise propensity, structural features obtained by DSSP, and Ankh embeddings, respectively. These models were evaluated on the test sets DNA_129_Test, DNA_181_Test, and RNA_117_Test, with DeepPANB trained by all feature types for comparison (shown in Fig. 4). On DNA_129_Test and DNA_181_Test, overall DeepPANB consistently achieves the best performance. Among different feature types, the Ankh embeddings contribute most, followed by structural features, interface pairwise propensity, and sequence properties. On RNA_117_Test, Ankh embeddings and structural features still provide significant benefits, and the interface pairwise propensity demonstrates a relative advantage in precision. The slightly weaker performance of Ankh_M on the protein–RNA dataset compared to the protein–DNA datasets may be attributed to the higher structural diversity and flexibility of RNA, which makes it more challenging for sequence-based language models to capture complex RNA-specific binding patterns. Overall, the results underscore the critical importance of structural features and pretrained embeddings for binding site prediction, while also showing that interface pairwise propensities capture distinctive residue–nucleotide pairwise relationships that provide complementary features.

We attribute its strong performance to several key factors. For feature representation, DeepPANB leverages the Ankh PLM to generate embeddings that capture evolutionary and contextual signals, and simultaneously incorporates residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensities derived from large-scale statistical analyses. On the model side, DeepPANB employs the PaiNN architecture to efficiently couple scalar and vector features, enabling the explicit modeling of anisotropic local environments and direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions which is critical for binding site prediction. In contrast, for GraphSite model, the corresponding results are F1 of 0.154/0.200 and MCC value 0.233/0.234. Notably, for 5zmn_A, DeepPANB attains optimal recall (REC = 1), correctly identifying all true binding residues, along with higher F1 and MCC scores than GraphSite. Some FP in 5zmn_A are clustered around sulfate ions in the protein crystal structure, and most of them are positively charged residues forming a local concave anion-binding pocket similar to the phosphate-binding environment of DNA, which may be the reason for the model’s misclassification. As visualized in Fig. 5B, DeepPANB also exhibits powerful predictive capability. For 7kuf_A, DeepPANB achieves near-perfect performance (F1 = 0.903, MCC = 0.889), successfully identifying all binding residues (recall = 1) with only a single FP, which substantially outperforms GraphSite (F1 = 0.640, MCC = 0.637). Similarly, for 7m99_G containing 257 residues but only two true binding sites, DeepPANB precisely detects both binding residues, whereas GraphSite identifies only one and generates multiple FP. Also for 6nua_A, DeepPANB exceeds GraphSite in both F1 and MCC.

As shown in Fig. 5C, DeepPANB consistently obtains excellent results in predicting protein–RNA binding residues. For 5o1y_A/6d12_A, DeepPANB substantially outperforms GraphBind, with improvement in MCC exceeding 77%/33%. Excitingly, in the case of 6fq3_A, GraphBind fails to detect any true binding sites, yielding an F1 of 0.000 and an MCC of −0.017, whereas DeepPANB attains an F1 of 0.800 and an MCC of 0.815. Thus, the above comparisons highlight the impressive predictive performance of our model in identifying protein–nucleic acid binding sites.

Conclusion

The identification of protein–nucleic acid binding sites is both a challenging and important problem. In this work, we present DeepPANB, a novel computational framework designed for the issue. For feature representation, we integrate a diverse set of residue-level features. Among these, residue–nucleotide interface pairwise propensities are extracted by us from a large-scale dataset, and the PLM Ankh can effectively capture evolutionary and contextual information. In addition, physicochemical descriptors, structure features, and intrinsic disorder scores are incorporated. The ablation experiments demonstrate that pretrained embeddings, structural features, and interface pairwise propensities all contribute significantly to the model’s performance.

On the model framework side, DeepPANB adopts the E(3)-equivariant PaiNN architecture, first applied to the protein–nucleic acid binding study. This framework efficiently couples scalar and vector features through a lightweight message-passing mechanism, and incorporates sinusoidal radial basis functions. This design maintains geometric equivariance while explicitly modeling anisotropic local environments as well as direction- and distance-dependent residue interactions. The ablation analyses confirm the critical role of equivariant vector representations in capturing the direction-dependent geometric patterns essential for binding site identification. Furthermore, the results highlight the necessity of scalar–vector coupling and radial distance modeling for effectively encoding anisotropic interactions and fine-grained geometric features. Comprehensive evaluations on test datasets demonstrate that DeepPANB consistently surpasses existing methods on both protein–DNA and protein–RNA binding site predictions. Overall, DeepPANB provides a promising and accurate approach for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction, and its geometric learning framework can be further extended to broader protein function prediction tasks.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. The current framework primarily relies on static structural inputs and does not explicitly capture conformational flexibility, which may be helpful for protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction. Furthermore, the present design models only the protein side of the interface, without explicitly incorporating nucleic acid structural information, which may limit its ability to fully characterize binding sites. In future work, PaiNN could be extended to a protein–nucleic acid docking graph model, where residues and nucleotides are treated as nodes and intra- and inter-molecular spatial contacts are modeled as edges. By learning the geometric and physicochemical complementarity between the two partners, such a model may help identify potential binding regions and even complex structures. Our work will focus on incorporating dynamic features and explicit representations of both protein and nucleic acid in future protein–nucleic acid binding site prediction.
