Revision hip arthroscopy: Current concepts and considerations in diagnosis, evaluation, and management
Authors: Nicole Rynecki Baker, Daniel J. Kaplan, Spencer Stein, Thomas Youm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHip arthroscopy has grown exponentially over the past decade, with a parallel rise in revisions, underscoring the importance of refined patient selection and diagnostic accuracy. This review outlines the most common causes of failure after primary hip arthroscopy and provides a framework for evaluation, imaging, and management. Notably, differentiating between impingement and instability is crucial, as misclassification can worsen outcomes. Revision procedures may provide clinically meaningful improvement in patient-reported outcomes and delay total hip arthroplasty in well-selected patients. However, recognizing when to shift from preservation to replacement is key to optimizing outcomes.

Introduction

Hip arthroscopy for the treatment of femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) has experienced a dramatic increase in utilization over the past decade. According to Bartlett et al.,1 the number of hip arthroscopies performed annually rose nearly 1500% from 2016 to 2022, with a concomitant increase in revision rates now estimated at 5.7%. As the field grows, so does the imperative to refine indications and techniques, particularly in revision cases where the stakes are higher and the outcomes less predictable. Not every patient with hip pain has FAIS, and hip arthroscopy is not a panacea. There is a wide spectrum of conditions that are perceived as hip pain from the patient's perspective. These range from intra-articular to extra-articular pathologies, from osseous to soft-tissue origins, and from issues involving static to dynamic stabilizers (Fig. 1).2–4 A comprehensive and individualized diagnostic workup is essential to differentiate these. This aim of this article was to comprehensively review the current evidence and strategies for evaluating and treating patients with persistent pain or dysfunction after previous primary hip arthroscopy. While the most common etiologies leading to revision hip arthroscopy are discussed herein, each represents a complex topic that could warrant an in-depth review of its own. Thus, this review serves as a broad overview rather than an exhaustive analysis of each etiology.

Clinical evaluation

A thorough history and physical examination remain the cornerstone of diagnosis before considering revision hip arthroscopy. Key elements include timing and character of symptoms. Pain that never improved postoperatively suggests incomplete correction or an incorrect original diagnosis. New or evolving pain in the short-term postoperative period may point toward complications such as iatrogenic capsular insufficiency resulting in hip instability, whereas initial improvement followed by recurrence suggests repair failure or arthritic progression. Equally important is the activities that cause pain. Pain with prolonged sitting or activities that involve deep flexion is often indicative of residual impingement.5,6 Pain with walking is more consistent with instability.5,7,8 Pain with weight bearing, particularly in patients demonstrating features of relative energy deficiency in sports (previously known as the female athlete triad) or a recent increase in activity, is concerning for an occult femoral neck stress fracture.9 Pain when lying on the ipsilateral side at night can indicate greater trochanter bursitis or abductor tears.10

The physical examination should progress systematically and include targeted palpation, with attention to the adductor and external oblique aponeurosis at the pubic symphysis, the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS), the greater trochanter, the ischiofemoral space and lesser trochanter, the ischial tuberosity, and the lumbar paraspinal musculature. Tenderness in these regions along with pain with resistance testing of the respective tendinous attachments could identify athletic pubalgia, rectus tendonitis, greater trochanter bursitis, ischiofemoral impingement, hamstring tendonitis, and/or lumbosacral etiologies as referred sources of pain.11–13 Range of motion (ROM) limitations and rotational asymmetries may indicate soft contractures, femoral version abnormalities, or osteoarthritis progression.14 Although the flexion, adduction, and internal rotation test is often associated with FAI, it can also elicit pain in the setting of a femoral neck stress fracture. The log roll test, involving passive internal and external rotation of the leg with the patient supine and the hip in neutral position, is an additional test that can indicate a femoral neck stress fracture.9,15 Special tests and provocative maneuvers can differentiate instability from impingement. Laxity tests, such as the anterior apprehension or dial test, may identify capsular insufficiency.8 The prone apprehension relocation test is also another test to identify symptomatic instability.16 A thorough neurovascular assessment can identify iatrogenic neuropraxias, most commonly those involving the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve or the pudendal nerve, or lumbar radiculopathy.17–19 It can also identify sciatic neuritis associated with ischiofemoral impingement.20

Imaging

A comprehensive radiographic workup is essential and should include standing anteroposterior (AP) pelvis, false-profile, cross-table lateral, 45° and 90° Dunn elongated neck views. A standing AP pelvis is imperative because it allows assessment of the functional pelvic tilt. The AP pelvis and false-profile view are particularly useful for assessing the acetabulum, allowing identification of dysplasia, overcoverage, undercoverage, and acetabular retroversion. The false-profile view also offers the most reliable visualization of subspine impingement. The cross-table lateral and Dunn views collectively provide a circumferential, 360-degree assessment of the femoral head-neck junction, which is critical for identifying cam-type impingement or evidence of previous over-resection.21

Advanced imaging is necessary in nearly every revision case. Three-tesla (3T) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allows evaluation of both the labrum and intra-articular pathology, and extra-articular soft tissue. A magnetic resonance arthrogram (MRA) offers improved assessment of the capsular volume and competency as well as cartilage, although capsular anatomy can oftentimes be appreciated on a good-quality MRI. Computed tomography (CT) with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction can quantify residual deformities in cases of under resection and guide surgical planning. It is also best for appreciating acetabular version and femoral head coverage.22 CT-based version studies are critical when rotational abnormalities are suspected.23,24

If there is associated back or radicular pain, lumbar spine radiographs and an MRI scan should be obtained. Finally, diagnostic injections can help localize the pain source. If there is overlap with lumbar spine pathology, an epidural or intra-articular injection can help distinguish the two.25 Similarly, ultrasound-guided injections to other extra-articular regions, such as the hamstrings or iliopsoas muscle, can help differentiate extra-articular from intra-articular pathology.26

Mechanisms of Failure, Strategies for Avoidance, and Potential Solutions

Osseous impingement

Residual impingement

Systematic reviews identify residual FAI as the most common indication for revision hip arthroscopy, accounting for 63%–90% of cases.27,28 The femoroacetabular impingement resection arc, measured on a 45° Dunn lateral radiograph, can be used to assess the adequacy of cam resection. A best-fit circle is drawn to incorporate the region immediately inferiorly to the AIIS, the subspine, and the lateral femoral neck base. The maximal radial distance (MRD) is then measured from the circumference of the circle to the apex of the cam lesions, which represents the residual cam deformity. Studies have demonstrated that patients with postoperative cam MRD <3.15 mm achieved significantly higher patient-reported outcomes at 2-year follow-up.29 Postoperative 3D CT reconstructions of patients with persistent impingement after their index hip arthroscopy demonstrate that residual cam lesions were most commonly found at the superolateral head-neck junction, with the greatest alpha angle at the 1:15 position.22 These preoperative imaging modalities combined with intraoperative systematic use of fluoroscopy and dynamic ROM testing are critical to ensuring that the residual impingement is appropriately addressed during revision surgery.

Although patients undergoing revision hip arthroscopy for residual FAI often demonstrate improvement in PROs postoperatively, their outcomes consistently fall short compared with those who had an adequate resection during their primary surgery.30–32 Therefore, it is imperative to make every effort to do an appropriate resection in the primary setting through careful preoperative planning. Using CT-based three-dimensional modeling, Ross et al. mapped alpha angles to a corresponding clockface (with 3:00 oriented anteriorly). They then correlated the clockface with 6 standardized fluoroscopic views that enable visualization of the region between 11:45 and 2:30. This region consistently captured the most symptomatic deformities. Three views are taken with the hip extended, and 3 with it in 50° of flexion. An extension and internal rotation view gives a more superolateral view of the neck (11:45), and as the hip is flexed and externally rotated, more of the anterior neck is visualized (moving toward the 2:45). This strategy optimizes the utility of fluoroscopy while limiting radiation.33 Current research is investigating use of intraoperative CT and robotics for cam resection, which may change current practice. However, currently, it is cost prohibitive, and concerns about radiation exposure in this younger patient population preclude widespread applicability. Thus, a systematic utilization of fluoroscopy is paramount to ensuring adequate resection.34,35

Heterotopic ossification

Although osseous impingement is most commonly a result of residual FAI, it also can occur secondary to heterotopic ossification (HO) in the setting of an adequate cam resection. Surgery around the hip is particularly susceptible to HO, but the use of routine prophylaxis (eg, celecoxib and indomethacin) may decrease rates from 25% to 5.6%.36 Risk factors of HO after hip arthroscopy include male sex, unrepaired capsulotomies, and extensive femoroplasties.37,38 Even in cases with radiographic evidence of HO, only a small percentage of these are symptomatic. However, in cases of symptomatic HO, patients can be indicated for arthroscopic, or in rare cases, open resection with good outcomes.37,39

Instability

Femoral over-resection

Hip pain exists on a spectrum, from instability to impingement. Unlike a dislocating shoulder, patients rarely describe their hip as unstable, and there is significant symptom overlap between instability and impingement despite them being on 2 sides of the disease continuum.8,11,40,41 This distinction matters because treating a patient for impingement when the root issue is instability can further destabilize the hip and worsen symptoms. There are several main etiologies for instability, which can be broadly categorized as either osseous pathoanatomy or capsular insufficiency.42,43 Both categories can be further differentiated by underlying cause, which includes inherent patient-related factors such as acetabular dysplasia in the former or capsular laxity related to connective tissue disorders in the latter. They can also be a result of iatrogenic factors such as excessive cam or pincer resection in the former or failure to repair a capsulotomy in the latter.44,45

Excessive cam resection contributes to instability by disrupting the suction seal between the femoral head and labrum.45,46 Mansor et al.42 reported that patients with over-resection after their index procedure have inferior PROs and increased conversion rates to total hip arthroplasty (THA). The most popular salvage technique for this problem draws from shoulder instability principles. It involves a labral reconstruction combined with a remplissage-type technique, which aims to restore the suction seal by augmenting the labrum and filling the osseous defect with soft tissue, similar to the approach for a Hill-Sachs lesion.47 Since original publication of the technique, there have been several variations of it, but the concept remains the same: First, reconstruct the labrum using an oversized autograft or allograft. Next, measure the dimensions of the femoral defect arthroscopically and prepare the appropriate graft size on the back table. Place suture anchors into the defect, and then use them to shuttle the graft in. Autograft, allograft, and dermal matrix have all been described as possible graft options.48 Long-term outcomes for this technique are limited because it is relatively new. Arner et al.49 reported on 13 patients followed for a mean of 3.1 years and found that all patients other than the one who required conversion to THA demonstrated significant improvement in PROs, with 83% and 67% meeting MCID for modified Harris Hip Score and both the Harris Hip Score for Activities of Daily Living (HOS ADL) and HOS Sport, respectively. Although PROs improved postoperatively, they remained inferior to those achieved after primary hip arthroscopy, a finding that is expected given the salvage nature of the procedure. It is essential to recognize that this technique uses a soft-tissue solution to address an underlying bony pathology, and thus long-term durability remains uncertain. Depending on patient age, activity demands, and the extent or arthritic change, THA may offer a more durable and definitive treatment option.

Acetabular dysplasia

Acetabular coverage exists along a spectrum, from dysplasia to overcoverage, reflecting the broader instability to impingement continuum. As our understanding of assessing a three-dimensional (3D) joint with two-dimensional (2D) imaging has improved, surgeons now combine a variety of radiographic measurements to understand acetabular morphology. These include the lateral center edge angle (LCEA) and Tönnis angle, both measured on the AP pelvis radiograph. An LCEA <20° or Tönnis angle >10° indicates dysplasia, denoting superolateral undercoverage and/or a steep and deficient roof, respectively. The acetabular quotient can also be calculated on the AP pelvis radiograph. It is the ratio of the acetabular depth (the perpendicular distance from the midpoint of the acetabular width line to the acetabular dome) to the acetabular width (the distance from the lateral edge of the acetabular sourcil to the most inferior portion of the acetabulum). A ratio of <0.25 is classified as frankly dysplastic.50 On a false-profile view, an anterior center edge angle should be measured, with angles <20° indicating anterior undercoverage (Fig. 2).

Acetabular retroversion produces a mixed morphology, with functional anterior overcoverage and posterior undercoverage. This can lead to both instability and impingement in the same hip.50,51 Qualitative radiographic indicators of acetabular retroversion on the AP pelvis radiograph include an ischial spine sign and crossover sign.52–54 To supplement radiographic assessment, CT-based measurements also provide important information regarding acetabular version. Anterior and posterior acetabular sector angles measured on axial CT images help characterize dysplasia. However, supine CT imaging does not account for weight-bearing pelvic tilt or rotation, both of which can significantly alter functional acetabular orientation.55,56 Accordingly, radiographic and CT measurements should be interpreted in conjunction to achieve a comprehensive evaluation of acetabular dysplasia.

Broadly, dysplastic patients as a cohort have femoral head undercoverage, and resultant increased hip contact forces due to eccentric edge loading of the acetabular rim. The severity of dysplasia is subdivided into frank dysplasia, defined as <18° degrees, and borderline dysplasia, between 18° and 25°. Hip arthroscopy can address intra-articular pain generators, such as labral tears or mechanical impingement from cam lesions in patients with dysplasia, but it carries an inherent risk of exacerbating structural instability, making it a controversial and ongoing area of research. In patients with borderline dysplasia, the labrum and acetabular cartilage often become hypertrophied, serving as secondary stabilizers to compensate for bony undercoverage, particularly superolaterally where joint forces are highest. Despite its hypertrophy, the labral tissue is structurally inadequate to withstand the abnormal loading across the diminished acetabular surface, making it prone to both intrasubstance tears and chondrolabral separation. This can lead to accelerated cartilage degeneration and early osteoarthritis. In some cases, the labrum becomes everted, preventing it from contacting the femoral head when traction is released. Without this contact, the suction seal fails, exacerbating instability. Therefore, while isolated hip arthroscopy may have a role in ameliorating these intra-articular soft-tissue pathologies in borderline dysplastic patients, it should never be performed without a concomitant periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) in frank dysplasia.57–59

The critical distinction between patients who may benefit from isolated hip arthroscopy and those who require a concomitant PAO lies in the accurate identification and appropriate classification of dysplasia. In cases of borderline dysplasia, patients with additional risk factors of instability or preexisting chondral damage may not be ideal candidates for further joint preservation procedures. Careful assessment of index procedure arthroscopic images is particularly useful for assessing the latter as chondral damage is often underestimated on MRI, especially in the presence of a hypertrophied labrum. Younger patients with frank dysplasia but preserved cartilage may still benefit from a PAO in combination with hip arthroscopy to effectively address both the underlying structural instability and intra-articular pathology.60 Outcomes after this combined approach are overall very positive. A study by Ruzbarsky et al. demonstrated a 92% survivorship at 2-year follow-up with significant improvement in PROs. In a systematic review by Lee et al.,61 the authors reported sustained survivorship at midterm and long-term follow-up, with included studies reporting a 0%–3% conversion to THA and 0%–10% secondary surgery rate between 5 and 13 years of follow-up. Critically, PAOs do not seem to dramatically alter outcomes after THA if eventually indicated. Performing a THA after a PAO presents unique challenges due to altered anatomy, healed osteotomies, and retained hardware, but outcomes are generally favorable.62,63

Capsular insufficiency

Capsular insufficiency lies at the intersection of patient-related factors and iatrogenic causes of hip instability. Certain individuals, particularly those with underlying collagen disorders such as Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, have inherently more patulous capsules, rendering them less tolerant to surgical insult. Preoperative screening for generalized hypermobility can be performed using the 5-step Beighton score, with a score of ≥4 indicating joint hypermobility.45

The iliofemoral ligament is the strongest and most critical soft-tissue component of the hip capsule, primarily resisting anterior translation, extension, and external rotation.64 From a bony alignment standpoint, both the acetabulum and femur are anteverted, approximately 15°–20° and 10°–15°, respectively. This configuration results in most of the femoral head coverage being posteromedial, leaving the anterolateral hip relatively uncovered and more dependent on the iliofemoral ligament for stability. This is even more important in patients with excessive femoral or acetabular anteversion. During hip arthroscopy, standard anterolateral and mid-anterior portals are placed through the iliofemoral ligament. Different types of capsulotomies result in varying degrees of iliofemoral ligament disruption, ranging from periportal capsulotomy to T-capsulotomy, representing a tradeoff between capsular preservation and surgical exposure. Biomechanical studies have shown that all capsulotomy types increase joint laxity, but proper capsular repair can restore near normal stability.65 Thus, although there is no universal consensus, routine capsular closure is increasingly advocated, especially in patients with ligamentous laxity, borderline dysplasia, or high functional demands such as athletes.66–68

Approach to instability

When evaluating a patient for postarthroscopy hip instability, it is essential to consider both surgical and patient-specific risk factors. These include the extent of previous bony work, labral treatment, type of capsulotomy, whether the capsule was repaired, and intrinsic factors such as generalized laxity or borderline dysplasia. Female patients are more frequently affected, likely because of greater ligamentous laxity and thinner capsules.69 In addition, noncompliance with postoperative restrictions, particularly excessive early motion, can contribute to failure of capsular repair. Physical examination should include provocative maneuvers aimed at stressing the anterior capsule. These include the anterior apprehension test, abduction-external rotation test, prone external rotation test, and dial test. Assessment for femoral version is also critical because excessive femoral anteversion can compound instability, which will be discussed later in this review. Advanced imaging, such as MRI or MRA, can assist in identifying capsular abnormalities, including redundancy, rents, or full-thickness defects.45

Patients with a known history of unrepaired capsulotomy or acute traumatic disruption of a previous repair may be candidates for direct capsular repair with or without augmentation, provided the tissue quality is sufficient. In cases of capsular redundancy without a discrete tear, plication may be performed.70,71 However, if the capsule is excessively patulous or the native tissue is poor in quality, capsular reconstruction is indicated. Capsular reconstruction is typically performed arthroscopically using either autograft or allograft. It is secured proximally on the acetabulum with suture anchors and distally with sutures into the inferior leaflet.72 Short-term and midterm studies have demonstrated it as an effective treatment for postarthroscopy instability, resulting in improved clinical outcomes and a low rate of conversion to THA.72,73

Rotational deformities and extra-articular pathology

Femoral anteversion and ischiofemoral impingement

Femoral version decreases from approximately 40° at birth to adult normal range (5°–20°) by age 8. Disruption of this process can lead to persistent, excessive anteversion.74–76 Excessive anteversion compromises anterior hip stability by shifting the arc of motion forward and increasing reliance on the iliofemoral ligament. This predisposes these patients to instability, as discussed earlier in this review. Clinically, this manifests as increased internal rotation. Without compensatory external tibial torsion, these patients often develop an in-toeing gait, which improves hip congruency but tightens the short external rotators and internally rotates the lesser trochanter, narrowing the ischiofemoral space. This predisposes patients to posterior extra-articular impingement. Posterior ischial or deep buttock pain reproduced by maneuvers such as long-stride walking, running, or passive hip extension with combined adduction and internal rotation should raise suspicion.77 It may also be associated with symptoms of sciatic nerve compression. Radiographs are typically normal in patients with ischiofemoral impingement, although cystic changes in the ischium or lesser trochanter may appear. MRI may reveal quadratus femoris edema acutely or fatty degeneration in chronic cases as well as associated proximal hamstring tendonitis. Ischiofemoral space ≤15 mm and quadratus femoris space ≤10 mm serve as the diagnostic criterion.20 However, the reliability of these measurements is debated because of the dynamic nature of the pathology and variability in leg positioning during scans.78

While many patients respond to nonoperative management and targeted physical therapy with focused abductor strengthening, surgery may be indicated in refractory cases. Limited arthroscopic procedures such as lesser trochanter resection can address localized symptoms and increase ischiofemoral space but do not correct the underlying torsional deformity if it is the underlying etiology.79 In patients with version extremes, typically >30°–35°, femoral derotation osteotomy may be required (Fig. 3). This is typically performed using an intramedullary nail with controlled subtrochanteric osteotomy and rotation of the distal fragment to restore alignment. Clinical outcomes have been favorable, with significant improvements in hip function and low complication rates. However, patients with excessive femoral anteversion often develop a compensatory external tibial torsion to maintain a neutral foot progression angle, creating a torsional mismatch. Failing to identify this preoperatively can lead to an unmasking of deformity after osteotomy, resulting in an out-toeing gait. Therefore, preoperative assessment must include hip rotation, thigh-foot axis in prone position, and evaluation of foot progression angle. In cases of significant mismatch, a concomitant tibial osteotomy, performed either acutely over an intramedullary nail or gradually using an circular frame, may be necessary.24

Clinical presentation of femoral rotational abnormalities and standard management.

Femoral retroversion and subspine impingement

Femoral retroversion refers to a more posterior orientation, and by convention, even 0° of version is considered “relative retroversion” compared with the normative 15°. It is 3 times less common than excessive femoral anteversion. Conversely, it results in increased femoral external rotation and an out-toeing gait. Retroversion may be associated with anterior extra-articular impingement in deep flexion, particularly in the subspine region distal to the AIIS. Clinical presentation often overlaps with intra-articular FAI, necessitating close scrutiny of imaging.24,80 Subspine morphology is best visualized on a false-profile radiograph or CT scan. Subspine impingement may be associated with a more distal cam lesion on MRI compared with classic FAI.81,82

In patients with a prominent subspine not addressed during primary hip arthroscopy, revision hip arthroscopy can decompress the subspine region while simultaneously assessing the labrum and revising any reinjury.83,84 However, in patients with an unimpressive subspine, but retroverted femora, a derotation osteotomy is required to correct the underlying torsional deformity (Fig. 3).24 In patients with less than 5° of anteversion, Hayden et al. reported that treatment with femoral derotation osteotomy either alone or combined with hip arthroscopy results in improvement in PROs.85

Combined rotational deformities

The McKibbin index is the sum of femoral and acetabular version and is used to assess the combined rotational alignment of the hip. It guides treatment planning for patients with version mismatches by identifying those at risk of instability or impingement due to abnormal combined version. A high McKibbin index (>50°) indicates excessive combined anteversion, which predisposes to anterior instability, especially in the context of dysplasia or capsular insufficiency. Conversely, a low McKibbin index (<20°) reflects combined retroversion, increasing the risk of impingement. In both combined extremes, an isolated PAO or femoral derotation osteotomy is typically insufficient; both the acetabular and femoral rotational abnormality should be addressed. The intermediate zone (typically 20°–50°) represents a range of combined versions where isolated PAO or femoral derotation osteotomy may be considered, either with or without a revision hip arthroscopy, depending on the predominant pathology and patient-specific factors. Caution must be exercised in patients with compensatory version mismatches (eg, increased femoral anteversion but acetabular retroversion) because correcting 1 pathology may worsen symptoms.74,86

Soft-tissue pathologies

Labral insufficiency

Much like the evolving understanding of the meniscus in the knee, the approach to the acetabular labrum has progressed significantly, shifting from debridement toward preservation and reconstruction. In the revision setting, repair of labral retears or previously inadequately repaired tissue remains a viable option when sufficient healthy labral tissue is present.87,88

When faced with a segmental labral defect and poor tissue quality, but with an intact chondrolabral junction, repair with augmentation may be considered. By contrast, when large defects are accompanied by poor tissue integrity, a calcified labrum, or chondrolabral separation, labral reconstruction is the preferred treatment.89 For smaller defects, segmental reconstruction may be performed, although concerns remain regarding stress at the graft-native tissue interface. Larger defects are best addressed with circumferential reconstruction, which provides a more uniform restoration of the labrum but is technically more demanding.90,91 Various reconstruction techniques exist and can be performed using either autograft or allograft, with no significant difference in outcomes reported. The graft may be premeasured and prepared on the back table or fixed first with later intra-articular resection of the residual segment.92,93

Both revision labral repair and labral reconstruction demonstrate significant improvements in PROs at 2-year follow-up.87,88 Therefore, the ultimate choice between repair and reconstruction depends on intraoperative assessment of labral quality. Importantly, outcomes after revision procedures are generally less predictable and often inferior to primary surgery, emphasizing the critical importance of optimal labral management during the index procedure.94

Cartilage lesions

Irrespective of the underlying etiology, the data consistently show poorer outcomes with hip arthroscopy in the setting of advanced chondral disease and <2 mm of joint space. Tönnis grade is the most common radiographic classification system used for qualifying hip osteoarthritis. Tönnis grade 1 patients have significantly higher THA conversion rates (25.8% vs 4.8%) and reduced survivorship (71.0% vs. 85.5%) compared with grade 0 patients at 10-year follow-up.95 Tönnis grade 2 patients show even more unpredictable results. While some studies report significant improvements in PROs at 2-year follow-up, others demonstrate notable deterioration over midterm follow-up with 7 times greater odds of conversion to THA compared with Tönnis grade 0 patients and 4 times greater odds compared with Tönnis grade 1 patients.96,97 Tönnis grade 3 patients demonstrate only a 25% survivorship at 10-year follow-up, and it is, therefore, not recommended that these patients undergo hip arthroscopy.98 Similarly, patients with full-thickness chondral defects or bipolar cartilage lesions are poor candidates for revision preservation procedures.99–101 Careful patient selection is critical. Patients with either more advanced arthritic changes or full-thickness chondral defects should be counseled regarding the limited utility and poor durability of joint-preserving procedures and are more likely to benefit from THA.

Conclusion

In summary, successful management of hip pain after hip arthroscopy hinges on understanding the difference between impingement and instability. This can then be used to synthesize the history, physical examination, and imaging to accurately diagnose the true etiology of hip pain: whether soft tissue or osseous, intra-articular or extra-articular, or even external to the hip joint itself. Treatment should be individualized. In appropriately selected patients, procedures performed during revision hip arthroscopy can serve as a durable, temporizing intervention aimed at delaying the need for THA. Similarly, there may be a role for concomitant osteotomies depending on the etiology. This is particularly impactful in younger populations, where preserving the native joint into the fourth or fifth decade can be clinically and psychosocially meaningful. However, it is critical to ensure that the patient is still a good candidate for hip preservation. If not, THA is the more appropriate option.
