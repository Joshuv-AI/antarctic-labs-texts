Sex-Focused Evaluation of Income and Education and the Risk of Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Diseases Within a Universal Healthcare Context
Authors: Kate Haichin, David Alter, Paul Poirier, Jiayi Ni, Jean-Claude Tardif, Shaun G Goodman, Andrew Yan, Dennis Ko, Marc-André d’Entremont, Patrick R Lawler, Marco Spaziano, Abhinav Sharma, George Thanassoulis, Jean-Pierre Dery, Michel Nguyen, Samer Mansour, Douglas S Lee, Erick Schampaert, Thao Huynh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough education and income are established determinants of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), whether sex differences in ASCVD risk are associated with these factors within a universal public healthcare system is unclear. We examined the independent associations of income and educational attainment with incident ASCVD and their potential variations by sex in Quebec, Canada.MethodsCARTaGENE is an ongoing prospective cohort study. We completed Cox models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for incident ASCVD, stratified by annual household income and educational attainment, among female and male participants without prior ASCVD. The primary endpoint was time to first ASCVD event.ResultsThere were 17,626 participants with a mean age of 54 ± 8 years (52% female), followed for a median of 6.6 years (2009-2016). Females with the lowest level of education, without collegiate education, had a 61% increase in the hazard of incident ASCVD compared to females with the highest level of education, with university studies (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.61; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.15, 2.26). Compared to those with an annual household income of ≥ $CAD100,000, males with an annual household income of < $CAD50,000 had a 61% increase in the hazard of incident ASCVD (HR:1.61; 95% CI: 1.23, 2.11).ConclusionsWithin a public healthcare system and with relatively affordable medication coverage, we still observed inequalities in ASCVD risk associated with educational attainment and household income. Future research is needed to confirm the potential sex differences in the impacts of educational attainment and income on the risk of ASCVD.Keywords: education, income, female, sex, cardiovascular disease

Beyond traditional cardiovascular (CV) risk factors, several social determinants of health are associated with increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events.1, 2, 3, 4 Educational attainment and income are 2 major social determinants of ASCVD.1, 2, 3, 4 Nevertheless, a point that remains to be determined is whether CV health inequalities associated with low income and limited education persist within a public universal healthcare context and with low out-of-pocket drug costs. In addition, whether sex can modify the effects of low educational attainment and income on the risk of ASCVD remains unclear.

Quebec is a Canadian province with universal coverage for in-hospital and ambulatory healthcare. Additionally, all Quebec residents are required to subscribe to their employer's or the provincial drug insurance plan. The required premium for the Quebec drug insurance plan is determined by the individual’s income, with a maximum of CAD$766 per year. The maximum monthly copayment for medications is $102 (equivalent to $1232 per year).5 Persons with low- or no-income benefit from free drug coverage and monthly financial subsidies. These measures may reduce financial obstacles in purchasing CV pharmacotherapy. Nonetheless, the pecuniary barriers may persist in individuals who have relatively low income but are insufficiently deprived to qualify for financial subsidies and no-cost drug acquisition.

Evaluation of the independent effects of educational attainment and income on ASCVD risk may be more accurate within Quebec's healthcare system, in which the risk of incident ASCVD may be less likely to be influenced by the affordability of healthcare and drug coverage. Consequently, we aim to evaluate the independent associations of income and education with the risk of incident ASCVD, and their potential variations in male vs female patients, within the Quebec healthcare system.

Methods

Population and study design

The CARTaGENE study's design has been described previously.6 In brief, the study is an ongoing population-based prospective cohort with a random sampling of individuals between the ages of 40 and 69 years from 4 metropolitan areas (Montreal, Quebec City, Saguenay, and Sherbrooke) in Quebec, Canada. Participants who lived in long-term healthcare facilities, lived in First Nations reserves, or were incarcerated were excluded. The composition of the final CARTaGENE cohort was comparable to that of the provincial population, with CARTaGENE participants having slightly higher levels of education and a modest overrepresentation of minority racial and ethnic groups.7

All participants provided informed consent and completed a detailed health questionnaire, which included questions about their annual gross household income, number of family members, and highest level of education completed. The research coordinators completed noninvasive anthropometric measurements and obtained central laboratory measurements of several biochemical and hematological parameters.

For this analysis, we excluded patients with a history of prior ASCVD events or interventions. The absence of preexisting ASCVD was determined by questionnaire and by verifying participants’ records in the provincial health administrative dataset during the 5 years preceding enrollment.

Education and income classification

Annual household income and education were self-reported. For this analysis, we excluded participants who did not provide their annual household income or educational attainment. We classified the participants into 4groups of annual household income, as follows: (i) < $50,000; (ii) $50,000-$74,999; (iii) $75,000-$99,999; and (iv) ≥$100,000.

Ethical concerns

All CARTaGENE participants consented to the use of their health data for research at the time of enrollment into the main study. McGill Health University Centre Research Institute approved ASCVD secondary analyses from the CARTaGENE database. All patients were identified only by a study code. Access to the provincial health datasets was approved by the Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec. As this analysis used de-identified data, the McGill Health University Center Research Institute institutional review board did not require patient consent specifically for this study.

Main endpoints and censoring

We defined the primary endpoint as the time to the first occurrence of an ASCVD event. An ASCVD event was defined as one of the following: CV death; acute coronary syndrome (myocardial infarction or unstable angina); coronary revascularization; ischemic stroke and/or transient ischemic attack; or an ischemic limb event or intervention. To censor ASCVD events, we linked the CARTaGENE dataset with the provincial health databases (Régie de l’assurance maladie du Québec [RAMQ]), the Ministry of Health and Social Services (MED-ECHO), and the Quebec death registry. These medical-administrative databases compiled diagnostic and procedure codes for all medical encounters, including inpatient and outpatient visits, discharge summaries, and causes of death. We used the International Classification of Diseases, ninth revision (ICD-9) and tenth revision (ICD-10). We listed the used ICD codes in Supplemental List S1. All participants were followed until one of the following events occurred: an ASCVD event, migration out of the province, or completion of the follow-up period (March 31, 2016).

Statistical analysis

We presented normally distributed continuous data, with their means and standard deviations, and performed statistical testing using 1-way analysis of variance tests. For dichotomous variables, we performed general χ2 tests. We used a significance level of 0.05 and 2-sided tests to compare the baseline characteristics. We then tabulated the incidence rates of the primary endpoint and its components, stratified by education attainment and annual household income, for female and male participants separately.

We compared the baseline characteristics between the participants included and excluded from this analysis to examine whether any non-randomness in missing data may have biased the results. We used proportional hazard Cox models to identify factors independently associated with time to the first ASCVD event. We adjusted for the following factos: living conditions (member of a couple or not); traditional ASCVD risk factors (age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, active smoking, non-high-density cholesterol (HDL); glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C); waist–hip ratio; glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min); born in Canada; prior or active depression; and non-White race. We performed a mutual adjustment for income and education in the final model using the Bonferroni adjustment. To examine the potential effect modifications on the hazard of incident ASCVD, we entered interaction terms of “sex and income” and “sex and education” into the final model.

In addition to annual household income, we also computed the household equivalence scale, which is the annual household income divided by the square root of the number of individuals within the household. The household equivalence scale had been advocated as a more accurate measurement of disposable household income as more economies of scale may be associated with expenditures generated by household size.8

We employed multiple imputations using fully conditional specification methods to handle missing data on covariates and outcomes. For missing outcomes, we imputed both outcome event status and time to event. We combined the 5 imputed datasets using the SAS MIANALYZE procedure (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). We did not detect any violation of the proportional hazards modelling assumption with Martingale residuals and the Kolmogorov-type Supremum tests. We constructed Fine and Gray models to detect potential competing risk of non-CV death. The Fine and Gray’s results were similar to the main results, suggesting no significant competing risk due to non-CV deaths.9 Finally, we completed the final Cox multivariate models with multiple imputation for missing income and education data. All analyses were completed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

We also completed a directed acyclic graph (Supplemental Fig. S1) to clarify causal relationships, as intermediate variables on the causal pathway may be influenced by both educational attainment and household income (active smoking and depression). As additional sensitivity analyses, we performed proportional hazard Cox models without adjustment for active smoking and depression. The results of these Cox models were essentially comparable to the results of the main model.

Results

The CARTaGENE cohort enrolled a total of 19,994 participants. After excluding participants with prior ASCVD disease (n = 1113), those who did not disclose their annual household income (n = 1138), and those who did not report their level of completed education (n = 117), we retained 17,626 participants for the final analysis. The median follow-up period was 6.6 years (quartile 1: 6.4; quartile 3: 6.8; years 2009-2016).

The mean age was 54 ± 8 years (52% female). Approximately one-quarter of participants had only a high school education or less. Roughly one-third of the patients (36%) had an annual household income of < $50,000. The participants with a high school education or less and those with low annual household income were older, with higher prevalences of active smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and increased waist-to-hip ratio. We presented the baseline characteristics of females and males stratified by educational attainment (Tables 1 and 2, respectively) and by annual household income (Tables 3 and 4, respectively). The characteristics of participants who reported both educational attainment and annual household income were compared with those who declined to report either, in Supplemental Table S1.

Compared with female participants with at least a university education, those with only a high school education or less had a 61% increase in the adjusted hazard for incident ASCVD. On the other hand, male participants in the lowest annual household income group (annual household income < $50,000) had a 63% increase in the hazard of incident ASCVD compared to males in the highest group of annual household income (≥ $100,000). Effect modifications by sex occurred for both education and income (P = 0.001 for both). No significant interaction occurred between education and income (P = 0.85).

We showed the magnitudes of the sex differences in the hazard of ASCVD in Table 7. For educational attainment, no conclusive association with ASCVD occurred for the overall cohort and for male participants, whereas female participants with a high school education or less had a 61% increase in the hazard of ASCVD. Although female participants with an annual household income of < $50,000 had no conclusive increase in the risk of ASCVD, an increase in hazard of 44% occurred for the overall cohort, and of 61% for male participants within the same income category.

In terms of absolute risk difference, female participants with low educational attainment had a 2.8% absolute increase in risk, compared to those with at least a collegiate education, and a 3.4% absolute increase in risk compared to female participants with university degrees. For male participants, a 2.3% absolute risk increase occurred in men with low annual household income (< $50,000), compared to those with income of ≥ $50,000, and a 6% absolute risk increase, compared to male participants with ≥ $100,000 in income (Tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

Our work showed that within a public healthcare system with relatively low out-of-pocket costs for drug acquisition, remarkable health inequalities remained among individuals with low educational attainment and low household income. We observed sex differences in ASCVD risk across different levels of education and household income. Female participants without a college education had a more than 60% increase in the hazard of ASCVD, although annual household income did not have a conclusive impact on ASCVD risk among female participants. Conversely, male participants with low annual household income had an approximately 60% increase in the hazard of ASCVD, whereas educational attainment did not conclusively affect their ASCVD risk.

The adverse influences of education and income level on the risk of ASCVD have been observed previously in public, private, and mixed healthcare settings, as well as in countries with varying levels of economic development.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Even with public healthcare, the inability to afford preventive pharmacotherapy may exacerbate health inequities. As Quebec has extensive drug coverage and subsidized dispensing, this province is an environment with fewer economic constraints than many other healthcare contexts. This context allows us to evaluate the independent impacts of educational attainment and income deprivation on ASCVD risk, with less confounding by the individual’s ability to pay for services and medications. In addition, the independent associations of low educational attainment and low household income with risk of ASCVD were controlled in our study for other important confounders, such as living condition (member of a couple or not), born in Canada or not, White or non-White race, and depression or not.

Only a few prior authors have examined sex differences in the association between ASCVD risk and education and income. Manor et al. reported a higher relative risk of CV mortality in female participants with ≤ 8 years of education, compared to male participants with the same level of education (relative risk of 2.06 vs 1.46, respectively).10 In a meta-analysis of 116 studies involving 22 million participants, Backholer observed a greater excess ASCVD risk associated with lower educational attainment among female vs male participants.15 In another meta-analysis of 31 studies, Wang et al. suggested that women may be more sensitive to income and educational deprivations.16

Our findings of increased ASCVD risk associated with low educational attainment, particularly among female participants, were consistent with the above-described literature. Individuals with low educational attainment may have poorer comprehension of healthy lifestyles and CVD risk factors. This level of understanding, in turn, can lead to unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, delays in seeking care, and reduced adherence to preventive medical therapies. Kershaw et al. demonstrated that behavioural and modifiable biological risk factors accounted for more than half of the effects of education on ASCVD in 15,067 Dutch individuals.17 Moreover, persons with low educational attainment may be subject to systemic discrimination, with healthcare providers being less inclined to offer them counselling relating to preventive medicine and healthy lifestyles.18

Economic deprivation is strongly associated with ASCVD risk and outcomes.1,10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Many reasons for this epiphenomenon are possible. Socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals may have more stress, which can worsen inflammation and ASCVD risk.19 Low-income men also have more unhealthy habits, such as smoking, consumption of processed foods, and physical inactivity, which aggravate preexisting obesity, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension.1 Low-income individuals may have nonflexible working hours, which can impede their access to preventive care.1

Healthcare providers also may be negatively biased toward individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds.20,21 Willems and colleagues demonstrated that patients from low socioeconomic classes received less advice and fewer opportunities for partnership-building to improve their health.21 In a scoping review of 67 papers, Job et al. showed that patients with low socioeconomic status often face physicians’ stereotyping and implicit bias.22

Low annual household income had differential effects on female and male CARTaGENE participants, with increased ASCVD risk observed primarily among male participants. Male individuals are generally the primary breadwinners for their households and may be more susceptible to financial and occupational stress. These stressors were associated with an increased risk of ASCVD in the INTERHEART study.23 Why low annual household income was not associated conclusively with increased ASCVD risk in female CARTaGENE participants is unclear. This result may be due to insufficient statistical power, given the small number of events among female participants.

Limitations

Although our study had several strengths—including a large sample size, a relatively long follow-up time, and access to self-reported income data on education and income—it also had notable limitations. First, the respondents may not have reported their education and income levels truthfully. Participants might have reported higher income and education levels than they actually had (social desirability bias).24 This reporting may have created a systematic bias and led to underestimation of the true associations between income, education, and ASCVD risk. Second, our results may not be generalizable to rural populations, as the participants were sampled exclusively from urban areas. Individuals residing in rural communities tend to have lower levels of education, lower incomes, and a higher prevalence of ASCVD risk factors.25 Access to care can be particularly problematic in these regions and can further aggravate the health inequities among individuals of low educational attainment and those who are economically deprived. Third, although we did include depression or not, born in Canada or not, and race (White vs non-White) in our multivariate models, our study may not be adequately powered to examine the above potential confounders due to the relatively small number of ASCVD events. We could not adjust for stress, as the CARTaGENE study did not use validated tools to evaluate stress.

Fifth, the CARTaGENE participants were more educated, with higher annual household income, and with fewer individuals of non-White races and fewer immigrants than the Canadian population.26 A strong possibility is that the required consent in the CARTaGENE study introduced consent bias, leading to the “healthy volunteer effect.”27 This selection bias may have further limited the external validity of our findings. Sixth, our follow-up period ended in 2016, and secular changes may have occurred in access to care, income distribution, cost of living, drug pricing, copayment costs, and preventive cardiology. Therefore, our findings may not be replicable in the contemporary era. This possibility highlights the need to replicate the study in a future cohort. Finally, as with any observational study, the sex differences in the potential impacts of income and education on ASCVD risk observed in our study are subject to residual confounding.

Conclusions

Within a public healthcare system with relatively affordable medication coverage, we still observed inequalities in ASCVD risk associated with educational attainment and income. Consistent with prior literature, we observed increased risks of ASCVD among individuals with low educational attainment and low annual household income. Future research is needed to confirm the effect of sex differences associated with educational attainment and household income on the risk of ASCVD.
