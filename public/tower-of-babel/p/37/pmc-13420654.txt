History of Presentation

We report the case of a 66-year-old nonsmoker man who was admitted to the emergency department for acute chest pain and cardiogenic shock. The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated a striking triangular QRS-ST-T (“shark-fin”) waveform in the aVR lead and ST-segment elevation in V1-V3 and I–aVL leads, accompanied by diffuse ST-segment depression (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included acute myocardial infarction, myocarditis, and pulmonary embolism.

Investigations

As the patient was admitted to the emergency department, electrocardiogram (ECG) and arterial blood gas analysis were executed, intravenous inotropic drugs were started, and a transthoracic echocardiogram was realized by a cardiologist, showing a severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction without pericardial effusion.

Management

The ECG showed a shark-fin or triangular QRS-ST-T pattern, which is a rare and dangerous pattern associated with massive ischemia.1 It has been described in acute coronary syndromes involving the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) or the left main coronary artery (LMCA). Urgent coronary angiography was performed, showing LMCA complete thrombotic occlusion (Figure 2). The right coronary artery did not have significant stenosis (Figure 3). The patient underwent primary percutaneous coronary intervention with deployment of a drug-eluting stent in the LMCA-LAD using a provisional technique, followed by final re-wiring of the circumflex artery and opening of the stent struts with optimal angiographic result (Figures 4 and 5), which resulted in hemodynamic stabilization without the need for invasive mechanical circulatory support. Postprocedural ECG showed resolution of the shark-fin pattern (Figure 6).

Outcome and Follow-Up

During the hospital stay the clinical course was favorable, no arrhythmic events were observed, and improvement in both clinical and hemodynamic status allowed a gradual and complete discontinuation of inotropic drugs. The patient was discharged home after 3 days, with a normal ejection fraction (55%). A short-term, presential, follow-up was realized after 2 months, confirming the clinically stable condition and absence of chest pain. Another transthoracic echocardiogram was realized after 5 months, confirming a completely normalized ejection fraction though mild hypokinesia in the anterior territory.

Discussion

The resting 12-lead ECG represents the first-line diagnostic tool in the evaluation of patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Current recommendations state that the ECG should be obtained without delay and interpreted within 10 minutes from first medical contact.2

Within the broad spectrum of electrocardiographic manifestations observed in ACS, the shark-fin ECG pattern has been reported to correlate with extensive myocardial ischemia and an increased global risk profile. It represents the fusion of QRS complex, S-T segment, and T wave into a single broad deflection.3 Although infrequently observed, the shark-fin sign has been associated with LMCA and/or proximal LAD disease and carries a poor prognosis. In fact, ACS involving LMCA as the culprit vessel is usually represented on the ECG as a non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction pattern, characterized by ST depression in 7 or more leads with reciprocal ST-elevation in aVR, and occasionally also in V1.4 These findings are more frequently associated with multivessel disease or LMCA subocclusion on angiography and, given the non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction pattern, may delay the diagnosis and an accurate, prompt, management.5

In the described clinical case, the presence of the shark-fin pattern in the aVR lead directly correlated with the angiographic finding of total LMCA occlusion, underscoring its diagnostic and prognostic value.

Conclusions

The shark-fin ECG pattern has been associated with diffuse and severe ischemia. When involving the aVR lead, it can be a marker of acute LMCA occlusion, leading to catastrophic consequences if not promptly recognized. Rapid diagnosis and urgent reperfusion remain the only strategies capable of improving survival in these patients. This case highlights the importance of promptly recognizing the shark-fin ECG pattern in the aVR lead and emphasizes the need for immediate revascularization.
