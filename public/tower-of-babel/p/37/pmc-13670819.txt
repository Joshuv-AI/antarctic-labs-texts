Direct analysis of transcription factor protected cfDNA in plasma by ChIP-seq: measurement of altered CTCF binding in cancer is a novel biomarker for liquid biopsy
Authors: Dorian Pamart, Brieuc Cuvelier, Adrien Govaerts, Muriel Chapelier, Marielle Herzog, Andrew Retter, Thomas Bygott, Jean-Valery Turatsinze, Benjamin P. Berman, Jacob Micallef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aggregate presence of cell free CTCF-DNA (cfCTCF-DNA) nucleoproteins in plasma has been reported. But the occupancy of individual CTCF binding sites in plasma cfCTCF-DNA has not been studied.ResultsFor the first time, we have isolated endogenous plasma cfCTCF-DNA by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and sequenced the associated cfDNA (ChIP-Seq) in samples from cancer patients and healthy subjects. The mean observed enrichment of plasma cfCTCF-DNA by ChIP was 180-fold with a mean CTCF recovery of 48%. Background cfDNA, primarily in the form of nucleosomes, was almost completely physically removed (> 99.7%) by ChIP and the remainder was removed bioinformatically. cfCTCF-DNA sequences were partitioned into low or high affinity binding cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in silico, on the basis of binding motif concordance with the consensus CTCF binding motif. We observed that cfCTCF-DNA was quantitatively and qualitatively different in samples from healthy subjects or cancer patients. High affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins were present only in the plasma of cancer patients and not in samples from healthy subjects. Testing of small developmental and validation sample cohorts for ChIP-seq peaks for a 14 member panel of high affinity CTCF loci sequences, showed excellent specificity and sensitivity for cancer detection.ConclusionsPlasma sequence data sets comprising > 99% pure CTCF protected ctDNA can be prepared using a 2-step process involving the physical removal of most background cfDNA in vitro, followed by removal of all remaining background cfDNA in silico. CTCF plasma ChIP-seq represents a novel liquid biopsy method for a novel class of liquid biopsy biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02205-x.

Background

CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) is a highly conserved zinc finger transcription factor (TF) that regulates chromatin structure, insulation and transcriptional control in health and disease [1]. Although CTCF protein expression is ubiquitous, CTCF occupancy of its binding sites (BS) is reported to be tissue specific, cancer specific and to vary with tumor type [1–6]. Tissue and cell line CTCF binding studies by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and sequencing of associated DNA (ChIP-Seq) have implicated cancer specific gain and loss of CTCF binding in carcinogenesis, cancer cell proliferation, development of chemoresistance, epithelial-mesenchymal transition and progression to metastatic disease in a variety of cancers including breast, hepatocellular, colorectal, lung and pancreatic cancer [6–13]. CTCF BS occupancy has been proposed as a potential biomarker and a therapeutic target in oncology [5, 10–13].

There is also increasing interest in the analysis of plasma cell free DNA (cfDNA) that circulates bound to CTCF as cell free CTCF-DNA (cfCTCF-DNA) nucleoprotein, as a biomarker for liquid biopsy [14, 15]. The vast majority of circulating plasma nucleoproteins are nucleosomes, in which an approximately 130–200 base pair (bp) cfDNA fragment is bound with high affinity to proteins in a stable histone-DNA complex. However, a small proportion of cfDNA circulates as ultrashort approximately 35–80 bp fragments protected from nuclease digestion by binding to TFs, including CTCF among others [14–16].

The human genome includes tens to hundreds of thousands of CTCF BS loci, with varying binding affinities. A ChIP-seq study of 67 cell lines identified 137,471 non-overlapping CTCF binding peaks, of which approximately 16,000–61,000 are occupied in a single cell type [1, 2, 17–20]. Lower affinity CTCF binding sites are reported to be associated predominantly with transcription regulation and high affinity binding sites with regulation of chromatin structure through topologically associated domains (TADs) [21]. High affinity recombinant CTCF-DNA nucleoproteins are stable with an experimentally measured half-life of up to 22 h [22]. The combination of a long half-life and a large number of binding sites means that a single cell death event may lead to the release of a large number of cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins that are sufficiently stable in plasma to provide a detectable level by liquid biopsy.

The presence of cfCTCF-DNA in plasma has been inferred from DNA footprinting and nucleosome fragmentomics analysis of cfDNA sequencing data [14, 15, 23]. Whilst these methods have established the aggregate presence of cfCTCF-DNA and other TF-DNA complexes in plasma, they have provided little or no information on the occupancy of each of the thousands of individual CTCF binding sites represented in the plasma as cfCTCF-DNA. ChIP-seq and related techniques such as ATAC-seq, are powerful tools that have underpinned our understanding of epigenetic control through transcription factor binding to chromatin. ChIP-seq analysis does provide individual TF binding site occupancy data, but direct measurement of endogenous cfCTCF-DNA nucleoprotein by plasma ChIP-Seq has not been reported.

The DNA binding domain of CTCF is an 11 zinc finger protein (ZFP) and ZFP-DNA binding requires zinc ions. Although zinc finger TFs bind strongly to zinc ions with dissociation constants (kd) in the range of 10−9–10−11 M, EDTA chelates zinc ions some 10,000 times more tightly (kd 10−16). Exposure of zinc finger TFs to low 0.1-5 mmol/L levels of EDTA, even for a few minutes, may result in significant stripping of zinc ions from ZFPs, disruption of ZFP-DNA binding and dissociation of TF-DNA [24, 25]. We hypothesized that cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins might be labile to disruption in plasma which typically contains 2-5 mmol/L EDTA.

TF-DNA ChIP-Seq analysis of cell or tissue sample chromatin typically involves formaldehyde crosslinking to stabilize nucleoprotein structures [26]. Although typical crosslinking protocols involve exposure to formaldehyde for 10 min or more, studies of the reaction kinetics of formaldehyde crosslinking of nucleoproteins report that crosslinking is rapid and complete within 5 s. The remaining time reflects the TF kon rate and allows for increased binding of low affinity TFs to bind to low occupancy TFBS [27]. As the time required for crosslinking is short (5 s) in relation to high affinity CTCF residence times (up to 22 h), we hypothesized that rapid crosslinking in whole blood might stabilize cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins to the ZFP zinc finger depletion effects of EDTA, rendering them amenable to analysis by plasma ChIP-Seq.

We now describe direct isolation and analysis of plasma cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins representing high affinity CTCF BS sequences in crosslinked plasma samples from healthy subjects and cancer patients to identify individual CTCF loci sequences selectively present as cfCTCF-DNA in the plasma of cancer, but absent from the plasma of healthy subjects, as potential liquid biopsy oncology biomarkers.

Methods

Crosslinking of cfCTCF-DNA in whole blood

Covalent crosslinking of cf-cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in whole blood was performed by collection of samples in Streck Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, La Vista, NE, USA) containing a formaldehyde-releasing agent [28, 29].

Patient samples

Except where otherwise specified, all plasma samples were collected in Streck Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes. Samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Separated Streck plasma was stored frozen in cryotubes at -80⁰C until assayed.

Three independent patient sample cohorts were collected. The first cohort was a developmental cohort that included 70 samples collected from 49 treatment naïve patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer (CRC) (n = 10), lung cancer (n = 10), breast cancer (n = 10), prostate cancer (n = 10), liver cancer (n = 5) or cancer of the bile duct (n = 4) purchased from Indivumed (Indivumed GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Samples collected from 11 healthy donors and 10 patients diagnosed with an inflammatory disorder were purchased from BioIVT (BioIVT LLC Westbury, NY, USA). These 70 samples were analyzed for CTCF protected cfDNA by ChIP-seq using a sample volume of 1 mL.

A second sample cohort including 4 samples from CRC patients was purchased from BioIVT. These samples were use to determine the enrichment and recovery of CTCF protected cfDNA from 1 mL plasma in ChIP-seq experiments.

A third independent validation cohort purchased from Indivumed and BioIVT included 24 samples collected from 10 healthy volunteers, 8 patients diagnosed with CRC and 6 patients diagnosed with lung cancer. These 24 samples were analyzed for CTCF protected cfDNA by ChIP-seq using a sample volume of 3 mL.

Inclusion criteria for healthy subject samples were age ≥ 18 and self-declared as not having any known disease diagnosis. Exclusion criteria for healthy subjects were age < 18. Inclusion criteria for cancer patient samples were age ≥ 18 with a confirmed cancer diagnosis. Exclusion criteria for cancer patient samples were age < 18. Inclusion criteria for inflammatory patient samples were age ≥ 18 with a diagnosis of a chronic inflammatory condition not currently in a flare. Exclusion criteria for inflammatory patient samples were age < 18, current hospitalization or current disease flare.

Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study and all experiments and methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for all human experimental investigations. All blood specimens were purchased from biobanks where the blood samples are ethically collected with IRB-approved protocols.

Patient sample cohorts

Native and crosslinked MCF7 cell chromatin digest materials

MCF7 breast cancer cells (ATCC) were cultured in MEM medium (Gibco, 31095–029) supplemented with 10% FBS Premium Plus (Gibco, A4766801), NEAA (Gibco, 11140–050), Penicillin–Streptomycin (Gibco, 15070063), and L-Glutamine (Gibco, 25030081). Cells were either used native or crosslinked by fixing for 10 min at room temperature in MEM containing 1% paraformaldehyde before quenching with 125 mM glycine.

Crosslinked or native cell pellets were washed with cold PBS and maintained on ice. Cells were lysed in buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 0.05% NP-40 with protease inhibitors) for 20 min on ice. Nuclei were collected by centrifugation, resuspended, and incubated in low-salt buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 0.2 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1% Triton X-100 with protease inhibitors) for 15 min on ice. Chromatin pellets were obtained by centrifugation and digested with MNase (0.1 U/µl) for 15 min with agitation. Digestion was stopped by addition of 500 mM EDTA pH 8, followed by 5 min incubation on ice. The supernatant containing fragmented crosslinked or native chromatin was collected after centrifugation.

Nucleosome measurements

Nucleosome measurements were carried out using the automated Nu.Q® H3.1 chemiluminescent immunoassay for nucleosomes containing the histone isoform H3.1 (H3.1-nucleosomes) in 50 µL of plasma as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Belgian Volition SRL, Isnes, Belgium).

Magnetic anti-CTCF-DNA reagent

Anti-CTCF antibody was coated onto magnetic beads (MyOne TosylActivated Dynabeads™) using standard methods. Briefly, antibody was incubated with magnetic beads for 18 h at 37 °C in a rolling bottle to maintain suspension of the beads. The beads were sedimented and resuspended in a blocking buffer comprising phosphate buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at 37 °C. The beads were then sedimented again, washed twice and stored in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, 1% BSA, and a preservative. Magnetic beads coated with anti-nucleosome antibody (Belgian Volition SRL, Isnes, Belgium) or magnetic beads coated with non-specific mouse IgG (Meridian Life biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) were used as a comparative or control reagent respectively.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) of plasma cfCTCF-DNA

Plasma samples were diluted in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer and anti-CTCF coated magnetic particles were added (30 µg particles/mL plasma). The mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature with rolling to maintain suspension of the beads. The beads were then sedimented and washed five times (Supplementary Fig. 1). Anti-nucleosome ChIP was performed in a similar manner using magnetic particles coated with anti-nucleosome antibody.

Western blot

Magnetic anti-CTCF reagent particles were exposed to 1.6 mL plasma suspended in a denaturing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer. The denatured protein was analyzed by western blot using anti-CTCF antibody and a labeled anti-IgG detection antibody (Abcam, VeriBlot (HRP) ab131366).

DNA sequencing

CTCF associated cfDNA isolated by ChIP from 1 ml Streck plasma was extracted from magnetic beads using a QIAamp DSP Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted cfDNA was amplified to produce a single strand library for sequencing using a SRSLY NGS Library Prep Kit (Claret Bioscience, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced at a coverage of approximately 30X using an Illumina NovaSeq Sequencing System.

The 70 developmental plasma samples were analyzed to identify cfCTCF-DNA in a single batch. The 4 CRC patient samples analyzed for CTCF sequence panel validation and refinement were sequenced in a second single batch. The 24 validation cohort samples were sequenced in a third single batch.

Processing of sequencing reads and fragment size selection

Sequenced reads were processed using the Nextflow [30] nf-core [31] ATAC-seq pipeline v2.1.2 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.2634132), including adapter trimming (Trim Galore v.0.6.7, using 20 bp trimming to accommodate NovaSeq chemistry), alignment (BWA-MEM v.0.7.17-r1188) [32], blacklist filtering (https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist/blob/master/lists/hg38-blacklist.v2.bed.gz), and low-quality read filtering (to filter out duplicate reads, multi-mapped reads and split (secondary and supplementary) reads, and reads that contained more than 4 mismatches or were improperly paired). Quality metrics on the sequencing reads were monitored using MultiQC [33]. Fragment size frequency diagrams were produced for filtered sequences using the CollectInsertSizeMetrics tool from Picard (v3.0.0), and plotted using Multi-QC. All sequence alignments and subsequent analyses used human genome assembly GRCh38.

The resulting alignment BAM files were used to create subsets of different fragment sizes (for example; 35‒80 bp, 135‒155 bp and 156‒180 bp, inclusive of the boundaries) using the Template Length (TLEN) of the Sequence Alignment/Map with SAMtools [34]. Any non-aligned reads were discarded, as were those with mapping quality below 30 (MAPQ -q 30) or if the reads failed one of the mapping criteria using the bitwise flags in SAM column 2 (-F 3852 = 4 + 8 + 256 + 512 + 1024 + 2048).

Read coverage (the number of fragments found to cover a specific gene locus) was calculated using a bin size of 1 bp. Read coverage was normalized to the total number of reads mapped to the human genome with the reads per genome coverage (RPGC) using the deepTools bamCoverage. Coverage profile plots were generated for each fragment size using deepTools plotProfile [35].

CTCF ChIP-Seq peak calling

ChIP-Seq peak calling was performed on BAM files of the 35-80 bp fragments using MACS3 (v3.03) narrow peaks [36] (the parameters used were -t, -g 2.7e + 9, -m 5 50, -f BAMPE). The peak files were used to detect transcription factor binding sites using the consensus probability matrix for CTCF described in the HOMER database [37, 38]. No correction for background binding to non-specific IgG was used in peak calling (parameter -c). Peaks were picked using False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjusted p-values between ≤ 0.05 to ≤ 0.00001 [39].

CTCF binding affinity

CTCF binds to a consensus 20 base DNA binding motif and the affinity of binding at particular loci can be estimated by its similarity to the consensus motif [4, 21, 37, 38]. CTCF binding site loci identified by plasma ChIP-seq were interrogated for a parameter of binding affinity based on a moving score for the conformity of the peak location sequence with the position probability matrix (PPM) reported for the 20 base consensus binding motif [4]. Starting at a genomic location (n) at the first base occurring at each end of the peak cfDNA fragment sequences observed, the affinity of the location sequence for CTCF binding was calculated as the sum of a score awarded to each of the 20 bases starting at that position. The score awarded corresponded to the probability for that base reported for the probability consensus sequence (e.g. if the location sequence included an adenine at a certain location where the probability for an A in the probability consensus sequence was 0.7, then the peak location sequence was awarded a score of 0.7 for that base). The parameter used for CTCF location and binding affinity was the sum of the scores for each of the 20 nucleotide bases. The process was repeated starting at genomic location (n + 1), (n + 2), (n + 3) etc. to cover the broader cfDNA fragment location sequence, and the highest score achieved in either direction was used to identify the CTCF binding motif location and its CTCF binding affinity. An average score of ≥ 0.5 for each base, or a total score of ≥ 10 for the 20 base pairs in this motif, was considered high affinity.

cfCTCF-DNA ChIP-seq differential peak analysis

We identified CTCF ChIP-seq peaks present in plasma samples but not in cancer patients and healthy subjects, using increasingly strict FDR p-value cutoffs for CTCF protected cfDNA fragments in the size range 35–80 bp that included a CTCF binding site sequence [39]. cfDNA sequences representing CTCF binding sites represented as short cfDNA in cancer samples, but not in healthy or inflammatory samples, were identified using the DiffBind package from BioConductor running on R using DESeq2 in the normalization step [31–33, 40, 41]. FRiP scores (Fraction of Reads in Peaks) were calculated for all samples according to the ENCODE guidelines [42].

UniBind analysis of peaks from validation sample cohort

We constructed a reusable autosomal CTCF reference from UniBind [43] by downloading the Homo sapiens TFBS archive, extracting 556 CTCF ChIP-seq sample files, and merging 561,898 unique strand-specific CTCF motifs contained in one or more of those samples (after excluding chrX and chrY). Motifs were grouped using a permissive ± 3 bp overlap rule, with final coordinates defined by the union of original intervals within each group and the motif score set to the maximum Position Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM) score, yielding 540,924 merged autosomal UniBind CTCF sites. We then selected the subset that overlapped MACS3 peak intervals from any validation set cfChIP-seq sample (q-value cutoff 0.05) and annotated each peak with its maximum overlapping MACS3 -log10(qvalue). In addition to these 10,753 UniBind-derived peaks regions, we merged all remaining peaks using the same ± 3 bp rule to result in 1,784 non-UniBind peaks. The final 12,537 peak regions were used for all downstream analysis. As an independent control, we randomly selected a set of 12,537 UniBind CTCF motif regions without respect to peak overlap.

For rank-based cfChIP-seq genomic analysis, fragment counts were obtained from indexed BAM files (including only reads with mapping quality at least 30 and TLEN fragment length 35–80 bp). Within each sample, counts were converted to ranks and visualized as rank-based heatmaps. To derive sample-level summaries for fractions overlapping ubiquitous CTCF sites and fractions overlapping RepeatMasker, we selected the top 1,000 ranked sites per sample and calculated fractions overlapping from these sites only. Ubiquitous CTCF sites were defined as those overlapped in at least 500 UniBind CTCF ChIP-seq samples. RepeatMasker was obtained from LOLA [44].

Results

Stability of exogenous MCF7 CTCF-cfDNA spiked into EDTA plasma

To determine the stability of native and formaldehyde cross-linked CTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in EDTA plasma, we spiked native and crosslinked MCF7 cell chromatin digests into healthy native EDTA plasma containing no formaldehyde releasing agent. CTCF-DNA nucleoproteins were isolated from the spiked plasmas by ChIP and analyzed for the fragment size profile of the isolated cfDNA sequence data. We observed a large peak in the ultrashort 35–80 bp size range reported for CTCF-associated DNA fragments [14] in the ChIP isolate of cross-linked chromatin digest spiked plasma, but no peak for native chromatin (Fig. 1a). Characterization of endogenous plasma protein isolated by CTCF ChIP

CTCF ChIP was performed on 1 mL Streck plasma samples collected from four colorectal cancer patients. The ChIP isolates were analyzed for the presence of CTCF protein by western blot. A band at approximately 140 kD, characteristic of CTCF protein [45], was present in the isolates of all four samples using specific anti-CTCF antibody coated magnetic beads (Fig. 1b). We concluded that the CTCF ChIP isolates derived from the 4 CRC samples contained CTCF.

Characterization of endogenous plasma cfDNA isolated by CTCF ChIP

CTCF ChIP isolated cfDNA would be expected to include ultrashort 35–80 bp fragments that include a CTCF binding motif and flanking sequences but not include nucleosome size cfDNA fragments including the same sequences (as nucleosomes are removed by ChIP). We therefore investigated the size range of cfDNA fragments present in cfDNA extracted from 4 CRC whole plasma samples (1 mL) as well as from CTCF ChIP isolates and nucleosome ChIP isolates from the same samples. The cfDNA fragment size profile of the whole plasma samples had a peak at approximately 170 bp characteristic of nucleosome associated cfDNA as well as a shoulder of smaller fragments of less than 100 bp in length (Fig. 2a). In order to determine whether the shorter cfDNA fragments present were of non-nucleosomal origin or were nucleosome derived from nicked nucleosomes containing one or more single or double stranded breaks, we investigated the cfDNA profile on nucleosome ChIP isolates from the same 4 samples using an anti-nucleosome antibody. The observed cfDNA size profiles observed for all 4 nucleosome ChIP isolates were similar to the whole plasma size profiles indicating that the ultrashort cfDNA was in each case comprised primarily of nucleosome associated cfDNA (Fig. 2a). The whole plasma cfDNA sequencing data was then analyzed for cfDNA fragments that included one of 9,780 previously published ubiquitous CTCF binding sites [46, 47]. The results for whole plasma sample cfDNA included 2 peaks: one peak at approximately 170 bp corresponding to nucleosome protected cfDNA and another peak at approximately 70 bp (Fig. 2b). V-plots of the sequence data showed that the CTCF associated cfDNA comprised predominantly fragments in the ultrashort 60-80 bp range and that the CTCF binding motif was non-randomly located approximately centrally within the ultrashort cfDNA fragments (Fig. 3c) [49]. In contrast, V-plots of the 9,780 CTCF binding site sequences isolated by ChIP using an anti-nucleosome antibody were 160-170 bp in length and the CTCF binding motif was randomly positioned within the fragments (Fig. 3d). Endogenous cfDNA fragments isolated by CTCF ChIP from plasma were: (1) bound by anti-CTCF coated magnetic beads but not bound by non-specific IgG coated beads, (2) were of the sub-nucleosomal size range expected for TF associated DNA, (3) were enriched for published genomic CTCF binding site sequences; and (4) included a centrally located CTCF binding motif. We concluded that CTCF ChIP isolates from Streck plasma samples were enriched for endogenous crosslinked cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins.

Enrichment of plasma cfCTCF-DNA by ChIP

As the vast majority of cfDNA is nucleosome-bound, the ChIP isolates retained only a small proportion of the total cfDNA present in the whole sample, but containing a higher proportion of CTCF-bound cfDNA subtypes. Quantitative investigation of plasma cfCTCF-DNA ChIP-seq peaks

We concluded that the presence of high affinity ChIP-seq peaks in the plasma of cancer patients, but not healthy subjects, is therefore likely to be related more to their absence from healthy plasma, rather than cancer specific occupation of binding sites only in cancer cells. In contrast, CTCF binding site loci identified in samples from healthy subjects had little overlap with ubiquitous UniBind sites and intersected predominantly with sequences included in the ENCODE blacklist and other repetitive elements that are likely to be poorly mappable (Fig. 5c–e) [50]. These findings are congruent with the finding that ChIP-seq peaks in healthy subjects are removed by filtering for binding strength score and by the use of stringent FDR adjusted p-values for their calculation.

Discussion

Immunochemical partitioning of plasma nucleoproteins to enrich circulating TF protected cfDNA has not previously been reported. For the first time, we isolated and analyzed endogenous plasma cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins by ChIP-seq. Although only a small fraction of total cfDNA is CTCF associated, we were able to isolate plasma cfCTCF-DNA by ChIP with a mean enrichment of 180-fold (18,000%) and a mean recovery of 48% of CTCF bound cfDNA fragments. Background cfDNA, primarily in the form of nucleosomes, was almost completely removed (> 99.7%).

Surprisingly, CTCF ChIP-seq studies demonstrated a complete, or near complete, absence of high affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in the plasma of healthy subjects, indicating that the thousands of distinct high affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins present in the plasma of cancer patients are tumor derived. This, in turn, indicates that high affinity CTCF ChIP-seq results obtained for cancer patients, represent pure ctDNA sequence data sets. The pure ctDNA data sets include only a minority of, not all, ctDNA fragment sequences present in the plasma, but contain no sequence data related to cfDNA fragments originating from healthy cells.

Plasma CTCF ChIP-seq therefore opens the possibility of a 2-step method for preparing pure ctDNA data sets for cancer patients. In the first step, cfCTCF-DNA is physically isolated in vitro with removal of > 99% of background non-tumor derived cfDNA. In the second step, remaining background cfDNA, including remaining nucleosome protected cfDNA and low affinity CTCF protected cfDNA, is removed in silico by bioinformatics to produce a data set of CTCF ChIP-seq sequences representing pure ctDNA fragments in the plasma sample.

To our knowledge, the observation of an absence of high affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in the plasma of healthy subjects is a novel finding for which the underlying biology is unknown. However, all cells are capable of depletion of CTCF bound to high affinity binding sites associated with the 10,000–20,000 TADs in the nucleus as this occurs during mitosis [51]. Possible speculations regarding the underlying biology include that circulating nucleoproteins in healthy subjects may derive primarily from neutrophils or other leukocytes by NETosis whereby unwound chromatin is released from the cell into the circulation and that unwinding of TADs may similarly be associated with this process.

The aggregate presence of cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins in plasma has been demonstrated previously through direct DNA footprinting of ultrashort CTCF bound cfDNA fragments and indirectly by footprinting of nucleosome bound cfDNA. However, detailed analysis of the occupancy of individual CTCF binding sites by footprinting of whole genome sequencing data of whole plasma sample cfDNA extracts has not been reported.

We investigated the proportion of sub-100bp cfDNA that is nucleosome bound by comparing the size frequency profile of cfDNA extracted from whole plasma with cfDNA isolated from plasma by binding to an anti-nucleosome antibody. The results in Fig. 2a demonstrate total cfDNA includes a substantial proportion of ultrashort sub-100bp cfDNA and that the vast majority of this ultrashort cfDNA is derived from nicked nucleosomes. The frequency of nicked nucleosome derived cfDNA fragments carrying CTCF binding motif sequences will likely exceed the frequency of low abundance true CTCF protected ultrashort cfDNA fragments confounding the analysis of whole plasma sequencing data for individual CTCF binding site occupation. ChIP-seq analysis removes this interference and facilitates detailed occupancy analysis.

ChIP-seq analysis of a plasma nucleoprotein by ChIP is dependent on a number of critical factors, including the amount and stability of nucleoprotein available in plasma for analysis and the sensitivity of DNA library preparation methods used for ultrashort cfDNA fragments. Plasma cell free ChIP-seq of cell free nucleosomes provides low numbers of ChIP-seq reads, for example the 4.5 million average reads reported for ChIP-seq of plasma nucleosomes carrying the histone modification H3K4Me3 [52]. The number of aligned ChIP-seq reads observed here for plasma cfCTCF-DNA of 877,664 was lower still. This likely relates to the 40,000-60,000 CTCF protected loci in the genome compared to the approximately 20 million nucleosomes present in each human cell of which 1-2% are modified by H3K4 trimethylation, as well as the greater stability of nucleosomes compared to low affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins. Critical elements to enable ChIP-seq analysis of cfCTCF-DNA were formaldehyde crosslinking of plasma to protect cfCTCF-DNA from the destabilizing effects of EDTA zinc ion depletion on ZFP DBDs, and the use of single strand DNA library preparation to maximize sensitivity for ultrashort sub-100 bp CTCF associated cfDNA fragments.

Histone octamers bind to DNA with no sequence specificity to form near meta-stable nucleosome complexes. In contrast, TFs bind to DNA in a sequence dependent manner with highly variable affinities and dissociation rates to form TF-DNA complexes with residence times varying from seconds, for dynamic TFs, to hours for stable TFs [22, 27, 53]. CTCF has at least tens of thousands of binding sites in the human genome bearing a wide range of motif sequences with varying DNA binding affinities according to their concordance with the consensus DNA-binding motif. High affinity motifs bear sequences close to the consensus sequence and are reported to be associated with topologically associated domain loops (TADs) and the control of chromatin architecture [54]. CTCF-DNA nucleoproteins including high affinity motif sequences have low dissociation rates with long residence times of up to 22 hours but are a minority of binding sites [21, 22]. Lower affinity motifs are less similar to the consensus sequence and are associated with transcriptional regulation. Lower affinity CTCF-DNA nucleoproteins have higher dissociation rates, shorter residence times and make up a majority of CTCF binding sites. Furthermore, CTCF binding affinity for a motif is not fixed but is reduced by methylation of cytosine bases within the CTCF motif and modulated by binding with transcription co-factors as well as by interaction with RNA molecules [55]. CTCF binding to cohesin is associated with TAD formation and insulator functions and stabilizes CTCF-DNA binding leading to increased residence times [3, 55].

CTCF binding is dysregulated in cancer and plays key roles in oncogenesis and tumor development through a variety of mechanisms [1–13]. Gain of CTCF binding may be induced by oncogenic transcription factors such as NOTCH1 in acute lymphoblastic leukemia [6]. In breast cancer cell lines CTCF binding may influence estrogen receptor (ER) binding to chromatin thereby modulating ER mediated altered gene expression [7]. CTCF Hi-C studies reported more than 500 cancer specific CTCF loci in triple-negative breast cancer cells that were undetectable, or barely detectable, in normal tissues [8]. CTCF is also involved in dysregulation of MYC oncogene expression in a variety of cancers including in prostate cancer, where MYC binds with CTCF at MYC/CTCF common binding regions, resulting in stabilization of CTCF-DNA at these regions and three-dimensional re-organization of chromatin [56, 57]. However, high affinity plasma CTCF ChIP-seq peaks observed here did not overlap published cancer specific sites.

Similarly, ChIP-seq studies of fragmented cellular chromatin report both CTCF sites that are cell specific in terms of occupation as well as others that are ubiquitously occupied in many cell types [1, 2, 17–20]. The majority of plasma ChIP-seq peaks identified here overlapped with peaks observed in many cell types in ENCODE data sets. Moreover, the number of patient samples studied was too small to investigate cell specific CTCF binding patterns. However, future studies involving larger patient cohorts may establish plasma CTCF binding patterns that are characteristic of the cell of origin of cfCTCF-DNA.

We characterized a plasma cfDNA fragment as CTCF bound if it was bound by an anti-CTCF antibody, was sub-nucleosomal in size and included an approximately centrally located CTCF binding motif sequence with upstream and downstream flanking sequences that identified a mappable CTCF binding locus in the human genome. Using a first developmental cohort of patient samples, we selected an example panel of 14 high affinity CTCF loci sequences at a false discovery rate adjusted p ≤ 0.01 that, in combination, gave rise to at least one ChIP-seq peak in all 49 cancer plasma samples tested, but no peaks for 21 samples from healthy subjects or those with an inflammatory condition. Testing of the panel for the 14 ChIP-seq peaks in a subsequent, blinded validation sample cohort produced a negative result for all healthy samples tested, and a positive result in 13 of 14 samples from stage III/IV cancer patients. We here tested a minimal panel for liquid biopsy whose 14 members were selected purely empirically as giving a positive result in the samples tested in the development cohort with no false negative results. However, we subsequently observed that CTCF ChIP-seq peaks for thousands of high affinity CTCF binding loci represent tumor derived ctDNA and are not present for healthy samples. The number of possible panels is therefore very large and analysis of any panel selected by whole genome sequencing or targeted gene sequencing, or analysis of all loci, would be possible.

Whilst the small sample cohort sizes tested to date are insufficient to make a determination of sensitivity and specificity for cancer, it is clear that high affinity cfCTCF-DNA nucleoproteins may be useful novel biomarkers in oncology and hold promise for early cancer detection using either whole genome sequencing or targeted gene sequencing for panels of CTCF sequences in plasma ChIP isolates. Analysis of ctDNA bound at high affinity to CTCF in plasma has one great advantage for liquid biopsy in that a combination of chemical, in vitro, and bioinformatic, in silico, processes allow the removal of all background non-tumor cfDNA from analysis.

cfCTCF-DNA is not the only TF-DNA nucleoprotein that occurs in the circulation [14–16]. Indeed, the occupancy of more than 370 cancer cell derived TFs, known to have more than 10,000 BS in the human genome, have been inferred by nucleosome positioning studies of plasma cfDNA and a number of these have been directly footprinted in plasma [58, 59]. It is likely that at least some of these TFs are represented by high affinity cfTF-DNA nucleoproteins that may similarly be amenable to analysis by plasma ChIP-seq methods. For example, liquid biopsy analysis of androgen receptor or estrogen receptor binding to their respective response elements are likely to be informative in prostate and breast cancer respectively [60–62].

In conclusion, we report the first isolation and analysis of cfCTCF-DNA from plasma. We observed that cfCTCF-DNA in plasma samples from healthy subjects and cancer samples differs both qualitatively and quantitatively. Plasma CTCF ChIP-seq has enabled the first liquid biopsy analysis of CTCF binding site occupancy and identified a novel class of cfDNA biomarkers comprising ultrashort plasma cfCTCF-DNA loci sequences. The cfCTCF-DNA results obtained here identified 49 of 49 cancers tested in a developmental cohort, and 13 of 14 cancers in a validation sample cohort, with no false positive results. Although the sample numbers tested in this first proof-of-concept experiment were small and need to be confirmed in studies of larger independent clinical cohorts, the results show feasibility for a novel liquid biopsy approach involving analysis of ultrashort cfDNA fragments bound to CTCF at high affinity. These ultrashort plasma cfDNA fragments may be detected by PCR or targeted gene sequencing of plasma cfCTCF-DNA isolated by ChIP or by whole genome sequencing.

Limitations of the study

This is a first description of the isolation of plasma CTCF-cfDNA nucleoproteins. We characterize these nucleoproteins and demonstrate proof-of-concept for their potential use as a new class of cfDNA biomarkers. The patient sample results reported provide proof-of-concept for the use of plasma CTCF-cfDNA nucleoproteins as biomarkers, However, the patient cohorts studied to date were small, not age and sex matched and were of insufficient size to make a determination of sensitivity and specificity. It is also unlikely that the sequence panels selected based on these small cohorts were optimal. Further studies to refine biomarker panels and any potential clinical utility of the biomarkers will require further investigations in larger numbers of patients. We have not assessed any potential patient selection, monitoring, or prognostic associations.
