Supra-sellar granular cell tumor: Report of a case with literature review
Authors: Farah Sassi, Alia Zehani, Abdelhafidh Slimane, Imed Ben Said, Khedija Bellil, Slim Haouet
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceGranular cell tumor (GCT) originating from the sellar and suprasellar regions, specifically from the neurohypophysis, is a rare neoplasm. Distinguishing GCT from other pituitary tumors, including pituitary adenoma, pituicytoma, and spindle cell oncocytoma, poses significant challenges. Here, we present a rare case of GCT originating from the posterior pituitary in the supra-sellar region.Case presentationA 41-year-old woman, with no past medical history, presented to neurology department with decreased visual acuity and peripheral facial paralysis since 3 months. The MRI showed a well-defined supra-sellar, retrochiasmatic, oblong, hypothalamic expansive process. It was isointense T1-weighted, discretely hypotensive T2-weighted, measuring 19x17x16 mm, suggesting pituicytoma or craniopharyngioma. An endoscopic transsphenoidal surgical resection was performed. Microscopic examination showed a proliferation of diffuse architecture made up of rounded polyhedral cells with granular eosinophilic cytoplasm. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells expressed diffusely TTF1, S-100 protein and SOX-10 confirming the diagnosis of supra-sellar GCT.Discussion and conclusionGCTs are rare neoplasms that predominantly exhibit benign behavior, while the malignancy rate remains at 2 %. Histopathology serves as the definitive diagnostic approach for GCTs. These tumors are resistant to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, necessitating surgical resection as the primary treatment modality. Due to the potential absence of distinct tumor masses and local tissue infiltration by tumor cells, complete excision is crucial, with resection extent extending beyond areas of infiltration.

Introduction

Pituicytoma, granular cell tumor of the sellar region (GCT) and spindle cell oncocytoma form a distinct section in the 5th edition of World Health Organization (WHO) of central nervous system tumors [1]. These are low-grade, benign and thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF1)-positive neoplasms that arise from pituicytes of the posterior pituitary or infundibulum. Preoperative diagnosis of GCT can be challenging due to its similarity to other sellar lesions such as adenoma, meningioma, chordoma, and teratoma [2]. Surgical intervention is the primary approach for treatment; however, achieving complete tumor excision is frequently hindered by factors such as tumor size, close proximity to the optic chiasm, and high vascularity, leading to a significant recurrence rate [2]. In this report, we present a case of a 41-year-old woman with a GCT and provide a concise overview of the subject matter.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman presented to neurology department with decreased visual acuity and peripheral facial paralysis starting 3 months prior to initial presentation, prompting a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The patient had no significant past medical history, and no family history of neoplasm or neuro-logical disorder on investigation. The MRI showed an expansile well-defined oval process observed above the sella turcica, with isointense signals on both T1 and T2 imaging. It was isointense T1-weighted, discretely hypotensive T2-weighted, measuring 19x17x16 mm. It abutted the optic chiasm and posteriorly with the mammillary bodies, laterally displacing the optic bands. There was no signal abnormality or contrast enhancement of the chiasma or optic nerves. There was no calcification. The anterior pituitary was normal. These radiological findings debated the diagnoses of pituicytoma or craniopharyngioma. The patient underwent an endoscopic transsphenoidal surgical resection. Microscopic examination showed a proliferation of diffuse architecture made up of rounded polyhedral cells with granular eosinophilic cytoplasm. Nuclei were eccentric, small and regular, without cellular atypia or mitotic figures (Fig. 2). On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells expressed diffusely TTF1, S100 protein and SOX 10. They were negative for CD68, synaptophysin and chromogranin (Fig. 3). Taking all these findings into consideration, the final diagnosis was GCT of the sellar area WHO grade I. The post-operative course was uneventful. At 1-year of follow-up, the patient was clinically stable with a normal vision field.Fig. 2H&E (A × 40, B × 200, C × 400 magnification). Discussion

Reporting this case aims to enhance medical awareness and knowledge regarding this rare disease.

GCTs were initially described in 1893 by Boyce and Beadles [4]. They account for <0.5 % of all sellar tumors, with <70 cases of symptomatic GCTs been reported in the literature thus far. The majority of them are benign, with a low rate of malignant transformation estimated at 2 % [5].

Women are more commonly affected than men, with the peak incidence occurring in the fifth decade of life [6]. Symptoms associated with GCTs include gradual onset of visual disturbances in up to 90 % of cases, as well as headaches and endocrine changes such as amenorrhea, galactorrhea, infertility, impotence, and weight fluctuations in approximately 50 % of cases. Hypopituitarism or hyperprolactinemia are frequently observed, although there have been rare cases of acromegaly [7].

Radiological findings are not specific and may resemble other central nervous system lesions like meningioma, pituitary adenoma, chordoma, or teratoma [8,9]. MRI imaging of this tumor mass commonly demonstrates an isointense signal intensity compared to the surrounding tissues on T1-weighted images and a slightly hypointense signal on T2-weighted images. Additionally, a consistent and homogeneous enhancement pattern is observed after the administration of contrast agents [8]. In our case, the tumor was isointense T1-weighted, discretely hypotensive T2-weighted.

Diagnosis of GCTs before surgery is challenging. In the present case, preoperative diagnosis were pituicytoma or craniopharyngioma. The definitive diagnosis is typically established on histologic features through surgical excision [2].

Histopathological examination of GCTs reveals neoplastic cells that exhibit both spindle and polygonal shapes, with eosinophilic granular cytoplasm. These cells are diffusely positive for certain markers such as PAS, S100, CD68, vimentin, and TTF1, but negative for GFAP and pituitary hormones expression. These characteristics differentiate GCTs from other oncocytic tumors of the sellar region, such as pituicytoma and spindle cell oncocytoma [1]. In the present case, CD68 was negative. The tumor cells expressed TTF-1, S100 and SOX 10. In a recent case described by Vajtai et al. [10], they presented a spindle cell oncocytoma with unique features, including multiple follicle-like structures. Despite diverse architectural variations, all components in their case exhibited positivity for TTF-1. The ultrastructural examination revealed epithelial specialization in the follicle-like regions, characterized by apical tight junctions and microvilli formation. This peculiar finding was interpreted as a metaplastic transition to the follicular epithelium, suggesting a supportive connection to the folliculostellate cell-lineage of the tumor. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis of expression profiles in three sellar tumor types (spindle cell oncocytoma, pituicytoma, and GCT) revealed varying patterns of marker expression, providing valuable insights into their distinct characteristics. Furthermore, the discussion considers the ultrastructural variants of normal pituicytes and raises the possibility that certain regions might represent ependymal pituicytes or ependymal differentiation within an oncocytic pituicytoma. The article also explores the impact of anatomic location on TTF-1 expression, suggesting that folliculostellate cells may share a common origin with pituicytes but exhibit differential TTF-1 expression based on their migration and developmental context. This multifaceted exploration contributes to the evolving understanding of sellar tumors and underscores the complexity of their histopathological features [11].

Approaching the Ki-67 index requires careful consideration. Among a cohort, 27 GCTs (51 %) had reported Ki-67 index values. Notably, patients experiencing tumor regrowth post-surgery exhibited Ki-67 values below 3 %. Intriguingly, tumors with the highest Ki-67 values remained stable during follow-up. Consequently, it is premature to assert that the proliferation rate, as indicated by the Ki-67 value, should be deemed a predictive factor for GCT recurrence [9]. Ki67 was not perfomed in this case.

Genomic characterization of GCTs to date has not been well studied in the literature [12].

In this recent study published in [Journal Name], researchers delved into the genomic characterization of GCTs. They conducted whole-exome sequencing analysis, uncovering previously unreported deleterious mutations in crucial oncogenes, specifically SETD2 and PAX8. The identification of these mutations not only enhances our understanding of the molecular basis of GCTs but also opens up potential avenues for targeted therapies. The study suggests that immune checkpoint inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors, known to act on these pathways, could be explored as novel treatment options [12].

Surgical removal is the primary treatment for GCTs, and the choice of approach depends on the size and extension of the tumor. However, complete resection is often difficult due to the tumor's tough and highly vascular structure, as well as its tendency to adhere to nearby brain structures. Therefore, partial removal is more common, with preservation of adjacent brain structures prioritized. Complete resection may involve sacrifice or damage to the pituitary stalk and other structures, which can lead to endocrine complications such as diabetes insipidus and hypopituitarism. The use of radiotherapy as a primary or adjuvant treatment is controversial and not routinely recommended. Radiotherapy may be considered in cases with aggressive features, progressive disease, or inoperable recurrence [13]. Thus, the prognosis of GCTs largely depends on the extent of surgical removal.

Conclusion

GCTs are rare neoplasms predominantly exhibit benign behavior. Histopathology serves as the definitive diagnostic approach. These tumors are resistant to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, necessitating surgical resection as the primary treatment modality. Due to the potential absence of distinct tumor masses and local tissue infiltration by tumor cells, complete excision is the gold standard of treatment. Meticulous recognition of the clinical manifestations, MRI features, and comprehensive assessment of immunohistochemical analyses are imperative to achieve precise diagnosis and optimal treatment strategy for GCTs located in the sellar region.
