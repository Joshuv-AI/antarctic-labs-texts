Global Epidemiology of Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Olessia Zorina, Omid Beiki, Matthew Holt, Jocelyn Buisker, Philippe Ferber, Jilles M. Fermont, Richard J. Kelly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParoxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare acquired hematological disorder characterized by chronic intravascular hemolysis and an increased risk of thrombosis. A systematic synthesis of disease epidemiology is essential to understand disease patterns, improve timely diagnosis, and guide healthcare policies. This review aims to consolidate contemporary estimates of PNH incidence and prevalence globally and describe any observed trends in these epidemiological parameters.MethodsA systematic search of electronic databases was performed in November 2024 and identified 27 studies published between 2006 and 2024 following PRISMA guidelines. All population-based observational studies reporting incidence or prevalence measures of PNH were included, with no restrictions on age or geography; the synthesis of results focused on studies evaluated as medium or high-quality based on a structured assessment.ResultsThe annual incidence of patients diagnosed with PNH ranged from 0.17 to 0.35 per 100,000 population between 2002 and 2022 in the majority of high-quality studies. Regional variability emerged: studies from countries with high healthcare system quality scores consistently reported higher incidence estimates than those from countries with low scores. Yearly prevalence estimates in 2010–2021 ranged from 1.1 to 2.1 per 100,000 population, while longer-period prevalence (10–17 years) ranged from 1.5 to 6.4 per 100,000 population.DiscussionThe prevalence of diagnosed PNH has been steadily growing since 2006 across all regions, possibly reflecting improved survival rates following the introduction of effective therapies or improved diagnostic practices. Despite disruptions observed during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021), this upward trend is expected to continue as treatment access improves globally. The lower incidence reported in the regions with lower healthcare quality scores reflects potential underdiagnosis of PNH or unequal access to healthcare, highlighting the need for strengthening healthcare systems to ensure timely diagnosis and access to treatment of PNH.ConclusionsThis review provides updated global estimates of the incidence and prevalence of PNH, offers new insights into epidemiological trends, and highlights potential regional inequities in diagnosis and treatment. The results aim to support future research initiatives and inform healthcare strategies to improve outcomes for patients with PNH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00537-8.

Background

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH; ORPHA code: 447) is a rare hematological condition. It is an acquired rare illness, with chronic hemolysis and diverse manifestations such as thrombosis or renal impairment. PNH often occurs on a background of bone marrow failure, such as aplastic anemia [1]. The disease results from the clonal expansion of a mutated hematopoietic stem cell; these mutations lead to a deficiency of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored proteins, such as complement decay-accelerating factor (CD55) and membrane inhibitor of reactive lysis (CD59), which are both complement regulatory proteins. Loss of CD55 and CD59 renders PNH erythrocytes susceptible to intravascular hemolysis, leading to anemia and thrombosis which is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality [1, 2].

Flow cytometry (FC) to identify GPI-deficient blood cells is the accepted method to confirm the diagnosis [2]. PNH has been classified into three categories: classical PNH (clinical manifestations of hemolytic anemia without evidence of bone marrow failure); PNH in the context of another bone marrow disorder (such as aplastic anemia [AA] or myelodysplastic syndrome); and subclinical PNH, in which patients have small PNH clones without clinical or laboratory evidence of hemolysis or thrombosis [3].

PNH can occur at any age [1, 4], with the median age at diagnosis usually reported in the 30 s [5, 6]. Children are rarely affected, with pediatric cases accounting for 5 to 15% of all reported PNH diagnoses [7, 8]. Men and women are equally affected [2, 5, 6], although the proportion of women with PNH is reported to be lower in Asian countries (44.9%) than in Western countries (54.9%) [4]. This observation may be linked to the unequal access to medical care between men and women in some regions [4].

Review articles published between 2017 and 2021 estimated the global incidence of PNH to be approximately 0.1 to 0.2 per 100,000 population, with point prevalence ranging from 1 to 2 cases per 100,000 population [1, 2, 9]. However, these estimates were based on a few studies with limited geographical scope, mainly from the United States (US) and the United Kingdom (UK). Furthermore, some recent estimates were outside of this range: e.g., the US National Organization for Rare Disorders (NORD) Rare Disease Report published in 2024 quoted the prevalence estimates as being between 0.5 and 1.5 per million (i.e. 0.05 to 0.15 per 100,000) population [10]; the same estimate appeared also in a recent review of PNH treatment landscape [11].

Hence, this systematic literature review was conducted with the aim to identify and consolidate contemporary estimates of the PNH incidence and prevalence, and to describe any trends in the epidemiological measures reported globally.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy

The study design including the search strategy has been registered with the Research Registry of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses [12] under the unique identification number reviewregistry1919.

This systematic literature review aimed to include all population-based observational studies (both longitudinal cohort and cross-sectional designs; prospective and retrospective) that reported incidence or prevalence measures of PNH or provided sufficient information to calculate these from the available data.

There were no restrictions on the diagnostic method used to confirm PNH, population age or geography. Peer-reviewed journal articles, as well as conference abstracts were included.

The database search, conducted on 19 November 2024, included Embase, PubMed, Orphadata, and Incidence and Prevalence Database. Only primary research studies (published as journal articles or conference abstracts) in the English language were considered, and the search was restricted by publication date on or after 01 January 2013, based on the initial scoping review that indicated that very few relevant articles were published prior to that year. Search terms related to disease epidemiology were combined with terms related to PNH and study design (see Supplementary Methods 1). Where possible, filters were applied to exclude the following study types according to the exclusion criteria: interventional clinical trials, case series/case reports, in vitro studies, practice guidelines.

The supplementary searches were performed in the Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases and Intractable & Rare Disease Research journal archives intended to capture publications with the epidemiological measures of interest where PNH was not mentioned among the abstract or article keywords (see Supplementary Methods 1). Relevant citations in the articles selected for full-text review were manually reviewed (applying the same inclusion and exclusion criteria) to identify any articles published prior to 2013.

Study Selection

Two reviewers independently reviewed titles and abstracts of all records that met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Findings were documented for each individual record, along with recommendations for the selection of articles for full-text review. Reasons for exclusion were systematically recorded during this phase to ensure transparency and reproducibility; disagreements were resolved via discussion or in case of persistent disagreement, consultation with the third reviewer.

All articles selected for full-text review were then independently evaluated by the same two reviewers. A final selection was made by comparing and reconciling the two sets of recommendations to ensure consensus.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction was made by one researcher and independently verified by a second reviewer for all articles meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Items for standardized data extraction included reference information, study period, geographical coverage, design, characteristics of population covered, number of patients with PNH, method of PNH ascertainment (including data source, treatment setting, and diagnostic method), and the reported epidemiological estimates. When articles referred to an external report (e.g., of a disease registry), data from the most recent version of the report was extracted and referenced together with the original publication.

Incidence measures extracted included incidence rate, annual incidence or cumulative incidence over varying periods of time. If cumulative incidence was reported over time periods longer than 1 year, we recalculated this measure into average annual incidence to enable comparison across studies. In the rare disease setting, cumulative incidence over one year and incidence rate calculated using person-years or patient-years could be treated as approximately equal measures, so both were presented in the results.

Prevalence estimates included those measured at a given point in time (point prevalence) or during a defined time period (period prevalence). For the purposes of this review, we considered all point prevalence estimates, and period prevalence estimates with the underlying time period of 1 year or less to be approximately equal and do not distinguish between these two types below, referring to both as point prevalence.

The structured quality assessment of the included studies was made using the approach presented in the Joanna-Briggs Institute checklist for prevalence studies [13] and included an assessment of 1 or 0 points for each of the four key study design aspects (setting and the population coverage, sample size, method of PNH ascertainment and details of the study methodology; see Supplementary Methods 1). The final quality score, therefore, could range from 0 to 4. For the synthesis of the results, “high quality” studies indicate those with 3 or 4 points in the quality assessment score. Studies with 0 or 1 point were considered of low methodological quality and not used in the synthesis of the results, although presented for completeness.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

The reported epidemiological measures were recalculated to the common denominator (per 100,000 population or patient-years) to standardize presentation.

For studies that claimed to cover more than 90% of the population in the defined geographical area but did not supply the population estimates, calculation was undertaken as described in Supplementary Methods 1; specific calculation steps with the underlying data are presented in Supplementary Methods 2.

All included data were summarized in tables or graphs, grouped by the epidemiological measures in scope of the study. No meta-analysis was conducted considering the heterogeneous nature of the reported data. The results of the study selection, data extraction, analysis and synthesis are reported according to the recommendations in the PRISMA statement [14].

Most of the reported annual incidence estimates in Europe during the 2002–2022 period were between 0.17 and 0.35 per 100,000 population [15–17, 22, 29]. Only one good-quality study, by Hill et al. [25], reported annual incidence from an earlier time period (1991–2006), where it was 0.13 per 100,000 population.

The annual incidence reported by medium-to-good quality studies (score 2–4) outside of Europe ranged from 0.03 to 0.33 per 100,000 population [19, 21, 27]. The authors of the studies that reported lower rates (e.g., from Oman and Brazil) attributed these findings to potential limitations in population coverage or unequal access to healthcare [19, 27].

Six studies reported incidence estimates over an extended period of time [17, 18, 21, 26, 27, 37] and further two studies reported incidence of PNH from the same central laboratory measured at different time periods [15, 25].

Hansen and colleagues [37] reported a doubling of PNH incidence rate in Denmark between 1980 and 1993 and 2008–2016: from 0.04 (95%CI: 0.03–0.06) to 0.08 (95%CI: 0.06–0.11) per 100,000 patient-years. Results from the two Yorkshire studies show a similar trend: an increase from 0.13 per 100,000 population in 1991–2006 to 0.35 in 2004–2018 [15, 25].

The remaining five studies reported more contemporary time trends, which are plotted in Fig. 2. The PNH diagnosis rates remained relatively stable between 2006 and 2019, with the exception of the study by Kang and colleagues, in South Korea [32], which indicated a sharp rise in cases in 2015–2016 but did not extend beyond this time period. A drop in PNH incidence rates was registered in several countries in 2020–2021, the time period coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic.

Prevalence of PNH

Prevalence studies generally received lower quality scores, primarily due to limited use of FC-confirmed diagnoses, considered the gold standard in PNH ascertainment. Yearly prevalence estimates among medium-to-high quality studies ranged from 1.05 to 1.76 per 100,000 population in Europe and North America [26, 30] and from 0.61 to 2.06 in Asia during 2010–2021 [20, 23, 24] (Table 3). The studies based on rare disease registries were mainly of low quality and uniformly reported prevalence estimates at the lower end of the range: from 0.33 to 0.92 per 100,000 population in 2010–2024 [23, 24, 31–34, 41].

Discussion

This is the first study to systematically synthesize the primary scientific research reporting global incidence and prevalence of PNH. The only previous review that summarized all available epidemiological information for PNH was the article published by Gulbis et al., in 2010 [44]. At the time of review by Gulbis et al. only one primary research publication was available, by Hill and colleagues [25], and could provide only limited conclusions. In addition, PNH epidemiological estimates were provided in several disease review articles, which were based on a few studies with limited geographical scope.

The point prevalence estimates of PNH in this review (1.1 to 2.1 per 100,000 population, excluding rare disease registry reports [n = 6] and further low quality studies [n = 5]) align with those previously reported by Hill et al., Cançado et al., and Bektas et al. [1, 2, 9]. Notably, the 15-year prevalence estimates of 1.59 and 3.81 per 100,000 population reported by the two studies in Yorkshire, UK [15, 25] are commonly cited in literature as simply “PNH prevalence”, which can be misinterpreted as point prevalence without clarification.

Annual incidence estimates appeared slightly higher than previously reported (0.17 to 0.35 per 100,000 population in this study vs. 0.1 to 0.2 cited in the previous literature, e.g. Hill et al., Cançado et al. [1, 2]). At the same time, considerable variability was noted, especially in the low-quality studies. Among the many factors that may impact the reported PNH incidence and prevalence, the following were likely to play a role in the observed differences:

Calculation Methodology

Studies varied in their use of epidemiological terminology and calculation methods. Several studies [17, 27] used the term “prevalence” erroneously to describe cumulative incidence. Selection of denominators was often inadequately described to verify the validity of the reported estimates: e.g. it was not always clear whether the denominators included all population or adults only; in the study by Jalbert et al. [36] prevalence was within the range reported by other studies, but the incidence estimate was considerably higher, and the methods were not described in sufficient detail to clarify the reasons for this discrepancy. In addition, eight studies did not provide information on the number of PNH cases underlying their calculations, which reduced the quality score.

PNH Ascertainment

FC-confirmed diagnosis is considered the gold standard; this method (exclusively, or in case of older studies, in addition to previously used diagnostic criteria) was used in all studies based on laboratory data, most of which reported incidence measures. In contrast, most studies that reported prevalence measures relied on coded diagnoses in administrative or registry data. Although this approach often allows to cover larger reference population, compared to central laboratory testing data, it introduces the potential to include (or exclude) cases in which the diagnosis of PNH has been misclassified. Incidence estimates that used FC-confirmed PNH diagnoses were therefore deemed more reliable and demonstrated lower variability between high-quality studies. However, they used varying lower limits of quantification for PNH clone detection, which could also contribute to heterogeneity of the estimates and precluded a meta-analysis of the results.

Population Coverage

In the quality assessment, zero points were assigned in the “setting” domain for administrative databases or central laboratory-based settings that explicitly excluded a subset of patients (e.g. studies from Denmark that only included hospitalized patients [26, 28, 37], or indicated residual concerns about population coverage, e.g. as the study from Poland by Spychalska and colleagues [18], as well as all rare disease registries. The latter were expected to under-estimate the disease frequency due to “severity bias” [31] whereas mild forms of the disease would not be referred to specialized expert centers that usually report cases to the registries [31, 45], resulting in lower prevalence estimates, and this was confirmed in this review.

Regional Healthcare System Limitations

Unequal healthcare access was noted as potential limitation e.g. by Menosi Gualandro and colleagues and by Al-Riyami and colleagues in the respective studies conducted in Brazil and Oman [19, 27]. This may lead to underdiagnosis or incomplete patient inclusion even in studies that otherwise received medium or high quality scores. Notably, the Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) Index as estimated by the Global Burden of Disease study in 2020 was above 80 points on a scale from 0 to 100 for more than two thirds (13 out of 19) of countries in this review [46]. All medium-to-high quality studies in these countries reported PNH annual incidence between 0.17 and 0.35 per 100,000 population (using only the most recent Yorkshire estimate). The Danish studies in the hospitalized settings mentioned above reported lower estimates and could be considered an exception, yet the one study in Denmark based on central laboratory testing also found an annual incidence of 0.21 per 100,000 population [29].

According to the Global Burden of Disease 2020 estimates, three countries (Russian Federation, China and Poland) had HAQ Index between 70 and 75, and further three (Brazil, Bulgaria and Oman) below 70 points [46]. Of these, only the study from Bulgaria by Beleva and colleagues [17] provided an incidence estimate that fell within the range above. It is plausible that estimates from the countries with more developed healthcare systems (as measured by higher HAQ Index scores) may be closer to the true epidemiological measures due to higher rates of disease diagnosis. A recent literature review by Venugopal et al. (2024) has also concluded that the low-to middle income countries lack robust methods to provide adequate prevalence and incidence estimates of rare diseases: the identified cases are often limited to patients with a certain spending capacity [45]. Thus, very low reported incidence or prevalence data obtained from high-quality studies in some regions with low HAQ Index likely reflect an underdiagnosis of PNH rather than the true biological differences.

Regional or Ethnic Differences

Earlier literature, e.g. Gulbis et al. [44] indicated that PNH was expected to be more common in Asian countries, possibly due to a higher incidence of aplastic anemia. However, at the time no data was available to confirm this statement. This systematic review included several studies from Asia, but the only high-quality publications (excluding rare disease registries) were from Japan [20] and South Korea [21, 23, 24]. Both the incidence and prevalence estimates from those studies were at the top of the range of that reported in Europe, but they relied on administrative claims rather than validated diagnoses, raising concerns about misclassification. The ecological and observational nature of cross-regional comparisons does not allow drawing definite conclusions about the true differences in PNH incidence across these regions based on the currently available data.

Impact of Evolving Treatment Landscape and Disease Awareness

Awareness of PNH has increased significantly over the past three decades, likely contributing to more widespread testing. The first British Society for Haematology guideline on aplastic anemia (including recommendations for PNH testing) was published in 2003 [47], followed by the establishment of the UK National PNH Service in 2009. Together, these developments may help explain the observed two-fold increase in incidence and prevalence of PNH between the 1991–2006 and 2004–2018 time periods in the UK [15, 25].

Improved survival could contribute to an increased number of patients living with PNH, independent of the disease incidence. Before the introduction of specific therapies, PNH had a poor prognosis, with a median survival of 10 to 15 years [48–50]. The introduction of eculizumab in 2007, an anti-C5 antibody, improved the life expectancy of patients with PNH by significantly reducing the thromboembolic risk [51]. From 2018, PNH treatment options expanded, including alternative C5 inhibitors (ravulizumab, crovalimab, pozelimab), C3 inhibitors (pegcetacoplan), Factor D and Factor B inhibitors [11]; of these, danicopan and iptacopan were approved as recently as 2024.

As demonstrated by the studies that followed PNH trends over extended time periods, the reported incidence of PNH was higher in the last two decades compared to the period prior to 2007 (introduction of eculizumab). Due to the very small number of newly diagnosed PNH cases within the individual studies, year-to-year variations in the recorded disease incidence after 2007 (Fig. 2) may be due to chance. Although no clear incidence trends chaould be discerned in 2007–2020, the prevalence of PNH in several countries during this period demonstrated a steady increase (Fig. 3). The limitations of the studies reporting prevalence time trends include small number of cases (although larger than underlying the respective incidence estimates) and in some cases, short time series covered by the study periods. Nonetheless, this finding indicates that improved survival may have played a role in increasing the number of patients living with the disease. As more effective treatment options become accessible to the patients with PNH, the global PNH prevalence may be expected to further increase with time.

Impact of COVID-19 Pandemic

One possible exception in the observed incidence and prevalence trends is the time period coinciding with COVID-19 pandemic, where several studies demonstrated a drop in diagnosed PNH incidence (Fig. 2). In addition, although prevalence was increasing across all studies, the only recorded decrease in the number of patients with PNH was in Taiwan in 2021 (Fig. 3). This observation could potentially reflect the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, whether due to the decreased number of new diagnoses or the increasing number of deaths; the following years again saw an increase in the disease prevalence, however the study sample was small, which does not allow drawing definite conclusions.

Global disparities in healthcare access remain, but collaborative initiatives such as those between the not-for-profit organizations International PNH Interest group (IPIG), The Max Foundation and the pharmaceutical industry partners have contributed to improved diagnosis and access to treatment for PNH in regions where the condition has been underrecognized. These efforts currently reach patients in more than 50 developing countries, with plans for expansion.

The launch of the global, prospective, multi-center observational IPIG PNH Registry in 2024 (NCT06524726), marks a significant step towards collecting comprehensive data on patients with PNH and enhancing disease awareness and knowledge. This registry aims to support a better understanding of the patient population characteristics globally, enable the identification of trends, contribute to the refinement of treatment protocols, and inform the advocacy for improved and more equitable healthcare policies. By expanding medical training and supporting healthcare providers, these efforts ensure that patients globally can receive appropriate and timely diagnosis and care.

Strengths and Limitations

Our review includes a considerably larger number of primary research publications compared to previously published reviews of the epidemiology of PNH: 27 individual studies, compared to 1–4 in previous reviews, i.e. publications by Hill et al., Cançado et al., Bektas et al. and Gulbis et al. [1, 2, 9, 44]. This was possible because our review complemented search in literature and epidemiological databases with manual search in the rare disease journal archives to identify studies that did not include PNH-related terms in the abstract or keywords; and because most of the additional articles were recently published, between 2022 and 2024. We calculated the PNH epidemiological measures where possible based on the data reported in the articles to further enrich the available data and to mitigate incorrect usage of terminology, a limitation noted previously by other systematic reviews in rare diseases, e.g. by Leadley at al. [52]. At the same time, only English-language publications were considered in the search, which potentially limited the geographical scope of our findings.

The large number of identified publications enabled us to implement a structured quality assessment based on a published checklist for prevalence studies [13] to address the common limitations of epidemiological studies such as e.g. incomplete population coverage, and to limit the synthesis of the results to good quality studies only. Twelve of the 27 studies received low quality scores of 0 or 1 and were omitted in the synthesis of the results, although presented in the summary and in the time trends assessment for completeness. Only two studies received the maximum possible score of 4, indicating the relative lack of high-quality research in this field.

The main limitation of this review is the small number of PNH cases underlying most calculations, as is typical in the rare disease setting. We were therefore constrained in making robust conclusions from the available data, especially in the evaluation of time trends. Only one of the included studies reported 95% CI and for a consistent presentation we also did not include those intervals in the additional calculations. In view of heterogeneous definitions of PNH cases, meta-analysis of the results was not feasible even in the good-quality studies.

In addition to the known limitations of epidemiological studies based on existing registries and databases, studies in rare diseases have unique challenges that lie in the ability of the healthcare systems to properly diagnose a rare disease [45]. Indeed, even in countries with well-developed healthcare systems such as Switzerland it has been demonstrated that for rare diseases the diagnostic precision of inpatient cases was inversely related to travel time to the next tertiary facility (a proxy measure for health care accessibility) [53]. Thus, even in the publications of highest methodological quality there remains a possibility of under- or misdiagnosis related to the inherent limitations of the healthcare systems.

Conclusions

This systematic review incorporates broader, more recent and more refined data on the epidemiology of PNH, compared to previously published reviews. While global PNH prevalence estimates generally align with previous reports, the incidence estimates are reported to be slightly higher.

PNH prevalence has steadily increased in the last two decades, likely due to improved diagnostic efforts in some countries, but also possibly driven by improved survival rates following the introduction of effective therapies with complement inhibitors. Despite disruptions observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, this upward trend is expected to continue as diagnostic and treatment accessibility improves across different regions. At the same time, studies in some countries reported markedly lower disease frequency estimates, which underscores the remaining potential for improvement of the timely diagnosis and access to treatment of PNH in these healthcare systems.
