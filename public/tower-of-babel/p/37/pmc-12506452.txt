O-GlcNAc transferase promotes immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance in uterine corpus endometrial cancer by targeting the glucocorticoid receptor
Authors: Jingjing Wang, Yang Xie, Li Liu, Shuo Rong, Huanhuan Cai, Hao Zeng, Li Zhou, Keqiong Deng, Mengqiao Dai, Chao Xu, Ying Zhu, Zhibing Lu, Xuemin Song, Xiangtai Zeng, Shi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough some tumors respond to immune checkpoint blockade therapy, checkpoint inhibitors have been unsuccessful in treating uterine corpus endometrial cancer (UCEC), and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsWe investigated glucose flux regulation in UCEC cells with a focus on the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP). The role of O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) transferase (OGT) and its interaction with the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) were examined using in vitro and in vivo models. A competitive peptide was designed to disrupt the interaction between OGT and GR.ResultsWe found that UCEC cells direct glucose flux to the HBP. OGT, a critical enzyme for protein O-GlcNAcylation, increased programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression while decreasing major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression, thereby promoting immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy. Mechanistically, OGT interacted with GR, leading to O-GlcNAcylation of GR at serine 132, which required prior phosphorylation of GR. Disruptions of the OGT–GR interaction with the competitive peptide reduced GR O-GlcNAcylation, decreased PD-L1 expression, and increased MHC-I expression. This, in turn, activated CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity against tumor cells in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionsOur findings reveal cross-talk between the HBP, steroid hormone pathway, and tumor immune evasion, and suggest potential strategies for sensitizing UCEC to immunotherapy.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immune modulatory, T cell, Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte - TIL

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

The cross-talk between GR signaling and the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway in UCEC remains poorly defined. This study reveals a link between O-GlcNAc, steroid hormone pathways, and tumor immune evasion. OGT interacts with GR and induces O-GlcNAcylation of GR at serine-132. Consequently, GR translocates to the nucleus, activates programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression, and represses components of the major histocompatibility complex class-I pathway, leading to immunotherapy resistance.

Background

Uterine corpus carcinoma is among the most common tumors of the female reproductive system.1 Approximately 410,000 new female cancer cases are recorded annually, of which uterine corpus endometrial cancer (UCEC) accounts for over 90%.2 UCEC is treated with surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.3 4 Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, such as monoclonal antibodies targeting programmed death-1 and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1), improve survival in subsets of patients with renal cell cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and melanoma.5 6 However, many patients with UCEC are insensitive to immunotherapy. Previous studies have shown that the tumor microenvironment (TME)—comprising blood vessels, immune cells, fibroblasts, and extracellular matrix—primarily influences immunotherapy efficacy.7 8 The immunosuppressive TME often seen in UCEC is typified by the absence of functional cytotoxic T cells.9 10 Identifying tumor-intrinsic and/or tumor-extrinsic targets may facilitate the conversion of the immunologically “cold” UCEC TME into a “hot” state, thereby augmenting responsiveness to ICB therapy.

Glucose is a primary cellular energy source.11 12 After uptake via glucose transporter, it enters several metabolic pathways, including the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), glucuronate pathway, polyol pathway, and glycolysis.13,15 Compared with glycolysis and PPP, only 2%–5% of intracellular glucose is directed to the HBP, producing uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) as the final product.16 Protein O-GlcNAcylation is regulated by O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) transferase (OGT) and O-GlcNAcase (OGA).17 OGT transfers GlcNAc from UDP-GlcNAc to serine (S) or threonine (T) residues, whereas OGA catalyzes its removal.17 This dynamic post-translational modification affects protein function, and O-GlcNAcylation disorders impact cell physiology and contribute to the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cancer.18 OGT expression is elevated in colon and lung cancers relative to normal tissues.19 In breast cancer, OGT overexpression and O-GlcNAc modification correlate with histological grade.20 OGT-mediated O-glycosylation exerts immunomodulatory effects, regulating macrophage inflammatory and antiviral responses, enhancing activated neutrophil function, inhibiting natural killer cell activity, promoting innate immune activation, and exacerbating septic inflammation.21 In antitumor immunity, OGT proteins in exosomes from esophageal carcinoma stem cells protect tumors from CD8+ T cells via PD-L1 upregulation.22 However, whether OGT exerts immunosuppressive effects in the UCEC TME remains unknown.

Glucocorticoids are steroid hormones produced through the metabolic breakdown of cholesterol.23 On binding glucocorticoid, the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), encoded by NR3C1, translocates from its inactive cytoplasmic location to the nucleus.24 In the nucleus, GR can control gene expression directly (transactivation) or indirectly (transrepression) by interacting with promoter regions of target genes.25 Since the 1950s, glucocorticoids have been used to treat excessive inflammation in individuals with autoimmune disorders and asthma by suppressing several inflammatory pathways.26 Patients undergoing ICB cancer treatment often receive glucocorticoids to manage treatment-related inflammation.27

This study demonstrates that OGT redirects glucose metabolism toward the HBP in UCEC cells and associates with GR, inducing GR O-GlcNAcylation. OGT or GR depletion enhances cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and activity via transcriptional regulation of PD-L1 and major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), improving immunosurveillance and sensitizing UCEC to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Results

Glucose uptake contributes to high O-GlcNAcylation in UCEC

We performed bulk RNA sequencing of UCEC and paracancerous tissue to identify genes highly expressed in UCEC. Compared with paracancerous tissue, UCEC expressed higher messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of immune-suppressive genes and metabolic enzymes, including PD-L1, CD86, OGT, and G6PD (figure 1A). In contrast, UCEC showed lower mRNA expression of MHC-I pathway genes, such as human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C, as well as interferon genes, compared with paracancerous tissue (figure 1A). RNA-sequencing results were confirmed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) (online supplemental file 1A–1C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed that immune inhibitory, interferon response, glucose metabolism, and MHC-I pathways were the most altered in patients with UCEC (figure 1B). Subsequent analysis revealed that, compared with paracancerous tissue, OGT, GR, PD-L1, and HLA-A were the most highly regulated genes in UCEC (figure 1C), and we selected OGT for further study. Total protein O-GlcNAcylation was higher in UCEC than in paracancerous tissue, consistent with increased OGT expression (figure 1D). To investigate OGT’s role in immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance, we generated OGT knockout (OGT−/−) cells and confirmed OGT deficiency (online supplemental file 1D). 13C6-glucose tracing metabolomics was used to elucidate the mechanism by which OGT influences glucose metabolism reprogramming. We measured 13C6-glucose incorporation through glycolysis, the HBP, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (figure 1E). Consistent with the “Warburg effect,”28 RL952 wild-type (WT) cells showed reduced 13C6-glucose incorporation into citrate, succinate, and α-ketoglutarate in the TCA cycle, but increased incorporation into glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), fructose-6-phosphate (F6P), fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, 2,3-phosphoglycerate, pyruvate, lactate, and UDP-GlcNAc (figure 1F–1I), indicating flux toward glycolysis (from G6P to lactate) and the HBP (from F6P to UDP-GlcNAc). By contrast, glucose flux from the HBP back to the TCA cycle in OGT−/− cells suggests OGT mediates glucose flux to the HBP in UCEC (figure 1F–1I). Glucosamine (GlcN) activates the HBP, producing UDP-GlcNAc for glycan synthesis and protein O-GlcNAcylation.29 We tracked the transfer of 15N from [amide-15N] glutamine ([γ-15N] glutamine) to UDP-HexNAc to assess HBP flux (online supplemental file 1E). WT cells displayed higher HBP activity, whereas OGT ablation reduced ManNAc and Neu5Ac levels (online supplemental file 1F). In six UCEC cell lines (HESC, HEC1, Ishikawa, RL952, AN3-CA, MFE280), we examined the expression of OGT and total protein O-GlcNAcylation. As shown in figure 1J and K, compared with the control cell line (HESC), RL952 cells displayed the highest OGT expression and total protein O-GlcNAcylation. These results suggest glucose flux to HBP through OGT in UCEC.

OGT expression is correlated with immune infiltration and prognosis in UCEC

The role of OGT in UCEC samples was analyzed using publicly available datasets. OGT expression was higher in UCEC samples than in control samples in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (figure 2A). OGT expression also varied across UCEC historical subtypes, microsatellite instability statuses, and TCGA molecular classifications (figure 2B–2D). The genetic alteration rate of OGT was approximately 8%, closely related to tumor mutation burden and TCGA molecular subtype (online supplemental figures 2A and B). High OGT expression was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in UCEC samples (figure 2E and F). In contrast, OGT genetic alterations were not associated with OS or DFS (online supplemental figure 2C).

Given the regulation of OGT and tumor immune response in UCEC (figure 1A–1C), we investigated the relationship between OGT expression and immune infiltration. Consistent with “cold” tumor characteristics, UCEC samples had fewer CD8+ T cells, activated natural killer cells, activated mast cells, and activated dendritic cells than normal tissue, according to the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) (figure 2G). OGT expression negatively correlated with Act-CD8+T cells, Tcm-CD8+T cells, CD56 bright and CD56 dim natural killer cells, macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, Tcm-CD4+T cells, memory B cells, monocytes, natural killer cells, interstitial dendritic cells, and γδ T cells in the Tumor Immune System Interaction Database (TISIDB) (figure 2H and I). TISIDB also showed a significant correlation between OGT expression and UCEC outcomes, depending on lymphocytic infiltration (online supplemental figures 2 and E). In addition, OGA expression was lower in UCEC samples than in control samples (online supplemental figure 2F); however, OGA did not affect OS or DFS (online supplemental figure 2G). Using TIMER, OGT expression negatively correlated with multiple immune-infiltrating cells, including CD8+ T cells, B cells, and CD4+ T cells (figure 2J), and positively correlated with the immune checkpoint ligand PD-L1 (figure 2K). These results suggest that OGT regulates UCEC development via immune infiltration.

OGT regulates the PD-L1 and MHC-I pathways to promote immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance

To assess OGT’s role in tumor immunity, we first tested GlcN, OGT, and OGA effects on cell proliferation. Neither GlcN treatment nor OGT/OGA overexpression altered proliferation (online supplemental figures 3A–D). Two specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting OGT were evaluated (online supplemental figure 3E); siRNA-OGT #2 was used in subsequent experiments, and OGT knockdown did not affect proliferation (online supplemental figure 3F), suggesting a tumor-extrinsic effect on the immune system (figure 2). qPCR showed that GlcN increased PD-L1 mRNA while decreasing MHC-I pathway components, including HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) (figure 3A). However, GlcN did not affect the mRNA levels of PD-L1 or MHC-I pathway genes in OGT−/− cells (figure 3A). Similar results were observed in OGT knockdown RL952 and HEC1 cells (online supplemental figures 3G and H). Because OGT has been reported not to affect interferon regulatory factor-1 (IRF1) expression,30 IRF1 was included as a negative control for comparison (online supplemental figures 3G and H). OGT overexpression promoted GlcN-induced PD-L1 mRNA levels while decreasing GlcN-induced mRNA levels of MHC-I pathway components (online supplemental figure 3I). By contrast, OGA overexpression decreased GlcN-induced PD-L1 mRNA levels and increased mRNA levels of MHC-I pathway components (online supplemental figure 3I). Consistent with the mRNA results, GlcN elevated PD-L1 protein levels and decreased MHC-I and B2M protein levels in WT cells, but not in OGT−/− cells (figure 3B). Flow cytometry showed that GlcN treatment increased surface PD-L1 levels and decreased surface MHC-I protein levels in WT cells, but not OGT−/− cells (figure 3C and D). Next, we performed T cell-mediated tumor cell-killing assays in vitro using activated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells co-cultured with tumor cells. As shown in online supplemental figures 3J and K, GlcN treatment inhibited cell cytotoxicity in a time-dependent and dose-dependent manner (online supplemental figures 3J and K). OSMI-4, a widely used OGT inhibitor that blocks protein O-GlcNAcylation,31 eliminated the effects of GlcN on cytotoxicity when used alone or in combination with PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (PD-L1 mAb) (online supplemental figure 3L). GlcN-induced T-cell activation, assessed by interferon-γ production, was also inhibited by OSMI-4, consistent with previous findings32 (online supplemental figure 3M). Interestingly, T-cell activation was inhibited when co-cultured with tumor cells. GlcN had no effect on cytotoxicity in OGT knockdown cells (online supplemental figure 3N). OGT overexpression suppressed cytotoxicity, which was reversed by PD-L1 mAb (online supplemental figure 3O), whereas OGA overexpression abolished GlcN-induced cytotoxicity suppression (online supplemental figure 3P).

Based on the above cell studies, OGT inhibition could sensitize tumors more to anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in vivo. To test this, we used a mouse model bearing orthotopic UCEC tumors and treated it with the indicated pharmaceuticals, assessing tumor growth in the presence of an anti-PD-L1/CD8 antibody (figure 3E). As anticipated, either OSMI-4 treatment or anti-PD-L1 therapy reduced tumor growth (figure 3F and G). Combined PD-L1 mAb and OSMI-4 treatment considerably decreased tumor weight and volume without affecting body weight (figure 3F and G, and online supplemental figure 3Q). Analysis of infiltrating immune cells showed that OSMI-4 combined with anti-PD-L1 increased the percentage of CD8+ T cells and granzyme B+ tumor-infiltrating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) (figure 3H). Similarly, PD-L1 mAb and OGT knockout synergistically reduced tumor weight and volume without affecting body weight (figure 3I and J, and online supplemental figure 3R), and the combination increased CD8+ T-cell and granzyme B+ CTL percentages (figure 3K). Furthermore, antibody-mediated depletion of CD8+ T cells abolished OSMI-4-mediated tumor growth inhibition without affecting body weight, demonstrating that OGT inhibition in UCEC acts via CTLs (figure 3L and M, and online supplemental figure 3S). As expected, CD8+ T-cell depletion reduced the percentages of CD8+ T cells and granzyme B+ CTLs (figure 3N). These findings suggest that OGT promotes tumor growth by inducing PD-L1 expression and decreasing MHC-I pathway gene expression, thereby reducing CD8+ T-cell infiltration.

OGT associates with GR and promotes the O-GlcNAcylation of GR

To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which OGT potentiates tumorigenesis, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the 127 OGT-correlated genes was constructed by STRING (online supplemental figure 4A). Using the cytoHubba plug-in in Cytoscape 3.0, 10 hub genes in the PPI network were identified using nine algorithms (online supplemental figure 4A and online supplemental table 1). Among them, GR and FLT1 were present in the output of all nine algorithms (online supplemental figure 4A). The expression of FLT1 was lower in UCEC than in normal tissues and was not related to UCEC outcomes (online supplemental figures 4B and 4C). In contrast, GR expression was higher in UCEC tissues than in control tissues (online supplemental figure 4D), and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that, similar to OGT, GR had unfavorable prognostic effects (online supplemental figure 4E). A positive correlation between OGT and GR was observed in TCGA-UCEC (online supplemental figure 4F). Therefore, GR was identified as a hub gene of the PPI network and is closely associated with OGT.

We hypothesized that OGT directly interacts with GR to regulate tumor growth. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and transient transfection studies revealed interactions between Flag-tagged GR (Flag-GR) and Myc-tagged OGT (Myc-OGT) in 293T and RL952 cells (figure 4A and online supplemental figure 4G). Reverse Co-IP confirmed this interaction (figure 4A and online supplemental figure 4G). By contrast, FLT1 did not interact with OGT (figure 4B). In UCEC cells, O-GlcNAcylation of exogenously expressed GR was weakly detected but was enhanced on coexpression with OGT (figure 4C). Preincubation of the anti-O-GlcNAc antibody with 500 mM GlcNAc eliminated the O-GlcNAc signal on GR, indicating specificity (figure 4C). Conversely, OGA overexpression inhibited the O-GlcNAc signal on GR (online supplemental figure 4H). Endogenous Co-IP experiments showed that, in unstimulated cells, OGT weakly interacted with GR. On stimulation with GlcN and the pharmacological GR agonist dexamethasone (DEX),33 the interaction and GR O-Glc-NAcylation increased (figure 4D and E). In contrast, OGA/GR interaction was abolished by GlcN treatment (online supplemental figure 4I). Endogenous GR O-GlcNAcylation was notably elevated in WT cells after GlcN treatment but not in OGT−/− cells (figure 4F). OGT enzymatic activity was required for GR O-GlcNAcylation, as the OGT K908A mutant34 could not catalyze this process in 293T and RL952 cells (figure 4G and online supplemental figure 4J). Truncation mutants of GR were constructed to map the OGT-interacting domain (figure 4H, upper panel). These experiments showed that GR’s N-terminal domain (NTD) is required for interaction with OGT (figure 4H). Microscale thermophoresis (MST) indicated that GR-△NTD binding to OGT had a higher Kd than GR-WT, confirming the NTD as the binding region (figure 4I). Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis identified several O-GlcNAcylation sites in full-length GR and the NTD segment (figure 4J). Based on the unique binding of the GR peptide (SIANLNRSTSVPEN) to OGT, we investigated the structural basis of their interaction. We used a previously published, readily crystallizable OGT construct (PDB: 3PE4) encompassing the catalytic region. As shown in figure 4K, the peptide binds directly to the catalytic region of OGT, with S1, A3, N4, N6, and N14 forming hydrogen bonds with D431, H558, Q839, K898, A896, T921, and T922 of OGT, respectively (figure 4L). Among metazoans, GR S132 is highly conserved (figure 4M). Mutation analysis (S132A) abolished the O-GlcNAc signal without affecting GR protein levels in 293T cells, indicating that GR is O-GlcNAcylated at S132 (figure 4N). Similar results were obtained in RL952 cells (online supplemental figure 4K). These findings suggest that GlcN and DEX treatment promote OGT-GR interaction, leading to GR O-GlcNAcylation at S132.

Phosphorylation of GR promotes its O-GlcNAcylation

The N-terminal transactivation domain of GR contains phosphorylation sites.35 The JNK pathway phosphorylates S226, whereas CDK and p38 kinases target S211.35 Phosphorylation of the receptor regulates GR subcellular localization and transcriptional activity.35 First, we examined how OGT affected GR subcellular localization. As shown in figure 5A, GlcN treatment induced GR translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus in WT cells but not in OGT–/– cells. To investigate whether OGT regulates the transcription function of GR, we constructed glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) within the promoter regulatory regions of the human CD274 gene.36 The luciferase assay revealed that GlcN treatment enhanced the activity of GRE promoter reporters, which was abrogated by OGT knockout (figure 5B). Similarly, OGT knockdown inhibited GlcN-induced GRE promoter activity in HEC1 and Ishikawa cells (online supplemental figure 5A). OGT WT, but not the OGT K908A mutant, enhanced GR translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and increased luciferase activity (figure 5C and D). These effects were consistent across HEC1 and Ishikawa cells (online supplemental figure 5B). Next, we investigated the relationship between GR phosphorylation and O-GlcNAcylation. Neither GlcN treatment nor OGT expression affected GR phosphorylation (online supplemental figures 5C and D), whereas DEX treatment served as a positive control (online supplemental figures 5C and D). LC-MS/MS analysis confirmed that GlcN treatment did not alter GR phosphorylation at S211 or S226 (online supplemental figures 5E). Co-IP and reverse Co-IP showed that OGT did not interact with JNK (online supplemental figure 5F). Intriguingly, JNK activator (anisomycin, ANI) and CDK activator (SSK1) treatment induced GR O-GlcNAcylation (figure 5E). To determine whether JNK and p38 regulate GR O-GlcNAcylation, two siRNAs were employed for each kinase, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed (online supplemental figures 5G and H). SiRNA-JNK #2 and siRNA-p38 #2 were selected for subsequent experiments. Knockdown of either p38 or JNK abolished GR O-GlcNAcylation (figure 5F). Notably, phosphorylated GR at 211 and 226 is O-GlcNAcylated and interacts with OGT in DEX-treated WT cells but not in p38 or JNK knockdown cells (figure 5G). OGT did not induce O-GlcNAcylation of GR mutants (S132A, S211A, and S226A) (figure 5H). An MST assay revealed that GR-WT has higher affinity for OGT than the GR mutants (S132A, S211A, and S226A) (figure 5I). Accordingly, OGT did not induce nuclear translocation of the GR mutants (figure 5J). The luciferase assay revealed that GR mutants (S132A, S211A, and S226A) did not affect GRE promoter reporter activity in RL952, HEC1, and Ishikawa cells (figure 5K and online supplemental figure 5I). To determine whether GR participates in OGT-regulated signaling, GR knockout (GR−/−) cells were generated and deficiency was confirmed (online supplemental figure 5J). GR knockout did not affect HBP flux using [γ-15N] glutamine (online supplemental figure 5K). Comparison of the timeline of GR phosphorylation and GR O-GlcNAcylation showed that DEX induced phosphorylation of GR at 211 and 226 as early as 30 min, whereas GR O-GlcNAcylation was detected as early as 12 hours (figure 5L and M). These findings suggest that the newly synthesized GR is O-GlcNAcylated independently of prior phosphorylation, as phosphorylated proteins may be degraded within 12 hours. Pulse-chase analysis confirmed that GR half-life and phosphorylation were not degraded at 12 hours in response to GlcN treatment (online supplemental figures 5L and M). Collectively, these results indicate that GR phosphorylation is an upstream event in response to GlcN or DEX treatment, which subsequently promotes O-GlcNAcylation.

GR promotes tumor development by regulating the PD-L1 and MHC-I pathways

Because OGT regulates the PD-L1 and MHC-I pathways in UCEC cells to promote immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance, and OGT interacts with GR, we hypothesized that GR also regulates these pathways to promote immune evasion and immunotherapy resistance. Consistent with the role of GlcN and OGT on cell proliferation, neither DEX treatment nor GR overexpression altered cell proliferation (online supplemental figures 6A –C). Treatment with the GR antagonist mifepristone (MIFE; also referred to as RU486)37 did not impact cell proliferation either (online supplemental figure 6D). As anticipated, DEX treatment induced PD-L1 expression while inhibiting several components of the MHC-I pathway, including HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and B2M (online supplemental figure 6E). However, DEX did not affect mRNA levels of PD-L1 and components in GR−/− cells (online supplemental figure 6E). Two distinct siRNAs targeting GR were used to investigate whether GlcN and DEX regulate tumor immune evasion through GR, and their efficacy was evaluated (online supplemental figure 6F). SiRNA-GR#2 was selected for subsequent experiments. GR knockdown abolished the effect of GlcN on mRNA levels of PD-L1 and MHC-I pathway components (online supplemental figure 6G). Consistently, GlcN regulates the PD-L1 and the MHC-I pathway through GR (figure 6A and B). Furthermore, GlcN treatment stimulated Klf9, Snai2, and Fn1, known GR-activated target genes,38 in WT cells but not in GR−/− cells (figure 6A and B). OGT overexpression modulated mRNA levels of PD-L1, MHC-I components, and GR-activated target genes in WT cells, but not in GR−/− cells (figure 6C and D). We also examine the impact of GR and OGT mutations on PD-L1 and MHC-I pathways. As shown in online supplemental figures 6H and I, the OGT K908A mutant and GR mutations (S132A, S211A, and S226A) did not affect mRNA levels of PD-L1 or MHC-I components.

Interestingly, GlcN treatment inhibited T-cell cytotoxic activity in vitro in WT cells but not GR−/− cells (online supplemental figure 6J). A peptide disrupting OGT/GR interaction and restoring the antitumor immune response

As indicated in figure 4, the GR peptide (aa 125–138) binds to the pocket of OGT. We generated fusion proteins in which green fluorescent protein (GFP) is linked to the GR peptide (aa 125–138) (referred to as GFP-PI) or to a control sequence containing 3SA mutations (referred to as GFP-NC) (online supplemental figure 7A). GFP-PI demonstrated a stronger O-GlcNAcylation signal than GFP-NC in the OGT overexpression system (online supplemental figure 7B). Ectopic expression of GFP-PI, but not GFP-NC, abolished the effect of OGT on GRE promoter reporters and the mRNA level of PD-L1 and MHC-I pathway components (online supplemental figures 7C and D). Similar results were obtained in GR-overexpressed cells (online supplemental figures 7E and F).

To deliver this competitive peptide into cells, we used cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs).39 We chemically synthesized a peptide sequence containing CPP, the GR peptide (referred to as CPP-PI (cell-penetrating peptide is linked to the GR peptide) and CPP-NC (cell-penetrating peptide is linked to a control sequence)), and the fluorescent marker fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (figure 7A). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay revealed that CPP-PI and CPP-NC were not cytotoxic (online supplemental figures 7G and H) and did not affect cell proliferation (online supplemental figures 7I and J). Notably, the MST assay revealed that CPP-PI had a stronger affinity for OGT than the GR peptide alone (figure 7B). As shown in figure 7C, the affinity of CPP-PI for OGT was weaker than that of OSMI-4; however, compared with OSMI-4, treatment with CPP-PI did not substantially affect global cellular O-GlcNAcylation levels (online supplemental figures 7K and L). Owing to the higher affinity between the CPP-PI peptide and OGT at the target site, treatment with CPP-PI effectively inhibited the OGT/GR interaction and GR O-GlcNAcylation (figure 7D). In contrast, treatment with the CPP-NC peptide had no observable effect (figure 7D). Endogenous Co-IP experiments showed that CPP-PI, but not CPP-NC, reduced GlcN-regulated and DEX-regulated GR glycosylation levels and OGT/GR interactions (figure 7E and F). CPP-PI inhibited GlcN-induced GRE promoter activity and PD-L1 mRNA levels while reversing the GlcN-induced reduction in MHC-I pathway components (figure 7G and H), and also suppressed GR-induced mRNAs of KLF9, SNAI2, and FN1 (online supplemental figure 7M). Similar results were observed in DEX-treated cells (figure 7I and J). T-cell cytotoxicity assays revealed that CPP-PI, but not CPP-NC, abolished inhibition of T-cell cytotoxicity activity induced by GlcN, DEX, OGT overexpression, or GR overexpression (figure 7K–7N). Tumor organoids isolated from mice bearing orthotopic UCEC tumors and co-cultured with activated CD8+ T cells showed reduced death after GlcN and DEX treatment; CPP-PI abolished this effect (online supplemental figures 7N and O).

We then investigated the in vivo immune cell dependency of CPP-PI. Flow cytometry confirmed that FITC-CPP-PI was taken up by CD8+ T cells in the spleen and tumor niche 2 hours after intraperitoneal injection (online supplemental figure 7P), justifying in vivo efficacy studies. CPP-PI treatment dose-dependently decreased tumor volume and weight without notable body weight loss (figure 7O and online supplemental figure 7Q). Analysis of CD8+ T cells in the TME showed that CPP-PI increased the percentage of CD8+ T cells and granzyme B+ CTLs in a dose-dependent manner (figure 7P). We then assessed whether combining CPP-PI with PD-L1 mAb enhances the antitumor immune response. Strikingly, the combination further attenuated tumor growth without substantial body weight loss compared with either treatment alone (figure 7Q and online supplemental figure 7R), and CD8+ TIL infiltration and effector function were enhanced (figure 7R). These results indicate that GR inhibition synergizes with ICB, leading to more pronounced tumor suppression.

Discussion

Although the immune system can combat cancer, signals in the TME actively suppress the body’s natural defenses. It remains unclear whether glucose metabolism and synthetic glucocorticoids interact in cancer cells to inhibit the TME and, if so, whether this effect can make “cold” tumors like UCEC more responsive to ICB therapy. Here, we identified an immunoregulator in UCEC, in which increased glucose flux through the HBP promotes an antitumor immune response (figure 8). Mechanistically, OGT interacts with GR and induces O-GlcNAcylation of GR at serine-132 (figure 8), causing nuclear translocation of GR, activation of PD-L1, and repression of MHC-I components, leading to immunotherapy resistance (figure 8). A recent study showed that OGT-mediated intracellular O-GlcNAcylation increases PD-L1 expression, promoting ferroptosis resistance and immune suppression in pancreatic cancer.40 Together, these findings suggest that OGT-mediated PD-L1 expression may serve as a potential therapeutic target of ICB therapy in pan-cancer.

Model for the OGT/GR axis’s immunosuppressive effects on the UCEC tumor microenvironment In UCEC, dexamethasone binds to GR and induces GR phosphorylation. On the other hand, UCEC takes up glucose and activates the HBP via OGT. Then, OGT is associated with phosphorylated GR, leading to the O-GlcNAcylation of GR on serine 132. Subsequently, activated GR enters the nucleus to induce PD-L1 expression and reduce MHC-I expression, thereby leading to dysfunction of the tumor microenvironment. Glucocorticoids and glucose/GlcN can originate from the local microenvironment or serum.27 29 In contrast, glucocorticoids produced by the adrenal cortex enter the bloodstream and bind to GR in all cells, causing systemic effects.24 Psychological stress in patients with cancer raises blood glucocorticoid levels and activates GR signaling.41 Glucose and GlcN, primary energy sources, are also present in blood.42 Furthermore, various TME cell types—including cancer cells, T cells, and macrophages—uptake and produce glucocorticoids, glucose, and GlcN.43 The connection between these hallmarks in cancer cells remains unclear. We demonstrate that UCEC cells divert a fraction of glucose metabolism into the HBP, promoting OGT–GR binding. Notably, DEX also induces OGT association with GR, resulting in GR O-GlcNAcylation, highlighting the OGT/GR axis as a hub of HBP and glucocorticoid signaling.

Phosphorylation of GR has been reported to modify its subcellular localization and transcriptional activity.35 Here, we describe a mechanism in which DEX induces GR phosphorylation, enabling phosphorylated GR to bind OGT, leading to O-GlcNAcylation. Interestingly, two post-translational modifications occur on one protein. Similar examples exist: influenza A virus induces OGT binding to interferon regulatory factor-5 (IRF5), causing IRF5 O-GlcNAcylation, which is required for K63-linked ubiquitination.16 Tumor necrosis factor triggers O-GlcNAc and acetylation on nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB).44 Cullin 3, a cullin family E3 ubiquitin ligase, promotes STAT3 phosphorylation by inhibiting STAT3 O-GlcNAcylation, protecting against intestinal inflammation.45

This mechanistic link between OGT and GR O-GlcNAcylation suggests that modulating O-GlcNAcylation could be a therapeutic target for ICB therapy. Our results show that OGT or GR inhibition combined with PD-L1 mAb suppresses tumor growth in mice. Small-molecule OGT inhibitors can also inhibit tumor growth in vivo; however, because OGT is essential for normal physiology and glycosylates many substrates, global inhibition may cause side effects. To circumvent this, we developed short peptides to competitively inhibit O-GlcNAcylation on target proteins. Here, a peptide specifically inhibited GR O-GlcNAcylation. Although its affinity was weaker than that of OSMI-4, it did not affect total O-GlcNAcylation levels, indicating specificity, and successfully overcame immune evasion, thereby sensitizing UCEC to ICB in vivo. This proof of concept demonstrates that intracellular protein O-GlcNAcylation can be specifically targeted. Protein-specific, site-specific O-GlcNAcylation regulates biological activities that contribute to cancer initiation and progression.46 In addition to inhibiting cancer pathways, peptides have been designed as versatile cancer vaccines that activate adaptive immunity.47 48 Typically, peptide vaccines must:47 48 (1) be 15–25 amino acids in length; (2) adopt conformations similar to native tumor antigens; (3) include two antigenic epitopes to induce a well-defined T helper cell‌ (TH) epitope and an epitope that elicits specific CTL or B-cell responses; and (4) include adjuvants. Due to these harsh conditions, a peptide vaccine is not yet available on the market. The antagonist CPP-PI peptide used here contains no antigenic epitopes, and no adjuvants were used, thereby avoiding neutralizing antibody responses that could limit repeated use in vivo. Although optimization is needed, this method is adaptable to other O-GlcNAcylated proteins, offering potential for anticancer therapy development.

This study has some limitations. (1) Glucocorticoids are commonly used to control inflammation in patients with ICB.49 We show that glucocorticoids promote immune evasion and resistance in UCEC; whether they should be used cautiously requires further study. (2) O-GlcNAcylation appears context-dependent: it supports T-cell function,50 yet also promotes UCEC immune evasion. The distinctions and interactions of O-GlcNAcylation across cell types need further exploration. Further studies addressing these questions will clarify OGT’s role in tumor immunotherapy.
