Photopolymerizable placenta-derived ECM hydrogel enhances hUC-MSC-mediated wound healing via coordinated immunomodulation and angiogenesis
Authors: Heng An, Yuehan Liu, Jiang Jiang, Yufei Bao, Wenlian Li, Kaixuan Lv, Huixin Wu, Yaping Yan, Wei Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) hold great promise for regenerative medicine due to their potent immunomodulatory properties and favorable safety profile. However, their low survival, poor retention, and limited engraftment in injured tissues remain major barriers to clinical efficacy. Here, we engineered a photopolymerizable composite hydrogel derived from human placental extracellular matrix (PEM) and methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) to provide a biomimetic three-dimensional microenvironment for human umbilical cord MSCs (hUC-MSCs). The resulting PEM/HAMA (PH) hydrogel formed an interconnected porous, cytocompatible, and mechanically stable scaffold that enabled uniform cell encapsulation and maintained hUC-MSC viability above 80% after 72 h of culture. In vitro, PH significantly enhanced hUC-MSC mediated endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation. In vivo, the hUC-MSC loaded PH hydrogel (PHM) preserved MSC activity under inflammatory stress, promoted macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype, stimulated angiogenesis, and markedly accelerated wound closure in a murine full-thickness skin defect model. Together, these findings identify a placenta derived photopolymerizable extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogel as an effective stem cell delivery platform and provide a translatable strategy to overcome current limitations in MSC survival and therapeutic efficacy.

Statement of significance

Although decellularized ECM- and HAMA-based hydrogels have been explored for tissue repair, their integration with hUC-MSCs into a placenta-derived photopolymerizable delivery system for coordinated immunomodulation and angiogenesis remains insufficiently investigated. In this work, we developed a PEM/HAMA hydrogel (PH) and its hUC-MSC-loaded formulation (PHM) as an injectable stem cell delivery platform. The PHM composite supports hUC-MSC viability and promotes wound repair by enhancing M2 macrophage polarization and angiogenic responses during tissue repair. These findings highlight the significance of PHM not as a single-component material innovation, but as an integrated, reproducible, and clinically adaptable platform for improving MSC-based regenerative therapy (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of PHM preparation and its application in full-thickness skin wound repair through coordinated immunomodulation and angiogenesis.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) have shown remarkable therapeutic potential in treating refractory diseases such as osteoarthritis [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]], neurodegenerative disorders [[7], [8], [9], [10]], and hematological diseases [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]], owing to their unique immunomodulatory and regenerative capacities. MSCs interact with a broad range of immune cells (e.g., T cells [16,17], B cells [15,18,19], natural killer cells [20,21]and dendritic cells [22,23]) through contact dependent and paracrine mechanisms. These interactions modulate inflammatory responses, suppress excessive immune activation, and promote transition of the microenvironment toward anti-inflammatory and reparative states [24]. In addition, MSCs secrete various trophic factors, including VEGF, HGF, FGF, IGF-1, EGF, and TGF-β, which facilitate tissue repair, angiogenesis and cell proliferation. Compared with induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or embryonic stem cells (ESCs), MSCs are considered safer and less ethically controversial, making them an attractive candidate for regenerative medicine and immunotherapy.

Despite these advantages, the clinical efficacy of MSC therapy remains limited by poor cell survival and low engraftment efficiency in vivo. Current delivery methods, including intravenous infusion and local injection, often result in rapid cell loss. After intravenous infusion, most MSCs are trapped in the pulmonary capillary bed and subsequently cleared within days in mice [25]. Similar biodistribution study in nonhuman primate has revealed transient MSC accumulation in lung and liver, with negligible cell persistence after three weeks [25]. Locally injected MSCs also face harsh microenvironments that compromise their viability. For instance, in osteoarthritic joints, pro-inflammatory cytokines and the lack of anchorage to the extracellular matrix induce anoikis-mediated apoptosis in MSCs [26,27]. In cutaneous wound injection, hypoxia, oxidative stress, and nutrient deprivation further limit MSC survival [28,29]. Consequently, less than 10% of transplanted MSCs remain viable within 36 h post injection[[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]], leading to inconsistent and short lived therapeutic outcomes. Overcoming these barriers requires a robust delivery platform that supports long term MSC survival, preserves their paracrine activity, and promotes targeted tissue repair.

Hydrogels have emerged as promising candidates to address these challenges due to their tunable physicochemical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and ability to mimic native extracellular matrix environments[[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Initially designed as passive scaffolds for cell encapsulation, modern hydrogel systems have evolved into bioactive and responsive matrices capable of modulating cell behavior and releasing therapeutic molecules in a controlled manner. In particular, tissue specific decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) hydrogels have attracted increasing attention, as they preserve native ECM components and ultrastructural features while exhibiting low immunogenicity [40,41]. The human placenta derived ECM (PEM) is especially attractive as a biomaterial source, as it is rich in collagens, fibronectin, and endogenous growth factors (e.g., TGF-β, FGF), thereby providing a naturally regenerative microenvironment.

In this study, we aimed to develop a bioactive and clinically translatable wound repair strategy by integrating an injectable PEM/HAMA composite hydrogel with hUC-MSCs derived from discarded perinatal tissues under an hPL-based serum-free culture system. The PEM component provides a tissue specific biochemical milieu that supports cell adhesion and paracrine function, while the HAMA network enables fine control over injectability and mechanical properties via photo-crosslinking. The resulting PH hydrogel exhibits an interconnected porous microstructure and time-dependent in vivo degradation behavior, thereby creating a supportive three-dimensional microenvironment that enhances hUC-MSC survival and function. Using both in vitro and in vivo models, we demonstrate that this system promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, facilitates angiogenesis, enhances collagen deposition, accelerates wound closure, and even induces the regeneration of skin appendages such as hair follicles. Collectively, this work not only establishes PH as a biologically inspired delivery platform for advancing MSC based regenerative therapies, but also elucidates the synergistic mechanisms of material cell interactions that orchestrate tissue regeneration.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sodium hyaluronate (MW 403.31 kDa), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and methacrylic anhydride (MA) were purchased from Macklin (H6350, Shanghai, China). Dialysis tubing MD77 (8000–14,000 Da) and EDTA (free acid) were obtained from Solarbio (YA1075, Beijing, China). Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit was obtained from Servicebio (G1707, Hubei, China).

Culture of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs)

Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) and placental tissues were obtained from three healthy donors who underwent elective cesarean delivery at The First People's Hospital of Yunnan Province. Placental tissues and umbilical cords were collected immediately after delivery under sterile conditions. Informed consent was obtained from all donors prior to tissue donation. All procedures involving human-derived tissues were approved by the Ethics Committee of Yunnan First People's Hospital (approval No. KHLCYJ20240050).

After removal of blood vessels, umbilical cord tissues were dissected into fragments(∼1 cm in diameter) and plated in MSC NutriStem XF basal medium (Biological Industries, USA) supplemented with 5% human platelet lysate (hPL) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Biological Industries, USA). Cells were passaged and expanded upon reaching approximately 80% confluence.

Preparation and characterization of novel placenta derived extracellular matrix (PEM)

Following optimized decellularization protocols adapted from previous studies[[42], [43], [44]], three systematic approaches were established and compared. PEM I: Placental tissue blocks (≈3 mm3) were treated with a composite decellularization solution (0.5% PMSF, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% ammonia) for 48 h under dynamic agitation, followed by 24 h washing in distilled water and subsequent antibiotic treatment with penicillin–streptomycin. PEM II: Tissue was treated with 1% SDS for 72 h (solution refreshed every 24 h), followed by EDTA chelation and DNase digestion (600 U/mL, 37 °C, 18 h) for deep decellularization. PEM III: Tissue was dynamically treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 and 0.1% ammonia for 72 h (solution refreshed every 12 h) with enhanced washing procedures. All decellularized samples were lyophilized and mechanically ground into powder. DNA residue (<50 ng/mg dry weight) was quantified using the Picogreen assay, and total protein retention was assessed using a Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA) to evaluate decellularization efficiency and ECM preservation.

The optimized PEM was further characterized as follows: fresh tissue and decellularized tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for staining. Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess cellular residue, Masson's trichrome staining for collagen fiber distribution, Sirius Red (SR) staining for collagen subtype identification, and Alcian Blue (AB) staining for glycosaminoglycan retention. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software analysis system. All procedures were conducted under standardized protocols to ensure reproducibility and quality control.

Synthesis and characterization of methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA)

HAMA was synthesized following a modified protocol. Briefly, a 1% (w/v) sodium hyaluronate solution was prepared in bicarbonate buffer, and DMF (N, N-Dimethylformamide) was added at an H2O:DMF ratio of 3:2 under continuous stirring. Methacrylic anhydride (3%v/v) was added dropwise, and the pH was adjusted to 8-9 using 0.5 M NaOH. The reaction mixture was stirred overnight at 4 °C, and then dialyzed against distilled water for 3 days to remove unreacted reagents. The purified product was lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for subsequent use.

The methacrylation of HA was verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Briefly, lyophilized HA and HAMA samples were dissolved in D2O, and spectra were recorded using an NMR spectrometer. Successful methacrylation was confirmed by the appearance of characteristic methacrylate vinyl proton peaks at approximately 5.6 and 6.1 ppm in HAMA, which were absent in pristine HA. The degree of methacrylation was calculated by comparing the integrated area of the methacrylate vinyl proton peaks with that of the N-acetyl methyl proton peak of HA at approximately 1.9 ppm. Based on this analysis, the degree of methacrylation of the HAMA used in this study was approximately 30% (Fig. S1).

Preparation and characterization of PEM/HAMA composite hydrogel (PH)

PEM and HAMA components were dissolved separately before blending. PEM (12 mg/mL) was digested in a pepsin solution (pH = 2) for 48 h and neutralized with 0.1 M NaOH, yielding Solution A, which was stored at 4 °C. HAMA (2%) containing 1% lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was dissolved in water or basal medium, stirred overnight, and designated as Solution B. Equal volumes of Solution A and Solution B were mixed thoroughly with continuous stirring at room temperature to obtain composite hydrogels with a final PEM concentration of 6 mg/mL and a final HAMA concentration of 1% (w/v). The prepared hydrogels were stored at 4 °C until further use. The in-situ crosslinking conditions for the hybrid hydrogel are 15 s of blue light irradiation.

Viscosity rheological measurements were performed using a Modular Compact Rheometer (Model: MCR302, Yunnan Institute of Medical Device Testing). For each test, 250 μL of the sample was loaded onto the rheometer stage, with a fixed gap size of 0.5 mm. The viscosity was measured under a constant shear rate of 0.1 s−1.Crosslinking rheological measurements were conducted on the same rheometer with the following parameters: sample volume of 250 μL, gap size of 0.5 mm, applied strain of 10%, and constant frequency of 1 Hz. The samples were exposed to UV light for 60 s during the crosslinking process to induce gelation, and rheological properties were recorded simultaneously. Compression tests were carried out using a Universal Testing Machine (Model: AG-IS10KN, Yunnan Institute of Medical Device Testing) at a constant compression rate of 1 mm/s. All tests were performed at room temperature unless otherwise specified.

The as-prepared hydrogel samples were subcutaneously implanted into the dorsal region of mice. At predetermined time points (day 1, 3, 5, 12 and 20), the remaining hydrogel specimens were surgically retrieved, and the residual mass was accurately weighed and recorded to quantitatively evaluate the in vivo degradation behavior. The rResidual mass ratio was calculated as follows: residual mass ratio (%) = Rresidual mass/Initial mass × 100%.

Fresh porcine skin tissues were obtained on the day of the experiment from freshly slaughtered 8-month-old pigs at a local abattoir. The skin samples had an approximate thickness of 6 mm. After collection, the tissues were immediately transported to the laboratory in an ice box at 4 °C and processed on the same day. A full-thickness square defect measuring 1 cm × 1 cm was created in the skin sample, followed by in situ injection and photo-crosslinking of the PH hydrogel. The treated samples were then subjected sequentially to running-water rinsing, 24 h PBS immersion, and bending tests, with photographic documentation, to evaluate the stability and handling performance of the PH hydrogel under simulated application conditions.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of PH hydrogel

To examine the microstructure, samples of HAMA, PHM and PH hydrogels were cut to expose flat fracture surfaces. Hydrogels were frozen in liquid nitrogen for 5 min to induce brittleness, sectioned, sputter coated with gold and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (Nova NanoSEM 450) at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Pore morphology and distribution were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Macrophage Co-culture experiment

Macrophages were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured for 24 h to reach ∼70% confluence. M1 polarization was induced using complete medium containing 20 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 36 h. The following groups were set up for Transwell co-culture over 48 h: Normal (untreated), Control (PBS), PH, MSC, and PHM. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining to assess polarization markers.

HUVEC Co-culture and functional assays

The effects of PH, MSC, and PHM treatments on human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVECs) function were evaluated in vitro through proliferation, migration, and tube formation assays. Proliferation assay: HUVECs (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates, cultured to ∼70% confluence, and co-cultured with PBS, PH, MSC, or PHM in Transwell systems for 48 h. Cells were fixed and stained with Ki67, and imaged by confocal microscopy. Proliferation indices were quantified using ImageJ. Migration assay: HUVECs (6 × 104 cells/well) were cultured to ∼90% confluence, scratched with a pipette tip, and co-cultured with each treatment group for 24 h. Scratch closure was monitored by an inverted microscope, and migration rates were calculated as the percentage of scratch closure. Tube formation assay: HUVECs (6 × 104 cells/well) were seeded onto Matrigel coated 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Tube length, branch points, and mesh formation were quantified using ImageJ.

Full-thickness skin defect mouse model

A standardized full-thickness skin defect model was established in 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. For anesthesia, a ready-to-use tribromoethanol solution (Cat. No. M2920, Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was administered intraperitoneally at a dosage of 15 μL/g body weight to ensure adequate anesthesia during surgical procedures. Throughout the experimental period, mice were monitored regularly for signs of distress, abnormal behavior, impaired mobility, or wound-related complications. Animals showing severe adverse reactions or signs of excessive distress were handled promptly according to the approved animal welfare protocol to minimize suffering. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming University of Science and Technology and conducted according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (AP-KUST-202501010015).

Two symmetric full-thickness wounds (8 mm in diameter) were created using a biopsy punch. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: PBS (control), PH hydrogel, MSC suspension (2 × 106 cells/ml), and PHM hydrogel (2 × 106 cells/ml) (n = 6 per group). Each wound received 50 μL of the designated treatment. Wound healing was recorded on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12 using standardized photography. Wound areas were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Digital photographs of wound sites were captured at predetermined time points under standardized lighting, distance, and positioning conditions. The wound boundaries were manually delineated, and the actual wound area was calculated using ImageJ based on the calibrated scale. Wound closure (%) was calculated as: Wound closure rate (%) = [(initial area − residual area)/initial area] × 100%.

Immunofluorescence staining

Wound tissues were collected on days 7 and 12 post treatment, embedded in Optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT, SAKURA, 4568), and cryosectioned at 8 μm thickness. Sections were fixed with cold acetone, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. For macrophage polarization analysis, F4/80 was used to identify total macrophages, CD68 was used to label M1-like macrophages, and CD206 was used to label M2-like macrophages. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software. Five random fields were selected from each sample, and the numbers of F4/80+, CD68+, and CD206+ cells were quantified. The proportions of M1-and M2-like macrophages were calculated as the percentage of CD68+ or CD206+ cells among the total F4/80+ macrophage population.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Results were expressed as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed before statistical comparison. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical significance levels were denoted as follows: p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, p < 0.0001; NS indicates no significant difference. Each experiment included at least five biological replicates to ensure data reliability.

Optimization of placenta derived ECM (PEM) preparation

The preparation of PEM consisted of sequential steps including tissue mincing, decellularization, lyophilization, and mechanical grinding (Fig. 2A). To identify the optimal protocol, three systematic decellularization protocols were compared based on DNA removal (50 ng/mg dry weight) and protein retention. Among them, PEM II achieved nearly complete removal of nuclear material but resulted in substantial protein loss (<80%), whereas PEM III preserved the highest protein content (∼95%) exhibited residual DNA contamination. PEM I, which combined Triton X-100 and ammonia treatment, achieved a balanced outcome between efficient decellularization and preservation of ECM components (Fig. 2B and C). Further confirmation by histological staining that the successful decellularization of the PEM I, characterized by the absence of nucleic acids and the concomitant preservation of collagen (Fig. 2D–F; Fig. S2A and B). Combined with the above characterization results, including residual DNA content, protein retention, GAG quantification, DAPI staining, and COL1A1 staining (Fig. 2B–G; Fig. S2B), histological evaluation further revealed distinct differences among the three decellularization protocols. PEM III retained relatively abundant ECM components but showed insufficient removal of residual nuclear material, whereas PEM II achieved more complete cellular removal but resulted in substantial loss of matrix components. In contrast, PEM I achieved a better balance between effective decellularization and preservation of ECM components and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments. PEM I was selected for subsequent experiments, with over 99% nucleic acid removal, minimal residual DNA, and well preserved bioactive matrix proteins. Synthesis and physicochemical characterization of the PEM/HAMA composite hydrogel

To enhance the structural stability and clinical applicability of PEM, HAMA (30% substitution) was incorporated to form a photo-crosslinkable composite hydrogel. The combination of PEM and HAMA endowed (PH) the system with tunable viscosity, injectability, and photostability suitable for minimally invasive delivery (Fig. 3A and E). Upon exposure to 405 nm blue light, the precursor solution rapidly gelated within seconds, forming a stable network structure (Fig. 3A and B). In vivo degradation studies in mice indicated that PH degraded in a time dependent manner—showing accelerated degradation within 3 days and ∼20% residual mass by day 12, consistent with the typical wound healing timelines (Fig. 3A). Rheological analysis confirmed a shear thinning behavior favorable for syringe injection, while compression tests demonstrated mechanical strength sufficient for soft tissue implantation. PH exhibited excellent flexibility and moldability, maintaining its integrity under bending and stretching (Fig. 3C and D). When tested on porcine full-thickness skin defects (10 × 10 mm), the PH precursor could be injected into the defect site and rapidly crosslinked in situ. The formed hydrogel remained attached after rinsing under running water for 5 min and after immersion in PBS for 24 h, with a swelling ratio of less than 15%. Moreover, the hydrogel maintained its integrity during bending/twisting deformation, indicating good adhesion, stability, and adaptability to skin deformation (Fig. 3E). Biocompatibility assessment of the PH hydrogel

The biocompatibility of the PH hydrogel was first evaluated by encapsulating hUC-MSCs within the 3D matrix. Live/dead staining after 72 h of culture showed over 80% cell viability, and the cell viability remained above 70% after 7 days of incubation, with cells uniformly distributed throughout the hydrogel (Fig. S3C and D). Three-dimensional confocal images confirmed homogeneous spatial distribution and good cellular retention, indicating that the hydrogel microenvironment supported sustained cell survival and proliferation (Fig. 4A). SEM imaging revealed a highly crosslinked porous network, in which the incorporation of PEM resulted in slightly thickened pore walls compared with HAMA alone. This structural modification enhanced cell anchorage and nutrient diffusion, enabling hUC-MSCs to adhere along the pore surfaces (Fig. 4B; Fig. S3E). Immunomodulatory effects of the PH hydrogel on macrophages

To investigate the immunomodulatory capacity of the composite hydrogel system, human macrophages were stimulated with LPS to induce a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype and subsequently co-cultured with PBS, PH, hUC-MSCs, or PHM. Immunofluorescence staining for macrophage markers (F4/80 for total macrophages, CD68 for M1, and CD206 for M2) showed distinct differences among groups (Fig. 5A). Compared with PBS and PH treatments, PHM significantly reduced the proportion of CD68+ M1 macrophages while increasing CD206+ M2 macrophages, indicating a pronounced shift toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 5A). Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed that PHM treatment significantly enhanced M2 polarization and suppressed M1 activation. (Fig. 5B and C). Immunofluorescence staining of Ki67 demonstrated that PHM treatment significantly enhanced HUVEC proliferation compared with hUC-MSCs, whereas the PH group showed modest effect, and the control group showed minimal proliferation (Fig. 6A). Quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed that PHM significantly promoted endothelial cell proliferation (Fig. 6D). Scratch assays further revealed that PHM accelerated HUVEC migration, while PH exerted a modest effect (Fig. 6B and E). In tube-formation assays, both PHM and hUC-MSCs significantly increased total tube length and the number of branch points, indicating enhanced angiogenic capacity (Fig. 6C–F, G). In vivo safety assessment of PHM

To evaluate the biosafety of the PHM, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested on day 12 post treatment for histopathological examination. H&E staining demonstrated normal tissue architecture without detectable inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or other pathological alterations in any treatment groups, confirming the excellent in vivo biocompatibility and safety of the cell laden PHM (Fig. S5).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated a photopolymerizable PH composite hydrogel as a bioactive delivery platform for hUC-MSCs. Our findings demonstrate that the hydrogel system can uniformly encapsulate hUC-MSCs at a clinically relevant density (1 × 106 cells/mL) while maintaining over 80% cell viability after a 72 h culture. These results confirm the excellent cytocompatibility and permissive microarchitecture of the hydrogel, fulfilling essential criteria for injectable cell carriers in regenerative medicine, underscoring the potential suitability of the PH system for downstream translational applications.

Compared with native placental tissue, PEM showed partial disruption of the macro-scale ECM texture after decellularization, likely due to decellularization, lyophilization, and mechanical grinding. This structural change reflects a trade-off between efficient cellular/nuclear removal and preservation of native tissue architecture. Since the goal of this study was to generate a low-immunogenic ECM-derived material for hydrogel fabrication and hUC-MSC encapsulation, preservation of intact placental macroarchitecture was not required. PEM I still retained representative ECM components, as shown by histological staining, GAG quantification, and COL1A1 immunofluorescence, while exhibiting low residual DNA content and reduced nuclear signals. Residual DNA content is a key indicator of the immunogenic risk of decellularized ECM. Commonly accepted criteria recommend residual DNA levels below 50 ng/mg dry tissue, DNA fragments shorter than approximately 200 bp, and no visible nuclear material by nuclear staining[[45], [46], [47]]. In this study, PEM I showed a residual DNA content of approximately 33 ng/mg dry ECM, below the accepted threshold. Together with reduced DAPI signals and histological evidence of cellular removal, these findings support effective decellularization of PEM I, although DNA fragment length should be further assessed in future quality-control studies.

The enhanced wound repair observed in the PHM group is likely attributable to the combined effects of the PEM/HAMA hydrogel microenvironment and the paracrine activity of encapsulated hUC-MSCs. The PH hydrogel provides an ECM-like three-dimensional niche that supports hUC-MSC survival and may help sustain the secretion of pro-regenerative mediators. hUC-MSC-derived factors, such as VEGF, HGF, FGF, TGF-β, IL-10, and PGE2, have been reported to regulate macrophage polarization, endothelial activation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [48,49]. Consistent with this concept, our in vitro macrophage co-culture assays showed that PHM promoted a shift toward the CD206+ M2 phenotype, while HUVEC assays demonstrated enhanced proliferation, migration, and tube formation. These in vitro findings were further supported by in vivo immunofluorescence staining, where PHM-treated wounds showed increased CD206+ macrophage accumulation and stronger CD31+ vascular signals. Together, these results suggest that PHM accelerates wound healing through coordinated immunomodulatory and pro-angiogenic effects, although further studies involving quantitative growth factor profiling and pathway-specific inhibition will be needed to define the precise molecular mechanisms.

MSCs exert their therapeutic benefits predominantly through paracrine signaling rather than direct differentiation, regulating inflammation and angiogenesis via secreted cytokines and extracellular vesicles [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. However, the reliance on high MSC dosages is often necessary because transplanted cells exhibit short retention times and low survival, which limits their clinical applicability. In this study, our PH composite hydrogel not only provides a protective 3D niche that supports cell viability and function but also synergizes with hUC-MSCs to amplify their paracrine and angiogenic effects. The PHM composite markedly enhanced HUVEC proliferation, migration, and tube formation—outperforming free MSCs and blank hydrogels—even at lower cell dosages. These findings suggest that the matrix-cell interaction within PHM plays a critical role in potentiating MSC function, enabling dose-efficient therapeutic outcomes that may improve scalability for clinical translation compared to the medical dressing hydrogel products in the clinical market.

Inflammation resolution and vascular reconstruction are pivotal determinants of wound repair quality [54,55]. Our findings revealed that PHM hydrogel effectively modulates macrophage polarization, promoting a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 phenotypes. This transition is essential for orchestrating the subsequent phases of tissue remodeling and angiogenesis. Consistent with previous reports linking sustained M1 dominance to chronic non-healing wounds, the PHM system significantly reduced inflammatory persistence while facilitating the establishment of a functional microvascular network. CD31 immunostaining confirmed robust neovascularization in PHM treated wounds, demonstrating its dual capacity for immunomodulation and vascular regeneration. These effects likely stem from the synergistic interplay between PEM derived bioactive cues and MSC secreted paracrine factors, which collectively reprogram the wound microenvironment toward a pro-healing state.

Current wound-healing strategies, such as growth factor therapy and skin grafting, still have important limitations. Growth factor-based treatments are often limited by rapid degradation, short retention time, and the need for repeated administration, whereas skin grafting may be constrained by donor-site availability, secondary injury, and graft-related complications. Compared with these approaches, PHM combines an ECM-derived hydrogel scaffold with hUC-MSCs, providing local cell retention, a protective three-dimensional microenvironment, and coordinated regulation of inflammation and angiogenesis. These features suggest that PHM may support multiple stages of wound repair rather than relying on a single bioactive factor or surgical tissue replacement.

Nevertheless, clinical translation of PHM still requires further optimization of bioactive component release, standardized large-scale manufacturing, strict batch-to-batch quality control, long-term safety evaluation, and regulatory assessment, particularly because it combines living cells with biomaterials.

Taken together, our study demonstrates a clinically relevant and resource-sustainable therapeutic platform that combines a PEM/HAMA injectable scaffold, a low-cost and stable hUC-MSC source, and an hPL-based serum-free culture system for wound healing applications. Compared with conventional MSC injections, this composite platform provides improved spatial control, prolonged cell survival, and enhanced functional outcomes. Nevertheless, further investigation is warranted to determine whether this hydrogel can be broadly applied to MSCs derived from alternative sources (e.g., bone marrow, adipose, amnion, or placenta) and whether long term remodeling outcomes are consistent across pathological conditions. Although PBS, PH hydrogel, free hUC-MSCs, and PHM groups were included, component-specific controls such as HAMA alone, PEM alone, and their hUC-MSC-loaded formulations were not evaluated. In addition, short-term biosafety was assessed mainly by H&E staining of major organs, while body weight and organ weight were not recorded. These issues should be addressed in future studies to better define component-specific effects and systemic biocompatibility.

Conclusion

This study developed a novel placenta derived decellularized extracellular matrix hydrogel (PH) for encapsulating and delivering human umbilical cord derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs). The PH hydrogel effectively maintained the viability, phenotypic stability, and paracrine function of MSCs. Furthermore, the PHM composite—comprising MSCs laden in photo-crosslinkable PH hydrogel—demonstrated a porous, stacked tubular microstructure, excellent cytocompatibility, and injectability, rendering it suitable for translational applications. Therefore, the PH hydrogel acts as a bioinspired delivery platform that not only preserves MSC functionality but also elucidates a synergistic material-cell mechanism underlying immunomodulation and angiogenesis.
