Her Health Behavior, Her Choice? Decision‐Making Power Over Key Health and Nutrition Behaviors Among Pregnant and Lactating Women in Jordan
Authors: Laurie C Miller, Regina Brown, Reema Safadi, Yanlin Ren, Afnan Alghanmi, Ian G Moore, Robin Shrestha, Patrick Webb, Shibani Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWomen's participation in family diet and health decisions is strongly associated with better family outcomes. We examined decision‐making power among participants in a Community Health and Nutrition Program in Jordan, which sought to improve maternal, infant, and young child nutrition (MIYCN) and modern contraception through service‐provider training and media campaigns. Cross‐sectional surveys in Amman, Karak, and Zarqa governates enrolled 3518 pregnant/lactating women (PLW) in the program (2021–23). PLW respondents indicated whether receiving messages about MIYCN and contraceptive use changed their practices, and whether their decision‐making was total, shared with other family members (husbands, mothers, mothers‐in‐law), or not possible for these choices. Results were analyzed using chi‐square tests and multiple linear regressions. After receiving program messages, 78% of PLW changed ≥ 1 practice. Between 52.8% and 90.3% of PLW had decision‐making power to accept or reject recommendations for the six targeted behaviors. Compared to Amman, Zarqa residence was negatively associated with decision‐making power over women's dietary diversity (OR = 0.418, p < 0.05) and early initiation of breastfeeding (OR = 0.493, p < 0.05), while Karak residence was negatively associated with decision‐making power over modern contraceptive use (OR = 0.419, SE = 0.116, p < 0.01). Number of children was positively associated with decision‐making power over exclusive breastfeeding (OR = 1.18, p < 0.01). Nationality, age, and wealth were not associated with decision‐making power over these practices. Decision‐making power varied among PLW in the program: PLW living outside Amman and those with fewer children had less autonomy to change practices. Further research is needed to understand how to best support decision‐making power among these populations. Background

The ability of women to make autonomous decisions—especially about their own health, diet, and reproductive choices—is considered a basic human right (United Nations 2022). However, around half of married women worldwide lack this power (Gebeyehu et al. 2022; Saaka 2020; UNFPA 2020; United Nations 2022), usually due to economic, religious, tribal, and cultural traditions (Osamor and Grady 2016).

The capacity of women to make autonomous decisions has been associated with better child and family health and nutrition (Albornoz‐Arias et al. 2025; Gebeyehu et al. 2022; Idris et al. 2023; Osamor and Grady 2016). Higher levels of women's autonomy, especially in countries with more gender discrimination, are associated with improved family planning, increased antenatal and postnatal care, better maternal diet diversity, higher birthweights, improved child feeding practices, increased child vaccination rates, and overall better child growth, as well as reduced maternal mortality, maternal depression and anxiety, and infant/child mortality rates (Carlson et al. 2015; Heckert et al. 2019; Idris et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2017; Salinger et al. 2024; Shroff et al. 2009). Compared to fathers, mothers who have control over income tend to direct a greater share of family resources towards food, and health‐ and nutrition‐related expenditures—especially for their children (Brunson et al. 2009; Carlson et al. 2015; Heckert et al. 2019; Lee et al. 2017). The relationships between women's empowerment and positive family outcomes have been described as a “virtuous cycle” (Lee et al. 2017).

However, other research indicates that women's autonomy has negligible (Agu et al. 2019; Saaka 2020) or even inverse associations with children's health and nutrition (Achana et al. 2024; Carlson et al. 2015; Chol et al. 2019; Cunningham et al. 2015; Shroff et al. 2009; Smith et al. 2003). These seemingly paradoxical results are usually attributed to the way autonomy was measured, unidentified additional stressors in the family or community, competing demands for family resources (including the mothers' time with the child), etc. Increased women's autonomy has also been associated with adverse personal health consequences, including elevated blood pressure and depression (Brunson et al. 2009; Carlson et al. 2015; Hadley et al. 2010), and escalated domestic violence, possibly due to disagreement and increased conflict between partners (Aden et al. 1997; Koenig et al. 2003). These factors could hinder the ability of women to care for themselves and their children (Brunson et al. 2009; Carlson et al. 2015). As these are largely cross‐sectional studies, causality cannot be determined.

Definitions of Autonomy

The definitions and the tools used to measure women's autonomy vary considerably in the literature. Many authors use standardized questions from their countries' national health surveys (e.g., Demographic and Health Surveys) (Osamor and Grady 2016), or the Women's Empowerment in Agriculture Index (WEAI) (Alkire et al. 2013). Others develop study‐specific measures. Women's autonomy is defined by various aspects of economic freedom, physical liberty, and decision‐making, operationalized by posing questions to the woman about her control of money (e.g., access to and control of her own or household money), freedom of movement (e.g., able to go alone to market, health clinic, family, or friend visits), and ability to make her own decisions (e.g., household purchases, health care for herself and her children, food preparation and consumption) without seeking approval from others (Acharya et al. 2010; Al Riyami et al. 2004; Arulampalam et al. 2016; Friedrich et al. 2021; Gebeyehu et al. 2022; Idris et al. 2023; Jones et al. 2019; Libbus and Kridli 1997; Linos et al. 2010; Nigatu et al. 2014; OECD Development Centre 2023; Osamor and Grady 2016). Some autonomy measures also include the woman's degree of acceptance or rejection of domestic violence, and occasionally other elements of inter‐spousal relations (Osamor and Grady 2016; Thapa and Niehof 2013). Surprisingly few autonomy questionnaires address who makes the decisions relating to feeding and care of children (other than traveling to the health clinic), focusing instead on decisions the mother makes for herself (Shroff et al. 2009). One early exception considered the dynamics of intrahousehold gendered decision‐making authority in relation to various spending decisions and women's time allocation (Webb 1989).

Usually, items in these questionnaires are combined to create a single autonomy index, although this reduces the ability to discern the relationship of different autonomy dimensions to specific health behaviors or outcomes (Shroff et al. 2009). For example, in India, women's access to money and freedom to go to the market was associated with lower rates of child stunting. However, her freedom to visit relatives or friends, choosing what to cook, deciding on major household purchases, and attitudes towards domestic violence did not relate to stunting (Shroff et al. 2009).

Likewise, some researchers consider women to have autonomy only if they have the complete and final say about decisions (Al Riyami et al. 2004; Brunson et al. 2009; Friedrich et al. 2021; Gender Roles, Women in Decision Making and Violence against Women 2020; Lee et al. 2017; Linos et al. 2010). Many consider that joint husband and wife decision‐making incorporates women's autonomy (Carlson et al. 2015; Gebeyehu et al. 2022; Idris et al. 2023; Shroff et al. 2009; Sultana 2011) and emphasizes the “partnership” dimension of a marital relationship and the benefits of respecting preferences and sharing consequences (Idris et al. 2023). Other researchers give “partial scores” for shared decisions with her spouse (Nigatu et al. 2014; Saaka 2020) or full scores if the woman participates at all in the decision (Mekonen et al. 2024).

In different contexts, women's autonomy has been associated with her age, education, household wealth, culture, religion, urban versus rural residence, workload, mental health and self‐esteem, social support, her husband's education and occupation, number of living children and their ages, number of sons, legal constructs, and various socio‐cultural factors and beliefs (Brunson et al. 2009; Carlson et al. 2015; Cunningham et al. 2015; Idris et al. 2023; Jones et al. 2019; Osamor and Grady 2016). While many of these characteristics are relatively fixed in adulthood, empowerment “is still considered to be malleable” (Heckert et al. 2019), and therefore of particular interest to development and intervention efforts.

Women's Autonomy in the Middle East

Most studies on women's autonomy globally have been conducted in Asia and Africa. Despite its relatively better economic status, undernutrition is more prevalent in South Asia than in Africa (Cunningham et al. 2015; Ramalingaswami et al. 1997). This is often attributed to the low levels of women's empowerment in South Asia, especially in countries with strongly patriarchal cultures (Shroff et al. 2009). However, relatively few studies of women's autonomy have been reported from Middle Eastern and North African (MENA), which share similar patriarchal traditions. These countries have the unusual demographic situation of very low female labor market participation (19% compared to a global average of 48%) in the face of women's increasing educational attainment (43% of women vs 39% of men enrolled in tertiary education) and declining fertility rates (Assaad et al. 2020; Friedrich et al. 2021; International Labour Organization 2022; Ousmane Dione et al. 2025; World Bank 2025).

Among MENA countries, Jordan is of particular interest. Jordanian women face barriers to employment, including childcare, safe transportation, and unequal pay, complicated by legal and social restrictions which limit women's ability to work outside the home (International Labour Organization 2022; The Jordanian National Commission for Women 2025; UN Jordan 2021; United Nations 2021). Despite high levels of female education and recent women's empowerment initiatives, Jordan has remarkably low levels of women's participation in the labor force (153rd/156; 14%, compared to 54% for males), economic productivity (133rd/156) and Global Gender Gap Index (131rd/156) (International Labour Organization 2022; Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; World Bank 2025). In 2018, Jordan had the lowest female labor market participation worldwide for a country not at war (Ait Ali Slimane et al. 2020; Friedrich et al. 2021; International Labour Organization 2022). In 2023, the Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development classified Jordan's level of female discrimination as “very high,” based on measures of discrimination in the family, restricted physical integrity, restricted access to productive and financial resources, and restricted civil liberties (OECD Development Centre 2023), along with Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia.

Moreover, some important indicators of women's autonomy have decreased over time, for example, the percentage of Jordanian women who make their own decisions about sexual and reproductive health (personal health care, contraceptive use, refusing sex with their husbands) declined from 72.6% in 2012 to 60.7% in 2018 (UNFPA 2020). The Jordanian government has instituted several initiatives to address these issues (Department of Statistics Jordan and ICF 2024; International Labour Organization 2022; Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; United Nations 2021; United Nations Jordan 2021; World Bank 2025).

Given this context, we examined reproductive health and diet‐related decision‐making power among Jordanian married, pregnant, and lactating women (PLW) for themselves and for their young children, and whether PLW were able to make these decisions independently, in conjunction with family members, or had no voice in making these choices. Because some evidence suggests that parenthood “triggers negotiation processes that may increase mothers' agency” (Friedrich et al. 2021), PLW were of special interest, and focusing on it allowed us to control for marital status and motherhood. We evaluated the characteristics of women with different levels of decision‐making power. We use the term “decision‐making power” rather than “autonomy” to emphasize the limited dimensions of autonomy which we addressed. Within this limited scope, we controlled for the influence of exposure to a large‐scale community health and nutrition intervention (described below) that was concurrent with our data collection. We focused on 6 nutrition and health behaviors for PLW that were targeted by this intervention (described below).

Methods

Our study was embedded in a large investigation addressing multiple aspects of the status of married pregnant and lactating women (PLW) in Jordan. To address the strong urban‐rural divide in Jordan (Khawaldeh and Abu‐Sabha 2022), the intervention was implemented in 21 selected districts in 3 (of 12) governorates: Amman, Zarqa, and Karak. Amman is the capital, and the wealthiest governate. Annual income in Zarka, although urban, is the 11th lowest governate in Jordan. Karak is among the poorest and most rural governates (Al Sharafat 2019; Qutieshat and Al‐Assaf 2024). Health and educational services in both Karak and Zarka are less developed than in Amman. These locations were selected to represent a range of socio‐economic conditions, female educational levels, and population densities.

Procedures

Three cross‐sectional quantitative surveys of PLW were collected by the Jordan Nutrition Innovation Lab (JNIL) in 2021, 2022, and 2023. These surveys were designed to evaluate the Community Health and Nutrition program (funded by United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and implemented by Family Health International 360) which aimed to improve the nutritional status of PLW and children under 2 years of age. The Community Health and Nutrition Program targeted six key health and nutrition behaviors of PLW, related to their health and dietary choices for themselves [PLW dietary diversity (DD‐W), use of postpartum modern contraception], and their infants and young children [early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF), exclusive breastfeeding (EBF), provision of appropriate foods for complementary feeding (CF), and minimal meal frequency (MMF)], according to international definitions and recommendations (UNFPA 2021; United Nations Children's Fund, & UNICEF Programming Guidance 2021; World Health Organization 2023; World Health Organization, & United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2021). The present study evaluated a subset of questions (Table 1) contained in this larger survey.

At the local level, the focus was on healthcare facilities, healthcare providers, lactation counselors, and community health workers. At the national level, mass media‐ and social media campaigns were employed to increase awareness, knowledge, and interest in the health behaviors. This dual‐pronged approach targeted both healthcare providers and PLWs to increase interest in both providing and receiving guidance on these health behaviors, respectively.

A step‐wedge cluster randomized control trial design was used, stratified by governorate (Amman, Zarqa, Karak) and by type of health care facility (public/private). A total of 22 facilities were randomly assigned to one of two groups, Cohort I or Cohort II. The original timeline planned for Panel 1 data collection to be conducted in 2021, prior to the intervention start. Panel 2 data was to be collected in 2022, 1 year into the intervention for Cohort I, and prior to Cohort II's intervention start. Panel 3 data was to be collected 2 years into the intervention for Cohort I and 1 year into Cohort II's exposure to the intervention. However, the data collection and intervention timelines were disrupted by the Covid pandemic. As a result, both Panel 1 and Panel 2 data were collected prior to intervention start, in 2021 and 2022, respectively. Panel 3 data was collected in 2023, 1 year after intervention start for Cohort I and prior to the intervention start for Cohort II. Surveys were administered only to married PLW who attended clinics targeted by the Community Health and Nutrition Program.

In preparation for data collection, field supervisors and enumerators underwent a 7‐day training led by Mindset, a Jordan‐based research firm, and study investigators from JNIL. Following their training, enumerators were assessed on their knowledge of the study tools and accuracy and precision in administering the questionnaire. Field supervisors ensured data collection was conducted accurately and according to the study protocol.

Survey Methods and Instruments

The survey instrument was compiled from standardized tools previously validated in Jordan found in the Demographic Health Survey (Department of Statistics and ICF 2019) and the WHO UNICEF infant young child feeding (IYCF) indicator manuals (World Health Organization 2023; World Health Organization, & United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2021)]. The questions on decision‐making were developed through an iterative process of literature review, stakeholder consultation, expert opinion, stakeholder consultation, and field testing. Surveys were prepared in English, translated into Arabic, then back‐translated into English.

Respondents were asked about the six behaviors of interest: dietary diversity for women, early initiation of breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding, provision of appropriate foods for complementary feeding (6–23 month‐olds), minimum meal frequency (6–23 month‐olds), and modern contraceptive use (Table 1). The survey logic used to inquire about these health behaviors is shown in Figure 1. For each behavior, PLW were first asked if they or anyone in their household had received information on the behavior. Those who reported receiving information on a given behavior were then asked if they changed their behavior because of receiving this information, and if yes, “Who decided to make this change?”. PLW were asked to select the individuals who made each decision among (1) self, (2) spouse, (3) mother/mother‐in‐law, and/or (4) other household members. PLW who did not adopt new behaviors were asked to specify the reasons for why not (decision made by other family members, external reasons (described below), etc. The survey instrument is found in Supporting Information S1: Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

We analyzed respondents' decision‐making power (DMP) separately for the six health and nutrition behaviors. We examined several scoring systems to assess the results, including a four‐level indicator (whether PLW had independent DMP, joint DMP with their spouse, joint DMP with someone else, or no DMP over each health behavior), a three‐level indicator (independent, joint, and no DMP); and a binary score (independent or jointly with one or more other individuals [spouse, mother/mother‐in‐law, or other household member] or no DMP). We chose to present the results of the binary scoring, given the theoretical support for this approach (Idris et al. 2023; Mekonen et al. 2024), and relatively small sample sizes across categories in both the 3‐ and 4‐level indicators, where joint spousal DMP accounted for 85% of all shared decisions [range 62% (EIBF) to 98.3% (use of modern contraception)] (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Three rounds of cross‐sectional data were combined for this analysis. We employed multivariate logistic regression models (using Stata/SE18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) to examine the factors associated with respondents' DMP over each health behavior separately. Factors previously shown to be associated with female autonomy in Jordan were incorporated as covariates in regression models, including governorate, nationality, maternal age, maternal education level, wealth (based on principal components analysis defined by DHS (Department of Statistics and ICF 2019), the number of children under 18 years of age, whether or not the respondent had a male child, and whether or not a parent/parent‐in‐law resided in the household (Ait Ali Slimane et al. 2020; Al‐Sheyab et al. 2021; Friedrich et al. 2021; Gender Roles, Women in Decision Making and Violence against Women 2020; International Labour Organization 2022; Khasawneh et al. 2020; Libbus and Kridli 1997; Linos et al. 2010; Oweis et al. 2009; USAID; World Bank 2025). Cohort assignment (I, II, III) and survey panel (2021/2022/2023) were also included in the model to control for time and possible differences in exposure to the Community Health and Nutrition Program. While maternal employment status has also been shown to be associated with female autonomy in Jordan (Department of Statistics Jordan and ICF 2024), the study survey tool did not ask PLW about their employment status, so this covariate could not be included in our analysis. No artificial intelligence‐generated content tools of any kind were used in this manuscript.

Decision to Adopt New Behaviors

The number of PLW who received information on each health and nutrition behavior varied (Table 3A). Most PLW reported that they had received information on modern contraception methods (78%), as well as on early initiation of breastfeeding (59.7%), exclusive breastfeeding (56.8%), complementary feeding (39.9%), minimum meal frequency (31.5%), and maternal dietary diversity (29%). The frequency of behavioral change after receiving information on these behaviors also varied. Many women changed their own diets or those of their children after receiving relevant information: ~93% of PLW began consuming foods from many different food groups and ~81% increased the meal frequency of their young children, and 74% of PLW practiced early initiation of breast‐feeding. However, fewer PLW who received information about other behaviors made changes in their practices: specifically, only 2.6% for complementary feeding and 24.8% for modern contraception. Overall, 76.6% of the respondents changed at least 1 behavior, while 23.4% did not change any of their behaviors.

Decision‐Making Power (DMP) and Choices to Adopt or Reject Behaviors

Next, we reviewed the degree to which the PLW had decision‐making power (DMP) to adopt or reject recommended health and nutrition behaviors after receiving information about these behaviors. The percentage of women who had complete or shared DMP to adopt or reject behavior change varied, from 52.8% (EBF) to 90.3% (DD‐W) (Table 3B). Nearly all PLW who had DMP—and who were not constrained by external circumstances (described below)—chose to adopt recommended behaviors (94.3% to 96.7%). Between 3.2% (child MMF) and 5.7% (DD‐W) changed their behaviors, even when the PLW themselves had no DMP in making those choices. Relatively few women used their DMP to reject the recommended health behaviors. Still, women's own choices accounted for these rejections in nearly all cases (89.8% for use of modern contraception to 100% for EIBF and child MMF), although in some families, other individuals made the decisions to reject the recommended behaviors. Notably, DD‐W recommendations were rejected in 7.4% of households and modern contraception recommendations in 10.2% of household by persons other than the PLW.

Role of External Circumstances in PLW's Rejection of Key Practices

Besides DMP, external factors (“externalities”) prevented some PLW from adopting some of the health and nutrition behaviors, even after hearing recommendations to do so. External barriers prevented 374 of the 2099 (17.8%) PLW who received information about EIBF from engaging in this practice, usually due to maternity hospital practices. For example, PLW stated that their baby was not handed over to them immediately after birth because of medical issues (n = 285), institutional policy (n = 58), and/or because their newborn had already been given formula (n = 71).

Other key behaviors were also affected by external circumstances, even after receiving recommendations. External factors, including lack of adequate support (n = 30), employment constraints (n = 16), and difficulties in breastfeeding (n = 12), prevented 76 (3.8%) PLW from engaging in EBF. Twenty women were unable to increase their diet diversity due to various external obstacles (n = 10) and/or expense (n = 12). Despite receiving information on modern contraception, 40 PLW did not employ these methods within a month of giving birth due to external factors including not having access to modern contraception (n = 22) and the expense (n = 6). Finally, externalities also prevented PLW from following both child CF (n = 7) and MMF (n = 8) recommendations.

Regression Model Results

Next, we examined the factors related to women's DMP as odds ratios (Table 4). Governorate was strongly associated with the women's DMP over health behaviors. Compared to PLW living in Amman, those living in Zarqa had about half the odds of having DMP over decisions to increase their dietary diversity (OR = 0.418, SE = 0.143, p = 0.011) or DMP to practice early initiation of breastfeeding (OR = 0.493, SE = 0.136, p = 0.010). PLW living in Karak had about half the odds (OR = 0.419, SE = 0.116, p = 0.002) of having DMP to begin using modern contraception compared to PLW in Amman. Parity of PLW was also strongly associated with DMP over the decision to engage in EBF: for each additional child, the odds of having DMP over the decision to engage in EBF increased by 18% (OR = 1.18, SE = 0.099, p = 0.047). Panel and cohort did not relate to decision‐making power. Lower education was associated with 81% lower odds of having DMP over child MMF (OR = 0.191, SE = 0.152, p < 0.05).

Discussion

“Gender Equity and Women's Empowerment” is the fifth of 17 Sustainable Development Goals set by the United Nations to be achieved by 2030 (UNFPA 2020; United Nations 2022). Success in attaining gender equality and women's empowerment could contribute to the achievement of other goals, such as the elimination of stunting and the reduction of maternal and child mortality (Mekonen et al. 2024). In this study, we assessed the involvement of 3518 Jordanian married PLW in their recent decisions to adopt or reject behavior changes promoted by a national community health and nutrition intervention. The intervention did not address DMP or other aspects of autonomy. Shared decision‐making with husbands ranged from 62% (early initiation of breast feeding) to 98.3% (modern contraceptive use).

Overall, Jordanian PLW had high levels of DMP, whether they decided to adopt or reject the behaviors promoted by the Community Health and Nutrition program. A sizable proportion of the women surveyed already engaged in desirable behaviors promoted by the intervention. For women who had DMP, most used this power to adopt behavior changes promoted by the intervention (94.3% to 96.7% of those who made behavior changes). Although relatively few women rejected the behaviors, the percentage of women who made these decisions themselves or in partnership ranged from 89.8% to 100%. Over 10% of women had no DMP for the use of modern contraception. Overall, 76.6% of the respondents chose to change at least 1 behavior, while 23.4% opted not to change any of their behaviors.

Based on regression models (Table 4), DMP was associated with specific health behaviors, location of residence (governate), parity, and maternal education. Compared to women in Amman, women in Zarka had lower odds of having DMP to engage in recommended EIBF and DD‐W behaviors, while women in Karak were less likely to adopt modern contraception. The differences by region were not surprising; the sociodemographic characteristics of women in Amman differ from those in Karak and Zarka. Amman is the capital of Jordan and its population is more urban and cosmopolitan compared to the more rural, peripheral cities of Zarka and Karak. However, the specificity of DMP in different domains in each governate was not expected. Additionally, the odds of choosing to practice EBF significantly increased related to the women's parity (1.18‐fold for each additional child), possibly because mother's personal experiences were more likely to be incorporated into her choices as she had more children. Her own increasing age (and probable prior successful breastfeeding experience) also likely contributed to these choices. However, the relationship of parity to EBF was not seen for EIBF or other MICYF behaviors, suggesting that these activities remain targets for further intervention even among multiparous women. Finally, women with lower levels of education had lower odds of following MMF recommendations for their young children.

In contrast to reports from other countries (Chol et al. 2019; Idris et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2017), the wealth index in Jordan did not relate to women's DMP for any of the domains. This disparity likely stems from complex factors related to infrastructure, policy, regulation, and socio‐economic and cultural factors in the MENA region (Albaddaw and Sipos 2025). As in other MENA countries, a deeply ingrained patriarchal system ranks women's duties toward the family higher than their rights as individuals (Zuhur 2003). In Jordan, the rapid reduction of the gender gap in education experienced in the last decades has been slow to be carried over into areas of social and economic life (Edres 2022). Gender research in Jordan and other MENA countries has not cited increasing household wealth as a pathway to expand women's decision‐making power (Al‐Serhan 2021; AlAzzawi and Hlasny 2019; Aref and Fallentine 2023; Edres 2022; Haghighat 2013; Zuhur 2003). Indeed, the higher the wealth index of a woman's household, the less likely the woman is to participate in the labor force (AlAzzawi and Hlasny 2019). Rather, women's empowerment in the region is still hindered by the need to enforce equal opportunity laws and regulations, and address cultural, social, and political norms that create barriers to women's participation in the economy and their personal autonomy (Aref and Fallentine 2023).

In recognition of the importance of MIYCF behaviors to future population health and well‐being, the Jordanian government has endorsed strong recommendations and proposed actions to improve these practices, via several national strategies (Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; Nutrition Working Group 2019; United Nations Jordan 2021; World Health Organization and Health 2006). Most of these policies also target MIYCF practices among the large Syrian refugee population in Jordan, which overall has worse nutrition indicators than the settled population (Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; Nutrition Working Group 2019). Many of these policies and actions have paid off, with notable improvements especially in rates of child stunting and wasting over the past ~20 years (United Nations Jordan 2021) and reduction in iodine deficiency (Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021).

Despite these improvements, other indicators have recently deteriorated in Jordan, in both refugee (Shaker‐Berbari et al. 2021) and non‐refugee populations (Ghosh et al. 2024; Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021). Increased rates low birth weight (Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; United Nations Jordan 2021) and reduced adherence to MICYF recommendations (Al‐Awwad et al. 2021; Ghosh et al. 2024; United Nations Jordan 2021) have been noted especially in refugee households where the husband or family members other than the mother made decisions on health care access (Shaker‐Berbari et al. 2021). Breast‐feeding indicators have been particularly poor (Al‐Awwad et al. 2021; Fendi et al. 2023; Ghosh et al. 2024; Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021; Khasawneh et al. 2020; Oweis et al. 2009), and poor diet and micronutrient deficiencies have increased among children and women of reproductive age. Concurrently, Jordan, like other MENA countries, is undergoing a dietary transition, with major changes in nutrition and personal habits and corresponding increases in overweight and obesity (Ghosh et al. 2024; Jordan Ministry of Health ‐ Nutrition Department and World Health Organization 2021). At present, Jordan is ‘off course’ to meet SDG targets for MIYCN (United Nations Jordan 2021).

Many of these trends seem to be paradoxical, given the improving socio‐economic indicators and the high educational achievements of women in Jordan. Although education is culturally valued for both males and females, this view does not consistently translate into employment opportunities for women. Instead, social expectations prioritize marriage and domestic roles over professional engagement for women. Thus, educational achievement alone is insufficient to overcome local gender norms, particularly in rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged areas. Cultural norms continue to shape family expectations and decision‐making structures, and often limit expectations for women's economic independence and participation in decision‐making despite legal frameworks which formally guarantee gender equality in education and employment. At the policy level, some of these issues could be addressed by labor market reforms to incentivize female employment and provide more female‐friendly work environments while social reforms could tackle the notable underutilization of human capital.

A better understanding of the dynamics driving diet and health behaviors among Jordanian women in this period of nutrition transition is needed, particularly cultural traditions. Grandmothers, mothers, mothers‐in‐law, and other family members are expected to contribute to many household decisions, including behaviors and practices of PLW. At the same time, the participation of married women in personal and child‐related decision‐making is not assured. Attention to this dynamic is essential to promoting recommended MIYCN behaviors, while respecting customs and cultural traditions. Although we found that many women independently or in partnership with their husbands adopted recommended MIYCN practices, future interventions which include their mothers, mothers‐in‐law, and husbands in educational efforts may further increase positive changes in Jordan and in other cultures where women's voices may not be heard. Finally, the psychological, political, and legal dimensions of women's DMP must be considered in designing appropriate interventions along with the importance of examining gender inequality through a sociocultural and structural lens rather than attributing disparities solely to individual choice or capability (Pratley 2016).

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had several strengths but also some limitations. We systematically collected data from 3518 Jordanian married PLW over 3 years in three different governates in Jordan. In contrast to other studies of autonomy, we focused on recent decisions and relevant behavior changes rather than theoretical examples of what the women might encounter or choose to do in a hypothetical scenario. We focused on current or very recent pregnancy, lactation, and feeding choices for themselves and their young infants plus the use of modern contraceptives. We analyzed each domain separately, allowing us to discern the relationship of DMP to specific health behaviors (Shroff et al. 2009). We did not address autonomy in financial decisions or freedom of movement in favor of examining elements directly related to MIYCN and contraceptive choices. Many studies of women's autonomy address reproductive health, while neglecting other maternal health issues (Osamor and Grady 2016). Likewise, many studies of autonomy do not address IYCF practices, as “nearly all” mothers report that they contributed to these decisions (Heckert et al. 2019). However, this was not the case in Jordan. These results from Jordan might be applicable to other MENA countries, and also possibly to Middle‐Eastern women who have immigrated to western countries, as immigrants usually retain their prior cultural beliefs, practices, and perceptions (Oweis et al. 2009).

Possible limitations include the cross‐sectional nature of the panels, as women may change their attitudes and perceptions over time. However, we included a broad range of women of differing parity, socioeconomic status, education levels, and location of residence. Our study focused only on women's perceptions. Male family members, especially husbands, may have had different perceptions about household decision‐making dynamics, or alternatively, may have been in agreement with their wives (Brunson et al. 2009; Osamor and Grady 2016). A multi‐informant approach addressing these questions would be enlightening. Some researchers suggest that women who have internalized cultural norms which assign them low status may have altered their own perceptions or description of their actual autonomy (Carlson et al. 2015). Additionally, feminist literature suggests that some women accustom themselves to expressing disempowerment as a means of leveraging their power elsewhere (Salinger et al. 2024). Moreover, women's choices about autonomy are important to decision‐making only if they prefer to making these choices (Salinger et al. 2024) and if the choices are feasible financially, materially (e.g., access to needed items or services [modern contraception, appropriate complementary foods for the infant]), and absence of deterrents (e.g., breast infections or other medical problems hindering breast feeding). We were unable to evaluate these possibilities.

The survey did not collect data on women's employment status, so we were unable to include this variable in our analyses. This is a significant exclusion, given that women's employment has consistently proven to have a positive impact on female autonomy in numerous settings (Acharya et al. 2010; Cunningham et al. 2015; Ristiana and Handayani 2018). Meanwhile, Jordan continues to have a relatively low rate of female labor force participation, estimated to be under 14% as of 2023 (World Bank Group, 2023). As concerted efforts are made to double Jordan's female labor force participation by 2033 (World Bank Group, 2023), an understanding of how this may influence women's autonomy over health, nutrition, and other behaviors would be particularly valuable.

Other variables not addressed in the survey included female decision‐making power over household financial decisions, women's individual financial autonomy and mobility, and husband's occupations (Idris et al. 2023; Osamor and Grady 2016). These variables would have all been helpful to include in our analyses. Additionally, we recognize that the use of a binary score to assess women's DMP—although supported in the literature (Idris et al. 2023; Mekonen et al. 2024)—may have oversimplified potentially complex family dynamics. We also recognize that the intensity and timing of exposure to the intervention (which we did not measure), as well as other factors, could also have affected choices to change behaviors.

Conclusions

Our survey found that Jordanian PLW had high levels of DMP, and most used this power to adopt behavior changes promoted by the intervention (94.3% to 96.7% of those who made behavior changes). However, over 10% of women had no DMP for the use of modern contraception. Nationality, governate of residence, and parity were associated with DMP for different health and nutrition behaviors. Our results indicate that certain levels of wealth and education may be necessary but are not sufficient to improve and sustain adoption of best behaviors; future interventions must account for these constraints. Combining questions on DMP with information on control of resources would enhance future survey instruments. Future interventions should be based on understanding of DMP patterns within households, as decisions to change behaviors are not the exclusive right of women in many parts of the world.
