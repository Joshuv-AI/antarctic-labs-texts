Top of basilar syndrome due to vertebral artery dissection: How high-resolution MRI and CD31 analysis of thrombus could help
Authors: Yu Wang, Yao Zhang, Yang Wang, He Liu, Yunpeng Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceVertebral artery dissection is a rare but serious condition that can lead to neurological deficits and even death. It is commonly associated with trauma or underlying vascular diseases. Top of basilar syndrome (TOBS) is a neurological condition that can result from vertebral artery dissection, either by direct ischemia or secondary occlusion due to distal embolization of injured inner vascular tissue. We here present a patient who was initially suffering from seizure then had TOBS due to a progressive vertebral artery dissection, with emphasis of high-resolution MRI imaging and immunohistochemistry study of the thrombus.Case presentationA young male presented to the emergency department with sudden onset of seizure. A history of recent neck exercise was reported. The patient had gaze palsy, adduction disability, and dysarthria. High-resolution MRI showed a vertebral artery dissection with evidence of occlusion of the top of basilar artery. After the thrombectomy, a pathologic study revealed CD31[+] tissue in the thrombus, indicating an origin from dissected vertebral artery.Clinical discussion and conclusionIt's important to recognize and treat vertebral artery dissection promptly due to its varying symptoms. Using high-resolution MRI at early stage and conducting pathologic analysis of CD31 on the thrombus can assist in the diagnosis, potentially leading to more precise treatment plans and better outcomes for patients.

Introduction

Vertebral artery dissection is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition that can result in neurological deficits and even death [1]. Trauma or underlying vascular diseases are commonly associated with this condition [2]. Top of basilar syndrome (TOBS), a neurological condition caused by impaired blood flow to the brainstem and cerebellum, can result from vertebral artery dissection either by direct ischemia or secondary occlusion due to drop-off of injured inner vascular tissue [3].

A timely and accurate diagnosis is crucial in managing this condition, and recent advances in diagnostic techniques such as high-resolution MRI have greatly improved our ability to identify the extent and location of the dissection [4]. This non-invasive imaging modality can detect even subtle changes in the vertebral artery and provide valuable information for treatment planning, especially the intravascular therapy.

Furthermore, the pathology of thrombosis, particularly the use of CD31 staining, is also an important diagnostic tool in identifying the components of the thrombus, which hold the potential to guide appropriate management and prevent recurrence [[5], [6], [7]]. In our case report, CD31 staining of the embolus confirmed the origin of the dissection in the vertebral artery.

Our case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in the diagnosis and management of vertebral artery dissection, with the integration of advanced diagnostic tools such as high-resolution MRI and thrombosis pathology. By utilizing these techniques, we could improve our ability to make an accurate and timely diagnosis, leading to better patient outcomes.

The case report is reported in line with the SCARE criteria and the PROCESS criteria [8,9].

Case presentation

A 29-year-old male without significant past medical history was brought to the emergency department. The patient's primary symptoms included twitching in his bilateral upper and left lower extremities, accompanied by a loss of consciousness. These symptoms had been occurring intermittently for 10 h, with seizure attacks presenting every 10–20 min, each lasting for approximately 10 s. The patient began smoking at the age of 11 and has an 18 pack-year history of smoking, due to the lax regulations on tobacco sales to adolescents in his hometown. He also has a 10-year history of alcohol consumption. A non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain was conducted after his arrival, and no hemorrhage but suspicious dot-like infarction was found at the level of midbrain (Fig. 1a). The patient was diagnosed as epilepsy for unknown reason at this time, and phenobarbital was administered for restriction of seizure attack. The computed tomography perfusion (CTP) imaging of the patient at the same day of arrival indicated hypoperfusion on his left cerebellar hemisphere and pons (Fig. 1b), and the results of computed tomography angiography (CTA) imaging of his cerebral arteries suggested a mild stenosis and irregular lumen of the V3 section of left vertebral artery, and a severe stenosis of basilar artery apex and the origins of posterior cerebral arteries on both sides. Also, a fenestration at the beginning of basilar artery was noticed (Fig. 1c). The laboratory examination found no specific positive result unless mildly increased levels of aminotransferases. Antiplatelet and fluid resuscitation were administered at this timepoint.

One day later, the patient developed left-oriented gaze palsy, ptosis and adduction disability of the left eye, moderately decreased muscle strength of the left upper arm, and dysarthria. All symptoms indicate damage to the pons and the midbrain. A 3.0-Tesla, high-resolution magnetic resolution imaging (HR-MRI) of the intracranial and cervical structure was conducted in the third day of admission. The black blood sequence of HR-MRI identified a dissection injury of V3 segment of left vertebral artery, along with abnormal signal in the basilar artery apex and the beginnings of bilateral posterior cerebral arteries in the time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (TOF-MRA) sequence (Fig. 1d), and acute infarcts on pons, left cerebellum, left occipital lobe and right thalamus were found due to altered signals in diffuse-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences. Thus, diagnosis of embolism on the basilar artery, along with the “top of the basilar syndrome” was almost clear. The embolus was highly suspected to be originated from the dissection the left vertebral artery.

As the patient's symptoms suddenly progressed and an abnormal signal at the apex of the basilar artery was identified on HR-MRI, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was conducted immediately to ascertain the extent of the issue and determine the appropriate intervention. An occlusion of upper part of basilar artery, and dissection of V3 segment of left vertebral artery were found, accompanied by an incomplete compensatory blood supply from bilateral internal carotid arteries, however there was poor visualization of posterior communicating arteries, indicating insufficient compensation. Following DSA, the intracranial access system (Neuron Max, Penumbra Inc., CA, USA) was introduced to V2 segment, then a 5-F suction catheter (Zylox-Tonbridge, Hangzhou, China), together with a microcatheter surpassed the lesion at V3 with the help of micro-guide wire, towards the top of basilar artery. An aspiration thrombectomy was conducted, and two pieces of clots in dark red color were extracted and preserved in 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for pathologic analysis. The basilar artery and bilateral posterior cerebral arteries were recanalized thereafter. Due to the nature of vascular dissection, stent insertion was considered for the purpose to avoid the impact of rough inner surface of the vessel. Two stents (Enterprise 4.0 mm × 39 mm, Cordis Neurovascular, FL, USA) were released with partial overlap to cover the whole section of dissection. After the operation, antiplatelet (Aspirin and Clopidogrel), atorvastatin, as well as antiepileptic drug were administered. One day after, it was observed that the patient got improved consciousness and muscle strength of left extremities, fully relieved gaze palsy. However, the ptosis and adduction disability of the left eye remained as before. By another time of questioning the patient's medical history, it was reported and confirmed that there had been intense neck movements the night before the onset of symptoms, which could be considered as a direct cause of the disease. After the patient got fully aware, he recalled he had a strenuous neck exercise, mainly Kung-Fu like quick movements of head and trunk in an uncoordinated way, at the evening before the symptom onset. 10 days after the operation, the patient was discharged from the hospital for further rehabilitative therapy.

To further clarify the origin of the thrombus, the clots extracted during the operation, was sent for pathologic analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H-E) staining indicated mixed structure with red blood cells and platelets (Fig. 3a). Immunohistochemistry staining for CD31, a biomarker of vascular endothelial cells, found positive stained, line-shape tissue, indicating the thrombus might be shedded from the dissected lesion of vertebral artery (Fig. 3b).Fig. 3The pathologic analysis of collected thrombus. Discussion

Cerebral artery dissection is recognized as a cause of ischemic stroke, particularly among young patients with no previous history of atrial fibrillation or atherosclerosis. The vertebral artery is notably vulnerable. According to Urasyanandana et al., less than half of the cases with cerebral artery dissection experience intracranial hemorrhage [10]. Studies have found that cervical injury, and excessive exercise, are two major causes of dissection of vertebral artery. For instance, Jones et al. reported young woman had cerebellar infarction due to vertebral artery dissection after a neck manipulation by her chiropractor [11]. In this case, the patient had a history of neck exercise before the seizure attack, although it was not acknowledged to his physician at the very beginning. Schlemn et al., by reviewing 190 cases of cervical artery dissection after sports, found the that swimming, jogging and combat were among the top three sports causing dissection [12]. About vertebral artery dissection, the impact of mastoid region by ball or puck, rapid movement like golf swing, and held position in yoga or fitness class were common causes [1]. The dissection of vertebral-basilar artery could lead to ischemia or infarction of brain stem, sometimes a classical “top of the basilar syndrome” could be observed, as known as rostral brain stem infarction, with visual or oculomotor deficits, behavioral abnormalities, reduced level of consciousness, like somnolence, and sometimes motor dysfunction [13,14]. In this specific case, the patient had seizure at the beginning, with transient unconsciousness. Clinical manifestations, including left-oriented gaze, ptosis and adduction disability of his left eye, reduced muscle strength, as well as dysarthria, were happened after the epilepsy already got controlled. All the symptoms and signs indicated a progressive lesion of brain stem. For a patient with a history of intense neck movements, either active or passive, a cerebral vascular injury, especially the dissection of vertebral/basilar artery, should be considered. The application of HR-MRI and CD31 staining in this case provided invaluable insights into the underlying pathology, enhancing our understanding of the clinical scenario and enriching the academic discourse surrounding similar complex cases.

In this case, HR-MRI clearly identified the dissection of the V3 segment of the left vertebral artery and diagnosed the brain stem infarction. The utilization of HR-MRI highlighted its underestimated yet significant role in acute ischemic stroke evaluation. This radiologic finding is significant as it not only highlights the existence of dissection, assisting physicians in evaluating potential surgical approaches to the lesion, but also suggests the likelihood of an arterial embolization causing the ischemic stroke. Vertebral artery dissection is usually correlated with acute ischemic stroke, and HR-MRI is among the most accurate diagnostic methods. According to Yun et al., for 29 patients with vertebral artery dissection, 13 were found to have acute ischemic stroke at the meantime, together with smaller mean length of dissection and less common presentation of aneurysmal dilation found in HR-MRI results, compared to dissection patients without stroke [15]. Also, intraluminal thrombus was commonly happened in patients of cervicocranial artery dissection with stroke symptoms at onset [16]. However, in the current case, the patients presented “top of basilar syndrome”, because of infarction to the rostral brain stem, suddenly happened after the patient got recovered from seizure. It is crucial to note that the brain stem infarction might not be solely attributed to the thrombosis of the vertebral artery, but also consider the presence of a thrombus shed from the dissected arterial wall. However, it should be noted that patient suitability for HR-MRI is dependent on their overall medical condition and ability to undergo longer examination times. In most cases, conventional imaging modalities, like MRI or CTA and CTP need to be firstly considered. A thorough assessment of each patient's individual needs and medical history is necessary to determine the most appropriate diagnostic approach for acute ischemic stroke.

The pathologic analysis of thrombus, extracted during mechanical thrombectomy of patients with ischemic stroke, could provide advanced information of vascular lesion. The major components in thrombi of acute ischemic stroke include red blood cells, platelets, leukocytes, fibrin and von Willebrand factor [17]. For example, thrombus from patients with cardioembolism contained higher level of platelet, while clot(s) from patients with large artery atherosclerosis had higher level of red blood cells [17]. A recent study found higher level of DNA, as well as lower level of glycoprotein VI (GPVI) in thrombi collected from stroke patients with cardioembolic origin, which might reflect higher leukocyte and neutrophil extracellular traps content [18]. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that the metabolomics of thrombus was different among patients in different subtypes. By mass spectrometry, they identified 6 metabolites, including diglyceride, phytosphingosine, etc., were significantly different between large artery atherosclerosis group and cardioembolic group [19]. CD31 is also known as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1), which is a transmembrane immunoglobulin-like glycoprotein [20]. It is recognized as a biomarker for the existence of endothelial cells [21]. However, the application of CD31 in identification of thrombus origin is rarely reported. In this case, we stained CD31 on collected clots, due to the suspicion of shedded tissue fragments from ruptured dissection of vertebral artery. We found line-shape CD31[+] tissue in thrombus, which might indicate its artery wall origin. In patients with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction, higher level of CD31 was observed in plaque specimen with ruptured fibrous cap, and the exposure of CD31 might amplify platelet adhesion and promoting inflammatory cascade [22], and some study have found the that the anti-CD31 antibody had effects in inhibition of platelet aggregation [23]. We believe immunohistochemistry study of endothelial tissue, especially in the thrombus, might give assistance to physicians to determine the exact cause of cerebral infarction, and provide instruction for following treatment. Moreover, since CD31 hold the ability to recruit immune components, the quantification of CD31 in thrombi collected from different patients might be a potential pathologic biomarker for local inflammation, and an indicator for prognosis.

About therapeutic strategy for this patient, it should be addressed that after comparatively thorough clinical and radiologic examinations, the mechanical thrombectomy for basilar artery occlusion, and stent insertion for vertebral arterial dissection, might provide fundamental benefits for the ideal prognosis. Two stents covering the whole segment of the stenosis could help to prevent the pathological progression of vascular dissection and secondary infarction to the basilar artery. For patients with basilar artery occlusion, pooled data from large retrospective studies showed 36.4 % favorable outcome (mRS, modified Rankin Scale: 0–2) at 3 months, and 4.6 % symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, while the prospective studies found similar outcome for about 30 % favorable outcome [24]. A recent multi-center study on patients with basilar artery occlusion, with thrombectomy conducted 6–24 h after stroke onset, showed that thrombectomy, compared to medical therapy alone, had higher percentage with good functional statues (mRS: 0–3, at 90 days), which gave implications for a broaden therapeutic window [25]. However, due the results from this study, only 6 % patients got a mRS 0, which meant no disability. Thus, the management of patients with basilar artery occlusion remains a challenge for clinicians, more rapid and accurate diagnosis, as well as adjuvant therapy and effective rehabilitative intervention, are necessary for the purpose to improve the general prognosis.

We have to admit that there are several restrictions of current case. To begin with, the initial diagnosis for the patient was idiopathic epilepsy, while in fact the CTA and CTP results at the day of his arrival indicated a possible diagnosis of dissection, thus maybe an earlier DSA might be more helpful. Next, it should be noticed that the intra-arterial operation could also break the integrity of vascular endothelium, therefore introduce endothelial fragments to the distal area along the blood flow. Also in this case, we could not exclude that the finding of line-shape, CD31[+] tissue in the clots might be due to the rupture of the dissected structure of vertebral artery, although it was relatively unlikely since the patient suffered from a sudden aggravation of neurological symptoms. Future study on the pathologic nature of cerebral vascular thrombus, particularly with dissected vascular components, could focus on fast intraoperative pathologic analysis, and the exploration the specific local immune responses between the shedded tissue and occluded vascular segment, which might help the physician to identify the direct origin of endothelial cells in the clots, and offer instructions for following medication.

Conclusion

In this case report and literature review, we presented a patient diagnosed with TOBS resulting from occlusion of the basal artery due to traumatic vertebral artery dissection. The patient underwent successful neurointerventional surgery and demonstrated a favorable prognosis. Utilizing HR-MRI in conjunction with thrombus pathologic analysis using CD31, an endothelial cell marker, allowed for precise lesion identification. Looking ahead, we identify two critical avenues for future exploration: firstly, a deeper investigation into HR-MRI to discern its potential correlation with disease severity and prognosis, and secondly, an expanded immunohistochemical analysis of thrombus to determine its origin and elucidate the causes of ischemic strokes more comprehensively. The integration of these advanced diagnostic modalities promises to enhance our understanding and management of ischemic cerebral vascular diseases.
