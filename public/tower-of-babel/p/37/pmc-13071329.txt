A Pangenome Framework Reveals Conserved Pathogenicity‐Associated Biosynthetic Gene Clusters in Fusarium equiseti, a Root Rot Pathogen of Panax notoginseng
Authors: Xuping Zhang, Xiang Li, Xiaojun Li, Chao Peng, Weiguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Panax notoginseng (Chinese ginseng, sanqi) is an economically and medicinally important herb whose cultivation is increasingly threatened by destructive soilborne root rot. Although Fusarium species represent the dominant pathogens in this disease complex, the virulence determinants underlying Fusarium–P. notoginseng interactions remain largely unknown, particularly the contribution of secondary metabolite (SM) biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in medicinal plants. Here, we present a pangenome‐informed and experimentally validated framework linking Fusarium SM to disease development. By constructing a genus‐scale BGC landscape from 54 Fusarium genomes, integrating it with infection‐stage transcriptomics of Fusarium equiseti P26, and establishing an efficient CRISPR/Cas9‐based genome‐editing system, we functionally prioritized and interrogated conserved, infection‐induced BGCs from dozens of predicted clusters. This integrative pipeline identified two broadly conserved and infection‐essential BGCs that are indispensable for disease development, including an evolutionarily conserved ICS–NRPS hybrid cluster and a ferrichrome‐type siderophore NRPS cluster widely distributed across Fusarium species. Notably, the pathogenic contribution of the ICS–NRPS pathway was independently corroborated in Fusarium oxysporum, supporting a conserved role for this BGC class across the genus. Together, our findings provide the first pangenome‐guided gene‐to‐phenotype dissection of SM‐associated virulence in P. notoginseng root rot, establishing conserved SM biosynthetic backbones as causal pathogenicity determinants and tractable targets for disrupting Fusarium disease development across BGC‐rich fungal genomes.

Introduction

Panax notoginseng (Chinese ginseng, sanqi) is a perennial medicinal herb in the family Araliaceae and a cornerstone of traditional Chinese medicine. Cultivated primarily in Yunnan Province, China, its roots are widely used for their haemostatic, anti‐inflammatory and cardioprotective activities and are increasingly incorporated into modern pharmaceutical products (Wang et al. 2016; Mancuso 2024). As commercial cultivation has expanded, however, soilborne diseases have emerged as major constraints on production. Among them, root rot is the most destructive: under continuous cropping, disease incidence often becomes high, severely reducing yield and compromising medicinal quality, thereby threatening the sustainability of commercial P. notoginseng production (Li et al. 2022; Han et al. 2025).

Across major production regions, Fusarium species are consistently associated with root rot of P. notoginseng and are widely recognized as predominant and aggressive causal agents (Mi et al. 2017; Ning et al. 2021; Jin et al. 2018; Cai et al. 2023). More broadly, Fusarium ranks among the most important genera of plant‐pathogenic fungi worldwide, owing in part to its capacity to deploy chemically diverse secondary metabolites (SM) that function as virulence factors and facilitate host colonization and disease development (Summerell 2019; Li et al. 2025; Pontes et al. 2020). Canonical examples include trichothecenes (e.g., deoxynivalenol) and fumonisins, which interfere with host immunity and disrupt cellular homeostasis (Brown et al. 2021; Bin‐Umer et al. 2011; Kumar et al. 2024). The biosynthesis of these metabolites is typically encoded by specialized SM biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), positioning BGCs as central genetic determinants of toxigenic potential and, in many cases, pathogenicity (Keller 2015; Rokas et al. 2020).

Despite this foundation, establishing causal links between Fusarium BGCs and disease phenotypes remains challenging. The genus encompasses more than 400 phylogenetically distinct species, with hundreds of genomes now sequenced, yet we still lack a systematic, pangenome‐level understanding of how BGC diversity is distributed across species and which cluster families are conserved, lineage‐enriched, or uniquely associated with pathogenic lifestyles (Meng et al. 2024; Yang 2024). Moreover, although Fusarium genomes often encode dozens of SM BGCs, only a small fraction have been functionally characterized, and it is rarely clear which metabolite(s) drive virulence in a specific host context. These gaps are particularly pronounced for medicinal plants, where Fusarium–host interactions remain far less studied than in model plants and major crops (Xu et al. 2022; Iqbal et al. 2024; Buttar et al. 2024). Consequently, the virulence factors and mycotoxins underlying Fusarium‐induced root rot in P. notoginseng are largely unexplored (Li et al. 2024). In this context, pangenome analysis offers a powerful framework to capture shared genomic features across related pathogens, providing a rational and scalable route to prioritize conserved, pathogenicity‐associated gene clusters for functional interrogation.

Within the P. notoginseng root‐rot pathosystem, Fusarium oxysporum and Fusarium solani are often cited as primary causal agents, yet increasing evidence indicates that additional Fusarium taxa contribute to disease and may have been underappreciated (Ma et al. 2019; Fang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024). One such species is Fusarium equiseti, a member of the Fusarium incarnatum–equiseti species complex, which is recognized as a soilborne pathogen of cereals, vegetables and medicinal plants, including P. notoginseng (Bai et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2020; Gu et al. 2025). Emerging work suggests that F. equiseti harbours a versatile SM repertoire and produces multiple classes of mycotoxins implicated in fungal–host interactions (Zhang et al. 2025; Avila et al. 2019). Yet, despite its apparent pathogenic and toxigenic potential, the molecular mechanisms by which F. equiseti promotes root rot in P. notoginseng remain poorly resolved (Li et al. 2024).

A key obstacle is causality: how can one move from a genome rich in uncharacterized BGCs to the specific clusters that actually drive disease? In F. equiseti, as in many Fusarium pathogens, dozens of predicted SM BGCs complicate target selection, and classical chemical genetics approaches are slow and often confounded by silent clusters. Functional genetics has also lagged, due in part to technical limitations, including the absence of robust and stable genome‐editing systems in some isolates. Consequently, the field has frequently relied on correlative evidence based on genome content, metabolite profiles and transcriptional induction, rather than on direct gene‐to‐phenotype validation required to define toxin‐mediated virulence in this medicinal‐plant disease system.

Here, we address these challenges by integrating comparative genomics, infection‐stage transcriptomics and reverse genetics to identify pathogenicity‐associated BGCs in F. equiseti. We performed a pangenome‐based analysis of Fusarium secondary metabolism to survey BGC diversity across the genus and organize clusters into families based on backbone‐gene homology, thereby defining conserved and core BGCs. By coupling this framework with transcriptome profiling during host infection, we prioritized candidate BGCs most likely to contribute to pathogenicity in P. notoginseng. To establish causality, we developed an efficient CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome‐editing platform in F. equiseti and systematically disrupted backbone genes in selected candidate clusters, followed by phenotypic characterization and pathogenicity assays. This integrative approach identified two BGCs that are essential for disease development, including an isocyanide synthase–nonribosomal peptide synthetase (ICS–NRPS) hybrid cluster (present in 43 of 54 analysed Fusarium genomes) and a ferrichrome‐type siderophore NRPS cluster (present in 51 of 54 genomes). Moreover, the essential contribution of the ICS–NRPS‐type cluster to pathogenicity was independently supported in F. oxysporum, pointing to a conserved role for this BGC class in Fusarium–plant interactions. Together, our work establishes a pangenome‐informed and experimentally validated route to link secondary metabolism with virulence and provides a mechanistic foothold for understanding toxin‐associated root rot in an economically important medicinal plant.

Results

Morphological Characteristics, Pathogenicity and Genome‐Wide Prediction of SM BGC in F. equiseti P26

Fusarium equiseti P26 was isolated from diseased roots of P. notoginseng (Liu et al. 2024). When grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA), P26 formed white to pale yellow aerial mycelia with concentric growth rings, while the reverse side of the colony displayed pronounced yellow‐brown pigmentation. Microscopic examination revealed abundant septate hyphae and curved, multicellular conidia, consistent with the typical morphological characteristics of Fusarium species (Figure 1a). The pathogenicity of F. equiseti P26 was evaluated using detached leaves, root slices and intact roots of P. notoginseng. Compared with mock‐inoculated controls, tissues inoculated with P26 developed pronounced disease symptoms, including extensive mycelial colonization, tissue browning and progressive maceration. In whole root assays, longitudinal sectioning revealed severe internal decay in infected roots, whereas control roots remained healthy and intact (Figure 1b), demonstrating the pathogenic capacity of F. equiseti P26 toward P. notoginseng.

To assess the SM biosynthetic potential of F. equiseti P26, SM BGCs were predicted from the genome using FunBGCeX (Tang and Matsuda 2024). A total of 50 putative BGCs were identified, including 13 terpene cyclase (TC) clusters, 7 highly reducing polyketide synthase (HR‐PKS) clusters, 7 nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) clusters, 4 NRPS‐like clusters, 5 hybrid clusters, 4 prenyltransferase (PT) clusters, 3 isocyanide synthase (ICS)‐containing clusters and 7 clusters belonging to other categories (Figure 1c; Table S1). Several predicted BGCs showed similarity to clusters associated with previously reported fungal SMs, including known mycotoxins (Table S1). Independent prediction with antiSMASH (Kai et al. 2025) yielded similar backbone class distributions, despite differences in total cluster counts, and consistently identified the key prioritized clusters (Table S2). Together, these results indicate that F. equiseti P26 harbours a diverse repertoire of SM BGCs, highlighting its substantial biosynthetic capacity. However, the evolutionary conservation and relative importance of these backbone enzymes cannot be inferred from a single genome alone. To determine which components represent broadly conserved biosynthetic backbones within the genus and which are lineage‐specific or accessory, a comparative analysis across multiple Fusarium species is required.

Pangenome Analysis Reveals Conserved Core SM Biosynthetic Backbone Genes Across Fusarium Species

To place the SM biosynthetic repertoire of F. equiseti P26 into an evolutionary context, we performed a genus‐wide comparative analysis across 54 Fusarium species. Sequence similarity network (SSN) analysis of all identified SM biosynthetic backbone enzymes revealed that the two largest network clusters corresponded to PKS and NRPS enzymes, indicating that these backbone classes represent the most abundant and diverse components of the Fusarium SM biosynthetic repertoire (Figure S1).

To systematically resolve the conservation patterns of these backbone enzymes, OrthoFinder‐based orthologous clustering was performed across the 54 Fusarium genomes, yielding a total of 101 orthologous groups (OGs). Based on their presence–absence distributions, these OGs were classified into core, accessory and species‐specific categories. Thirty‐two OGs were conserved in more than 80% of the analysed species and were therefore defined as core backbone enzymes, whereas four OGs were detected exclusively in a single species and were classified as species‐specific. The remaining 65 OGs exhibited intermediate conservation and were designated as accessory backbone enzymes. Functional annotation of the core backbone enzyme set revealed a strong enrichment of NRPSs (n = 10) and PKSs (n = 7), together with 12 terpene biosynthesis‐related enzyme OGs, two isocyanide synthase (ICS) OGs, and one hybrid NRPS–PKS OG. Within F. equiseti P26, 30 OGs corresponding to these core backbone enzymes were identified, including 15 NRPSs, 12 PKSs, 13 terpene‐related enzymes and two ICSs. Together, these results indicate that F. equiseti P26 retains a broad and diverse set of conserved SM biosynthetic backbones shared across the Fusarium genus (Figure 2).

Transcriptome Profiling During Infection Identifies Infection‐Associated SM BGCs

To examine transcriptional changes associated with host infection, RNA‐seq analysis was conducted on F. equiseti P26 cultured in the presence or absence of P. notoginseng root tissues. Comparative analysis identified 4143 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 2516 upregulated and 1627 downregulated genes under infection conditions (Figure S2). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that genes involved in SM biosynthesis were significantly over‐represented among the DEGs (Figure 3a). At the level of SM BGCs, more than half of the predicted backbone genes were significantly upregulated during infection, indicating broad activation of SM biosynthesis. The strongly induced backbone genes were predominantly associated with ICS, NRPS and PKS classes, which largely correspond to conserved backbone types identified in the pangenome analysis. Integration of transcriptomic and pangenomic data further revealed that a substantial fraction of these infection‐induced backbone genes belonged to conserved core orthologous groups shared across the Fusarium genus. Among them, g7615, encoding an ICS–NRPS hybrid backbone, exhibited the strongest induction (11.1‐fold increase) relative to the control (Figure 3b). Based on combined criteria of strong infection‐associated induction and evolutionary conservation, g7615, g5698, g1084, g7669, g10523, g9510 and g8639 were prioritized as candidate backbone genes for subsequent functional characterization (Figure S3).

Development of a CRISPR/Cas9‐Mediated Genome Editing System in F. equiseti P26

To facilitate functional validation of conserved and infection‐induced SM backbone genes, we established a CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome editing system in F. equiseti P26. Antibiotic sensitivity assays showed that mycelial growth was completely inhibited by G418 at 300 μg/mL and by hygromycin B at 80 μg/mL, concentrations that were subsequently used for transformant selection (Figure 4a). Microscopic examination showed that conidia germinated into short, actively growing hyphae within 12 h, providing suitable material for protoplast preparation (Figure 4b). Under optimized enzymatic digestion conditions using driselase, snailase and cellulase (each 10 mg/mL), protoplast yields reached approximately 9.7 × 106 protoplasts per gram of wet mycelia per millilitre (Figure 4c; Table S3). For sgRNA expression, the Gln‐tRNA promoter from Ustilaginoidea virens was adopted (Liang et al. 2018), supported by substantial sequence conservation between U. virens and F. equiseti P26 (Figure 4d). Gene deletion efficiency was subsequently assessed by comparing conventional homologous recombination with CRISPR/Cas9‐assisted homologous recombination. Diagnostic PCR screening confirmed that conventional homologous recombination yielded 1 correct mutant among 16 transformants (6.2%), whereas CRISPR/Cas9‐assisted homologous recombination produced 35 correct mutants among 39 transformants, corresponding to an efficiency of 89.7% (Figure 4f; Table 1). Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful establishment of a robust and highly efficient CRISPR/Cas9‐based genome editing platform in F. equiseti P26, providing a powerful genetic tool for functional characterization of infection‐associated genes. Importantly, this system relies on intracellular Cas9 expression rather than transient Cas9 ribonucleoprotein delivery (Ge et al. 2021), providing a stable and scalable platform for functional genomics in F. equiseti.

Phenotypic Analysis of SM Backbone Gene Deletion Mutants in F. equiseti P26

Following confirmation of successful gene deletion by diagnostic PCR (Figure S4), seven deletion mutants were examined for vegetative growth and conidiation on PDA, potato sucrose agar (PSA) and yeast extract‐peptone‐dextrose agar (YPD) media. All mutants showed wild‐type (WT)‐like colony morphology and radial growth, with no significant differences in colony diameter under any condition, indicating that these genes are dispensable for vegetative growth. In contrast, conidiation was differentially affected: Δg10523 exhibited increased conidial production on PDA and PSA, whereas Δg5698, Δg1084 and Δg7669 showed reduced sporulation. Conidiation remained uniformly low on YPD across all strains (Figure 5a). These results indicate that a subset of infection‐associated SM backbone genes modulates asexual sporulation in a medium‐dependent manner without broadly affecting vegetative growth.

Stress sensitivity assays further revealed gene‐specific roles in stress adaptation (Figure 5b). Under osmotic stress (1.8 M NaCl), Δg7615 and Δg10523 displayed increased sensitivity, whereas Δg7669, Δg9510 and Δg8639 showed reduced growth inhibition. Membrane stress (0.03% SDS) had minimal effects overall, except for increased sensitivity of Δg5698 and Δg1084. In contrast, all mutants exhibited enhanced sensitivity to cell wall stress imposed by Congo red, with the strongest effects observed in Δg7615, Δg5698, Δg1084, Δg10523 and Δg9510.

Pathogenicity Assays of Candidate SM Backbone Gene Deletion Mutants

To assess the contribution of candidate SM backbone genes to pathogenicity, the WT strain and deletion mutants were inoculated onto surface‐sterilized P. notoginseng root slices, intact roots and detached leaves. The WT strain caused extensive tissue maceration and vigorous hyphal growth on all tested tissues. Deletion mutants Δg1084, Δg5698 and Δg8639 exhibited infection phenotypes comparable to the WT, whereas Δg10523 and Δg7669 showed moderately reduced virulence, characterized by delayed surface colonization and limited tissue penetration. Notably, Δg9510 and Δg7615 displayed a severe loss of pathogenicity, with little to no detectable hyphal growth on host tissues (Figure 6).

These phenotypes were consistently observed in three independent deletion transformants of both Δg7615 and Δg9510 (Figures S5 and S6). Longitudinal sectioning of inoculated whole roots confirmed extensive internal colonization and tissue softening in WT‐infected samples, whereas tissues inoculated with Δg7615 or Δg9510 remained indistinguishable from mock controls. Importantly, both mutants exhibited normal vegetative growth on PDA, PSA and YPD media (Figure 5a), indicating that the loss of virulence is not attributable to general growth defects. These results indicate that g7615 and g9510 are critical for host colonization by F. equiseti P26, with g7615 being essential for infection.

Genetic Complementation Restores the Pathogenicity of Δg7615 and Δg9510

To confirm that the loss of pathogenicity observed in the Δg7615 and Δg9510 mutants was specifically caused by deletion of the target genes, genetic complementation was performed for both mutants. The full‐length coding sequences of g7615 and g9510, together with their native upstream promoter regions, were cloned into the hygromycin‐resistant vector pHygRI and introduced into the corresponding deletion mutants (Figure 7a). Correct integration of the complementation constructs was confirmed by PCR analysis (Figure 7b).

Pathogenicity assays on P. notoginseng showed that the complemented strains (Δg7615 C and Δg9510 C) exhibited infection phenotypes comparable to the WT strain (Figure 7c,d). In contrast to the severe virulence defects observed in the corresponding deletion mutants, the complemented strains regained the ability to colonize host surfaces, invade internal tissues and cause typical disease symptoms. Longitudinal sections of infected roots further confirmed that internal colonization was restored in the complemented strains, closely resembling the infection pattern of the WT. Importantly, restoration of pathogenicity was consistently observed in at least three independent complemented transformants for each gene and across three biological replicates. For each gene, at least three independent complemented transformants were obtained and subjected to phenotypic analyses demonstrating that the recovered virulence phenotype was stable and reproducible (Figures S5 and S6). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the pathogenicity defects of the Δg7615 and Δg9510 mutants are directly attributable to loss of the corresponding genes and confirm that both g7615 and g9510 play essential roles in host infection by F. equiseti P26.

A Conserved ICS–NRPS Backbone Gene Is Required for Pathogenicity Across Fusarium Species

Given the pronounced virulence defects of the Δg7615 mutant in F. equiseti P26, we asked whether this phenotype reflects a species‐specific effect or a conserved pathogenic mechanism within Fusarium. Comparative gene cluster analysis showed that homologous ICS–NRPS clusters are widespread among aggressive plant pathogens, including F. oxysporum, F. graminearum and F. fujikuroi, as well as the opportunistic pathogen Aspergillus fumigatus (Figure 8a). Genome‐wide homology searches using cblaster identified g7615 homologues in 776 fungal strains, with 172 detected in Fusarium (Figure S7). This pronounced enrichment supports the classification of g7615 as a conserved core SM backbone gene and independently corroborates the pangenome analysis. Collectively, these findings indicate that the g7615‐associated ICS–NRPS backbone is widely retained across Fusarium species and represents an evolutionarily conserved module involved in host interaction.

To directly test whether the pathogenic function of this conserved ICS–NRPS backbone gene is maintained across different Fusarium species, the g7615 homologue Fog9597 was deleted in F. oxysporum strain 21SP129, another major causal agent of P. notoginseng root rot. Pathogenicity assays showed that deletion of Fog9597 resulted in a marked attenuation of virulence on P. notoginseng tissues (Figure 8b). Notably, the ΔFog9597 mutant displayed infection phenotypes highly similar to those observed for the Δg7615 mutant in F. equiseti P26, indicating that the contribution of this ICS–NRPS backbone gene to pathogenicity is functionally conserved across species boundaries. Overall, these results demonstrate that this conserved ICS–NRPS biosynthetic backbone gene represents a core pathogenicity determinant shared among multiple Fusarium species, highlighting its central role in fungal infection of P. notoginseng.

Discussion

Fungal pathogenicity is rarely governed by a single virulence determinant; rather, it typically reflects the coordinated action of chemically diverse SMs during host infection (Pontes et al. 2020; Kumar et al. 2024). Many SMs function as mycotoxins and have long been implicated in disease, as illustrated by trichothecenes, fusaric acid‐related toxins and host‐specific metabolites such as T‐toxin and HC‐toxin (Keller 2019; Sweany et al. 2022; Perincherry et al. 2019; Wagacha and Muthomi 2007; Ismaiel and Papenbrock 2015). Consistent with this, the predicted SM repertoire of F. equiseti P26 and infection‐stage RNA‐seq showing enrichment of SM biosynthetic functions supports the view that SM is actively engaged during P. notoginseng root colonization.

Comparative pangenome analysis provides a powerful framework to dissect fungal pathogenicity (Kato et al. 2020; Van Westerhoven et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2022). Pangenome analysis identified SM backbone genes broadly conserved across Fusarium, and integration with transcriptomic data during P. notoginseng infection revealed a subset of conserved backbones that are preferentially induced, providing a rational basis for candidate prioritization. To enable causal tests in F. equiseti, we established a stable, high‐efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 system based on intracellular Cas9 expression, improving reproducibility relative to transient Cas9 ribonucleoprotein delivery (Ge et al. 2021). Using this platform, disruption of two conserved backbones g7615 (ICS–NRPS hybrid; Table S4) and g9510 (predicted siderophore‐associated NRPS; Table S5) was consistently associated with a marked reduction in pathogenicity, and complementation restored virulence. Deletion of the g7615 homologue in F. oxysporum resulted in a comparable attenuation, supporting a conserved contribution of this pathway class across Fusarium.

The involvement of these pathway types is biologically plausible. ICS‐associated clusters have been linked to metal‐responsive programmes and isonitrile chemistry, including xanthocillin‐related routes, and recent work shows ICS–NRPS hybrids can generate isonitrile‐containing products with pronounced phytotoxic activities, connecting ICS‐centered SM to plant–fungus interactions (Lim et al. 2018; Won et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2025). In parallel, iron acquisition is a major constraint during root infection due to host sequestration, making siderophore‐associated NRPS pathways plausible contributors to in planta fitness (Takemura et al. 2025; Swayambhu et al. 2021; Choi et al. 2024).

While the precise metabolites produced by g7615 and g9510 clusters remain to be chemically resolved, our conclusions emphasize the functional importance of these conserved SM backbone modules as virulence determinants, rather than definitive assignment of specific chemical entities at this stage. The combination of pangenome conservation, infection‐stage induction, targeted gene disruption leading to significantly reduced lesion development and colonization capacity, and restoration of pathogenicity upon complementation provides convergent genetic evidence that these conserved SM backbones contribute to Fusarium–P. notoginseng root rot.

Taken together, our study links conserved, infection‐induced SM backbones to root rot phenotypes through direct genetic validation, providing a scalable path from pangenome‐scale BGC inventories to experimentally supported virulence determinants. This framework should facilitate prioritization of broadly shared chemical pathways for mechanistic follow‐up and may inform the development of more targeted strategies to manage Fusarium‐induced root rot in P. notoginseng and related root‐associated pathosystems.

Experimental Procedures

Microorganisms and Plant Materials

Fusarium equiseti P26 was originally isolated from root rot‐infected P. notoginseng plants by Dr. Yong Tan (Kunming University of Science and Technology). This strain has been previously reported as a causal agent of P. notoginseng root rot (Liu et al. 2024). The strain was maintained on PDA (potato 200 g/L, glucose 20 g/L and agar 20 g/L) at 30°C. Three‐year‐old healthy P. notoginseng plants were obtained from Wenshan Dongbin Sanqi Sales Co. Ltd. Leaves were excised and surface‐sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 3 min, followed by 2.5% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min, and then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water (SDW). Roots from the same plants were cut into slices of uniform thickness and subjected to the same surface sterilization procedure. For inoculation assays using intact roots, excess lateral roots were removed prior to surface sterilization. Plasmids were constructed and propagated in Escherichia coli DH5α, which was cultured in Luria Bertani (LB) medium (tryptone 10 g/L, yeast extract 5 g/L, NaCl 10 g/L) supplemented with appropriate antibiotics.

DNA Extraction, Genome Sequencing and Assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted from mycelia of F. equiseti P26 using a commercial fungal genomic DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Whole‐genome sequencing was performed by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. using the Illumina HiSeq platform with a paired‐end 150 bp (PE150) strategy, generating a total of 5.28 Gb of high‐quality clean data. Raw sequencing reads were quality‐filtered using NGSQCToolkit2 (v. 2.3.3) (Patel and Jain 2012). Adaptor contamination and low‐quality reads were removed, and duplicate reads were eliminated using FastUniq (v. 1.1) (Xu et al. 2012). Sequencing error correction was performed using BLESS (v. 1.02) (Heo et al. 2014). De novo genome assembly was conducted using SPAdes (v. 4.0.0) (Bankevich et al. 2012) with default parameters. Gene structure annotation was carried out using AUGUSTUS (Stanke et al. 2006) with the Aspergillus oryzae gene model as the training set, followed by manual inspection and curation where necessary. The predicted protein‐coding genes were subsequently used for SM BGC prediction using FunBGCeX (Tang and Matsuda 2024), run in fungal mode with default parameters.

Pangenome‐Inspired Analysis of Backbone Enzyme‐Encoding Genes in BGCs

To construct a comprehensive dataset for pangenome‐level analysis of SM backbone genes in BGCs across Fusarium, a total of 54 Fusarium genomes were included in this study. Among them, four genomes were newly sequenced and assembled in our laboratory, including F. equiseti P26, while the remaining genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database. From these public datasets, 50 high‐quality Fusarium reference genomes with well‐assembled sequences and complete gene annotations were selected for downstream analyses. Backbone enzymes play a pivotal role in SM biosynthesis by defining the core scaffold and chemical class of products encoded by BGCs. To systematically identify SMs potentially involved in plant infection across Fusarium species, a pangenome‐inspired framework was applied to prioritize backbone enzyme‐encoding genes that are broadly conserved among Fusarium genomes. Amino acid sequences of backbone enzymes were extracted from the output files generated by the BGC prediction tool FunBGCeX. The aggregated backbone enzyme sequences were used to construct sequence similarity networks using EFI‐EST (Gerlt et al. 2015) to assess global sequence relationships and clustering patterns. In parallel, orthology inference was performed using OrthoFinder (v. 2.0) (Emms and Kelly 2019), and the distribution of backbone enzyme orthogroups across the Fusarium genomes was statistically analysed to identify core and accessory backbone enzymes. Backbone enzymes with high prevalence among Fusarium genomes were prioritized as candidate pathogenicity‐associated biosynthetic components for further investigation.

RNA Sequencing and Differential Expression Analysis

For transcriptome analysis, fresh conidia of F. equiseti P26 were inoculated onto PDA plates overlaid with sterile cellophane membranes. For the infection‐mimicking treatment, surface‐sterilized P. notoginseng root tissues were placed beneath the cellophane membrane, whereas plates without root tissue served as controls. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 5 days, after which fungal mycelia were harvested from the cellophane surface. Three independent biological replicates were collected for each condition. Total RNA extraction, library construction and transcriptome sequencing were performed by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Raw sequencing reads were quality‐filtered and mapped to the F. equiseti P26 reference genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2019). Transcript assembly and gene expression quantification were performed with StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015), and expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression analysis between treatment and control groups was conducted using DESeq2, with genes exhibiting log2 fold‐change ≥ |1| and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 considered significantly differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were subsequently performed to assess the functional categories enriched among DEG. Heatmaps and other visualizations were generated using R.

Establishment of a Genetic Transformation System for F. equiseti P26

Antibiotic Susceptibility Assay

To assess antibiotic sensitivity for subsequent genetic transformation, an antibiotic susceptibility assay was performed using G418 and hygromycin B. Conidia of F. equiseti P26 were spread onto PDA plates supplemented with a gradient of G418 (0, 50, 100, 200, 300 and 400 μg/mL) or hygromycin B (0, 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 μg/mL). Plates were incubated at 30°C for 5 days, and fungal growth was recorded to evaluate sensitivity to each antibiotic concentration.

Preparation of Protoplasts

Fusarium equiseti P26 was cultured in potato dextrose broth (PDB) at 30°C for 7 days to induce conidiation. Conidia were harvested from PDA plates using sterile 0.1% Tween 80 solution, filtered through Miracloth to remove hyphal fragments, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in YEPD liquid medium (yeast extract 5 g/L, glucose 20 g/L, peptone 10 g/L). To monitor spore germination, conidial suspensions were incubated at 30°C, and germination and hyphal development were examined microscopically at 0, 4, 8 and 12 h after incubation. For cell wall digestion, germinated mycelia were subjected to enzymatic digestion, and different cell wall‐degrading enzymes and enzyme combinations were evaluated. Protoplasts were generated using enzyme solutions prepared in 0.7 M NaCl and incubated at 30°C with shaking at 90 rpm. Following digestion, the resulting suspension was filtered to remove undigested hyphal debris and centrifuged to collect intact protoplasts. Protoplasts were resuspended in STC buffer (sucrose 200 g/L, CaCl2.2H2O 7.4 g/L, 0.5 M Tris–HCl, pH 8.0) and adjusted to a final concentration of 107 protoplasts/mL for subsequent transformation.

Ppolyethylene Glycol‐Mediated Transformation

DNA fragments or plasmids were mixed with 150 μL of protoplast suspension (107 protoplasts/mL), followed by dropwise addition of 1 mL PTC solution (40% polyethylene glycol [PEG] 8000 in STC buffer) and incubation at room temperature for 20 min. The transformation mixture was then diluted with 10 mL of TB3 regeneration medium (yeast extract 3 g/L, casein acid hydrolysate 3 g/L, sucrose 200 g/L) and incubated at 30°C overnight to allow protoplast regeneration. Regenerated protoplasts were plated onto TB3 bottom agar medium (TB3 supplemented with agar 10 g/L and 300 μg/mL G418) and overlaid with TB3 top agar medium (TB3 supplemented with agar 15 g/L, 350 μg/mL G418) for primary selection. After 3 days of incubation at 30°C, antibiotic‐resistant colonies were transferred to PDA plates containing 400 μg/mL G418 for two additional rounds of selection and verified by diagnostic PCR.

Construction of a CRISPR/Cas9‐Based Gene Knockout System

Genomic DNA was extracted from mycelia of the WT strain grown on PDA for 7 days. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S6 and the fungal strains and plasmids are summarized in Tables S7 and S8, respectively. To establish a CRISPR/Cas9‐based gene editing system, key functional components of the CRISPR/Cas9 machinery were systematically evaluated for compatibility with this species, using the previously described pCas9‐tRp‐gRNA vector as a backbone (Liang et al. 2018). Cas9 nuclear localization was assessed using two alternative nuclear localization signals (NLSs), SV40NLS and histone H2B–derived HTBNLS, while sgRNA expression was tested under the control of different promoter elements, including endogenous tRNA promoters, the U6 small nuclear RNA (U6 snRNA) promoter, and the 5S rRNA internal promoter (Figures S8 and S9).

For homologous recombination‐mediated gene deletion, approximately 1 kb upstream and downstream flanking regions of each target gene were amplified by PCR and fused with a geneticin resistance (Neoᴿ) cassette via double‐joint PCR (Yu et al. 2004). For CRISPR/Cas9‐assisted gene replacement, the same fragments were used as donor DNA templates. Single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting each gene were designed using sgRNAcas9 software (v. 3.0). Complementary sgRNA oligonucleotides were annealed and ligated into BsmBI‐digested pCas9‐tRp‐gRNA, and all resulting constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. Finally, the CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid and the corresponding donor DNA fragments were co‐transformed into F. equiseti P26 protoplasts using a PEG‐mediated transformation protocol, as described above.

Functional Complementation of Gene Deletion Mutants

For functional complementation, the full‐length coding sequence of each candidate gene, together with its native promoter region (~1.5 kb upstream of the start codon), was amplified from F. equiseti P26 genomic DNA. The amplified fragments were cloned into the hygromycin B‐resistant vector pHygRI, which had been linearized with HindIII. Seamless cloning was performed using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (C115) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. All recombinant plasmids were verified by restriction enzyme digestion prior to fungal transformation. The verified complementation constructs were introduced into the corresponding gene deletion mutants via PEG‐mediated protoplast transformation, as described above. Transformants were initially selected on TB3 agar supplemented with hygromycin B (80 μg/mL in the bottom layer and 100 μg/mL in the top layer). After 3 days of incubation, putative transformants were transferred to fresh PDA containing 120 μg/mL hygromycin B for secondary selection. Correct integration of the complementation constructs was confirmed by diagnostic PCR analysis. For each target gene, three independent deletion mutants and three corresponding complemented strains were selected for subsequent pathogenicity assays, as described above.

Biological Phenotypic Assays

To evaluate biological phenotypes, the WT strain and gene deletion mutants were cultured on PDA at 30°C for 7 days to induce sporulation. Conidia were harvested and suspended in sterile water, and spore suspensions were adjusted to a final concentration of 107 spores/mL for subsequent assays. For vegetative growth assessment, 1 μL of spore suspension was inoculated onto PDA, PSA (potato 200 g/L, sucrose 20 g/L, agar 20 g/L), and YPD agar (yeast extract 10 g/L, peptone 20 g/L, dextrose 20 g/L, agar 20 g/L) and incubated at 30°C for 5 days. Colony diameters were measured, and radial growth was evaluated accordingly. For sporulation analysis, spore suspensions were inoculated onto PDA, PSA and YPD agar plates and incubated at 30°C for 5 days. Conidia were collected from each plate, and spore production was quantified using a haemocytometer under a light microscope. To assess stress sensitivity, 1 μL of spore suspension was inoculated onto PDA plates supplemented with different stress agents, including 1.8 M NaCl, 0.03% (w/v) SDS, or 0.8 mg/mL Congo red. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 5 days. Colony diameters were measured and compared with those on non‐treated PDA plates to evaluate stress‐induced growth inhibition. All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each consisting of three technical replicates.

Pathogenicity Assay

Pathogenicity assays were performed using the WT F. equiseti P26 strain, gene deletion mutants, and the corresponding complemented strains. For whole‐root inoculation, fresh conidial suspensions were prepared in SDW and adjusted to a final concentration of 107 conidia/mL using a haemocytometer. Surface‐sterilized P. notoginseng roots were wounded at 10 evenly distributed sites using a sterile needle, after which 500 μL of conidial suspension was injected into each root with a sterile syringe. SDW was used as the mock‐inoculated control. For root slice and detached leaf assays, agar plugs of equal size excised from PDA plates and covered with fungal mycelia of comparable density were placed onto the surface of surface‐sterilized tissues. An equal volume of SDW was used as a negative control. All treatments were performed with three independent biological replicates. After inoculation, whole roots were incubated at 25°C under high‐humidity conditions for 10 days, whereas root slices and detached leaves were incubated at 30°C under humid conditions for 5 days. Disease development was monitored by assessing surface colonization and lesion formation. To further evaluate internal infection, inoculated whole roots were longitudinally sectioned and examined for tissue discolouration, softening, and the extent of internal fungal colonization.

Evolutionary Conservation Analysis of g7615 in Fungi

To assess the evolutionary conservation and phylogenetic distribution of g7615 homologues across fungi, homologous sequences were identified using cblaster (Cameron et al. 2025) against the NCBI nr database. The search was performed with the following parameters: maximum E‐value threshold of 0.01, minimum sequence identity of 30%, minimum query coverage of 50%, and a maximum intergenic gap of 20,000 bp. Accession numbers of the retrieved homologous sequences were collected (Table S9), and the corresponding amino acid sequences were downloaded from NCBI. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MAFFT with default parameters (Katoh and Daron 2013). The resulting alignment matrix was subsequently trimmed using trimAl to remove poorly aligned regions (Salvador et al. 2009). The curated alignment was then used to construct a maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ‐TREE v. 2.3.4 with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support (Minh et al. 2020). The optimal substitution model was automatically selected by the built‐in ModelFinder module according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with Q.plant+I+R8 identified as the best‐fit model (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017).
