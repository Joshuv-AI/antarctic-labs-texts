Hamstring fascicle behavior during leg curl exercises performed at varying knee joint angles and loading intensities in individuals with a history of unilateral hamstring injury: a randomized cross-over design study
Authors: Taspol Keerasomboon, Toshiaki Soga, Pasawee Rachasena, Pipatpong Intasri, Parunchaya Jamkrajang, Norikazu Hirose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShorter resting fascicle length of the biceps femoris long head (BFlh) has been associated with a history and risk of hamstring strain injury (HSI). However, acute mid-region BFlh fascicle operating length under different knee joint angles and contraction intensities remain unclear.ObjectiveTo examine the effects of knee joint angle and contraction intensity on acute mid-region BFlh fascicle operating length and to determine whether these responses differ between previously injured and uninjured limbs.MethodsFourteen male university athletes with a history of unilateral HSI participated in a randomized crossover study. Mid-region BFlh fascicle length was assessed using ultrasonography during isometric prone leg curl exercise at three knee joint angles (30°, 60°, and 90°) and four contraction intensities (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of maximal voluntary isometric contraction [MVIC]). Measurements were obtained at 50% of thigh length using B-mode ultrasonography. Knee joint angle and force were measured using a manual goniometer and a hand-held dynamometer, respectively. The effects of limb, knee joint angle, and contraction intensity were examined using a three-way within-subject repeated-measures ANOVA.ResultsSignificant main effects of knee joint angle (p = 0.002) and contraction intensity (p < 0.01) were observed for mid-region BFlh fascicle length. However, neither the main effect of limb nor any interaction involving limb was statistically significant (p > 0.05). Exploratory condition-specific comparisons indicated longer fascicle operating lengths at 30° than at 60° or 90° under some loading conditions. At 30° in the uninjured limb, fascicle operating length was longer at 25% than at 100% MVIC (p = 0.004); however, the corresponding difference was not significant in the previously injured limb. Because the interactions involving the limb were not significant, this finding does not demonstrate a difference in response between limbs. The absolute normalized between-limb difference in fascicle length was approximately 10%, with no significant effects of knee joint angle or contraction intensity.ConclusionAcute mid-region BFlh fascicle operating length varied according to knee joint angle and contraction intensity during isometric prone leg curl exercise. Longer fascicle operating lengths were observed at 30° under some loading conditions. However, the present findings do not demonstrate that the previously injured and uninjured limbs responded differently. Background

Hamstring strain injury (HSI) is a prevalent injury in sports that involve sprinting, including track and field [1], soccer [2], and rugby [3]. Prior studies indicate that the incidence rate of HSIs among male professional soccer players increased from 12% to 24% of all injuries between 2001 and 2022 [4], with a recurrence rate of approximately 35% [5]. Recurrence is particularly concerning: approximately 50% of athletes with a prior HSI experience re-injury within 25 days of returning to play [6], and severe cases require 1.5–3.5 months before returning to play [7]. Consequently, preventing both initial HSI and recurrence is critical for maintaining athletic performance.

A prior history of HSI constitutes a key non-modifiable risk factor for recurrent HSI, with evidence indicating up to an 11.6-fold increase in risk among professional soccer players [8]. In addition, HSI demonstrates greater vulnerability in the biceps femoris long head (BFlh) muscle compared with the semitendinosus (ST) and semimembranosus (SM), which are also biarticular hamstring muscles [9–11]. This heightened risk has been attributed to structural factors such as reduced fascicle length of the biceps femoris long head (BFlh FL) [12], muscle atrophy [13], scar tissue formation [14], and neurological maladaptation, including reduced voluntary activation [15], which may explain the observed decrease in hamstring strength [16]. Following HSI, BFlh FL is shorter in the previously injured limb relative to the uninjured limb [12, 17], and shorter BFlh FL is itself a recognized risk factor for recurrent HSI [12]. Optimizing hamstring exercises to promote fascicle lengthening is therefore considered essential for preventing hamstring strain recurrence.

Although eccentric hamstring strengthening is widely recommended for HSI prevention, isometric contractions are typically favored in the early phases of rehabilitation to minimize the risk of reinjury [12, 18–20]. Insufficient isometric strength, resulting in excessive eccentric lengthening during the late swing phase of high-velocity running or deceleration, has been proposed as a potential contributor to injury [21, 22]. However, limited research has examined fascicle length behavior during hamstring exercises. Despite the clinical relevance of isometric contraction, how BFlh fascicle length responds across varying joint angles and contraction intensities during leg curl (LC) exercise is not well established. Previous research has indicated that leg curl (LC) exercises performed at knee flexion angles ranging from 30° to 60° activate the semimembranosus (SM) and biceps femoris long head (BFlh), both of which are prone to injury during rehabilitation [23]. This study suggested that variations in morphological characteristics may explain differences in hamstring muscle activity in relation to moment arm and knee joint angle [23]. Clarifying BFlh fascicle behavior across variations in LC exercises at different joint angles and contraction intensities, based on prior findings, may help to characterize the neuromechanical demands imposed on the muscle under controlled laboratory conditions. This study aims to investigate variations in muscle architectural characteristics between previously injured limbs and uninjured limbs under different biomechanical conditions, with the objective of generating mechanistic insights into how prior hamstring injury may influence fascicle behavior. It is hypothesized that a prior hamstring injury may influence neuromechanical contraction strategies under specific muscle length and loading conditions, rather than solely altering resting muscle morphology. These findings may serve as a foundation for future hypothesis-driven research into rehabilitation and injury prevention.

Methods

Design and setting of the study

In this study, a randomized crossover design was conducted between September 2025 and November 2025 at laboratory of college of sport science and technology, Mahidol University. This study investigated hamstring architecture during variations of isometric leg curl exercises. First, the study aimed to examine fascicle behavior in a non-linear and condition-specific manner, rather than as a simple static architectural deficit. Second, it aimed to determine whether a history of hamstring injury influences neuromechanical contraction strategies under specific muscle length and loading conditions, rather than merely altering resting morphology. The study followed the CONSORT 2010 Statement extension for reporting randomized crossover trials [24, 25].

Participants

The sample size for this investigation was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The analysis was conducted using repeated-measures ANOVA with a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.8, and an effect size of 0.25, which was adopted as a conventional medium effect assumption [26], consistent with a previous study [27]. Based on these parameters, a sample size of fourteen participants was deemed sufficient. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 35 years (2), a history of HSI within the past four years, and (3) HSI affecting only one leg. HSI was defined as sudden posterior thigh pain occurring during athletic activities. Detailed information regarding injury diagnosis, location, severity, and rehabilitation was unavailable, as it relied on self-reported data. Classification of HSI as sprint- or stretch-related followed a previously established framework [28]. Fourteen male university athletes volunteered to participate in this study. All participants were actively engaged in competitive training. The previously injured limb was identified solely based on self-reported histories of clinically confirmed hamstring injuries, as no medical or team records were available for verification. The exclusion criteria included individuals with anterior cruciate ligament injuries and those with a recent history of lower-limb injuries (excluding hamstring strains) within six months prior to the experiment. Subsequently, the included participants were randomly divided by the examiner. The sequence of experimental conditions (joint angle and contraction intensity combinations) was randomized independently for each participant using a computer-generated block randomization procedure produced prior to data collection. Condition order was fully counterbalanced across participants such that each condition appeared approximately equally often in each ordinal position. To control fatigue effects inherent to this study design, conditions were separated by a rest interval of at least 1 min. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Mahidol University (approval number: MU-CIRB 2023/226.1407), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of the study purpose and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained by signing a consent form after being advised of any potential risks associated with the procedures.

Procedures

The pre-test (resting fascicle length [FL] measurements) involved the initial assessment of the middle region of the BFlh using ultrasonography. Ultrasonographic images were obtained twice. Prior to testing, participants performed dynamic stretching and a specific warm-up for the isometric leg curl intervention. Following the warm-up, participants performed a maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) of the prone leg curl, consisting of two maximal-effort repetitions lasting 5 s each, with a 1-minute rest interval between trials, for both legs at knee flexion angles of 30°, 60°, and 90°. During testing, participants’ hips were maintained in a neutral position, and a manual goniometer was used to measure the knee flexion angle. Peak force was taken as MVIC for each limb and used to calculate target loads for each target condition. Ultrasonographic measurements, force, and knee flexion angle assessments were subsequently obtained following the MVIC test and during the isometric prone leg curl interventions under randomized conditions. The experimental conditions included knee flexion angles of 30°, 60°, and 90°, each performed at four intensities: 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of MVIC. The MVIC percentage was calculated using the peak force obtained during the maximal voluntary isometric strength test. Experimental conditions were presented in a randomized sequence, counterbalanced across participants as described above. To monitor and manage fatigue across the testing session, participants’ perceived exertion was assessed using the Borg 6–20 Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale [29] prior to each experimental condition. If a participant reported elevated perceived exertion indicative of fatigue, the rest interval before the subsequent condition was extended at the examiner’s discretion based on the participant’s verbal report. A minimum 1-minute rest interval was maintained between all conditions. Ultrasonographic imaging was performed simultaneously with each isometric contraction to measure fascicle behavior. All data collection was conducted by two examiners with clearly defined, non-overlapping roles throughout the study period. All ultrasound data acquisition and fascicle length analysis were performed by a single Examiner (1) However, prior to fascicle length analysis, all video files were anonymized to remove any identifying information about limb status. Examiner 1 was therefore blinded to limb status during image analysis. Examiner 2 was solely responsible for resisting the participant’s isometric force production via a purely hand-held dynamometer, monitoring the dynamometer display for force output, and providing verbal feedback to guide participants toward and maintain the target contraction intensity. This division of roles ensured that probe stability and force resistance were each managed as dedicated single tasks, without either examiner being required to perform both functions simultaneously. The force recording system and the ultrasound imaging system were operated on separate devices without synchronization. However, before initiating each ultrasound video recording, Examiner 2 confirmed that the force output on the recording system was stable at the target intensity level. Examiner 1 commenced the 5-second video recording only upon receiving this confirmation from Examiner (2) If force output was observed to be unstable at any point during the 5-second recording window, the trial was stopped and repeated in full before proceeding to the next condition. No adverse events occurred during the intervention protocol. All experimental procedures were conducted under the supervision of an examiner certified as an Australian Strength and Conditioning Association Specialist.

Isometric prone leg curl intervention

Prior to the intervention, MVIC strength was assessed by the prone leg curl for both limbs at knee flexion angles of 30°, 60°, and 90° on a trainer bed with the examiner 2 holding the purely hand-held dynamometer rigidly against the participant’s effort (Fig. 1). Each limb completed two maximal-effort contractions of 5 s duration, separated by a 1-minute rest interval. The examiner braced the dynamometer with both hands and maintained a fixed, unyielding resistance throughout each contraction; no accommodating movement of the examiner’s position was permitted. Force was recorded using a digital hand-held dynamometer (Lafayette, USA) connected to the DynoData application, which provided real-time visual feedback of force output throughout the contraction. During the test, the participant’s hip was maintained in a neutral position, and a manual goniometer was used to adjust the knee flexion angle. The rotation axis of the goniometer was aligned with the lateral epicondyle of the femur. Knee flexion angle was defined as the angle formed by the line connecting the greater trochanter to the lateral femoral epicondyle and the line connecting the lateral femoral epicondyle to the lateral malleolus [30]. Peak force was taken as MVIC for each limb and used to calculate target loads for each target condition. Each Isometric contraction was sustained for 5 s at the target percentage of MVIC. All conditions (30°, 60°, and 90°) were performed at four intensities (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% MVIC) for both limbs. During each condition, participants received real-time visual feedback of force output via the dynamometer display and were verbally guided by the examiner to reach and sustain the target intensity. Participants were instructed to match and hold this target intensity for 5 s. The examiner maintained identical hand placement and bracing posture across all trials. Rest intervals between trials were set at 1 min. These knee flexion angles were selected based on previous findings indicating that angles between 30° and 60° preferentially activate the BFlh, whereas semitendinosus (ST) activation exceeds BFlh activation at angles greater than 60° during leg curl exercises [23]. All strength assessments were performed by a single examiner 2, who had 2 years of experience using digital handheld dynamometers.

Hamstring architecture

To determine the measurement locations of the middle region of the hamstring muscles, thigh length was measured for both the previously injured limb and the uninjured limb by examiner 1. Thigh length was defined as the distance from the greater trochanter (0% of thigh length) to the popliteal crease (100% of thigh length). Measurement locations were marked along the longitudinal axis of the thigh at 50% of the total thigh length [31]. This landmark was marked on the skin prior to each session to ensure consistent probe placement across repeated measurements and experimental conditions.

Participants were positioned prone on a trainer bed, with both the hip and knee joints fully extended. Ultrasound data were acquired as continuous video recordings throughout each 5-second isometric contraction using the B-mode cine-loop function (Philips Lumify, Bothell, WA, USA) equipped with an L12–4 linear probe (frequency: 4–12 MHz; width: 3.4 cm; depth: 8.0 cm). After applying transmission gel to the skin, the probe was placed at the designated marked site with minimal pressure and aligned along the longitudinal axis of the muscle. Following the initial video acquisition, the probe was fully removed from the skin surface, the landmark was reconfirmed, and a second video was acquired using the identical placement procedure. During each recording, examiner 2 confirmed that the hand-held dynamometers displayed stability at the target intensity before examiner 1 commenced video recording. The frame selected for fascicle measurement from each 5-second video was chosen based on image quality. The two trials were averaged to produce the value used in all subsequent analyses. This probe-removal protocol was applied consistently across all conditions. All ultrasound measurements and fascicle length analyses were performed by an examiner1 across the entire data collection period. Intra-rater reliability for BFlh FL measurement was examined in a previous study [32]. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.97, with a standard error of measurement (SEM) of 7.86% [33], indicating that ultrasound is a reliable tool for estimating BFlh FL.

All ultrasonographic images were analyzed using Tracker software (version 6.2.0) [31]. The biceps femoris long head fascicle length (BFlh FL) and muscle thickness (MT) were measured with the probe aligned along the longitudinal axis of the muscle at the 50% thigh length location. In many images, the entire fascicle length could not be visualized within the ultrasound field; therefore, the superficial and intermediate aponeuroses were linearly extrapolated beyond the image frame. Fascicle length (FL) was defined as the linear distance between the points at which the fascicle intersected the extrapolated lines of the superficial and intermediate aponeuroses (Fig. 2) [34]. The fascicle length was determined by identifying a clearly visible fascicle located near the center of the image. When either the superficial or intermediate aponeurosis extended beyond the ultrasound image, the visible portion of the aponeurosis was aligned with a straight line and extrapolated linearly beyond the field of view. Fascicle length was then calculated as the linear distance between the intersection points of the selected fascicle and the extrapolated aponeuroses. Pennation angle (PA) was defined as the angle formed between the trajectory of a muscle fascicle and the intermediate aponeurosis at the point of insertion [35]. Muscle thickness (MT) was defined as the perpendicular distance between the superficial and intermediate aponeuroses [36]. The same anatomical region was scanned in all trials, with probe placement standardized using consistent anatomical landmarks.

Data analysis

All ultrasound measurements of the hamstring muscles were averaged across two trials per condition. During the strength test, peak force for each set was defined as the maximum isometric hamstring strength recorded across the two repetitions, and these peak values were subsequently averaged. In addition, isometric hamstring strength was also normalized by body weight. The BFlh asymmetrical fascicle length (%) was calculated as the absolute difference between the previously injured and uninjured limbs, normalized to the non-injured limb, and expressed as a percentage. A higher percentage reflects greater fascicle length asymmetry, indicating larger morphological differences between limbs.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. The results indicated that multiple variables met the assumption of normality. To compare resting architectural variables (mid-region BFlh fascicle length, pennation angle, and muscle thickness) and peak isometric knee flexion strength between the non-HSI and prior HSI limb, paired-samples t-tests were conducted for each variable. All preliminary comparisons were treated as exploratory; no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. To examine the effects of limb, knee joint angle, and contraction intensity on mid-region BFlh fascicle length, a within-subject three-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted. The model included three within-subject factors: limb (2 levels: uninjured limb, previously injured limb), knee joint angle (3 levels: 30°, 60°, 90°), and contraction intensity (4 levels: 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% MVIC). A separate two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to assess the effects of knee joint angle (30°, 60°, 90°) and contraction intensity (25%, 50%, 75%, 100% MVIC) on BFlh fascicle length asymmetry. To evaluate peak isometric strength, a repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted across the three knee joint angles (30°, 60°, and 90°) at 100% MVIC. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Where significant main or interaction effects were observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests were performed for pairwise comparisons among exercise conditions. Effect sizes were reported as partial η² and interpreted as follows: trivial (< 0.01), small (0.01–0.06), medium (0.06–0.14), and large (> 0.14). Cohen’s d was interpreted as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.5), medium (0.5–0.8), and large (> 0.8). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP (version 0.19.1).To account for multiple comparisons, mid-region BFlh fascicle length was designated a priori as the primary outcome variable. All other outcomes (BFlh asymmetrical fascicle length, muscle thickness, and isometric strength) were classified as secondary or exploratory. Intra-rater reliability of mid-region BFlh fascicle length measurement was assessed at rest and at each of the 24 experimental conditions (3 knee joint angles × 4 contraction intensities, for each limb) using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC₃,₁, two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement) and the coefficient of variation (CV%), calculated from the two repeated ultrasound measurements obtained at each condition as described in the Procedures. Within-session reliability of peak isometric knee flexion force (MVIC) was assessed at each knee joint angle (30°, 60°, 90°) for both limbs, using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC₃,₁, ) and the coefficient of variation (CV%), calculated from the two maximal-effort trials performed at each limb–angle combination as described in the Procedures. ICC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90), and CV values below 10% were considered to indicate acceptable measurement precision.

Results

Participant characteristics

Fourteen male university athletes participated in the present study (mean ± SD: age, 20.7 ± 1.3 years; height, 1.76 ± 0.07 m; body mass, 71.7 ± 10.4 kg). Injury mechanism distribution was as follows: sprint-related HSI (28.5%), stretch-related HSI (64.2%), and contact-related injury (7.1%). The mean time since the most recent injury was 17.3 ± 15.4 months (range: 1–48 months), based on self-reported data.

Reliability of MVIC assessment

Within-session reliability of peak isometric knee flexion force at 100% MVIC, assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC₃,₁) and coefficient of variation (CV%) from the two maximal trials performed at each knee joint angle, was good to excellent in both limbs. In the previously injured limb, ICC was 0.93 (CV = 5.71%) at 30°, 0.90 (CV = 7.42%) at 60°, and 0.92 (CV = 6.32%) at 90°. In the uninjured limb, ICC was 0.80 (CV = 8.02%) at 30°, 0.95 (CV = 5.59%) at 60°, and 0.95 (CV = 5.30%) at 90°. Across both limbs and all three knee joint angles, ICC ranged from 0.80 to 0.95 and CV ranged from 5.30% to 8.02%, with every condition meeting the threshold for good-to-excellent reliability (ICC ≥ 0.75) and acceptable measurement precision (CV < 10%).

Reliability of mid-region BFlh fascicle length measurement

Intra-rater reliability of mid-region BFlh fascicle length measurement, assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC₃,₁) and coefficient of variation (CV%) from duplicate ultrasound measurements at rest and across all experimental conditions, was good to excellent across the conditions tested in both limbs. At rest, reliability was good in both the previously injured limb (ICC = 0.88, CV = 3.19%) and the uninjured limb (ICC = 0.86, CV = 4.88%). In the previously injured limb, reliability remained good to excellent across all 12 contraction conditions. At 30° of knee flexion, ICC was 0.91 (CV = 6.66%) at 25% MVIC, 0.90 (CV = 8.01%) at 50% MVIC, 0.94 (CV = 9.55%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.86 (CV = 2.49%) at 100% MVIC. At 60°, ICC was 0.83 (CV = 9.31%) at 25% MVIC, 0.85 (CV = 5.55%) at 50% MVIC, 0.96 (CV = 8.21%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.89 (CV = 8.38%) at 100% MVIC. At 90°, ICC was 0.82 (CV = 6.43%) at 25% MVIC, 0.98 (CV = 5.05%) at 50% MVIC, 0.88 (CV = 6.60%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.90 (CV = 6.73%) at 100% MVIC. In the uninjured limb, reliability was similarly good to excellent for 11 of the 12 contraction conditions. At 30°, ICC was 0.92 (CV = 5.25%) at 25% MVIC, 0.93 (CV = 5.38%) at 50% MVIC, 0.92 (CV = 5.55%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.66 (CV = 9.08%) at 100% MVIC. At 60°, ICC was 0.95 (CV = 5.14%) at 25% MVIC, 0.83 (CV = 11.73%) at 50% MVIC, 0.96 (CV = 4.48%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.95 (CV = 5.76%) at 100% MVIC. At 90°, ICC was 0.89 (CV = 7.98%) at 25% MVIC, 0.95 (CV = 7.13%) at 50% MVIC, 0.86 (CV = 6.59%) at 75% MVIC, and 0.88 (CV = 6.16%) at 100% MVIC.

Discussion

This study examined mid-region BFlh fascicle behavior across different knee flexion angles and varying percentages of MVIC. The primary finding was that mid-region BFlh fascicle length varied according to both knee flexion angle and load intensity. However, neither the main effect of limb nor any interaction involving limb was statistically significant. Thus, the present results provide no statistical evidence that fascicle responses to knee joint angle or contraction intensity differed between the previously injured and uninjured limbs. Exploratory condition-specific comparisons indicated that longer fascicle operating lengths were observed at 30° than at 60° or 90° under some loading conditions. However, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the angle-by-intensity and limb-by-angle-by-intensity interactions were not significant.

First, this study demonstrated no significant mean difference in resting BFlh fascicle length between previously injured and uninjured limbs. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding between-limb differences, with some indicating shorter fascicles in the previously injured limb [37], while others, particularly those using extended field-of-view (EFOV) ultrasound, reported no difference [38]. These discrepancies may be attributed to individual variability, including time since injury, injury characteristics, rehabilitation history, and training history. Recent evidence further highlights substantial inter-individual variability in fascicle asymmetry following HSI, challenging the notion of a universal pattern [39]. In the present study, the absolute between-limb fascicle asymmetry at rest was approximately 13% (SD 9%), consistent with prior findings demonstrating large individual variation [39]. However, because this asymmetry measure was calculated using the absolute difference between limbs, it does not indicate whether the previously injured limb had longer or shorter fascicles. The observed value may also include normal biological variation and measurement error. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis reported no significant differences at rest between injured and contralateral limbs, with differences primarily emerging during contraction or when compared with external control groups [40]. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear. The present cross-sectional findings cannot determine whether the observed individual differences resulted from the previous injury, bilateral adaptations, or pre-existing inter-limb variability.

The primary findings indicated that mid-region BFlh fascicle length varied with knee flexion angle and load intensity, with no difference between limbs. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has examined mid-region BFlh fascicle operating length during isometric contractions across varying knee joint angles and contraction intensities. Prior research has shown that during passive stretching, increasing hip flexion (0° to 90°) and knee extension increases both fascicle length and muscle–tendon unit length, with fascicle length accounting for approximately 50% of total lengthening [41]. Additionally, during slow concentric and eccentric contractions, fascicle length changes are greater at longer muscle lengths (i.e., more extended knee positions), regardless of contraction intensity [42]. Consistent with this general mechanical relationship, the present study found longer mid-region BFlh fascicle operating lengths at 30° than at 60° or 90° under some loading conditions. Specifically, condition-specific comparisons collapsed across both limbs indicated that mid-region BFlh fascicle length at 30° was significantly greater than at both 60° and 90°. However, because the angle-by-intensity and three-way interactions were not significant, these condition-specific findings should be regarded as exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of different responses between limbs. This aligns with findings by Van Hooren et al., who reported minimal changes in BFlh fascicle length until the terminal phase of the Nordic hamstring exercise [43]. Previous studies also indicate that BFlh fascicles often operate quasi-isometrically throughout much of the movement, with more pronounced lengthening occurring only in the final phase, while the muscle–tendon unit continues to lengthen [44, 45]. Collectively, the present findings suggest that shallower knee flexion angles may be associated with longer acute mid-region BFlh fascicle operating lengths during isometric prone leg curl exercise. This observation reflects acute fascicle behavior during contraction and should not be interpreted as evidence of chronic fascicle-length adaptation. The potential influence of fatigue on this relationship was not examined and should be investigated in future studies.

Regarding load intensity, previous research has demonstrated that BFlh fascicle behavior varies primarily with muscle length, whereas the effects of joint torque and contraction mode are less pronounced [42]. In the present study, contraction intensity had a significant main effect on mid-region BFlh fascicle length. An exploratory within-limb comparison indicated that, at 30° of knee flexion, fascicle length was greater at 25% than at 100% MVIC in the uninjured limb, whereas no corresponding pairwise difference reached statistical significance in the previously injured limb. Importantly, the presence of a significant difference in one limb and the absence of a significant difference in the other do not demonstrate a statistically significant between-limb difference. Because the interactions involving limb were not significant, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that previous HSI altered the fascicle response to contraction intensity.

Differences in fascicle operating length across contraction intensities may be related to activity-dependent muscle–tendon interactions. Recent evidence suggests that reduced voluntary activation shifts the torque–fascicle length relationship toward longer fascicle lengths during fixed-end contractions [46], potentially due to increased series compliance [47]. This is consistent with findings showing that fascicles may shorten while tendinous tissues elongate during isometric contractions [48, 49]. However, fascicle shortening does not necessarily correspond directly to tendon elongation due to factors such as slack and muscle–tendon geometry [50]. Accordingly, the greater fascicle length observed at lower intensities may reflect differences in muscle–tendon interaction across force levels, whereas higher force levels may be associated with shorter fascicle operating lengths. However, muscle activation, tendon elongation, and series elastic behavior were not directly measured in the present study. These explanations, therefore, remain speculative and require direct investigation.

Regarding asymmetry, the absolute normalized difference in mid-region BFlh fascicle length between limbs was approximately 10% across the tested conditions. No significant effects of knee joint angle or contraction intensity on this asymmetry measure were identified. This result indicates that the study did not detect systematic variation in the magnitude of absolute asymmetry across conditions; it does not establish statistical equivalence or stability. Moreover, because the asymmetry measure was based on the absolute difference between limbs, it does not indicate the direction of the difference and should not be interpreted as showing that the previously injured limb consistently had shorter fascicles. The magnitude of the observed asymmetry should also be considered relative to the measurement variability under active contraction conditions. A systematic review reported that resting asymmetry is generally small, whereas contraction-induced asymmetry is more pronounced in previously injured limbs [40]. In contrast, healthy individuals typically demonstrate symmetrical fascicle length and contraction behavior between limbs [37]. However, the present study did not include an uninjured control group and therefore cannot determine whether the observed asymmetry was greater than the normal between-limb variation in comparable athletes.

Current evidence identifies shorter absolute fascicle length, older age, and prior HSI as key risk factors [51]. Although strength asymmetry (> 9%) has been associated with increased HSI risk [52], thresholds derived from strength measurements cannot be directly applied to fascicle length asymmetry. Furthermore, no universal threshold for clinically meaningful fascicle length asymmetry has been established. Therefore, the present findings of asymmetry should be considered descriptive rather than evidence of persistent maladaptive remodeling or increased injury risk. Future longitudinal studies should investigate whether fascicle length asymmetry exceeds measurement variability and predicts subsequent HSI.

Several limitations should be considered. The relatively small sample size (n = 14) limits statistical power and generalizability. In particular, the study may have limited power to detect interactions involving the limb. Although the sample size was determined a priori, future studies should include larger and more diverse populations. Second, HSI history was based on self-reported data and the absence of a clinical or imaging-confirmed diagnosis. Participants could not provide verified information regarding the precise anatomical location of injury, the specific muscle injured, injury severity, rehabilitation history, or recurrence status, which may introduce heterogeneity and misclassification bias. Third, ultrasound measurements were obtained only from the mid-region of the BFlh at 50% of thigh length. Because BFlh architecture and injury-related changes may vary along the proximal-to-distal axis, the present findings should not be generalized to the entire muscle or to the proximal musculotendinous region where many HSIs occur. Fourth, ultrasound measurements and image analysis were performed by a single non-blinded investigator, which may have increased the risk of measurement bias. Ultrasound images were obtained using manual probe holding. Moreover, the intramuscular tendon of the BFlh was not consistently visible at the 50% thigh length measurement site. As the fascicle length measurement was anchored to the superficial and intermediate aponeuroses, this did not compromise measurement validity, but it is acknowledged as a feature of mid-belly imaging at this site. During high-intensity contractions, changes in muscle shape, skin movement, probe pressure, probe orientation, and fascicle visibility may have affected the estimation of fascicle length. In addition, some fascicles and aponeuroses required extrapolation beyond the ultrasound field of view. Although within-session reliability was generally acceptable, the observed condition-specific differences should be interpreted in relation to this measurement variability. Fifth, the use of a purely hand-held dynamometer without external stabilization represents a major methodological limitation, particularly during higher-intensity contractions (75% and 100% MVIC) where force output in trained athletes may approach or exceed the examiner’s resistive capacity. Although the examiner’s position, dynamometer placement, and feedback procedures were standardized, minor variations in stabilization and resistance cannot be excluded. Moreover, the force recording system and the ultrasound imaging system were operated on separate devices without synchronization. Consequently, the instantaneous force at the exact moment of the selected ultrasound frame cannot be verified from the recorded data, and the intensity-related findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory observations based on nominal target intensities supported by contraction-level force accuracy data. Finally, this acute cross-sectional study cannot determine whether the tested conditions induce chronic architectural adaptations, improve rehabilitation outcomes, or reduce future HSI risk. The present findings should therefore be regarded as mechanistic observations that may inform the design of future longitudinal and interventional studies rather than as evidence supporting a specific rehabilitation or injury-prevention protocol.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the acute mid-region BFlh fascicle operating length varied according to knee flexion angle and contraction intensity. Longer fascicle operating lengths were observed at 30° of knee flexion under some loading conditions. In an exploratory within-limb comparison, fascicle operating length in the uninjured limb was shorter at 100% than at 25% MVIC at 30° of knee flexion, whereas no corresponding significant difference was observed in the previously injured limb. However, because the limb-related interactions were not significant, this finding does not demonstrate a difference in response between the previously injured and uninjured limbs and should be interpreted with caution as exploratory. The descriptive absolute normalized between-limb difference in fascicle length was approximately 10%; future studies are needed to determine its clinical significance and relationship with measurement variability. Knee flexion at 30° may represent a mechanically interesting condition for investigation in future longitudinal intervention studies.
