DATTs: A Database of Disease‐Associated Therapeutic Targets With Required Actions for Treatment
Authors: Ryusuke Sawada, Noriko Yuyama Otani, Michio Iwata, Tomokazu Shibata, Shujiro Okuda, Hiroyo Nishide, Ikuo Uchiyama, Yoshihiro Yamanishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteins involved in pathophysiological mechanisms are widely recognized as promising therapeutic targets for drug discovery. The therapeutic effects of drugs are primarily mediated through the inhibition or activation of target proteins. Therefore, beyond identifying the appropriate molecular target, determining the direction of action on the target protein is a critical factor that shapes the efficacy and characteristics of a treatment. In this paper, we present DATTs (Disease‐Associated Therapeutic Targets), which is a new database of therapeutic protein targets with required actions for medical treatment. The information is extracted from public documents such as scientific papers and medical textbooks. The available information includes disease–target protein associations and the actions of drugs on these proteins required for therapeutic effects (e.g., activation, inhibition, or targeting). The DATTs currently includes information on 613 diseases, 1475 therapeutic target proteins, and 7415 disease–protein associations. Additional information such as links to other databases containing data on the molecular functions of target proteins and classification of diseases is also accessible. The DATTs can provide useful clues for investigation of disease mechanisms, novel drug design, and drug repositioning. The DATTs is accessible at https://datts.nibb.ac.jp/.Keywords: activation, disease‐associated therapeutic targets, disease–target protein associations, drug targets, inhibition

Introduction

Proteins involved in disease onset, progression, and reparative processes are closely linked to pathophysiological mechanisms and are widely recognized as promising therapeutic targets for drug discovery. Information on these therapeutic target proteins provides important clues not only for the development of novel drugs but also for drug repositioning, which aims to identify new therapeutic indications for approved drugs [1, 2]. Well‐organized knowledge of therapeutic target proteins is therefore essential for advancing disease understanding at the molecular level and for facilitating the development of effective treatment strategies. However, the organization and utilization of such information remain challenging, as identical biomolecules are often referred to by different names or abbreviations across the literature, and meaningful integration of data across basic research, clinical studies, and pharmacology is required.

The therapeutic effects of drugs are primarily mediated through the inhibition or activation of target proteins. Therefore, beyond identifying the appropriate molecular target, determining the direction of action on the target protein is a critical factor that shapes the efficacy and characteristics of a treatment. For instance, activation of dopamine receptors is a central therapeutic strategy for Parkinson’s disease, whereas inhibition of the same receptors is employed in the treatment of psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and depression. These examples highlight that accurate specification of the required action on therapeutic target proteins is essential for the development of disease‐specific treatment strategies. However, comprehensive databases that systematically curate whether inhibition or activation of disease‐associated proteins is required for therapeutic benefit remain insufficiently developed.

Currently, several databases have been developed to collect and provide information on associations between diseases and genes or therapeutic target proteins, including OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man) [3], KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) DISEASE [4], TTD (Therapeutic Target Database) [5], and Open Targets Platform [6]. OMIM is a valuable resource that primarily focuses on hereditary diseases and their causal genes, but its scope is largely limited to monogenic disorders. KEGG DISEASE links disease‐associated genes to molecular networks such as metabolic pathways; however, the coverage of therapeutic target information remains limited. In contrast, TTD collects a relatively large amount of data on therapeutic target proteins based on published literature and experimental evidence. The Open Targets Platform integrates multiple data sources, such as genomic variants, gene expression profiles, pharmacological evidence, and existing literature, to comprehensively evaluate disease–target relationships. Despite their utility, none of these resources explicitly curate information on whether inhibition or activation of target proteins is required for disease treatment, which limits their direct applicability to therapeutic strategy design and drug discovery research. In response to these challenges, there is a strong demand for a cross‐disciplinary resource that systematically organizes disease–therapeutic target protein relationships from the perspective of the regulatory action required for treatment, namely inhibition or activation.

Here, we present DATTs (Disease‐Associated Therapeutic Targets), a new database that systematically compiles therapeutic target proteins together with the specific actions required for disease treatment. The database was constructed by manually extracting information from public resources, including scientific literature and medical textbooks. DATTs provides disease–therapeutic target associations, indicates whether inhibition or activation of each target protein is required for therapeutic efficacy, and offers links to external databases that describe molecular functions of target proteins and standardized disease classifications. DATTs is designed as a comprehensive resource to support mechanistic disease analysis, rational drug development, and drug repositioning studies. DATTs is accessible at https://datts.nibb.ac.jp/.

Experimental Section

For data collection, KEGG gene IDs and UniProt IDs were assigned to proteins, and the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) ID [7] and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) [8] were assigned to diseases. The UMLS is a medical terminology glossary designed to extract and manage disease information from various sources in a unified manner, thereby mitigating the problems created by differences in descriptions across studies and techniques. The unique feature of DATTs is that the relationships between diseases and therapeutic protein targets are characterized according to whether inhibition or activation of the target protein is required for treatment. Information concerning disease–therapeutic target protein relationships and modes of action (activation, inhibition, target) in DATTs was primarily collected manually by curators from peer‐reviewed papers and specialized medical texts. In cases where the interpretation of disease–target relationships or modes of action was ambiguous, the content was reviewed and discussed among several researchers to confirm its validity before entry into the database. The DATTs resources are accessible via most common web browsers (Edge, Safari, Google Chrome, Firefox). The user can input disease names, gene names, protein IDs (e.g., KEGG GENES IDs, UniProt Accession IDs), and obtain a list of diseases for each therapeutic target protein or a list of therapeutic target proteins for each disease as output.

Currently, there are 613 diseases, 1,475 therapeutic target proteins, and 7,415 disease–therapeutic target protein relationships in the DATTs database. Furthermore, 454 diseases have information on 1,136 inhibitory therapeutic target proteins, with 5,468 disease–inhibitory therapeutic target protein pairs. On the other hand, 328 diseases have 406 activatory therapeutic target proteins, with 1,548 disease–activatory therapeutic target protein relationship pairs. Several cases are presented to illustrate the utility of DATTs. Figure S1a shows the HOME view, and Figure S1b summarizes the DATTs database statistics.

We compiled data statistics for the diseases in DATTs according to ICD‐10 disease classification. ICD‐10 is a standard medical classification used to systematically classify, record, and analyze diseases and causes of death worldwide using a common alphanumeric code. Of the 22 disease classifications in ICD‐10, we examined the number of diseases within DATTs for 12 major disease categories (Figure 1a) and the number of disease‐associated proteins (Figure 1b). The 12 diseases categories are as follows: A00‐B99; Certain infectious and parasitic diseases including tuberculosis, HIV, and viral hepatitis; C00‐D48; Neoplasms; D50‐D89; Diseases of the blood and blood‐forming organs and certain disorders involving the immune mechanism, including anemia and disorders of the blood and immune system; E00‐E90; Endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases, including diabetes and obesity; F00‐F99; Mental and behavioral disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease and schizophrenia; G00‐G99; Diseases of the nervous system, including Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and epilepsy; I00‐I99; Diseases of the circulatory system, including ischemic heart disease, lung disease, and cerebrovascular disease; J00‐J99; Diseases of the respiratory system, including asthma, pneumonia, and COPD. K00‐K93: Diseases of the digestive system, including Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, and liver diseases such as fatty liver; L00‐L99: Diseases of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, including atopic dermatitis and psoriasis; M00‐M99: Diseases of the musculoskeletal system and connective tissue, including rheumatoid arthritis; N00‐N99: Diseases of the genitourinary system, including nephritis.

In the Endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases area, which had the second highest number of diseases within DATTs, GLP‐1 and the glucagon receptor are therapeutic target molecules of the obesity drug Survodutide, currently under development by Boehringer Ingelheim [9]. Both proteins are registered as activatory therapeutic targets in DATTs. Furthermore, Amycretin, which has the potential to be a promising treatment option for metabolic diseases including obesity and type 2 diabetes, is under development by Novo Nordisk. Amycretin is a novel, unimolecular GLP‐1 receptor and amylin receptor agonist. The amylin receptor forms a heterodimer with the calcitonin receptor and one of three co‐receptors (RAMP1, RAMP2, or RAMP3) [10, 13]. It is available in both oral and subcutaneous formulations [11, 12]. The target molecules can also be searched using DATTs. In addition, the therapeutic target molecule of the obesity drug S‐309 309, currently under development by Shionogi, is 2‐acylglycerol O‐acyltransferase 2 (MGAT2; EC:2.3.1.22), and this molecule is listed as an inhibitory therapeutic target protein [14].

DATTs can also be used to identify therapeutic target proteins for metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). MAFLD is a new concept proposed in 2020 that redefines the traditional definition of “nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)” [15]. MAFLD affects more than 30% of the global population. MAFLD is a multi‐organ disease strongly associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD), the leading cause of death worldwide. Common key mechanisms such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and genetic predisposition link MAFLD to an increased risk of coronary artery disease, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure [16]. MAFLD is characterized by the presence of fatty liver despite the absence of significant alcohol consumption and has the potential to progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [17]. Among the 163 therapeutic target proteins associated with MAFLD in DATTs, 70 were inhibitory target proteins, and 89 were activatory target proteins.

To illustrate the utility of DATTs, we present an exemplary case using the disease search term “Narcolepsy” (Figure 2). In the “Search for protein or diseases” box of the DATTs HOME View, a user inputs the name of the disease “Narcolepsy”, which retrieves Disease ID C0027404 (Figure 2a). After clicking on “C0027404”, the page switches to the “DISEASE LIST” view. The output page provides the Disease ID, Name, UMLS ID, and ICD10 ID (Figure 2b). Below this information, therapeutic target proteins relevant to Narcolepsy are listed together with Protein IDs, relationships between diseases and target proteins (Activation, Inhibition, or Target), GeneSymbol, KEGG gene ID, UniProt ID, and references. For example, clicking on the therapeutic target protein “hypocretin neuropeptide precursor (HCRT)/Orexin” (Protein ID hsa:3060) associated with Narcolepsy switches the page to the “PROTEIN LIST” view [18, 19]. The output page provides the GeneSymbol, Definition, KEGG gene ID, and UniProt ID for the HCRT/orexin protein (Figure 2c). Below this protein information, associated diseases and the required regulation are provided. In the case of Narcolepsy, activation of the HCRT/orexin protein is required for treatment; conversely, HCRT/Orexin protein is therapeutic against Sleep Initiation and Maintenance Disorders/Insomnia [20, 21].

The tab to the right of the DATTs Home view is “Disease List”. Clicking this tab displays these diseases as they are listed in ICD‐10. Entering a disease name in the search box displays the code and classification in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10) (Figure S2). Additionally, in the “Protein List” view, proteins are classified based on “KEGG Pathways” (Figure S3). Details regarding these are provided in the Supporting Information.

Conclusion

The DATTs contains a collection of associations between diseases and therapeutic target proteins. In contrast to other such databases, the DATTs distinguishes the type of drug–protein interactions that lead to therapeutic effects (inhibition or activation). DATTs is expected to be useful as a valuable tool for research on disease mechanisms, novel drug development, and drug repositioning.

DATTs prioritizes the inclusion of high‐quality disease‐target relationships where the required actions (activation, inhibition, or targeting) for medical treatment can be clearly interpreted from the literature. At present, DATTs includes relatively few proteins from nonhuman species as therapeutic target molecules; thus, the number of entries involving nonhuman target proteins may be limited. The curation and development of the DATTs database are ongoing. We continuously collect disease‐therapeutic target protein relationships and are progressively expanding the database’s coverage.
