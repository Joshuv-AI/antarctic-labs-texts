Hemodynamics Beyond Ischemic Zone Underlies Early Neurological Deterioration in Minor Stroke with Large Vessel Occlusion
Authors: Zhijiao He, Lan Hong, Tingyu Yi, Xinru Wang, Juehua Zhu, Weijie Chen, Siyuan Li, Anqi Zhang, Nan Cao, Yifeng Ling, Guangjian Zhao, Dan Li, Ying Guo, Meihua Wu, Guozhi Lu, Ziran Wang, Danhong Wu, Qi Fang, Wenhuo Chen, Qiang Dong, Jean-Claude Baron, Xin Cheng, CHABLIS Collaboration
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early neurological deterioration (END) occurs in a substantial proportion of minor stroke patients with large vessel occlusion or severe stenosis (LVO/SS) and is a strong predictor of poor outcome, yet its underlying mechanisms remain debated. The cerebral blood volume (CBV) index is defined as the ratio of the mean CBV in the hypoperfusion (Tmax>6s) region to the mean CBV in the normal brain tissue, incorporating hemodynamics beyond the ischemic zone into its calculation. In this multicenter retrospective study, we evaluated the predictive value and pathophysiological implications of CBV index for END in patients with anterior circulation LVO/SS and mild symptoms. Among 401 eligible patients, END occurred in 21.7%. Stepwise logistic regression analysis revealed that higher CBV index was independently associated with END (adjusted OR [95% CI]: 3.85 [1.14, 12.96], p = 0.030). Mediation analysis revealed that 27.9% of the effect of CBV index on END was explained by increased hypoperfusion volume (indirect effect: 0.06, 95% CI: [0.02, 0.11], p = 0.007). These findings indicate that although a higher CBV index reflects favorable baseline collaterals, it may also indicate insufficient hemodynamic reserve and vulnerability to hypoperfusion progression, potentially facilitating extension of ischemia and subsequent END.

Introduction

Previous studies indicate that approximately one-third of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients with mild or transient neurological symptoms have large vessel occlusion or severe stenosis ((LVO/SS) [1,2] and that nearly 20–40% of patients with mild symptoms due to LVO/SS experience early neurological deterioration (END) [[3], [4], [5]], which is a strong predictor of permanent disability or mortality [6,7]. Nevertheless, the underlying pathophysiology and mechanisms of END in this setting remain incompletely understood.

Cerebral collateral circulation is recruited as a crucial compensatory mechanism to mitigate ischemic damage after vessel occlusion or severe stenosis. Notably, the mild symptoms in patients with LVO/SS may primarily be attributed to the presence of favorable leptomeningeal collaterals at symptom onset [8], which alleviates the burden of severely ischemic tissue and maintains relatively adequate perfusion within the affected vascular territory [[9], [10], [11]]. However, individual variability in cerebral hemodynamic reserve capacity may contribute to different risks of END, as insufficient blood flow reserve could eventually lead to collateral collapse and infarct expansion. Previous studies have identified independent imaging predictors of END, among which the volume of hypoperfusion tissue is commonly reported [4,5,12]. Nonetheless, hypoperfusion volume mainly reflects the degree of downstream perfusion [13], rather than the upstream capacity of collateral reserve. The potential contribution of collateral reserve to END in mild stroke patients with LVO has not been specifically investigated.

The cerebral blood volume (CBV) index is a quantitative perfusion imaging parameter representing the relative CBV in the hypoperfusion regions compared to normal brain tissue, and is interpreted as the degree of collateral vasodilation after an ischemic event [14]. A high CBV index has been validated as a reliable marker of robust collateral flow supply [[14], [15], [16]], and has shown a significant correlation with the outcome of reperfusion therapy [17]. By incorporating the perfusion status of non-ischemic regions into its calculation, the CBV index provides a comprehensive assessment of cerebral hemodynamics, and in turn may reflect the compensatory capacity of collateral circulation beyond the ischemic territory [16]. In this context, the CBV index may be mechanistically associated with END among patients with minor stroke due to LVO/SS, as END has been linked to the collateral collapse and extension of the penumbra into the oligemic tissue and possibly beyond [[18], [19], [20]]. However, prior studies have not specifically investigated whether the CBV index could predict END in mild stroke patients with LVO/SS.

In this study, we aimed to explore the predictive value of the CBV index for END. To further elucidate the role of the CBV index in END, a mediation analysis was performed to examine whether the relationship between the CBV index and END was mediated by hypoperfusion volume. We hypothesized that although a higher CBV index suggested favorable collateral flow status at baseline, it would paradoxically serve as an independent predictor of END in minor stroke patients due to LVO/SS. This would potentially be attributable to the fact that an elevated CBV index may reflect compromised cerebral blood flow reserve, with impending exhaustion of upstream collateral circulation and extension of brain ischemia, thereby increasing the risk of END.

Methods

Patient selection and clinical parameters

This retrospective study included patients from the CHinese Acute Tissue-Based imaging selection for Lysis In Stroke-Tenecteplase trials (CHABLIS-T and CHABLIS-T II), the CHABLIS registry, and the AIS registry of Huashan hospital. The CHABLIS-T and CHABLIS-T II trials were multicenter, randomized clinical trials that explored the efficacy and safety of intravenous tenecteplase treatment in the extended time window among patients with anterior circulation LVO/SS under the selection of perfusion imaging. Patients were enrolled in the CHABLIS-T trial from November 2019 to September 2021 and in the CHABLIS-T II trial from October 2021 to June 2023. Details of the CHABLIS-T trial and CHABLIS-T II trial have been published previously [21,22]. The CHABLIS registry is a nationwide, prospective multicenter cohort initiated in 2023, enrolling AIS patients with LVO/SS within 24 h from the last known well. This study included patients in this registry enrolled from September 2023 to November 2024. The AIS cohort of Huashan hospital prospectively and consecutively enrolls AIS patients who arrived in the ER within 24 h from last known well and underwent multimodal CT at baseline. This study included patients from this single-center AIS cohort enrolled between August 2015 and November 2024.

Patients meeting the following inclusion criteria were included: (1) presented within 24 h from last known well; (2) with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score (NIHSS)≤5 when arrived at the ER; (3) underwent baseline multimodal CT scanning, including non-contrast CT (NCCT), CT angiography (CTA), and CT perfusion (CTP); (4) had anterior circulation LVO/SS (≥70%) on baseline CTA. The degree of stenosis was assessed according to the method of NASCET (North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial) [23] or Warfarin-Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) study [24]; (5) completed follow-up NIHSS assessment at 72 h. Patients were excluded from our study if they (1) underwent endovascular treatment (EVT) prior to the occurrence of END, to minimize the potential confounding influence of procedural factors and post-recanalization hemodynamic changes; (2) had parenchymal hemorrhage or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage within 72 h; (3) exhibited symptoms that were inconsistent with the site of occlusion or severe stenosis; or (4) with poor CTP imaging quality for CBV index calculation. Patients who accepted rescue EVT after the occurrence of END were included in our study.

END was defined as either a 4-point increase in the total NIHSS score or a 2-point increase for any item within 72 h, without evidence of parenchymal hemorrhage on follow-up imaging.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized proxies.

Imaging analysis

At baseline, all patients completed multimodal CT scanning. CTP imaging was post-processed by F-STROKE (Neuroblam, Ltd. Co., Shanghai, China) [25]. Previously validated thresholds were applied to measure the volume of hypoperfusion lesion (time to maximum>6 s [Tmax>6s]) and core volume (relative cerebral blood flow [rCBF]<30%).

The CBV index was calculated automatically using F-STROKE (Fig. S1) according to the following formula:

CBV index=mean CBV within Tmax>6s lesion of the ischemic hemisphere/mean CBV of all Tmax≤4s area [14]

Hypoperfusion Intensity Ratio (HIR) was defined as the volume ratio of Tmax>10 s/Tmax>6 s, which was also automatically computed using F-STROKE. Higher HIR was considered a marker of a poorer tissue collateral flow [13,26,27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical Analysis was performed on STATA 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Figures were generated using Adobe Illustrator 2021(Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to test for the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while continuous variables with skewed distribution were described as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were depicted as numbers and percentages. Differences in demographic, clinical, and imaging variables between patients with and without END were assessed using the student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. A two-tailed p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variables with p-value<0.1 in univariable analyses or with clinical relevance were included in the backward stepwise regression to identify independent factors of END. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of the stepwise regression models were presented. A two-tailed p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A sensitivity analysis was performed in patients with vessel occlusion to eliminate the potential impact of differences in collateral circulation between patients with occlusion and severe stenosis on the association between CBV index and END.

To further evaluate the potential influence of cohort source on our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the single-center AIS cohort from Huashan hospital. In addition, we included cohort source as a fixed effect in the multivariable regression model and performed an interaction analysis to examine whether the association between CBV index and END differed by cohort source.

Mediation analysis was conducted to investigate the underlying mechanism by which the CBV index predicted END. Given our hypothesis that the CBV index reflects collateral reserve, which is associated with the extension of ischemic lesions, hypoperfusion volume was treated as the potential mediator, and END as the binary outcome. The association between CBV index and hypoperfusion volume was assessed by linear regression, while the relationship between hypoperfusion volume and END was tested using logistic regression models. Mediation analysis was further conducted after adjusting for baseline NIHSS, history of atrial fibrillation, and site of occlusion/severe stenosis in each model. Indirect, direct, and total effects were estimated using 1500 bootstrap replications, and statistical significance was defined by 95% confidence intervals (CI) excluding zero.

Discussion

In this study, focusing on minor stroke patients with LVO/SS treated with best medical management, we demonstrated that (1) a higher CBV index served as an independent risk predictor of END, even if patients initially presenting robust collateral flow; (2) a higher CBV index was associated with larger hypoperfusion volumes; and (3) the association between higher CBV index and a higher risk END was partially mediated by increased hypoperfusion volume.

The incidence of END in minor stroke with LVO/SS varies widely across cohorts, owing to differences in patient selection and the definition of END. Prior studies have reported a rate of END ranging from 12 to 40% in minor stroke with LVO [4,5,12,28], consistent with the incidence of approximately 20% observed in our cohort, highlighting the importance of understanding the mechanisms underlying non-hemorrhagic END.

Insufficient baseline collateral supply has been recognized as an important predictor of END in patients with LVO [29]. However, in our study, although patients who developed END exhibited significantly higher HIR in univariate analysis, indicating poorer collateral circulation, only higher CBV index remained as an independent predictor of END in the stepwise model. The low HIR values observed across the cohort suggest a predominance of slow progressors with relatively preserved collateral compensation at baseline [30,31], potentially limiting the discriminative ability of HIR for predicting END. The CBV index has also been considered a surrogate marker of collateral flow, with higher CBV index correlating with better collateral grades on CTA [14]. Interestingly, our findings revealed that a higher CBV index was independently associated with an increased risk of END, which may at first sight seem paradoxical. We propose that the CBV index may not only reflect the presence of collateral flow at baseline but also indicate the “reserve capacity” of the collateral circulation. Unlike other collateral metrics such as the HIR [13,32], the CBV index incorporates the perfusion status of hemodynamically normal brain tissue in its denominator [16], and therefore represents the relative difference in CBV between the hypoperfusion area and the non-ischemic regions. When vessel occlusion or severe stenosis occurs, arteries in the affected brain tissue dilate to preserve blood flow supply in the ischemic region, and normal brain tissue functions as a hemodynamic reservoir, sustaining perfusion in ischemic regions through collateral pathways. A higher CBV index, which reflects initially robust collateral circulation through extensive vasodilation, may paradoxically indicate an exhausted hemodynamic reserve in the hypoperfused territory, as the residual capacity for further dilation is limited. Once substantial vasodilation has occurred, the ability to further augment cerebral blood flow in response to progressive hypoperfusion becomes constrained, leaving the tissue susceptible to hemodynamic failure and subsequent END. Additionally, we found that the volume of hypoperfusion was also significantly associated with END, consistent with previous studies [4,12]. To further clarify the pathophysiological significance of our findings, we explored the relationship among CBV index, the volume of hypoperfusion and END using mediation analysis. Consistent with our hypothesis, higher CBV index was significantly associated with larger volume of hypoperfusion and its impact on END was partially mediated through this pathway. These findings suggest that impaired collateral reserve, as reflected by an elevated CBV index, contributed to END through expansion of the hypoperfusion tissue. As discussed above, a higher CBV index indicates greater vasodilation capacity within the hypoperfused territory, requiring increased recruitment of blood flow from adjacent regions to maintain stable perfusion pressure in persistent LVO/SS. This redistribution may enlarge the zone of compromised perfusion, leading to an expanded hypoperfusion lesion, which is vulnerable to infarction if any hemodynamic event occurs, such as blood pressure drop and extension of the thrombus [18,20,33] (Fig. 3).

To our knowledge, it is the first study to explore the association between the CBV index and END in patients presenting with mild symptoms due to LVO/SS, providing insights that may optimize reperfusion therapy strategies in this patient population. The efficacy and safety of reperfusion therapy in minor stroke patients remain controversial. The PRISM (NCT02072226) [34] and ARAMIS (NCT03661411) [35] trials confirmed the non-inferiority of antiplatelet therapy compared to IVT. Moreover, the TEMPO-2 study (NCT02398656) [36] revealed no benefit and highlighted potential risks of intravenous tenecteplase in patients with minor stroke and proven vessel occlusion, including in disabling minor stroke with LVO [37]. Observational studies have indicated possible benefits of EVT for minor stroke patients exhibiting target mismatch [[38], [39], [40]]. In contrast, recently published studies on EVT for medium vessel occlusion (MeVO), which enrolled a large number of minor stroke patients, demonstrated no significant benefit of EVT in this population [41,42]. Although current evidence does not routinely recommend reperfusion therapy for minor stroke patients with LVO/SS, nearly one-third of individuals managed with medical treatment alone experienced END, which is associated with poor prognosis [3]. This phenomenon highlights the need for early identification of patients at high risk of END in minor stroke with LVO/SS, in order to explore the potential benefits of reperfusion treatment in preventing END and improving functional outcomes specifically in this high-risk population. Our study demonstrated that higher CBV index was independently associated with the incidence of END in minor stroke patients with LVO/SS, suggesting its potential clinical utility in screening patients at high risk of END for reperfusion therapy. Moreover, the CBV index can be quickly obtained using commercial software following perfusion imaging, supporting its feasibility.

Several limitations of our study should be considered. Firstly, as patients included in this study derived from both observational studies and clinical trials, which may introduce cohort heterogeneity and potential selection bias. Although higher CBV index remained independently associated with END after adjustment for cohort source as a fixed effect and no statistically significant interaction was observed between CBV index and cohort source (Tables S5–S6), the association was attenuated after excluding the single-center AIS cohort (Table S4), which may be attributable to the reduced sample size and limited number of END events. Our findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in independent prospective multicenter cohorts. Second, the NIHSS scores were assessed by physicians from different centers, exposing to potential for inter-rater variability. Third, patients who underwent primary EVT were excluded from this study to minimize the confounding influence of procedural factors on END, which potentially resulted in selection bias. Fourth, The low HIR values in our cohort suggest that most included patients were slow progressors, which may be explained by the high proportion of patients with large-artery atherosclerosis (LAA) in our cohort (75.8%). LAA is common among Chinese patients with minor stroke and characterized by better preserved collateral compensation, compared with cardioembolism (CE). Therefore, whether our findings can be generalized to rapid progressors, patients with CE or patients with neurological deterioration occurring before imaging assessment requires further investigation. Moreover, due to the limited sample size, we did not explore the efficacy of reperfusion therapy in minor stroke patients with LVO/SS stratified by baseline CBV index. Further studies are warranted to investigate the clinical utility of the CBV index in identifying appropriate candidates for reperfusion therapy in this patient population. In addition, the specific pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the predictive value of the CBV index in END were not confirmed in this study. We hypothesize that the CBV index may serve as a surrogate marker of hemodynamic reserve, which is traditionally assessed by cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) [43]. A study of the relationship between CVR, CBV index and END in patients with LVO/SS is ongoing.

Conclusion

In minor stroke patients with LVO/SS, higher CBV index at baseline was an independent predictor of END, which might indicate hemodynamic impairment beyond the ischemic zone and impending exhaustion of blood flow reserve. Further studies are needed to evaluate the mechanism behind the role of CBV index and explore the clinical utility of CBV index in guiding reperfusion therapy selection for this patient population.
