Early Anticoagulation After Large Ischemic Stroke in the Initiation of Anticoagulation After Cardioembolic Stroke Study
Authors: Jayachandra Muppa, Shadi Yaghi, Liqi Shu, Nils Henninger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeClinical trial data indicate that early anticoagulation is generally safe after an ischemic stroke, but there is a paucity of real-world data regarding the safety of early initiation in large strokes. We sought to determine the association between the time to the initiation of anticoagulation and the 90-day risk of recurrent ischemic and major hemorrhagic events.MethodsWe analyzed consecutive patients with an acute large ischemic stroke (volume ≥60 mL) who were included in the multicenter Initiation of Anticoagulation After Cardioembolic Stroke (IAC) study. We assessed recurrent ischemic events (transient ischemic attack, ischemic stroke, and systemic embolism), major hemorrhagic events (symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage [sICH] and major extracranial hemorrhage [ECH]), and the combined outcome within 90 days.ResultsThis study included 155 patients (57.4% females) with a median NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) score of 14. Only 11 (7.1%) events occurred, comprising 4 (2.6%) ischemic events, 2 (1.3%) sICHs, and 5 (3.2%) major ECHs. The median time to anticoagulation initiation did not differ significantly between subjects with and without recurrent ischemic events (13.5 days versus 9 days, p=0.221), major hemorrhagic events (4 days versus 10 days, p=0.334), or combined events (13 days versus 9.5 days, p=0.980). None of the patients who were started on anticoagulation within 7 days (n=71, 45.8%) experienced an ischemic event, and only one had an sICH, which occurred 20 days later. Two of the three recurrent strokes occurred prior to anticoagulation initiation in patients in whom this was done after 7 days. ConclusionsEarly anticoagulation after large ischemic stroke (volume ≥60 mL) in the IAC study was associated with a low risk of sICH and no recurrent ischemic strokes.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with cardioembolic stroke caused by atrial fibrillation (AF) have a higher risk of recurrent ischemic stroke.123 Anticoagulation is highly effective in preventing recurrent stroke in patients with AF, but the ideal time point for anticoagulation initiation after a large ischemic stroke is uncertain due to the concern that early initiation could increase the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH). This is an important issue because AF-related strokes are associated with greater stroke severity and an increased risk of hemorrhagic transformation (HT).4567 The guidelines vary between different organizations, but they generally recommend delaying anticoagulation initiation further as the stroke size increases.38 The Early versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Post-ischemic Stroke Patients with Atrial Fibrillation (ELAN) trial recently showed that there was no excess hemorrhage risk associated with early anticoagulation administered at 6 or 7 days in the 229 (22.8%) patients with large stroke included in that study.9 However, there is a paucity of real-world data regarding the safety of early initiation after large ischemic strokes.

In this study we sought to determine whether there is an association between the time to anticoagulation initiation and the 90-day risk of subsequent hemorrhagic and ischemic events in patients with large AF-related strokes using data from a large multicenter real-world cohort.

METHODS

Study population

We included patients enrolled in the multicenter Initiation of Anticoagulation After Cardioembolic Stroke (IAC) study, which pooled data from stroke registries across eight comprehensive stroke centers in the United States.10 Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at each of the participating centers. The study methodology has been described in detail previously.10 In brief, the study enrolled consecutive patients hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke in the setting of AF (history of AF or AF diagnosed on admission) and who were started on anticoagulation at 0 to 30 days from the index stroke. To make our observations consistent with the ELAN trial, which defined early anticoagulation in major stroke as initiation after 6 or 7 days, we investigated outcomes relating to anticoagulation initiation within the first 7 days. We were also interested in the ultraearly period during the first 0–4 days, and so to achieve a similar distribution of cases across epochs, we defined the following four time-to-anticoagulation epochs of interest: 0–4 days (n=34), 5–7 days (n=37), 8–14 days (n=47), and 15–30 days (n=37).

The location of the largest ischemic lesion was determined on brain MRI (or CT if MRI was unavailable) using the a×b×c/2 method by local study investigators.1011 For the purpose of the present study and in line with a previous report,12 we defined a large stroke as having a volume ≥60 mL. In the IAC study, ischemic lesion volumes ≥60 mL were categorized into 60–80, 81–100, and >100 mL, which we combined for the main analysis of this study.

The clinical variables of interest included age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, CHA2DS2-VASc (congestive heart failure, hypertension, age [65 to 74 years=1 point, ≥75 years=2 points], diabetes, prior stroke [doubled], vascular disease, and sex category [female]) score on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) at admission, use of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) without mechanical thrombectomy (MT), and MT (with and without IVT). We collected information on preadmission antiplatelet, anticoagulant, and statin use, as well as the low-density lipoprotein level. Early HT was defined as hemorrhage occurring within 48 hours from the index event on CT or MRI, categorized as hemorrhagic infarction (HI) or parenchymal hemorrhage (PH).101314 Finally, we recorded the time to anticoagulation after the index stroke and the type of oral anticoagulant used (warfarin or direct oral anticoagulant).

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcomes of interest were recurrent ischemic events (stroke, TIA, and systemic embolism), major hemorrhagic events, as well as the combined recurrent ischemic events and major hemorrhagic events within 90 days as described previously.1015 Specifically, stroke was defined as the sudden onset of a new focal neurological deficit of vascular origin at a site consistent with the territory of a major cerebral artery and categorized as ischemic or hemorrhagic. TIA was defined as a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain ischemia without an acute infarction. Systemic embolism was defined as an acute vascular occlusion of an extremity or organ confirmed in imaging, surgery, or autopsy. Major hemorrhagic events consisted of major extracranial hemorrhage (ECH) and sICH. ECH was defined as major ECH that required blood transfusion, was associated with a decrease in hemoglobin of ≥2 g/dL, or symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ. sICH was defined as neurological deterioration in the setting of any new or worsening hemorrhage, and hemorrhage being the cause of a neurological deterioration quantified as a change in NIHSS score of ≥4 points.1016

Statistical analyses

The normality of a distribution was assessed graphically and by using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. Bivariable relationships were tested using Spearman's correlation. Multiple linear regression was used to identify variables associated with the time to anticoagulation initiation. The model was adjusted for factors that differed between subjects in a univariable analysis at p<0.05 (including anticoagulation prior to the index event, admission NIHSS score, stroke location [anterior circulation, posterior circulation, or both], and MT) as well as additional clinical variables that could conceivably affect the decision-making regarding anticoagulation initiation (trichotomized infarct volume, HI, PH, and thrombolysis). Additional sensitivity analyses were applied to patients with infarct volumes of >80 mL and >100 mL. To avoid model overfitting, variables were removed sequentially from the models based on likelihood ratios (p>0.1). The normality and homoscedasticity of the data in linear regression analyses were confirmed using residual diagnostics. Collinearity diagnostics rejected the presence of collinearity. One-way analysis of variance on ranks was used for univariable comparisons of continuous variables between groups. Two-sided significance tests were used throughout, with a two-sided probability value of p<0.05 considered statistically significant except where stated otherwise. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 24, IBM Corp.).

Ethical approval

The Institutional Review Board at each of the eight participating comprehensive stroke centers in the USA granted approval for this study; the original study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Rhode Island Hospital (IRB number 1347342-1). The study procedures followed the institutional guidelines as well as the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Due to the study's retrospective nature, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) waiver of authorization was granted.

RESULTS

Study population

Among the 2,084 patients included in the IAC study, 155 subjects fulfilled the criteria of the present study and so were included in the analyses (Fig. 1). Table 1 compares baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients, and Supplementary Table 1 (in the online-only Data Supplement) summarizes the baseline characteristics of included patients stratified by outcome events. Fig. 2A depicts the individual patient data showing the association of the admission NIHSS score with the time to anticoagulation, and Fig. 2B illustrates the time between anticoagulation initiation and outcome events.

Factors associated with the time to anticoagulation initiation after the index stroke

In the included cohort, the median time to anticoagulation was 10 days (interquartile range [IQR]=5–14 days). Anticoagulation was initiated at 0–4 days after the index stroke in 34 (21.9%) patients, at 5–7 days in 37 (23.9%), at 8–14 days in 47 (30.3%), and at 15–30 days in 37 (23.9%). Table 2 summarizes the unadjusted bivariable relationships of patient characteristics with the time to anticoagulation initiation after the index stroke. Analyses using time to anticoagulation as a continuous variable revealed that prior anticoagulation (p=0.004) and stroke in both the anterior and posterior circulations (p=0.004) were associated with earlier anticoagulation initiation, whereas stroke restricted to an anterior location (p=0.004) and MT (p=0.049) were associated with later anticoagulation initiation (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). Notably, the time to anticoagulation initiation did not differ between patients with and without early HT (p=0.276).

There was a positive correlation between the admission NIHSS score and the time to anticoagulation initiation (ρ=0.223, p=0.006) (Table 2). To gain additional insight into whether selection bias was present toward a perceived lower risk with ultraearly anticoagulation (after 0–4 days), we reanalyzed the associations between admission NIHSS score, stroke location, and trichotomized stroke size according to the defined time-to-anticoagulation epochs. It was particularly interesting that this analysis revealed that the admission NIHSS score did not differ between patients who were started on anticoagulation after 0–4 days and those who were started on anticoagulation at later time points (Fig. 2C). Moreover, there was a trend toward the presence of both anterior and posterior infarctions (Fig. 2E) and an overall greater infarct burden (Fig. 2F) among patients with ultraearly anticoagulation initiation.

In the multivariable linear regression analysis, a lower admission NIHSS score, prior anticoagulation, and stroke involving both the anterior and posterior circulations were independently associated with earlier anticoagulation initiation (Table 3). When we restricted analyses to patients with stroke volumes of >80 mL and >100 mL, the only factors independently associated with later anticoagulation initiation were 1) a higher admission NIHSS score among patients with an infarct volume of >80 mL and 2) strokes including both the anterior and posterior circulations among patients with an infarct volume of >100 mL (Table 4).

Associations of timing of anticoagulation initiation and patient characteristics with recurrent ischemic events and major hemorrhagic events

We observed a relatively low event rate, with only 11 (7.1%) patients experiencing a recurrent ischemic event or major hemorrhage within 90 days of the index stroke. Most (81.8%) of these occurred within 30 days from the index stroke: 85.7% of major hemorrhagic events and 75.0% of recurrent ischemic events. We observed two (1.3%) sICHs, four (2.6%) recurrent ischemic events (three recurrent ischemic strokes and one systemic embolism), and five (3.2%) major ECHs.

In univariable analyses, hypertension was less common in subjects with recurrent ischemic events than in those without recurrent events (25.0% versus 79.2%, p=0.029). The median [IQR] admission score on the NIHSS was higher in subjects with major hemorrhagic events than in those who did not have a major hemorrhagic event (25 [20.5–28.5] versus 13 [6–20], p<0.001). The other baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between patients with and without recurrent events (all p>0.05; data not shown). Overall, there were no significant associations between the time-to-anticoagulation epoch and the distributions of recurrent ischemia and sICH (Fig. 2D).

The median [IQR] time to anticoagulation initiation did not differ between subjects with and without combined events (13 [3.5–15] days versus 9.5 [5–14] days, p=0.980), recurrent ischemic events (13.5 [12.5–17.5] days versus 9 [5–14] days, p=0.221), or major hemorrhage (4 [2.5–15] days versus 10 [5–14] days, p=0.334). None of the subjects who were started on anticoagulation within 7 days experienced an ischemic event, while two of the three recurrent strokes occurred prior to anticoagulation initiation (Fig. 2A and B). Conversely, four subjects who were started on anticoagulation within 7 days developed a major hemorrhage. One subject who was started on the day of the index stroke experienced a fatal sICH 20 days later; they had an admission NIHSS score of 36, stroke volume of 81–100 mL, and evidence of an early PH. Three patients had a major ECH, of which one was fatal, occurring on day 4 after anticoagulation initiation. The nonfatal ECHs occurred at 1 and 6 days after anticoagulation initiation, with both associated with a 90-day score on the modified Rankin Scale of 3. Approximately one-third of subjects (n=52) had evidence of early HT, 19 (36.5%) of whom were started on anticoagulation within the first 7 days after their stroke. As mentioned above, a fatal sICH occurred at 20 days after anticoagulation initiation in a patient with an early PH. In contrast, the second sICH occurred within 1 day in a patient with early HI after MT (with an admission NIHSS score of 21 and an infarct size of 81–100 mL) who was started on anticoagulation at 16 days after the index stroke (Fig. 2A and B).

DISCUSSION

Anticoagulation is effective at preventing recurrent ischemic stroke in patients AF.1718 The ELAN trial showed that there was no excess hemorrhage risk associated with early anticoagulation initiated after 6 or 7 days in patients with large stroke. The definition of large stroke in ELAN was pragmatic, and this facilitates its adoption in clinical practice. The data support initiating anticoagulation at 6 or 7 days after a large stroke, including in those with HT.91920 However, there is a paucity of real-world data regarding the safety of early anticoagulation initiation after large strokes, particularly within the first 6 days.

Our multivariable analysis revealed that the time to anticoagulation was positively associated with the admission NIHSS score but negatively associated with stroke severity as assessed by the involvement of both the anterior and posterior circulations and with the trichotomized stroke volume. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by our analysis being stratified according to the time-to-anticoagulation epochs. This analysis showed that the NIHSS score of subjects who were started on anticoagulation during the ultraearly epoch (after 0–4 days) did not differ significantly from those during other epochs, and actually showed a trend toward a more-severe deficit as indicated by a higher admission NIHSS score. At the same time, it is important to note that the admission NIHSS score does not necessarily reflect the NIHSS score at the time when treatment decisions about anticoagulation are made, such as due to improvements with thrombolysis and MT. Moreover, the NIHSS score may underestimate the severity of strokes in the right hemisphere and the posterior circulation. Nevertheless, while our study design precluded us from drawing firm conclusions, this observation suggests that patients with the greatest stroke burden were likely considered to have a very high risk of recurrent embolization, favoring earlier anticoagulation.

More importantly, we found no associations of the time to anticoagulation initiation with recurrent ischemic and major hemorrhagic events among patients with large stroke, approximately 45% and 22% of whom were started within 7 days and within 4 days from their index stroke, respectively, in this contemporary, real-world cohort.2122 Similar to recent observations,20 early anticoagulation initiation in patients with HT appeared to be safe; we observed only a single case of sICH, which occurred 20 days after initiating anticoagulant therapy, which argues against the notion that the early initiation per se predisposed this patient to hemorrhage. These are important observations because current guidelines support delaying anticoagulation initiation for 12–14 days in patients with large strokes based on the presumed higher risk of hemorrhagic events.2324 Consistent with previous observations that earlier anticoagulation initiation mitigates the risk of ischemic events,91725 none of our patients who was started on anticoagulation within the first 7 days experienced a recurrent ischemic event. This is consistent with previous observations, such as from the VISTA (Virtual International Stroke Trials Archive) cohort demonstrating that earlier anticoagulation initiation mitigates the risk of ischemic events.91725 Together our observations thus add to the increasing evidence challenging the prevailing safety concerns regarding early anticoagulation after a large stroke and supports the notion that anticoagulation should not be withheld or delayed in these patients.369172226

The strengths of our study were the inclusion a large multicenter cohort and the use of an unbiased imaging-based definition of a large stroke. Since patients were recruited from multiple stroke centers and our total major hemorrhagic event rate within 90 days was similar to previous published data,9151722 our results are likely to be generalizable to the broader stroke population. The limitations of this study relate to its retrospective design, modest sample size, and small number of outcome events. The IAC study was completed prior to the publication of the recent ELAN trial that stratified the timing of treatment initiation according to the size of the infarct or the severity of the stroke. Therefore, the collection of clinical variables of interest for the IAC study was not informed by this trial, which precluded a one-to-one translation. Stroke volumes were assessed locally without central adjudication, and the exact volumes of strokes larger than 100 mL were unknown. A more granular understanding of the association of the exact stroke volume, particularly among patients with very large strokes, could help explain why the NIHSS score in this cohort was associated with the time to anticoagulation. While this may help to translate our results to other studies based on the NIHSS score, this would arguably not materially impact the main finding of a low risk of sICH with early anticoagulation initiation. Moreover, possible factors leading to the decision for early anticoagulation initiation were unknown, rendering our study vulnerable to selection bias. Specifically, it is possible that patients were selected based on a perceived lower risk, which could contribute to the favorable event rates seen in the early initiation group. However, this concern might have been alleviated by our observation that stroke severity as assessed by the NIHSS score, stroke volume, and stroke distribution was not significantly lower in the earliest anticoagulation group. Thus, our observations provide important data on early anticoagulation initiation after large strokes. Since our study was performed at comprehensive stroke centers, its results might not be applicable to other clinical settings. For these reasons, the present results should be utilized to generate hypotheses that can be tested in future larger multicenter cohorts.

In conclusion, this multicenter study involving a cohort of patients with large strokes (volume ≥60 mL) found an overall low event rate for both major hemorrhage and recurrent ischemic events without an overt association between the timing of the anticoagulation initiation and outcome events. Our observations add to the accumulating data challenging prevailing safety concerns regarding the use of early anticoagulation after a large stroke, and might alleviate concerns about early anticoagulation initiation in such patients with strong indications for anticoagulation.
