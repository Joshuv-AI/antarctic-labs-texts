Predicting breast cancer response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy with ultrasound-based deep learning radiomics models —— dual-center study
Authors: Jinhui Liu, Xiaoling Leng, Zhifan Yuan, Yuning Li, Haibo Luo, Junjun Chen, Ruoxin Li, Xia Li, Zhizhong He, Qiao Zou, Changchun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this study is to develop and validate a deep learning fusion model based on early (two-cycle) ultrasound images, using stacking fusion technology for the intratumoral and peritumoral regions, to predict early tumor response in breast cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC).Materials and methodsThis study enrolled breast cancer patients who received 6 to 8 cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) between May 2019 and September 2023 from two medical centers. In the training set, the ResNet deep learning method was employed to construct a deep learning model based on ultrasound image data from intratumoral and peritumoral regions. A deep learning fusion model was established using stacking technology, which was tested on internal and external validation sets to calculate the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and clinical decision curve (DCA).ResultsA total of 469 breast cancer patients were included in this study. The AUC values of the fusion model DLRS3 in the training set for 3 mm ROI (ROI3mm), 5 mm ROI (ROI5mm), 10 mm ROI (ROI10mm), and intratumoral region (ROIin) were 0.858, 0.872, 0.919, and 0.848, respectively. The AUC values in the internal validation set were 0.890, 0.965, 0.915, and 0.840, respectively, while in the external validation set, they were 0.844, 0.886, 0.938, and 0.905, respectively.ConclusionThis study successfully developed a fusion model for predicting early tumor response in breast cancer patients receiving NAC. The model combines deep learning methods based on early NAC ultrasound images and demonstrates high predictive performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15148-y.

Background

Female breast cancer became the leading cause of global cancer incidence in 2020, surpassing lung cancer for the first time, making it the “number one cancer worldwide” [1]. Employing comprehensive and personalized treatment plans while accurately predicting patient outcomes is crucial for improving the cure rate of breast cancer. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is a key therapeutic approach for achieving breast-conserving surgery in locally advanced breast cancer. The high heterogeneity of breast cancer results in potential variations in patient outcomes post-treatment, particularly evident in the efficacy of NAC [2, 3]. Therefore, assessing the differences in tumor heterogeneity in breast cancer before and after NAC aids in evaluating treatment efficacy. If precise biological information reflecting tumor heterogeneity can be utilized to predict the ultimate efficacy of NAC in its early stages, it could timely and effectively influence clinicians’ decisions regarding personalized breast cancer treatment, thus enhancing the cure rate. However, the majority of studies focus on imaging features of the primary lesions of breast cancer, overlooking the potentially informative peritumoral imaging characteristics that may contain tumor microenvironment information. The tumor microenvironment plays a significant role in tumor progression and metastasis [4].

Radiomics (RS) is a technique that applies computer technology to extract quantitative features from the region of interest (ROI) in medical images, which are often unidentifiable by naked eye examination or measurable by conventional imaging methods [5]. Compared to RS, deep learning radiomics (DLR) possesses significant advantages. It can directly analyze raw image pixels, enabling in-depth exploration of imaging data through automatic learning and quantification of features [6, 7]. Multiple studies have confirmed the positive role of transfer learning in the clinical application of breast cancer, including predicting axillary lymph node status [8], evaluating molecular subtypes [9], and assessing treatment responses [10]. Based on these studies, this research established a deep learning model based on the intratumoral and peritumoral regions of ultrasound breast cancer. By utilizing DLR-based processing of ultrasound image data, this model is expected to offer relevant information for predicting the status of NAC and enhance the accuracy of predicting chemotherapy responses. The combination of ultrasound imaging and radiomics techniques (RS and DLR) can complement the deficiency of quantitative parameters in conventional ultrasound while addressing the issue of lower frequency in other imaging examinations, thus showcasing the advantages of timely and accurate quantitative assessment of the efficacy of NAC in breast cancer [11].

Stacking model fusion is a method that employs ensemble learning strategies aiming to enhance overall predictive performance by integrating predictions from multiple base classifiers [12]. Its primary advantage lies in overcoming the limitations of individual classifiers, enhancing overall predictive performance by synthesizing predictions from multiple classifiers [13]. When dealing with complex issues, stacking model fusion can offer superior generalization abilities and also exhibits a certain level of robustness towards imbalanced data features or higher noise levels [14]. Utilizing stacking model fusion to combine deep learning models facilitates predicting the status of NAC, thereby further enhancing the predictive performance of deep learning models.

This research aims to utilize ultrasound radiomics technique based on intratumoral and peritumoral regions to capture the time nodes when significant changes in tumor heterogeneity occur during the early stages of chemotherapy for breast cancer. By employing RS and DLR methods to reflect tumor heterogeneity through radiomic features, this study intends to conduct model fusion on DLR using the stacking model fusion technique. Furthermore, it aims to construct a joint model combining clinical and ultrasound models. The goal is to make a more valuable and timely contribution to predicting efficacy in the early stages of NAC. Ultimately, this approach aims to enable clinicians to make more optimal personalized treatment decisions promptly for NAC patients.

Materials and methods

The review boards of all participating tertiary referral centers (the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University [hereafter, Xinjiang center] and The Tenth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University [hereafter, Dongguan center] approved this retrospective multicenter study. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Pathological selection and general information

A total of 469 patients with breast cancer from two medical centers (from May 2019 to September 2023) who treated with 6 to 8 cycles of NAC were considered for inclusion in this study (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria: (Ⅰ) Histologically confirmed unilateral primary invasive breast cancer without distant metastasis; (Ⅱ) Completed a full course of NAC without receiving other treatments; (Ⅲ) Pathological examination post-surgery confirmed either pathological complete response (pCR) or non-pCR; (Ⅳ) Underwent breast ultrasound examination before surgery and after two cycles of NAC. Exclusion criteria: (Ⅰ) Incomplete clinical data or lack of pre-processed ultrasound images; (Ⅱ) Unavailability of ultrasound measurements of the tumor. This study received approval from the hospital’s ethics committee.

For model development, patients from the Xinjiang center were randomly assigned to the training and internal validation sets in a 7:3 ratio using stratified sampling based on pCR status, ensuring a balanced distribution of responders and non-responders. External validation was performed using an independent cohort from the Dongguan centers.

Instruments and methods

Both medical centers (Xinjiang and Dongguan) used the same brand and model of ultrasound equipment (GE LOGIQ E9) and high-frequency linear array transducers (L-16-5), with the mechanical index consistently set at 0.16. Image acquisition protocols were harmonized across centers, with standardized settings for key parameters including probe frequency (8–12 MHz), gain range (50–60 dB), and dynamic focal position. All sonographers underwent standardized training to ensure consistency in scanning planes (transverse and longitudinal planes of the tumor’s maximal diameter), patient positioning (supine or lateral decubitus), and image storage format (DICOM with lossless compression). Ultrasound examinations were conducted by ultrasound physicians with over 10 years of experience. Patients were positioned in a supine position, with both arms raised and extended above the head at a 90° angle to fully expose the breasts and bilateral axillae, and if necessary, patients were asked to turn on their sides. Ultrasound images after two cycles of NAC were collected for all female breast cancer patients in this study to observe tumor longitudinal, transverse, and anteroposterior diameters, calcifications, and Doppler blood flow characteristics.

The quality evaluation criteria for ultrasound images are as follows [15, 16] (1) Morphological contour: Clear and distinguishable boundaries; (2) Image appearance: Uniform and delicate; (3) Ductal structure: Clear, with the liver showing four levels of branching.

Evaluation of NAC and pathological response

Based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network standards in the United States, all enrolled patients, categorized by molecular subtypes, underwent 6–8 cycles of NAC. Chemotherapy regimens primarily involved taxanes or anthracyclines combined with taxanes, and HER2-positive patients received trastuzumab. The Miller-Payne grading system was utilized to assess the pathological response after NAC [17]. This system consists of 5 grades:

Grade 1: No change or some alteration to individual malignant cells but no reduction in overall cellularity;

Grade 2: A minor loss of tumor cells but overall cellularity still high; up to 30% loss;

Grade 3: Between an estimated 30% and 90% reduction in tumor cells;

Grade 4: A marked disappearance of tumor cells such that only small clusters or widely dispersed individual cells remain; more than 90% loss of tumor cells;

Grade 5: No malignant cells identifiable in sections from the site of the tumor, only vascular fibroelastotic stroma remains often containing macrophages. However, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) may be present.

Based on postoperative pathological evaluation, patients were categorized into the pathological complete response group (pCR, Grade 5) and the non-complete response group (non-pCR, Grade 1–4). All surgical specimens were evaluated by specialized pathologists using a single-blinded method.

Image segmentation and expansion

Using a single-blinded method, reader 1 (L.X.L.) with over 10 years of ultrasound experience imported ultrasound images after two cycles of NAC into ITK-Snap (version 4.0.1, http://www.itksnap.org/) from the University of Pennsylvania and the University of Utah. Reader 1 manually delineated the tumor boundary along the edge of the tumor and identified the ROI, encompassing angular, lobulated, and surrounding crab-like and spiculated signs. With the ROI edge as the baseline, the ROI was expanded outward by 3 mm, 5 mm, and 10 mm, resulting in segmented images ROI3mm, ROI5mm, ROI10mm, and ROIin. All ultrasound images were spatially calibrated using the pixel spacing parameter (PixelSpacing tag) from the DICOM metadata to ensure that expansion distances of 3 mm, 5 mm, and 10 mm accurately corresponded to real-world physical dimensions. To accommodate tumors of varying shapes and sizes, a morphological approach was employed to radially expand outward from each point along the tumor boundary, preserving the geometric characteristics of the original tumor contour. To evaluate the feature stability, reader 1 (L.X.L.) repeated the segmentation twice in 1 week, and reader 2 (Z.F.Y.) independently delineated the segmentation in 50 randomly chosen patients. The interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the agreement of the feature extraction between observers and within observers. Ratings of ICC were assigned as follows: an ICC of less than 0.40 was considered ‘Poor’, 0.40–0.59 was labeled ‘Fair’, 0.60–0.74 was categorized as ‘Good’, and 0.74–1.00 was deemed ‘Excellent’.

Construction of traditional radiomics models

The delineated ROI images were imported into pyradiomics (version 3.0.1, https://github.com/AIM-Harvard/pyradiomics). Utilizing the Wavelet filter on the acquired ultrasound images while retaining the original image, image feature extraction was performed. In total, 851 radiomics features were extracted for each tumor. Additionally, in order to eliminate batch effects caused by different medical institutions, different ultrasound scanning devices, and different image scanning protocols, the open-source software package neuroCombat (https://github.com/Jfortin1/neuroCombat) was utilized to remove batch effects from the data collected from two medical institutions.

We devised a procedure for reducing dimensions and selecting robust radiomics features in the training set (n = 238 [Xinjiang center]). The extracted features were normalized using Z-score normalization. Before the feature selected process, the abnormal and missing values were replaced by the median, the ICC was calculated to ensure repeatability and stability of features with the threshold of 0.74. Initial radiomic feature selection was conducted via student’s t-test, followed by Pearson correlation coefficient assessment to evaluate inter-feature correlations. Features above a threshold of 0.8 were selected to identify robust radiomic predictors of NAC response. Features were standardized (zero mean, unit variance) prior to model fitting in order to balance complexity and performance. The selected features were weighted by their LASSO regression coefficients to generate a radiomics score for each patient [18]. The random forest method was employed to establish a radiomics model using the selected features. Subsequently, a radiomics model was constructed using random forest, with the open-source SHAP package (https://github.com/shap/shap) quantifying feature contributions through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values, enabling the visual interpretation of key predictors.

Training and construction of neural network models

The ResNet neural network models were utilized for training, with ResNet comprising ResNet18 (DLR18), ResNet34 (DLR34), ResNet50 (DLR50), ResNet101 (DLR101), and ResNet152 (DLR152) selections [19]. To optimize model parameters and hyperparameters, 50% of patients were randomly selected from the training set (n = 238 [Xinjiang center]). The accuracy (ACC) was computed to evaluate validation performance, and training was halted if there was no increase in ACC over ten consecutive epochs. Breast cancer ultrasound images after two cycles of NAC were input into the trained convolutional networks. Each image underwent feature extraction with equally weighted deep learning features, and gradient-based Class Activation Maps (CAMs) were generated from the last convolutional layer to visualize coarse localization maps highlighting regions crucial for predicting lesions. Feature selection and deep learning model construction followed the aforementioned procedure. For comparative analysis, deep learning models are fused based on different residual blocks. DLR18 and DLR34, using BasicBlock as the residual block, are fused to form DLRS2, while DLR50, DLR101, and DLR152, using BottleBlock as the residual block, are fused to form DLRS3. Further details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Selection of clinical and ultrasound indicators and model construction

The data collection involved two patient groups, including their age, menopausal status, T stage (according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Breast Cancer TNM staging criteria), NAC regimens, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), Ki-67, and histological grading clinical data. Additionally, routine ultrasound features of breast cancer after two cycles of NAC were recorded, encompassing differences in tumor longitudinal, transverse, and anteroposterior diameters. Single-factor and multi-factor analyses were conducted, and indicators correlated with NAC were selected. These selected indicators were then integrated into logistic regression models to respectively establish clinical and ultrasound models.

Construction and performance evaluation of combined models

The combined model integrating the aforementioned RS model, DLRS, clinical, and ultrasound independent predictive factors was established and presented in the form of a nomogram.

Comparison with physicians’ assessment

To validate clinical utility, three board-certified breast physicians with 25 years of experience independently reviewed pre-treatment ultrasound images from the external validation cohort (n = 129) under double-blind conditions. The physicians were provided with anonymized images and baseline clinical data but were blinded to the NAC regimens and final pCR status. Their assessments were based on standard sonographic criteria, including tumor border irregularity, posterior acoustic shadowing, and internal vascularity. The majority voting result was used as the consensus of the physicians. The model performance was statistically compared using DeLong’s test for AUC differences and McNemar’s test for discrepancies in sensitivity and specificity.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.0, https://www.R-project.org) and Python (version 3.10, https://www.python.org/). Count data was represented as percentages (%) and compared using the chi-square test. For continuous data, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized to assess normality. Data conforming to normal distribution were presented as x ± s and compared using the Student t-test. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to filter radiomic features. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess predictive performance of each model, and the DeLong test was applied to compare the superiority of models. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV) of each model were calculated based on the optimal cutoff value. Calibration curves were used to assess model consistency, and decision curve analysis (DCA) quantified the net benefit of models at different probability thresholds. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

For more details, please see Supplementary Methods.

Results

Clinical pathological and ultrasonic characteristics

This study included a total of 340 breast cancer patients diagnosed by surgical pathology and treated with 6 to 8 cycles of NAC from May 2019 to September 2023. Among them, there were 340 patients from the Xinjiang center[age, years (mean(SD)), 48.26(9.49)] and 129 patients from the Dongguan center[age, years (mean(SD)), 49.02(10.06)]. In the training set, there were 90 cases of pCR (37.8%) and 148 cases of non-pCR (62.2%), while in the internal validation set, there were 34 cases of pCR (33.3%) and 68 cases of non-pCR (66.7%). There was no significant statistical difference observed between the groups (p = 0.507). Clinical and ultrasound data for the patients are detailed in Table 2 (Table 2 is placed at the end of the article.). Results of the multivariate analysis are available in Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2 (Supplementary Figures S1). Stratified sampling effectively maintained consistent pCR proportions across datasets; the training set (37.8% pCR) and the internal validation set (33.3% pCR) showed no significant difference (χ² = 0.441,​​ p = 0.507). The external validation set included 38.8% pCR cases, reflecting the real-world clinical distribution observed at the second center.​ In both the training and internal validation sets, significant correlations were found between patients achieving pCR and ER, Her-2, as well as differences in ultrasound anteroposterior diameters (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in other clinical pathological and ultrasonic characteristics (p > 0.05). Additionally, there was no significant association between NAC regimen and whether patients achieved a pCR. Logistic regression models incorporating these predictive factors were established for clinical model and ultrasound model, and detailed performance metrics (area under the ROC curve(AUC), accuracy(ACC), sensitivity(SEN), specificity(SPE), positive predictive value(PPV), negative predictive value(NPV)) can be found in Table 1.

Feature selection and model construction of radiomics models

Before batch correction, except for the relatively small differences in the distribution of radiomics features in the peritumoral area of 10 mm (Fig. 2), significant differences were observed in the distribution of other radiomics features (p < 0.001). After preliminary screening using Student’s t-test and Pearson correlation coefficient analysis, the LASSO regression method was employed to further filter radiomic features. Construction and validation of deep learning models

In the ResNet deep learning models, DLR152 exhibited the most optimal predictive performance, with detailed metrics (AUC, ACC, SEN, SPE, PPV, NPV) provided in Table 3. Across models of 3 mm, 5 mm, 10 mm, and the intratumoral region, the AUC for DLR152 was higher than in other ResNet models (Supplementary Figures S9). To enhance the predictive accuracy of the ResNet deep learning models and effectively mitigate model overfitting, stacking classifier models were further employed to fuse ResNet18 and ResNet34 and ResNet50, ResNet101, ResNet152 based on the ResNet residual network layers. This fusion resulted in models DLRS2 and DLRS3. Across the various ROI models, the fusion stacking models of deep learning outperform single deep learning models, with DLRS3 demonstrating superior predictive performance compared to DLRS2 (Fig. 3). Among various ROI models, In the training set, DLRS3(ROI10mm) exhibited the optimal predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.919 (0.852–0.986), while in the external validation set, DLRS3(ROI5mm) displayed the best predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.965(0.883–1.047). Further details regarding AUC, ACC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV can be found in Table 3 (Table 3 is placed at the end of the article.).

Discussion

This study successfully developed and validated a fusion model for early-stage tumor response in breast cancer to NAC. The model combines deep learning methods based on early-stage ultrasound images under NAC. Following rigorous training and testing, the fusion model DLRS3 demonstrated high predictive performance. The training set had respective AUC values of 0.858, 0.872, 0.919, and 0.848 for ROI3mm, ROI5mm, ROI10mm, and ROIin, while the internal validation set AUC values were 0.890, 0.965, 0.915, and 0.840. The AUC values of the external validation set are 0.844, 0.886, 0.938, and 0.905, respectively. Compared to models solely reliant on images or clinical parameters, the deep learning fusion model exhibited significant advantages. This achievement highlights the enormous potential of this integrated predictive approach, aiding doctors in predicting potential adverse reactions in patients before chemotherapy, thereby enabling timely adjustments to treatment plans and enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

According to foreign studies, assessing the efficacy of NAC in non-pCR patients through ultrasound, and subsequently changing to other treatment regimens, has shown a significant improvement in long-term prognosis [1, 20]. In this study, the factors included in the univariate analysis were ER, Her-2, and changes in tumor size before and after treatment. It was observed that non-triple-negative breast cancer might exhibit a lower pCR rate due to the rich stroma [21, 22]. Conversely, breast cancers with significant changes in tumor size before and after treatment tend to have higher pCR rates. This is attributed to NAC inducing necrosis and fibrosis in breast cancer tissue, leading to the collapse of its internal structure [23]. These findings were validated in our study.

The study found that the tumor microenvironment is associated with tumor growth, proliferation, and metastasis, correlated with lymphocyte infiltration, lymphatic invasion, and vascular invasion [24], indicating that the peritumoral region can provide valuable information for the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of breast cancer. Increasing evidence from studies demonstrates that CT, MRI, and ultrasound examinations reveal unique information about the biological characteristics and outcomes of breast cancer in the peritumoral tissue [25–29]. Qi et al. [8] found that a CT-based peritumoral radiomics model exhibited better predictive performance for pCR after NAC compared to intratumoral radiomics models. A multicenter study [30] found that a diagnostic nomogram constructed through three radiomics models (comprising intratumoral region, peritumoral 5 mm region, and intratumoral plus peritumoral 10 mm region) demonstrated strong predictive ability in both internal and external validation sets, with AUCs of 0.907 and 0.904, respectively. Jiang et al. [29], in a 316-case dual-center study, discovered that an MRI-based peritumoral radiomics model (RS-Peri-DCE and RS-Peri-DWI, 4 mm) had better predictive performance for pCR after NAC than intratumoral radiomics models, indicating the peritumoral region’s contribution to identifying axillary lymph node metastasis (LVI). However, these studies have not precisely identified the optimal range of peritumoral radiomic features around the primary tumor of breast cancer that maximizes the predictive value for enhancing neoadjuvant treatment efficacy. This study delineated four ROI models including intratumoral regions and peritumoral regions of 3 mm, 5 mm, and 10 mm. The findings showed that both RS and DLR models generally exhibited poorer predictive performance in the 3 mm peritumoral radiomic model compared to the other three ROI models. This could be attributed to the extraction of a limited 1 mm extension from the tumor edge, where the actual tumor edge was challenging to precisely match during tumor segmentation. The smaller peritumoral range might not adequately encompass the complete microenvironmental information of the breast cancer peritumoral area. In the RS model, the optimal predictive performance was consistently observed using a 10 mm peritumoral area, with AUCs of 0.846 (0.759–0.933) in the training set and 0.908 (0.792–1.023) in the internal validation set. In the deep learning model DLR152, the optimal predictive performance was observed using 5 mm and 10 mm peritumoral areas, with AUCs of 0.890 (0.814–0.966) and 0.903 (0.831–0.975), respectively, in the training set (Delong test: −0.347, P = 0.728). In the internal validation set, the AUCs were 0.970 (0.892–1.047) and 0.975 (0.901–1.048), respectively (Delong test: −0.161, P = 0.872), which is consistent with previous research [31, 32].

In recent years, with the advancement of artificial intelligence technology, the application of deep learning in medical imaging has become increasingly widespread. Unlike traditional machine learning algorithms, deep learning does not rely on precisely segmented tumor inputs designed by human experts [33, 34]. Instead, it autonomously extracts high-level abstract features from neural networks [35]. Through transfer learning using pre-trained models, knowledge from a source domain can be transferred to the medical imaging domain, effectively addressing overfitting issues in small and unbalanced medical image datasets [36, 37]. This study utilized five models of the residual neural network architecture (ResNet) - ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, ResNet101, and ResNet152 (with the numbers representing the layers) - to construct the DLR models (DLR18, DLR34, DLR50, DLR101, DLR152). Among these, the DLR152 model exhibited the best performance. This model demonstrated favorable performance in both 5 mm and 10 mm settings, indicating that the ultrasound image’s subtle features identified by DLR possess high accuracy and potential advantages in predicting the efficacy of NAC.

To overcome the limitations of a single DLR model and redundancy, models’ overall predictive performance was enhanced by integrating multiple models’ predictive results. Li et al. [27] developed a fusion model merging radiomic and deep learning features. In assessing axillary lymph node status in breast cancer, the AUC was 0.910 (95% CI: 0.879–0.937), surpassing other models, while the decision fusion model incorporating clinical features achieved an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.899–0.951), outperforming other clinical feature models. This study innovatively introduced stacking model fusion to deep learning image grouping techniques. Deep learning model stacking fusion is performed based on different ResNet residual blocks, specifically fusing ResNet18 and ResNet34 (DLRS2), and ResNet50, ResNet101, and ResNet152 (DLRS3). Compared to various ROI models, DLRS3 exhibited superior predictive performance to DLRS2 and RS models. In the training set, the optimal predictive performance was observed in ROI10mm, with an AUC of 0.919 (0.852–0.986), sensitivity of 0.867 (0.785–0.949), and specificity of 0.902 (0.830–0.975). In the internal validation set, ROI5mm showed the best predictive performance with an AUC of 0.965 (0.883–1.047), sensitivity of 1.000 (0.953–1.047), and specificity of 0.950 (0.858–1.042). In the external validation set, ROI10mm showed the best predictive performance with an AUC of 0.938(0.845–1.032), sensitivity of 0.923(0.821–1.025), and specificity of 0.900(0.788–1.012). Additionally, in comparison with the ultrasound physicians, the superior performance of DLRS3 over the physicians’ consensus (AUC: 0.938 vs. 0.752, DeLong’s test, p < 0.001). This performance gap may arise from the model’s ability to quantify subtle imaging patterns, which are challenging for visual assessment. However, physicians’ expertise remains indispensable for contextual interpretatio. A hybrid workflow that integrates AI predictions with expert review could optimize NAC management.

The nomogram was constructed by combining RS model, DLRS2, and DLRS3 with independent clinical ultrasound predictive factors. Calibration curves indicated good consistency between the radiomics nomogram predictive results and the actual outcomes, and clinical decision curve analysis demonstrated the clinical utility of the nomogram. In comparing various ROI regions, the highest clinical decision curve scores were observed in Nomo[RS], followed by Nomo[DLRS3], and finally Nomo[DLRS2], wherein Nomo[RS] and Nomo[DLRS3] were optimal for ROI10mm, with scores of 0.340 and 0.177 in the training group and 0.229 and 0.139 in the internal validation group, respectively. In the external validation set, Nomo[DLRS3] performs weaker than Nomo[DLRS2], with a value of 0.153. Although DLRS3 outperformed the RS model in the ROC analysis, in the Nomogram with combined clinical ultrasound data, the DCA analysis indicated that DLRS3 was superior to the RS model. This might be due to the fact that ROC reflects diagnostic information value while DCA reflects the clinical information value in the comparison between the RS model and DLR model. The features in the RS model hold physical significance, having high clinical value, while the features in the DLR model lack interpretability. Secondly, deep learning models require larger sample sizes for training. Utilizing the deep learning fusion model as an auxiliary tool, the clinical decision scores were slightly weaker than the RS model, falling short of the study’s expectations.

However, this study still has its limitations: firstly, the training of deep learning models necessitates larger sample sizes. Secondly, due to individual patient factors such as tumor size, location, and breast tissue thickness, the parameters of the ultrasound equipment used for examination were not standardized, potentially impacting the final results with varying parameters. Additionally, although CAM visualization was used to illustrate the decision-making process of the DLR model, the biological and genetic mechanisms associated with the image features generated by the DLR have not been thoroughly investigated. To enhance the interpretability and reliability of personalized treatment plans, future research needs to further explore these mechanisms and consider incorporating the findings of radiogenomics studies into the research. Third, while our dual-center design enhanced geographic diversity, the NAC regimens at both centers exhibited limited variability. This homogeneity precluded meaningful subgroup analyses based on treatment subtypes. Future studies involving multicenter collaborations with broader treatment protocols are necessary to assess model performance across diverse therapeutic contexts. Finally, while the stacking strategy utilizes multi-scale ResNet variants, all base models share the same core CNN architecture. This architectural homogeneity may limit the diversity of extracted features, particularly for subtle patterns that fall outside ResNet’s inductive bias. Future work should integrate complementary architectures to address this limitation. It should be noted that although this study achieved physical standardization of expansion distances through spatial resolution calibration and morphological methods, it did not account for tumor biological heterogeneity—specifically, variations in stromal responses at different distances. Future work could incorporate histopathological features of the tumor microenvironment to develop adaptive expansion algorithms. Notably, our multicenter design deliberately preserved inter-institutional heterogeneity in NAC regimens to assess the robustness of the model. The absence of a significant association between regimen types and pCR outcomes suggests that the developed biomarkers reflect intrinsic tumor biology rather than regimen-specific effects. This regimen-agnostic property enhances the clinical applicability of the model across diverse chemotherapy protocols. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating targeted therapies and immunotherapy combinations are warranted to further validate this hypothesis.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a fusion model for predicting early tumor response in breast cancer patients receiving NAC. The model combines deep learning methods based on early NAC ultrasound images and demonstrates high predictive performance. This achievement highlights the immense potential of this integrated predictive approach, aiding physicians in predicting potential adverse reactions in patients before chemotherapy, thereby adjusting treatment plans promptly and improving treatment outcomes.
