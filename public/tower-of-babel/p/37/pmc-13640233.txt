Clinical characteristics and long-term treatment outcomes in childhood myelodysplastic neoplasm: a nationwide population-based study in Korea
Authors: Minwoong Kang, Eun Sang Yi, Jung Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood myelodysplastic neoplasm (cMDS) is a rare condition with distinct biological and clinical characteristics. To characterize the epidemiological features of cMDS, including incidence, demographics, and underlying etiologies. We also aimed to identify prognostic factors and assess long-term outcomes associated with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) in patients with cMDS.MethodsThis nationwide Korean study analyzed 484 patients < 19 years of age diagnosed with cMDS between 2003 and 2021, using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service database.ResultsAnnual incidence ranged from 1.7 to 4.3 per million children, with 7.2% had a prior history of malignancy. A total of 180 patients underwent allo-HSCT for cMDS. The 5-year overall survival (OS) rates were 76.6% in the transplant group and 80.5% in the non-transplant group. Among non-transplanted patients, key prognostic factors included later period of diagnosis (hazard ratio [HR] 0.372, P = 0.012), history of prior malignancy (HR 3.329, P = 0.002), and chemotherapy for cMDS (HR 3.701, P = 0.002). Only CBT was significantly associated with increased mortality in univariate analysis of the transplant group (HR, 3.911; P = 0.008). Introduction

Myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) is a clonal hematopoietic disorder characterized by peripheral cytopenia, ineffective hematopoiesis, and an increased risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Although MDS predominantly affects the elderly, childhood MDS (cMDS) is exceedingly rare, with an annual incidence of only 1 to 4 cases per million children [1]. cMDS presents with distinct biological and clinical features, frequently involving hypocellular bone marrow (~ 80%) [2, 3] and may arise de novo or in association with predisposing factors such as prior exposure to cytotoxic agents, inherited bone marrow failure syndromes, or genetic predisposition syndromes [4]. The classification of cMDS has evolved, from pediatric adaptations of the original World Health Organization (WHO) classification to the 2022 WHO and international consensus classifications, which incorporate refinements based on histopathological features and blast percentages [5–7].

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains the standard of care for high-risk cMDS, particularly in cases involving increased blasts, therapy-related cMDS, or low-blast disease with high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities [8]. However, due to the rarity of cMDS, data on its clinical characteristics, long-term treatment outcomes, and prognostic factors following allo-HSCT remain limited. Current understanding is primarily based on analyses from relatively small or single-institution cohorts [9–15].

Furthermore, population-based studies investigating the true incidence, demo graphic patterns, and underlying etiologies of cMDSare particularly limited in the literature [4, 16–20]. To date, no comprehensive population-based study of cMDS has been conducted in Korea. Here, we performed a population-based analysis using Korea’s National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database. The objective was to characterize the epidemiological features of cMDS, including incidence, demographics, and underlying etiologies. Additionally, we aimed to identify prognostic factors and assess long-term outcomes associated with allo-HSCT in patients with cMDS.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

Korea operates a universal single-payer national health system, and the NHIS maintains comprehensive national records of all covered inpatient and outpatient visits, procedures, and prescriptions. The NHIS database includes personal demographic information, health insurance claim codes (for procedures and prescriptions), diagnostic codes based on the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases, 7th Revision, which is aligned with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), as well as death records from the Korean National Statistical Office and general health checkup data [21].

This retrospective cohort study utilized claims data from the NHIS database spanning from 2002 to 2022. The study population consisted of individuals under the age of 19 who were diagnosed with cMDS, identified by ICD-10 code D46, between 2003 and 2021 (n = 644). To include newly diagnosed cMDS patients, we excluded individuals diagnosed with cMDS in 2002 (n = 30), and those who had a diagnosis of leukemia (ICD-10 codes C91–C95) prior to baseline (n = 141). The final study cohort consisted of 484 patients (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Measurements

Childhood MDS with increased blasts (cMDS-IB) was defined as having an ICD-10 code of D46.2 at the time of diagnosis. All other diagnoses (D46.0–D46.9) were classified as cMDS with low blasts (cMDS-LB). Transplantation procedures were categorized as allogeneic bone marrow (BM), peripheral blood (PB), or cord blood (CB) transplants based on relevant procedure codes (Supplementary Table 1). Patients who underwent transplantation after leukemic transformation were excluded from the transplantation outcome analysis. History of malignancy was defined as having any ICD-10 code within the C category prior to the initial diagnosis of cMDS. In transplant patients, chemotherapy for cMDS was defined as administration of any of the following agents more than one month before transplantation: cytarabine, mitoxantrone, thiotepa, daunorubicin, busulfan, etoposide, thioguanine, fludarabine, idarubicin, azacitidine, decitabine, or lenalidomide. In non-transplant patients, chemotherapy was defined as treatment with any of above agents from the cMDS diagnosis until the development of leukemia. Conditioning regimens were identified using claim codes (Supplementary Table 2) within 14 days before transplantation and were classified as either total body irradiation (TBI)-based or busulfan-based. The incidence of leukemia was defined by the appearance of ICD-10 codes C91-C95 after the initial diagnosis of cMDS.

Demographic data, including age, sex, area of residence (metropolitan vs. provincial), and household income (based on insurance premium percentiles: 0–25, 26–50, 51–75, 76–100, and unknown), were obtained from the insurance eligibility database. Metropolitan areas were defined as cities with populations exceeding one million.

Statistical analysis

The annual incidence of cMDS in the general population aged ≤ 18 years was estimated using data from the Korean Statistical Information Service [22]. Participant characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables depending on the normality of the data distribution and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Non-transplant patients were followed until death within five years of cMDS diagnosis or until the end of the study period (December 31, 2022), whichever occurred first. Transplant patients were followed from the date of transplantation. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using log-rank tests. Furthermore, patients who underwent transplantation after leukemic transformation were excluded from the analyses of transplant characteristics and survival outcomes. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models to identify factors associated with 5-year mortality in both groups. Multivariable analyses included covariates that were statistically significant in univariable analyses. The cumulative incidence of leukemic transformation was estimated for all patients, including those excluded from the survival analyses, using the cumulative incidence function with death before leukemic transformation treated as a competing event. Group comparisons were performed using Gray’s test. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using log(-log) survival plots and Schoenfeld residuals. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Study population and incidence of cMDS

A total of 484 patients were newly diagnosed with cMDS between 2003 and 2021. The most common subtype was cMDS-LB, accounting for 96.9% of cases (469/484). The annual incidence of cMDS during this period ranged from 1.7 to 4.3 per million children aged 0–18 years, with a mean annual incidence of 2.5 per million (95% CI: 2.3–2.7 per million). No consistent trend in incidence was observed over time (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 3).

The orange line represents the annual incidence rate of cMDS per million children (right y-axis). The incidence rate was calculated based on the total population of children aged 0–18 years in Korea for each corresponding year

The overall male-to-female ratio was 1.2, and the median age at diagnosis was 10 years (Table 1). When stratified by age group, the most common age at diagnosis was 15–18 years (26.9%), followed closely by the 5–9 years group (26.2%). Patients with cMDS-IB had a lower median age at diagnosis than those with cMDS-LB, although the number of cMDS-IB cases was small (n = 15). This difference showed a trend toward statistical significance (median age: 6 years vs. 10 years, P = 0.066). Geographically, the distribution of cMDS cases across prefectures did not significantly differ by level of urbanization. However, patients with cMDS-IB were more likely to reside in metropolitan areas compared to those with cMDS-LB (60.0% vs. 46.1%, P = 0.011).

Among all patients with cMDS, 7.2% had a prior history of malignancy, and the mean interval between the diagnosis of the primary malignancy and the subsequent development of cMDS was 3.0 years. Of these patients, 26 had prior solid tumors and 9 had prior hematologic malignancies. The most common preceding malignancies were central nervous system (CNS) tumors (n = 7), followed by bone tumors (n = 5) and soft tissue tumors (n = 4) (Supplement Table 4). The mean age at cMDS diagnosis in these patients was 11.1 years (95% CI: 6.1–16.1 years), which was older than in patients with de novo cMDS.

Characteristics of treatment and transplantations

Chemotherapy was administered to 10.9% of the total patient population (Table 1). The proportion of patients receiving chemotherapy was significantly higher in the cMDS-IB group compared to the cMDS-LB group (46.7% vs. 9.8%, P < 0.001). Among the chemotherapeutic agents used, cytarabine was the most commonly administered (73.6%), followed by etoposide (35.9%), idarubicin (20.8%), and daunorubicin (17.0%).

Of the 484 patients, 215 underwent allo-HSCT. Among them, 35 were excluded from further analysis because transplantation occurred after a diagnosis of leukemia. Therefore, the final cohort for cMDS outcome analysis consisted of 180 patients in the transplant group and 269 in the non-transplant group (Table 2). When classified by hematopoietic cell source, PB stem cells were the most commonly used (80.6%), followed by BM (13.9%) and CB (5.6%). The use of PB as a stem cell source increased in the latter half of the study period (2013–2021) compared with the first half (2003–2012) (33.8% vs. 66.2%). In contrast, the use of BM (72.0% vs. 28.0%) and CB (70.0% vs. 30.0%) decreased over the same period. In transplants using BM or PB, busulfan-based conditioning regimens were more commonly employed than TBI-based regimens (BM: 36.0% vs. 4.0%; PB: 46.9% vs. 17.9%). Conversely, in cord blood transplants (CBT), TBI-based regimens were more frequently used than busulfan-based regimens (60.0% vs. 30.0%).

In the non-transplant group, a later period of diagnosis (2013–2021) was associated with significantly reduced mortality compared to the earlier period (2003–2012), while history of malignancy, the cMDS-IB subtype, and receipt of chemotherapy for MDS were associated with increased mortality. In multivariate analysis, a later period of diagnosis remained significantly associated with reduced mortality (HR, 0.372; 95% CI, 0.173–0.802; P = 0.012), while history of malignancy (HR, 3.329; 95% CI, 1.584–6.996; P = 0.002) and chemotherapy for MDS (HR, 3.701; 95% CI, 1.597–8.576; P = 0.002) were independently associated with increased mortality. In the transplant group, only CBT was significantly associated with increased mortality in the univariate analysis (HR, 3.911; 95% CI, 1.313–11.650; P = 0.008).

Development of leukemia after cMDS diagnosis

A total of 97 patients developed leukemia following a diagnosis of cMDS, including 89 patients from the cMDS-LB group and eight from the cMDS-IB group. This cohort includes the 35 patients who were excluded from the survival analyses due to undergoing transplantation after leukemic transformation. Accounting for competing risks, the 10-year cumulative incidence of leukemia was 20.5% (Fig. 3A). Most cases of leukemic transformation occurred within three years of the initial cMDS diagnosis, with a 3-year incidence of 18.9%. The incidence of leukemia was significantly higher in the cMDS-IB group compared to the cMDS-LB group. At 10 years, the cumulative incidence was 53.3% in the cMDS-IB group versus 19.4% in the cMDS-LB group (P = 0.002) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

We conducted a nationwide study of 484 patients with cMDS, finding an annual incidence of 2.5 per million children in Korea. Most were diagnosed with cMDS-LB, with 7.2% having a prior history of malignancy. Notably, many with cMDS-LB achieved stable disease without allo-HSCT, with 5-year OS rates of 76.6% for the transplant group and 80.5% for the non-transplant group. The 10-year incidence of leukemia was 20.5%.

Similar to other landmark population-based analyses [16–18], our nationwide cohort provides unique insights through comprehensive and unbiased case identification over an extended timeframe. Leveraging the NHIS database enabled us to generate robust risk estimates and clinical outcome data for cMDS, extending beyond childhood and into adulthood. This prolonged follow-up is a key methodological strength, allowing us to capture the full natural history of cMDS and document long-term outcomes with greater accuracy.

In our study, the cMDS-IB subtype accounted for only 3.1% of cases, which is lower than the 10–25% range reported in previous studies [23, 24]. This discrepancy can be explained by several factors. First, following WHO guidelines, patients with Down syndrome showing MDS-like features were likely classified under “myeloid leukemia associated with Down syndrome” rather than cMDS-IB. Previous research has demonstrated that excluding Down syndrome patients reduce advanced MDS diagnoses by approximately 50% [25]. Additionally, in the Korean ICD system, refractory anemia with excess blasts in transformation (RAEB-t) is included as a subcategory of AML (ICD code C92.0), thus RAEB-t cases would have been excluded from our cMDS-IB cohort. Furthermore, the significantly higher rate of cMDS-IB diagnoses in metropolitan areas suggests potential underdiagnosis in non-metropolitan regions, likely due to differences in diagnostic infrastructure or specialist availability. The low proportion of cMDS-IB cases likely contributed to the lower overall incidence of leukemic transformation observed in our cohort (20.0%) compared to previous reports (31% in pediatric and young adult MDS patients, and approximately 40% in children) [13].

While the cumulative incidence of therapy-related MDS/AML ranges from 5% to 11% in children treated with standard solid-tumor protocols [8, 26, 27], our study was unable to directly assess therapy-related etiology due to the limitations of claims-based data. Instead, we identified prior history of malignancy as a significant adverse prognostic factor, particularly in the non-transplant group, with a median interval of 3.0 years from prior malignancy diagnosis to cMDS onset. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating prior malignancy history in the diagnostic and prognostic evaluation of cMDS.

Allo-HSCT, the only curative treatment for cMDS, is typically indicated for patients with high-risk features, such as transfusion dependence, severe neutropenia, excess blasts, monosomy 7, del(7q), complex karyotype, or therapy-related disease [8, 9]. Transplantation may be considered at any disease stage, regardless of cMDS subtype, pretransplant treatment, or remission status, and offers the potential to prevent progression to AML [13, 28]. Historically, HSCT has achieved cure rates of 60–80% in refractory cytopenia of childhood (RCC) and approximately 50–60% in advanced MDS [9–15]. Patients with cMDS-LB generally show more favorable outcomes compared to those with excess blasts [11]. The European Working Group on Childhood MDS (EWOG-MDS) reported 3-year OS and event-free survival rates of 84% and 74%, respectively, among RCC patients who underwent reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) with unrelated donor transplants [29]. Similarly, the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) reported 8-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates of 65% and 40% for matched and mismatched unrelated donor bone marrow transplants, respectively, in RCC [30]. In our study, the 5-year post-transplant OS rate for patients with cMDS-LB was approximately 77%, consistent with these previously reported outcomes.

Treating patients with cMDS-IB remains particularly challenging. The EWOG-MDS study reported a 5-year OS rate of 63% in children with advanced MDS who underwent HSCT from HLA-matched sibling or unrelated donors [31]. In the CIBMTR study, 8-year DFS rates following unrelated donor transplants were 48% for patients with refractory anemia with excess blasts (RAEB) and 28% for those with RAEB-t [30]. While transplant-related mortality is the leading cause of treatment failure after myeloablative conditioning in RCC [10], relapse is the predominant cause of failure in advanced MDS [31, 32]. Notably, in our study, patients with cMDS-IB in the non-transplant group had poorer survival compared to those with cMDS-LB. In contrast, no significant survival difference was observed between subtypes in the transplant group. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of patients with excess blasts.

The optimal conditioning regimen prior to HSCT in MDS remains a subject of debate. In our cohort, approximately one-third of transplant recipients did not receive TBI or busulfan, suggesting the use of RIC. However, due to the lack of detailed information on drug or radiation dosages, the actual proportion of patients receiving RIC may be higher. Busulfan was the most frequently used conditioning agent, while TBI was more commonly employed in CBT, consistent with previous findings that TBI is administered in approximately half of cases [33]. We found no significant differences in survival based on conditioning regimen, although the absence of dosage data limits definitive conclusions regarding conditioning intensity. Previous studies have suggested that myeloablative conditioning regimens—including agents such as busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and melphalan—are preferred in patients with increased blasts [8], while RIC regimens incorporating thiotepa, treosulfan, fludarabine, or aplastic anemia–oriented approaches may be more appropriate for patients with RCC [29, 34, 35].

Although CB is a valuable alternative for patients lacking a matched sibling or suitable unrelated adult donor, outcomes following CBT are generally inferior to those achieved with BM or PB stem cell transplants [36]. In our study, CB was associated with increased mortality in univariate analysis. However, considering the characteristics of this study, this association may have been influenced by factors beyond graft source, including disease status, conditioning regimen, or transplant center experience. CBT outcomes have improved over time with advances in technique. A large registry-based study reported an increase in 3-year DFS from 27% before 2001 to 50% in subsequent years [33]. More recently, a study in children with high-risk myeloid malignancies showed improved graft-versus-host disease-free, relapse-free survival following T cell–replete CBT, although only 10 patients in the cohort had MDS [37]. Further data in pediatric MDS populations are needed to validate these findings.

In our study, 10.9% of patients received chemotherapy for MDS; however, it was not associated with improved survival. Rather, it was associated with a higher mortality rate in the non-transplant group. However, due to limited availability of detailed clinical information on treatment indications (e.g., baseline disease risk) and treatment-related mortality (e.g., cause of death), the contribution of chemotherapy to mortality cannot be definitively determined in this study. likely due to both the presence of adverse prognostic factors in patients who received chemotherapy and increased treatment-related toxicity. Although some reports suggest that cytarabine-based induction therapy may result in favorable responses [38], its role in advanced MDS remains controversial [8]. Among non-transplant patients, AML-type chemotherapy is associated with poor outcomes (OS < 30%) due to high induction failure and relapse rates [31], and is therefore not routinely recommended. The hypomethylating agent azacitidine has been shown to induce bone marrow responses and improve event-free survival [28, 39], although its effect on post-HSCT OS remains uncertain [13].

This study has several inherent limitations related to the use of NHIS data. Important clinical and transplant-related variables, including cytogenetic findings, genomic profiles (such as germline predisposition syndromes and genetic mutations), donor type, and conditioning intensity, were not available, limiting adjustment for potential confounders and raising the possibility of residual confounding. Future large-scale studies incorporating genetic variation, a core factor in the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of cMDS, are warranted to complement population-based findings. Additionally, the absence of data on relapse and transplant-related mortality limits our ability to fully interpret the causes of treatment failure. Furthermore, the findings from the multivariable Cox regression analyses should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for the transplant subgroup, given the limited number of outcome events and the resulting potential for model instability. Because variable selection was based on univariable significance in order to limit the number of covariates and reduce overparameterization, the possibility of overfitting cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the study’s strengths include the use of a nationwide, population-based database encompassing nearly the entire Korean population. This enabled a robust analysis of clinical characteristics and outcomes in a rare disease population that is otherwise difficult to study at scale.

In conclusion, this nationwide Korean study provides comprehensive epidemiological data, treatment outcomes, and prognostic insights for cMDS in a large cohort. Our findings highlight that while allo-HSCT remains a cornerstone of curative treatment, a substantial proportion of patients, particularly those with cMDS-LB, who represented the majority of our cohort, achieved stable disease control without undergoing transplantation. However, careful risk stratification is critical, as therapy-related etiology and increased blast percentage were associated with significantly poorer survival in non-transplanted patients and a higher risk of leukemic transformation, respectively.
