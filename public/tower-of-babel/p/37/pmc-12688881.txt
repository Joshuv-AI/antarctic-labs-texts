To develop prognostic markers related to drug resistance in pancreatic cancer patients based on multiple machine learning methods
Authors: Song Xu, Chuanmin Deng, Zhongran Man, Song Yang, Ming Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies by focusing on drug resistance-related phenotypes. We integrated multi-omics data from various databases encompassing sequencing, clinical information, resistance-related gene sets, and genomic alteration data. ssGSEA was employed to calculate resistance scores for individual samples, which were subsequently applied in survival analysis. Furthermore, we utilized machine learning algorithms to develop a robust prognostic model validated across multiple independent datasets. Our findings revealed 12 genes consistently linked to pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) prognosis in diverse datasets. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the high-risk group was enriched in pathways associated with systemic lupus erythematosus and the cell cycle, whereas the low-risk group showed significant enrichment in neuroactive ligand−receptor interaction pathways. Additionally, immune cell infiltration analysis exhibited substantial differences between risk groups, with the high-risk cohort presenting lower levels of activated CD8+ T cells but higher levels of regulatory T cells. The random survival forest model demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving a concordance index of 0.634 and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic area under the curve values of 0.973, 0.978, and 0.996 at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively. In conclusion, this study identifies 12 critical drug resistance genes in PAAD and highlights the associated immune differences in patient risk, paving the way for targeted immunotherapy research to improve therapeutic strategies against this formidable disease.

Introduction

Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is one of the most lethal malignancies, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% and increasing incidence worldwide[1]. In 2022, approximately 510 566 new cases and 467 005 deaths are estimated to occur globally, highlighting the urgent need for effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies[2]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and chemotherapeutic regimens, the prognosis of PAAD remains poor, largely because of late-stage diagnosis and inherent or acquired chemoresistance[3]. First-line chemotherapies, such as gemcitabine and FOLFIRINOX, have been utilized in an attempt to combat this disease. However, the response rates to these therapies remain suboptimal, and many patients eventually develop resistance to these drugs, leading to a significant decrease in treatment efficacy. Drug resistance in cancer treatment has been extensively investigated in various malignancies. For example, in non-small cell lung cancer, resistance mechanisms have been linked to alterations in drug−target genes and activation of alternative signaling pathways[6]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, resistance to targeted therapies has been linked to mutations in downstream effectors and compensatory pathway activation[7]. These findings imply that a comprehensive understanding of resistance mechanisms might offer new opportunities for therapeutic interventions. However, the identification of reliable biomarkers for drug resistance and patient prognosis in PAAD remains a significant challenge[8]. Previous studies have focused primarily on single-gene markers or limited gene panels, which often fail to capture the complexity of resistance mechanisms[9]. Therefore, there is a critical need to develop robust multigene signatures that can accurately predict drug resistance and patient outcomes in PAAD.

In this study, we aimed to construct a prognostic model for PAAD based on drug resistance-related biomarkers using various machine-learning algorithms. We obtained and processed sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TGCA)-PAAD and four GEO datasets, standardized the data, and calculated the drug resistance scores using the single-sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 analysis, and a comprehensive prognostic model was developed and validated across multiple datasets. Additionally, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) enrichment, mutation, and immune infiltration analyses to elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms associated with drug resistance in PAAD. This study did not utilize any artificial intelligence (AI) technologies and strictly complied with the provisions outlined in the TITAN Guidelines 2025 – governing declaration and use of AI.[10]

Materials and methods

Data download

We downloaded the PAAD dataset (TCGA-PAAD) from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) through the R package TCGAbiolinks[11] and used it as a test set for analysis. After excluding data samples lacking clinical information, we obtained a total of 173 counts of sequencing data and corresponding clinical data for pancreatic cancer patient samples with clinical information. The pancreatic cancer count sequencing data were standardized to fragments per kilobase per million formats. The corresponding clinical data were obtained from the UCSC Xena database[12] (http://genome.ucsc.edu).

In addition, we downloaded pancreatic cancer datasets from the GEO database through the R package GEOquery[13] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE62452[14], GSE57495[15], GSE28735[16], and GSE71729[17]. The GSE57495, GSE28735, and GSE71729 datasets were obtained from Homo sapiens. The chip platform used for GSE62452 was GPL6244, and the chip platform used for GSE57495 was GPL15048. The chip platform of the GSE28735 dataset was GPL6244, and that of the GSE71729 dataset was GPL20769. The single-cell dataset GSE155698 contained 16 pancreatic cancer samples. The sequencing platform used was GPL20301, and the samples were obtained from Homo sapiens. We downloaded the set of drug resistance genes associated with “The general information of molecules associated with hold” from the DRESIS database[18] (http://dresis.idrblab.net/); see Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A985 for detailed information.

The microsatellite instability (MSI), mutation count, and tumor mutation burden data were downloaded from the cBioPortal database[19]. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and fraction genome altered (FGA) scores were used to measure the percentage of chromosome copy number-altered regions outside the region.

This study strictly relied on data from public databases such as TCGA-PAAD and GEO. Due to the absence of several key clinical variables (e.g., tumor grade, specific treatment regimens, and performance status scores) in most samples within these databases, it was not possible to systematically incorporate them into the multivariable Cox analysis.

Phenotypic score

ssGSEA was used to quantify the relative abundance of each gene in a dataset sample. We used the R package GSVA[20] to calculate the drug resistance scores of each sample in the TCGA dataset based on the expression matrix of DRRGs for each sample using the ssGSEA algorithm. Based on the median expression value of the drug resistance score, the samples of patients with pancreatic cancer in the TCGA-PAAD dataset were divided into high- and low-score groups. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves were constructed by combining the TCGA-PAAD dataset with clinical data according to the drug resistance score.

Differential gene analysis of the dataset

We used R’s DESeq2 package[21] to perform differential expression analysis between the high- and low-score groups of the dataset for DEGs between different groups. Differential expression analysis between the high- and low-score groups was performed using the DESeq2 package. To control for false positives resulting from multiple hypothesis testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to adjust P-values. Genes that met the criteria of |logFC| > 1 and Padj < 0.05 were considered upregulated, while those with logFC < −1 and Padj < 0.05 were considered downregulated. We performed univariate Cox analysis of DEGs in five datasets (TCGA-PAAD, GSE62452, GSE57495, GSE28735, and GSE71729). Genes with a P-value <0.05 and the same hazard ratio (HR) in at least three datasets were selected as stable pancreatic cancer prognosis-related genes and were used as our resistance score feature genes.

Multimachine learning to achieve one-stop feature gene screening and prognostic model construction

To develop a robust prognostic model for TCGA-PAAD, we initially integrated 10 established algorithms: random survival forest (RSF), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), gradient boosting machine, survival support vector machine, supervised principal component, ridge regression, Cox partial least squares regression (PLS-Cox), CoxBoost, stepwise Cox, and elastic net algorithms. Among these, RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, and stepwise Cox are capable of dimensionality reduction and variable selection, and we combined them with other algorithms to form 112 machine learning ensembles. Next, employing the TCGA-PAAD dataset as the training cohort, we utilized these 112 algorithm combinations to screen critical genes and construct a prognostic model based on previously identified characteristic genes. Finally, in four validation cohorts (GSE62452, GSE57495, GSE28735, and GSE71729), we calculated the risk score for each cohort using the features identified in the training cohort. Based on the average C-index of the two validation cohorts, we ultimately selected the optimal consensus prognostic model for pancreatic cancer and calculated its risk score (riskscore). The genes included in the model were designated our hub genes. Based on the median score, TCGA-PAAD samples were categorized into high- and low-risk groups. Subsequently, we employed survival analysis and multivariate Cox regression to assess the independent prognostic significance of this risk score.

Screening criteria and validation of drug resistance genes

We obtained drug resistance-related genes from the DRESIS database (http://dresis.idrblab.net/), conducted GSVA analysis to derive drug resistance scores for each sample, and divided them into high and low scoring groups based on the median value. Subsequently, we performed differential analysis on the high and low scoring groups, using |logFC| > 1 and Padj < 0.05 as screening criteria to obtain candidate drug resistance genes. We validated the consistency of drug resistance genes across different datasets through univariate Cox analysis, providing support for the stability of the drug resistance genes.

Risk score comparison with other signatures

To further evaluate the predictive value of the model score, we compared it with previously reported prognostic models for pancreatic cancer biomarkers, including those of Huang et al[22], Su et al[23], and Tian et al[24]. In terms of the prediction performance of pancreatic cancer biomarkers, the C-index of the other biomarker models was calculated for all TCGA and GEO datasets and compared with the C-index of the model scores. The codes and algorithms for the above three molecular markers were derived from previous studies.

Gene set enrichment analysis

We performed GSEA using the clusterProfiler R package[25] to calculate the normalized enrichment scores for each gene set and to identify the signaling pathways enriched in the TCGA-PAAD high- and low-risk groups. GSEA is a computational method used to analyze whether a specific gene set is significantly different between two biological states. It is commonly used to estimate changes in pathways and biological process activities in datasets. The gene set “c2.cp.kegg.symbols.gmt” was downloaded from the MSigDB database for GSEA to evaluate the impact of the high- and low-risk groups on tumor KEGG enrichment[26]. P-values <0.05 and FDR (Q-values) <0.25 were considered for inclusion in the analysis. We separately present the five pathways with the highest enrichment scores in the high- and low-risk groups.

Mutation analysis

To analyze the single nucleotide polymorphisms in the different risk score groups of patients diagnosed with TCGA-PAAD, the maftools package[27] was used to analyze the frequently mutated genes in patients in the high- and low-risk groups. The drugInteractions function was used to evaluate the interactions between drugs and gene mutations. The goal of this study was to identify the genetic mutations associated with susceptibility or resistance to specific drugs. In addition, a biological oncogenic pathway analysis was performed on the mutation data to understand which biological oncogenic pathways were affected by gene mutations. To achieve this goal, we used OncogenicPathways and PlotOncogenicPathways.

Immune infiltration and differential analysis of immunomodulators

ssGSEA[28] quantifies the relative abundance of each immune cell infiltrate. First, each infiltrating immune cell type, including activated CD8+ T cells, activated dendritic cells, gamma-delta T cells, natural killer cells, and a variety of human immune cell subtypes, such as regulatory T cells, was labelled. Subsequently, the enrichment scores calculated by ssGSEA were used to represent the relative abundance of infiltrating immune cells in each sample to construct an immune cell infiltration matrix for the TCGA-PAAD cohort. The correlation between immune cells was calculated based on the Spearman algorithm, and the R package pheatmap was used to construct a correlation heatmap to visualize the correlation analysis results of the immune cells themselves.

Immune checkpoint genes are ligand‒receptor pairs that inhibit or stimulate immune responses. Immune checkpoints play important immunomodulatory roles in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmunity. Immune checkpoint blockade therapy has brought significant clinical benefits to patients with solid malignancies. We obtained 47 immune checkpoint genes from the literature[29] published on the PubMed website (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, Available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A986). We used the Mann‒Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank sum test) to analyze the differences in the expression of immune checkpoint genes (ECIs) between the high- and low-score groups in the TCGA-PAAD dataset and constructed a group comparison diagram.

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a specific variant of regulated cell death that is driven by stress and can induce adaptive immunity against dead cell antigens. We obtained 32 ICD genes from the PubMed website[30], and the specific gene names are shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table S3, Available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A987. We used the Mann‒Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank sum test) to analyze the differences in the expression of ICD genes between the high- and low-model score groups in the TCGA-PAAD dataset and constructed a group comparison diagram.

Immunotherapy analysis and development and validation of potential therapeutic drugs

We used the Cancer Treatment Response Portal (CTRP, https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ctrp/, including 835 cancer cell lines (CCLs) of 481 compounds) and the mixture simultaneously to analyze the relative inhibitory effect datasets (PRISM; sensitivity data from 1448 compounds in 482 CCLs were used to develop potential drugs for patients with PAAD in the high-risk group). Based on the protocol of Yang et al[31], we first retrieved the drug sensitivity data for CCLs from the CTRP and PRISM reuse datasets and gathered expression data for CCLs from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia database. The CTRP and PRISM datasets feature area under the curve (AUC) values, with lower AUC values indicating heightened sensitivity to the compounds. Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, we assessed differences in drug responses between the high- and low-risk groups based on the risk score, establishing a logFC threshold of >0.02 to identify compounds with low AUC values within the high-risk group according to the risk score. Subsequently, we employed Spearman’s correlation to further filter compounds exhibiting a negative correlation between their AUC and risk score values (with a threshold set at r < −0.1). Ultimately, we pinpointed potential therapeutics for patients in the high-risk group based on the risk score by identifying intersections among the compounds we obtained.

The Connectivity Map (CMap) database (https://clue.io/) was used to explore potential drug−target gene interactions. Gene expression differences after treating human cells with different distractors (including small molecules) were used to establish a biological database of the interrelationships between distractors, gene expression, and disease. Through this database, we identified the details of the potential drug−target gene interactions of patients in the high-risk group for breast cancer.

Quality control for single-cell datasets

We used the “CreateSeuratObject” function from the R package Seurat v4.0[32] to import the count matrix from the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE155698, which we then established as a Seurat object. The following quality control steps were applied: (1) genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded; (2) cells expressing fewer than 500 genes were filtered out; (3) cells with total RNA counts exceeding 250 were removed; and (4) cells with mitochondrial gene content higher than 20% were also excluded. Subsequently, cell cycle scores were computed using the “CellCycleScoring” function. Finally, the “SCTransform” method based on regularized negative binomial regression was applied to perform technical noise correction, gene expression normalization, selection of highly variable genes, and data scaling. During this process, we set parameters to ensure that each individual cell expresses at least 500 genes. The ratio of mitochondrial genes to total gene expression can indicate the physiological state of a cell. Specifically, we hypothesized that a cell might be under stress if the proportion of mitochondrial genes exceeds 20% of its total gene expression. As a result, we excluded any cells with more than 20% mitochondrial gene content from our analysis.

Following this, the sequencing depth of the scRNA-seq dataset GSE155698 was standardized using the “SCTransform” function. Thereafter, principal component analysis (PCA)[33] was conducted to discern the notable principal components (PCs), and the “Elbowplot” function was employed to illustrate the distribution of the P values. We selected 31 PCs for the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis, which is designed for dimensionality reduction. We used the default parameters of the “FindNeighbors” function with these 31 PC dimensions to determine the k-nearest neighbors based on Euclidean distance within the original PCA space. Following this, we applied the “FindClusters” function and utilized the “clustree” function to identify a resolution of 0.4, which allowed us to categorize the cells into distinct clusters. Ultimately, we employed the “RunUMAP” function to perform dimensionality reduction, facilitating the visualization and examination of the dataset. We utilized the R package “SingleR[34],” which leverages reference datasets from the SingleR Human Primary Cell Atlas Data and Blueprint Encode Data, to annotate cell types in the scRNA-seq dataset GSE155698.

Pseudotemporal analysis

According to the temporal[35] gene expression of each cell, each cell could be arranged in the corresponding trajectory according to the proposed time. According to the gene expression status, the sample could be divided into multiple cell populations under different differentiation states, and an intuitive lineage development dendrogram could be generated to predict the cell differentiation and development trajectory. The results of the pseudotime analysis are needed to confirm the origin and endpoint of differentiation according to the trajectory distribution of cell types and the changes in the expression of signature genes.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.2.2) was used for all the statistical analyses. Differences between two groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Kruskal–Walli’s test was used to evaluate differences between more than two groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Prognostic model establishment based on RSF machine learning combination

By applying Cindex analysis to 112 machine learning ensemble models, we identified the most effective RSF algorithm, which we then used to create a prognostic prediction model for patients diagnosed with PAAD. We categorized samples from the TCGA-PAAD dataset into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score generated by our model, which served as the cutoff point. To visualize our findings, we created a heatmap illustrating the risk factors (Fig. 4A), a KM survival curve (Fig. 4B), and a time-dependent ROC curve (Fig. 4C). The KM curve revealed that individuals in the high-risk group had significantly poorer prognoses, as indicated by a statistically significant difference in survival rates compared to the low-risk group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). We subsequently incorporated additional clinical parameters of the patients, such as stage, age, and sex, to evaluate the model’s independent prognostic capability. Variables that showed a significance level of P < 0.1 in the univariate Cox regression analysis were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis to construct the multivariate Cox prognostic model. The results from the univariate Cox regression analysis were systematically organized and are presented in a forest plot (Fig. 4D). Factors affecting the results of univariate Cox regression: Sample distribution: In the dataset, patients in stage II generally have a longer survival period, while the occurrence of drug resistance may manifest earlier. Clinical characteristics: Stage II patients are relatively more likely to receive first-line treatment, demonstrating more sensitivity in the manifestation of drug resistance. Subsequently, we performed a nomogram analysis to assess the prognostic capability of the established model, as shown in Fig. 4E. A nomogram is a graphical tool created through multivariate regression analysis, which uses a specific scale to assign scores that represent the contribution of each variable in the multivariate framework. These scores are then combined to estimate the probability of an event occurring. As depicted in Fig. 3E, this phase highlights the improved utility of the proposed model.

We also performed a calibration analysis on the nomogram, as shown in Fig. 4F, using the 2-year (Fig. 4G) and 3-year (Fig. 4H) survival data from the patients included in our prognostic model, which allowed us to construct a calibration curve. In this curve, the horizontal axis represents the survival probability predicted by the model, while the vertical axis shows the actual survival probability observed in the data. Different colored lines and points on the curve correspond to the model’s predictions at various time intervals. The closer these colored lines are to the gray line, which indicates an ideal scenario, the more accurate the predictions are at those specific time points. As depicted in Figure 3F–H, the results from the prognostic calibration analysis for the nomogram demonstrate that the predictive ability of our constructed prognostic model is superior. Furthermore, we utilized decision curve analysis (DCA) to evaluate the clinical relevance of the prognostic models at 1 year (Fig. 4I), 2 years (Fig. 4J), and 3 years (Fig. 4K), with the results summarized in these figures. The model’s line showed stability and consistently remained above the lines representing both all-positive and all-negative scenarios within a defined range; a wider range was associated with greater net benefit and improved model performance. Our findings indicated that the clinical predictive efficacy of the multivariate Cox regression model developed in this study ranked as follows: 3 years > 2 years > 1 year.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most challenging malignancies to treat effectively, with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis[36]. Surgical resection offers the best chance of cure, but unfortunately, the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages when surgical intervention is no longer feasible. Chemotherapy, particularly the use of gemcitabine and FOLFIRINOX, remains the backbone of treatment for advanced pancreatic cancer. However, the response rates to chemotherapy are often suboptimal, and even when initial responses are observed, the disease eventually relapses or progresses[37]. Given these challenges, there is an urgent need for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes. This study aimed to address these issues by identifying resistance-related genes and constructing a prognostic model that could guide personalized treatment strategies and improve survival rates for patients with PAAD[38].

In this study, we identified 12 key prognostic genes associated with PAAD: ADAMTS12, ARX, EREG, FBXL15, GBP6, IGF2BP3, KRT6A, NPDC1, PTPRN2, SPOCK1, TMPRSS11E, and UCA1. These genes consistently demonstrated significant HRs across at least three datasets, all with P values < 0.05. The identification of these genes as potential prognostic biomarkers is crucial because it lays the groundwork for predicting patient survival and elucidating the mechanisms underlying drug resistance in PAAD. The expression patterns and correlations of each gene with patient survival across different datasets merit further exploration. For example, ADAMTS12 has been implicated in various cancer-related processes, including extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenesis. Recent studies have shown that ADAMTS12 is involved in tumor progression and metastasis in several cancers, including PAAD[39]. ARX is a transcription factor involved in the regulation of gene expression during development, particularly in the central nervous system and pancreas,[40] but its role in cancer, particularly in PAAD, is less well understood. In our study, EREG emerged as a key prognostic gene in PAAD, and elevated EREG expression was associated with poor prognosis. This finding aligns with previous reports that EREG can enhance tumorigenicity and contribute to chemoresistance by activating ERK signaling pathways[41]. A recent study indicated that GBP6 may be involved in cancer immunity and tumor microenvironment modulation[42]. In our analysis, GBP6 was identified as a prognostic marker, with higher expression associated with poorer survival in patients with PAAD. This finding suggests that GBP6 could contribute to immune evasion mechanisms in PAAD, highlighting its potential as a target for immunotherapy and as a biomarker for predicting patient response to treatment. IGF2BP3 has been implicated in other cancers[43,44], indicating its broader role in tumorigenesis and highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Future studies should aim to unravel the molecular mechanisms of these genes in PAAD and investigate their potential for developing targeted therapies.

Compared to previously published PAAD prognostic models, our approach demonstrates the following innovations: (1) a comprehensive integrated machine learning pipeline was employed for feature selection, significantly improving the robustness and stability of the identified features; (2) a specific focus on drug resistance-related genes systematically revealed the relationship between chemotherapy resistance mechanisms and patient prognosis; and (3) extensive external validation was conducted, demonstrating superior predictive performance. These findings not only enhance the molecular understanding of PAAD progression but also provide a valuable tool for individualized risk assessment and optimization of immunotherapy strategies.

We have identified 12 key genes associated with drug resistance in PAAD. These findings offer novel insights for the development of targeted immunotherapies and exhibit significant potential for clinical translation. Specifically, the multigene risk score model developed in this study may aid clinicians in precise risk stratification, enabling early detection of high-risk individuals and informing tailored follow-up and treatment strategies. Furthermore, the identified genes represent promising targets for drug development and targeted therapies, providing a molecular rationale for novel mechanism-based treatments. Collectively, this work furnishes new tools and directions for enhancing personalized management and improving prognostic outcomes in pancreatic cancer patients.

Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the high-risk groups were significantly enriched in pathways such as SLE, cell cycle, and adherens junction, whereas the low-risk groups were enriched in neuroactive ligand‒receptor interaction. These findings indicate that the identified pathways are integral to PAAD progression and resistance to treatment. High-risk pathways are associated with crucial processes such as cell cycle regulation, the DNA damage response, and cell adhesion, all of which are essential for tumor growth and metastasis. Elucidating the precise mechanisms through which these pathways influence PAAD might offer new perspectives on tumor biology and uncover novel therapeutic targets. Furthermore, the pathways enriched in the low-risk group might confer protective effects against tumor progression, suggesting potential therapeutic interventions to improve patient outcomes.

Our analysis revealed significant disparities in immune cell infiltration between the high- and low-risk groups. High-risk patients exhibit reduced levels of activated CD8+ T cells, activated dendritic cells, and gamma-delta T cells, whereas regulatory T cells are more prevalent. This finding implies that the immune microenvironment significantly contributes to PAAD drug resistance. The diminished presence of effector immune cells in high-risk patients might promote an immunosuppressive environment, facilitating tumor growth and therapy resistance. Conversely, the low-risk group showed greater infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, which are crucial for antitumour immunity[45]. The differential ECIs and ICD markers between the high- and low-risk score groups further supports the notion that immune modulation is a key factor in PAAD outcomes. High ECIs such as PD-L1 and CTLA-4 in the high-risk score group suggests potential targets for immune checkpoint blockade therapies, which have shown promise in other cancer types[46]. These findings highlight the potential of modulating the immune microenvironment to improve therapeutic efficacy. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific roles of these immune cells in PAAD and exploring strategies to enhance antitumour immunity, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies.

In this investigation, we computed the resistance scores of genes associated with drug resistance and generated KM curves alongside survival data to ascertain the relationship between resistance scores and survival outcomes. Subsequently, we conducted differential analysis on the TCGA-PAAD dataset and performed univariate Cox analysis on DEGs across various datasets (TCGA-PAAD, GSE62452, GSE57495, GSE28735, GSE71729), identifying 12 genes of prognostic significance as resistance score feature genes, thereby ensuring the consistency of the influence of resistance score genes on prognosis.

After assessing the efficacy of models utilizing a combination of 112 machine learning algorithms, we selected the most effective RSF algorithm (C-index: 0.634) to develop a prognostic prediction model for patients with PAAD. KM curve analysis and time-dependent ROC analysis revealed that the model possesses commendable predictive capability. Calibration analysis and DCA outcomes indicated that the model’s predicted survival probabilities closely aligned with actual survival probabilities, further affirming the clinical applicability of the prognostic model. However, further optimization and validation in larger independent cohorts are essential to enhance the predictive accuracy. In addition, investigating the applicability of the model to other cancer types could extend its utility and provide broader insights into cancer prognosis and treatment.

In this study, the distribution patterns of 28 types of immune cells were assessed using the ssGSEA method, and differences in immune cell infiltration between high-risk and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test. The results were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 7A), with group comparison plots illustrating disparities in immune cell infiltration abundance across subgroups. The immune infiltration analysis was primarily conducted based on risk stratification derived from the prognostic risk model, rather than through direct correlation analysis between individual core gene expression and immune cell subsets. This approach was designed to systematically capture the overall impact of drug resistance-associated gene signatures on the tumor immune microenvironment. We fully acknowledge the academic value of further investigating the direct relationships between each core gene and specific immune cell types, and such analyses are planned for subsequent research.

We utilized cell-line drug sensitivity data to identify potential therapeutic agents. However, it is important to note that such data have inherent limitations when translated to clinical applications. Cell lines fail to fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment, heterogeneity, and pharmacodynamic processes present in patients. Therefore, drug sensitivity results derived from these models may be biased. Future studies should integrate organoid models, animal studies, and real-world patient data to further validate drug screening outcomes.

Our study possesses several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the absence of experimental validation for the key genes identified constrains the immediate clinical applicability of our findings. Despite aggregating data from multiple public databases, the sample size remains relatively limited, potentially affecting the generalizability of our results. Second, the integration of heterogeneous datasets introduces potential batch effects, which could compromise the robustness of our predictive models. Third, the lack of large-scale clinical validation necessitates further evaluation of the proposed prognostic models and therapeutic targets in diverse patient populations to ascertain their clinical relevance. Furthermore, critical clinical variables – such as tumor grade, treatment history, and performance status– were not systematically available in the TCGA-PAAD and GEO cohorts, precluding their inclusion in multivariate analyses. This omission may limit the interpretability and clinical utility of our model. In upcoming research, we intend to confirm our model’s effectiveness using larger datasets with more detailed annotations to enhance its precision and dependability. Further research should also explore the applicability of this model to other cancer types to advance broader insights into cancer prognosis and treatment.

Conclusion

We successfully identified a set of 12 key genes associated with drug resistance in PAAD using multiple machine learning algorithms. Additionally, the significant differences in immune cell distribution and immune regulatory gene expression between patients with high- and low-risk PAAD underscore the potential for leveraging the immune microenvironment in prognostic assessments and therapeutic interventions. These insights pave the way for future research to explore targeted immunotherapies that could improve outcomes for patients diagnosed with PAAD, particularly those identified as high-risk based on our risk score model.
