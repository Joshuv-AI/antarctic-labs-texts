Submucosal urothelial cancer of bladder: A case report
Authors: Sama Sappayasarn, Pokket Sirisreetreerux, Tarapon Setthawong, Ponthakorn Srithongsongsaeng, Jiraporn Chaikitti, Varagorn Jirajan
Published: 2026

Introduction

Bladder tumors can be either benign or malignant, but the majority of malignant cases are urothelial carcinoma, accounting for approximately 95% of all bladder cancers. Of these, 70–80% are non-muscle-invasive (NMIBC) while 20–30% are muscle-invasive (MIBC).1 The latter generally carries a poorer prognosis and is typically managed with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy or, in selected patients, bladder-preservation strategies.2, 3, 4

However, when urothelial carcinoma presents as a purely submucosal lesion confined to the detrusor muscle layer with no visible mucosal involvement, the clinical picture becomes far more challenging. This presentation is exceptionally rare. Most bladder cancers arise from the surface urothelium and invade inward, so a tumor hidden beneath an intact mucosa challenges the conventional understanding of disease origin and is difficult to recognize on routine cystoscopy. At present, reports of true primary submucosal bladder cancer remain extremely scarce. Two main hypotheses have been proposed to explain its pathogenesis: malignant transformation of aberrant urothelial cells rest within the submucosal tissue or carcinoma arising inside a bladder diverticulum whose neck subsequently closes.5

We report an asymptomatic 48-year-old woman with a localized submucosal bladder cancer discovered incidentally on surveillance ultrasonography. She required surgical intervention to achieve oncological control while preserving urinary function and long-term quality of life. By sharing this case we hope to provide urologists with a useful educational example that can inform clinical and surgical decision-making when they encounter similar rare presentations of submucosal bladder cancer.

Case report

A 48-year-old female patient was incidentally detected of a bladder tumor from abdominal ultrasonography performed for surveillance of a previously treated giant cell tumor of the rib. Her past medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a giant cell tumor of the rib. She was a non-smoker, although her husband was a smoker. There was no personal or family history of bladder or other malignancies. Ultrasonography revealed a 2.0 × 1.6 cm mass located on the anterior bladder wall.

(Fig. 1). The patient was asymptomatic, no gross hematuria, lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS) nor abdominal pain. Laboratory investigations showed no anemia or renal impairment. Urinalysis was negative for microscopic hematuria or pyuria. Cystoscopy demonstrated a submucosal bladder mass measuring approximately 2.0 × 2.0 cm. on the anterior wall, with an normally mucosal surface.

Imaging studies of the submucosal bladder tumor. (A) Transabdominal ultrasonography showing a 2.0 × 1.6 cm mass on the anterior bladder wall (calipers). (B) Magnetic resonance urography demonstrating the localized submucosal lesion without evidence of muscle invasion, extravesical extension, or pelvic/intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy (red arrow). (C) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen confirming the submucosal mass without evidence of muscle invasion, extravesical extension, or pelvic/intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy (red arrow).

Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and MR urography (Fig. 1) confirmed a localized submucosal lesion with minimal heterogeneous enhancement. There was no evidence of muscle invasion, extravesical extension, or pelvic/intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy. A preoperative diagnosis of submucosal bladder tumor at the anterior bladder wall was made, and transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) was performed. During resection, a typical urothelial bladder tumor with small papillary morphology was revealed beneath the normal bladder mucosa (Fig. 2). Because the tumor appeared to involve the full thickness of the detrusor muscle and was very close to the bladder neck, a partial resection was performed owing to intraoperative concern for bladder perforation and difficult angulation. Histopathological examination of the TURBT specimen demonstrated low-grade urothelial carcinoma, but no muscularis propria was identified in the sample. The patient was counseled regarding further treatment options, including bladder-preserving approach, such as partial cystectomy or Re-TURBT, versus radical cystectomy. After thorough discussion of risks and benefits, she chose the laparoscopic partial cystectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection.

Cystoscopic findings during transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT). (A) Normal-appearing overlying mucosa. (B) Papillary urothelial tumor revealed after resection of the mucosa.

Under general anesthesia and in the Trendelenburg position, an extraperitoneal space was created (Fig. 3). The tumor margin was marked 1.0 cm. away from the lesion using transillumination guidance with a flexible cystoscope (Fig. 3). Partial cystectomy was performed, and frozen sections from all margins were sent for confirming negative margins. The bladder defect was closed with a continuous barbed suture using the Heineke-Mikulicz technique to prevent bladder neck contracture. Standard template pelvic lymph nodes dissection was performed.

Intraoperative laparoscopic partial cystectomy. (A) View after creation of extraperitoneal space. (B) Tumor margin delineated by transillumination with flexible cystoscope (white outline and arrow indicating “Tumor”).

A suprapubic cystostomy (SPC) tube was placed and successfully removed on postoperative day 14 after and no bladder leakage was observed on cystography. The final pathology revealed a high-grade invasive urothelial carcinoma, measuring 2.0 cm. in its greatest dimension. The tumor invaded the deep outer half of the muscularis propria, but all surgical margins were free of tumor (Fig. 4). No metastatic carcinoma was found in the dissected pelvic lymph nodes.

Pathological examination of the partial cystectomy specimen. (A) Gross appearance of the resected tumor. (B) Histopathology showing high-grade invasive urothelial carcinoma invading the deep outer half of the muscularis propria.

An oncologist was consulted to discuss adjuvant treatment options. After being informed of the potential role of immunotherapy versus surveillance, the patient chose for imaging follow-up due to concerns regarding the adverse effects of medications and financial limitation. At 3 months post-surgery, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen demonstrated no evidence of local recurrence, lymph node enlargement, or distant metastasis. During postoperative follow-up, the patient had neither voiding lower urinary tract symptoms nor urinary incontinence. Cystoscopic surveillance at 3 and 6 months after partial cystectomy showed no evidence of local recurrence (Fig. 5).

Cystoscopic surveillance at 6 months postoperatively demonstrating healthy bladder mucosa with no evidence of local recurrence.

Discussion

Primary submucosal bladder cancer is a rare and poorly understood condition. Two main hypotheses have been proposed regarding its pathogenesis.5 In this patient, we could not be entirely certain from imaging alone whether the disease represented muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), even though MRI generally performs well in local staging.

Urothelial carcinoma presenting purely as a submucosal lesion without any visible mucosal abnormality is exceptionally uncommon. Niimi et al. described what appears to be one of the earliest documented cases in 2021.5 In our patient, the.

2 cm anterior-wall mass was found incidentally on surveillance ultrasonography performed for a prior giant cell tumor of the rib. She had no hematuria, no lower urinary tract symptoms, and cystoscopy showed only a smooth, normal-looking mucosal surface overlying the mass. This highlights a key diagnostic pitfall that standard cystoscopic inspection can easily miss these lesions and underscores why clinicians should keep submucosal tumors in mind when imaging reveals a bladder mass despite unremarkable cystoscopic findings. The differential diagnosis for a submucosal bladder mass is broad, ranging from benign mesenchymal tumors like leiomyoma to rare presentations of paraganglioma or urothelial carcinoma.

Multiparametric MRI, often interpreted with the Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS), has become a valuable tool for preoperative staging of bladder cancer, with reported sensitivity and specificity in the range of 80–90% or higher for detecting muscle invasion in many series.6 Nevertheless, it is not perfect. Equivocal findings, inter-reader variability, and technical factors can lead to staging uncertainty.

In our case, contrast-enhanced CT and MR urography suggested a localized submucosal lesion without clear muscle-invasive features, yet we still needed tissue confirmation. TURBT was performed as the initial step, but it had important limitations here. The resected specimen did not include muscularis propria, deeper resection carried a real risk of bladder perforation, and the anterior location made complete removal technically difficult because of angulation. These issues are well known and frequently contribute to under-staging of MIBC when TURBT is used alone. This limitation is further highlighted by Kijima et al., who showed that even patients judged to have a complete response (T0) after chemoradiation still harbored residual muscle-invasive tumor in 10% of cases when partial cystectomy was performed.7 The discordance between our initial TURBT (low-grade, no muscularis propria) and the final specimen (high-grade pT2b) reflects two limitations of endoscopic sampling: under-staging and under-grading. Detrusor muscle in the first resection is a recognized surrogate of resection quality and predicts early recurrence8; in its absence, upstaging to muscle-invasive disease reaches 40–50%, versus 15–20% when muscle is present.9A submucosal location magnifies every pitfall—the tumor lies beneath intact mucosa, the loop must be driven deeper at greater perforation risk, and an anterior/bladder-neck site adds unfavorable angulation—forcing a trade-off between sampling depth and bladder integrity. The grade shift seen here is thus best understood not as biological progression but as a sampling phenomenon, with the full-thickness specimen finally capturing the true grade and depth of invasion. This supports mandating a restaging resection when muscularis propria is absent,9 and reinforces that, in such tumors, a full-thickness specimen may be both more accurate and more definitive than repeated endoscopic sampling. In contrast, laparoscopic partial cystectomy allowed us to obtain a full-thickness specimen of the bladder wall. This provided accurate pathological staging revealing high-grade invasive urothelial carcinoma invading the deep outer half of the muscularis propria (pT2b) along with reliable margin assessment and complete tumor removal in one procedure.

The case therefore illustrates that while modern imaging is helpful for planning, histopathological evaluation of a full-thickness specimen remains the most dependable method and partial cystectomy can offer clearer staging than TURBT in anatomically challenging submucosal tumors.

For partial cystectomy combined with pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) can be a reasonable bladder-preserving option that does not appear to compromise oncologic outcomes. Suitable candidates typically have a solitary lesion, no concomitant carcinoma in situ (CIS), no hydronephrosis, no multifocal disease, and achievable negative margins with preserved bladder function. Recent evidence confirms that when these strict criteria are met, partial cystectomy yields 5-year recurrence-free survival of 39–67%, cancer-specific survival of 62–84%, and overall survival of 45–70%, with population-based and matched case-control studies showing no significant difference in these endpoints compared with radical cystectomy.10 In our patient, the tumor was solitary and located on the anterior wall without CIS or hydronephrosis. Although its proximity to the bladder neck added technical difficulty, we used transillumination with a flexible cystoscope to mark a 1 cm margin, sent multiple frozen sections, and closed the defect using the Heineke-Mikulicz technique to avoid stenosis. Standard-template PLND was included because MIBC could not be ruled out preoperatively and because even in node-negative disease, thorough nodal clearance may offer staging and potential survival benefits (as currently recommended by NCCN and IBCG guidelines).

After final pathology confirmed pT2bN0 disease with negative margins, we discussed adjuvant options with the patient, including immunotherapy.11 She opted for surveillance because of concerns about side effects and cost. At six-month follow-up, CT imaging and cystoscopy showed no local recurrence, no lymph node enlargement, and no distant metastasis. Voiding function remained excellent, with no incontinence or lower urinary tract symptoms. This outcome supports the idea that partial cystectomy with PLND can achieve good cancer control in highly selected MIBC patients while preserving quality of life and offering lower morbidity and better functional results than radical cystectomy.

Overall, there are still no standardized diagnostic or therapeutic guidelines for primary submucosal bladder cancer. Cystoscopic findings can be misleading when the mucosa appears normal, and although multiparametric MRI plays an increasingly important role in staging, histopathological confirmation is essential. Treatment decisions must balance oncologic control with preservation of urinary function and the patient's quality of life. Radical surgery may be required in some situations, but organ-preserving approaches such as partial cystectomy deserve consideration when technically feasible and when strict selection criteria are fulfilled.12,13

This case emphasizes three essential clinical considerations. First, urothelial carcinoma may manifest as an isolated submucosal lesion, entirely escaping routine cystoscopic detection. Second, while multiparametric MRI is a valuable staging adjunct, it has inherent limitations; TURBT often results in under-staging of these tumors, whereas full-thickness resection via partial cystectomy provides more definitive histopathological evidence of muscle invasion. Third, in carefully selected patients, partial cystectomy combined with pelvic lymph node dissection serves as a viable bladder-preserving strategy, offering oncologic outcomes comparable to radical surgery without compromising cancer control or patient quality of life.

Continued reporting of similar cases will be important to build a better evidence base and guide future management of this uncommon entity.

Conclusion

Primary submucosal urothelial carcinoma of the bladder is a rare entity that poses a significant diagnostic challenge and currently lacks an established standard of treatment. This case emphasizes that a normal-appearing mucosa does not exclude underlying muscle-invasive malignancy. While multiparametric MRI is a crucial staging adjunct, histopathological confirmation through definitive resection remains essential for accurate diagnosis. Our findings suggest that with strict patient selection and meticulous surgical technique, partial cystectomy serves as a viable bladder-preserving alternative to radical surgery, achieving excellent oncological control while preserving urinary function and quality of life. Ultimately, individualized management is key to optimizing outcomes in this rare patient population.
