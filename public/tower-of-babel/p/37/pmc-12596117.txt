Hashimoto's thyroiditis and congenital long QT syndrome: a dangerous addition causing torsades—case report
Authors: Ramesh Acharya, Vaishnavi Sabesan, Deepti Pandit, Dan Schuller, Byomesh Tripathi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypothyroidism leads to a prolonged QT interval due to modulation of the potassium channel by low circulating T3, leading to a disproportionate lengthening of the action potential. Patients with long QT syndromes (LQTS) have an increased risk of symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (SCD).Case summaryA 40-year-old female with a previous history of congenital LQTS, Type 1 diabetes mellitus, anaemia, and untreated hypothyroidism was brought to the hospital with an abrupt onset of palpitations and disorientation following multiple (>30) automated implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shocks with no identifiable triggers. Emergency medical services telemetry monitoring en route to the hospital revealed torsades de pointes (TdP), following which she received 4 g of magnesium sulfate intravenously and reverted to sinus rhythm. Electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm with a prolonged QTc of 515 ms. The laboratory testing revealed high TSH with low T4. The myxoedema coma score was markedly elevated at 40, indicating severe hypothyroidism. The thyroid peroxidase antibody was >600 IU/mL, indicating Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Echocardiography showed left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%–60% with no wall motion abnormalities. We treated the patient with intravenous hydrocortisone, levothyroxine, and liothyronine. After the treatment, she had no further episodes of torsades, and QTc improved to her baseline of 479 ms.DiscussionHypothyroidism is linked to electrocardiographic abnormalities such as sinus bradycardia, QT interval prolongation, malignant ventricular arrhythmias such as ventricular premature beat, ventricular tachycardia, TdP, and ventricular fibrillation. Assessment of thyroid function is of high value in QT prolongation syndromes to avoid SCD incidence. Thyroid hormone influences ventricular inhomogeneity, and L-thyroxine replacement treatment may minimize malignant ventricular arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death in patients with primary hypothyroidism. Our case highlights the importance of urgent electrolyte replacement and levothyroxine therapy to avoid the risk of ventricular arrhythmias.

Introduction

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) are two distinct clinical entities that, when coexistent, significantly elevate the risk of severe cardiac complications. Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is an autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic lymphocytic inflammation of the thyroid gland, often leading to hypothyroidism. Hypothyroidism, in turn, can prolong the QT interval and predispose individuals to ventricular arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes (TdP).1–3 Congenital LQTS, a genetic channelopathy, also results in QT prolongation and is associated with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death due to malignant arrhythmias.4 The coexistence of both conditions presents a synergistic risk, with hypothyroidism exacerbating the electrophysiological instability already present in congenital LQTS. This case underscores the complex interplay between these disorders, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and prompt intervention to mitigate the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old woman with a medical history of type 1 diabetes mellitus, anaemia, congenital LQTS, and hypothyroidism presented with complaints of palpitations and disorientation after receiving multiple shocks from her automated implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (AICD). She denied fever, chills, headache, blurred vision, dyspnoea, or syncope. Her history included a 20-pack-year of tobacco use. She reported no recent dehydration or viral illness. The onset of symptoms was abrupt, and she experienced approximately 30 AICD shocks before admission.

At presentation, vital signs were within normal limits, although the patient appeared mildly distressed. Physical examination revealed lipohypertrophy near the left periumbilical region, consistent with repeated insulin injections. Examination of the lower extremities showed dry skin and a full-thickness ulcer on the right calcaneus, without signs of active bleeding or purulent discharge.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) performed before admission demonstrated TdP, for which she received 4 mg of intravenous magnesium sulfate. Upon arrival, repeat ECG showed a markedly prolonged corrected QT (QTc) interval of 515 ms as shown in Figure 1. Serum troponin T was elevated at 46 ng/L, prompting continuous cardiac monitoring. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%–60% with no regional wall motion abnormalities or valvular pathology.

Following initiation of thyroxine therapy, the corrected QT interval normalized to 479 ms (bottom panel). The corrected QT interval was calculated using Fridericia’s formula.

A computed tomography angiogram of the left lower extremity revealed abrupt arterial cut-offs suggestive of occlusion or advanced atherosclerotic disease. No abscess formation was identified. Thyroid and adrenal function tests were ordered. The patient received intravenous fluid resuscitation, and serial ECGs were conducted every 4 h to monitor for evolving ischaemic changes.

Thyroid function testing revealed profound hypothyroidism, with markedly elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and suppressed free thyroxine (T4) levels. Management included intravenous hydrocortisone (100 mg), intravenous levothyroxine (100 µg), and oral levothyroxine (10 µg every 8 h). Left heart catheterization revealed no significant coronary artery disease. Standard post-catheterization care was provided, with continued monitoring of the QTc interval due to persistent thyroid dysfunction and ongoing thyroid hormone replacement.

The patient’s prerenal acute kidney injury resolved following adequate fluid resuscitation. Thyroid peroxidase antibody levels were elevated (>600 IU/mL), confirming a diagnosis of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. She was maintained on levothyroxine and initiated on propranolol 10 mg three times daily. Follow-up ECG showed normalization of the QTc interval to 457 ms after initiation of thyroid hormone therapy. Additional management included local wound care for the calcaneal ulcer, podiatry referral, and optimization of diabetes control with reinforcement of offloading strategies. The patient’s post-discharge follow-up was uneventful.

Discussion

This case highlights the intricate interplay between hypothyroidism, congenital LQTS, and the risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias, particularly TdP. Although ventricular arrhythmias in the setting of hypothyroidism are relatively uncommon, their occurrence can be life-threatening, especially in patients with predisposing conditions such as congenital LQTS.2

Severe hypothyroidism is known to produce electrocardiographic abnormalities, including sinus bradycardia, atrioventricular block, QT interval prolongation, and a spectrum of ventricular arrhythmias—ranging from premature ventricular contractions to ventricular tachycardia, TdP, and ventricular fibrillation.5 The QT prolongation in hypothyroidism is primarily due to delayed ventricular repolarization, which markedly elevates the risk of malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. This repolarization delay is attributed to altered ion channel function, particularly involving L-type calcium channels and transient outward potassium channels.6 Low levels of circulating triiodothyronine (T3) modulate potassium channel activity, disproportionately prolonging the cardiac action potential and thereby extending the QT interval.7

The mechanisms underlying arrhythmogenesis in prolonged QT syndromes are multifaceted. One prevailing hypothesis involves an imbalance in the autonomic nervous system between the right and left sympathetic branches, which creates spatial heterogeneity in ventricular repolarization and predisposes individuals to arrhythmias.8 In the present case, such pathophysiological changes likely contributed to the observed malignant arrhythmias.

Moreover, diabetes mellitus—a known arrhythmogenic condition—further complicates the clinical picture. Studies have demonstrated positive correlations between elevated TSH levels and fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and other metabolic risk factors.9 Patients with poorly controlled diabetes, particularly those at risk of diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemia, frequently exhibit electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia, which are known to exacerbate QT prolongation.10 In our patient, the combination of congenital LQTS, anaemia, diabetes-related complications, and electrolyte imbalances created a high-risk substrate for arrhythmogenesis, emphasizing the multifactorial aetiology.

Thyroid hormones significantly influence cardiac electrophysiology, and thyroid hormone replacement therapy has been shown to reverse QT prolongation and mitigate the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias in patients with primary hypothyroidism.11,12 In this case, the administration of intravenous and oral levothyroxine led to a significant reduction in the QTc interval—from 515 to 457 ms—demonstrating the efficacy of early intervention.

The management of arrhythmias in hypothyroid patients with coexisting LQTS also requires judicious selection of antiarrhythmic agents. Amiodarone, although widely used, is contraindicated in such scenarios due to its potential to exacerbate hypothyroidism via the Wolff–Chaikoff effect and prolong the QT interval, thereby worsening the arrhythmic risk. Alternative agents such as lignocaine may offer safer options for rhythm stabilization without further QT prolongation.13

Given the established association between hypothyroidism and QT interval prolongation, thyroid function testing should be an integral part of the diagnostic workup in patients presenting with unexplained QT prolongation or ventricular arrhythmias.14 In this patient, timely diagnosis and comprehensive management—including thyroid hormone replacement, magnesium supplementation, and correction of electrolyte imbalances—were pivotal in stabilizing cardiac rhythm and improving clinical outcomes.
