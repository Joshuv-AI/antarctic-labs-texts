Hypertension (HTN) is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, yet only one in 4 people with HTN in the U.S. achieve optimal control.1 Longstanding uncontrolled HTN in the majority of Americans leads to increased rates of HTN-related morbidity and mortality. Residency-affiliated primary care clinics serve patient populations facing greater socioeconomic barriers, leading to disproportionately higher rates of uncontrolled HTN compared with national benchmarks. Addressing discrepancies in HTN care is critical to mitigating the long-term burden of cardiovascular disease within this vulnerable population.Take-Home Message•Our quality improvement project successfully reduced the prevalence of uncontrolled hypertension in our clinic population by 11% through clinic process changes and same-day education interventions.

Project Rationale

The American Heart Association (AHA) estimates that 122.4 million U.S. adults, nearly 47% of the population, have HTN.1 Baseline data for our residency clinic showed of the 2,752 patients cared for in the clinic, 50% had HTN as defined by the Joint National Committee threshold of ≥140/90 mm Hg, and 39% of clinic patients had uncontrolled HTN.2 When using the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/AHA guideline-recommended target of <130/80 mm Hg, an even larger number was identified as having uncontrolled HTN. Similar to national trends, the patients with HTN in our clinic were 50% female, predominantly Caucasian, and the majority were between 40 and 64 years old.3 More than 40% of patients were affected by at least 1 social determinant of health (see Table 1), 43% had depression, and 68% were tobacco users. The aim of this quality improvement (QI) initiative was to reduce the prevalence of uncontrolled HTN by at least 5% over a 12-month period. Project Description

Electronic health record data were reviewed to evaluate current performance and root causes behind uncontrolled blood HTN within the patient population. Baseline data over the 6 months before project initiation demonstrated a median HTN control rate of 61%. The control rate was determined by those patients who followed with the clinic for primary care, who had a diagnosis of HTN in the electronic health record, and whose last documented office blood pressure was <140/80 mm Hg. Additionally, a random-sample chart review was performed to identify key drivers to less-than-ideal blood pressure control. This random chart review of patients with uncontrolled HTN who were seen over a 1-month window demonstrated deficits in patient medication adherence (∼25%), HTN not identified and/or addressed (∼32%), and lack of appropriate provider management (∼20%).

As part of existing office workflow, a collaborative approach toward obtaining a second blood pressure measurement for all patients with an initial elevated blood pressure reading was continued throughout the project. In this workflow, the rooming medical assistant (MA) obtained the initial blood pressure reading. If it was elevated, a sign was placed on the examination room door to indicate a repeat blood pressure reading was needed, which would then be obtained by the physician performing the office visit that day. Key performance indicators (KPIs) were recorded daily, and the preceding clinic day's values were reported out at the clinic's daily team huddle. KPIs measured throughout the duration of the project included the number of patients who presented with elevated blood pressure (defined as ≥140/90 mm Hg), the proportion of patients with an initial elevated blood pressure measurement who had a second reading obtained, and a third KPI, added as part of PDSA cycle 1, that reported the number of patients with 2 elevated blood pressure readings who had arranged a follow-up appointment for HTN within the following 6 weeks. In addition to the daily KPI reports, the primary outcome of clinic HTN control rates was tracked via the electronic health record and was reported out on the final day of every month.

PDSA Cycle 1 (June 2024–December 2024)

As part of the initial random-sample chart review process, we found that the majority of patients with elevated blood pressures in the office were not being scheduled within 2 to 4 weeks for follow-up, as recommended by the ACC/AHA guidelines for HTN. To align with the ACC/AHA recommendations, PDSA cycle 1 was aimed at standardizing clinic-wide follow-up appointments to within 6 weeks for every patient who had 2 elevated blood pressure readings. The time frame was expanded from 4 weeks to 6 weeks to account for the x + y scheduling cadence of our residency clinic, in which residents were in the office every fifth and sixth week, to ensure patient continuity with the resident physician serving as their primary provider. In addition to the follow-up time frame expectations being discussed at daily clinic huddles, all residents and faculty attended an education session reviewing the ACC/AHA guidelines on HTN diagnosis and management.

PDSA Cycle 2 (December 2024–March 2025)

Accurate blood pressure measurement is pivotal to appropriate and optimal blood pressure management. On review of our own clinic, we found that there was no standard education or assessment of MAs and other patient rooming staff to ensure that blood pressure measurements were being obtained accurately. A fishbone diagram was completed to identify possible factors involved in accurate clinic blood pressure measurements; this included policy, procedure, equipment, and data collection. Process mapping of the rooming process revealed inconsistencies among rooming staff as well as inaccurate measurement techniques.

To begin to improve measurement accuracy, all of the automated cuffs in the office were calibrated and validated. Next, a dedicated education session was delivered to rooming staff. The session addressed the patient rooming process, appropriate cuff sizing, measurement technique, alternative methods for obese patients, and mitigation of confounding factors such as caffeine and nicotine. Educational materials were additionally distributed to staff unable to attend the live session and to resident physicians. Blood pressure measurement checklists were placed in examination rooms to serve as a visual aid (Figure 1). A postintervention knowledge assessment was completed by rooming staff. Skills assessments of rooming staff were then performed to ensure adherence to standardized procedures (Figure 2), with repeat assessment scheduled for 6 months postintervention. PDSA Cycle 3 (April 2025–July 2025)

In cycle 3, we aimed to improve patient engagement and self-management through structured, nursing-led HTN education sessions. Sessions led by registered nurses (RNs) were designed using AHA/ACC patient education materials. The RNs discussed home blood pressure monitoring and technique, individualized target blood pressure goals, symptoms of uncontrolled HTN, and modifiable risk factors. Emphasis was placed on medication adherence. Patients received AHA blood pressure logs and were scheduled for follow-up. Education sessions were tracked by the use of a dot phrase entered after each session.

Project Deliverables

Outcome

At baseline (February-May 2024), 61% of clinic patients with HTN were able to control their blood pressure. Baseline KPI data are limited before the beginning of PDSA cycle 1, however the recorded data showed the second blood pressure check rate was around 97%. In PDSA cycle 1 (6 months), the focus shifted toward scheduling follow-up visits for those patients with 2 elevated blood pressure measurements. During this period, HTN control rates improved from 61% to 67%. KPI process outcome data revealed a second elevated blood pressure check was completed 92% of the time, and a follow-up visit baseline was established 72% of the time.

During PDSA cycle 2 (December 2024–March 2025), all MAs, RNs, and residents completed dedicated blood pressure process and measurement education. A posteducation survey was distributed to MAs and RNs. The survey completion rate was 100%, with respondents reporting that the training improved both their understanding and execution of proper blood pressure measurements. Direct observation of all rooming staff members was conducted, and checklists were placed in all patient examination rooms. Unfortunately, blood pressure control declined during this cycle, from 67% to 65%. As this cycle occurred during the winter months of December to March, we attributed this drop to known seasonal variability in HTN control. National trends have shown systolic blood pressure is higher in the winter months, and the control rate also declines during this time.5 Of note however, the rate of second elevated blood pressure checks stayed stable at >90%, and 6-week follow-up visit scheduling improved from 72% to 79%.

PDSA cycle 3 (16 weeks) was aimed at patient education and self-management. During this period, 10 RN-led patient education sessions occurred. This process was dependent on the resident physician engaging with the nurse. HTN control improved from 65% to 70% during this cycle. Data showed second elevated blood pressure checks were steady at >90%, and follow-up visit scheduling compliance jumped from 79% to 87%. Impact

This QI initiative aimed to improve blood pressure control among an underserved patient population in hopes of reducing long-term cardiovascular outcomes. Over a 13-month period, control rates improved through the implementation of low-cost, high-impact interventions. Targeted patient education efforts promoted active engagement in self-management through encouragement to record their home readings; however, the number of patients returning for follow-up with their logs was not tracked. The blood pressure checklist resource was made available to an additional 80 primary care clinics across the health system, allowing for standardized blood pressure measurement across the organization's primary care footprint.

Future directions

Future interventions will continue building from the existing PDSAs, which have now become standard process in the clinic, and will be targeted to address the specific barriers of our patient population, particularly social determinants of health and patient self-management. Screening for social determinants of health will continue to be performed at every visit for all patients. A remote blood pressure monitoring program pilot is currently underway in hopes of reducing patient transportation barriers and allowing for more rapid medication titration to expedite the time it takes to reach blood pressure control targets. Education sessions will continue, however the process of delivery will be revamped to help more early identification of those patients who would benefit and remove the variability among resident physicians in the use of this resource. Hypertension management lectures will remain part of resident training to maintain knowledge. A standardized documentation template for HTN-focused visits will also be initiated to help enhance clinical efficiency and adherence to care standards and to allow for data collection. Future integration of pharmacist-led interventions will be explored to further strengthen management and foster a lasting culture of QI.8

Discussion

In nearly half of adults with HTN, the condition remains uncontrolled despite effective therapies and evidence-based guidelines.4 Our clinic's baseline performance was already slightly above national averages, however simple interventions and process changes enabled significant improvement. HTN control is greatly influenced by patient characteristics, including income, education, language, access to healthy foods, transportation, and residence in health professional shortage areas, but also care site–specific factors such as provider knowledge and practice patterns, blood pressure measurement techniques, and health-system resources.6,7

The AHA and the American Medical Association note additional barriers to HTN control, including limited health and digital literacy, lack of home blood pressure monitoring devices or telehealth, and competing demands such as unstable housing and inflexible work schedules.7 The ACC emphasizes strategies such as multidisciplinary team-based care to strengthen HTN management.9 These determinants are particularly relevant in residency clinics, where rates of uncontrolled HTN often exceed national averages.6 These factors likely contribute to the >40% of patients in our clinic with uncontrolled HTN, highlighting the need for targeted, context-specific interventions.

Our QI initiative addressed process, provider, and patient drivers contributing to poor HTN control, including inconsistent measurement, patient self-management, and therapeutic inertia. Evidence demonstrates that team-based care, performance feedback, and patient-centered interventions significantly improve HTN outcomes.2,3 We implemented evidence-based standardized blood pressure measurement protocols, provider and patient education materials, and enhanced self-management support, while maintaining feedback on HTN control performance through daily clinic huddles and KPI board tracking. These interventions culminated in an absolute 11% and relative 18% improvement in HTN control rates over a 14-month period from June 1, 2024, to July 31, 2025. Some improvement is likely also attributed solely to generalized increased HTN awareness by nature of having a devoted resident QI project surrounding the topic.

Conclusions

Uncontrolled HTN carries significant long-term health implications and remains a major challenge in residency clinics with limited resources. Through standardized measurement, team-based care, and nurse-led education, we improved blood pressure control from 61% to 72% in 14 months. Future interventions will focus on enhancing access, improving the continuity and quality of HTN care, and ensuring the sustainability of current initiatives.
