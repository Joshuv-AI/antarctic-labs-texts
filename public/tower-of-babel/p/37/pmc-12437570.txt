Antimicrobial resistance of Mycoplasma genitalium in Korea for two decades
Authors: Gilho Lee, Heeyoon Park, Yumi Seo, Seung-Ju Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAntimicrobial-resistant (AMR) Mycoplasma genitalium has become a global concern. To assess AMR patterns in Korea, we analyzed the genetic diversity of the AMR-determining sites (ARDS) in M. genitalium across in 285 samples collected over two decades.Materials and MethodsMycoplasma-positive samples were collected from various groups: males at one referral center (ORC) from 2014 to 2019 (G1; 43) and from 2020 to 2023 (G2; 54), males at primary care clinics (PCCs) in 2018–2019 (G3; 84), female sex workers at a regional healthcare center in 2004 (G4; 51), and females at PCCs in 2018–2019 (G5; 53). Sanger sequencing was performed at positions 2058/2059 in the 23S rRNA gene and positions GyrA95, GyrA99, ParC83, and ParC87 of topoisomerase IV genes in M. genitalium.ResultsIn 2004, mutations were rare with S83I (0.0%), M95I (3.4%), and A2059G (10.5%). By 2020–2023, ARDS mutations had become common, peaking at S83I (83.3%) and A2059G (70.4%). No sex differences were observed in ARDS mutations between G3 and G5. A comparison of mutations in males at the ORC showed significant differences in A2059G and S83I between pre-2020 (G1) and post-2020 (G2). Males at the ORC (G1) had higher mutation rates in A2059G, GyrA95, and GyrA99 compared to males at PCCs (G3).ConclusionsCurrently, referred males exhibit a rapid increase in AMR, and the synergy between A2059G and S83I/R/N mutations raises concerns regarding the emergence of pan-drug-resistant M. genitalium, potentially leading to an irreversible crisis in treatment options in Korea.Keywords: Drug resistance, Korea, Mycoplasma genitalium

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma genitalium is associated with an increased risk of urethritis and epididymitis in men, and pelvic inflammatory disease and infertility in females [1]. Beta-lactams are inherently ineffective to M. genitalium. Moreover, it naturally exhibits high minimum inhibitory concentrations for ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin [2]. Currently, only azithromycin and moxifloxacin are clinically effective against M. genitalium [2,3,4]. However, misuse of these limited treatment options has led to its rapidly evolving antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

Due to the difficulty in culturing M. genitalium [2], genetic marker analysis at AMR-determining sites (ARDS) has become the gold standard for assessing its AMR [2,3,4]. Treatment failure with macrolides is well established to be associated with genetic variations at positions 2058 and 2059 in the 23S rRNA gene, whereas moxifloxacin resistance is linked to genetic diversity in the quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDR) of gyrA and parC genes [2,3,4,5,6].

The genetic variation in ARDSs differs across countries [5,6,7], influenced by factors such as antimicrobial availability and use, laboratory testing guidelines, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) treatment recommendations [6,8,9,10]. Additionally, AMR is not evenly distributed within a nation. Certain population groups are at higher risk of acquiring AMR M. genitalium [11]. Consequently, prevalence studies in specific populations may either underestimate or overestimate true resistance rates. To date, studies identifying the key factors contributing to AMR M. genitalium are limited.

Over the past two decades, several national policies in Korea may have contributed to the increased risk of AMR in M. genitalium. In 2004, a strict law prohibiting sex trafficking was enacted, driving commercial sex work underground. As a result, commercial sex workers (CSWs) became less willing to undergo routine STI screenings at regional healthcare centers. Prior to 2015, multiplex amplification tests for STIs were performed only in patients with acute symptoms of urethritis or vaginitis. However, when the Korean National Medical Insurance (KNMI) began to cover the test financially in 2015, it has been routinely performed in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. In 2020, moxifloxacin was added to the KNMI drug list for treating M. genitalium, allowing clinicians in primary care clinics (PCCs) to prescribe it easily without the need for resistance testing. The combination of over detection through multiplex testing and over prescription of the two available antimicrobials may have contributed to the acceleration of AMR in M. genitalium in Korea.

In 2015, we established a unique M. genitalium research center in Korea. The center has systematically collected infected samples from referred patients across the country, analyzed genetic variation related to AMR, and administered resistance-guided antimicrobial treatments. Using the accumulated data over the past two decades, we estimated the genetic diversity of M. genitalium and characterized its infection patterns in Korea.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Sample sources

We utilized stored DNA samples from the first voided urine (FVU) of males referred to Dankook University Hospital from 2014 to 2019 (Group 1 [G1]) and from 2020 to 2023 (Group 2 [G2]). Additionally, we used stored DNA samples from the FVU of males (Group 3 [G3]) and FVU or vaginal swabs of females (Group 5 [G5]) who visited the PCCs between 2018 and 2019. Finally, we utilized long-stored DNA samples that were collected from female commercial sex workers (FSWs) in 2004 (Group 4 [G4]) (Supplementary Table 1).

2. Sequencing of AMR-determining sites in M. genitalium

The stored DNA samples of G1, G3, and G5 were thawed at room temperature, while DNA from G2 was extracted from FVU samples as previously described [10,11]. We then amplified three specific regions using primers targeting the ARDS of M. genitalium: the 2058 and 2059 loci in the 23S rRNA gene for macrolide resistance, and regions in the gyrA and parC gene for QRDRs (Supplementary Table 2). The amplicons were visualized on a 1%–1.5% agarose gel, purified, and sequenced using the conventional Sanger method (Bioneer and Macrogen) [11].

We extracted DNA from 650 endocervical samples of FSWs (G4) who attended a regional healthcare center between April and October 2004 for screening of microbial agents causing STIs, including AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome), syphilis, gonorrhea and chlamydial infection [12]. These DNA samples were stored at a -70℃. The frozen DNA samples were thawed at room temperature and were screened for M. genitalium using two amplification primers (MgPa-355F and MgPa-432R) and one MgPa-380 TaqMan probe [13]. For this experiment, we used a synthesized target gene with partial MgPa sequences inserted into a plasmid vector pBHA (Bioneer) as the positive control (Supplementary Table 2). Untreated polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-grade water served as the negative control. Among the DNA samples, 51 tested positive for M. genitalium (cycle threshold [Ct] value <35) by real-time PCR. We then amplified three regions of the ARDS in M. genitalium using the same primers. For faint or non-visible products from the 23S rRNA and gyrA regions, we performed seminested re-PCR. The amplified products were visualized on a 1%–1.5% agarose gels, purified and sequenced using the conventional Sanger method (Bioneer).

3. Statistical analyses

We categorized the genetic data into wild-type and mutant groups. Fisher's exact test was used to assess genetic differences between groups. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using logistic regression analysis. A two-sided null hypothesis of no significant difference was rejected if the p-value was less than 0.05. We conducted all analyses using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 software for Windows (IBM Corp.).

4. Ethical considerations

The present study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University College of Medicine (approval number: 2024-11-007A). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants upon their enrollment.

RESULTS

Good-quality DNA sequences for the six ARDSs were obtained from all cases in G1 (43 cases, 100.0%), G2 (54 cases, 100.0%), G3 (84 cases, 100.0%), and G5 (53 cases, 100.0%). However, some amplified DNA sequences in G4 were ambiguous and difficult to analyze. We successfully obtained readable sequences for only 29 cases for the gyrA gene (56.9%), 33 cases for the parC gene (64.7%), and 38 cases for 23S rRNA loci (74.5%) (Table 1).

1. Genetic variations over time intervals

The variation rate of GyrA at position 95 (GyrA95) among the groups remained relatively stable over time. In contrast, variations at GyrA99 and ParC83 in the QRDR, as well as at position 2059 in the 23S rRNA gene, exhibited significant differences across the study groups (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Samples from FSWs in 2004 (G4) showed fewer genetic variations in all six ARDS of M. genitalium, with only one case of M95I (3.4%) and four cases of A2059G (10.5%) identified (Table 1).

Females (G5) and males (G3) who visited PCCs in 2018–2019 exhibited slightly higher genetic variations, but generally similar resistance patterns compared to FSWs in 2004 (G4). In contrast, males who visited the referral center (G1) from 2014 to 2019 displayed significant genetic variations in GyrA99 (34.9%), ParC83 (30.2%), and at position 2059 in 23S rRNA gene (44.2%). Males who visited the same referral center from 2020 to 2023 (G2) showed even more pronounced genetic variations, particularly in ParC83 (83.3%) and at position 2059 (70.4%) in the 23S rRNA gene (Table 1).

We observed marked genetic variations in males who visited the referral center before and after 2020. The wild-type in ParC83 was present in 30 cases (69.8%) in 2014–2019 (G1) and 9 cases (16.7%) in 2020–2023 (G2) (p=0.015, OR 11.538, 95% CI 4.386–30.357, univariate analysis). Additionally, the A2059G mutation in the 23S rRNA was found in 19 cases (44.2%) in G1 and 38 cases (70.4%) in G2 (p=0.013, OR 3.000, 95% CI 1.297–6.940). However, no significant differences were observed for GyrA95, GyrA99, or at position 2058 in the 23S rRNA gene between G1 and G2 (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

AMR develops when bacteria evolve mechanisms to evade bactericidal effect [14,15]. Resistance is often driven by the overuse or misuse of antimicrobials [15]. Additionally, antimicrobial prescription patterns in clinical practice may be influenced by various socio-medical factors [16].

Several socio-medical factors may have contributed to the rise of AMR in M. genitalium in Korea over the past two decades [11,12,17]. In the present study, we used samples from 2004 (G4) as the baseline. Socially, prostitution was not explicitly illegal in Korea at the time. Until the late 2004, FSWs were required to undergo regular medical examinations for STDs. However, in the spring of 2004, a strict anti-sex trafficking law was enacted, making both buying and selling sex illegal. By autumn of 2004, CSWs were no longer officially recognized [17]. Clinically, M. genitalium was not widely acknowledged as a pathogen, and individual infections were difficult to diagnose due to the lack of PCR-based detection methods. Furthermore, moxifloxacin had not yet been approved for treating various infections, while azithromycin was not commonly prescribed for M. genitalium. At that time, the first-line therapy for non-gonococcal urethritis or cervicitis was tetracycline derivatives, with treatment failures managed using second-generation fluoroquinolones or erythromycin.

Since FSWs engage in consensual sex for money, they are theoretically at high risk of contracting various STDs, including those with AMR. In Korea, 20% of FSWs have been found to have chlamydial infections [18]. In this study, 7.84% were also infected with M. genitalium. This suggests that FSWs may serve as a core group in the spread of STDs in the country.

Although the quality of DNA sequences from the stored specimens was compromised due to over 20 years of deep-freeze storage, we still found that genetic variations associated with AMR in M. genitalium were rare in 2004. The A2059G mutation in the 23S rRNA gene and the M95I mutation in GryA95 were detected in 10.5% and 3.4% of cases, respectively. No variants were found in the parC gene or at position 2058 of the 23S rRNA gene. The use of erythromycin may have contributed to the A2059G mutation, while certain fluoroquinolone prescription patterns may be linked to the mutation in gyrA gene [4].

AMR is not uniformly distributed, and certain groups are at high risk of acquiring AMR M. genitalium. Studies have reported higher AMR rates in specialized STD centers compared to PCCs [6,11,19]. To investigate, we compared the AMR profiles of two groups; males who visited a PCC from 2018 to 2019 (G3) and males who were referred to Dankook University Hospital from 2014 to 2019 (G1) (Supplementary Table 1). Mutations at the positions 95 and 99 of the GyrA gene and at position 2059 of the 23S rRNA gene were more frequently observed in referred males than those who attended PCCs. However, no significant differences were found in the mutations at positions 83 and 87 of the ParC gene.

Before 2020, moxifloxacin could not be openly prescribed as it was not officially approved by the KNMI for M. genitalium treatment. As a result, only selected cases could receive it on a self-payment basis. Additionally, levofloxacin or ciprofloxacin were frequently prescribed to patients with urethritis or prostatitis patients [20]. All referred males in the present study had prior exposure to various fluoroquinolones and azithromycin at PCCs. During this period, urologists were more likely to prescribe azithromycin as a first-line therapy for M. genitalium infections compared to gynecologists or general physicians [11].

We observed significant genetic differences in males referred after 2020 (G2) compared to those who were referred before 2020 (G1). The wild-type ParC83 site was present in 30 cases (69.8%) from 2014 to 2019 (G1) but dropped to 9 cases (16.7%) from 2020 to 2023 (G2) (p=0.015). Furthermore, the A2059G mutation in the 23S rRNA gene increased from 19 cases (44.2%) in G1 to 38 cases (70.4%) in G2 (p=0.013, OR 3.000, 95% CI 1.297–6.940). However, no significant differences were observed in other ARDS between G1 and G2 (Table 2).

DNA gyrase is unique among DNA topoisomerases because of its ability to introduce negative supercoils into DNA molecules, while topoisomerase IV, encoded by the parC gene, is responsible for chromosome catenation and decatenation during DNA replication and transcription [14,21]. Sequence variations at position 83 of ParC gene have been strongly linked to moxifloxacin failure, whereas mutations in the gyrA gene are considered minor determinants of such failure [2,4,22]. The rapid increase in the ParC83 variants in G2 may be associated with the use or overuse of moxifloxacin af ter 2020 (OR 11.538, 95% CI 4.386–30.357).

We compared sequence variations between two sexes of individuals who visited PCCs from 2018 to 2019 and found no significant differences between the two groups (Table 3).

To combat AMR in M. genitalium, several strategies are essential. These include the appropriate use of antimicrobials, implementation of molecular resistance testing, adherence to national therapeutic guideline, and development of new drug therapies [3,8,9].

The present study had several limitations. DNA samples in G4 had been stored for over 20 years before the genetic diversity analysis of ARDSs. This led to partial degradation and reduced PCR yields that affected the detection of genetic variations in G3. Although we employed semi-nested PCR to enhance their fidelity, the high amplification failure rate may have affected the statistical power of the present study. Additionally, our clinic is a unique center in Korea that conducts molecular studies on AMR in M. genitalium. Treatment failure cases, which had been inappropriately prescribed multiple courses of antimicrobials over at least six months in PCCs across the country, were referred to our clinic. Therefore, the high prevalence of ParC83 and A2059G variants observed in G2 may be overrepresented and may not accurately reflect the general AMR landscape of M. genitalium in Korea. Finally, we were unable to obtain complete antimicrobial prescription histories of participants from PCCs due to limited access to personal medical information.

CONCLUSIONS

Genetic variations in ARDS of M. genitalium were rare in 2004, but new variants have emerged and increased in prevalence over the past 20 years. Referred males exhibited higher AMR patterns than those from PCCs. Notably, we observed a rapid increase in ParC83 mutations and A2059G mutations in the 23S rRNA gene, which are associated with moxifloxacin resistance and azithromycin resistance, respectively, among the referred men. The combination of A2059G and S83I/R/N mutations in G2 raises concerns about the recent emergence of pan-drug-resistant M. genitalium in Korea, suggesting that we may have already passed a critical point of no return.
