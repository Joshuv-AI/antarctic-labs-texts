Taurine reduces intracellular glucose accumulation in fructose-treated Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Joelma Capungo Machado, Bruno Ayala Vidal, Bruna Haendchen Sant’Ana, Gabriel Pedroso Viçozzi, Flávia Suelen de Oliveira Pereira, Ana Olivia Laurentino, Jaqueline Nascimento Picada, Mirna Bainy Leal, Solange Cristina Garcia, Rosane Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regulation of glucose homeostasis is a central feature of antidiabetic drugs such as metformin. However, a subset of individuals exhibited limited therapeutic response, highlighting the need for complementary treatment strategies. Taurine, a semi-essential amino acid, has been reported to improve glycemic control in mammalian models, supporting its potential as an adjuvant therapy for metabolic disorders. In this study, we established a fructose-induced metabolic dysfunction model in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), characterized by increased intracellular glucose accumulation, and evaluated the effects of taurine. A metabolic dysfunction state was induced in L1-stage N2 Bristol worms by exposing them to 500 mM fructose for 48 h. Metformin (25, 50, and 100 µM) was initially used to establish an effective pharmacological control, and the optimal dose (50 µM) was subsequently employed as a positive control to assess the effects of taurine (10, 50, and 100 µM) under fructose-induced conditions. Fructose exposure significantly increased intracellular glucose levels. Metformin reduced intracellular glucose at all concentrations tested and improved survival at the lowest dose, without affecting morphological parameters. Taurine reduced intracellular glucose accumulation only at the highest concentration tested (100 µM), whereas lower concentrations selectively increased survival, indicating dose-dependent and functionally distinct effects on glucose regulation and organismal viability. These findings show that taurine modulates intracellular glucose accumulation and survival in fructose-treated C. elegans. Given the evolutionary conservation of key pathways involved in glucose metabolism, this model provides a simple and reproducible platform to investigate mechanisms of glucose dysregulation and to screen compounds with potential metabolic or antidiabetic activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-026-03529-x.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global epidemic and a major challenge for healthcare systems. It is characterized by peripheral insulin resistance associated with a relative deficiency in insulin secretion, resulting in impaired glucose uptake, storage, and utilization in adipose, hepatic, and skeletal muscle tissues (Abel et al. 2024). Chronic hyperglycemia causes widespread vascular damage, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality (Li et al. 2023).

Metformin remains the first-line pharmacological therapy for T2DM due to its clinical efficacy and favorable safety profile (Bailey 2024). Its therapeutic effects are largely attributed to improved insulin sensitivity, although its precise mechanisms of action remain incompletely elucidated (Bailey 2024). Proposed mechanisms include inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, with subsequent activation of intracellular adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), modulation of cellular redox balance, and changes in gut microbiota composition, all of which contribute to metabolic regulation (Agius et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024). In both clinical and experimental settings, metformin effectively lowers glycemic markers and improves metabolic outcomes (Cabreiro et al. 2013; Foretz et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2022; Berk et al. 2024). Despite its widespread use, a subset of individuals shows limited response or intolerance to metformin, highlighting the need for complementary therapeutic strategies.

In this context, taurine, a semi-essential amino acid with osmoregulatory, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, has been shown to improve glycemic control and insulin regulation in rodent models (Carneiro et al. 2009; Ribeiro et al. 2012). Studies from our group and others further support its role in modulating metabolic and inflammatory parameters in rodents and humans (Caletti et al. 2012, 2018, 2023; Maleki et al. 2020). Taurine supplementation has been associated with increased longevity in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), an effect similarly observed with metformin (Cabreiro et al. 2013; Singh et al. 2023; Berk et al. 2024). However, no study has been performed to evaluate its potential role in modulating glucose metabolism in this organism.

C. elegans is a free-living nematode widely used as an experimental model for metabolic research due to its short life cycle, genetic tractability, and high degree of conservation of insulin and energy-regulating pathways (Shen et al. 2018; Alcántar-Fernández et al. 2018). Key components of insulin signaling, including homologous receptors (INSR/DAF-2) and transcription factors (FOXO/DAF-16), are conserved between worms and mammals, supporting its relevance for diabetes-related studies and compound screening (Morcos and Hutter 2009; Pereira et al. 2024). Exposure to high-sugar diets, including fructose, has been shown to induce metabolic stress in C. elegans, leading to glucose dysregulation, metabolic imbalance, and reduced lifespan (Hannou et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2020). Such effects have been linked to perturbations in conserved insulin/IGF-1–like signaling pathways (Murphy and Hu 2013; Pereira et al. 2024). In this study, we evaluated the effects of taurine on intracellular glucose levels in a fructose-induced metabolic dysfunction model in C. elegans, using metformin as a pharmacological reference, with intracellular glucose accumulation used as the primary metabolic endpoint.

Materials and methods

Animals

Wild-type C. elegans N2 Bristol strains (CGC, University of Minnesota, USA) were cultured on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates (100 mm), seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 and maintained in an incubator at 20 °C. For larval stage synchronization, a lysis solution containing NaOCl (1%) and NaOH (0.25 M) was used to disrupt the worm cuticle and release the eggs (Brenner 1974; Stiernagle 2006). The eggs were then washed with M9 buffer solution (KH₂PO₄ 22 mM; Na₂HPO₄ 42 mM; NaCl 86 mM; MgSO₄ 1 mM). Approximately 12–14 h after synchronization, the eggs hatched, resulting in a population at the first larval stage (L1). All procedures were conducted in duplicate across three independent experiments.

Solutions

Fructose (10, 80, and 500 mM), metformin (25, 50, and 100 µM), and taurine (10, 50, and 100 µM) solutions were prepared in distilled water. Concentrations were selected based on previously published studies using fructose to induce metabolic alterations in C. elegans (Zheng et al. 2016; Pereira et al. 2024), as well as metformin (Cabreiro et al. 2013; Mejia-Martinez et al. 2017; Berk et al. 2024) and taurine (Singh et al. 2023). All compounds were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Standardization of a fructose-induced glucose accumulation model in C. elegans

To induce intracellular glucose accumulation, synchronized L1-stage worms (3,000 per group) were exposed for 48 h to fructose (10, 80, or 500 mM) on NGM plates containing heat-inactivated E. coli OP50 (70 °C for 1 h) to prevent bacterial fructose metabolism (Alcántar-Fernández et al. 2018; Pereira et al. 2024). Plates without fructose served as negative controls. Intracellular glucose levels were assessed in the L4-stage worms.

Metformin treatment and dose selection

After identifying the fructose concentration that significantly increased intracellular glucose levels, synchronized L1-stage worms (3,000 per group) were exposed to metformin (25, 50, and 100 µM) for 48 h on NGM plates (Cabreiro et al. 2013). Plates without fructose served as negative controls, whereas plates containing 500 mM fructose served as positive controls. Glucose levels were measured at the L4 stage, as described below. Based on the dose–response analysis, the 50 µM concentration was selected for subsequent experiments because it reduced elevated glucose levels without lowering glucose levels below those observed in control group.

Taurine treatment protocol in C. elegans

After identifying the optimal metformin dose (50 µM) for reversing elevated glucose levels in worms (3,000 per group) were exposed for 48 h to different taurine concentrations (10, 50, and 100 µM) (Singh et al. 2023) on NGM plates. Plates without fructose served as negative controls, and plates containing 500 mM fructose served as positive controls for fructose-induced metabolic alterations. Metformin at 50 µM was used as a positive control for the metabolic effect. Upon reaching the L4 stage, as confirmed by stereomicroscopic observation, glucose levels were measured.

Survival assay

For each experiment, worms were maintained on solid NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50 containing fructose and the indicated treatments (metformin or taurine) without washing or transfer. Survival was scored at the end of treatment (48 h) by manual counting under a stereomicroscope. Worms were considered dead when they failed to respond to a gentle touch with a platinum wire. Survival was expressed as the percentage of live worms relative to the Control group (plates without fructose). This survival assessment was performed before glucose measurements to ensure that the treatments did not alter C elegans population viability. All experiments were independently repeated three times, and each plate was considered an experimental unit.

Body length and area measurements

To evaluate the effects of metformin and taurine on worm growth, animals were treated with the concentrations described above. At the L4 stage (young adult), worms were collected from the plates and washed to remove excess bacteria. Worms were then mounted on microscope slides and cooled at − 20 °C to induce immobilization. Images of five worms per slide in triplicate were captured using a microscope-mounted camera (total = 45 images) (Kontrol/F500, Fortel Indústria e Comércio Ltda, São Paulo, Brazil). Body length and body area were quantified using ImageJ software (Schroeder et al. 2021). Worms were randomly selected and identified by number for blinded assessment by two independent investigators. An inter-rater agreement of ≥ 98% was required; values below this threshold were re-evaluated by consensus before averaging. Data were normalized to the control group (without fructose) and expressed as a percentage of control values.

Determination of glucose levels

After 48 h of treatment, the L4- stage worms were collected from plates with distilled water and exposed to lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.1 mM EDTA), followed by three freeze–thaw cycles to disrupt the cuticle. Samples were homogenized using a probe sonicator (3 cycles of 10 s each at 100% amplitude) while kept on ice. The lysates were then centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was used to determine glucose levels via an enzymatic glucose assay kit (Bioclin, São Paulo, Brazil). Glucose levels were normalized to total protein content determined by the Bradford method (Bradford 1976), using bovine serum albumin as the standard. For intracellular glucose quantification, approximately 120 worms per condition were analyzed per experiment, with measurements performed in duplicate across three independent experiments. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

Data were compiled in spreadsheets and assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to identify treatment effects, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons when appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8, with significance set at p < 0.05. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Fructose exposure increased intracellular glucose levels in C. elegans

Exposure to 500 mM fructose significantly increased intracellular glucose levels in C. elegans, indicating a fructose-induced metabolic alteration (F(3, 8) = 31.33; P < 0.001). Worms exposed to fructose showed a 38% increase in glucose levels compared with the Control group (P = 0.0265) (Fig. 1). In contrast, exposure to the lowest fructose concentration resulted in a significant reduction in intracellular glucose levels relative to Control and 500 mM fructose, whereas intermediate concentrations did not produce significant changes.

Metformin reduces intracellular glucose levels and increases survival in fructose-exposed C. elegans

All tested metformin concentrations significantly reduced intracellular glucose levels in fructose-exposed worms (F(4, 10) = 17.55; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Among them, the 25 µM concentration produced the largest reduction (P = 0.0002), reducing glucose levels to values below those observed in the Control group (P = 0.0319). Regarding survival (Fig. 2B), only the 25 µM metformin concentration significantly increased survival compared with the Fructose group (P = 0.0390). In contrast, fructose exposure reduced survival relative to the Control worms (P = 0.029). No significant differences in survival were observed at higher metformin concentrations when compared with either the Control or Fructose groups. Taurine reduces glucose levels and increases survival in fructose-exposed C. elegans

Following the experimental pipeline, the 50 µM metformin concentration was selected for subsequent experiments based on its ability to reduce fructose-induced glucose elevation without lowering glucose levels below those observed in the Control group. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference among the experimental groups (F(5, 9) = 18.06, P = 0.0002). Post hoc analysis showed that taurine treatment significantly reduced intracellular glucose levels only at the highest concentration tested (100 µM) when compared with the fructose group (P = 0.0218; Fig. 3A). Lower taurine concentrations did not significantly affect glucose levels. In contrast, only the lowest taurine concentration (10 µM) increased worm survival compared with the fructose-exposed group (P = 0.0385; Fig. 3B). No significant changes in survival were observed at higher taurine concentrations.

Discussion

In this study, fructose exposure induced a metabolic phenotype in C. elegans, characterized by increased intracellular glucose accumulation. Pharmacological validation was provided by the glucose-lowering effect of metformin, which was used as a positive control and supporting the use of this model for investigating mechanisms of glucose dysregulation. Furthermore, taurine reduced intracellular glucose levels at the highest concentration tested, indicating a modulatory effect on glucose homeostasis under fructose-induced metabolic conditions.

Although glucose has been used to induce metabolic alterations in C. elegans, particularly in studies of aging and stress responses (Schlotterer et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2009), fructose remains comparatively unexplored as a dietary factor capable of inducing metabolic alterations relevant to metabolic dysfunction. This is noteworthy given the strong association between excessive fructose intake and insulin resistance in humans, mediated in part by redox imbalance and insulin-independent mechanisms (Tappy and Lê 2010; Softic et al. 2020). In C. elegans, fructose exposure has been shown to reduce lifespan, increase reactive oxygen species production, and impair stress resistance (Zheng et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2020; Franco-Juárez et al. 2021; Pereira et al. 2024). Mechanistically, elevated fructose levels are associated with increased glycolytic flux, accumulation of toxic intermediates and reactive oxygen species, dysregulation of insulin-like signaling, suppression of DAF-16/FOXO and SKN-1/Nrf2 activity, and trigger maladaptive autophagy responses, ultimately leading to decreased organismal viability (Franco-Juárez et al. 2021). In the present study, fructose exposure was therefore used as a metabolic stress model to induce glucose dysregulation, based on previous reports demonstrating that high-sugar diets in C. elegans lead to metabolic imbalance, altered insulin/IGF-1–like signaling, and reduced lifespan. By demonstrating fructose-induced intracellular glucose accumulation, our findings extend these observations and support the use of fructose as a useful experimental approach to induce metabolic dysfunction associated with glucose dysregulation in C. elegans. Notably, the modest reduction in intracellular glucose levels observed at the lowest fructose concentration likely reflects an early adaptive metabolic response, in which mild nutrient exposure transiently enhances substrate utilization before metabolic dysregulation becomes evident at higher fructose levels.

In C. elegans, glucose uptake is primarily mediated by the intestinal FGT-1 (Facilitated Glucose Transporter-1) transporter, which is functionally analogous to the mammalian GLUT transporters (Zhou et al. 2019; Pereira et al. 2024). This FGT-1 also facilitates fructose uptake, albeit with lower efficiency (Kitaoka et al. 2013). The activity of FGT-1 is regulated by the insulin/IGF-1 signaling (IIS) pathway, via the DAF-2 receptor, an orthologue of the mammalian insulin/IGF-1 receptor, underscoring the evolutionary conservation of key mechanisms involved in energy metabolism and glucose regulation (Altintas et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2019; Pereira et al. 2024). These conserved features reinforce the relevance of C. elegans as an experimental model for studying glucose metabolism and related metabolic processes.

Consistent with its known metabolic actions, metformin reduced elevated intracellular glucose levels at all concentrations tested. In mammals, metformin improves glycemic control primarily by suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancing insulin sensitivity, effects that are largely attributed to inhibition of mitochondrial complex I and activation of AMPK (Foretz et al. 2014; Agius et al. 2020; Bailey 2024). AMPK signaling is conserved in C. elegans, where it is encoded by the aak-2 gene and acts as a central sensor of cellular energy status (Onken and Driscoll 2010). The increased survival observed at the lowest metformin concentration is consistent with previous reports linking AAK-2 activation to improved metabolic regulation and longevity (Onken and Driscoll 2010; Cabreiro et al. 2013). The matrix-based pairwise analysis (Table S1) further clarifies the dose-dependent effects of metformin. While all concentrations reduced fructose-induced glucose accumulation, the lowest dose lowered glucose levels below those of control animals, whereas 50 µM normalized glucose levels without overcorrection. This profile supports the selection of 50 µM metformin as a reference condition for comparative analyses, minimizing confounding effects while preserving metabolic efficacy.

In the present study, taurine significantly reduced intracellular glucose levels in C. elegans at 100 µM, providing the first evidence of a possible glucose-lowering effect of taurine in a C. elegans fructose-induced glucose accumulation model. This finding aligns with mammalian studies showing improved glycemic control in diabetic (Nandhini et al. 2004; Gomez et al. 2018) and high-fructose-fed rodents, as well as enhanced insulin secretion in pancreatic β cells (Carneiro et al. 2009). Together, these observations suggest that taurine exerts conserved metabolic effects across species. Future studies will be required to elucidate the molecular pathways underlying these effects in C. elegans. Based on the known roles of insulin/IGF-like signaling in glucose homeostasis, it is plausible that taurine may modulate components of the IIS pathway, including DAF-2, AGE-1, or AAK-2 (Ogg et al. 1997; Morcos and Hutter 2009).

In addition to its glucose-lowering effect, taurine improved survival in fructose-exposed C. elegans, suggesting a protective role under conditions of metabolic overload. Taurine availability has been linked to lifespan regulation in C. elegans, with supplementation improving survival and stress resistance across different experimental contexts (Kim et al. 2010; Singh et al. 2023). Mechanistically, taurine-mediated protection has been associated with attenuation of endoplasmic reticulum stress and enhanced cellular resilience (Kim et al. 2010). In C. elegans, metabolic stress responses are tightly regulated by IIS and its downstream effector DAF-16/FOXO, whose activity is modulated by nutrient availability and AMPK/AAK-2 signaling (Altintas et al. 2016; Alcántar-Fernández et al. 2018). Although the precise molecular pathways underlying taurine’s effects remain to be elucidated, modulation of these conserved signaling networks provides a plausible mechanistic framework for the observed improvements in glucose homeostasis and survival rates.

Neither taurine nor metformin affected body length or body area in C. elegans, indicating that their effects were restricted to metabolic regulation rather than developmental or morphological processes. Similar findings have been reported in fructose-exposed worms, in which glucose dysregulation occurred independently of changes in body size (Wang et al. 2020). This specificity supports the use of this model for studying metabolic interventions without confounding effects on growth.

Caenorhabditis elegans was chosen as the experimental model due to its well-established use in metabolic research and the evolutionary conservation of key insulin/IGF-1–like signaling pathways. Compared with cell-based systems, it enables the assessment of integrated whole-organism responses, while offering greater experimental tractability and throughput than mammalian models. This balance between physiological relevance and experimental simplicity makes C. elegans particularly suitable for comparative screening of candidate metabolic or glucose-lowering compounds.

Although the fructose-induced C. elegans model represents a valuable platform for investigating glucose dysregulation and enables dose-response evaluation of taurine, it has some limitations. First, this model does not fully capture the systemic, hormonal, and tissue-specific complexity of metabolic disorders observed in mammals and should be extrapolated to humans with caution. Further studies are needed to validate the pathways affected by taurine. Second, although intracellular glucose levels, survival, and general phenotypic parameters were assessed, molecular signaling pathways and downstream mechanisms were not directly investigated. Consequently, the precise pathways through which taurine modulates glucose metabolism remain to be elucidated. An additional limitation relates to the use of heat-inactivated E. coli as a food source, which is known to affect physiological parameters in C. elegans. This approach was used to prevent bacterial metabolism of fructose, and all experimental groups were maintained under identical feeding conditions to ensure consistent comparisons. Finally, the present study did not include experimental groups treated with metformin or taurine in the absence of fructose, which would provide an additional baseline to further characterize compound-specific effects under non–metabolic stress conditions. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted within a translational framework, serving as complementary evidence to mammalian models and future clinical investigations.

In summary, fructose exposure induces a metabolic state characterized by intracellular glucose accumulation and responsiveness to metformin in C. elegans. Within this model, taurine reduced intracellular glucose levels and improved survival, suggesting a role as a metabolic modulator. Together, these findings establish a simple, reproducible, and cost-effective platform for investigating conserved mechanisms of glucose dysregulation and for screening compounds with metabolic or glucose-lowering activity.
