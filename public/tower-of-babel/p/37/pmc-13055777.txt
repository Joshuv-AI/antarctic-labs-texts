Impact of Perivascular Adipose Tissue Attenuation Around the Aortic Valve on Clinical Outcomes After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Hiroya Okamoto, Takayoshi Toba, Yoichiro Sugizaki, Hiroyuki Kawamori, Takashi Hiromasa, Daichi Fujimoto, Yu Izawa, Yuto Osumi, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Seigo Iwane, Shota Naniwa, Chiaki Yoshida, Yuki Sakamoto, Koshi Matsuhama, Yuta Fukuishi, Hiroshi Tsunamoto, Kotaro Higuchi, Nobuhiro Watanabe, Ken Takata, Keisuke Iida, Tatsuya Kitagawa, Mayuka Masuda, Shotaro Yoshida, Hiromi Hashimura, Takamichi Murakami, Hiromasa Otake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) attenuation interacts with local and systemic vascular inflammation and consequently contributes to unfavorable clinical outcomes in patients with various cardiovascular diseases.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients with severe aortic stenosis to investigate the association between PVAT attenuation measured before transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) and subsequent clinical outcomes. PVAT attenuation was measured using computed tomography angiography images around the aortic valve. The primary outcome was defined as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), a composite of all‐cause death, stroke, and heart failure rehospitalization.ResultsMACE was observed in 23.6% of 233 patients during a median follow‐up of 2.2 years. The MACE group had significantly higher PVAT attenuation than the non‐MACE group (−74.0±9.8 Hounsfield unit [HU] versus −78.9±9.2 HU, P=0.001). In the Cox regression model, higher PVAT attenuation was independently associated with MACE (hazard ratio: 1.52 [95% CI: 1.13–2.04], P=0.006). Incorporating PVAT attenuation into established prognostic factors following TAVR improved the predictive and reclassification performance of MACE risk.ConclusionsElevated PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve was associated with MACE in patients undergoing TAVR. Measuring PVAT attenuation before TAVR can help identify patients at higher risk of developing heart failure or cardiovascular death after TAVR, thereby aiding in treatment strategy decisions.RegistrationURL: https://www.umin.ac.jp/ctr/; Unique Identifier: UMIN000057107.Keywords: aortic stenosis, computed tomography angiography, perivascular adipose tissue, transcatheter aortic valve replacementSubject Categories: Computerized Tomography (CT), Aortic Valve Replacement/Transcather Aortic Valve Implantation, Heart Failure

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Incorporating perivascular adipose tissue attenuation into established transcatheter aortic valve replacement risk models may improve risk stratification and help identify patients who could benefit from closer follow‐up and earlier optimization of cardioprotective therapies to reduce heart failure rehospitalization.

Over the past 2 decades, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been shown to reduce mortality compared with conservative medical treatment in patients with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis (AS), establishing it as a viable alternative to surgical aortic valve replacement. 1 , 2 , 3 Recent large‐scale clinical trials in low‐risk patients further demonstrated improved clinical outcomes with TAVR compared with surgical aortic valve replacement. 4 Despite these advances and the expanding indications for TAVR, some patients experience only modest symptomatic improvement or early mortality after TAVR. 5 , 6 Previous studies identified frailty, anemia, renal insufficiency, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) as predictors of unfavorable clinical outcomes following TAVR. 7 , 8 Accordingly, several clinical risk models incorporating these variables have been developed to predict prognosis after TAVR, including Optimized transCathEter vAlvular interventioN–Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation registry (OCEAN‐TAVI) risk score, which was developed and validated using data from a large multicenter TAVR registry and uses a point‐based system comprising sex, body mass index (BMI), clinical frailty score, atrial fibrillation, peripheral artery disease, prior cardiac surgery, serum albumin, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and pulmonary disease to estimate 1‐year mortality risk after TAVR. 9 In addition to these established factors, inflammation has emerged as a key determinant of prognosis. hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) hs‐CRP, a systemic inflammatory marker, has been associated with atherosclerotic disease progression and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 10 Notably, elevated baseline CRP levels have been linked to unfavorable post‐TAVR prognosis, implying a potential role of inflammation in postprocedural outcomes. 11 , 12

Recently, perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT), measured by computed tomography angiography (CTA), has emerged as a novel marker reflecting local inflammation. 13 Healthy PVAT regulates homeostatic vascular function; however, dysfunctional PVAT remodeling due to inflammation or oxidative stress shifts its phenotype from anti‐inflammatory to pro‐inflammatory. 14 , 15 Inflamed PVAT exhibits higher PVAT attenuation and Hounsfield unit (HU) in CTA than healthy PVAT. Recent studies have shown that PVAT attenuation in the abdominal aorta and cervical artery is associated with abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) and cervical artery stenosis, respectively. 16 , 17 In coronary artery disease, PVAT attenuation around the right coronary artery is associated with cardiovascular death and adverse outcomes following percutaneous coronary intervention, suggesting that it may reflect systemic inflammation. 18 , 19 Therefore, PVAT is increasingly recognized as an important prognostic marker in several cardiovascular diseases. However, few studies have investigated the association between post‐TAVR clinical outcomes and PVAT attenuation, 20 , 21 and its clinical significance has not been fully established. This study aimed to clarify the relationship between preprocedural PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve and subsequent post‐TAVR clinical outcomes.

METHODS

Study Design

This single‐center, retrospective, observational study enrolled consecutive patients who underwent TAVR for severe AS between January 2015 and December 2022 at Kobe University Hospital. The exclusion criteria included patients (1) with insufficient computed tomography (CT) data quality, (2) who underwent enhanced CT for TAVR planning >180 days before TAVR, (3) who underwent surgical aortic valve replacement for aortic valve disease before TAVR, (4) with <1 month of post‐TAVR follow‐up, and (5) who underwent CT at tube voltage other than 70 kVp. Insufficient CT quality was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: (1) severe motion artifacts that precluded delineation of the aortic annulus and surrounding adipose tissue, (2) poor contrast enhancement with inadequate scan coverage, or (3) metallic artifacts preventing accurate placement of the region of interest. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the Institutional Review Board of Kobe University Hospital approved the protocol. Informed consent was obtained in an opt‐out form on the website of the Division of Cardiovascular Medicine at Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine. The study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trial Registry (UMIN000057107). The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Echocardiographic Measurement

Commercially available echocardiography systems were used in this study. All parameters were measured by experienced cardiologists before the TAVR procedure, at discharge, and during follow‐up after discharge. LVEF was measured using the modified Simpson's method. The left ventricular mass index (LVMi) was calculated using the cube formula. The peak aortic‐jet velocity was measured using the continuous‐wave Doppler mode. The mean aortic valve pressure gradient was calculated by tracing the velocity‐time integral of the transaortic valvular flow, measured using continuous‐wave Doppler mode. The aortic valvular orifice area was calculated using the continuity equation and subsequently indexed to the body surface area. Hemodynamic valve deterioration was defined as an increase in mean transvalvular gradient ≥10 mmHg, resulting in a mean gradient ≥20 mmHg with concomitant decrease in effective orifice area ≥0.3 cm2 or ≥25% and/or decrease in Doppler velocity index ≥0.1 or ≥20% compared with echocardiographic assessment performed 1 to 3 months post‐procedure, or a new occurrence or increase of ≥1 grade of intra‐prosthetic aortic regurgitation resulting in greater than moderate aortic regurgitation ≥90 days post‐TAVR. 22 The severity of prosthesis–patient mismatch was graded using the aortic valve area indexed to body surface area, with the absence of mismatch defined as >0.85 cm2/m2, moderate mismatch >0.65 and ≤0.85 cm2/m2, and severe mismatch ≤0.65 cm2/m2. In patients with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2), lower cut‐off values of the indexed valve area were used to define moderate (>0.55 and ≤0.70 cm2/m2) and severe (≤0.55 cm2/m2) prosthesis–patient mismatch. Aortic regurgitation was assessed before and after the procedure using a multiparameter integrative approach; graded as none/trace, mild, moderate, or severe; and classified as transvalvular or paravalvular leakage. Pulmonary hypertension was defined as an estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure >40 mmHg on transthoracic echocardiography, calculated using the tricuspid regurgitation velocity and estimated right atrial pressure. Valve thrombosis was defined as clinically suspected bioprosthetic valve thrombosis following TAVR, confirmed by CT demonstrating thrombus attached to the bioprosthetic valve. 22 Echocardiographic parameters were obtained by multiple experienced cardiologists in accordance with our predefined institutional standard protocol. All interpreters were blinded to clinical outcomes to reduce potential bias.

CT Image Acquisition

All image acquisitions were performed using a commercially available third‐generation dual‐source CT scanner (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Image acquisition is described in the Supplementary File. Briefly, aortic CTA was performed using a non‐gated Turbo Flash Spiral technique at 70 kV, with a pitch of 3.0 and a reference of 950 mAs. Images were reconstructed with a 1‐mm slice thickness and a 1‐mm interval using a vascular smooth kernel (Bv 36). The contrast injection protocol began with a 10‐s bolus of Omnipaque 240 at 14.3 mgI/kg/s, followed by a 10‐s injection of a 50% dilution of the same contrast agent at the same rate, and concluded with a 20 mL saline flush administered at the same rate. 23

CT Image Analysis

CT images were analyzed on a commercially available workstation (Ziostation2 version 2.4.2.3, Ziosoft Inc, Tokyo, Japan). The CT data were analyzed by 2 experts with at least 2 years of experience in CT analysis, both blinded to the patients' clinical information. Scans with severe artifacts or missing slices were excluded. Vessel contours were automatically detected and manually corrected when necessary. The aortic annulus was defined as the luminal contour within a virtual plane aligned with the basal hinge points of the 3 aortic valve cusps. The aortic annular perimeter and area were measured during systole, the phase at which the annulus reaches its maximal dimension. For accuracy, the phase with the largest annular dimensions was selected from the electrocardiography‐synchronized, multiphasic data set. Measurements were obtained by manually tracing the annular contours. The aortic valve calcium score was calculated using the Agatston method and expressed in Agatston units. 23

Measurement of PVAT Attenuation

PVAT attenuation was measured from aortic CTA images using a dedicated CTA analysis software (SYNAPSE VINCENT, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). First, spatial planes were reoriented along the long and short axes of the aortic root, ensuring the aortic annulus plane was perpendicular to the ascending aorta by manual adjustment. Second, we traced the aortic root from the annulus to the sinotubular junction, and defined a volume sample as 3‐dimensional layers within a radial distance of 5 mm from the outer aortic wall border and a 10 mm height from the aortic annulus (Figure 1). 24 , 25 Finally, PVAT attenuation within this volume was automatically calculated by the software based on the attenuation histogram within the range of −190 HU to −30 HU. PVAT attenuation was defined as the mean attenuation within such contamination‐free volumes of interest.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of all‐cause death, stroke, and heart failure rehospitalization, based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3. 22 Other clinical outcomes included hemodynamic valve deterioration, severe prosthesis–patient mismatch, moderate‐to‐severe paravalvular leakage, valve thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and any vessel treatment during the clinical follow‐up duration. Myocardial infarction was defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium criteria 3. 22 Any vessel treatment was defined as any arterial intervention, such as percutaneous coronary intervention, endovascular therapy, carotid artery stenting, carotid endarterectomy, endovascular aortic repair, thoracic endovascular aortic repair, abdominal aortic aneurysm repair, thoracic aortic aneurysm repair, renal intervention, transcatheter aortic valve‐in‐transcatheter aortic valve, or redo‐surgical aortic valve replacement during follow‐up. Post‐TAVR clinical events were confirmed by investigators through review of electronic medical records and/or telephone contact with patients, their families, or physicians.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, presented as means ± SDs or median (first quartile [Q1]–third quartile [Q3]), and compared using the Student t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test based on their distributions. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and compared using the Chi‐square or Fisher exact test. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Variables with right‐skewed distributions were log‐transformed before analysis. Potential nonlinearity in the exploratory association between PVAT attenuation and variables was evaluated using natural spline functions.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs and p values were calculated using the Cox proportional hazard model to evaluate associations between clinical, CTA, echocardiographic, and PVAT attenuation parameters and clinical outcomes. Covariates were selected based on clinical and pathophysiological relevance and prior evidence. 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Linearity of continuous variables with respect to the log hazard in the Cox proportional hazards model was assessed using restricted cubic spline functions, and no violation of model assumptions was detected. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the optimal cutoff values (Youden index) of PVAT attenuation for predicting MACE after TAVR. Internal validation was performed using nonparametric bootstrapping (300 resamples) with re‐estimation of the optimal cutoff in each bootstrap sample. The stability of the data‐derived cutoff and the robustness of the associated hazard ratio across bootstrap resamples were evaluated to assess potential overfitting.

When stratified by the cut‐off of PVAT attenuation, the cumulative event rates over 3 years post‐TAVR were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using a log‐rank test. Two prediction models for MACE were constructed to evaluate the incremental discriminatory and reclassification performance of PVAT attenuation. In the first analysis, Model 1 included established prognostic factors following TAVR (age, sex, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], creatinine, hemoglobin, hs‐CRP, LVEF, and non‐transfemoral [non‐TF] approach). 7 , 8 , 11 , 12 Model 2 comprised all Model 1 variables plus PVAT attenuation. In a separate analysis, we assessed the incremental prognostic value of PVAT attenuation beyond an established risk score. In this analysis, Model 1 consisted of the OCEAN‐TAVI risk score, which is a validated clinical risk model derived from a large Japanese multicenter TAVR registry and incorporates sex, BMI, Clinical Frailty Scale, atrial fibrillation, prior cardiac surgery, serum albumin, estimated glomerular filtration rate, peripheral artery disease, and pulmonary disease. 9 Model 2 included the OCEAN‐TAVI risk score with the additional inclusion of PVAT attenuation. The discriminatory ability was assessed using Harrell's concordance statistic (C‐index), and reclassification performance was compared using the relative integrated discrimination improvement and category‐free net reclassification index.

Inter‐ and intra‐observer reproducibility of PVAT attenuation measurements was evaluated using concordance correlation coefficient analysis in a randomly selected subset of 30 patients.

This study had minimal missing data, limited to a few baseline characteristics, such as medical history, aortic valve calcium score at pre‐procedural CTA, balloon pre‐ and post‐dilatation data, and medication use at follow‐up. As the primary endpoints and clinical outcomes were complete, imputation was not necessary, given that the missing data were unlikely to impact the results.

All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and significance was defined as P<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft R Open software version 4.2.2 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Comparison Between the MACE and Non‐MACE Groups

A total of 287 consecutive patients with severe AS who underwent TAVR at our institution between January 2015 and December 2022 were screened (Figure 2). Among them, 54 patients were excluded for the following reasons: insufficient CT image quality (24 patients), prior surgical aortic valve treatment for aortic valve disease (8 patients), did not undergo CTA within 180 days before TAVR (8 patients), 1 month follow‐up duration (8 patients), and underwent CTA at high CT tube voltage (80, 110, or 120 kVp; 6 patients). Ultimately, 233 patients with a median follow‐up of 2.2 years were analyzed (Q1–Q3: 1.6–3.9).

Among patients who underwent TAVR, 23.6% (55/233) had MACE during clinical follow‐up (MACE group), whereas 76.4% (178/233) did not (non‐MACE group). The MACE group had lower BMI and hemoglobin levels and higher hs‐CRP levels and OCEAN‐TAVI risk score than those without MACE (Table 1). No intergroup differences were observed in the pre‐procedural CT and echocardiographic, procedural, or postprocedural echocardiographic findings (Tables 2 and 3). However, the MACE group had higher PVAT attenuation than the non‐MACE group (−74.0±9.8 HU versus −78.9±9.2 HU, P=0.001) (Table 2). Inter‐ and intra‐observer agreements for measuring PVAT attenuation were 0.984 and 0.967, respectively.

Association Between PVAT Attenuation and Clinical Outcomes

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified a PVAT attenuation cut‐off of −70 HU for predicting MACE, with a sensitivity of 46%, specificity of 85%, and area under the curve (AUC) of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.57–0.75). Bootstrap internal validation demonstrated minimal optimism, indicating that the observed discriminative performance was not materially influenced by overfitting. In multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis, BMI (HR: 0.89 [95% CI: 0.81–0.97], P=0.01), hs‐CRP (HR: 1.20 [95% CI: 1.09–1.33], P<0.001), creatinine (HR: 2.01 [95% CI: 1.13–3.55], P=0.02), and PVAT attenuation (HR: 1.52 [95% CI: 1.13–2.04], P=0.006) were associated with MACE (Table 4). Furthermore, PVAT attenuation was associated with cardiovascular death (HR: 1.59 [95% CI: 1.01–2.52], P=0.04) (Table S1).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the prognostic impact of PVAT attenuation in patients who underwent TAVR for subsequent MACE. The main findings are as follows: (1) higher PVAT attenuation was significantly associated with a higher incidence of MACE, cardiovascular death, and heart failure rehospitalization during a median follow‐up of 2.2 years; (2) incorporating PVAT attenuation into established prognostic models improved the discriminatory and reclassification ability to predict MACE; and (3) PVAT attenuation was not associated with AS severity or hemodynamic valve deterioration occurrence.

Associations Between PVAT Attenuation and Post‐TAVR Clinical Outcomes

We found that higher PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve is independently associated with MACE in patients who underwent TAVR in the current study. To date, only 1 clinical study has examined the prognostic impact of PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve after TAVR. The study demonstrated that PVAT attenuation around either the right coronary artery or aortic valve offered no additional prognostic value beyond established risk factors in 372 patients who underwent TAVR, with a median follow‐up of 3.0 years, 21 contrasting with our findings. A possible explanation for this discrepancy is the difference in study endpoints. The previous study defined the primary outcome as a composite of cardiac death, non‐fatal stroke, non‐fatal myocardial infarction, and peripheral vascular intervention, whereas our study includes all‐cause death, stroke, and heart failure rehospitalization, excluding non‐fatal myocardial infarction and peripheral vascular intervention. Consistently, we observed no significant differences in the incidence of non‐fatal myocardial infarction and peripheral vascular intervention between the low and high PVAT attenuation groups. Notably, our study demonstrated that heart failure rehospitalization was a main driver of MACE, suggesting a strong association between high PVAT attenuation and worsening heart failure.

Several mechanisms may explain the relationship between inflammation, represented by PVAT, and heart failure. First, inflammation activates the renin‐angiotensin system and the sympathetic nervous system, promoting volume expansion, increased peripheral vascular resistance, and myocyte hypertrophy and apoptosis. In animal studies, dysfunctional PVAT, representing high PVAT attenuation, is associated with local renin‐angiotensin system overactivation, 26 , 27 contributes to vascular dysfunction via oxidative stress and impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, and promotes myocardial remodeling, including hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis. 28 Second, dysfunctional PVAT releases proinflammatory cytokines, which may impair myocardial contractility, influence left ventricular remodeling, and lead to pulmonary edema and endothelial dysfunction. An animal study reported that dysfunctional PVAT upregulates pro‐inflammatory molecules such as interleukin‐6, monocyte chemotactic protein 1, and tumor necrosis factor α, and downregulates adiponectin, an anti‐inflammatory adipokine, 29 highlighting PVAT as an important “signaling organ” involved in systemic metabolism, vascular function, and inflammation. These structural changes in the blood vessels and myocardium can cause irreversible cardiac dysfunction and cardiac hypertrophy even after AS is treated, ultimately leading to the development of heart failure.

Incremental Value of PVAT Attenuation for Predicting Post‐TAVR Clinical Outcomes

Studies have associated age, sex, COPD, creatinine, hemoglobin, LVEF, and a non‐TF approach with poor post‐TAVR clinical outcomes. 7 , 8 , 11 , 12 However, prognostic models incorporating these factors often have inadequate predictive power. 7 , 8 Hs‐CRP, a systemic inflammatory biomarker, is associated with atherosclerotic disease progression and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 10 Further, studies have associated elevated baseline CRP levels with unfavorable post‐TAVR prognosis. 11 , 12 In our cohort, hs‐CRP levels measured before TAVR were associated with MACE; however, traditional models incorporating hs‐CRP lack sufficient discriminative power for clinical application.

Furthermore, we found that incorporating PVAT attenuation analysis into the existing prognostic factors improved MACE prediction, highlighting its clinical relevance. Hs‐CRP is a dynamic biomarker that reflects systemic inflammatory activity at the time of measurement, influenced by various transient factors such as infections or acute inflammatory diseases, which may limit its specificity for chronic or localized inflammation. In contrast, PVAT reflects irreversibly altered inflammations attributable to vascular conditions, 14 offering a more stable and chronic inflammatory assessment. Moreover, because of its anatomical location, PVAT surrounding the aortic valve may affect adjacent structures, including the coronary arteries and myocardium, thereby more specifically reflecting the whole coronary‐ and organ‐level inflammation. Accordingly, measuring PVAT attenuation surrounding the aortic valve provides more effective risk stratification for patients at risk of post‐TAVR adverse outcomes. Consistently, PVAT attenuation was identified as an independent MACE predictor, and its incorporation into conventional TAVR risk models enhanced the discrimination of patients likely to experience subsequent MACE.

Associations Between PVAT and AS Severity or LV Function

In this study, PVAT attenuation was not significantly associated with AS severity, as determined by transthoracic echocardiogram and CT, consistent with a post‐hoc analysis of cardiac CT data from 2 randomized controlled trials by Botezatu et al., which demonstrated that PVAT attenuation at the level of the aortic valve is not associated with AS presence, severity, or progression. 25 Although inflammation contributes to AS progression, 30 , 31 PVAT may not accurately reflect aortic valve inflammation. Conversely, patients with higher pre‐TAVR PVAT attenuation exhibited lower LVEF and higher LVMi, indicating systolic and diastolic dysfunction that significantly correlated with PVAT. Sugiyama et al. highlighted the clinical characteristics of patients with PVAT attenuation who underwent sequential coronary CTA, noting that baseline high PVAT attenuation around the right coronary artery was independently associated with lower LVEF and higher LVMi. 32 These findings imply that PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve may reflect inflammation of the whole heart, potentially leading to left ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction, followed by heart failure and cardiovascular death, similar to previous observations associating PVAT attenuation around the right coronary artery with patient‐level outcomes. 18 , 19

Given that PVAT attenuation is irrelevant to AS severity, our findings suggest that PVAT attenuation around the aortic valve might reflect heart failure status. Recent studies have proposed that administering sodium‐dependent glucose transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors after TAVR may help reduce the risk of heart failure rehospitalization. 33 , 34 Although speculative, PVAT attenuation could guide the initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors, potentially contributing to the prevention of post‐TAVR heart failure rehospitalization.

To confirm and generalize our findings, a prospective, multicenter investigation should be undertaken to externally validate the prognostic significance of PVAT attenuation and verify the robustness of the results across different patient cohorts and CT platforms.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and the decision to perform TAVR was at the operator's discretion. A relatively large number (24 of 233 patients) were excluded because of insufficient CT quality, introducing potential selection bias. Second, we did not directly measure aortic valve inflammation. However, some studies suggest that PVAT attenuation may reflect local inflammation. 14 , 16 , 17 Further research is needed to clarify the association between aortic valve inflammation and PVAT attenuation. Third, despite adjusting for a comprehensive set of variables, unmeasured confounders may have affected our endpoints. Fourth, we did not include an independent validation cohort to confirm the generalizability of the PVAT attenuation threshold. Because of the retrospective design and limited availability of additional data, external validation could not be performed. However, the cutoff value used in this study (−70 HU) aligns with those reported in previous studies, 17 , 19 in which similar thresholds were associated with increased cardiovascular risk and adverse outcomes, supporting the robustness of the threshold applied in our analysis. Finally, patients with higher pre‐TAVR PVAT attenuation exhibited lower LVEF and greater LVMi, suggesting a possible link to underlying cardiomyopathy. In particular, cardiac amyloidosis, increasingly recognized in patients with AS, may contribute to this finding. However, systematic screening for cardiac amyloidosis using nuclear scintigraphy or myocardial biopsy was not routinely performed during the study period. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the association between PVAT attenuation and cardiac amyloidosis.

CONCLUSIONS

Higher pre‐TAVR PVAT attenuation emerged as an independent predictor of MACE in patients with severe AS who underwent TAVR. Assessing PVAT attenuation before TAVR may help identify patients at increased risk of post‐TAVR heart failure or cardiovascular death, thereby informing treatment strategy decisions.
