Accuracy of patient, care partner, and clinician health care contact day recall
Authors: Kendall Lin, Rick J Jansen, Manju George, Allison Breininger, Samuel X Stevens, Christopher M Booth, Helen M Parsons, Jeanette Y Ziegenfuss, Rachel I Vogel, Anne H Blaes, Arjun Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContact days are increasingly used as a measure of time toxicity, but current measurement approaches (eg, claims data) face limitations. To determine whether participant recall of contact days could serve as a practical approach, we evaluated contact day recall accuracy among patients, care partners, and clinicians across multiple recall windows.MethodsThis single-center cross-sectional survey study enrolled adults with cancer, their informal care partner, and their primary oncologist. Participants were asked to recall the number of patient contact days and associated care burden over the prior 7, 14, 30, and 60-days. Electronic medical record (EMR) data provided gold standard contact day data. The primary outcome was recall accuracy (within ±1, ±2, ±4, and ±8 days of EMR counts across windows).ResultsWe enrolled 42 patients, 29 care partners, and 8 clinicians. Median EMR-derived contact days were 2, 3, 7, and 11 across the 7-, 14-, 30-, and 60-day periods. Accuracy was high for all groups at 7 and 14 days (≥75%) and declined with longer windows. Participant-estimated contact days were moderately correlated with participant-assessed patient burden for patients and clinicians. We did not observe a meaningful correlation between EMR-derived contact days and patient-reported burden.ConclusionA single-item patient (or care partner or clinician)-reported outcome of recalling contact days may be a practical and accurate method of determining recent contact days, particularly over shorter recall periods. The subjective recall of contact days may be more associated with burden than the exact objective contact day number.Keywords: contact days, patient-reported outcomes, recall accuracy, time toxicity

“Contact days” are increasingly used as a measure of time toxicity, and have key associations with health care costs, quality-of-life, and mortality. A key need is operationalizing a real-time and practical method to determine contact days. In this study, asking persons with lived or professional experience with cancer, “How many contact days has the patient experienced in the past X days?” was generally answered accurately by patients, care partners, and oncologists, with higher accuracy over shorter recall periods. A single-item patient (or care partner or oncologist)-reported outcome of recalling contact days is a practical method of determining recent contact days.

Introduction

Contact days—the number of days patients spend interacting with the health care system—have rapidly emerged as a key measure of how much someone’s life is occupied by health care.1–10 Contact days are associated with health system financial costs, patient out-of-pocket spending, and worse quality-of-life, and are predictors of increased mortality.11–13 With recent efforts to streamline clinical trials and reduce participant and trial team burden, contact days may serve as a practical surrogate measure of several aspects of care. Contact days estimations have thus far relied on extracting existing data from the electronic medical record (EMR), claims, or clinical trial forms.14–19 While practical and broadly successful, current approaches are subject to inaccuracies, resource-intensiveness, and delayed data availability, along with issues with interoperability of data access across health systems. This may limit real-time utility.

A real-time, scalable approach to measuring contact days is needed as more research into the utility, scalability, and applicability of contact days as a measure is conducted. Recall of contact days by the person(s) directly involved in the care may represent one such avenue—people with lived experience (patients and their informal caregivers) and professional experience (the oncology care team) are likely best suited to recall their recent care experiences. Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are now routinely used for assessing patient symptoms, with clinicians often underestimating true symptom burdens.20,21 Additionally, recall accuracy can vary by the length of the recall period,22,23 with longer timeframes associated with lower accuracy. Asking persons with lived or professional experience of cancer to recall patients’ contact days over different recall periods could guide if and how to best collect contact days data with a simple question.

Thus, we evaluated the accuracy of patients, their informal care partners, and their primary oncologists in recalling the number of contact days experienced by the patient over different recall periods.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional single-center guided survey-based pilot study at the University of Minnesota Health System in Minneapolis, MN, USA. The study was approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board, and we obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Participant selection

We included three separate participant groups: patients, care partners, and treating clinicians. Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years of age) diagnosed with advanced stage (AJCC stage 3 or 4) solid tumors or lymphoma who had ever received systemic therapy (cytotoxic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy). Participating patients were invited to nominate a single primary adult informal care partner using the question, “Is there an adult individual who helps you with important aspects of care?” Eligible care partners were invited to participate in the study. Patients without or declining to nominate a care partner remained eligible. Eligible clinicians were medical or gynecological oncology physicians serving as the primary oncologist for an enrolled patient(s). We specifically included only physicians (vs other members of the care team) since each patient’s care was under a single physician, but an individual patient’s care was shared by several advanced practice providers, nurses, and other team members.

Participants were identified during routine oncology visits by their oncology care team (including their primary oncologist) and referred to the study team. Primary oncologists of included patients were then approached and recruited. A priori, to allow clinician-level estimates of contact day recall accuracy, each clinician could have between 5 and 10 (minimum-maximum) patients attributed to their care in the study. Trained research staff unaffiliated with the clinical care team obtained informed consent and conducted study procedures. Patient and care partner participants received a $50 gift card at the completion of the survey; clinicians did not receive compensation per local regulations.

Data collection

Surveys were designed by the interdisciplinary study team consisting of patient and care partner advocates, researchers with expertise in survey methods, and oncologists after a thorough literature review. All 3 participant groups were asked similar questions in the surveys, with minor differences in framing addressing that they were answering questions about patients’ contact day experiences. Surveys (Table S1) were uploaded in REDCap, and all participants completed surveys only once (single time point).24 A trained research staff member completed in-person surveys with patient and care partner participants in private clinic rooms. Given the need to first explain the contact days measure in detail, assess understanding, and then ask participants to recall patients’ contact days and burdens with the attendant complexity of the design, all surveys were guided by and administered by study staff with expertise and experience in survey administration—the staff asked questions verbally, and entered reported data into a secure iPad into REDCap live. Patients and care partners completed surveys independently and separately, but on the same date and only in-person.

Clinicians completed surveys either in the clinic in-person the same day as the patient and care partner, or if not possible that day, virtually on Zoom within one week. Surveys were guided and administered for clinicians as well. Since the clinician survey could be completed up to a week after the patient/care partner surveys, and each oncologist could have up to 10 patients enrolled, the research staff member provided the patient’s name and the date the patient completed the survey as the index date for the clinician to recall patients’ contact days. For all questions, the frame of reference and the unit of analysis were the patient—each patient answered questions about their recall of their own experience of contact days, and their care partner (if enrolled), and the oncologist recalled the patients’ experience of contact days. After an introduction to what constituted a contact day (any day with in-person health care contact for any reason for any length of time on that day in any setting, inpatient or outpatient, planned or unplanned) and confirming understanding with teach back, participants were asked to recall how many contact days the patient had had in the past 7, 14, 30, and 60 days, including the survey day (by definition a contact day since patient surveys were done in clinic).

Patient and caregiver advocates and co-authors strongly suggested keeping survey items limited and to only collect necessary information. Heeding this, recognizing the lack of a perfect and standardized “burdens” of cancer care measure, and noting how simple single-item tools of quality-of-life have shown validity, we opted to use a single-item burden assessment question.25,26 Participants were asked to rate how burdensome they felt the patient’s care was—from a time burdens perspective—on a scale of 0-10 (0 = not burdensome, 10 = very burdensome). Questions were framed to assess patients’ burden for patient and clinician participants, and the patient-care partner dyads’ burdens for care partner participants. To avoid survey fatigue, recall periods for burden were split into short-term (7–14 days) and longer-term (30–60 days).

Participants were additionally asked how they generated their contact day estimate (eg, memory, looked at calendar, looked at patient portal, etc.) and what led them to their burden rating (eg, back-and-forth trips, missed social occasions, missing work etc.). We did not instruct participants to or prevent them from using tools (eg, opening the EHR portal on their smartphone) if they so wanted. Only one recall strategy could be selected, with an “other” write-in option available.

We assessed participants' characteristics (eg, education level) through surveys and clinical information (eg, primary cancer site) from the EMR.

Using a validated algorithm, we extracted the number of contact days experienced by patients in the different recall periods (7, 14, 30, and 60 days) indexed from the patient survey date using EMR data.5 This would not necessarily capture health care contact (and associated contact days) outside the health system, although the CareEverywhere feature was active and captured care outside our health system.27

Outcomes

Using the EMR data as the gold standard, the primary outcome was recall accuracy, measured as the participant group’s (patients vs care partner vs clinician) percentage of contact day recall estimates falling within absolute error thresholds of ±1, ±2, ±4, and ±8 days of the EMR contact days across the 7-, 14-, 30-, and 60-day recall periods respectively. These absolute error thresholds were determined arbitrarily after the study team discussion. Secondary outcomes included the direction and magnitude of differences between recall and EMR contact days by participant group (with the hypothesis that clinicians would underestimate contact days), the association between reported contact days and burden (with the hypothesis that a higher number of contact days would be associated with greater burden), summarizing how contact days were recalled and burden estimated, and clinician-level variation in recall accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients, care partners, and clinicians were summarized with median and interquartile range (and mean) for continuous variables and number and percentage for categorical variables. Contact day recall and EHR contact days measures, and recall accuracy by participant group and across recall windows, were statistically compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Patient, care partner, and clinician-reported contact days were compared with EMR and visualized using boxplots for each recall period (7, 14, 30, and 60 days). Accuracy across recall periods for each participant group was visualized using line plots. Spearman coefficients were used to determine correlations between participant-reported burden and reported contact days. We evaluated whether patient variables were associated with recall accuracy using Chi-squared tests. Since this is a pilot study, the focus of the analyses was descriptive and not statistical. P-values were included in tables and figures; however, estimates and P-values should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. Data were collected in REDCap, and analyses were conducted using R [version 4.5.1].

Results

Baseline participant characteristics

Between November 2024 and August 2025, we enrolled 42 patients, 29 care partners, and 8 clinicians to participate in this study. Imbalance in participant numbers across groups was intentional, since patients could participate without care partners, and clinicians cared for multiple (5-10) patients. Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics for all groups are shown in Table 1. Patients had a median age of 62 years (IQR, 52-69), and most were women (67%). The most common primary cancer site was breast cancer (29%), and most (83%) were actively receiving systemic therapy at the time of enrollment in the study. Care partners were most often spouses/domestic partners (69%), and more often men (55%). Among clinicians, 38% were women, and 38% were in practice for 10 years or less.

Broad contact day patterns

For patients, across the longest (60-day) recall window using the gold standard EMR data, the mean contact days were 11.2; this corresponded to 1.3 contact days per week or 18.3% of days with health care contact. Median (IQR) EMR contact days were 2 (2,3) for a 7-day period, 3 (2,5) for a 14-day period, 7 (4,10) for a 30-day period, and 11 (7,16) for a 60-day period (Table S2).

Participant group recall and accuracy compared to EMR

Across all participant groups and recall windows, median participant-reported contact days generally aligned with EMR-derived counts (Figure 1). For a 7-day period, clinicians reported fewer median contact days vs EMR (median [IQR]; 1 [1,2] vs 2 [2,3]; P = .011). At 14 days, no statistically significant differences were observed in median contact days among any groups compared to the EMR. At 30 and 60 days, although results did not reach statistical significance, median reported contact days were slightly lower relative to EMR-derived days. Note that these represent median values and not actual accuracy.

Over shorter recall windows (7 and 14 days), recall accuracy was high (≥73%) for all groups (patients 79%; care partners 73%; and clinicians 80%) but declined at longer intervals, with care partners demonstrating a more pronounced decrease, for example, 68% accuracy at 30 days (Figure 2). Patient accuracy was high and stable (79%-81%) across all windows, clinicians demonstrated the highest accuracy at shorter windows (85% at 7- and 14-day windows). Across windows, exact agreement with the EMR occurred in approximately 20% of estimates across participant groups (patients 21%; care partners 20%; and clinicians 21%). Groups had similar rates of underreporting contact days (patients 16%, care partners 18%; and clinicians 15%) but care partners more often overestimated contact days (9%) compared to patients and clinicians (5% each).

Among clinicians, accuracy varied widely among individual clinicians (Figure S1) over the same recall period (clinician-level accuracy, range, 0%-100% for 7-day recall) and within-clinician over different recall periods (within clinician accuracy, range, 0%-100% over all recall periods). Accuracy tended to decrease over longer recall periods in the same clinician.

Perceived burden and associations with contact days

Correlations between the perceived burden of care across the three participant groups and contact days are reported in Table S3. All participant groups reported a median burden rating of 5 (scale, 0-10) in the 30- to 60-day window. Using the Spearman rank correlation (ρ), moderate correlations were observed between participant group-reported contact days and participant group-perceived short-term and longer-term patient burden for both patients (Ρ = .43 for short-term; Ρ = –.56 for longer-term; P < .01 for both) and clinicians (Ρ = .57 for short-term; Ρ = .68 for longer-term; P < .01 for both). In contrast, care partner-reported contact days showed a nonsignificant, weak correlation with care-partner perceived patient burden (Ρ = .04-.34, P > .05). Similarly, no meaningful correlation was observed between EMR-derived contact day counts and patient-reported burden (Ρ = −.08 to .14, P > .05).

Recall strategies

Participants most often thought back to the treatment schedule (eg, infusions every week, etc.) or looked at calendars on their smartphones to recall contact days, with some anchoring on specific clinical events (Table S4). Care partners demonstrated the highest reliance on structured scheduling tools. Few participants relied on “gut feeling” or used patient portals. Clinicians often used different strategies for different patients, sometimes using treatment schedules and sometimes recalling a specific clinical event such as a hospitalization.

Recall accuracy by patient characteristics

In exploratory analysis, patient participants with higher education level (college/graduate vs high school or less) had greater recall accuracy (86% vs 71%, P=.016) (Table S5).

Discussion

In this single center prospective survey study, patients, care partners, and clinicians all had reasonably and similarly accurate recall of patients’ contact days during shorter (7-14 day) windows. Exactly accurate recall was uncommon, approximately 20%. Patients’ and clinicians’ recalled contact days were moderately correlated with burden, but the correlation between care partner recalled contact days and EMR-derived contact days and burden was weak. Together, these data demonstrate the potential of a short-term contact days PRO (or care partner- or clinician-) , and that the subjective recall of contact days may be more associated with burden than the actual contact day number.

The primary finding of the study—that all participant groups were reasonably accurate while recalling contact days—should be interpreted in the context of the underlying clinical context. Per the EMR-derived gold standard measure, patients experienced approximately a 18% contact day rate over the largest 60-day recall period. This rate is very similar to rates reported from several global studies, including patients with advanced solid tumors receiving systemic cancer-directed therapy,15,28 and raises confidence in the current study’s generalizability—that the study’s patient cohort was a “real-world” population mirroring usual clinical practice. The reasonably accurate short-term recall across participant groups (80%-85% accuracy for 7 days, and 75%-85% accuracy for 14 days; with the lowest levels for care partners), with a decline to approximately 70% accuracy for care partners at 30-60-day windows, indicates that of the three groups, care partners may be the least accurate at contact day recall. This in no way diminishes the role care partners play as part of the cancer care team, but rather perhaps indicates the “24 × 7” nature of informal caregiving, where attending appointments with their loved one (the patient)—ie, a contact day—blends into all the other things that care partners support patients with, even at home,29–34 potentially limiting their accuracy of contact days recall over longer recall periods. It may also signal that the primary care partner may not attend every appointment, and that multiple care partners may share responsibility. In contrast, patients who have adopted a sick role may remember clinic visits (contact days) as more discrete events, and clinicians only interact with patients during contact days, potentially increasing their accuracy.35 The finding that clinicians were quite accurate in recalling contact days went against our a priori hypothesis—given prior work that clinicians tend to underestimate patients’ symptom burden and toxicity experiences,20,21 we had expected that clinicians would underestimate contact days. Given the limited number of clinicians, and that they were aware of multiple ongoing projects regarding time toxicity at the study site, it is possible that clinicians were more attentive to and recognized (and recognized the need to recognize) contact days and patient burdens in the study (vs in routine practice), a Hawthorne effect.36 Additionally, oncologists in the current study provided longitudinal care to patient participants. In some trial settings, patients may be under the care of multiple clinicians, and/or non-trial care may occur separately from the trial, limiting clinician accuracy.

The decline in recall accuracy over longer windows mirrors findings from other contexts, where the limits of memory expound as recall periods lengthen.37–39 Prior work has found that 7-day reporting of PROs, such as for symptoms, has very little information loss compared to daily reporting, and has psychometric properties consistent with daily recall.23,40 Clinicians, particularly the primary oncologist for a patient, may also hold promise in accurately recalling patients’ contact days. The variability in accuracy among clinicians, between clinicians and within clinicians at different recall periods, requires further study and identifies possible opportunities—whether through training, prompts, or aids—to train clinicians to potentially enhance recall in future work. While recall is clearly not 100% accurate—only approximately 20% of recalled values match exact EMR values—it appears sufficiently reliable to function as a pragmatic outcome in settings where objective data about health care use are unavailable, delayed, or costly to obtain. This should not detract from the importance of accurately reporting contact days in cancer clinical trials.16,17 We also observed that participants used a variety of approaches when recalling contact days; unfortunately, the small sample sizes preclude our ability to analyze whether participants using certain approaches (eg, looking up the patient portal) resulted in greater accuracy. Further work can evaluate the impact of providing structured aids, such as chemotherapy calendars or visit logs, in improving accuracy—however, any such intervention will decrease applicability in routine practice. This is the first study to our knowledge that prospectively sought to explore the accuracy of recalled contact days, and these preliminary data will require further evaluation in larger studies. Alongside recalled contact days, mobile health technologies with embedded geolocation services that can passively collect what days a patient has a health care contact represent a parallel approach to data collection.41,42

Patient and care partner participants noted higher burden over 30-60 day windows compared to 7-14 day windows. This may represent a “Peak-End” effect, a cognitive bias where people judge experiences based on how they felt at the peak and at the end, rather than the average over the duration.43 Since participants were attending appointments on the study day, often to receive systemic therapy, and likely felt “good enough” to receive it, and several noted they derived great meaning from receiving cancer-care, they may have had a “pretty good” last 1-2 weeks (no bad symptom peaks) and “pretty good” end (fit enough for treatment on the day. In contrast, the 1-2 months prior provided a longer window for peak (bad) events. It may also represent response shift (internal recalibration), whereby the internal standards of patients may change over time as their experience in managing the condition changes.44 Two months ago, 6/10 fatigue might have felt like 9/10 because it was new and frightening. More recently, they have learned to adjust and adapt, and 6/10 is “just another day”. We also investigated the association between participant group-estimated patient burden and recalled contact days. Two findings deserve specific mention. First, supporting the earlier stated hypothesis that patients and clinicians may have higher accuracy at recalling contact days (as compared to care partners) because contact days represent more discrete episodes for them (as compared to more diffuse responsibilities for care partners), we found a moderate correlation between patient and clinician-estimated patient burden and their recall of contact days, but not for care partner-estimated burden and contact days. This is in line with prior work investigating the association between contact days and indirect costs in a clinical trial, which found that contact days have a very weak association with unpaid hours provided by informal care partners (correlation co-efficient = 0.06, 0.7 hours/contact day, P = .247), whereas the association was stronger for costs associated with more formal care.12 These findings suggest that while patient- or clinician recall of contact days have promise as markers of (at least some aspects of) patient-experienced care burden, contact days measures alone may underestimate or fail to characterize the full scope of burdens and experiences by care partners (as viewed for the patient and care partner dyad, by the care partner), which may be shaped by factors beyond formal health care encounters (eg, home care responsibilities, logistical coordination, emotional labor).30 The observed correlation between recalled contact days and perceived burden could also reflect shared method variance (both recall and burden are self-reported at the same time on the survey) or cognitive consistency bias (persons who feel burdened may estimate more contact days) on the part of the patient. Second, we did not observe an association between EMR-derived contact days and patient burden. This highlights that care “burden”, as it relates to its association with contact days, is perspective-driven for patients and clinicians—that subjective recall of health care contact dictates perceived burden more than objective EMR-derived counts.31–33,45 Thus, recalled contact days may represent a clinically meaningful outcome rather than simply a substitute for EMR counts. These findings support the idea that objective and subjective measures capture related but distinct constructs. EMR-derived contact days may provide a structured, operationalizable measure of exposure to care, while person-reported burden reflects the experiential meaning of that exposure. Both components are necessary to advance the measurement of time toxicity.

Limitations

This single institution study where there is ongoing work on time toxicity may have alerted and biased clinicians towards recognizing contact days. The limited power with relatively small numbers precludes definitive conclusions, and the predominantly White race/ethnicity among patients and care partners and female sex among patients may limit generalizability. Most patients were receiving active systemic cancer-directed treatment on a set schedule (eg, every 2 weeks) and were fit enough to do so. Recall and sense of burdens may be different for patients with short illness trajectories and poor prognosis.

Due to small numbers, we are unable to establish if using assistive methods (eg, opening the patient portal) was associated with greater recall accuracy. If certain groups are more likely to use assistive methods, it could further limit generalizability. Given that research staff in the current study individually educated each participant about contact days, and the finding that higher education status was associated with greater recall accuracy, future work should evaluate whether digital interventions, including instructional videos and knowledge-check quizzes, can teach participants about contact days. Associations between contact days and burden were unadjusted and exploratory. The EHR contact day estimates were used as the gold standard in the study, but may not have captured care outside the health system.

Conclusion

This single-institution multi-shareholder survey study found that a single-item patient (or care partner- or clinician-)-reported outcome of recalling contact days may be a practical and accurate method of determining recent contact days, particularly over shorter recall periods. Patient- and clinician-recalled contact days were moderately correlated with patient burden. The lower accuracy of care partners and the weak correlation between care partner-estimated contact days and burden suggest that caregiver burdens extend beyond contact days alone. This is the first study to our knowledge that prospectively explored the accuracy of recalled contact days, and these hypothesis-generating data will guide the discipline on how best to collect contact days data with minimal lag.
