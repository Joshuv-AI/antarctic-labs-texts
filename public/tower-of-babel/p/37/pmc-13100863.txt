Advancing Earth System Science With the NASA Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, Ocean Ecosystem (PACE) Satellite Mission
Authors: P. Jeremy Werdell, Brian Cairns, Skyelar A. Caplan, Ivona Cetinić, Sean R. Foley, Bryan A. Franz, Meng Gao, Zachary T. Fasnacht, K. Fred Huemmrich, Amir Ibrahim, Kirk D. Knobelspiesse, Antonio Mannino, Lachlan I. W. McKinna, Carina Poulin, Chamara Rajapakshe, Andrew M. Sayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarth observation satellites transform our understanding of Earth's biological, atmospheric, and surface systems. The Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, ocean Ecosystem (PACE) mission, launched in 2024, represents NASA's latest investment in multidisciplinary Earth system science, building upon multi‐decadal heritage while introducing revolutionary capabilities. Since launch, PACE has extended and improved upon NASA's 30+ years of global satellite observations of our living ocean, atmosphere, and land, and initiated an advanced set of climate‐ and applications‐relevant data records. Mission goals include extending and improving upon systematic ocean color, aerosol, cloud, and terrestrial data records for Earth system studies, and addressing emerging science questions related to socioeconomic applications. PACE helps assess ocean health by determining the distribution of aquatic phytoplankton. In doing so, it is the first mission to provide daily, global measurements that will enable prediction of the “boom‐bust” cycle of fisheries, the appearance of harmful algae, and other factors that affect commercial and recreational industries. PACE also observes clouds and tiny airborne particles known as aerosols that influence air quality and absorb and reflect sunlight, thus warming and cooling the atmosphere. Many stakeholders rely on these key data to forecast weather, visibility, and air quality. Also, PACE provides novel and near‐daily views of land surfaces, collectively extending heritage vegetation time series while introducing novel terrestrial measurements previously only achievable at local scales. PACE ultimately provides observations that benefit Earth system research and society as a whole in ways that other current satellites cannot. As it passes its two‐year anniversary, the mission exemplifies how combining hyperspectral radiometry with multi‐angle polarimetry enables transformative Earth system science, addressing critical uncertainties while establishing new paradigms for integrated observations of our changing planet. This review examines how PACE fills critical gaps across aquatic, terrestrial, and atmospheric science disciplines.

Introduction

Earth observation (EO) satellites have revolutionized our understanding of how we interact with our home planet (e.g., NRC 2007, 2018). Related to Earth's biological systems, satellite radiometers provide invaluable information on terrestrial vegetation and land surface properties, aquatic biology and biogeochemistry, atmospheric microphysical properties and gases, radiation budgets, precipitation, and land‐ocean–atmosphere temperatures, to name only a few broad disciplines. Likewise, satellite‐borne radars provide essential data on forest and crop biomass, structure, and classification, terrestrial hydrology, topography, aquatic hydrography, ice sheet dynamics, precipitation, and clouds. Also, lidar provides information on forest canopy and urban vertical structures, mass balance, atmospheric microphysical properties and vertical structure, ocean topography, and aquatic biological and physical properties. Individually, these various eyes‐in‐the‐sky provide temporally‐rich, global or near‐global imagery on time scales that are unobtainable from conventional ground‐ or aircraft‐based sampling platforms. Together, the data from EO satellites offers a cornucopia for monitoring and improving our understanding of the intertwined Earth system, while also fueling a virtual time machine via their permanent archival and the potential to reprocess and improve mission‐long scientific data records.

The Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, ocean Ecosystem (PACE) mission represents a critical recent investment in Earth system science by NASA (Werdell et al. 2019). At its core, PACE builds upon NASA's multi‐decadal legacies of passive multispectral radiometry from the Coastal Zone Color Scanner (1978–1986), Sea‐viewing Wide Field‐of‐view Sensor (SeaWiFS; 1997–2010), Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometers (MODIS) onboard Terra (1999–present) and Aqua (2002–present), and Visible Infrared Imaging Spectroradiometer (VIIRS) onboard Suomi NPP (2012–present), NOAA‐20 (2017–present), and NOAA‐21 (2022–present), among others in the U.S. and abroad. The ongoing, interdisciplinary research and applied data records from these instruments changed the way we view our home planet and—to this day—continue to offer opportunities to expand our senses into space, compress time, and measure life itself.

Briefly, the relevant passive satellite instruments for this review detect spectral radiances emanating from the top of the atmosphere (TOA) at wavelengths that typically span the ultraviolet (UV) through the visible and near‐infrared (NIR) into the shortwave infrared (SWIR) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. This total signal contains contributions from and, thus, information about anything that sunlight interacts with on its journey through the Earth system and back into the field of view of the satellite instrument, inclusive of land/ice, ocean, and atmosphere. Various disciplines apply specific techniques to isolate their signal of interest and convert the optical signal measured by the instrument into geophysical variables of interest, the process we colloquially refer to here as “retrieval”. Aquatic and terrestrial disciplines, for example, either remove or simultaneously solve for the atmospheric contribution to the total signal to generate spectral surface reflectances, which can subsequently be related to biological parameters such as the concentration of the photosynthetic pigment chlorophyll‐a (Chl, mg m−3) (Frouin et al. 2019). Conversely, atmospheric disciplines often assign surface boundary conditions to enable the generation of atmospheric aerosol and cloud microphysical and thermodynamic properties or retrieve those surface conditions simultaneously with those of the atmosphere (Dubovik et al. 2019).

Originally described in NASA (2010) and launched on 8 February 2024, PACE extends and significantly improves upon these 30+ years of global satellite observations of our living ocean, atmosphere, and land and introduces a highly advanced set of new and unprecedented data records to address novel and emerging science questions related to Earth system research and socioeconomic applications. Our raison d'être here is to showcase the latter, namely the niches that PACE has and may eventually fill. Through NASA (2025), the foundational scientific objectives prescribed to guide the formulation of the mission included: Extend key systematic aerosol, cloud, and ocean data records;Make global measurements of ocean color data products that are essential for understanding the global carbon cycle and ocean ecosystem responses to a changing climate, as well as managing marine fisheries and water quality;Collect global observation of aerosol and cloud properties, focusing on reducing the largest uncertainties in climate and radiative forcing models of the Earth system, in addition to understanding air quality; andImprove our understanding of how aerosols influence ocean biogeochemical cycles and how ocean biological and photochemical processes affect the atmosphere.

The primary instrument, the Ocean Color Instrument (OCI), is an advanced scanning radiometer developed at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center designed to measure total spectral radiance (W m−2 sr−1 nm−1) over a continuum of ocean, land, and atmosphere (Meister et al. 2024). Although heritage EO radiometers typically employ 10 or fewer discrete wavelength bands spanning the blue to SWIR, OCI acquires hyperspectral measurements across a continuous spectral range extending across the UV–visible–NIR, as well as at seven discrete SWIR channels. This capability represents the first spaceborne hyperspectral radiometer to provide daily global coverage imagery, enabling unprecedented continuous spectral characterization of phytoplankton community composition in aquatic systems, as well as enhanced retrieval of atmospheric aerosols, cloud properties, trace gases, and terrestrial biophysical parameters (despite the latter two not being original objectives) (PACE Science Definition Team 2018; Werdell et al. 2019).

PACE also carries two complementary polarimeters: the Spectro‐polarimeter for Planetary Exploration (SPEXone), developed by a Netherlands‐based consortium consisting of the Space Research Organization of the Netherlands (SRON) and Airbus Defence and Space Netherlands (Hasekamp et al. 2019), and the Hyper‐Angular Rainbow Polarimeter (HARP2), developed by the Earth and Space Institute at the University of Maryland Baltimore County (Martins et al. 2018). Unlike OCI, these instruments image the TOA at multiple angles with detectors sensitive to the polarized state of reflected light. SPEXone acquires hyperspectral UV–visible–NIR observations across a narrow swath at five viewing geometries, whereas HARP2 captures wide‐swath multispectral data at 10 viewing angles in its blue, green, and NIR bands, and 60 viewing angles in its red band. Collectively, these polarimetric capabilities enable enhanced retrieval of aerosol microphysical properties, cloud parameters, and surface bidirectional reflectance characteristics (Remer, Knobelspiesse, et al., Remer, Knobelspiesse, et al. 2019), while establishing continuity with other multi‐angle polarimetric instruments, such as the ESA Multi‐viewing Multi‐channel Multi‐polarisation Imager (3MI), as well as offering new opportunities for deriving bio‐optical and biogeochemical terrestrial and aquatic properties.

PACE celebrated its two‐year launch anniversary at the time of writing. This two year span included routine instrument calibration and science algorithm refinements that led to three full mission data reprocessings (https://oceancolor.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/reprocessing/), a comprehensive aircraft‐ and ship‐based field campaign (Knobelspiesse et al. 2025), the deployment of validation and algorithm development science teams, and a surfacing wellspring of potential discovery. Details on mission activities, inclusive of instrument calibration updates and science data product performance assessments, can be found in Werdell, Franz, and al. (Werdell and Franz 2026) and the references therein. Here, we present a review that focuses on the latter post‐launch activity, namely the emergence of novel and advanced science data products from PACE that have filled or will fill gaps and new niches in satellite‐based Earth system science. We present opportunities for aquatic, terrestrial, and atmospheric science, as well as interdisciplinary science, with each subsection noting the state‐of‐the‐art before PACE followed by arenas for advancement enabled by PACE. Through this review, we hope to stimulate and invigorate the exploration of PACE capabilities to usher in the next era of Earth system science and applications research from space.

Aquatic Science

So called “ocean color” from space currently spans nearly four decades, with the processing rubric commonly proceeding as follows. Atmospheric correction algorithms remove the atmospheric contribution from the total measured TOA signal, generating estimates of spectral remote sensing reflectances (Rrs(λ), sr−1) that represent the ratio of light exiting the water to the incoming solar radiation that penetrates the sea surface. This component of the rubric remains the most critical, as the signal from the water represents at best ~10% of the total observed at TOA. Rrs(λ) describe the color of the water (thus, the discipline moniker “ocean color”), which is governed by its inherent optical properties (IOPs), specifically the spectral absorption and scattering of light by optically active constituents such as phytoplankton, non‐algal particles, and colored dissolved organic matter (CDOM). Analytical and empirical linkages between Rrs(λ) and IOPs constitute the theoretical foundation upon which bio‐optical algorithms are subsequently used to derive useful quantitative estimates of aquatic geophysical parameters (e.g., CDOM) from the satellite‐based observations. Historically, aquatic retrievals predominantly arise from single‐view‐angle observations of total radiometric intensity (e.g., SeaWiFS, MODIS, and VIIRS), but the use of polarized, multi‐angle measurements provides a highly viable alternate or complementary approach (Frouin et al. 2019; Jamet et al. 2019 provide useful overviews).

PACE represents a watershed moment in aquatic remote sensing primarily through its unprecedented hyperspectral and polarization capabilities (Figure 2). Most notably, OCI addresses the spectral limitations of multiband sensors that confine the retrieval space available to study aquatic ecosystem processes and the oceanic carbon cycle. For example, although the spectra measured by heritage sensors, such as SeaWiFS, MODIS, and VIIRS, enable separation of phytoplankton signatures from a background of other seawater constituents, they do not universally or unequivocally contain information to identify the corresponding phytoplankton community (see, e.g., Bracher et al. 2017; IOCCG 2014; Mouw et al. 2017). Furthermore, no heritage ocean color instrument spans deeply into the UV (< 380 nm), maintains adequate radiometric sensitivity to reveal chlorophyll‐a fluorescence features across water types, or includes a suite of SWIR bands with suitable performance to support both ocean color atmospheric correction and aquatic geophysical retrievals. Measurements in the UV and SWIR offer opportunities for improved or novel retrievals of CDOM and mycosporine‐like amino acids (MAAs), as well as suspended particulate matter (e.g., Knaeps et al. 2015). Observations of fluorescence inform on phytoplankton physiology and marine primary production (Westberry et al. 2016). The improved atmospheric correction in complex water enabled by the use of SWIR bands extends the geographic reach of all retrievals (Ibrahim et al. 2019; Ibrahim et al. 2022).

HARP2 and SPEXone provide complementary approaches for retrieving aquatic bio‐optical properties, extending traditional ocean color radiometry through sensitivity to particle microphysical properties and surface–atmosphere interactions (e.g., Aryal et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2018; Loisel et al. 2008). Although historically applied within aerosol‐focused retrieval frameworks, the angular and spectral dependence of linear polarization enables simultaneous constraints on aerosols, air‐sea interface effects, and polarized water‐leaving radiance, providing information on oceanic constituents unavailable from single‐view, radiance‐only observations (Chami 2007; Chami and McKee 2007; Chami et al. 2001; Chowdhary et al. 2006; Chowdhary et al. 2012; Ibrahim et al. 2012; Tonizzo et al. 2011).

Phytoplankton Community Composition

The original promise of PACE centered on advanced retrievals of phytoplankton community composition (PCC) (Cetinić et al. 2024; PACE Science Definition Team 2018). OCI's hyperspectral measurements reveal deeper insight than its predecessors by enabling differentiation of PCCs on the basis of their unique bio‐optical signatures, which are governed by features such as size, morphology, pigmentation, and physiology. The first operational PACE PCC product that exploits the full spectral resolution of OCI uses the Multi Ordination Analysis (MOANA) algorithm (Lange et al. 2020), which describes Atlantic taxonomic variability of picophytoplankton that dominate oceanic phytoplankton in numbers despite being the smallest in size (Figure 2a). Forthcoming PCC algorithms from OCI offer alternative, but complementary, views of global oceanic taxonomy through retrieval of photosynthetic pigment composition (e.g., Chase et al. 2017; Kramer et al. 2022) or taxa specific carbon content (e.g., Chase et al. 2022) (Figure 2d) from spectral matching approaches that additionally resolve other aquatic IOPs.

OCI's capture of solar‐induced fluorescence (SIF) from chlorophyll provides complementary information about photosynthetic efficiency of the resident community that cannot be obtained from absorption‐based measurements alone (e.g., Behrenfeld et al. 2009) (Figure 2g). The ensuing use of such products to predict the “boom‐bust” cycle of fisheries represents one of PACE's most significant practical applications. For example, large diatom blooms often support productive fisheries through efficient energy transfer, whereas certain dinoflagellate species may indicate conditions unfavorable for commercial species (Goes et al. 2018). We anticipate that new and refined retrieval approaches will offer taxa or size specific primary productivity estimates that provide value for climate and fisheries models (Cetinić et al. 2024; Vandermeulen et al. 2025), estimations of carbon export (Siegel et al. 2023), and detection of higher tropic levels from space (Shunmugapandi et al. 2025).

PACE additionally supports enhanced detection of nuisance communities and harmful algal blooms (HABs) in coastal and inland freshwater bodies (Grunert et al. 2025). For example, OCI's radiometric performance (Meister et al. 2024) provides opportunities to detect cyanobacteria‐specific pigments and features over a greater dynamic range of concentrations and environmental diversity than its predecessors (Kumar et al. 2026), which will enhance ongoing monitoring efforts (e.g., Schaeffer et al. 2015). As for the beneficial PCCs addressed above, the ability to detect community‐specific spectral features throughout the UV–visible spectral range, in combination with retrieved optical signals that relate to the physiological aspects of the phytoplankton such as SIF, provides new opportunities for HAB detection. Several approaches that have already shown promise enable the identification of Lingulodinium polyedra blooms offshore of California (Kahru et al. 2021), Karenia spp. blooms in West Florida Shelf and Australian waters (building upon Soto et al. 2015) Phaeocystis globosa blooms in Belgian waters (Lavigne et al. 2026), and Atlantic Sargassum (Qi et al. 2026) (Figure 2). The potential of OCI to identify nuisance and harmful (including those containing toxins) communities on daily, global scales will unquestionably enable improved, targeted public health responses.

Utility of an Ultraviolet Signal

OCI's extended spectral range into the UV provides value beyond detection of PCCs. UV radiation plays critical roles in marine photochemistry, affecting both phytoplankton physiology and biogeochemical cycling. For example, different communities exhibit varying sensitivities to UV radiation, with some developing protective mechanisms while others remaining susceptible to UV damage. Photoprotection strategies, such as UV‐absorbing compounds like MAAs (e.g., Karentz 2001), can be detected through their specific spectral signatures (e.g., Kahru et al. 2021). OCI's hyperspectral UV signal also offers potential to better distinguish CDOM from other optically‐active constituents, to characterize its optical properties and the sources and transformations of DOM, and to generally improve our understanding of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) pools and fluxes (Fichot et al. 2023; Hooker et al. 2021; Hooker et al. 2020; Mannino et al. 2014; Mannino et al. 2016; Signorini et al. 2019). The ensuing superior characterization of CDOM should improve detection and tracking of terrigenous dissolved organic matter through rivers, estuaries, and coastal ocean (Figure 2b). The changing composition of the DOC pool from photodegradation should be accompanied by resolvable changes in CDOM absorption and spectral slope, which could facilitate improved understanding of carbon cycling, particularly related to altered bioavailabilities of humic‐like compounds. Observations in the UV may also enhance oil spill detection (Suo et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023).

Polarimetry in Support of Aquatic Retrievals

Although OCI provides novel hyperspectral coverage for characterizing aquatic biogeochemistry, the inclusion of multi‐angular, polarimetric measurements from HARP2 and SPEXone substantially increases the information content available for ocean–atmosphere retrievals. This capability directly addresses two critical challenges in ocean color remote sensing: improving aerosol characterization in complex atmospheric conditions to enable more accurate ocean color atmospheric correction, particularly in the UV‐blue; and enabling simultaneous ocean–atmosphere retrievals and polarimetric algorithms dedicated to aquatic retrievals.

As discussed, a central challenge in ocean color remote sensing remains the separation of the weak water‐leaving signal from the dominant atmospheric contribution at TOA. Multi‐angle, polarimetric measurements provide sensitivity to particle microphysical properties, including particle size distribution, effective radius, refractive index, and particle shape for both aerosols and hydrosols, which remain difficult to uniquely constrain using single‐view, radiance‐only observations (Chowdhary et al. 2005; Knobelspiesse et al. 2012; Mishchenko and Travis 1997). Because atmospheric scattering dominates the polarized top‐of‐atmosphere signal, polarimetric observations provide strong constraints on these properties, which maintain direct relevance for atmospheric correction over coastal and inland waters where aerosol complexity and spatial variability pose a challenge for OCI retrievals at shorter wavelengths.

Polarimetric measurements additionally enable a more physically consistent treatment of the coupled ocean–atmosphere system. Although the polarized component of the water‐leaving signal is relatively small, it contains information on the angular structure of surface and subsurface light fields, as well as on bulk aquatic IOPs and hydrosol microphysics. Sensitivity to particle size distribution and refractive index further enables discrimination between particle types (e.g., organic versus inorganic), improving phytoplankton and colored detrital particle separation (Chami 2007; Chami and McKee 2007; Chami et al. 2001; Chowdhary et al. 2006; Ibrahim et al. 2016; Ibrahim et al. 2012; Loisel et al. 2008; Tonizzo et al. 2011).

Algorithms that simultaneously retrieve atmospheric and oceanic properties using PACE polarimetric observations are now actively being developed and applied (e.g., Aryal et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2025; Gao et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2018), including approaches that jointly leverage OCI hyperspectral radiances with HARP2 and/or SPEXone measurements (Stamnes et al. 2023) (Figs. 2f and 2l). Additionally, multi‐angular measurements provide an opportunity to directly derive angular radiometric signals, which adds a new dimension to aquatic studies (Gao et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2025). These developments can both improve OCI atmospheric correction and formalize a pathway toward dedicated polarimetric retrievals of aquatic properties. Together, the synergistic use of hyperspectral radiometry and multi‐angle polarimetry significantly increases the information content of PACE observations, enabling more accurate and physically consistent retrievals of atmospheric and oceanic particle properties.

Terrestrial Science

Space‐based observations of Earth's land surface began in the early 1970's and have evolved continuously over the last five decades. The starting measurement for terrestrial analyses is most often spectral surface reflectance, which describes the amount of incoming light that is reflected by the ground in any given wavelength as a unitless ratio. As for aquatic retrievals, generating surface reflectances from a passive instrument requires correction for atmospheric effects. Typically, Rayleigh scattering and contributions from absorbing gases such as ozone (O3), water vapor, and carbon dioxide are removed. More robust algorithms also account for aerosol particles such as dust or smoke, and for angular effects caused by viewing geometry (see Section 3.4). The shape of an atmospherically corrected surface reflectance spectrum relates directly to the observed material, much in the way that Rrs(λ) relates to ocean color. As such, studies often employ the spectral shape of leaf reflectance to distill information in the form of vegetation indices (VIs) (Bannari et al. 1995; Xue and Su 2017). VIs characterize how plant function manifests in different spectral regions, given that pigment content governs visible wavelengths, cell structure influences the NIR, and water content controls the SWIR.

Heritage missions such as the NASA‐USGS Landsat program, MODIS, or the ESA Sentinel‐2 program, retrieve multispectral land surface reflectances in several to a dozen broad (i.e., > 10 nm wide) channels. VIs work well for these sensors because they offer useful information about a given pixel without requiring full, fine‐scale spectral resolution. However, these bands are often spectrally offset from each other across sensors, which precludes comparability. In addition, useful features of the spectrum are often not sufficiently resolved because of the limited number of bands and/or because the broad bandwidths average them out. OCI provides new opportunities in this domain with its ~270 continuous, narrower‐width (5 nm) channels, enabling continuity with previous missions while also supporting the production of more terrestrial parameters than otherwise possible with multispectral measurements. Regarding spatial footprints, heritage and contemporary (including commercial) instruments typically have high resolution (< 60 m), which describes the imaged landscape in detail, but also results in intermittent temporal coverage (5–16 days). Although no PACE instrument approaches that spatial resolution, they provide near‐daily global coverage that is valuable on its own and offer spatial and temporal context for the interpretation of imagery from those specialized instruments.

Although land surface product generation is not required from the mission (see, e.g., NASA 2025), PACE provides high‐quality global, near‐daily hyperspectral and multi‐angular polarimetric observations over land. Given its oceanic and atmospheric priorities, OCI was optimized for observing both dark water and very bright clouds, making terrestrial targets easily retrievable without saturation. Multi‐angular and polarimetric measurements from HARP2 and SPEXone enable novel retrieval of bidirectional reflectance distributions of the land surface and enhanced characterization of surface properties. As such, the mission's unique capabilities are leading to significant advances in terrestrial science, answering questions about global ecosystems that previously could only be studied on small scales.

Vegetation Indices

Legacy VIs are produced by averaging OCI's hyperspectral bands to simulate multispectral bands from, e.g., MODIS and Landsat. In doing so, PACE extends the data record of useful bulk greenness and abundance metrics that have become standard in the terrestrial community. Given that MODIS is at end‐of‐life, the ability to augment these well‐used and ~25‐year time series is critical to extend long‐term ecological analyses. OCI's narrow bandwidth, high spectral resolution reflectances additionally allow generation of advanced VIs that characterize new biochemical properties of foliage, pushing the field forward. The Chlorophyll Index Red Edge (CIRE), modified Anthocyanin Reflectance Index (mARI), and the Carotenoid Content Index (Car) estimate relative concentrations of the three major plant pigments, chlorophylls, carotenoids, and anthocyanins, providing information on plant productivity and health status (Gitelson et al. 2001; Gitelson et al. 2005; Gitelson et al. 2002; Roberts et al. 2018) (Figure 3). Moreover, the Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) exploits narrowband measurements in the green region to capture shifts in xanthophyll pigments that describe photosynthetic efficiency related to plant stress (Grace et al. 2007). These indices are key examples of the advantages of OCI as a global sensor. Neither MODIS nor VIIRS can retrieve PRI, CIRE, Car, or mARI.

As these hyperspectral‐enabled indices are now produced at near‐daily, canopy‐level scales, they actively enable understanding of regional pigment dynamics and thus global ecosystem status. Seasonal changes in leaf‐out and senescence timing can be refined by examining the pigments directly related to pigment shifts rather than using proxy greenness metrics such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Wu et al. 2017). Relating CIRE, mARI, and Car has been shown to identify precise timings of leaf color change in autumn (Huemmrich and Caplan 2026) and the onset of photosynthesis in boreal evergreens (Wong and Gamon 2015) that are examples of important ecological transition periods. With PACE, we can achieve more accurate measures of the timing of these transitions and describe their spatial characteristics, improving land cover classifications and phenological descriptions.

Gross Primary Productivity

OCI's full‐spectrum measurements have already proven useful in retrieving important ecological functions such as Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) (Figure 3). GPP describes the total uptake of carbon in an ecosystem. Previously, regional and global GPP measurements had to be inferred indirectly from satellite proxies like VIs, or with models driven by multi‐satellite inputs and ancillary meteorological data (Running et al. 2004; Xiao et al. 2019). Because OCI's hyperspectral reflectance retrievals retain minute spectral features associated with relevant biological functions, they can be used on their own for high‐fidelity GPP retrievals (Huemmrich et al. 2025). These measurements can then be used to examine spatial and temporal responses to environmental conditions independently. An additional advantage of PACE's daily global coverage is the frequent overpasses of training and validation sites needed for these algorithms. Ground networks can be relatively sparse spatially despite their temporal density, making frequent overpasses essential for determining algorithm accuracy.

The Cryosphere

Beyond vegetation monitoring, PACE provides new insights into climatic effects on Earth's cryosphere by investigating glacial landscapes like Greenland. With its imaging spectroscopy capabilities and SWIR bands, OCI can characterize snow grain size (Ottaviani et al. 2015). HARP2's sensitivity to polarization will enable more robust characterization of impurities in snowpack by differentiating dust or soot embedded in snow from aerosols in the atmosphere. This problem is typically intractable using total reflectance measurements, and refining these measurements directly affects quantification of surface albedo and, therefore, surface energy balance (Ottaviani 2022; Ottaviani et al. 2024).

Multi‐Angular Observations in Support of Land Surface Retrievals

Generation of consistent, long‐duration time series of surface reflectances across missions requires minimizing sensor‐to‐sensor differences and effects to the best possible degree. Many artifacts manifest from differences in sensor viewing geometry, arising from variability in instrument design and orbit. Angular measurements from HARP2 and SPEXone allow characterization of the Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) of the land (and ocean) surface, describing angular effects on retrieved reflectances. Both instruments also provide opportunities to evaluate the Bidirectional Polarized Distribution Function (BPDF), which describes an aspect of canopy leaf orientation relating roughness and cover, important characteristics for describing atmospheric mixing and energy and CO2 transfer (Litvinov et al. 2010; Vanderbilt et al. 1985). The mission currently distributes BRDF and BPDF parameters over land from SPEXone using the Remote Sensing of Trace Gases and Aerosol Products (RemoTAP) approach (Hasekamp et al. 2024). Producing these observations alongside measurements of plant and ecosystem function builds on previous multi‐temporal approaches (Lyapustin et al. 2018; Schaaf et al. 2002) and will enable PACE to describe these effects directly as they relate to the corresponding geophysical variables, further constraining the uncertainty of these retrievals. Improvements to GPP retrievals using multi‐angular data have been proposed as well and could be achieved with a combination of PACE data, given its hyperspectral capabilities (Hilker et al. 2012). These measurements have not previously been made globally, nor on the timescale PACE will be able to provide them.

Atmospheric Science

PACE atmospheric science focuses on aspects of the sensor observations that confound their use for aquatic and terrestrial science, to wit, suspended particulate matter (aerosols), clouds, and trace gases, all of which are affected by and impact biological systems. The first EOs were made by the Television Infrared Observation Satellite (TIROS‐1), which observed clouds for weather forecasting in the 1960s (Kidder and Haar 2010, provide a brief history). Quantitative, systematic EOs of aerosol and cloud properties started with the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR), a multispectral passive imager first launched in 1979 (Holben et al. 1992; Nagaraja Rao et al. 1989), which was the key sensor in the creation of the first satellite cloud (Schiffer and Rossow 1985) and aerosol (Mishchenko et al. 2002) climatologies. Similarly, EOs of the trace gas ozone started in 1978 with the launch of the Total Ozone Mapping Spectrometer (TOMS).

AVHRR and its successors retrieved spectral aerosol optical depth (AOD), a unitless measure of light extinction due to aerosols in the vertical atmospheric column. Passive sensors infer AOD from the modification of light observed by the satellite sensor versus that expected given surface reflectance and atmospheric gas scattering and absorption. Conversely, active sensors, such as the Cloud‐Aerosol Lidar with Orthogonal Polarization (CALIOP) (Winker et al. 2009), measure the vertical profile of aerosol backscattering and depolarization. Other information indicating aerosol type is retrieved from such datasets, but their quality is limited by the fundamentally underdetermined nature of their retrieval space (Mishchenko et al. 2004). This uncertainty contributes to the ~50% spread in various estimates of radiative forcing (Li et al. 2022). Reducing measurement uncertainty requires observations with increased information content, such as those from multi‐angle polarimeters (Dubovik et al. 2019), to enable needed retrievals of aerosol absorption, size distribution, and refractive index.

EO retrievals of cloud properties provide key constraints on climate model radiative effects and feedbacks, which is particularly important given the difficulties in parameterizing their highly heterogeneous natures across comparatively spatially coarse model grid cells (Rossow 2024). Cloud properties that control these effects include opacity (optical thickness) and temperature, with droplet size also important to understand how clouds and aerosols interact. Nakajima and King (1990) popularized a bi‐spectral approach to simultaneously determine cloud optical thickness (COT; unitless) and cloud effective radius (CER; μm) using a pair of bands. One band is located spectrally where clouds are minimally absorbing (visible or NIR), with the other located where they are moderately absorbing (SWIR). Historically, cloud temperature has been determined using infrared measurements, but the hyperspectral capability of OCI is used to determine cloud top height, and thereby temperature, for PACE as described below.

Atmospheric Aerosols

Aerosols encompass a wide range of atmospheric constituents, including smoke from biomass burning, sea salt from breaking waves, sulfate aerosols from fossil fuel combustion, lofted desert dust, and more. They have many sources, some of which are anthropogenic, whereas others are naturally generated. The physical, chemical, and optical properties of aerosols are similarly variable, and their impact on ecosystems, weather, climate, and air quality is often regional and transient. Some are biogenic, created directly by plants (e.g., pollen) or indirectly by emitted gaseous precursors that condense to form aerosols in the atmosphere (L. Wang et al. 2024). For example, emissions of dimethylsulfide and other gases from phytoplankton condense to form aerosols over the ocean (reviewed in Ayers and Cainey 2007). Deposited aerosols impact ecosystems and, therefore, the carbon cycle (e.g., Mahowald et al. 2017). In the broadest sense, aerosols play an important yet poorly constrained role in the Earth system regardless of origin, by both direct impacts on radiative forcing and indirect impacts to cloud formation, properties, and lifetime (Bellouin et al. 2020; Zhou et al. 2021).

PACE observations continue the heritage observations of prior missions and provide novel retrievals of aerosol absorption, size distribution, and refractive index that facilitate the differentiation between the many aerosol types noted above. Heritage aerosol properties, such as AOD and mid‐visible fine mode fraction (FMF) over water surfaces, are currently produced by OCI using the Unified Aerosol Algorithm (UAA) (Remer, Davis, et al. 2019) (Figure 4). The novelty of this approach lies in its exploitation of OCI's full spectrum signal such that it combines two MODIS and VIIRS approaches (Hsu et al. 2013; Lee et al. 2024; Levy et al. 2013; Remer et al. 2005; Tanré et al. 1997), with OMI algorithms that retrieve UV optical thickness, Aerosol Index (UVAI), and single scattering albedo (Torres et al. 2013; Torres et al. 2018). By considering this extended spectral range, UAA is also able to provide additional absorption and aerosol layer height retrievals (Remer, Davis, et al. 2019).

Monthly aggregates of PACE aerosol data sets from July 2025. Grid cells without retrievals are shaded in grey. From top to bottom, rows indicate AOD, SSA, and FMF at 550 nm. From left to right, columns show results from OCI, HARP2, and SPEXone. These reveal the highly variable nature of the global aerosol system. Overall, OCI data are spatially smoothest as its wide swath acquires the largest number of samples over the month. SPEXone data are the least smooth because of its much narrower swath. OCI has sparser coverage for SSA as the UAA algorithm only retrieves this parameter under limited circumstances (high aerosol loadings) that are not often satisfied, whereas the polarimeters are able to retrieve it under a wider range of circumstances. The same is true for FMF, where UAA is restricted to over‐ocean scenes. Differences between the monthly aggregates reflect both these differences in sampling and specific instrument characterizations.

As previously indicated, several approaches available to HARP2 and SPEXone retrieve a suite of aerosol metrics, including single scattering albedo (SSA), FMF, size distribution, refractive index, layer height, and other microphysical properties and surface parameters simultaneously (Figure 4). The Fast Multi‐Angular Polarimetric Ocean coLor (FastMAPOL) algorithm also provides angular Rrs(λ), Chl, and wind speed over oceans (Gao et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2018) (Figure 2c,l). Aryal et al. (2024) build upon FastMAPOL by employing a more detailed bio‐optical model for coastal waters and an aerosol component approach that independently treats different aerosol types. RemoTAP also produces a suite of aerosol products from SPEXone over both land and oceans, together with BRDF and BPDF (Fu et al. 2020; Fu et al. 2025). Collectively, these combined aerosol‐ocean‐land retrievals, as well as those under consideration for future implementation (Chen et al. 2020; Dubovik et al. 2014; Stamnes et al. 2023) should facilitate the reduction of uncertainties in radiative forcing models.

Clouds

OCI extends many MODIS and VIIRS‐era heritage cloud data records with the bi‐spectral approach as implemented by the Cross‐platform HIgh resolution Multi‐instrument AtmosphEric Retrieval Algorithm (CHIMAERA) (Platnick et al. 2016; Wind et al. 2020). One primary advance of OCI for cloud property retrievals is its inclusion of SWIR bands centered near both 2.1 (MODIS‐like) and 2.2 μm (VIIRS‐like). This enables investigation of differences between MODIS and VIIRS CER retrievals that arise from the slight differential absorption of water (and hence photon penetration depth) at these wavelengths (Platnick et al. 2021). Having both also increases the ability to distinguish between liquid and ice‐phase clouds, as shown by Coddington et al. (2017), an approach in the queue for implementation at the time of writing.

Unlike MODIS and VIIRS, however, OCI lacks thermal infrared bands, which are used for retrieving cloud top height (CTH; km). To address this limitation, Sayer et al. (2023) developed the Cloud Height Retrieval from oxygen (O2) Molecular Absorption (CHROMA) approach, using hyperspectral OCI O2 A‐band measurements. Prior to PACE's launch, CHROMA was applied to ESA Ocean and Land Colour Instrument (OLCI) measurements and validated against ground‐based observations of CTH (Sayer et al. 2025). They found comparable performance overall to MODIS thermal retrievals, with MODIS tending to perform better in cases of larger temperature contrast (i.e., higher clouds) and the A‐band technique tending to perform better where pressure varies more rapidly with altitude (i.e., lower clouds). The development of this technique realized a second OCI‐adjacent advance in cloud property retrievals.

HARP2 maintains sensitivity to the optical properties of clouds in an alternate manner that relies on hyper‐angular polarimetry. By observing the characteristics of cloudbows, optical phenomena similar to rainbows but appearing in fog or clouds, the CER and cloud effective variance (CEV; dimensionless) can be determined for the upper portion of the cloud. This technique was pioneered with the POlarization and Directionality of Earth Reflectances (POLDER) instrument (Breon and Goloub 1998; Goloub et al. 2000; Parol et al. 2004; Waquet et al. 2009). Whereas POLDER retrievals required a 150 × 150 km spatial grid, the 60 view angles in HARP2's red channel allow CER and CEV determination on a 5.2 × 5.2 km grid, a first‐ever measurement made on this spatial scale. The algorithm used for PACE further traces its heritage to approaches for the airborne Research Scanning Polarimeter (RSP) (Alexandrov, Cairns, Emde, et al. 2012; Alexandrov, Cairns, and Mishchenko 2012; Alexandrov et al. 2015). Cloudbow observations provide a robust measure of cloud thermodynamic phase, and for ice clouds, aspect ratio and roughness can additionally be determined (van Diedenhoven 2018; van Diedenhoven et al. 2013; van Diedenhoven et al. 2012). The combination of polarimetric (HARP2) and spectral (OCI) cloud properties enables investigations on the characteristics of the long‐term MODIS‐to‐VIIRS‐to‐OCI spectral dataset, as both approaches have different sensitivity to three‐dimensional (3D) and vertical structure, such as Miller et al. (2018) and Rajapakshe and Zhang (2020).

Last, the hyper‐angular nature of HARP2 observations enables three‐dimensional (3D) cloud structure reconstruction via parallax (e.g., Alexandrov et al. 2021; Foley et al. 2024; Loveridge et al. 2023). Cloud structure provides an important control of, and response to, a changing Earth system. A 3D cloud product is under development that makes use of differentiable rendering (Mildenhall et al. 2021; Müller et al. 2022) (Figure 5). Angle‐resolved cloud masks have also been developed within the FastMAPOL suite of products, highlighting another benefit of the multi‐ and hyper‐angular capabilities of HARP2 and SPEXone (Gao et al. 2021).

Trace Gases

Although not originally part of the planned PACE data product suite (NASA 2025), Joiner et al. (2023) and Fasnacht et al. (2025) demonstrated that OCI's spectral resolution enables the quantification of trace gases, including nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and O3, despite the fact that OCI's spectral bands are significantly broader (5 nm FWHM) than those of spectrometers routinely used for trace gas retrieval (< 1‐nm), such as OMI and TROPOMI (Figure 6). A key advantage of OCI, however, is its finer spatial resolution relative to these spectrometers and reanalysis or forecast model data (e.g., Rienecker et al. 2011). At nadir, TROPOMI provides NO2 and O3 at approximately 5.5 × 3.5 km, whereas OMI's footprint is much coarser at roughly 12 × 24 km, whereas OCI measurements approach 1.2 × 1.2 km. These trace gases, together with aerosol particles, remain key factors influencing air quality, which affects both human health (Huang 2025) and ecosystem health and productivity (Lobell et al. 2022). Additionally, they are inputs to the atmospheric correction needed to generate high‐quality ocean and land surface reflectance data and further downstream derived products.

Comparison of total column NO2 retrievals from PACE OCI (left) and TROPOMI (right) over the Colstrip coal‐fired power plant in Montana, USA, for 2 days in September 2025. TROPOMI, launched in 2017, has provided the highest‐resolution global trace‐gas observations to date, but PACE OCI improves upon this capability with its finer spatial resolution. Because NO2 has a short atmospheric lifetime, day‐to‐day variability is pronounced, and plume dispersion is strongly influenced by wind direction. Although TROPOMI clearly detects elevated NO2 over the source region, the enhanced spatial resolution of PACE OCI more accurately resolves plume structure and direction, including the shift in plume orientation associated with changing winds between the 2 days.

Synergies Across Disciplines

The PACE instrument suite provides innumerable opportunities for the exploration of multidisciplinary synergies (Figure 7). Approaches for their investigation come in multiple forms. Some algorithms applied to individual instruments are inherently multidisciplinary. For example, FastMAPOL and RemoTAP simultaneously determine aerosol and ocean optical properties from HARP2 and SPEXone data, respectively (Gao et al. 2021; Hasekamp et al. 2024) (Figs. 2 and 4). These algorithms allow exploration of the relationship between multiple Earth system geophysical properties with data produced under uniform conditions. Internally consistent treatment of these complex interactions ensures a robust understanding of calibration, algorithm assumptions, and ancillary input influences on the output data.

The background is the OCI true color image, with a similar true color overlay from the coarser spatial resolution SPEXone. HARP2 is not imaged here, but its swath is indicated by the western swath edge marking at top left. The topmost plot in each box shows the OCI spectra in intensity (W m−2 sr−1 μm−2), with distinctly different features among the three pixels, such as the red edge over land. The middle plot shows HARP2 multi‐angle observations for intensity (purple) and Degree of Linear Polarization (DoLP; unitless), which is defined as the ratio of sum of the vertical and horizontal polarized signal to the total observed intensity. DoLP complements intensity measurements, as it reveals additional unique information on atmospheric and surface properties (Yan et al. 2020). The bottom plot shows SPEXone spectra, with varying colors of blue or green indicating how they change with view angle and polarization state.

PACE also provides observations that allow for multiple algorithms to be applied to an individual instrument or to coincident measurements from multiple instruments (Figure 7). We illustrate this throughout the collection of examples provided in Sections 2, 3, and 4. Looking toward a future with extended multi‐platform synergy (NRC 2018), developing strategies for co‐locating data streams and for conscientious assimilation of data product‐specific uncertainties and nuances will be essential for creating future fused products, both for same‐mission instruments and for multi‐mission dataset harmonization attempts.

On the first point, the PACE team eases intra‐mission comparisons by providing a file format (Level‐1C) that enables multi‐sensor fusion (Knobelspiesse et al. 2024). This format uses a uniform 5.2 × 5.2 km equal‐area grid, chosen to accommodate the coarser resolutions of HARP2 and SPEXone relative to OCI (Figure 1). This is the finest spatial resolution grid achievable for both polarimeters, and their science data products inherit this resolution. OCI's finer spatial capabilities allow its products to be delivered at its native resolution as well as on this grid. The Level‐1C format enables co‐registration of all three datasets, thus allowing direct comparison and analysis without additional regridding.

On the second point, the PACE team strives to provide other means beyond file formats to synergistically combine datasets, enabling multidisciplinary study. Bayesian inference has shown promise, where multi‐sensor data are combined as geophysical parameters rather than direct satellite observations. Thus far, this approach has been investigated for simultaneous retrieval of atmospheric and ocean surface properties, but in theory could be applied in a broader context (Ibrahim et al. 2022; Knobelspiesse et al. 2021). Regarding uncertainties, although not introduced in previous sections, the PACE team commits to producing and distributing standard (e.g., 1 − σ) uncertainties, which remain critical for the interpretation of all Earth system studies. Multiple data products previously introduced report their uncertainty (McKinna et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). The uncertainty propagation tools developed for PACE can also be applied retroactively to other EO datasets, thus enabling rigorous interpretation of long‐term EO data records.

Ultimately, PACE provides an endless reservoir for future multidisciplinary research. A short list of high‐level examples includes studies of aerosol influences (e.g., deposition) on ocean ecosystem biogeochemical cycles (Mahowald et al. 2017; Westberry et al. 2023), ocean biological and photochemical process effects on the atmosphere (Ayers and Cainey 2007), cloud formation brightening by the ocean (Mace et al. 2023; Vergara‐Temprado et al. 2018; Wilson et al. 2015), and the pan‐disciplinary effects of wildfires (Kramer et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2021; Y. Wang et al. 2022), to name only a few. As we underscore the promise of PACE, we conclude this section with a simple case study on the latter.

We used OCI and HARP2 data products to investigate the Eaton and Palisades fire that devastated communities in and near Los Angeles, California, in early January 2025 (Figure 8). OCI captured the affected areas through the difference in normalized burn ratio (dNBR) (Key and Benson 2006), a multispectral index that exploits how the NIR reflectance of vegetation changes when it is burned. dNBR provides a means of delineating burn scars and quantifying burn severity, thus offering a remotely‐sensed estimate of where the worst damage from a fire occurred (Wimberly and Reilly 2007). Although its spatial resolution exceeds that of instruments typically used for such analyses, OCI demonstrated its value through its daily, global coverage. Its rapid revisit time enabled analyses to be conducted quickly after the fires were extinguished, as the instrument captured the very first and all subsequent clear sky days after the fires ended.

Perspectives of the Los Angeles, California fires on 7 and 9 January 2025 from OCI and HARP2. Top row: OCI‐derived dNBR and burn severity in the Los Angeles region on 7 January. Far right: OCI “true color” imagery of smoke transport from the fires, as well as dust from Baja, California, on 9 January. Red and orange text and shapes indicate smoke and dust, respectively. Bottom row from left to right: HARP2 FastMAPOL retrievals of AOD, SSA, and FMF on 9 January. These indicate the aerosol composition, where smoke particles are more absorbing (lower single scattering albedo) and smaller (higher fine size mode fraction) relative to dust. For a larger analysis, additional pre‐fire data were acquired between 7 and 18 December 2024 and post‐fire data between 13 and 15 January 2025.

HARP2 provided insight into the characteristics and downwind transport of smoke from these fires. In this case, the smoke was transported offshore instead of toward populated areas, leading to worsened air quality (Holloway et al. 2021). FastMAPOL applied to HARP2 produced variables that differentiate smoke from other types of aerosols (Gao et al. 2021), namely SSA (a measure of light absorption) and FMF (a measure of size). These parameters enabled differentiation of smoke from the fires and dust blowing off the coast of Baja, California. Because of the temporal resolution of PACE, the motion of these plumes was tracked by the day. Ultimately, PACE captured these fires from multiple perspectives, providing insight into vegetation status, air quality, and aerosol transport. Additional data, such as synthetic aperture radar, could be brought into these analyses in the future to detail other post‐fire dangers such as landslide potential, extending the use of PACE for disaster response.

Synopsis and Concluding Perspectives

PACE represents NASA's latest flagship contribution to EO, building upon decades of heritage passive satellite radiometry from the U.S. and abroad. It extends critical multi‐decadal data records while introducing unprecedented capabilities through three complementary instruments: a wide‐swath, hyperspectral radiometer (OCI); a wide‐swath, multispectral, hyper‐angular polarimeter (HARP2); and a narrow‐swath, hyperspectral, multi‐angular polarimeter (SPEXone) (Figures 1 and 7). Together, these instruments enable revolutionary observations across aquatic, terrestrial, and atmospheric domains. Our review to this point highlighted the niches to be filled by PACE across a spectrum of Earth system science disciplines. In this final synopsis, we reiterate its potential once more but through the lens of the instruments—that is, a concluding synthesis of capabilities offered by global hyperspectral radiometry and multi‐angle polarimetry.

Global Hyperspectral Radiometry

OCI addresses fundamental spectral limitations of heritage ocean color sensors (e.g., SeaWiFS, MODIS, and VIIRS) that constrained aquatic ecosystem research. Its hyperspectral capabilities enable unprecedented characterization of phytoplankton community composition through detection of unique bio‐optical signatures. These advanced PCC retrievals support critical applications on daily, regional‐to‐global scales, including fisheries prediction, carbon export estimation, and HAB detection. Enhanced HAB detection capabilities have already proven successful for multiple species across a dynamic range of water. Furthermore, OCI's high sensitivity throughout the spectral range of chlorophyll fluorescence will enable improved evaluation of phytoplankton physiological status, whereas its UV measurements will facilitate improved characterization of CDOM and DOC, as well as detection of photoprotective compounds. These superior characterizations will improve tracking of organic matter through the aquatic continuum, which will, in turn, facilitate improved understanding of carbon cycles.

OCI provides the first global, daily hyperspectral observations over land. Although land surface products were not mission requirements, PACE extends legacy vegetation indices that are essential for maintaining the MODIS‐VIIRS‐era time series while also enabling production of advanced indices that could not be generated with the wavebands available on heritage sensors. The now‐available CIRE, mARI, CCI, and PRI indices, for example, characterize specific foliar biochemistry, revealing precise phenological transitions and ecosystem stress responses. OCI's spectral fidelity also enables direct daily, global GPP retrievals without requiring ancillary meteorological inputs, which additionally provides an independent validation source for terrestrial carbon cycle models.

OCI continues heritage aerosol particle and cloud data records while also offering several extended, alternative, or novel atmospheric property retrievals. Its current aerosol retrieval approach exploits the full observed spectrum by combining three heritage methods, which ultimately enables calculation of additional microphysical aerosol properties. Although OCI currently uses a heritage approach for cloud optical thickness and effective radius retrievals, its 2.1 and 2.2 μm bands enable novel investigation of MODIS‐VIIRS differences. A newly developed algorithm addresses OCI's lack of thermal infrared bands by using O2 A‐band measurements for CTH retrievals. Finally, OCI's 5 nm spectral resolution was unexpectedly shown to allow accurate trace gas retrievals despite this resolution being broader than those from dedicated trace gas spectrometers. Individually and in combination, this suite of atmospheric data records provides unprecedented support for air quality applications, climate and radiative forcings research, and improved ocean color atmospheric correction.

Global Multi‐Angle Polarimetry

Spaceborne multi‐angle polarimetry enables comprehensive aerosol and cloud characterization, while additionally providing emerging opportunities for aquatic and terrestrial science. The former will unquestionably facilitate the reduction of uncertainties in climate and radiative forcing models while additionally supporting studies on aerosol‐ocean relationships. The coupled ocean–atmosphere models applied to HARP2 and SPEXone, for example, have already proven successful at retrieving metrics of aerosol absorption, size distribution, and refractive index on daily, global scales, as well as surface properties of both land and ocean. Moreover, HARP2's hyper‐angular observations of cloudbows allow calculation of unique cloud microphysical properties, such as ice crystal aspect ratios and roughness, on spatial scales that realize a 30‐fold improvement over heritage capabilities. These hyper‐angular measurements also enable 3D cloud reconstruction to support studies on radiation and energy balance, cloud‐aerosol interactions, water cycle and precipitation, and carbon cycle and ecosystem productivity.

Spaceborne multi‐angle polarimetry has the potential to catalyze a renaissance in studies of Earth's surface. As mentioned, the joint retrieval algorithms applied to HARP2 and SPEXone provide coupled ocean–atmosphere products with enhanced information content and potential for new product generation, such as angular Rrs(λ). The improved fidelity of the aerosol retrievals alone could additionally revolutionize traditional OCI ocean color atmospheric correction, particularly in complex atmospheric environments. Retrievals of polarized, angular surface effects, such as BRDF and BPDF, enable unprecedented characterization of the geometric light field to reveal novel hydrosol and particle properties, as well as surface structure properties over vegetation, snow, and ice. Ultimately, frontiers for new research and applications span the aquatic, terrestrial, and atmospheric realms, making the PACE polarimeters one of the most exciting recent contributions to Earth system science from space.

Significance and Future Outlook

As PACE passes its 2‐year launch anniversary, its place atop the contemporary constellation of EO satellites has crystallized. This mission exemplifies how technological advancement—specifically the marriage of hyperspectral and polarimetric measurements with computational innovations—enables transformative Earth system science. By simultaneously observing coupled ocean‐land‐atmosphere processes with unprecedented spectral, angular, and temporal resolution, PACE addresses critical uncertainties in climate forcing, ecosystem functioning, and biogeochemical cycling, and provides new resources to support socioeconomic, food security, and air quality applications. The mission establishes new paradigms for integrated EO while maintaining essential climate data records, positioning the community to address emerging science questions about our changing planet.
