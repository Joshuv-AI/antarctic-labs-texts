Evaluation of the utility of slow mode for pulmonary vessel resection using a surgical stapler in anatomical pulmonary resection: a prospective randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Ryo Karita, Terunaga Inage, Kohei Takahashi, Takeshi Sugawara, Hidemi Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraoperative bleeding from the pulmonary vessel stumps is a well-known complication of anatomical lung resection performed with surgical staplers. While advancements in stapling devices have improved safety, staple line bleeding (SLB) remains a serious concern as it can prolong operative time and increase complications risk. The SigniaTM stapling system features both adaptive firing technology plus mode (AFTM) and manual slow mode (MSM), allowing for adjustable staple firing speed. A retrospective study has suggested that MSM could minimize SLB risk; however, these findings lack confirmation from prospective studies. This study is aimed to investigate the utility of MSM for SLB prospectively.MethodsA total of 140 patients will be randomized into two groups. One group will undergo pulmonary vessel resection using AFTM and the other using MSM. The primary endpoint is intraoperative observation of SLB on pulmonary vessel stumps. Data analysis is scheduled for the period from 2025 to 2027.DiscussionThis trial investigates whether MSM lowers the risk of SLB from pulmonary vessels and reduce the need for additional hemostatic procedures. If a slower firing speed is proven to minimize intraoperative SLB, selecting MSM could significantly improve the safety and efficiency of thoracic surgery. This could reduce complications, decrease the need for additional hemostatic interventions, and lower overall medical resource consumption.Trial RegistrationThis trial was registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/re/reports/detail/91923).

Introduction

Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with surgery as the standard for early-stage lung cancer. A large-scale Japanese registry study has demonstrated high 5-year overall survival rates following surgery, ranging from 97.0% for stage 0 to 58.1% for stage IIB (1). In these thoracic procedures, surgical staplers are indispensable tools for the ligation and transection of pulmonary vessels (2,3). The SigniaTM Stapling System is one such widely adopted device, featuring adaptive firing technology plus mode (AFTM), which automatically adjusts firing speed based on tissue thickness, and manual slow mode (MSM), which allows surgeons to manually reduce the firing speed (4,5). AFTM firing speed is variable depending on tissue thickness In MSM mode, the stapler was set to the device’s specific low speed firing mode (4-6). This mode ensures a mechanically standardized and constant firing speed, regardless of the surgeon’s manual operation and tissue thickness. Additionally, the procedure requires a switching operation on the device handle, which introduces an obligatory 3–5 seconds pause from clamping vessels to firing.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Despite the convenience of stapling, staple line bleeding (SLB) remains a significant complication (4). Given that hemorrhage from pulmonary vessels can be life-threatening, meticulous management is paramount (7-9). While a retrospective study by Suzuki et al. suggested that using MSM during pulmonary artery (PA) resection could reduce stump hemorrhage (4), there is a lack of high-level evidence from prospective trials directly comparing MSM to AFTM. We hypothesize that a slower staple firing speed facilitates more precise and consistent staple formation (e.g., the ideal “B-shape”), thereby minimizing the risk of SLB. Currently, however, the clinical impact of specifically adjusting firing speeds during pulmonary vessel closure remains poorly understood.

Objective

This prospective randomized controlled trial aims to investigate whether a slower firing speed setting during lobectomy or segmentectomy reduces the risk of SLB and the subsequent need for additional hemostatic procedures compared to AFTM. By clarifying this, we seek to establish a technique that reduces perioperative complications, limits the use of costly hemostatic agents like blood coagulation factor products, and ultimately optimizes medical resource consumption in thoracic surgery. We present this article in accordance with the SPIRIT reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0041/rc).

A total of 140 patients will be included in this study, with the recruitment planned from November 2025 to March 2027 at department of general thoracic surgery, Chiba University Hospital. The presence or absence of intraoperative SLB will be compared between the MSM and AFTM groups. According to a result from a previous report (4), the expected intraoperative SLB rates in the MSM was 10% and AFTM groups was 30%. With a significance level of 5%, and a power of 80%, a sample size was calculated to be 62 patients per group, with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Accounting for potential ineligibility cases and dropouts, a total of 140 patients (70 per group) will be enrolled.

Patients will be randomized preoperatively to each group. The allocation is based on the firing mode of surgical stapling device: MSM versus AFTM. Randomization will be performed at a 1:1 ratio using central registration at an independent data center to minimize selection bias. Sex and surgical approach will be used as stratification factors in randomization, as they may relate to potential confounding variables. Pulmonary function, smoking history and general frailty could affect vessel fragility, and the mechanical injury during stapling may vary by procedure. Groups A and B will be allocated based on the aforementioned factors. The following firing modes are used in each group: group A: MSM; group B: AFTM.

The SigniaTM Small Diameter Reload (SDR), which is a vascular stapler from COVIDIEN, and will be used in both groups with a white 30 mm cartridge.

The study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol has been approved by the Chiba University Certified Clinical Research Review Board (No. CRB0117-25) and registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT1032250468). Investigators will explain the trial to all patients and obtain written informed consent from each participant, including consent for the use of their data in study publications. The initial protocol version was approved on March 8, 2025, and patient recruitment started in November 2025. The latest version (1.1) was approved on October 5, 2025.

Procedure

Intraoperative findings

We will assess operative time, blood loss, the presence or absence of SLB, and the hemostatic methods used, such as direct pressure, electrocautery, topical hemostatic agents, and additional ligation or clipping, or additional suturing. Cases will be considered ineligible if intraoperative judgement determines that an automatic suturing device is not required for pulmonary vessel resection, leading to a change from the planned procedure such that anatomical pulmonary resection is not performed.

After pulmonary vessel closure, we will observe for SLB. When bleeding occurs, the hemostatic procedure and the time required to achieve hemostasis (in minutes) will be recorded. The criteria for discontinuation of individual cases are as follows:

To ensure reproducibility across institutions, the MSM protocol was standardized as follows. Vessel clamping undergoes using the standard technique such as AFTM. Immediately after clamping, the surgeon manually switches the device setting from ATFM to MSM via the handle interface. Then, the stapler is fired. In MSM mode, the device automatically maintains a constant slow blade advancement speed, regardless of tissue resistance or surgeon manipulation. This protocol eliminates subjective variations in manual speed, ensuring a consistent mechanical interaction with the tissue.

Postoperative findings

Postoperative assessments will evaluate the occurrence of complications and adverse events. Since this study involves a comparison within the scope of standard of care, no specialized clinical trial insurance has been purchased. In the event of any complications, the best possible medical treatment will be provided under the patient’s standard health insurance.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is the presence of intraoperative SLB from the pulmonary vessel stumps. SLB will be assessed using a modified Likert scale (11), with the following grades: grade 1, no SLB at the tissue site after initial blotting of the staple line; grade 2, SLB that stops within 5 seconds; no intervention needed; grade 3, progressive SLB that continues after 5 seconds, requiring compression; grade 4, SLB requiring mild intervention, such as electrocautery, topical hemostatic agents; and grade 5, significant SLB requiring major intervention, such as ligation with clips or sutures, or additional sutures. A 5-second cutoff value was established with reference to previous reports (4).

For all pulmonary vessels closed with staplers, the degree of bleeding will be evaluated using a scoring system, and the highest grade observed per same case will be recorded. A maximum grade of two or less will be judged as no SLB, while a maximum grade of three or more will be judged as SLB. When SLB occurs, the hemostatic procedure will be documented. Pulmonary vessels resected different from the assigned study setting will be excluded from this analysis.

The secondary endpoints are as follows: (I) modified Likert scale grade for all pulmonary vessels resected with a stapler in the same case; (II) hemostatic method employed; (III) intraoperative blood loss volume; (IV) number of surgical staplers used for pulmonary vessel resection; (V) operating surgeon; and (VI) final surgical procedure.

The exploratory variables are as follows: (I) clinical diagnosis (primary lung cancer, metastasis, benign tumor, infectious disease); (II) pathological diagnosis, pathological stage (Union for International Cancer Control 9th edition); (III) comorbidity; (IV) history antiplatelet or anticoagulant use; (V) surgical approach (complete thoracoscopic surgery, thoracoscopic-assisted surgery, open thoracotomy); (VI) operation time (min); (VII) pulmonary vessels resection time (min); (VIII) presence of postoperative complications and their Clavien-Dindo classification grade; (IX) pulmonary function tests; and (X) maximum preoperative computed tomography (CT) of the pulmonary vessels resected with stapler.

Data collection and management

The study schema is shown in Figure 1. Patient registration will be conducted via a central registration system at the Clinical Research Data Center (Department of Clinical Trials, Chiba University Hospital). From 90 days prior to surgery until the day before surgery, when the patients are considered eligible for enrollment, a screening test will be performed after obtaining consent. If eligible to participate, the patients will be enrolled before the day of surgery and will be assigned clinical trial subject numbers. The registration must be completed by the day before the scheduled surgery. The patients will be assigned to the MSM group or the AFTM group according to sex and the planned surgical approach. These factors are selected because vascular fragility is thought to be influenced by sex, and the surgical approach can affect the degree of mechanical stress exerted on the vessels during stapling (6). Random allocation will be performed at a 1:1 ratio with central registration at an independent data center to minimize bias. In the assigned group, surgical stapler will be used to resect pulmonary vessels with the assigned mode. Then, the presence or absence of SLB will be assessed by three thoracic surgeons participating in the surgery. If SLB occurs, the degree of bleeding will be graded according to a modified Likert scale for every vessel resected by the stapler. If participation in the study is terminated for any reason, the test results will be retained to the greatest extent possible.

Upon completion of the trial, the results will be disseminated promptly after taking all necessary measures to protect the human rights of the participants and the rights and interests of the investigators. The methods of dissemination will include presentations at scientific conferences, publication in peer-reviewed journals, and registration in public databases. Specifically, the study protocol, statistical analysis plan, and summary reports will be made available through jRCT.

Statistical analysis

For the primary endpoint, proportions will be calculated and group comparisons will be performed using the Chi-squared test. Secondary endpoints will be analyzed without multiplicity adjustments. For each secondary endpoint, group comparisons will be conducted using the t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The safety endpoint is the frequency of adverse events, for which we will record the frequency and proportion of adverse events, and calculate frequencies and proportions, and generate a listing. All comparisons are planned; P values are two-sided, and the statistical significance level is set at P<0.05. All statistical analyses will be performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS).

Quality control

An independent data-monitoring committee (IDMC) was established for this study. The committee will be responsible for protecting the rights of participants, ensuring the study is conducted according with the protocol, and verifying the accurate collection of data. The IDMC will perform safety monitoring, including comparative analysis of adverse events incidence and detailed review of serious adverse events, as necessary, to ensure patient safety. Based on its findings, the committee may recommend study designs modifications, such as changes in the inclusion criteria, to mitigate risk, or advice on whether to continue the study. The principal investigator will decide to discontinue the study if continuation is deemed untenable due to unforeseen adverse events, illnesses, or other factors.

Discussion

Intraoperative bleeding during lung resection is primarily caused by surgical manipulation. Minimally invasive approaches, such as robot-assisted and thoracoscopic surgeries, have become mainstream and offer significant benefits, including shorter hospital stays and reduced postoperative pain, compared with conventional open thoracotomy (12). However, intraoperative bleeding remains one of the most serious complications. Its incidence during thoracoscopic surgery ranges from 4.11% to 10.83% (13), making it challenging to avoid entirely. This complication is associated with prolonged operative time, higher transfusion rates, increased conversion to thoracotomy, extended hospital stay, and greater overall morbidity (9,13). Technical factors, such as vessel tension or torsion during resection, can cause intraoperative bleeding (14). Given these factors, we designated surgical approach as a stratification variable to adjust for potential imbalance between the groups.

SLB from the PA occurs in at least 4.2% of cases (3). This complication can result from various factors, including stapler malfunction, ligature slippage, and unintentional injury during dissection (4,9). A B-shape staple formation is considered the gold standard for safe stapling (5,15,16). In other surgical contexts, such as gastric and tracheal resections, a continuous slow firing speed has been shown to produce a more favorable formation than normal firing modes (5,16). While the benefit of MSM for B-shape staple formation has been reported in thick and hard tissues such as the stomach and trachea, its utility in thin and fragile tissues like PA remains unclear. PA is thinner than the pulmonary vein, which has a thicker adventitia, despite the PA being an arterial vessel subject to higher pressure. We consider this theoretical advantage in MSM mode to be particularly relevant under thoracoscopic conditions. Narrow working spaces and restricted maneuverability can increase the risk of misalignment and mechanical stress. We believe that the deliberate use of MSM helps mitigate this risk by ensuring stable, gradual compression and knife advancement. In studies of SLB, direct comparisons of incidence rates have been difficult because previous research has included various types of dissected arteries, staplers, and definitions (3,14,17-19). Since SLB is suggested to be influenced by mechanical stress (6), we used the surgical approach as a stratification factor to mitigate its confounding effect and minimize its impact on the study results. We acknowledge limitations regarding the potential bias in the objectivity of bleeding assessment. Therefore, we used a modified Likert scale as the objective measurement method for this study. Despite the measurement, this study is unblinded due to the nature of the intervention. Surgeons are aware of the stapler firing mode. This introduces a risk of observer bias, as surgeons believing in the superiority of MSM might subconsciously grade bleeding more leniently. Moreover, the assessment of SLB remains surgeon dependent. Although we standardized the definition based on the necessity of manual compression to improve objectivity, some degree of subjective judgment in deciding whether to compress is unavoidable.

Rapid and effective hemostasis is crucial for managing unexpected intraoperative bleeding. Standard management varies with severity and includes methods such as direct pressure, electrocautery, additional suturing, or the application of blood coagulation factor agents. If adjusting surgical stapler firing speed proves effective in reducing SLB risk, this simple, modifiable technique would enhance the safety of thoracic surgery and provide significant healthcare economic benefit.

Regarding feasibility, the necessary research environment is well established at Chiba University Hospital, as demonstrated by our prior successful completion of a prospective trial evaluating stapler reinforcement agents for intraoperative air leakage (10). Although this is a single-center study, it represents the first prospective randomized controlled trial to evaluate the impact of stapler firing speed on SLB. The standardized assessment criteria and the rigor of the protocol established here will serve as a critical foundation for future multi-center large-scale trials.
