Study on the molecular mechanism of colony strength of Apis cerana cerana based on transcriptomics
Authors: Lina Guo, Xiaoyin Deng, Ke Sun, Yu Zhang, Kai Xu, Yuan Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApis cerana cerana exhibits highly sensitive olfactory capabilities, resistance to cold and heat, and strong foraging abilities. It plays a critical role in sustaining ecosystem stability and enhancing agricultural productivity and economic yields of farmers in mountainous areas. Given that colony strength is fundamental to the reproductive capacity and productivity of Apis cerana cerana, maintaining strong colonies is essential for enhancing their disease resistance, survival, and productivity. In this study, Apis cerana cerana in Zizhou County of the Lvliang Mountains was used as the research object. To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying colony strength differences, we performed transcriptome sequencing on the head and thorax of worker bees from strong and weak colonies, with the goal of identifying differentially expressed genes and regulatory pathways associated with sensory perception in the head and energy metabolism in the thorax.The results showed that 751 differentially expressed genes were identified between the heads of strong and weak colonies, primarily enriched in odorant-binding protein, royal jelly major protein, taste receptor gene, olfactory receptor family genes, and others. A total of 852 differentially expressed genes were identified between the thorax of different colonies, mainly enriched in cytochrome P450, zinc finger protein, serine / threonine kinase, and cytochrome C oxidase family genes. Gene function enrichment analysis revealed that the strength of colony is associated with growth and immune response pathways, such as macromolecular biosynthesis, organic nitrogen biosynthesis, and ribosome metabolism in the head of bees, as well as energy metabolism pathways, including sugar metabolism, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism in the thorax of bees, which have been previously associated with extended lifespan in other insect models, suggesting a potential link to individual longevity in strong colonies. The strength of the colony is significantly related to energy metabolism, immunity, heat stress, and phosphorylation regulation, Worker bees in strong colony exhibit stronger antioxidative stress, energy metabolism, resistance to external environmental temperature and harmful substances, and anti-inflammatory and antibacterial capabilities.Keywords: Lvliang Mountains, Transcriptomics, Colony strength, Apis cerana cerana

Introduction

Apis cerana cerana exhibits a highly sensitive olfactory ability, making it the dominant pollinating insect in China. The Apis cerana cerana exhibits remarkable tolerance to both cold and heat, displays exceptional foraging capabilities, and is widely distributed. As a keystone species in natural ecosystems, Apis cerana cerana plays a pivotal role in maintaining ecological balance and significantly contributes to enhanced agricultural productivity [1, 2]. The Lvliang Mountains span approximately 400 km from north to south and has a width ranging from 80 km to 120 km from east to west, key nectar plants such as locust flowers, jujube flowers, sunflowers, Vitex negundo, Liudaomu, and apple trees provide rich foraging resources for bees. Therefore, the Lvliang mountains are also one of the regions in Northern China with abundant genetic resources of Apis cerana cerana [3].

Compared with Apis cerana cerana from southern of China, the Apis cerana cerana in the Lvliang Mountains exhibit a larger body size and demonstrate higher cold tolerance [4]. This subspecies has the characteristics of relatively small colony size and a docile temperament, with strong adaptability. It can maintain the population of more than 7 combs during the honey production period, but it also has great weaknesses, such as poor resistance to nest worms, easy infection of Apis cerana cerana cystic larvae (sacbrood) and larvae rot disease. The colony strength of Apis cerana cerana is influenced by multiple factors, including regional variations, environmental conditions, temperature fluctuations, intrinsic colony characteristics, genetic diversity, and nectar source quality, which collectively contribute to significant inter-colony variability [5]. Notably, current research demonstrates that worker bee traits, particularly cold tolerance and disease resistance, play a pivotal role in determining colony strength within apiaries [6]. It was observed that the supercooling point and freezing point of the worker bees in the strong colony were lower than those in the weak colony during various overwintering periods. This suggests that the strong colony exhibited enhanced cold resistance, material activity and energy reserve. Consequently, it was able to effectively regulate the nest temperature, ensuring the rapid reproduction of the following spring and high collection efficiency during the summer production period [7].

In addition to the change of ambient temperature, the occurrence of disease may directly lead to the rapid decline of bee colonies. For instance, the cystic larval disease of Apis cerana cerana can infect larvae aged 1–3 days, thereby inhibiting their pupation. This disruption in the developmental process subsequently leads to a reduction in the adult bee population within the colony, ultimately resulting in a rapid decline in the overall population [8]. Metabolic disorders in honeybees can be triggered by ingesting pathogen-contaminated pollen during foraging, leading to compromised colony fitness. A notable example is the susceptibility of honeybees to jujube witches’ broom (JWB, a phytoplasma-associated pathology in Z.jujuba) following pollen ingestion from infected host plants. In severe cases, the number of foraging honeybees can be reduced by 30% -50%, which directly leads to a sharp decline in colony population and productivity [9]. In apicultural production, colony strength is a fundamental determinant of survival under environmental stressors and directly influences reproductive success and apicultural yield. High-performance colonies exhibit enhanced productivity with optimal honey and pollen collection capacity, as well as superior environmental adaptability and stress tolerance. Conversely, weak colonies demonstrate compromised foraging efficiency, reduced winter survival rates, heightened disease susceptibility, and increased vulnerability to predation, potentially leading to colony collapse.

Maintaining colony strength has become a critical prerequisite for enhancing disease resilience, colony viability, and apicultural productivity in honeybees. Consequently, optimizing colony performance constitutes a fundamental objective in Apis cerana cerana breeding and management programs [10]. The advancement of technology in genetic diversity research has transitioned from classical genetics to contemporary genomics, encompassing various stages of technological development and application. The degree of genomic variability is the most straightforward indicator of this diversity [11].

Compared with Apis mellifera, Apis cerana cerana exhibits distinct colony development characteristics, with significant variations observed even among colonies within the same apiary [12]. Transcriptomics plays a crucial role in honeybee research, elucidating key biological processes such as the antioxidant system, heat shock protein expression, cold tolerance-related genes and pesticide resistance mechanisms. Transcriptomic studies on queens and workers of Apis cerana cerana revealed that differentially expressed genes were associated with cytochrome P450 genes, waxy protein genes, oxidoreductases, mitochondrial genes and transporters, as well as antennae-specific olfactory receptor genes [13]. Further transcriptomic analysis of Apis mellifera identified genes related to brain learning and memory as well as feeding behavior, demonstrating that age significantly affects learning and memory capacity, while neural mechanisms involved in feeding play crucial roles in maintaining colony stability and royal jelly production [14]. Additionally, two gene families (Glutaredoxins and Thioredoxins) were found to potentially play important roles in antioxidant defense during various developmental stages of Apis cerana cerana [15].

Currently, there has been no reported identification of the genetic resources (germplasm) of Apis cerana cerana in the Lvliang Mountains. By accurately identifying and breeding these unique populations, the stable inheritance of their exceptional characteristics can be enhanced the stable inheritance of the exceptional characteristics within the population of Apis cerana cerana in the Lvliang Mountain area. This approach could enhance the economic viability and sustainable development of apiculture in the region, while contributing to increased farmer incomes and rural revitalization efforts.

Materials and methods

Test insects

The experimental bee colonies were sourced from beekeepers in Zizhou County (38°01’06” N, 110°03’52” E), located within the Lvliang Mountains. Sample collection was conducted in May 2023. Based on the reproductive characteristics of Apis cerana cerana in the Lvliang Mountains and in accordance with the local Beijing standard “Bee Breeding and Application Quality Control Specification,” as well as conventional beekeeping practices, colonies with 8 combs were classified as strong groups (S), while those with 4–5 combs were classified as weak groups (W). Although no formal power analysis was performed, n = 3 biological replicates per group aligns with common practice in honeybee transcriptomic studies, where three replicates per condition provide adequate power for differential expression analysis( e.g., DESeq2). To ensure colony health, all selected colonies were visually inspected for overall health and only those without sacbrood, chalkbrood, or heavy Varroa infestation and with normal brood patterns were included. Three colonies were selected per category (strong, n = 3; weak, n = 3). From each colony, 30 adult forager bees were randomly collected at the hive entrance between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM. For each colony, the heads and thoraces of all 30 bees were dissected on ice and pooled separately: all 30 heads into one head sample and all 30 thoraces into one thorax sample. This yielded six samples per colony (2 tissues × 3 colonies) and a total of 12 samples across all colonies. Each pooled sample was treated as one biological replicate.

RNA extraction and quality inspection

Total RNA was extracted from each of the 12 pooled samples (either head or thorax from a singlecolony) using Trizol reagent. Specifically, for each pooled sample (comprising 30 heads or 30 thoraces from one colony), tissue was homogenized in 1 mL Trizol reagent with a tissue grinder (60 Hz, 4 min). The extraction procedure included the following steps: homogenization of tissue samples (60 Hz, 4 min), followed by incubation for lysis, lysis, addition of chloroform and centrifugation to separate the aqueous phase, precipitation of the RNA with pellet with 75% ethanol, and final dissolution in RNase-free water. The concentration and purity (A260 / A280 ratio) of the RNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was determined using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. All 12 RNA samples had RIN values above 8.0 (mean ± SD: 8.6 ± 0.3). The qualified RNA samples were stored at-80 ℃ for subsequent use. Throughout the entire process, an RNase-free environment was strictly maintained to ensure RNA quality.

cDNA library construction and transcriptome sequencing

The samples were sent to Beijing Novogene Co., Ltd., where the cDNA library was constructed using the Illumina platform and transcriptome sequencing was performed. Initially, mRNA was enriched by Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, followed by random fragmentation and synthesis of double-stranded cDNA. After purification, the cDNA fragments underwent end repair, A-tail addition, and adapter ligation. The library was completed through fragment size selection and PCR amplification. Subsequently, the concentration of the library (> 1 ng / µL) was measured using the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer, and the size of the inserted fragments was assessed using the Qsep400 system.After passing quality control (Q30 > 80%), the library was subjected to paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA).

Quality control and comparison of sequencing data

The raw data obtained by Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencing need to be filtered through strict quality control to generate high-quality clean data. This includes the removal of reads containing adapters, reads with N ratio > 10%, and reads where the proportion of bases with a quality score Q ≤ 10 accounted for ≥ 50%. Subsequently, the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome to determine their source genes and to ensure the accuracy of subsequent transcriptome analysis. The reference genome selected is GenBank accession number is GCA _ 001442555.1, named ACSNU-2.0.

Data analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.32.0) with thresholds of |log₂(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05. Normalization was performed with the median-of-ratios method to account for sequencing depth and RNA composition. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure, with padj for significance. To assess batch effects, library preparation and sequencing of head and thorax samples were performed separately; PCA of variance-stabilized counts showed negligible batch effects (Fig. 1). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted with the goseq package (v1.44.0), which corrects for gene length bias. KEGG annotation used KEGG.db (v3.2.4). GO terms and KEGG pathways with padj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Excel was used for result organization.

Intersample correlation

To compare gene expression levels across individuals within each group, we quantified transcript abundance using Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments (FPKM). Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed based on the FPKM values of all samples (Fig. 1). The results revealed significant inter-group variation, while intra-group replicates showed strong reproducibility, supporting further downstream analyses.

Differentially expression gene screening

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with the threshold criteria of |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05. In this study, a total of 751 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the SH and WH groups, including 457 up-regulated genes and 294 down-regulated genes that were specifically highly expressed in SH group (Fig. 2A). Additionally, 852 DEGs were identified between the ST and WT, including 371 up-regulated genes and 481 down-regulated genes that were specifically expressed in the SH group (Fig. 2B). The large number of DEGs between these groups indicates that the specific expression of head and thorax genes in Apis cerana cerana is related to the differences between strong and weak colonies.

Discussion

The strength of a honeybee colony is closely linked to the flight and foraging abilities of individual bees. The head serves as the primary organ for perception and feeding; its antennae possess highly sensitive olfactory functions that detect floral scents, pheromones, and intruders [16]. This enhances foraging efficiency and risk avoidance, ultimately contributing to colony stability and prosperity. The thorax, as the center of locomotion, directly influences flight capability and energy expenditure. Robust thoracic muscles improve flight efficiency, reduce energy consumption, and extend foraging duration, thereby increasing honey production and resource reserves [17]. Given the critical roles of the head and thorax in honeybee biology, this study performed transcriptomic analyses on these two body parts in Apis cerana cerana. By identifying differentially expressed genes and performing GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, we aimed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Olfaction is essential for insect survival and reproduction, enabling detection of chemical signals, food location, mating, defense, and communication [18]. Insect odorant-binding proteins (OBPs), including pheromone-binding proteins (PBP) and general odorant-binding proteins (GOBP), play key roles in odor perception; over 177 OBPs have been identified in bees [19, 20]. Olfactory receptors (ORs) detect volatile substances, aid defense and mating [21], while gustatory receptors (GRs), first identified in Drosophila, are distributed in antennae, mouthparts, and forelegs, helping insects identify food and companions [22]. The major royal jelly proteins (MRJPs), synthesized in worker bee hypopharyngeal glands, nourish queens and larvae and contain proteins, amino acids, fatty acids, sugars, vitamins, acetylcholine, and insulin [23]. Stronger colonies with more royal jelly-producing bees secrete more royal jelly, increasing production and resource reserves [24].

Ten major royal jelly proteins (MRJPs 1–10) have been identified in honeybees [25]. In this study, MRJPs 1–5 were detected in the heads of worker bees. MRJPs provide essential nitrogen for larval growth, with MRJP3 rich in available nitrogen, supporting rapid larval development and queen fertility. Notably, MRJP1 has antibacterial properties that enhance bee immunity during foraging, contributing to strong colony development [26].

Zinc finger proteins (ZFPs) are key regulators of gene expression and play roles in honeybee heat stress adaptation by participating in metabolism, protein folding, and degradation [27, 28]. Serine/threonine kinases (STKs) also contribute to stress tolerance, with genes implicated in responses to cold and heat [29]. The fam20c-like extracellular STK further regulates cell proliferation, growth, and metabolism [30]. Cytochrome c oxidase subunits are essential for cellular respiration and ATP synthesis, as well as energy metabolism and phosphorylation regulation [31–33].

In this study, OBPs, taste receptors (TRs), olfactory receptors (ORs), and MRJP genes were screened from the heads of Apis cerana cerana, highlighting their roles in odor perception, defense, mating, oviposition, antibacterial activity, growth, fecundity, and immunity. In the thorax, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) included ZFPs, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and cytochrome c oxidase, indicating involvement in heat stress response and energy metabolism. GO analysis divided DEGs into molecular function, cellular component, and biological process categories, with biological processes showing the most DEGs, suggesting a pivotal role in colony performance. Consequently, metabolic pathways related to biological processes were further analyzed.

The head of the honeybee is a complex and vital part of its body structure, performing a variety of key functions. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that the functions related to the head were mainly enriched in the macromolecular biosynthesis and organic nitrogen compound biosynthesis pathways. From a biological perspective, macromolecular biosynthesis is a key metabolic pathway for the synthesis of proteins, nucleic acids, and other macromolecules in cells [34], and these macromolecules play a critical role in the energy metabolism and immune response of honeybees. The synthesis of macromolecules requires substantial energy, and normal energy metabolism can effectively support these synthesis processes, thereby maintaining daily activities and reproduction, and enhancing honeybee health [35]. Organic nitrogen compounds are important nitrogen-containing bioactive substances in honeybees, involved in various physiological functions such as energy metabolism, signal transduction, and immune response. The feces produced by honeybees during foraging are rich in organic nitrogen, which can be converted into inorganic nitrogen after decomposition, providing important nutrients for plants. This process offers adaptive benefits for plant growth, as nitrogen is a key limiting nutrient for plants [36]. Studies have shown that female beewolves (Philanthus triangulum) apply a secretion containing symbiotic Streptomyces bacteria to their brood cells; these bacteria produce nitric oxide (NO) and antibiotic compounds that protect the larvae from fungal pathogens. Nitric oxide serves as a key defense mechanism in bees, enhancing larval survival, colony reproduction, and population growth potential [37]. The thorax is the primary energy-consuming organ, especially during flight, relying on glucose and fatty acid metabolism. Bees convert body fat into glycogen, most of which is distributed in the thorax. This study found that thorax-related metabolic pathways were mainly enriched in glucose metabolism (redox, carbohydrate derivative, carbohydrate, nucleoside phosphate, and transmembrane transport pathways), lipid metabolism (ATP metabolism and biosynthesis), amino acid metabolism (amino sugar and purine nucleotide metabolism), and other metabolic pathways.

Redox reactions produce NADPH and NADH for biosynthesis and antioxidant responses. For example, the pentose phosphate pathway generates NADPH for lipid/amino acid synthesis and glutathione maintenance. Carbohydrate metabolism (glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis/degradation) breaks down glucose for energy; glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate, which enters the TCA cycle to produce ATP. Nucleoside phosphate metabolism involves nucleotide synthesis/degradation for nucleic acid synthesis. Ribose-5-phosphate from the pentose phosphate pathway is a nucleotide precursor, and NTPs (ATP, GTP) serve as energy sources and signaling molecules. Transmembrane transport facilitates glucose uptake. Amino acids enhance glucose oxidase activity, improving energy metabolism in honeybees. These pathways collectively provide energy and nutrients, affecting both individual health and colony maintenance.

Ribosomal metabolism integrates multiple pathways (pentose phosphate, glycolysis, TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, gluconeogenesis, etc.). Ribosomes are essential for protein synthesis; their impairment reduces honeybee viability [38]. Ribosomal protein L17 (RpL17) is highly transcribed during larval and pupal stages in Apis cerana cerana, and UV or heat stress can inhibit DNA repair and protein activity, indicating ribosomal roles in early development and abiotic stress [39]. In this study, 57 upregulated DEGs in ribosomal pathways suggest stronger colonies exhibit enhanced ribosomal activity in these associated pathways.

The bee thorax controls muscle movement [40]. The citric acid cycle, occurring in mitochondria, oxidizes nutrients to produce CO₂, H₂O, NADH, FADH₂, and ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. Bees obtain carbohydrates from nectar (glucose, fructose, sucrose). Amylase converts starch into glucose and fructose. After cellular uptake via transporters, glucose and fructose are converted to glucose-6-phosphate, enter glycolysis, and produce ATP. Sucrose is hydrolyzed by α-glucosidase into glucose and fructose, which rapidly participate in glycolysis [41].

The MAPK signaling pathway is vital for honeybee cellular activity, energy metabolism, immunity, and stress response. It may activate AMPK via adenylate cyclase and increased cAMP, a process linked to the extended lifespan of queen bees [42]. The MAPK signaling pathway is activated upon Glomus infection and plays a key role in bee immunity [43].Our results align with previous transcriptomic studies in honeybees. In Apis mellifera, colony strength is linked to energy metabolism and immune-related genes, similar to our findings in the thorax of Apis cerana cerana. Other studies in Apis cerana highlight the importance of olfactory perception and royal jelly proteins in colony productivity, corroborating our head-specific results. Notably, our work is the first to directly compare strong and weak Apis cerana cerana colonies from the Lvliang Mountains, providing new insights for this subspecies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size (n = 3 per group) is modest; larger cohorts are needed for validation. Second, colony classification relied mainly on comb count, with no quantitative metrics (e.g., brood area, adult bee population, honey/pollen stores). Third, sampling only in May may miss seasonal variation. Fourth, forager age was not precisely controlled; potential age differences between colonies could contribute to transcriptional variation. Fifth, the findings are correlative; functional validation (e.g., RNAi, enzyme assays) is required. Sixth, protein-level validation (e.g., Western blot, mass spectrometry) was not performed. Despite these limitations, this study provides a valuable transcriptomic resource for understanding colony strength in Apis cerana cerana.

Conclusion

The differentially expressed genes in worker bees from strong and weak colonies of Apis cerana cerana were primarily enriched in odorant-binding protein, royal jelly major protein, taste receptor gene, olfactory receptor family gene, cytochrome P450, zinc finger protein, serine/threonine kinase, cytochrome C oxidase family gene. These genes are associated with the growth and development of bees, macromolecule biosynthesis, organic nitrogen biosynthesis, ribosome metabolism, immune response, glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism and other energy metabolismpathways, and may also be linked to individual longevity based on previous studies.
