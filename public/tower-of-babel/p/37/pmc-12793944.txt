Characterization of Beta‐Lactam Allergy and Outcomes Associated With Perioperative Antibiotic Choice in Renal Transplant Recipients
Authors: Annelise Nolan, Johanna Papanikolla, Sidney Marenkovic, Madeline Owen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeBeta‐lactam allergies (BLAs) are commonly reported and may include true allergy or adverse effects. BLAs can lead to the use of suboptimal antimicrobials, resulting in adverse outcomes. At our institution, cefazolin is the preferred surgical prophylaxis for renal transplant (RT), with clindamycin used for BLAs. There is limited data available examining outcomes with the use of non‐standard surgical site prophylaxis in RT.MethodsThis study evaluated 1523 adult RT patients from December 1, 2019 to December 1, 2024, with 257 (16.9%) having a documented BLA. BLA reactions were classified as immediate/allergic and non‐allergic. Secondary outcomes included surgical site infections (SSIs) within 30 days and Clostridium difficile within 60 days, and were compared between groups that received cefazolin and clindamycin.ResultsA total of 13.0% of the population had true allergic‐type reaction. The most common BLA was to penicillins (71.2%), then cephalosporins (46%) or multiple classes (10.8%). Allergic‐type reactions were reported by 186 (72.4%), and non‐allergic reactions were reported by 35 (13.6%), with 12 patients reporting both types, and 24 without recorded reactions. The most common immediate/allergic reaction was hives/rash (155 patients). The most common non‐allergic reaction was gastrointestinal symptoms.For perioperative prophylaxis, 182 patients received clindamycin and 67 received cefazolin. There was no difference in C. difficile in the first 60 days. There was a higher rate of SSI in the first 30 days in patients who received clindamycin (29/182, 15.9%) compared to cefazolin (2/67, 2.99%), p = 0.006.ConclusionBLA is commonly reported, but the type of reactions should be critically evaluated, as non‐standard antibiotics may increase SSI risk.

Introduction

Self‐reported medication allergies to beta‐lactam antibiotics, such as penicillins and cephalosporins, are commonly reported in medical literature in 10%–20% of patients [1, 2, 3]. The incidence of true immunologically mediated drug hypersensitivity or allergy to beta‐lactam antibiotics is much lower, reported as less than 5% [4]. Nonallergic drug reactions or adverse effects may result in mislabeling patients with drug allergy. In particular, the risk of cross‐reactivity with cefazolin in patients with penicillin allergy has been found to be very low (less than 1%) [5].

In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that alternative non‐beta‐lactam antibiotic prescribing in patients with beta‐lactam allergy (BLA) may be associated with worse outcomes, such as higher hospitalization costs, increased rates of adverse effects associated with antibiotics, including Clostridium difficile infection, increased risk of surgical site infections (SSIs), and mortality [1, 6, 7].

In solid organ transplant patients, the incidence of BLA as well as the impact of BLA on outcomes is not well defined, with reported BLA incidence ranging from 2.2% to 17%, and outcomes including increased use of non‐preferred SSI prophylaxis and non‐beta‐lactam antibiotics [8, 9]. However, prior studies have not demonstrated increased risk of SSI in the transplant patient population.

SSIs are defined as post‐surgical infections of the incision, organ, or organ space in the first 30 days after surgery [10]. The reported incidence of SSIs in renal transplant (RT) recipients ranges from 3% to 11%, with the most common organisms causing infection including Gram‐positive organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase‐negative staphylococci, and Enterococcus species. The recommended perioperative antibiotic regimen per American Society of Transplantation (AST) guidelines is a first‐generation cephalosporin for less than or equal to 24 h postoperatively. The recommendations for penicillin allergic patients include vancomycin or clindamycin plus gentamicin [11]. In RT, several risk factors associated with SSI have been identified, including diabetes mellitus, chronic glomerulonephritis, re‐operation, obesity, and increased age [12, 13, 14].

The purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence and characterization of patient‐reported beta‐lactam antibiotic allergies in RT recipients at our institution, as well as review perioperative antibiotic use and associated outcomes, including SSIs.

Methods

This institutional review board approved study was a retrospective, single‐center review of RT recipients aged 18 or older transplanted from December 1, 2019 to December 1, 2024 at the Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center.

Patients were included if they had a documented beta‐lactam antibiotic allergy in the electronic medical record, including allergy to penicillins, cephalosporins, or carbapenem antibiotics. Reported BLA reactions were classified as immediate/allergic and nonallergic. Reactions were categorized as allergic/immediate if any of the following were noted: hives, rash, itching, anaphylaxis, angioedema, and shortness of breath or swelling. Nonallergic reactions were any other symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, or other. Secondary outcomes included incidence of SSI infections within 30 days post‐transplant and C. difficile infections within 60 days post‐transplant in patients who received preferred (cefazolin) versus alternative agent (clindamycin) as perioperative prophylaxis. SSI were ascertained from medical record review utilizing NHSN criteria for SSI [10]. C. difficile infection was diagnosed via PCR test prior to September 5, 2023, after which date, two‐step (PCR and toxin testing) became standard at our institution. Patients who did not receive perioperative antibiotics or who received off protocol antibiotics were excluded from secondary analysis.

Statistical analysis was completed using STATA, version 13.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas). Descriptive statistics were used for baseline characteristics. A chi‐squared test was used for categorical variables, and a two‐tailed Student's t‐test was used for normally distributed continuous variables. A p value of less than or equal to 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

This study evaluated 1523 adult RT patients from December 1, 2019 to December 1, 2024, with 257 meeting inclusion criteria of a documented BLA. Baseline characteristics of patients with BLA are described in Table 1. The overall incidence of reported BLA in our population was 16.9%, with 198 patients (13.0%) of the total population with true allergic‐type immunologically mediated reaction. The most common BLA was to penicillins (n = 183), followed by cephalosporins (n = 46), or multiple classes (n = 28). Allergic‐type reactions were reported by 186 (72.4%), and non‐allergic reactions were reported by 35 (13.6%), with 12 patients reporting both types, and 24 without recorded reactions. The most common type of immediate/allergic type reaction was hives/rash reported by 155 patients, with 22 patients reporting anaphylaxis symptoms. The most common type of non‐allergic reaction reported was GI symptoms, including nausea/vomiting or diarrhea, in 28 of 35 patients.

To further evaluate surgical outcomes in this group of patients with BLA, patients who received cefazolin were compared with those that received clindamycin. At our institution, in alignment with AST guidelines, preferred prophylaxis is cefazolin for the first 24 h post‐op, and clindamycin for 24 h for those with documented BLA. Overall, 182 patients received clindamycin as perioperative antibiotics and 67 received cefazolin. As seen in Table 2, comparing between patients that received clindamycin and those who received cefazolin, there was no difference in baseline characteristics of age, sex, or hemoglobin A1c (HgbA1c). There was no difference in C. difficile infection in the first 60 days, with four infections in each group, p = 0.134. However, there was a significantly higher rate of SSIs in the first 30 days posttransplant in patients who received clindamycin (29 out of 182, 15.9%) compared to the cefazolin group (2 out of 67, 2.99%), p = 0.006. SSIs were recorded at an average of 19 days post‐transplant.

Among the group of 31 patients with post‐operative infections, 26 (83.9%) required further medical interventions for wound management, such as wound packing or wound vac placement. A total of 18 (58%) required re‐admission to the hospital for management (including return to OR, placement of wound vac, or management of wound dehiscence). Antibiotics were prescribed in 18 patients (58%). Bacterial culture data were available for only one patient in the cefazolin group (Enterococcus, coagulase‐negative staphylococci species). In the clindamycin group, culture data were available in 16 of 29 patients. As expected, wound cultures were often polymicrobial, with the most common organisms identified as coagulase‐negative staphylococci species in 10 patients, followed by E. coli in 4, Klebsiella pneumoniae in 4, Pseudomonas aeruginosa in 3, and Enterococcus species in 3 patients.

Thus, patients who received clindamycin were more likely to develop SSI compared to those who received cefazolin with an odds ratio 6.16 (95% CI 1.42–26.57). Finally, it was noted that 36 patients with documented allergic‐type BLA did receive cefazolin, including 12 with documented cephalosporin allergy, with no reported adverse outcomes in these patients.

Discussion

Self‐reported BLAs are common in the general population, and the rates in our RT population were similar to those reported in previous studies. The overall incidence of true allergic‐type BLA in our population was 13.0%. The majority of patients reported hives or rash as their primary type of reaction, with only 1.44% reporting anaphylaxis.

Our study found that in patients with documented BLA, the use of clindamycin instead of cefazolin for RT perioperative antibiotics resulted in higher rates of SSI. This led to adverse outcomes such as return to the operating room, additional courses of antibiotics, or requirement for increased wound care devices and nursing wound management. The rates of SSI in the clindamycin group are higher than previously reported in RT literature.

The limitations of this study include the retrospective chart review nature of the study, especially when reviewing data regarding allergy information, as well as SSI. Infections may have occurred earlier than the first documentation of the infection in the medical record. In addition, this retrospective study was not powered to compare cefazolin and clindamycin as a secondary outcome; however, including all available patients did result in clinically significant findings. Finally, there were some patients who did not have allergy data available in the medical record.

In conclusion, given these outcomes, the use of preferred beta‐lactam antibiotics should be strongly encouraged whenever possible in RT patients. Prior studies have demonstrated low risk of cross‐reactivity with the use of cefazolin in these patients. Allergy information should always be clarified in the medical record prior to ordering preoperative antibiotics, and adverse effects such as gastrointestinal symptoms should not be recorded as medication allergies. BLAs are commonly reported, but efforts should be made to administer preferred antibiotics whenever possible to optimize outcomes in this RT population.
