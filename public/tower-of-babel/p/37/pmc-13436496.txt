Anxiety and Aggression Among College Students With High‐Risk Adverse Childhood Experiences: The Role of Bullying Perpetration
Authors: Jeoung Min Lee, Hailey Hyunjin Choi, Sei‐Young Lee, Jaegoo Lee, Joo Hyun Kim, Sangmi Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study examines the potential associations between anxiety and aggressive behaviors in college students with high‐risk adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and explores how this relationship may be influenced by bullying perpetration, applying general strain theory. A sample of 282 college students aged 19 to 29 (M age = 21.59, SD age = 2.48; 74.5% female; 66.3% White) was analyzed. Results indicated a positive association between anxiety and aggressive behaviors among college students with high‐risk ACEs. Higher levels of anxiety were linked to increased bullying perpetration, which, in turn, was associated with greater aggressive behaviors. These findings suggest that college students with high‐risk ACEs who experience negative emotions, such as anxiety, are more likely to engage in bullying perpetration, which subsequently contributes to higher levels of aggressive behaviors. To mitigate the consequences of delinquency (e.g., bullying perpetration and aggressive behaviors), ongoing attention and intervention from school personnel, social workers, human service professionals, community leaders, and policymakers are essential to supporting and protecting students with high‐risk ACEs.Keywords: aggressive behaviors, anxiety, bullying perpetration, college students with high‐risk ACEs

Introduction

As a result of the significant psychological and physical consequences of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) in adulthood, many researchers have focused on exploring the traumatic effects of childhood physical and emotional neglect, abuse, and family dysfunction on young adulthood (Bellis et al. 2019; Campbell et al. 2016; Carlson et al. 2019; Madigan et al. 2024). College students are in an emerging adulthood stage, facing challenges such as becoming financially independent, making important life decisions, developing personal identity, and establishing romantic and social relationships (Butterbaugh et al. 2020; Curran et al. 2018). These demands may lead to significant emotional strain during this pivotal time. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2021), 61% of adults have experienced at least one ACE, and 16% have experienced four or more, which is commonly used as the threshold for high ACE exposure (Swedo et al. 2023). Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have found that 41%–97% of individuals have experienced multiple ACEs (Carlson et al. 2019; Madigan et al. 2024). If college students suffer from ongoing trauma from ACEs, they may be hindered from experiencing positive emotions and healthy outcomes in navigating their challenges (Almeida et al. 2024; Lipsky et al. 2022; Merrin et al. 2024).

Research has shown that individuals with four or more ACEs are at high risk for experiencing long‐term health consequences, including anxiety, depression, substance use, risky sexual behavior, and disability due to health issues (Bellis et al. 2019; Campbell et al. 2016). Studies have also conceptualized ACEs as polyvictimization, which may predict psychological aggressive behaviors and polyperpetration (Voith et al. 2020). Those who have experienced four or more ACEs are more likely to exhibit negative mental and physical outcomes than those with fewer than four ACEs. However, research is scarce regarding how emotional distress from high‐risk ACEs affects peer relations and aggressive behaviors in college environments. College students with high ACE scores may face unique challenges during a critical developmental period, which can influence their emotional well‐being, peer relationships, and behavioral patterns. This gap in research highlights the need for further studies to understand the broader behavioral, social, and emotional impacts of ACEs on college students.

Studies have revealed that high‐risk ACEs are significantly related to persistent and continued fear and anxiety in young adults (Sabella et al. 2024). ACEs are also strongly connected to aggressive behaviors in young adults (Almeida et al. 2024; Burke et al. 2022; Mumford et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2024). These experiences specifically impact emotional regulation, altering the brain's stress response system and increasing the likelihood of engaging in aggressive behaviors to cope with stressful events (Heim and Nemeroff 2001). A large‐scale study with 2284 young adults in the United States suggests that ACEs and recent stressors were associated with verbal and physical aggressive outcomes, indicating lasting effects of ACEs (Mumford et al. 2019). In addition, a longitudinal study with the National Epidemiological Survey on Alcohol and Related Conditions–III suggests that high‐risk ACEs groups were at significantly higher risk of engaging in violence, meaning that severe ACEs trauma was associated with increased risk for violence in later adulthood (Burke et al. 2022).

Moreover, one longitudinal study found that high‐risk ACEs were associated with anxiety and poor social skills, which, in turn, led to bullying perpetration (Hamstra and Fitzgerald 2022). Individuals with a history of high‐risk ACEs may be more prone to bullying due to difficulties with emotional regulation and the use of positive coping mechanisms stemming from their traumatic experiences. Additionally, bullying perpetration is likely to manifest as aggressive behaviors. In a sample of more than 8000 middle and high school students in North Carolina, Evans et al. (2019) found that cumulative bullying perpetration over five years was significantly associated with increased aggressive behaviors and decreased future optimism.

General strain theory (GST; Agnew 1992) helps us understand how high‐risk ACEs can lead to individuals' negative emotions and behaviors. A central premise of GST is that strain alone does not directly cause delinquency; rather, the emotional response to strain—particularly negative emotions such as anger, frustration, and depression—determines whether an individual engages in delinquent behavior (Agnew 1992; Ostrowsky and Messner 2005). While Agnew (1992) posits that anger is the most direct motivator for delinquency because it energizes individuals for retaliation, he explicitly recognizes anxiety as a significant emotional response to strain that can also drive externalizing behaviors. Despite this recognition, the role of anxiety has remained relatively underexplored in GST literature compared to anger (Agnew 1992; Broidy and Agnew 1997; Ostrowsky and Messner 2005). Anxiety, one of the most common negative emotions, is associated with physiological arousal, cognitive distortions, and difficulties in emotional regulation, which may lead some individuals to externalize their distress through aggressive behaviors rather than resorting to avoidance strategies (Li et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025).

This study distinguishes between bullying perpetration and aggressive behaviors to clarify the developmental pathway for college students with high‐risk ACEs. While aggressive behaviors are defined as a broad range of behaviors intended to cause injury or harm to another person (Didden et al. 2012), bullying perpetration is a specific subtype of proactive aggression characterized by an intentional power imbalanced relationship (Olweus 1993). In this pathway, anxiety does not inevitably lead to withdrawal; rather, under conditions of cumulative strain, it can drive aggressive behaviors through physiological hyperarousal and maladaptive externalization (Agnew 1992, 2006). Specifically, physiological hyperarousal provides the internal impulse for action, while the attempt to shift from a state of victimization to a position of power provides the motivation for that aggressive behavior (Cho and Lee 2018). For students with high‐risk ACEs, bullying perpetration thus serves as a calculated coping mechanism and compensatory strategy to manage the “agitated unease” of anxiety by asserting dominance in their social environment (Punia et al. 2024).

In particular, college students with high‐risk ACEs are likely to develop anxiety disorders in adulthood (Lipsky et al. 2022). High‐risk ACEs, combined with anxiety, may negatively impact social (peer) relationships and aggressive behaviors as well. For example, young adults with high‐risk ACEs may seek to assert power within a peer group, potentially leading to physical, verbal, and social harassment of others (Dixon Everett et al. 2025). Additionally, bullying perpetration may ultimately contribute to increased overall aggressive behaviors later in life (Homel 2013; Sapouna 2025).

While evidence is relatively abundant that individuals who have experienced cumulative high‐risk ACEs are at greater risk for anxiety, bullying perpetration, and aggressive behaviors (Almeida et al. 2024; Burke et al. 2022; Mumford et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2024), further research is necessary to investigate these developmental sequences among college students. Therefore, guided by GST, this study aims to understand how the strain developed from high‐risk ACEs influences negative emotions and aggressive behaviors later in life. The findings suggest that addressing anxiety and preventing bullying may play a critical role in reducing aggressive behaviors and delinquent behavior among young adults. This underscores the need for more comprehensive mental health and social intervention strategies to mitigate risk factors associated with aggressive behaviors.

The Current Study

The purpose of this study is to examine the potential associations between anxiety and aggressive behaviors in college students who have high‐risk ACEs and how this association may be explained by levels of bullying aggressive behaviors. Significant relationships have already been shown between ACEs and anxiety (Lipsky et al. 2022), bullying (Merrin et al. 2024), and aggressive behaviors (Almeida et al. 2024). While these bivariate links are documented, the specific behavioral pathway through which early trauma translates into adult interpersonal violence remains unclear. This study addresses this gap by testing whether bullying perpetration serves as the primary mechanism linking internalizing distress (i.e., anxiety) to externalized aggressive behaviors among college students with high‐risk ACEs. Building on General Strain Theory, we propose the following hypotheses:

College students who have high‐risk ACEs with anxiety will report high levels of aggressive behaviors.

College students who have high‐risk ACEs with anxiety will report high levels of bullying perpetration, and those students with high levels of bullying perpetration will report high levels of aggressive behaviors.

Methods

Participants and Data Collection Procedures

The hypothesized indirect relationship between anxiety and aggressive behaviors through bullying perpetration was examined using a sample of 282 college students aged 19 to 29 (Mage = 21.59, SDage = 2.48; 74.5% female; 66.3% White). Participants were recruited from four universities across the Midwestern, South Central, and Southern U.S. regions, each obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board for the current study. Researchers provided participants with detailed information about the study and consent procedures via email, and only those aged 18 to 29 who agreed to participate were invited to the online survey.

Online surveys were created in Qualtrics and conducted between November 2022 and January 2023. A total of 790 college students responded to a series of questionnaires asking about their psychological health, social relationships, and demographic characteristics. The current study primarily focuses on high‐risk ACEs young adults; thus, we used the Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE‐IQ; World Health Organization 2020) to select subsamples with an ACE score greater than four.

Participants were asked to recall their childhood and answer the questions on a binary variable (i.e., yes = 1, no = 0), indicating whether each ACE occurred. When participants answered “yes” to at least one of the items in each category, they scored 1. The ACE‐IQ contains 21 items categorized into 10 types of ACEs (e.g., physical [k = 2; n = 218, 46% ], emotional [k = 2; n = 281, 59.3% ], sexual abuse [k = 4; n = 147, 31%], physical abuse [k = 3; n = 124, 26.2% ], emotional neglect [k = 2; n = 451, 95.1% ], and family dysfunction such as household violence [k = 3; n = 283, 59.7%], mental health issues [k = 1; n = 180, 38%], incarceration [k = 1; n = 67, 14.1%], substance use [k = 1; n = 114, 24.1%], and parental divorce or separation [k = 2; n = 177, 37.3%]). The total ACE score was computed by summing the responses across the 10 categories, indicating the number of ACEs that participants experienced during childhood. The potential scores for ACEs ranged from 0 to 10. The participants with a score of four or more ACEs were considered to have high‐risk ACEs, leading to long‐term health consequences (Bellis et al. 2019; Campbell et al. 2016). In the current study, 282 students who scored greater than four on the ACE‐IQ were included.

Measures

Anxiety: Young adults' levels of anxiety were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 (GAD‐7; Spitzer et al. 2006), which was developed to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms and to screen for anxiety disorder. GAD‐7 consists of seven self‐reported items asking the degree to which participants experienced anxiety‐related symptoms in the past 2 weeks, which included, for example, feeling nervous, not being able to stop worrying, or having trouble relaxing. Each item was rated on a four‐point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” = 0, “several days” = 1, “more than half the days” = 2, to “nearly every day” = 3, with higher scores indicating severe anxiety symptoms. The potential score ranges from 0 to 21, and a cut‐off score of 10 is recommended for further evaluation. GAD‐7 has been validated as a screening tool and severity measure for patients and the general populations (Williams 2014). Internal consistency across seven items in the study sample was calculated as 0.94.

Aggressive behaviors: The 12‐item Buss‐Perry Aggression Questionnaire–Short Form (BPAQ‐SF; Bryant and Smith 2001) was used to assess aggressive behaviors. The BPAQ‐SF is an abbreviated version of the 21‐item BPAQ (Buss and Perry 1992), which includes four factors of aggression: physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility. Previous studies revealed that the BPAQ‐SF also fits well with the four‐factors models (e.g., Bryant and Smith 2001; Diamond et al. 2005), including physical aggression (four items; e.g., Given enough provocation, I may hit another person), verbal aggression (three items; e.g., I can't help getting into arguments when people disagree with me), anger (two items; e.g., Sometimes I fly off the handle for no good reason), and hostility (three items; e.g., At times I feel I have gotten a raw deal out of life). Participants were asked how likely each of the statements is to describe their characteristics, and they self‐rated each item on a five‐point scale ranging from 1 (very unlike me) to 5 (very like me). The present study used a total score of responses on 12 items, with higher scores indicating more aggressive characteristics. Cronbach's alpha of the 12‐item BPAQ‐SF in the study sample was 0.87.

Bullying perpetration: To evaluate bullying perpetration, we utilized the Multidimensional Peer Bullying Scale (MPVS‐RB; Betts et al. 2015), which was developed based on the 16‐item Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (Mynard and Joseph 2000). The psychometric validation of this adapted perpetration version confirmed its factor structure and construct validity (Betts et al. 2015), and it has been utilized in large‐scale trials (e.g., Karmaliani et al. 2017). This scale was originally designed to capture individuals' experiences over the previous school year regarding physical victimization, verbal victimization, social manipulation, and property attacks. The language pertaining to victimization was modified to reflect perpetration (e.g., Physical perpetration: I punched them; Verbal: I made fun of them because of their appearance; Social manipulation: I tried to make their friends turn against them; Property attacks: I took something of theirs without permission). Participants reported the frequency of these events occurring using a three‐point Likert scale (i.e., never = 0, once = 1, more than once = 2). This scoring system was maintained to ensure consistency with the original instrument's metrics. A total peer perpetration index was computed by summing item responses, where higher scores represent a greater frequency of engaging in bullying. In the current sample, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89).

Statistical controls: We added demographic factors, including age, sex assigned at birth (male = 1), and race (White, African American, Hispanic, Asian, and other races). Participants were allowed to select multiple racial categories.

Analytic Strategies

We tested the direct and indirect associations between anxiety and aggressive behaviors through bullying perpetration by running PROCESS Model 4 in SPSS (Hayes 2022). The significance of the indirect effect was confirmed using 5000 bootstrap samples. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) indicated no issues with multicollinearity. The descriptive statistics and bivariate correlation were examined using SPSS version 29. We set the cut‐off for statistical significance at α = 0.05.

Indirect Effect of Bullying Perpetration

Next, we examined whether the association between anxiety and aggressive behaviors is through bullying perpetration among college students with high‐risk ACEs. By running Model 4 in the PROCESS macro, we found that among college students who were exposed to more than four types of ACEs, severe anxiety symptoms were associated with aggressive behaviors, in part via committing bullying perpetration (Figure 1).

The overall model explained 36% of the variance in aggressive behaviors (R2 = 0.36, F (9, 211) = 13.41, p < 0.001). Anxiety was also found to be directly associated with aggressive behaviors (b = 0.60, SE = 0.08, CI [0.45, 0.76]), showing that those with higher levels of anxiety tended to be more aggressive. Regarding the indirect path, bullying perpetration was indirectly linked to the positive relationship between anxiety and aggressive behaviors (b = 0.06, SE = 0.03, CI [0.01, 0.12]) in that those with greater levels of anxiety were more likely to commit bullying perpetration (b = 0.06, SE = 0.03, CI [0.00, 0.11]), which, in turn, led to more aggressive behaviors (b = 1.00, SE = 0.18, CI [0.64, 1.35]). Discussions

This study examines the relationship between anxiety and aggressive behaviors in college students with high‐risk ACEs to investigate bullying perpetration as a potential indirect path. Building on previous literature (e.g., Almeida et al. 2024; Burke et al. 2022; Mumford et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2024), this study provides two noteworthy contributions. First, it establishes a link between anxiety and aggressive behaviors, particularly among college students who were exposed to high levels of early adversity. Second, it suggests bullying perpetration as a possible mechanism by which anxiety is associated with aggressive behaviors, thus highlighting the relationship among early adversity, mental health, and behavioral outcomes.

Anxiety and Aggressive Behaviors

More specifically, our findings reveal that college students with high‐risk ACEs who experience anxiety exhibit greater levels of aggressive behaviors. This is in line with previous studies on anxiety among young adults with ACEs (e.g., Sabella et al. 2024), showing that greater anxiety is linked to greater aggressive behaviors (e.g., Chung et al. 2019; Desai et al. 2023). In this regard, Hammen et al. (2000) argued that early adversity results in lasting sensitization or vulnerability that amplifies the risk of mental health issues, including anxiety. One mechanism underlying this effect is toxic stress stemming from early adversity, as it disrupts the body's stress response and physiological regulation systems, thus resulting in chronic dysregulation (Shonkoff et al. 2012). Consequently, when anxiety levels increase, college students with high‐risk ACEs may struggle to regulate their negative emotions due to disruptions in their regulation systems. This can increase their susceptibility to externalizing behaviors, such as aggressive behaviors, as coping mechanisms for excessive anxiety.

This finding provides a valuable perspective on the association between anxiety and aggressive behaviors using GST, which emphasizes that negative emotional responses to adversity can be related to deviant and aggressive behaviors. Specifically, the link between anxiety and aggressive behaviors in college students with high‐risk ACEs supports GST by demonstrating how anxiety, as a negative emotional response to high‐risk ACEs, can be associated with aggressive behaviors. While previous research has primarily considered anger, impulsivity, and substance use as pathways to aggressive behaviors (e.g., Voith et al. 2020), this study confirms anxiety as another potential key emotional factor associated with aggressive behaviors.

Bullying Perpetration Linking Anxiety to Aggressive Behaviors

Furthermore, our findings confirm the role of bullying perpetration in the relationship between anxiety and aggressive behaviors among college students with high‐risk ACEs. The indirect effect of bullying perpetration from anxiety to aggressive behaviors indicates that anxiety may first manifest as bullying perpetration, which subsequently grows into broader aggressive behaviors. Our findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that high‐risk ACEs contribute to anxiety and poor social skills, which can lead to bullying perpetration (Hamstra and Fitzgerald 2022), which, in turn, is significantly linked to increased aggressive behaviors (Evans et al. 2019). One possible explanation is that bullying perpetration might be a maladaptive coping mechanism among college students with high‐risk ACEs. Marks et al. (2022) explained that college students with high‐risk ACEs may activate their body's survival responses, particularly when experiencing anxiety. As a result, they may develop maladaptive coping behaviors, such as bullying, as they prioritize immediate survival over long‐term well‐being (Marks et al. 2022). Given that bullying itself is a form of aggressive behavior (Chu et al. 2019), bullying perpetration may reinforce aggressive behaviors. Moreover, Evans et al. (2019) explained that people who use bullying to gain social dominance and supremacy, for instance, to gain influence within a group, might persist and escalate their aggressive behaviors in a bid to maintain their status. Collectively, this finding contributes to the existing body of literature by mapping out a developmental pathway that connects anxiety experienced by college students with high‐risk ACEs to aggressive behaviors, specifically through bullying perpetration.

Limitations and Future Studies

This study is a cross‐sectional design, which prevents the establishment of causal relationships among anxiety, bullying perpetration, and aggressive behaviors. Also, whether anxiety precedes bullying perpetration and aggressive behaviors or if these behaviors mutually reinforce one another is yet to be established. Therefore, future longitudinal research should follow participants over extended periods to determine the directionality of these associations.

In addition, the use of self‐reported data in this study can lead to potential biases such as social desirability and recall bias. To improve measurement accuracy, future studies should incorporate multi‐informant assessments, such as the assessor's behavioral observations and physiological testing. These alternative data sources could provide a more comprehensive and objective assessment of anxiety‐ and aggressive behaviors‐related behaviors.

Moreover, the generalizability of the study's findings is also constrained by the characteristics of the sample. The participants were exclusively college students from four U.S. universities, limiting the applicability of the results to the broader young adult population, particularly those not pursuing higher education. Furthermore, the racial composition and gender imbalance may restrict the extent to which these findings apply to more diverse populations. To address this, a moderation analysis was conducted, which revealed no significant interaction between anxiety and gender on aggression (b = 0.14, p = 0.56), suggesting the relationship was consistent across both groups in this sample. Additionally, racial/ethnic categories were not significantly associated with aggressive behaviors (see Table 2). Future research should include more diverse samples, encompassing individuals from different educational backgrounds, racial and ethnic groups, and gender identities.

In addition to sample limitations, investigating protective factors, such as strong family support, positive peer relationships, and high resilience, could provide valuable insights into why some individuals with high‐risk ACEs do not develop anxiety or aggressive tendencies.

Finally, considerable research has recently provided evidence of comorbid internalizing (i.e., anxiety) and externalizing (i.e., aggressive behaviors) symptoms in individuals with ACEs (Duprey et al. 2020; van den et al. 2025). To better capture this complexity, future studies need to employ more advanced statistical methods, such as latent profile analysis, to examine concurrent symptoms of anxiety and aggressive behaviors and their relationship to bullying perpetration. Additionally, researchers should compare high‐risk and low‐risk ACE cohorts within college students to explore differences.

Implications for Practice and Policy

With the study's limitations in mind, the study findings offer critical perspectives for practical implications. First, for children and young adults growing up with high‐risk ACEs, trauma‐informed interventions are needed to regulate their negative emotions (i.e., anxiety) and reduce their fearful experiences in their family environments. Also, it is urgent to implement “counter‐ACEs” interventions, which are advantageous childhood experiences that act as protective factors (Crandall et al. 2019). Counter‐ACEs can mitigate the effects of high‐risk ACEs and enhance protection by fostering a safe family environment or having a positive relationship with an alternative parental figure in the community, which appears to buffer the harmful impact of ACEs (Lee et al. 2021; Narayan et al. 2018).

Second, as anxiety developed through high‐risk ACEs is persistent and continues into young adulthood (Sabella et al. 2024) and chronic dysregulated high anxiety can lead to externalizing behavior (Hammen et al. 2000; Shonkoff et al. 2012), there is an urgent need to treat anxiety among young adults with high‐risk ACEs with, for example, emotional regulation and mindfulness interventions to avert externalizing behaviors. Specifically, managing insomnia, which is a typical symptom of anxiety, can be helped by practicing trauma‐informed mindfulness and a regular sleep schedule, avoiding substance use, and limiting digital device use (Stein and Sareen 2015).

Third, interventions on bullying are needed to prevent later aggressive behaviors, not only at the individual level to teach healthy coping strategies in children and young adults with high‐risk ACEs but also at the community level through caregivers, school personnel, and community leaders. In particular, building trauma‐sensitive schools would help improve school climate and create safe learning environments to deter bullying behaviors. Building trauma‐sensitive schools requires changes at all levels, including the school's practices, policies, and cultures, to ensure students' social, emotional, and educational success (Guarino and Chagnon 2018). For college students, efforts at the university level will be most effective by educating and training students and staff on the association between anxiety and bullying, providing students with behavior guidelines for class and on campus, providing clear guidelines to respond to bullying incidents on campus, and enhancing the university's capacity to provide counseling centers or mental health services to students with high‐risk ACEs (Hughes 2001). University counselors and other health personnel who work with students struggling with interpersonal relationships or mental health issues may assess anxiety, bullying perpetration, and victimization for immediate actions and prevention against further aggressive behaviors (Lund and Ross 2017).

Although bullying policies, counter‐ACEs interventions, and trauma‐informed interventions have existed for some time, they are outdated or not actively implemented in the field (Forkey et al. 2021; Leitch 2017). To provide effective services (e.g., educating, training, and counseling) for students with high‐risk ACEs, colleges must longitudinally monitor students' anxiety, provide trauma‐informed counseling, and enhance access to mental health services (Majebi et al. 2024).

Conclusion

This study enhances GST by incorporating anxiety as a fundamental emotional response in the trajectory from strain to aggressive behaviors through bullying perpetration among college students exposed to high‐risk ACEs. Mitigating aroused negative emotions, bullying perpetration, and aggressive behaviors necessitates a safe school climate for the holistic health of college students with high‐risk ACEs. The Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention Act highlights the need for community‐centered and trauma‐informed care programs to address the root causes of delinquency (Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention, n.d.). To create a safe learning environment, practitioners and policymakers must consider providing inclusive, trauma‐informed interventions for college students, informed by developed policies and guidelines.
