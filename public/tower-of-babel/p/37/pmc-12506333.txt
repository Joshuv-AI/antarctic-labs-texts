Association between the triglyceride glucose-body mass index and cardiometabolic diseases risk in chronic liver disease: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Tianchen Qian, Pengyao Lin, Qingjing Zhu, Shisong Wang, Yiyun Liu, Jiarong Xie, Lei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between cardiometabolic diseases and the triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) has been proved. However, the relationship between the TyG-BMI and cardiometabolic complications in chronic liver disease (CLD) patients has not been clearly elucidated. We aimed to investigate whether the TyG-BMI and its trajectory are associated with cardiometabolic comorbidities, especially Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic syndrome (CKM) in patients with CLD.MethodsWe obtained data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2020. The exposures were baseline TyG-BMI value at baseline and its change between the first and third waves. The change in TyG-BMI was classified via dynamic time wrapping clustering. The outcomes were CKM syndrome and other cardiometabolic diseases. Adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression was used as the main statistical method.ResultsThere were 765 participants in the CKM cohort, of whom 426 (55.7%) were male and 339 (44.3%) were female. High baseline TyG-BMI values were associated with an HR (95% CI) of 2.404 (1.889–3.060; P < 0.001) for CKM. Compared with those in the steadily low TyG-BMI group, the HR (95%CI) in the steadily high TyG-BMI group was 1.405 (1.051–1.879; P = 0.022), the HR (95% CI) in the increasing TyG-BMI group was 1.602 (1.154–2.223; P = 0.005). Results in other cohorts also confirm the relationship between TyG-BMI and CKM-related diseases.ConclusionBaseline high levels and steadily high or increasing high levels of TyG-BMI are associated with cardiometabolic diseases in CLD patients. Monitoring TyG-BMI might improve the prognosis of patients with CLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03231-w.Keywords: Chronic liver disease, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, CHARLS, Triglyceride-glucose-body mass index

Introduction

Chronic liver disease (CLD) refers to a group of liver disorders characterized by persistent hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and damage over a period of 6 months or longer [1]. Deaths caused by CLD are increasing, making it one of the major global disease burdens [2]. CLD can lead to numerous complications, including compensated and decompensated liver cirrhosis, ascites and varices, hepatic encephalopathy, and hepatorenal syndrome [3, 4]. Extensive clinical evidence suggests that CLD not only increases morbidity and mortality related to liver conditions but also increases the risk of developing other significant extrahepatic cardiometabolic diseases [5, 6]. The impaired hepatic function in chronic liver diseases might exacerbate metabolic disorders, leading to impaired glucose tolerance and diabetes within the endocrine system; glomerulosclerosis, tubulointerstitial inflammation and renal fibrosis in the kidneys; atherosclerosis and myocardial injury in the cardiovascular system [7, 8].

Recently, American Heart Association proposed Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic syndrome (CKM) which is a systemic disease associated with metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and pathophysiological interactions within the cardiovascular system that can lead to multiple organ dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [9]. The definition of CKM integrates disorders of adipose tissue, kidneys, and cardiovascular system, highlighting their potential shared pathophysiological mechanisms [10, 11]. Given the important role of the liver in metabolism, whether people with CLD need to be more concerned about the risk of cardiometabolic complications such as CKM syndrome than the general population has not been well established. If this is the case, could the efficacy of the predictive indicators of cardiometabolic complications in the general population be the same in individuals with chronic liver disease, this remains uncertain.

The triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI), which serves as a good surrogate marker for insulin resistance, has shown predictive potential in metabolic diseases and cardiovascular disorders [12–16]. A recent study demonstrated that the TyG-BMI could effectively predict cardiovascular disease in the CKM population [17]. Research on TyG-BMI and liver diseases has focused mainly on its role in predicting fatty liver and its association with mortality and cardiovascular disease in individuals with fatty liver [18]. Few studies have examined the use of the TyG-BMI in patients with other chronic liver diseases. As a metabolic indicator, its use for long-term monitoring is definitely better than that of a single indicator. However, current research on the TyG-BMI trajectory is limited.

Therefore, we aimed to explore the relationships between the TyG-BMI index and cardiometabolic comorbidities in individuals with CLD using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database. Additionally, we sought to determine whether changes in TyG-BMI trajectories affect the probability of developing cardiometabolic comorbidities, thus providing insights into chronic liver disease management and prognosis.

Methods

Study population and data source

The data used in this study were sourced from the CHARLS database. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey targeting individuals aged 45 and older, employing a multistage stratified sampling method based on probability proportional to size. Baseline data were collected from 150 counties or districts and 450 villages or urban communities across 28 provinces in China starting in 2011, with follow-up surveys conducted biennially. In the 2011 baseline survey, 17,708 individuals from 10,027 households were included. A total of 8,165 additional individuals were newly enrolled in the four subsequent waves from 2013 to 2020. Other detailed information on the study design, survey methods, and response rates of the CHARLS database has been previously described [19].

Data from the first to fifth waves of the CHARLS database were used in this study, encompassing a total of 25,873 individuals. Figure 1 illustrates our data processing workflow. We initially excluded 552 and 9,005 individuals with missing CLD data and TyG-BMI values, respectively. We then removed the non-CLD group, which included 14,611 individuals. On the basis of our research objectives, we subsequently excluded individuals with missing CKM, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome data from the remaining dataset and then excluded individuals whose target diseases occurred before the onset of CLD. Ultimately, 1,319 individuals were included in the CVD cohort, 1,502 in the CKD cohort, 1,476 in the diabetes cohort, 765 in the CKM cohort and 898 in the metabolic syndrome cohort.

CHARLS data processing workflow

Exposure

The exposures in this study were the baseline TyG-BMI value and its trajectory during follow-up. Since TyG-BMI data were only available in the first and third waves, the changes in TyG-BMI focused on between these two waves. TyG-BMI was calculated using the following formula: TyG-BMI = ln[TG (mg/dl) × FBG (mg/dl)/2] × BMI (kg/m2). The Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention collected fasting venous blood samples following standard procedures and analyzed them in a central laboratory. Standard enzymatic methods were used to measure triglycerides and glucose. BMI is calculated by dividing weight by height squared, which was measured by trained nurses.

Outcome

The main outcome of the study was CKM syndrome which we defined as the presence of any of the following conditions: CVD (atherosclerotic cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, or atrial fibrillation), kidney disease (CKD defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), metabolic syndrome and diabetes according to the AHA position statement [20]. The secondary outcome of this study was other cardiometabolic comorbidities, including CVD, CKD, type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. CVD was determined by the diagnosis of heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems/stroke by a doctor. CKD was determined by the diagnosis of kidney disease (except for a tumor or cancer) by a doctor. Type 2 diabetes was determined by the diagnosis with diabetes or high blood sugar by a doctor or with abnormal serum markers of glucose metabolism. Metabolic syndrome was determined by diagnosis of dyslipidemia or hypertension by a doctor. The date of diagnosis for each of these cardiometabolic comorbidities was measured from the date of the last interview to the date of the interview in which new-onset disease was reported.

Covariates

This study's covariates included baseline age, sex, marital status, place of residence, educational attainment, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption. Place of residence was categorized as rural or urban, whereas educational attainment was classified into the following categories: no formal education, primary school, middle or high school, and college or higher. Smoking and alcohol use were further categorized into never, former, and current users.

Statistical analysis

We used a variety of statistical methods for data processing. Continuous variables were described primarily by means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were described primarily by frequencies and percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics between different groups were compared via the χ2 test and analysis of variance. The missing data were assumed to be missing at random. Owing to the lack of critical clinical values for the TyG-BMI, to better group participants, we used unsupervised k-means machine learning techniques to divide patients into three classes according to their TyG-BMI at baseline. The K-means algorithm divides the dataset into K clusters by minimizing the square of the distance within each cluster via a centroid-based clustering method. To determine the suitable number of classes, we tried the grouping results of 2–8 groups and finally chose to divide them into 3 classes on the basis of the within-cluster sum of squares, the silhouette coefficient and the results after grouping. The mean TyG-BMI of class 1 in the five cohorts was approximately 170, the mean TyG-BMI of class 2 was approximately 215, and the mean TyG-BMI of class 3 was approximately 270.

Person-years were measured from recruitment to either the diagnosis of the target disease, loss to follow-up, or study conclusion. To determine the relationship between the TyG-BMI and cardiometabolic comorbidities, multivariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with class 1 as the reference and time-to-event included in the model. We used Schoenfeld residuals to test the proportional hazards assumption, and no potential violation was observed in the test results. We did not adjust for any covariates in Model 1, age and sex were adjusted for in Model 2, and marital status, residence, educational attainment, smoking habits, alcohol consumption were further adjusted for in Model 3.

Then, to group different patients according to the trajectory changes between the two waves of TyG-BMI, we used the K-means segmented clustering method based on dynamic time warping (DTW). This method performs cluster analysis by calculating the similarity matrix between time series. The missing TyG-BMI values in waves 1 and 3 were imputed via chained equations with five imputations. Similarly, to determine the appropriate number of groups, we tried 3–8 groups of grouping results. Finally, we chose to use the classification results of 8 groups. We merged similar groups according to the location and trend of each group's centerline and ultimately divided them into four groups, namely, the low-value stable group, high-value stable group, rising group and falling group, and we recalculated the centerlines of the groups after regrouping. Then, on the basis of the grouping results, the multivariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazard model was used to calculate the HR and 95% CI, the low-value stable group was used as the reference, and time events were incorporated into the model. No covariates were adjusted for in Model 1, age and sex were adjusted for in Model 2, and all covariates were adjusted for in Model 3. R statistical software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation) was used to perform all of the analyses.

Results

The prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases were greater in CLD patients

We initially utilized logistic regression to compare the incidence rate of cardiometabolic diseases between the general population and individuals with chronic liver disease. We analyzed data from five waves of the CHARLS from 2011 to 2020, after excluding individuals with missing data, 87,169 individuals were included in the analysis. The results of the logistic regression, as shown in Fig. 2, indicate that, compared with the non-CLD population, individuals with CLD had an odds ratio (OR) of 2.12 (1.99–2.28; P < 0.001) for developing CKM, an OR of 2.57 (95% CI 2.43–2.74; P < 0.001) for developing CVD, an OR of 4.28 (95% CI 4.00–4.59; P < 0.001) for developing CKD, an OR of 1. 78(95% CI 1.65–1.91; P < 0.001) for developing diabetes, and an OR of 1.54 (95% CI 1.45–1.64; P < 0.001) for developing metabolic syndrome. These results indicate that CLD patients need to pay more attention to cardiometabolic diseases than non-CLD patients do and that is the reason we focus on chronic liver disease patients in this study.

Relationships of high baseline TyG-BMI with cardiometabolic comorbidities risk in CLD patients

During the follow-up period of up to 8 years, 591 participants developed CKM syndrome, with 230 (72.1%) in Class 1, 254 (76.3%) in Class 2, and 107 (94.7%) in Class 3. The incidence of CKM syndrome significantly differed across baseline TyG-BMI classes (P < 0.001). The multivariate Cox regression results presented in Table 3 reveal that higher baseline TyG-BMI was significantly and positively associated with CKM syndrome and its related diseases, even after adjusting for age, sex, residence, smoking, and alcohol consumption. After adjusting for multiple variants, compared with Class 1, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for Class 2 was 1.364 (1.135–1.640; P < 0.001), and for Class 3, it was 2.404 (1.889–3.060; P < 0.001). In the CVD cohort, compared with Class 1, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for Class 2 was 1.293 (1.052–1.589; P = 0.015), and for Class 3, it was 1.483 (1.152–1.909; P = 0.002). In the CKD cohort, Class 2 had an adjusted HR of 1.258 (1.006–1.575; P = 0.044), whereas Class 3 had an adjusted HR of 1.426 (1.069–1.915; P = 0.018). In the type 2 diabetes cohort, the adjusted HR for Class 2 were 1.677 (1.287–2.186; P < 0.001), and those for Class 3 were 3.888 (2.941–5.140; P < 0.001). In metabolic syndrome cohort, the adjusted HR for Class 2 patients were 1.578 (1.301–1.915; P < 0.001), and those for Class 3 patients were 2.935 (2.308–3.733; P < 0.001). The results here suggest that a higher TyG-BMI is associated with cardiometabolic comorbidities in CLD patients.

Relationships of TyG-BMI trajectory with cardiometabolic comorbidities risk in CLD patients

We employed the K-means segmentation clustering method based on DTW to categorize the TyG-BMI trajectory curves into four groups: Group 1, low and stable trajectory; Group 2, high and stable trajectory; Group 3, decreasing trajectory; and Group 4, increasing trajectory, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The multivariate Cox regression results presented in Table 4 indicate that a high and stable or elevated TyG-BMI is significantly and positively correlated with the occurrence of CKM syndrome, even after adjusting for all of the covariates, compared with Group 1, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for Group 2 was 1.405 (1.051–1.879; P = 0.022), the adjusted HR (95% CI) for Group 4 was 1.602 (1.154–2.223; P = 0.005). In the CVD cohort, compared with Group 1, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for Group 4 was 1.444 (1.027–2.030; P = 0.034) and no significant association was found in Group 2. In the CKD cohort, compared with Group 1, the HR for Group 2 was 1.682 (1.142–2.477; P = 0.008), the HR for Group 4 was 1.620 (1.085–2.419; P = 0.018). In the diabetes cohort, the HR for Group 2 was 1.851 (1.213–2.825; P = 0.004), the HR for Group 4 was 2.197(1.455–3.318; P < 0.001). In these cohorts, the 95% confidence intervals of the HR for Group 3 all encompassed 1, indicating that the risk of above cardiometabolic diseases in CLD patients with decreased TyG-BMI from higher levels was not statistically different from that of patients who consistently maintained low values. These results suggest that a reduction in TyG-BMI may confer a certain protective effect against above cardiometabolic diseases. In terms of metabolic syndrome, however, a statistically significant difference was not found between the increasing or high trajectories and target diseases. Overall, these findings suggest that a continuously high or elevated TyG-BMI may play an important role in predicting the occurrence of cardiometabolic comorbidities in CLD patients.

Discussion

This cohort study suggests that patients with CLD and high TyG-BMIs are much more vulnerable to developing cardiometabolic comorbidities, as exemplified by CKM syndrome. Moreover, compared with CLD patients with a steadily low TyG-BMI, those with a steadily high TyG-BMI or an increasing TyG-BMI are at greater risk of contracting cardiometabolic comorbidities. Our study highlights the incidence of CKM syndrome in patients with CLD, provides insights into the prognosis of CLD patients, indicating that CLD patients need to be aware of the progression of cardiometabolic comorbidities, monitoring and controlling their TyG-BMI might be a good way to predict and mitigate the risk.

As a vital metabolic organ of the human body, when hepatic function is impaired due to a certain etiology of CLD, the probability of developing cardiovascular diseases such as diabetes, CKD and CVD increases [21–23]. As confirmed in this study, CLD patients are more vulnerable to cardiometabolic comorbidities, particularly CKM syndrome. The typical characteristics of CKM syndrome are the abnormal function of adipose tissue and the release of inflammatory factors by abnormal adipose tissue, which can lead to damage to the blood vessel walls, such as vascular sclerosis, which in turn affects the kidneys and ultimately leads to CVD events [24–27]. The "hepato-adipo-cardio-renal axes" have been recommended due to the crosstalk and interplay between these organs [28]. The impairment of liver function can lead to disturbances in lipid and glucose metabolism, which in turn can trigger a series of comorbidities. Additionally, the progression of CLD is linked to inflammatory cytokines which generates the activation of Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription pathways [29]. These and various related intracellular signals lead to ongoing release of proinflammatory mediators, profibrotic factors, and immune cell recruitment, resulting in CKM syndrome [30]. The TyG index has been extensively studied and is recognized as a marker associated with a broad spectrum of cardiometabolic disorders. Beyond its established relationship with diabetes mellitus and its complications, the TyG index has been implicated in conditions such as myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, cognitive impairment, and obstructive sleep apnea [31]. For instance, Liu et al. reported a U-shaped association between the TyG index and the incidence of atrial fibrillation [32]. Given the high risk of cardiometabolic diseases among CLD patients, whether the TyG index can be applied for risk stratification to improve their prognosis remains an important issue warranting further investigation. As demonstrated in the current study, the risk of type 2 diabetes was 3.88 times greater in CLD patients with high TyG-BMIs than in those with low TyG-BMIs; 1.42 times greater for CKD; 1.48 times greater for CVD and all these led to 2.40 times greater for CKM. This finding is consistent with studies by Sehrish Kamal showing that the use of statins reduces all-cause mortality in patients with CLD, which also indirectly verifies the significance of our results [33]. With the increasing number of CLD patients and growing global burden of CLD, employing TyG-BMI as a predictive marker for the development of cardiometabolic diseases in this population may constitute a promising strategy for early risk stratification and management.

In our further study on the relationship between the TyG-BMI trajectory and the occurrence of cardiometabolic comorbidities in CLD patients, we also proved that if CLD patients do not control their TyG-BMI and allow it to increase or remain at a high value, their chances of developing CKM conditions will also increase. Compared to low and stable TyG-BMI trajectory, the probability of CVD increased by 1.44 times if TyG-BMI remained high; the probability of CKD increased by 1.68 times if TyG-BMI remained high and by 1.62 times if TyG-BMI increased; and the probability of diabetes increased by 1.85 times if TyG-BMI remained high and 2.19 times if TyG-BMI increased. However, our study did not find a positive association between decreased TyG-BMI and lower risk of developing CKM conditions. We speculate that this may be related to the relatively short duration of the window for the decline in TyG-BMI in our study. Therefore, the decreased TyG-BMI in the past 1–2 years may not have a significant protective effect on CKM in the short term. However, our data lacks longer-term follow-up, so we cannot draw the above conclusions at present.

Strengths and limitations

Our study underscores the clinical value of TyG-BMI as a practical and reliable indicator for predicting cardiometabolic comorbidities in patients with CLD. Given the increasing burden of liver diseases, early identification of high-risk patients is critical. Our study suggested that TyG-BMI is an excellent indicator for predicting cardiometabolic comorbidities in CLD patients. Continuous monitoring of TyG-BMI trajectories in CLD patients may facilitate the early identification of individuals at elevated risk for cardiometabolic comorbidities, thereby enabling timely interventions and improving clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the TyG-BMI index is simple to measure and easy to apply, enhancing its practicality for routine clinical use. Taken together, incorporating TyG-BMI into routine clinical assessment may facilitate early intervention, individualized management, and potentially improve long-term outcomes of CLD patients.

Notably, our study has several limitations. First, owing to the particularity of this database, this study can represent only the Chinese population, and the relationship of the TyG-BMI with cardiometabolic comorbidities in other ethnic groups with CLD needs to be further confirmed. Second, this is a prospective cohort study, so confounding factors may not have been fully adjusted for, and residual confounding may still exist. Third, the TyG-BMI data in CHARLS database are currently limited, so the relationship between TyG-BMI trajectories and diseases over a longer time frame still requires further research.

Conclusion

Overall, our study revealed that CLD patients were more vulnerable to cardiometabolic complications, especially CKM syndrome and that high levels of baseline TyG-BMI was a valuable indicator for identifying CLD patients who were at greater risk of developing cardiometabolic comorbidities. Moreover, steady high or increasing levels of TyG-BMI were associated with cardiometabolic comorbidities as well, monitoring TyG-BMI could be a good way to predict and mitigate the risk. We provide new insights into the extrahepatic complications and prognosis of patients with CLD and offer preventive measures.
