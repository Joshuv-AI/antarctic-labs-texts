Six-year antimicrobial resistance patterns of Escherichia coli isolates from different hospitals in Gaza, Palestine
Authors: Nabil Abdullah El Aila, Khalid Ibrahim Abu El Aish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEscherichia coli is the foremost agent responsible for infections in humans, encompassing those affecting the urinary tract, ears, wounds, and various other regions. The escalating global issue of antimicrobial resistance in E. coli is raising significant apprehension. The aim of this study was to assess the frequency and susceptibility to antibiotics of E. coli based on gender, age, and the type of specimen.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on culture outcomes from a total of 7,656 E. coli specimens primarily from urine, pus, sputum, vaginal swabs, and other specimens including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), catheter tips, ear discharges and eye swabs, and ascitic fluid. Patients included 5,285 females (69%) and 2,371 males (31%), with age groups ranging from neonates (1 day to 29 days) to adults (> 12 years). The antimicrobial susceptibility was tested using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method.ResultsE. coli strains displayed the highest levels of resistance to ampicillin and co-amoxiclav, with resistance rates reaching 90.3% and 78.7%, respectively. Conversely, these strains showed the lowest resistance to colistin (6.3%), followed by meropenem (9.9%) and amikacin (10.6%). Resistance patterns appeared to vary between males and females across most antibiotics, with similar trends observed across different age groups. Colistin resistance was notably higher among younger patients. Among the various specimen types analyzed, sputum samples generally exhibited the highest resistance rates for many antibiotics when compared to other specimen types.ConclusionThe resistance pattern of E. coli varies depending on gender, age, and specimen sources, and this should be taken into account when treating E. coli infections.

Introduction

E. coli is among the most prevalent bacteria worldwide, commonly found in the human and animal gut, as well as in water, soil, and vegetation. Certain strains possess virulence factors—such as adhesins, toxins, and invasins—that enable them to cause a wide range of infections, including such as urinary tract infections [1–3] bloodstream infections [4], skin infections, ear infections [5], and diarrhea [6]. It is also a leading cause of food- and waterborne diarrhea, particularly in developing regions, contributing to high mortality rates among children under five. The increasing antibiotic resistance of E. coli has become a significant global public health concern [7].

The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains poses challenges for effective treatment, necessitating continuous surveillance and updated resistance data.

On a global scale, antimicrobial resistance represents a significant and pressing public health issue, especially in developing nations characterized by endemic infectious diseases, malnutrition, and poverty [8–10]. This resistance stands as a primary reason for the failure of treatments for infectious diseases, including those stemming from E. coli infections, which in turn lead to heightened rates of illness, death, and healthcare expenses [11]. The resistance of E. coli to antimicrobial drugs poses significant challenges to healthcare systems [12, 13]. This situation complicates the results of treatments, raises treatment expenses, and reduces the available therapeutic choices, thus contributing to the emergence of a post-antimicrobial era where some of the most potent drugs become less effective [14].

The prevalence and susceptibility patterns of E. coli display significant disparities across different geographical regions, diverse populations, clinical specimens, and various settings [15].

Gaza faces unique healthcare challenges, including limited access to advanced antimicrobial therapies and a high burden of infectious diseases. The region’s constrained healthcare infrastructure may contribute to increased antibiotic misuse, further driving resistance. This study is crucial in providing localized resistance data that can guide treatment strategies and inform public health policies in the region.

Regular surveillance of antibiotic resistance yields valuable information for guiding antibiotic treatment and managing resistance. To our knowledge, this is the first study in Gaza that comprehensively analyzes the antibiotic resistance patterns of E. coli isolates from clinical samples, helping to bridge the knowledge gap in antimicrobial resistance in the region. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli sourced from a variety of clinical origins.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the results of cultures and the antimicrobial susceptibility of a total of 7,656 E. coli isolates from urine, pus, blood, sputum, vaginal swabs, and other specimens, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), catheter tips, ear discharges and eye swabs, and ascitic fluid constituted only (43) 0.6% of the total. This study was descriptive and observational in nature, with no random or stratified sampling technique employed. Overall, a total of 7,656 E. coli isolates were identified across both hospitals. They were collected between November 1, 2017, and June 31, 2023, with 3,435 (44.9%) originating from Rantisi-Nasr Hospital which specialized on children and 4,221 (55.1%) from Al Shifa Medical Complex, which is the general referral hospital in Gaza City, utilizing the hospital’s information and support system. The study targeted patients across different age groups, categorized as infants (0–2 years), children (3–12 years), adolescents (13–18 years), adults (19–60 years), and elderly (> 60 years), to assess the distribution of E. coli infections among various age groups and genders. The data for this study were retrospectively extracted from the hospitals’ information and support systems, which archive routine clinical microbiology records. The culture isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results were obtained as part of standard diagnostic procedures rather than being generated specifically for this study. Both hospitals followed the same standardized protocols established by the Ministry of Health for culture and AST and adhered to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. All E. coli isolates identified during the six-year period were included in the study, while those with incomplete AST data were excluded from the analysis. The number of excluded isolates was very small, constituting only a negligible portion of the dataset, and their exclusion is not expected to have affected the study’s overall results or resistance patterns in any significant way.

Ethical consideration

All ethical considerations and guidelines relating to the collection of these data were followed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Department of Human Resources and Development in the Ministry of Health-Gaza. The ethical committee of the Health Information Unit – Health Research Department, within the Department of Human Resources and Development of the Ministry of Health – Gaza, granted informed consent and approval for data usage under reference number 1,035,897.

Culture of clinical isolates

Specimens were collected in a sterile manner, following established procedures. The clinical samples that have been collected from the hospital include urine, pus, blood, and sputum. Early morning urine samples were collected using clean-catch midstream techniques and placed in wide-mouth glass containers. These urine samples were then cultured on MacConkey agar, and blood agar (HiMedia, India). Calibrated wire loops were used for inoculation, and the plates were aerobically incubated at 37 °C for 24 h [16]. Significant bacteriuria was defined as a urine culture yielding more than 105 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml of urine. E. coli were identified using standard microbiological techniques [17]. Samples from discharging ears, vaginal swab, pus from wound, eye swab, were collected using sterile cotton swabs. Sputum specimens were collected in wide-mouth sterile containers. Blood samples were taken from patients aseptically in blood culture bottles. In addition, C.S.F. and ascitic fluids were collected aseptically in sterile tubes. These specimens were plated on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar plates (HiMedia, India). These plates were aerobically incubated at 37 °C and examined after 24 and 48 h.

Biochemical characterization

Suspected E. coli colonies were subjected to additional identification procedures involving Gram stain and biochemical tests [18]. The identification process encompassed a battery of tests, including the nitrate reduction test, citrate utilization test, oxidase test, H2S gas production test, methyl-red test, indole test, urease test, Voges-Proskauer test, and lactose fermentation test [19]. Further testing for E. coli confirmation included biochemical identification using API 20E test strips (BioMe′rieux, Marcy I′Etoile, France).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The modified Kirby Bauer disc diffusion method, as recommended by CLSI guidelines [20], was utilized for antibiotic susceptibility testing on Mueller-Hinton agar. Before inoculation, the swab stick was dipped into a bacterial suspension with visual turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standards. The study included several antibiotics including: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20/10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), cephazolin (30 µg), ampicillin (2 µg), nalidixic acid (30 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), colistin (10 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), pipracillin-tazobactam (100/10 µg), cephalexin (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), co-trimoxazole (25 µg), meropenem (10 µg), and doxycycline (30 µg). After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, diameter of the zone of inhibition was measured to the nearest millimeter and isolates were classified as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to CLSI-specified interpretive criteria.

Standard quality control strains (E. coli ATCC 25922) were used in antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were organized and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, including frequencies and percentages. Antibiotic resistance patterns were presented across demographic variables and specimen types. No inferential statistical tests were applied due to unequal sample distribution and the descriptive nature of the study.

Results

The distribution of E. coli specimen types and their corresponding percentages across the two hospitals is summarized in Table 1. The data illustrate the distribution of E. coli isolates at Rantisi-Nasr Hospital and Al Shifa Medical Complex. Urine samples were the most common, comprising 74.7% of all isolates, with a significantly higher proportion from Rantisi-Nasr Hospital (94.3%) compared to Al Shifa (58.7%). Pus samples represented 18.7% of the total, primarily from Al Shifa (32.6%). Blood, sputum, and vaginal swabs accounted for smaller proportions (1.6%, 1.5%, and 2.9%, respectively), with vaginal swabs exclusively collected at Al Shifa. Other specimens, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), catheter tips, ear discharges and eye swabs, and ascitic fluid, constituted only (43) 0.6% of the total. Overall, a total of 7,656 E. coli isolates were identified across both hospitals.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of E coli isolates to different antibiotics

The resistance results of E. coli isolates are presented in Table 2, showcasing the susceptibility of these isolates to various antibiotics. Notably, E. coli isolates exhibited the highest resistance rates to ampicillin and co-amoxiclav, with 90.3% and 78.7% resistance, respectively. In contrast, E. coli isolates displayed the lowest resistance to colistin, which was only 6.3%, followed by meropenem (9.9%) and amikacin (10.6%). Approximately 42.3% of E. coli isolates showed resistance to ciprofloxacin. Additionally, high resistance rates were observed for third-generation cephalosporins, with 47.3% resistance to ceftazidime, 55.2% to ceftriaxone, and 56.0% to cefotaxime.

As shown in Table 3, resistance rates varied between male and female patients for most antibiotics, except for nitrofurantoin, chloramphenicol, co-trimoxazole, and ampicillin, where resistance levels appeared more comparable. Specimen-Based differences in resistance of E. coli isolates to antibiotics

Significant disparities in the antibiotic resistance profiles of E. coli isolates were observed across different specimen types, as noticed in Table 5. Notably, pus specimens exhibited the highest resistance rates for many antibiotics, including chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, doxycycline, ceftriaxone, cefuroxime, and co-trimoxazole. In sputum specimens, E. coli isolates demonstrated high resistance to meropenem, amikacin, piperacillin-tazobactam, gentamycin, cefotaxime, and cefazolin. Discussion

E. coli is a Gram-negative bacterium commonly associated with antibiotic resistance in hospital settings. This microorganism is responsible for a range of infections, such as urinary tract infections, gastroenteritis, septicemia, and meningitis [21]. As far as we know, this is the first study conducted in Gaza-Palestine to examine the antibiotic susceptibility patterns of E. coli in relation to gender, age, and specimen type. The ongoing antimicrobial resistance trends observed in E. coli represent a significant global public health concern, resulting in severe issues like extended hospital stays and treatment ineffectiveness [8, 9]. As a result, this research sought to identify the antibiotic susceptibility patterns of E. coli samples collected from diverse clinical sources within Gaza City, Palestine. Most E. coli isolates in this study were obtained from urine samples, accounting for 74%. These findings are consistent with previous studies [22–25]. Other studies have reported a high isolation rate of E. coli from wound and sputum samples [26].

Within the patient population, the most extensive category, encompassing 5,285 individuals (69.0%), comprised females. A similar trend was observed in an Iraqi study, where E. coli isolates were more prevalent among females (70.7%) [27]. This was inconsistent with a study from Saudi Arabia, which reported that 71.6% of their clinical samples were derived from male patients, while only 28.4% were obtained from females [26]. In this research, E. coli exhibited a notable level of resistance to antimicrobial drugs.

Antibiotic resistance in Escherichia coli arises through several mechanisms, including β-lactamase production, target site modifications, efflux pump overexpression, and decreased membrane permeability [28]. These adaptations reduce treatment efficacy and complicate clinical management [29, 30]. Resistant E. coli strains are associated with longer illness duration, increased healthcare costs, and greater morbidity and mortality, presenting serious challenges in both community and hospital settings. This public health threat necessitates continuous surveillance, responsible antibiotic use, and new antimicrobial strategies [29, 30]. The development of resistance is driven by factors such as antibiotic overuse, horizontal gene transfer, and healthcare-associated transmission. Although molecular analysis was not conducted in this study, the observed resistance patterns are consistent with global trends involving β-lactamases, efflux systems, and target al.terations [31].

In Gaza, the high burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is driven by a range of local challenges, including the unregulated and excessive use of antibiotics, self-medication enabled by over-the-counter access, poor adherence to prescribed treatments, the circulation of substandard medications, and the consumption of food products derived from animals treated with antibiotics and the transmission of resistant strains among individuals [32]. Compounding these issues are restricted access to advanced antimicrobial therapies, a healthcare system repeatedly disrupted by conflict, and inadequate implementation of infection prevention and control (IPC) practices. Inappropriate empirical antibiotic prescribing is widespread, largely due to the absence of reliable diagnostic tools and the easy, unregulated availability of antibiotics. Additional factors such as the presence of comorbidities, repeated antibiotic exposure, and environmental contamination—particularly from untreated hospital wastewater and inadequate sanitation—further accelerate the spread of resistant organisms.

While the resistance patterns observed in this study are consistent with those reported globally and regionally, Gaza’s unique circumstances provide important context. The prolonged humanitarian crisis—characterized by recurrent violence, chronic electricity shortages, and overburdened health infrastructure—poses significant obstacles to combating antimicrobial resistance. Diagnostic limitations delay the identification of resistant infections, while disruptions in medication supply and unregulated antibiotic access promote misuse. Together, these conditions foster the selection and transmission of drug-resistant E. coli strains.

The current study found the highest resistance rates in E. coli against ampicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanate, which is consistent with prior findings reported in similar regional and international studies [24, 27, 33–38]. Among tested antibiotics, colistin, meropenem, amikacin, and piperacillin-tazobactam were the most active agent against E. coli with a susceptibility rate of 92%, 88.8%, 84.4% and 80.3% respectively. In their study, Niranjan and Malini found that E. coli isolates exhibited susceptibility to amikacin (82.6%), piperacillin-tazobactam (78.2%), nitrofurantoin (82.1%), and imipenem (98.9%) [36]. Similarly, Sharma and colleagues reported that the smallest resistance level was observed against imipenem, ranging from 11.86% in 2012 to 11.36% in 2014 [27, 39, 40].

Carbapenems, such as meropenem, are among the last-resort antibiotics used to treat various bacterial infections, including those caused by E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Recently, there has been growing concern about the emergence of drug resistance to carbapenem antibiotics in these coliform bacteria, primarily due to the production of New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM-1) and other carbapenemase enzymes. While the development of new antibiotics targeting these resistant strains remains challenging, several therapeutic options, including combination therapies and older antibiotics, continue to be explored for treating carbapenem-resistant infections. The global spread of these resistance mechanisms remains a serious concern [41]. In contrast to global reports of rising carbapenem resistance, the current study observed a relatively low resistance rate to meropenem (9.9%), suggesting its continued effectiveness in this region.

In our study, a high resistance rate of E. coli to third-generation cephalosporins was reported which coincides with other studies [35, 42, 43].This may be due to the increasing clinical use of third-generation cephalosporins. While fluoroquinolone is generally regarded as an effective broad-spectrum antibiotic, the present study found a relatively high level of resistance (42.3%). This result is in agreement with previous investigations [42]. High rate of fluoroquinolone resistance by E. coli has also been detected in the Asia-Pacific region [44, 45]. Such high level of resistance of 58.5% and 75% was documented from studies done in Nepal and India respectively [35, 40, 46].

The data of antibiotic profile presented in this study coincides with previous studies in Gaza and West bank [47–57]. The present study observed gender-based differences in antibiotic resistance among E. coli isolates, with resistance rates generally higher in males than females for most antibiotics. Resistance levels for nitrofurantoin, chloramphenicol, co-trimoxazole, and ampicillin appeared comparable between the two genders. These observations are descriptive and should be interpreted with caution. In another study, Hossain et al. [58] concluded that the isolates obtained from males exhibited greater resistance to amikacin, colistin, and nitrofurantoin.

In addition to the type and location of the infection, it’s important to consider the patient’s age when choosing antibiotics [59]. Differences in E. coli resistance patterns were observed between Al Shifa Medical Complex (mainly serving adults) and Rantisi-Nasr Hospital (specializing in pediatric care). Resistance appeared higher in younger patients for some antibiotics, such as colistin. These findings are based also on descriptive trends and should be cautiously interpreted. In accordance with our study, Hossain et al. [58], reported that amikacin and nitrofurantoin were more effective against E. coli isolates obtained from people of 60 years of age than their younger counterparts. Furthermore, Wang et al. [60] demonstrated that the prevalence of levofloxacin resistance was greater among elderly patients compared to children.

Variation in antibiotic resistance among E. coli isolates was observed across different specimen types. Notably, sputum specimens showed relatively higher resistance rates for many antibiotics compared to other specimen types, based on the descriptive data.

In a similar study, Wang et al. [60] found that E. coli strains exhibited higher rates of drug resistance in respiratory tract infections compared to urinary tract and blood infections [56].

This research possesses several notable strengths. It stands as the inaugural investigation to shed light on the antibiotic susceptibility and resistance profiles of E. coli samples derived from various clinical specimens, taking into account distinctions in age, gender, and specimen type. These findings hold significant value for physicians, serving as a foundation for the prescription of appropriate antibiotics for hospital patients. This, in turn, aids in the prevention of the development of new antibiotic-resistant strains due to inaccurate usage. Furthermore, this information could assist authorities in developing antibacterial prescription guidelines. Although gender- and age-based differences in resistance patterns were observed descriptively, these trends should be interpreted with caution due to potential demographic and institutional sampling biases, as well as the absence of statistical analysis.

In the Gaza Strip, a conflict-affected and resource-limited setting with high rates of antimicrobial resistance, these findings highlight the urgent need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship, rational antibiotic use, and strengthened infection control measures.

High resistance rates may reflect widespread, unregulated antibiotic use, while lower rates could indicate restricted application and efficacy of last-resort drugs. Gender and age-related differences in resistance may relate to variable antibiotic exposure and immune responses, warranting further research [58]. Longitudinal and environmental surveillance studies are essential to monitor resistance dynamics and assess the contribution of contaminated water and food sources to the dissemination of resistant E. coli strains.

Limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, due to the highly unbalanced distribution of specimen types—most notably urine samples accounting for 74.7%—and the absence of adjustments for multiple comparisons, all statistical tests and reported p-values were removed to avoid over interpretation and ensure scientific integrity. Second, the retrospective nature of the study may have resulted in incomplete or inconsistent data, particularly concerning individual antibiotic usage and clinical outcomes. Also, due to data access constraints and the fragmented nature of medical record systems in Gaza, it was not feasible to incorporate these additional clinical parameters in this analysis. The lack of patient-level clinical data limited the ability to explore associations between resistance trends and variables such as antibiotic prescribing practices, treatment adherence, or healthcare access. Third, Molecular data are not presented in this study due to the lack of molecular microbiological capacity within Ministry of Health laboratories, with no access to molecular diagnostic platforms to investigate resistance genes or mechanisms, largely due to chronic resource constraints and infrastructure damage resulting from prolonged conflict. Additionally, although the study was conducted in two major hospitals in Gaza City, the findings may not be representative of rural or less urbanized areas. The reliance on non-standardized sampling and the predominance of urine specimens may have introduced selection bias and affected the comparability of resistance patterns across specimen types. The overrepresentation of female patients and the varying age distributions at the participating hospitals may also have influenced gender- and age-related resistance patterns. Furthermore, the study did not assess environmental sources of antimicrobial resistance, such as contaminated water or food, which could be relevant in this setting.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data on E. coli resistance patterns in Gaza over a six-year period. It highlights the need for prospective studies with standardized specimen collection, integration of clinical data, and molecular diagnostics to improve understanding of antimicrobial resistance and guide more effective interventions in resource-limited and conflict-affected settings.

Conclusion

The study concluded that the resistance pattern of E. coli varies depending on gender, age, and specimen sources, and this should be taken into account when treating E. coli infections. The findings of this study highlight the urgent need for context-specific antimicrobial stewardship initiatives and health policies tailored to Gaza’s distinctive epidemiological, infrastructural, and socio-political realities.

Further research on resistance mechanisms and alternative therapeutic approaches is essential to address this growing public health concern.
