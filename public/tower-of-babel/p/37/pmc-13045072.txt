Does transcranial direct current stimulation enhance sensorimotor recovery in chronic ankle instability? A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhaohong Zeng, Zhen Xu, Bin Shen, Xin Liang, Xuekai Zong, Jiaxin Luo, Junhong Zhou, Weijie Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChronic ankle instability (CAI) is related to sensorimotor dysfunction and maladaptive neuroplasticity. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is considered a potential method for CAI rehabilitation, but its efficacy and underlying mechanisms remain inconclusive. This study aimed to comprehensively synthesise the current evidence of tDCS for sensorimotor recovery in CAI and evaluate its efficacy, moderating factors and potential mechanisms.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Embase, SPORTDiscus and Scopus were searched in January 2026. The random effects model was employed to meta-analyses to estimate pooled effect sizes. Subgroup analyses and meta-regressions were performed to reveal potential moderators. Risk of bias was evaluated using the RoB 2 tool, and methodological quality was assessed using the PEDro scale.ResultsEleven trials involving 310 participants were included. Compared with control, tDCS significantly improved balance control (g = 0.27, p = 0.009) and sensorimotor functional performance (g = 0.16, p = 0.023), whereas no statistically significant change was observed in kinematics (g = 0.16, p = 0.081), neural excitability (g = − 0.07, p = 0.633) and patient-reported outcomes (g = 0.11, p = 0.558). Subgroup analyses showed that online stimulation, High-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) and the combination with foot core exercise (FCE) appeared to yield enhanced improvements in balance.ConclusionstDCS is of great promise to improve balance control and sensorimotor function in CAI. Still the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms should be more explicitly characterized. Future studies are warranted to examine such benefits of interventions using tDCS for individuals suffering from CAI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01916-9.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Chronic ankle instability, Sensorimotor recovery, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Lateral ankle sprain is amongst the most common musculoskeletal injuries, with an estimated 712,000 cases occurring worldwide each day [1–4]. Approximately 40% of individuals develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) after the initial sprain, which is characterized by recurrent sprains, “giving way”, and sensorimotor deficits (e.g., diminished balance control performance) [5]. These deficits not only diminish quality of life but also accelerate the progression of post‑traumatic osteoarthritis, imposing a considerable socioeconomic burden [6]. Thus, effective rehabilitation strategies/programs for CAI and its related functional impairments are highly demanded.

Traditional CAI rehabilitation mainly focuses on ligament protection [7], muscle strengthening [8], and proprioceptive training [9]. However, these programs are often time-consuming with modest effects that many patients still experience recurrent sprains after the rehabilitation [10, 11]. This may be because these traditional protocols primarily focus on the peripheral elements (e.g., muscular strength and joint stability), while overlooking a critical aspect pertaining to CAI-related deficits of sensorimotor function, that is, the alterations in supraspinal regions and networks (e.g., altered brain activity and gray matter volume) [5, 12–16]. Xiao et al. [17] have shown that, for example, individuals with unilateral CAI (n = 31) exhibited lower cortical activation in the sensorimotor network during a dorsi-plantarflexion task compared to healthy controls (HC); notably, this diminished activation was negatively associated with sensorimotor function (plantarflexion position sense). Thus, it is of great promise to optimize the rehabilitation strategy for sensorimotor deficits by targeting these supraspinal elements that are altered in CAI [18].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is one such promising approach with the ability to modulate the cortical excitability via micro-level direct current as delivered by scalp electrodes non-invasively [19]. Studies have shown that using tDCS targeting the primary motor cortex (M1) can augment the sensorimotor function in different populations, including HC [20] and stroke survivors [21]. For example, anodal tDCS over the M1 significantly improved motor performance in chronic stroke patients when combined with motor training [21]. Recently, evidence has emerged that strategies of using tDCS only or in combination with other interventions can help improve sensorimotor function in CAI [22, 23]. For example, Bruce et al. [23] observed that a 4-week program of tDCS paired with eccentric training significantly improved dynamic balance in CAI (n = 13) compared to sham combined with eccentric training. However, the observations on such effects of tDCS on sensorimotor function are inconsistent across studies. Despite those with positive results, Beyraghi et al. [24], for example, observed that 4-week tDCS combined with balance training did not induce significantly greater effects on the Y-balance test in CAI (n = 15) compared to sham combined with balance training. Several factors may contribute to such inconsistent observations, including the different designs of intervention protocols (e.g., stimulation modality, stimulation timing, current intensity, combined rehabilitation protocol, and number of stimulation sessions). Recently, Zheng et al. [25] summarized the effects of tDCS on balance outcomes in CAI, and suggested that tDCS improves balance only when paired with non-balance training, but did not examine the potential moderators to such effects. Thus, a comprehensive synthesis is needed to quantify efficacy and clarify key moderators. Meanwhile, the neurophysiological mechanisms that mediate the impact of tDCS on sensorimotor function in CAI, which are critical to the efficacy of tDCS, have remained poorly understood. Taken together, it is highly demanded to comprehensively summarize current research progress and examine potential contributors to such inconsistency of observations across studies, as well as the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms, thus providing critical knowledge to inform appropriate design of tDCS in future studies and rehabilitative practice for CAI.

To address this critical need, we here performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize the current evidence on the effects of tDCS on sensorimotor function in CAI, quantify its efficacy, identify moderating factors, and explore underlying neurophysiological mechanisms.

Methods

This systematic review followed the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [26] and the best-practice methods for conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses in the health sciences [27]. The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/e46cw), and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Information sources

From inception to January 13, 2026, a systematic literature search was performed in five databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, SPORTDiscus, and Scopus). Supplemental searches involved a hand-search of bibliographies from the selected studies and associated meta-analyses. Eligibility was restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) available as full-text articles in English.

Search strategy

An extensive search strategy was applied to ensure the comprehensive identification of relevant studies. The search terms covered ankle instability (“Ankle Injuries“[Mesh] OR Injury, Ankle OR Ankle Sprains OR Sprains, Ankle OR chronic ankle instability OR ankle instability OR ankle sprain OR functional ankle instability OR mechanical ankle instability OR Ankle) and (“Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation“[Mesh] OR tDCS OR Cathodal Stimulation OR Anodal Stimulation) OR (“Transcranial electrical stimulation” OR TES). The database search strings and retrieval counts are showed in Supplementary Table S2.

Selection process

The systematic literature retrieval was performed by two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and X.L.). All retrieved records were deduplicated using EndNote 20 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA) and manually verified by a reviewer (Z.H.Z.). Two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and Z.X.) independently screened the remaining records in two stages: first, title and abstract screening, followed by full-text evaluation. Any discrepancies were addressed through discussion, with an experienced reviewer (W.J.F.) making the final decision when necessary. The screening and selection process is presented in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1A).

Workflow diagram. A PRISMA flow diagram, B data extraction details and C data analysis

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included on the basis of the criteria established by the population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and study design framework [28].

Participants were required to meet the International Ankle Consortium selection criteria for CAI [29]. Eligible interventions included tDCS, delivered either alone or in combination with rehabilitation. Control included conventional rehabilitation (e.g. foot core exercise (FCE), balance exercise and BOSU exercise) and sham stimulation or no treatment control. Eligible studies had to report at least one sensorimotor outcome, including measures of balance control, such as the Y-Balance Test (YBT), Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT), and metrics of center of pressure (COP); neural excitability, such as contingent negative variation (CNV) and corticospinal excitability; functional performance of sensorimotor task, such as the side-hop test, reaction time; kinematics, such as ankle kinematics, range of motion (ROM), angle, and velocity and patient-reported outcomes such as the score of Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT). Studies focusing exclusively on molecular/cellular mechanisms were excluded. Only RCTs were included, and all other study types (e.g. non-randomized or acute experimental studies, case reports and reviews) were excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and J.X.L.) independently extracted data in duplicate. As shown in Fig. 1B, the extracted variables included (1) study characteristics, (2) participant details, (3) intervention characteristics, (4) comparator details and (5) outcomes (means and standard deviations at baseline, post-intervention and follow-up, when available). When data were incomplete or presented graphically, the corresponding authors were contacted, and absent responses were addressed by digitizing figures with the WebPlotDigitizer online tool (version 4.6) [30]. Studies with unavailable essential data were excluded from the meta-analysis. Extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy by two reviewers (X.K.Z. and B.S.), and any discrepancies were addressed and resolved via discussion.

Risk-of-bias and quality of method evaluation

The risk of bias for each included RCT was assessed independently by two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and Z.X.) with the use of the Cochrane RoB 2 tool [31, 32]. Any discrepancies were addressed through discussion, with an experienced reviewer (W.J.F.) making the final decision. Meanwhile, two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and B.S.) evaluated methodological quality by using the PEDro scale [33], with scores ≥ 6 indicative of high-quality studies. The results of both assessments are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. The use of these two tools allows for the assessment of both the potential for bias and the quality of reporting specific to the rehabilitation field [34]. If the two tools yielded discordant appraisals, we prioritized RoB 2 for risk-of-bias interpretation and reported PEDro scores descriptively.

Certainty assessment

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology was used to assess certainty of evidence [35, 36]. Two reviewers (Z.H.Z. and Z.X.) independently evaluated the certainty of findings with a third author (W.J.F.) reviewing the assessments.

Statistics

The data analysis procedure (Fig. 1C) followed established methodological recommendations. Effect sizes (Hedges’ g; positive values favoring the intervention) and their variances were computed in Comprehensive Meta Analysis software [37]. Effect sizes were classified as small (Hedges’ g < 0.20), moderate (0.20–0.49), and large (≥ 0.50) [38]. All subsequent analyses, including three-level models and meta-regression, were performed in R (version 4.1.2) with the metafor package (version 3.0; Viechtbauer, 2010), and figures were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.3.6) [39].

Three-level meta-analytic models were fitted following the approach of Assink and Wibbelink to address dependence amongst outcomes within studies (e.g. multiple measures of balance control) [40]. The model partitions variance into level 1 (sampling), level 2 (within-study) and level 3 (between-study) components, enabling inclusion of correlated outcomes and yielding more precise effect estimates [40, 41]. To account for potentially reused subject data across multiple publications (e.g., Huang, Ge, and Gao), effect sizes were nested within a single study cluster in our three-level model. Heterogeneity was assessed using I²; values < 25%, 25%–50%, 50%–75% and > 75% were categorized as low, moderate, substantial and considerable, respectively [42, 43]. Robustness was examined using leave-one-out analyses and influence diagnostics [44, 45]. Studies with Cook’s distance greater than three times the mean or absolute studentized residuals > 3 were classified as influential. Analyses were repeated after their exclusion to assess the stability of the overall effect [46, 47]. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test at level 2 and level 3 [48, 49].

Potential moderators and heterogeneity were investigated using subgroup analysis and meta-regression with categorical and continuous variables [50, 51]. Fitting linear and cubic models evaluated functional form, and the model with the lowest AICc was retained [52].

Results

Retrieved studies

As illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1A), the initial database search yielded 515 records. After deduplication (n = 207), 308 records remained for title and abstract screening. Among these, 26 full texts were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 11 studies [22–24, 53–60] met al.l the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis.

As shown in Table 1, the study characteristics and intervention details are summarized. The 11 studies included 310 participants. Eight of the studies assessed balance control [22–24, 55, 57–60], three studies evaluated neural excitability [23, 24, 58], two studies reported measures of sensorimotor functional performance [23, 58], five studies examined kinematic outcomes (e.g., ankle range of motion, joint angles, and angular velocity) [24, 53, 54, 56, 57], and three studies evaluated patient-reported outcomes (FAAM and CAIT) [23, 24, 57]. Notably, several studies contributed data to multiple outcome domains.

With respect to stimulation protocols, the stimulation targets included the M1 (n = 8) [22, 23, 54–57, 59, 60], the supplementary motor area (SMA; n = 2) [24, 53], or the prefrontal cortex (F3/F4; n = 1) [58]; and stimulation intensities were between 1.5 and 2.0 mA (i.e., 1.5, 1.8, or 2.0 mA). Main effects

It was observed that as compared to control, tDCS induced significantly moderate improvements in balance control (g = 0.27, 95% CI [0.07, 0.47], p = 0.009, Q(136) = 188.33, p = 0.002, τ²-level 2 = 0.0015, τ²-level 3 = 0.0627, I²-level 2 = 0.7%, I²-level 3 = 30.1%, PI [-0.27, 0.81]) and small improvements in sensorimotor functional performance (g = 0.16, 95% CI [0.02, 0.30], p = 0.023, Q(28) = 25.98, p = 0.574, τ²-level 2 = 0, τ²-level 3 = 0, I²-level 2 = 0.1%, I²-level 3 = 0%, PI [0.02, 0.31]). However, pooled estimates indicated no significant change in kinematics (g = 0.22, 95% CI [− 0.03, 0.48], p = 0.081, Q(25) = 33.09, p = 0.128, τ²-level 2 = 0. 018, τ²-level 3 = 0.029, I²-level 2 = 10.7%, I²-level 3 = 17.1%, PI [− 0.29, 0.74]), neural excitability (g = − 0.08, 95% CI [− 0.34, 0.21], p = 0.567, Q(175) = 259.57, p < 0.001, τ²-level 2 = 0.066, τ²-level 3 = 0.049, I²-level 2 = 26.2%, I²-level 3 = 19.6%, PI [− 0.79, 0.66]) and patient-reported outcomes (g = 0.11, 95% CI [− 0.29, 0.52], p = 0.558, Q(16) = 10.59, p = 0.833, τ²-level 2 = 0, τ²-level 3 = 0.065, I²-level 2 = 0%, I²-level 3 = 30.6%, PI [− 0.56, 0.79]) (Fig. 2).

Moderator analysis

We examined whether stimulation timing (online: during task execution; offline: at rest before or after the task [61]), current intensity (1.5, 1.8, and 2.0 mA), stimulation protocol (HD-tDCS vs. conventional tDCS), target region (M1, SMA, and PFC), and rehabilitation protocol (balance training vs. FCE) moderated the effect on balance control.

Significant moderation effects were found for stimulation timing, stimulation protocol and rehabilitation protocol on balance control (test of moderators/QM, p < 0.05, Fig. 3). Specifically, online stimulation significantly improved balance control (g = 0.33, 95% CI [0.10–0.57], p = 0.006), while offline protocols showed no effect (p = 0.894). Similarly, HD-tDCS yielded a significantly larger effect size (g = 0.51, 95% CI [0.23–0.79], p < 0.001), whereas conventional tDCS did not (p = 0.225). Finally, combining tDCS with FCE resulted in significant improvements (g = 0.40, 95% CI [0.28, 0.51], p < 0.001), whereas pairing tDCS with balance training did not (p = 0.276). Although current intensity and target region did not demonstrate significant moderation, the exploratory analysis showed notable efficacy within specific subgroups (test of moderators/QM, p > 0.05). Significant improvements were found for the 2.0 mA intensity (g = 0.37, 95% CI [0.13, 0.60], p = 0.002) and the M1 target region (g = 0.28, 95% CI [0.05, 0.51], p = 0.017).

As shown in Fig. 4, the linear and nonlinear meta-regression models examined the association between the total number of intervention sessions and improvements in balance. The linear model indicated a modest but statistically significant positive association (β = 0.10, p < 0.001). Risk-of-bias and study quality

As shown in Fig. 5, the risk of bias was assessed for all included studies. Most trials used randomization of participants, only four trials [24, 53, 56, 60] clearly described the randomization procedures and being judged as having low risk of bias. By contrast, the remaining trials provided insufficient detail, and they were judged as having some concerns. For blinding, three trials [22, 58, 60] used double-blinded designs, and they were judged to have low risk. The remaining trials were single-blind, and they were judged as having some concerns. Three trials [54, 56, 58] had missing outcome data due to attrition, and they were judged to have some concerns. The remaining trials were judged as low risk. Ten trials [22–24, 53–56, 58–60] that used standardized, validated instruments for outcome measurement were judged to have low risk, whereas one trial [57] that relied on more subjective measures was classified as having some concerns. In general, most trials had some concerns. Notably, the average PEDro score was 6.82, indicating moderate to high methodological quality (Supplementary Table 3).

Risk of publication bias and certainty of evidence

Publication bias was evaluated for each outcome domain using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for asymmetry (Fig. 6), separately at level 2 (within-study) and level 3 (between-study). At Level 2, Egger’s test suggested significant asymmetry for balance control (p < 0.001), neural excitability (p = 0.032), sensorimotor functional performance (p = 0.010), while no significant asymmetry was found for kinematics (p = 0.964) and patient-reported outcomes (p = 0.484). However, funnel plots at level 3 were more symmetrical, and Egger’s tests suggested no significant publication bias (p > 0.05). Furthermore, Trim-and-Fill analyses suggested no missing studies were imputed for kinematics or patient-reported outcomes (k = 0), and the pooled effect sizes remained consistent. For balance control, only one missing study was imputed at level 3 (adjusted g = 0.202), whereas the level 2 analysis imputed 23 missing studies (adjusted g = 0.049). For sensorimotor functional performance, three missing studies were imputed at level 3, attenuating the effect estimate (adjusted g = 0.103). For neural excitability, two missing studies were imputed at level 3, but the adjusted pooled effect remained non-significant.

Risk of publication bias

Evidence of overall certainty for each outcome domain ranged from very low to low (Table 2). In detail, certainty was low for balance control, sensorimotor functional performance and kinematics outcomes, and as very low for neural excitability and patient-reported outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis and leave-one-out analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses indicated that the overall effect size estimates remained stable when any single effect size was removed (Supplementary Figure S2). In particular, the overall effect sizes for balance control, sensorimotor functional performance, neural excitability and kinematics consistently remained within the original 95% CI.

An influence analysis was conducted to determine whether any individual study unduly influenced the results. Two outlying effect sizes were identified for neural excitability outcomes (ID #115 and #116, Supplementary Figure S3). These two studies were removed from neural excitability outcome analyses. No influential cases were detected for other outcomes (Supplementary Figure S2–S5 ).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized current research progress and evidence on the effects of tDCS on sensorimotor function in CAI. We observed that tDCS produced moderate improvements in balance control, small but significant improvements in sensorimotor functional performance; and the subgroup moderator analyses showed that online stimulation, HD-tDCS, and FCE protocols are associated with larger improvements in balance control. These observations suggest that tDCS is of great promise to help balance and functional recovery in CAI, and using the online HD protocol in combination with FCE may help maximize its benefits.

Effects of tDCS on sensorimotor recovery in CAI

This meta-analysis showed that tDCS can induce significant improvements in balance control and sensorimotor functional performance. In the presence of peripheral sensorimotor deficits in CAI, the supraspinal sensorimotor network is reorganized to adapt those peripheral conditions (e.g., diminished afferent sensorimotor inputs), as indicated by alterations in the excitability and resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) in these supraspinal networks (e.g., within-network rsFC of sensorimotor network), an oftentimes overlooked element contributing to the diminished balance performance and sensorimotor functional decline [62]. Therefore, interventions using tDCS targeting these supraspinal regions/networks, either combined with other training or not, may help restore and improve such supraspinal regulatory elements, thus enhancing the sensorimotor performance and balance in individuals with CAI.

No significant pooled effects on neural excitability and patient-reported outcomes are shown. This may be attributed to a very small number of included studies targeting these two aspects with heterogeneous stimulation protocols, thus largely limiting statistical power. Emerging neuroimaging and neurophysiological evidence suggests that individuals with CAI exhibit altered brain activity [12–14]. These alterations have been linked to diminished postural control and sensorimotor performance [63]. In addition, Bruce et al. [23] have demonstrated that tDCS can modulate indices of corticospinal excitability in CAI. Therefore, it is worthwhile to further explore and characterize the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the effects of tDCS in future well-powered studies with rigorous design of intervention.

Moderating factors of tDCS in balance improvement

The efficacy of tDCS in balance improvement appears to be modulated by several factors. Therefore, we further examined potential moderators to help explain variability and to identify conditions under which tDCS may be more likely to yield balance improvements, focusing on stimulation timing, current intensity, stimulation protocol, target brain region, and rehabilitation protocol.

Firstly, online application generated larger balance control gains than offline application. A plausible explanation is that pairing stimulation with active balance training may preferentially influence task-engaged networks, thereby supporting task-specific processing efficiency and practice-related consolidation compared with stimulation delivered at rest [64, 65]. This supports the view that synchronous neural drive with task execution more effectively reinforces task-specific circuits than the same dose applied at rest [20, 66]. However, because the offline subgroup included only a small number of studies, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires confirmation in adequately powered trials.

Secondly, HD-tDCS yielded a significantly larger effect size, whereas conventional tDCS did not. This likely stems from the evidence that conventional tDCS typically uses large sponge electrodes, resulting in diffuse current flow that may lack the spatial precision required to target specific maladaptive circuits in CAI [67]. In contrast, HD-tDCS utilizes smaller, multi-electrode arrays (e.g., 4 × 1 ring configuration) to offer improved focality, potentially allowing for more precise modulation of the sensorimotor cortex [68], thereby maximizing the neuro-modulatory impact on relevant motor networks.

Thirdly, combining tDCS with FCE yielded significant balance improvements, while the combination with balance training did not show a reliable additional effect from stimulation. FCE serves as a targeted strategy that activates the foot core system by enhancing neural drive to intrinsic foot muscles, which are typically inhibited in CAI [69]. Notably, FCE has been shown to reduce short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI), and its bottom-up sensorimotor activation may synergize with the top-down neuromodulatory bias of tDCS to further downregulate SICI and enhance balance control [70]. In contrast, balance training is a more global postural task that is less specific to foot–ankle control, potentially limiting consistent engagement of this inhibitory pathway.

On the other hand, current intensity and target region did not significantly moderate balance outcomes overall, despite significant within-subgroup effects for 2.0 mA and M1. This lack of overall moderation is likely attributable to the limited study count and small cumulative sample sizes in alternative categories, which restricted the statistical power to detect significant between-group variance. Notably, the 2.0 mA of current intensity and M1 of target region were used by substantially large numbers of studies, which can increase precision and the likelihood of statistical significance. While M1 has received the primary focus in these publications, more and more recent evidence has highlighted the importance of the PFC excitability for sensorimotor control in other cohorts (e.g., healthy elders) [71]. Given that CAI patients are more injury-prone when cognitive resources are distracted [72], future studies are worthwhile to explore the effects of tDCS targeting both M1 and PFC simultaneously on the performance in CAI.

Finally, the meta-regression analysis showed an incremental gain in each treatment, indicating a dose–response effect. This pattern means that repeated tDCS may have a cumulative promoting effect on the sensorimotor network along with the increase in stimulation session number. Such progressive enhancement is consistent with the concept of experience-dependent plasticity, where successive stimulation–training pairings consolidate adaptive cortical reorganization [20, 73]. However, this should be taken with cautiousness since only very small number of studies (n = 2) explored the effects of tDCS with more than 17 sessions; therefore, it is highly-demanded to more explicitly explore the optimal number of stimulation sessions that can induce maximum effects on sensorimotor function and balance control performance in older adults.

Publication bias, heterogeneity, certainty of evidence

The interpretation of our findings requires a critical assessment of bias and heterogeneity. Egger’s tests revealed asymmetry at level 2, likely driven by within-study reporting variation or small-study effects, as this asymmetry diminished when aggregated to study level 3 [48, 74]. This suggests that the effect observed may be biased due to small number of included studies. Importantly, Trim-and-Fill analysis confirmed that results for primary outcomes (balance and sensorimotor functional performance) remained robust. Regarding heterogeneity, balance control showed moderate between-study variance (I2 = 30%), suggesting that its efficacy may be parameter-dependent [75]. In contrast, sensorimotor functional performance demonstrated high homogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating consistent benefits across different functional tasks.

According to GRADE methodology, the certainty of evidence ranged from very low to low, primarily downgraded due to risk of bias, imprecision, and small-study effects. While current evidence supports tDCS as a promising adjunct to CAI rehabilitation, the very low certainty for neural excitability and patient-reported outcomes necessitates caution. Future research should prioritize larger, rigorously designed, and preregistered RCTs to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these neural and clinical effects.

Practical implications

The results of this review and meta-analysis suggest the use of tDCS would be a promising approach for the sensorimotor rehabilitation in CAI, which can be implemented in future rehabilitative practice. More importantly, this work provided several parameters that may augment the effects of tDCS, including online stimulation protocol of high-definition montage in combination with FCE. It is important to implement this technique under safety consideration, though no major adverse event of this non-invasive technique is reported.

Future research directions and limitations

This study highlighted several knowledge gaps informing future research, while also acknowledging limitations that constrain the interpretation. First, stimulation parameters and intervention protocols varied substantially, and outcomes were inconsistently defined, which likely contributed to heterogeneity and limited our ability to isolate optimal protocols [76]. Although three-level models were used to account for within-study dependence arising and may have attenuated pooled estimates in some domains [40, 77]. Second, most trials were modestly sized and several outcome domains included few studies, limiting power and precision, particularly for subgroup and meta-regression findings, which should be considered exploratory [78, 79]. Third, long-term durability is largely untested; future trials should include multiple follow-up time points and clinically meaningful endpoints such as re-injury rates to assess durability and translational value [80]. Fourth, few studies linked behavioral change to neurophysiology; future work should integrate structure–function mapping and neural-feature–behavior coupling (e.g., cortico-muscular coherence, network connectivity metrics, multiscale entropy). Finally, methodological concerns (e.g., incomplete blinding, attrition, selective reporting) warrant rigorous design, including allocation concealment, adequate sample size power to examine and confirm the impact of tDCS on balance control performance and sensorimotor function in CAI.

Conclusion

This work suggests that tDCS is of great promise to improve balance control and sensorimotor function in CAI. Still the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms should be more explicitly characterized. With the knowledge obtained from this work, further high-quality studies are needed to examine and confirm such benefits of interventions using tDCS for individuals suffering from CAI.
