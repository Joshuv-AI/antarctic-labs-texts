Butyrylated Smilax glabra starch relieves atopic dermatitis through gut–skin axis modulation via colon-targeted delivery
Authors: Nan Wang, Lingling Wu, Yaya Su, Chi Zhang, Xu Chen, Hailong Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a prevalent cutaneous condition with chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation, posing a public health concern owing to its long-lasting and recurrent nature. Butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid produced by gut microbiota, exhibits significant anti-inflammatory effects in AD. Yet, its delivery via butyrylated starch for prolonged release in the colon has not been adequately investigated. In this study, butyrylated Smilax glabra starch (BSGS) was synthesized and its therapeutic potential against AD via modulation of the gut–skin axis was examined. BSGS exhibited a C-type crystalline structure and highly resistance to gastrointestinal digestion. In vitro anaerobic fermentation showed that BSGS effectively promoted the generation of short-chain fatty acids, especially butyrate, and positively influenced the gut microbial composition. In AD mice, BSGS administration considerably mitigated cutaneous inflammation, lowered serum proinflammatory cytokines, restored intestinal barrier integrity, and modulated gut microbiota by increasing Bacteroides and norank_f__Prevotellaceae while decreasing Alistipes and norank_o__RF39. This therapeutic effect was associated with butyrate release and NF-κB pathway suppression, as evidenced by the reduced phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα. These findings establish that BSGS, a novel colon-targeted butyrate donor, holds promising potential in AD treatment by modulating the immune system via the gut–skin axis.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD), a chronic skin condition characterized by recurring inflammatory episodes, intense pruritus, and a weakened skin barrier, can considerably affect patients’ quality of life. The global prevalence of AD has increased consistently, now impacting more than 10% of the adult population and posing a major public health burden1. Current therapeutic approaches primarily include topical steroids and systemic immunosuppressive agents. Although these treatments provide temporary relief, they often lead to undesirable side effects such as elevated blood glucose levels, Cushing-like symptoms, and disrupted sleep patterns2. Moreover, the symptoms often reappear following drug discontinuation, emphasizing the pressing need for safer and more effective therapies or long-term management strategies.

With this background, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of the gut microbiota in skin health, highlighting the concept of the “gut–skin axis.” This emerging hypothesis suggests that gut dysbiosis may trigger systemic immune responses via microbial metabolites, thereby disrupting skin immune homeostasis3. Patients with AD often exhibit reduced gut microbial diversity, marked by a decrease in the levels of beneficial bacterial genera, including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, along with an overgrowth of potentially pathogenic species such as Staphylococcus aureus4. In addition, AD is often comorbid with gastrointestinal disorders, including food allergies and inflammatory bowel diseases, further highlighting the gut–skin link5. Therapeutic strategies that target the gut microbiota—such as probiotic supplementation and fecal microbiota transplantation—have shown potential in alleviating AD symptoms6, indicating that microbiome modulation is a promising avenue for intervention.

One of the key mechanisms through which the gut microbiota influences host immunity is via the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)7. Of these, butyrate has attracted immense attention owing to its potent anti-inflammatory activity and its central role in immune modulation8. Preclinical studies have asserted that butyrate supplementation ameliorates skin inflammation in AD mouse models by inhibiting the STAT1 and NF-κB signaling pathways and enhancing the integrity of the skin barrier9. Nonetheless, the direct oral administration of free butyrate is constrained by its unpleasant odor, low palatability, and poor colon-targeting efficiency10. Therefore, developing efficient colon-targeted delivery systems for butyrate is a current research priority.

Butyrylated starch, a representative of type IV resistant starch (RS), resists enzymatic hydrolysis in the small intestine and is instead fermented by colonic microbiota to generate butyrate, a metabolite that has been shown to aid in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis11. Emerging evidence suggests that this structurally modified starch can mitigate intestinal inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and allergic responses12. The rhizome of Smilax glabra, a traditional Chinese medicinal and edible plant, is rich in RS13, making it a promising raw material for the development of functional starch. However, research on butyrylated starch derived from Smilax glabra remains limited; its structural characteristics, biological activities, and immunomodulatory effects have not been adequately explored. Moreover, its role in AD has not been investigated so far.

In this study, butyrylated Smilax glabra starch (BSGS) was synthesized and its structural characteristics, digestibility, and prebiotic capacity were systematically evaluated using simulated gastrointestinal digestion and in vitro fecal fermentation models. Furthermore, its therapeutic effects were assessed in an AD mouse model. The mechanism by which BSGS enhances gut microbiota balance and augments butyrate synthesis, ultimately modulating the NF-κB pathway to exert its anti-inflammatory effect, was examined by comprehensively analyzing the inflammatory biomarkers, gut microbial profiles, SCFA concentrations, and related protein levels. The findings are expected to provide new theoretical and scientific support for the application of BSGS in AD and associated immune disorders. Meanwhile, the present findings also demonstrated the potential of SGS as a safe, biodegradable carrier for functional ingredient delivery, promoting the innovative use of medicinal plant starches in drug delivery applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Smilax glabra was sourced from an authorized medicinal herb provider located in Shanxi Province, China. Porcine pancreatic α-amylase (activity ≥5 U/mg) and pancreatin (8 × USP standard) were sourced from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Amyloglucosidase (100,000 U/mL), 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB) and butyric anhydride were supplied by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Megazyme Ltd. (Wicklow, Ireland) supplied the d-Glucose Assay Kit used in the experiments. All other chemicals, unless mentioned, were of certified analytical grade and were used directly without any further processing.

Preparation of butyrylated Smilax glabra starch

Starch isolation from Smilax glabra and its subsequent butyrylation were carried out based on our previously established protocols13,14. Briefly, native starch was isolated through ethanol precipitation. For the synthesis of butyrylated starch, butyric anhydride (at 50% and 100% w/w relative to the starch content) was incrementally added to a 35% (w/v) starch suspension maintained at approximately 40 °C. The pH of the suspension was maintained at 8.5–9.0 with 1 mol/L NaOH during the 1 h addition period. The reaction mixture was then incubated to proceed for an additional 2 h. To terminate the reaction, pH was regulated to 6.0 with 1 mol/L HCl. After centrifugation (1800×g, 10 min), the resulting products were harvested, rinsed successively with ethanol and distilled water, followed by drying at 40 °C for 24 h to obtain butyrylated SGS with low (BSGS_L) and high (BSGS_H) degrees of substitution (DS), respectively.

Structural characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology of starch granules was examined using the Sigma 360 SEM (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). To enhance conductivity, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer before imaging. All observations were conducted at 10 kV accelerating voltage, with micrographs captured at both 500 × and 1.5K × magnification for detailed structural assessment.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra were recorded on the Nicolet iS20 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Briefly, starch samples (1 mg) and potassium bromide (100 mg) were ground, pressed into translucent pellets, and analyzed across the mid-infrared range of 400–4000 cm−1. The spectra were acquired at 4 cm−1 resolution, averaging 32 scans per measurement to ensure signal clarity.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis

Approximately 10 mg of starch was dissolved in 500 μL of deuterated DMSO. The mixture was vortexed to ensure complete dispersion and dissolution of the sample, after which the homogeneous solution was transferred into an NMR tube, and the 1H NMR spectra were acquired with the Avance III HD 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin, Rheinstetten, Germany), with chemical shifts calibrated against the solvent proton signal. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and the DS values were calculated according to Eq. (1) 15.(1)DS=4A/(3B+A)Where A is the peak area of butyryl methyl protons (1.90–2.30 ppm) and B is the combined peak area of hydroxyl and H-1 protons in α-d-glucopyranose units (>4.5 ppm).

13C NMR spectra were also recorded on the same instrument operated at 500 MHz. A total of 3000 scans were performed to ensure an adequate signal-to-noise ratio. Carbon signals corresponding to the glucopyranose backbone and the introduced butyryl groups were assigned to confirm the substitution pattern.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD measurements were conducted with Cu-Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA using the Ultima IV instrument (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Diffractograms were collected from 4° to 40° (2θ) at 5°/min with 0.01° step resolution. Relative crystallinity (RC) was quantified using Jade 6.0 software (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA, USA).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

TGA was performed using the STA200 thermogravimetric analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). Approximately 10 mg of the starch sample was placed in a platinum crucible and heated from room temperature to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 mL/min. The TG curve and the corresponding differential thermogravimetric (DTG) curve were recorded simultaneously.

Physicochemical characterization

The detailed procedures for contact angle measurement, molecular weight determination, and water solubility index (WSI) analysis were provided in the Supporting Information methods (Sections S1.1–S1.3).

In vitro digestion

A modified protocol based on Dong et al.16 was employed to assess the in vitro digestibility. In brief, 80 mg of starch was dispersed in 35 mL of sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.2) containing several glass beads and then incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of an enzyme solution (30 U/mL glucoamylase and 290 U/mL α-amylase) was added. At 0, 20, and 120 min, 0.1 mL aliquots were collected from each tube and immediately combined with 0.9 mL anhydrous ethanol to halt the enzymatic activity. The amount of glucose released was determined by the GOPOD assay kit. Each test was performed in triplicate. Rapidly digestible starch (RDS, %), slowly digestible starch (SDS, %), and RS (%) were calculated according to Eqs. (2), (3), (4).(2)RDS(%)=(G20−G0)×0.9/W(3)SDS(%)=(G120−G20)×0.9/W(4)RS(%)=1−RDS%−SDS%Where W denotes the weight of starch, with G20 and G120 indicating the glucose levels at 20 and 120 min.

In vitro fecal fermentation

As described in a past study17, all experimental materials and the carbonate–phosphate buffer solution were autoclaved at 121 °C for 30 min before application. Fresh fecal samples were collected from three healthy adults (aged 20–30 years, two females and one male, BMI range 18.5–23.9 kg/m2), None of the participants had a history of gastrointestinal disorders, had taken antibiotics within the past 3 months, or had consumed any probiotic products in the 2 weeks before sample collection. The collection was performed on the experiment day after receiving authorization from the Chinese PLA General Hospital's Ethics Committee (Protocol No. S2022-379-01). The samples were transported under anaerobic conditions and processed within 4 h of collection. The fecal material was diluted 1:10 (w/v) in sterile carbonate–phosphate buffer and filtered through quadruple-layered sterile gauze to remove the debris. The prepared fecal slurry served as the inoculum for fermentation with the in vitro digested starch. For fermentation, 100 mg of starch and 1 mL of fecal slurry were mixed in a sealed anaerobic vial, to which 4 mL of sterile carbonate–phosphate buffer was added. The control solution did not contain any fermentable carbon source. All mixtures were incubated in an anaerobic chamber at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged (14,000×g, 5 min), and the supernatants and pellets were stored separately at −80 °C. The supernatants were reserved for subsequent SCFAs analysis, and the pellets for 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Design of animal experiments and sample collection

Six-week-old male BALB/c mice (SPF grade; Sibefu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were housed at 22 °C under a relative humidity of 30%–40% and a 12 h photophase. A 7-day acclimation period was maintained before the formal experimental procedures. Procedures involving animals were executed with prior authorization from the Animal Ethics Committee of the Air Force Medical Center (Approval No. 2023-99-YJ01, China). Following acclimation, mice were randomly and uniformly assigned to the following four groups: Control, Model, BSGS_L (0.3 g/100 g BW/day), and BSGS_H (0.3 g/100 g BW/day). During the initial sensitization week, the Control group mice received PBS applied to their dorsal skin, whereas the Model and treatment group mice were administered 1% DNCB in a 3:1 acetone:olive oil solution every alternate day. From the second week, the DNCB concentration was lowered to 0.5% and applied topically on the dorsal area every 3 days over a period of 4 weeks. Upon the completion of the study, the mice were anesthetized for blood collection and subsequently euthanized for tissue sampling. The tissue samples were preserved at −80 °C until subsequent analyses.

Dermatitis scoring

Clinical evaluation involved photographing the dorsal skin of mice following each treatment and assessing the severity of dermatitis with reference to the SCORing Atopic Dermatitis scoring system. The severity scores were assigned based on the presence and extent of edema, erythema/hemorrhage, excoriation/erosion, lichenification, and desquamation/dryness; the score ranged from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe).

Histological assessment

The skin tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%–100%), embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 6 μm thickness, and dried overnight at 42 °C. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the sections were immersed in hematoxylin and eosin for 5 min each and then differentiated in acid alcohol. Toluidine blue staining was performed by incubating the sections in a staining solution for 3–5 min, followed by washing, dehydration, clearing, and mounting. Epidermal thickness and mast cells were observed by light microscopy.

Immunofluorescence staining

The paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by rinsing thrice with PBS for 5 min each time. After blocking with 10% donkey serum (or 3% BSA) for 30 min, rabbit primary antibodies against ZO-1 (Cat# GB151981, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and Occludin (Cat# GB111401, Servicebio) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were washed and incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat# GB21303, Servicebio) for 50 min in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min, followed by autofluorescence quenching (Reagent B, Servicebio), and sealed with anti-fade mounting medium. The images were captured using appropriate fluorescence channels: DAPI (Ex 330–380 nm, Em 420 nm) and Cy3 (Ex 510–560 nm, Em 590 nm).

Cytokine assessment

Skin and serum cytokine concentrations, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-10, were measured with ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) as per the manufacturer's protocols. The absorbance values at 450 nm were collected via a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

16S rRNA sequencing analysis

Microbial genomic DNA from the microbial sources was isolated by using the FastDNA Soil Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA). DNA concentration and purity were assessed with the NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The primers 338F and 806R were used to amplify the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene. The PCR products were subsequently validated, purified, and quantified. Purified amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit, followed by sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 system. Raw data were quality filtered with fastp and merged with FLASH. Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering (97% similarity) was performed using UPARSE, and the taxonomy was assigned with the RDP classifier against the Silva database. Downstream analyses were conducted on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Alpha diversity was calculated using Mothur and compared with the Wilcoxon test, while beta diversity was visualized using principal component analysis (PCA) and Bray–Curtis principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Multiple testing correction was performed by using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for relevant analyses.

SCFA analyses

After thawing at 4 °C, fermentation supernatant (100 μL) was combined with 0.5% HCl in ethanol (1 mL), vortexed for 30 s and sonicated for 40 min. For the colon samples, 20 mg of the colon tissue was homogenized in 800 μL of 0.5% phosphoric acid solution, which included 10 μg/mL 2-ethylbutyric acid serving as an internal standard. After centrifugation, 200 μL of the supernatant was blended with an equal volume of n-butanol, vortexed for 30 s, and then examined via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). SCFAs were measured using the 8890-7000D GC/MSD system equipped with an HP-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Carrier gas consisted of ultrapure helium with a constant flow set at 1.0 mL/min. The GC oven's temperature sequence featured a 2.5 min hold at 80 °C, a 20 °C/min ramp to 120 °C, followed by a slower ramp of 5 °C/min to 160 °C, with a concluding hold at 220 °C for 3 min. Electron ionization at 70 eV was applied for detection, and the operation was performed in selected-ion monitoring mode. Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using MassHunter Workstation software (version10.0, Agilent Technologies).

Transcriptomics

RNA was isolated from the mouse colon with the QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The purity and quantity of the RNA were then assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and through the process of agarose gel electrophoresis. mRNA was then subjected to Oligo(dT) enrichment, fragmentation, and cDNA synthesis, followed by adapter ligation, size selection, and library amplification. The Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform was used for high-throughput sequencing. Raw sequencing reads were quality filtered using fastp and mapped to the reference genome with HISAT2. The gene expression was quantified using RSEM. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the groups were identified with DESeq2 (FDR <0.05 and |log2FC| ≥1). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using GOATOOLS, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was performed using KOBAS. For enrichment analyses, multiple testing correction was applied using FDR, and the adjusted P-values ≤0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from mouse colonic tissues via RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), whereas nuclear protein fractions were obtained by using a dedicated nuclear extraction kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The protein concentrations were quantified with the BCA method (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and adjusted to 1 mg/mL. Samples containing equal protein amounts were subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by electrotransfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by washing with TBST and a subsequent incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibodies used included phospho-NF-κB p65 (Abways, Shanghai, China, Cat# BY0127), NF-κB p65 (Abways, Cat# CY5034), phospho-IκBα (Abways, Cat# CY7246), and IκBα (Abways, Cat# CY5026). Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Vazyme, Shanghai, China), and the band intensity was analyzed with Lab software.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular docking was performed by adopting a semi-flexible docking strategy. The 3D structure of the target protein NF-κB p65 was predicted using AlphaFold3, and the 3D structure of the ligand (butyrate) was constructed and energy-minimized using Chem3D. After standard preprocessing with AutoDock Tools, including hydrogen addition, charge assignment, and rotatable bond definition, the docking grid was set, and docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. The binding affinities were recorded, and the interaction modes were visualized using LigPlot v1.4.

Subsequently, a 100 ns MD simulation of the NF-κB p65–butyrate complex was performed using the AMBER 20 package. The complex was parameterized with ff14SB (protein) and GAFF2 (ligand) force fields, and solvated in a TIP3P water box (10 Å buffer) with 0.15 mol/L NaCl. Following neutralization, a stepwise energy minimization was performed, followed by equilibration under both NVT and NPT conditions. The system was then subjected to a 100 ns production run at 300 K and 1 atm. Trajectory analyses were performed using CPPTRAJ to evaluate the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bonds, and MM/GBSA-based binding free energy (ΔG).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and Origin 2021. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Molecular structure analysis of BSGS

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, while SGS particles exhibited polyhedral, spherical, or oval morphologies with smooth surfaces, BSGS particles displayed a rough surface with small protrusions and cracks. This morphological alteration can be attribute to the substitution of the hydroxyl groups with bulky and hydrophobic butyryl groups, which disrupts the extensive inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding network within the starch matrix. The resulting reduction in molecular cohesion increases granule amorphousness, thereby contributing to the surface roughness18. Furthermore, as the DS increased, the surface roughness of BSGS particles became more pronounced and some particles aggregated into irregularly shaped clusters. This aggregation can be attributed to the introduction of butyrylated groups, which alter intermolecular interactions and thereby promote starch molecule aggregation and adhesion. These observations suggest that butyrylation significantly modifies the surface morphology of SGS starch granules, with these alterations becoming more pronounced with the increase in DS. This phenomenon may be explained by the fact that butyrylation predominantly occurs in the amorphous regions of the starch granule surface, without substantially affecting its overall shape and size. Compared with the SGS sample, the BSGS sample demonstrated additional signals at 0.90 ppm (methyl group), 2.31 ppm (methylene group), and 1.55 ppm (methylene group), showing the successful acylation of starch. The 13C NMR spectra exhibited characteristic signals of starch, with peaks at around 100, 79, 73, 71, and 60 ppm corresponding to the C-1, C-4, C-2, C-3,5, and C-6, regions, respectively (Fig. 1D). When compared with SGS, new signals appeared at 35 and 18 ppm in BSGS samples, attributed to the CH2 groups of butyryl moieties, whereas the peak at 13 ppm corresponded to the CH3 group, confirming the successful introduction of the butyryl groups. Although the FT-IR spectra displayed a carbonyl stretching vibration near 1740 cm−1, the expected CO signal in the 170–180 ppm range was not detected in the 13C NMR spectra. This value could be attributed to the long T1 relaxation time and the inherently weak signal of non-protonated sp2 carbons (such as carbonyl carbons), particularly in samples with a low DS. For example, Li et al.20 reported that a distinct carbonyl signal was only detected by 13C CP/MAS NMR when the DS reached 0.84. In addition, the random distribution of esterification may cause significant signal broadening or dispersion into the baseline, further reducing the detectability of the carbonyl peak.

The XRD patterns of SGS displayed typical C-type diffraction peaks at 15.23°, 17.18°, and 23.02°, with a weak peak at 5.66° (Fig. 1E), which agrees with a previously reported pattern of C-type starch21. The diffraction peak positions of BSGS_L and BSGS_H were not significantly altered, indicating that the butyrylation reaction did not change the type of starch crystal. Nevertheless, compared with SGS, the RC of the BSGS samples was significantly reduced and further decreased with increasing DS (Table 1). In their study, Li et al.22 observed that as the DS increased from 0.00 to 0.87, the RC of butyrylated lotus seed starch (C-type) decreased from 50.70% to 10.06%. This trend may be attributed to the gradual substitution of –OH groups in the starch molecules by butyryl groups, weakening the intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonds, thereby reducing the orderliness of the crystalline regions. Moreover, the increase in the concentration of butyric anhydride may introduce additional reaction sites in the crystalline regions, exacerbating the disruption of the crystal structure11.

Thermal and physicochemical properties of BSGS

TGA (Fig. 1F) displayed that all starch samples underwent a three-stage thermal decomposition. The initial weight loss (50–120 °C), attributed to moisture evaporation, was slightly lower in BSGS samples (9.5%–9.6%) than in SGS samples (10.0%), indicating reduced hydrophilicity after butyrylation. During the second (250–380 °C) and third (400–600 °C) stages, BSGS samples exhibited a lower cumulative weight loss (74.6%–74.8%) than SGS (78.2%), suggesting improved thermal stability and altered degradation behavior. DTG curves (Fig. 1G) exhibited major peaks near 300 °C, with Tmax values of 304.7 °C (SGS), 316.1 °C (BSGS_L), and 303.7 °C (BSGS_H). The higher Tmax of BSGS_L implies delayed degradation, possibly due to butyryl-induced structural modification. In contrast, the broader and lower DTG peak of BSGS_H suggested a more gradual degradation process, possibly resulting from increased steric hindrance at higher DS, which restricted the thermal cleavage of polymer chains23.

The molecular weight distribution profiles of SGS and BSGS samples were analyzed using gel permeation chromatography with refractive index and multi-angle laser light scattering. Supporting Information Fig. S1 displays that SGS exhibited a typical bimodal distribution, indicating that it primarily consists of high-molecular-weight amylopectin. When compared with the conventional starches, SGS showed a relatively higher molecular weight, which may be attributed to its unique botanical origin. After butyrylation, the major peaks of both BSGS_L and BSGS_H shifted leftward and became narrower. Meanwhile, the Mn and Mw values shown in Supporting Information Table S1 increased, suggesting significant changes in the molecular weight and its distribution profile. Unlike most reports wherein A-type starches (e.g., maize, glutinous maize) exhibited reduced molecular weight after butyrylation, we observed an increase in the molecular weight for Smilax glabra starch post-modification. This discrepancy may be related to the C-type crystalline structure of SGS. Past studies have reported increased molecular weight after acylation in B-type crystalline potato starch, possibly due to the differences in the hydroxyl group exposure, accessibility of reactive sites, and structural stability associated with distinct crystalline forms24. When compared with SGS, the polydispersity index (Mw/Mn) of BSGS_L decreased, potentially because low-degree butyrylation did not induce significant chain degradation, but rather promoted intermolecular aggregation, prompting a more uniform molecular weight distribution. In contrast, a slight increase in Mw/Mn was observed in BSGS_H, which may be attributed to the structural complexity induced by higher DS25.

WSI reflects the solubility of starch in high-temperature aqueous environments. As shown in Table S1, SGS exhibited a relatively high WSI value, indicating strong hydrophilicity. After butyrylation, the WSI values of both BSGS_L and BSGS_H significantly decreased (P < 0.05), with the high-substitution sample BSGS_H exhibiting further reduction in water solubility. This change can be attributed to the introduction of butyryl groups. The butyrylation process replaces a part of the hydrophilic hydroxyl groups with hydrophobic fatty acid ester groups, thereby reducing the polarity of the starch molecular surface, enhancing hydrophobicity, and suppressing solubility. Contact angle measurements (Supporting Information Fig. S2) further confirmed the enhanced surface hydrophobicity of starch after butyrylation.

In vitro digestibility of BSGS

The SGS possessed a relatively high content of RS, at 57.76% (Table 1), which was significantly higher than that of common traditional starches, such as maize starch (11.17%)15 and lotus seed starch (16.3%)26. This elevated RS content can likely be attributed to the C-type crystalline structure of SGS. Previous studies have demonstrated that owing to its unique dual crystalline structure, combining the denser A-type and the looser B-type polymorphs, C-type starch forms partially ordered regions that confer resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis27. Moreover, in our previous research, we found that SGS possesses a relatively high amylose content (21.54%13), which is notably higher than that of maize and potato starches (15.6%–19.3%)28, further enhancing its resistance to enzymatic digestion. Overall, SGS demonstrates excellent enzymatic resistance and exhibits great potential as a colon-targeted delivery material.

Compared with SGS, both BSGS_L and BSGS_H displayed significantly higher RS contents, accompanied by considerable reductions in SDS and RDS levels. As the DS content surged, the RS content gradually increased and the proportions of SDS and RDS decreased, which agrees with earlier findings from Li et al23. The increase in the RSS content suggests that butyrylation enhances the ability of the starch to resist enzymatic digestion, permitting more of it to reach the colon for microbial fermentation. Butyrylation significantly enhances the enzymatic resistance of starch, possibly due to both increased surface hydrophobicity and steric hindrance introduced by the butyryl groups. The replacement of hydrophilic hydroxyl groups with hydrophobic ester bonds reduces enzyme-substrate affinity, as supported by the increased contact angles (Fig. S2). Meanwhile, the bulky butyryl groups distributed throughout the granule structure impose spatial hindrance and shielding effects, obstructing enzyme access and substrate recognition, and thereby suppressing hydrolysis26. This increased resistance can be ascribed to the steric hindrance introduced by acylation, which limits the access and activity of digestive enzymes. In addition, owing to its relatively loose structure, SDS is more readily permeated and hydrolyzed by enzymes, and as the DS increases, a part of the SDS fraction may be further transformed into RS16.

In contrast, BSGS_H, with a higher DS and more extensive butyrylation, exhibited a denser structure and stronger resistance to enzymatic and microbial degradation, suggesting its selective utilization by specific butyrate-producing bacteria, thereby enhancing butyrate production. In addition, metabolic interconversion occurs among SCFAs. Certain butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, and Lachnospiraceae, could convert exogenous acetate into butyrate via the butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase pathway, thereby increasing the butyrate levels while simultaneously consuming acetate32. In addition, cross-feeding interactions occur within the gut microbiota. For instance, Bifidobacterium can produce lactate and acetate, which are subsequently utilized by butyrate-producing bacteria such as F. prausnitzii for butyrate synthesis33. This metabolic redistribution may synergistically enhance butyrate production in the BSGS_H group and help regulate the overall SCFA profile, thereby contributing to the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. According to Fig. 2C, relative to the SGS group, butyrate production increased by approximately 1.21-fold and 3.12-fold, respectively, in BSGS_L and BSGS_H, indicating that it increased with rising DS, which is consistent with a previous report22. The BSGS_H group exhibited a continuous and rapid increase in butyrate levels throughout fermentation, whereas the BSGS_L group showed a markedly slower growth rate after 12 h. These findings imply that butyrylated starch may serve as an effective butyrate donor, with the potential to specifically enhance butyrate production during colonic fermentation.

Gut microbiota modulation by in vitro fermentation of BSGS

To investigate the effect of BSGS on the gut microbiota, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed on samples from each group after a 24 h in vitro fermentation. PCA based on OTU levels revealed distinct clustering patterns in the Control, SGS, and two BSGS groups (BSGS_L and BSGS_H), suggesting considerable alterations in the composition of the microbial community after SGS and BSGS treatment (Fig. 3A).

At the phylum level (Fig. 3B), both SGS and BSGS treatments substantially increased the proportions of Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota relative to the Control group; in contrast, the abundance of Pseudomonadota was significantly decreased. Bacteroidota has been reported to participate in carbohydrate degradation and fermentation, contributing to the production of SCFAs. The enrichment of this phylum suggests that SGS and BSGS may enhance SCFAs production, which agrees with the previously observed SCFA levels. Pseudomonadota comprises various opportunistic pathogens34; its depletion indicates that SGS and BSGS play a potential role in suppressing harmful bacteria and promoting a healthy microbial community structure. Moreover, BSGS treatment significantly increased the Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio, a common indicator of gut microbial homeostasis35, implying its ability to restore microbial balance.

At the genus level (Fig. 3C), both SGS and BSGS significantly increased the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium. This well-known probiotic genus that enhances mucosal barrier integrity and modulates host immune function36. Meanwhile, the abundance of the opportunistic pathogen Escherichia-Shigella was markedly reduced, suggesting that SGS and BSGS exert their protective effects via stabilization of the gut microbiota and inhibition of the inflammation-associated taxa37. In addition, higher relative abundances of Agathobacter and Faecalibacterium were observed in the BSGS groups. While the butyrate-producing capacity of Agathobacter has been well documented38, Faecalibacterium is known to be a major butyrate-producing genus with potent anti-inflammatory properties and is essential for maintaining gut barrier function and resisting pathogen invasion39. These findings suggest that BSGS may have a superior capacity to modulate microbial metabolism and promote beneficial taxa. Furthermore, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis (Fig. 3D) confirmed these findings, demonstrating that two key butyrate-producing taxa—Oscillospirales and Ruminococcaceae40—were significantly enriched in the BSGS_H group. This enrichment reveals the augmented potential of high-DS BSGS to stimulate butyrate production and regulate gut microbial ecology. Collectively, these in vitro results suggest that BSGS has the potential to play an immunomodulatory role in vivo, prompting further investigation in an AD mouse model.

Discussion

Recent investigations on the metabolic and immunomodulatory effects of butyrylated starch have shown significant intervention effects in models of allergic rhinitis and colitis51,52. Nonetheless, the application of butyrylated starch in AD has not been thoroughly examined to date. This study is the first to establish that butyrylated starch can effectively mitigate AD-related inflammatory responses.

To preliminarily explore its potential mechanism of action, the effect of BSGS on SCFAs production was initially assessed using an in vitro anaerobic fermentation system. The observations showed that BSGS markedly enhanced SCFAs production, especially butyrate. Therefore, its butyryl groups can be effectively released by gut microbiota and bacterial esterases, acting as a key substrate source for butyrate. These findings align with previous research demonstrating that butyrylated corn and potato starches enhance butyrate production during in vitro fermentation24,53. The yield of butyrate increased with increasing DS, a result also observed by Li et al.22 in their study on butyrylated lotus seed starch. Notably, under comparable conditions, fermentation of 100 mg butyrylated SGS (BSGS, DS = 0.15) yielded 5.5 mmol/L butyrate, whereas 200 mg butyrylated maize starch (DS = 0.15) produced only 0.79 mmol/L19, highlighting the remarkable advantage of BSGS in butyrate release.

Additionally, BSGS demonstrated beneficial regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. Particularly, butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium and Agathobacter, were considerably enriched in the BSGS_H group. In contrast, beneficial bacteria, including Bifidobacterium, were increased, and pathogenic bacteria, such as Escherichia–Shigella, were significantly decreased, suggesting a capacity for remodeling the gut microbiota. This effect may be explained by the high RS content and crystalline structure of BSGS, which are more easily utilized by specific beneficial microbes, selectively promoting the growth of butyrate-producing bacteria11. Moreover, earlier studies have reported that butyrylated starches with different structures and DS exhibit certain specificity in regulating microbiota, being able to enrich various butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., Roseburia and Ruminococcaceae) and inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria14,54. Furthermore, a phase II double-blind crossover trial demonstrated that oral administration of butyrylated high-amylose maize starch increased the abundance of SCFAs-producing bacteria such as Ruminococcus and Parabacteroides55. Collectively, these results indicated that BSGS could effectively improve butyrate production and regulate the gut microbiota. Notably, differences in the fermentation models, inoculum sources (i.e., donor microbiota), and substrate concentrations across studies may influence the absolute levels of SCFAs and the specific microbial changes observed. Therefore, further comparative studies of BSGS and other commonly used butyrylated starches under standardized conditions are warranted in the future.

In recent years, the concept of the “gut–skin axis” has gained increasing attention, with gut microbiota homeostasis recognized as a key element in the development and progression of AD6. This study observed that the gut microbiota of the model mice was dysregulated and the α-diversity index was reduced, which agrees with previous findings on gut microbial imbalance in AD mice56. Past studies have demonstrated that DNCB-induced AD mouse models exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, as characterized by a decreased abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Butyricicoccus57,58, as well as impaired intestinal barrier function59. These changes may be related to systemic immune responses affecting the migration and activation of immune cells, particularly Treg cells. Through the expression of adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors, Treg cells can migrate between the gut and peripheral tissues such as the skin to regulate immune homeostasis, thereby influencing the structure and function of the gut microbiota6. Moreover, clinical studies have also reported lower levels of Lactobacilli, Bifidobacteria, and Prevotella in patients with AD60, and fecal microbiota transplantation can effectively alleviate AD symptoms61. The gut microbial composition of AD mice was significantly improved after BSGS intervention, and the microbial community structure became more similar to that of the healthy Control group. Specifically, BSGS improved the abundance of beneficial microbes, including norank_o__Clostridia_UCG-014, Bacteroides, norank_f__Prevotellaceae, and norank_f__Oscillospiraceae. At the same time, it reduced the relative levels of potentially harmful bacteria, such as norank_o__RF39, Alistipes, and norank_f__Peptococcaceae. Notably, BSGS_H markedly increased the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria including Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Ligilactobacillus, and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001. An earlier study has also established that butyrylated starch can modulate gut microbial structure in mice and augment the proliferation of Lactobacillaceae and Lachnospiraceae20. The present study has expanded its application potential in AD models.

SCFAs, as primary metabolites generated via yeast microbial fermentation, act as essential signaling molecules within the gut–skin axis. Of the various SCFAs, butyrate is especially important for maintaining gut barrier integrity and regulating immune responses62. It has been observed that the levels of SCFAs, especially butyrate, are reduced in patients with AD and in the DNCB-induced AD mouse model63. Moreover, higher concentrations of butyrate during childhood have been reported to be associated with a lower risk of atopic sensitization, suggesting that increasing butyrate levels could be a novel dietary intervention strategy for allergic diseases in children64. Our previous in vitro fermentation experiments indicated that butyrylated starch considerably enhances butyrate production. Based on this finding, the effect was further validated in an AD mouse model, and the results indicated that BSGS substantially augmented butyrate levels in the colonic contents. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating that butyrylated starch resists digestion in the upper gastrointestinal tract and that it is mainly fermented in the cecum and colon, which causes a marked elevation in the colonic and fecal butyrate levels65,66. These findings support its potential as an effective colon-targeted butyrate donor. However, additional in vivo validation through fluorescence imaging can further strengthen the evidence supporting the colon-targeting properties of BSGS. In addition, in the BSGS_H group, other SCFAs—such as acetate, propionate, and i-butyric acid—and total SCFAs were significantly elevated. This finding is consistent with that from a previous study in which sodium butyrate supplementation alleviated AD symptoms67, supporting the potential role of butyrate elevation in improving AD. Subsequently, the correlations linking SCFAs to the microbiota were evaluated. The findings indicated a significant positive correlation between norank_f_Prevotellaceae and both n-butyric and n-valeric acids, norank_f_Oscillospiraceae and propionic acid, and Odoribacter and i-butyric, n-valeric, and i-valeric acids. The relative abundance of these taxa was increased in the BSGS intervention group, suggesting their potential involvement in SCFA synthesis. Consistent with prevailing studies, these taxa possess the ability to produce corresponding SCFAs68, 69, 70, implying that BSGS increases SCFA levels indirectly by stimulating the proliferation and metabolic activity of acid-producing bacteria. In addition, the concurrent increase in the levels of various SCFAs in the BSGS intervention group may be explained by its slow-release substrate structure. As a butyrate ester-RS, BSGS gradually releases butyrate in the colon, continuously providing fermentable substrates for gut microbiota, which facilitates sustained and stable SCFAs production. In contrast, free butyrate can transiently increase butyrate levels; however, its absorption in the upper digestive tract or rapid metabolism limits its ability to maintain effective concentrations in the colon, hindering prolonged effects71. BSGS circumvents application challenges, such as the unpleasant odor of butyrate, and improves the gut microbiota structure and metabolic functions, coregulating gut microbiota homeostasis. Therefore, BSGS may offer sustained and systemic benefits in alleviating chronic inflammatory diseases such as AD.

To further explore the anti-inflammatory mechanism of BSGS, the transcriptomic profiles of the colonic tissues were analyzed. The findings demonstrated that DEGs were predominantly enriched in immune-related pathways, suggesting that BSGS exerts its anti-inflammatory effects via immune modulation. NF-κB, a central mediator of inflammation, promotes the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-4, and IL-6, thereby contributing to skin inflammation and tissue injury72. This observation agrees with our ELISA results, which demonstrated that BSGS significantly reduced the skin and serum levels of several inflammatory cytokines in AD mice. Mechanistically, BSGS significantly lowered p65 and IκBα phosphorylation, thereby preventing the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. To further validate this hypothesis, molecular docking and MD simulations were performed. The results indicated that butyrate could stably bind to the active site of p65, forming hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces that maintained the conformational stability of the complex throughout the simulation. These findings suggest that butyrate may inhibit NF-κB signaling by directly interfering with p65 activity. In addition to this direct interaction mechanism, past studies have identified two principal pathways through which butyrate suppresses NF-κB signaling: (1) the inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs), particularly HDAC3 and HDAC6, thereby enhancing p65 acetylation and limiting its transcriptional activity and (2) the activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as GPR43 and GPR109A on immune and epithelial cells. Butyrate also influences histone acylation (e.g., butyrylation, crotonylation), altering chromatin accessibility and gene expression73. Similarly, chickpea-derived type III RS has been reported to inhibit the expression of p65 and IκBα, thereby alleviating AD symptoms and attenuating NF-κB pathway activation67. Thus, BSGS may regulate distal skin immune responses by suppressing of the NF-κB pathway along the gut–skin axis. Notably, BSGS significantly improved intestinal barrier function. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that BSGS upregulated the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, thereby enhancing the epithelial barrier integrity. As the key active metabolite of BSGS, butyrate is believed to strengthen the intestinal barrier and reduce epithelial permeability, contributing to the maintenance of mucosal immune homeostasis and playing a role in the regulation of systemic immune responses. These findings further support the mechanistic basis through which BSGS may exert its anti-inflammatory effects via the modulation of the gut–skin axis. In addition, future studies could incorporate functional assays, such as FITC-dextran permeability testing and serum LPS measurement, in combination with histological evaluations to provide a more comprehensive and systematic validation of gut barrier integrity.

This study observed a considerable surge in serum IL-10 levels following BSGS intervention. IL-10 is a critical immunosuppressive cytokine that restrains excessive immune activation and maintains immune tolerance. Treg cell populations and functions were not directly assessed in this study; however, earlier reports have indicated that butyrate can stimulate Treg cell differentiation. Butyrate inhibits HDAC activity and upregulates the transcription factor Foxp3, thereby enhancing Treg-mediated immunoregulation74. Furthermore, butyrate-induced Treg activation is essential for maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and may also influence cutaneous immune responses via the gut–immune–skin axis. Chen et al.51 observed that butyrylated starch intervention significantly increased IL-10 expression in a murine model of allergic rhinitis, confirming that butyrate derivatives possess anti-inflammatory potential. In addition, dietary fibers such as olive-derived antioxidants have been shown to enhance butyrate production, elevate serum IL-10 levels, and alleviate AD-like symptoms75. Together with our findings of lowered proinflammatory cytokines and elevated IL-10 levels, BSGS may regulate Treg-mediated immune tolerance via butyrate-associated immune pathways, thus activating endogenous anti-inflammatory mechanisms to alleviate AD-related inflammation. Cumulatively, these findings suggest that BSGS may modulate immune homeostasis along the gut–skin axis via both direct inhibition of NF-κB and butyrate-mediated immunoregulatory pathways, including Treg activation. Nevertheless, further mechanistic studies are required to fully elucidate these pathways.

In this study, AD model mice receiving BSGS intervention exhibited no obvious abnormalities in body weight, behavioral status, or gross appearance of major organs throughout the experimental period. Immunofluorescence analysis of the colon revealed an intact mucosal structure, accompanied by reduced serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and amelioration of splenomegaly. Together with its colonic targeted release profile, these findings indicate that BSGS possesses good oral safety and tolerability. Building on this favorable safety profile, the present study further demonstrated that BSGS effectively alleviates AD-related inflammation, likely via regulation of gut microbiota, increased production of SCFAs (especially butyrate), and suppression of NF-κB signaling pathway activity. These observations confirm that BSGS is a promising dietary intervention for chronic inflammatory diseases such as AD. However, individual differences in the gut microbial composition may influence fermentation efficiency and butyrate release, thereby potentially affecting the therapeutic outcomes. The microbiota-dependent effects of BSGS warrant further elucidation and may be validated through fecal microbiota transplantation in the future. Considering the limited resolution of 16S-rRNA sequencing in functional analysis, metagenomic sequencing may be applied to more comprehensively explore the metabolic potential of the gut microbiota and its relationship with host immune regulation. In addition, comparative studies with free butyrate or commercial prebiotics may further highlight the unique therapeutic potential of BSGS. Although no adverse effects were observed in the present study, future investigations are warranted to assess its long-term safety, optimal dosing regimen, and the underlying mechanisms to support clinical translation.

Conclusions

In this study, BSGS was structurally characterized as a C-type crystal with typical butyrylation functional groups that confer excellent resistance to gastrointestinal digestion. In vitro fecal fermentation studies indicated that BSGS considerably enhanced gut microbial ecology and stimulated butyrate production. Furthermore, in the AD mouse model, BSGS intervention effectively alleviated dermatitis symptoms, reduced inflammation, restored intestinal barrier integrity, and remodeled the gut microbiota composition. Additional studies demonstrated that BSGS enriched butyrate-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Ligilactobacillus, and Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, which enhanced the generation of butyrate and other SCFAs. Mechanistically, BSGS alleviated inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway via butyrate-driven inhibition, as evidenced by lower levels of p-p65 and p-IκBα. Collectively, BSGS, a butyrate-donating functional dietary component, can regulate the “gut–skin axis”. The findings from this study provide new theoretical foundations and promising applications for interventions in AD and other immune-related diseases.
