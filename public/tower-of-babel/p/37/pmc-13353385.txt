Compartment syndrome is associated with acute kidney injury following revascularisation in patients with lower extremity arterial occlusive disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Renzhong Ding, Yan Yang, Shiyi Xiang, Wenjun Wu, Ye Yuan, Tingting Li, Yi Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompartment syndrome (CS) is a severe complication after revascularisation for lower extremity arterial occlusive disease (LEAOD), but its association with systemic complications remains unclear.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 153 patients with LEAOD who underwent revascularisation between January 2020 and December 2025. Postoperative CS was the primary exposure, and patients were classified into CS and non-CS groups. Clinical outcomes, including final amputation and acute kidney injury (AKI), were compared using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsCS occurred in 29 patients (19.0%). Patients with CS showed more severe ischemia and metabolic disturbance. The CS group had a higher crude final amputation rate than the non-CS group, but CS was not independently associated with final amputation after adjustment (OR 1.56; 95% CI 0.27–8.97; p = 0.618). AKI occurred more frequently in the CS group (69.0 vs. 12.1%, p < 0.001), and CS remained strongly associated with AKI after adjustment (OR 20.94; 95% CI 5.62–78.10; p < 0.001), with consistent findings in sensitivity analyses. Among patients with both CS and AKI, CS preceded AKI in all cases.ConclusionsPostoperative CS may represent a clinically recognizable marker of severe ischemia–reperfusion injury and systemic injury burden associated with AKI, rather than a definitive causal determinant of AKI or limb loss.Keywords: Compartment syndrome, acute kidney injury, revascularisation, lower extremity arterial disease, ischemia-reperfusion injury, rhabdomyolysis

Introduction

Lower extremity arterial occlusive disease (LEAOD) is a common but serious vascular surgical disorder that, when it progresses acutely or subacutely, can contribute to severe limb ischemia, tissue necrosis, and even amputation, thereby increasing the risk of disability and death [1,2]. For patients with acute or severe limb ischemia, prompt restoration of blood flow is crucial to save limbs and improve prognosis [3,4]. However, revascularisation can also trigger a series of complications, thereby affecting patients’ local and systemic outcomes.

Compartment syndrome (CS) is a severe complication after revascularisation that can result in disability or even death. CS is closely associated with ischemia-reperfusion injury. After revascularisation, increased capillary permeability and tissue edema lead to increased fascial compartment pressure, impaired microcirculation, muscle necrosis, and nerve damage [5,6]. Most previous studies have focused on the effects of CS on local tissue damage and limb outcomes, but whether CS is an independent factor for increasing amputation rate remains controversial. CS is not merely a local complication. Severe ischemia–reperfusion injury accompanied by CS is often associated with rhabdomyolysis and the release of myoglobin and electrolytes, reflecting a systemic injury process that may also involve metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and acute kidney injury (AKI) [7–9]. Although the aforementioned mechanism has a certain degree of theoretical validity, clinical evidence of the relationship between CS and systemic complications, especially AKI, is still limited in patients receiving revascularisation. Previous studies have reported that fasciotomy for acute compartment syndrome after revascularisation for acute limb ischemia occurs in approximately 10% of patients, and recent database studies have specifically evaluated the incidence of CS after intervention for non-traumatic acute limb ischemia. AKI is also a recognized complication after lower-extremity peripheral vascular intervention or revascularisation. Registry and multicenter studies have reported postoperative AKI rates of 7.4% after inpatient lower-extremity peripheral vascular intervention and 11.7% after femoropopliteal endovascular intervention for peripheral artery disease [10–13].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between postoperative CS and limb-related as well as systemic outcomes in patients with LEAOD undergoing revascularisation, with particular focus on AKI. Given that both CS and AKI may occur during the early postoperative period and may reflect the same ischemia–reperfusion injury cascade, CS was interpreted as a clinically identifiable marker of systemic injury burden rather than as a definitively causal exposure. We hypothesized that CS would not show a robust independent association with amputation after accounting for ischemic severity, but would be associated with AKI and other systemic complications.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-center retrospective exposure-based cohort study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Daping Hospital, Army Medical University (Approval No. 2026-110; approved on 22/04/2026). Data were retrospectively accessed and analyzed between 23/04/2026 and 25/04/2026. Given the retrospective observational design and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Daping Hospital, Army Medical University. The study included patients with lower extremity arterial occlusive disease (LEAOD) who underwent revascularisation between January 2020 and December 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed LEAOD; (2) receipt of revascularisation treatment; and (3) complete clinical records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) previous amputation; (2) substantial missing data; and (3) other severe diseases that could markedly affect prognosis. Postoperative compartment syndrome (CS) was treated as the primary exposure variable. Patients were classified into CS and non-CS groups based on the occurrence of CS after revascularisation, and clinical outcomes were subsequently compared between the two groups. Revascularisation procedures included open surgical, endovascular, and hybrid approaches, selected according to ischemic severity, anatomical characteristics, thrombotic burden, and the treating vascular surgeon’s judgment. Patients were also categorized according to ischemic presentation as acute limb ischemia or acute deterioration on a chronic ischemic background. The main indications for revascularisation were threatened limb viability, severe ischemic symptoms, progressive ischemic pain, tissue-threatening ischemia, or imaging-confirmed arterial occlusion requiring urgent or semi-urgent restoration of blood flow.

Definitions

The diagnosis of CS was made by the attending vascular surgery team based on typical clinical manifestations, compartment pressure assessment, and/or intraoperative findings [5]. Clinical features suggestive of CS included progressive severe limb pain, tense swelling, pain on passive stretch, sensory or motor impairment, and worsening distal perfusion after revascularisation. When compartment pressure was measured, an absolute intracompartmental pressure ≥30 mmHg was considered supportive of CS. Delta pressure criteria were not routinely available in this retrospective cohort and were therefore not used as a mandatory diagnostic criterion. In patients who underwent surgical exploration, intraoperative findings such as marked muscle swelling, bulging muscle after fascial release, impaired muscle contractility, or muscle necrosis were also considered in the final diagnosis.

AKI was defined and staged according to the KDIGO criteria. Stage 1 AKI was defined as an increase in serum creatinine of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or 1.5–1.9 times baseline; stage 2 as 2.0–2.9 times baseline; and stage 3 as ≥3.0 times baseline, serum creatinine ≥4.0 mg/dL, or the initiation of renal replacement therapy [14,15]. The time to AKI was defined as the first documented time point at which KDIGO creatinine criteria were met or renal replacement therapy was initiated.

The time to CS was defined as the interval from the end of revascularisation to clinical diagnosis of CS. Fasciotomy was classified as prophylactic when performed at the time of revascularisation before a definite postoperative diagnosis of CS, and therapeutic when performed after clinical diagnosis or strong suspicion of CS [16]. Delayed fasciotomy was explored descriptively because of the limited number of CS cases and fasciotomy events.

The severity of limb ischemia was evaluated using the Fontaine classification. WIfI classification was not included because complete wound and foot infection variables were not consistently available in the retrospective records, preventing reliable reconstruction of WIfI stages. Technical success was defined as successful restoration of target-limb blood flow at the end of the revascularisation procedure based on operative records and postoperative clinical assessment.

Data collection

Data were extracted retrospectively from electronic medical records. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age and sex), comorbidities (such as diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease), ischemia-related parameters (including duration of ischemia and Fontaine classification), and preoperative laboratory values (including lactate, creatinine, serum potassium, and pH). Perioperative data, including surgical success and postoperative complications, were also collected. Postoperative complications of interest included acute kidney injury (AKI), rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), ICU admission, infection, and reoperation.

For patients who developed both CS and AKI, the temporal sequence between AKI and CS was manually reviewed using postoperative laboratory records, clinical progress notes, and the documented time of CS diagnosis. AKI onset was defined as the first documented time point at which KDIGO creatinine criteria were met or renal replacement therapy was initiated. Because postoperative creatinine measurements were not obtained at uniform time intervals, exact time-to-AKI values could not be reliably calculated for all patients; therefore, the temporal sequence was summarized descriptively. The direction of temporal sequence was classified according to whether CS diagnosis preceded AKI onset, AKI onset preceded CS diagnosis, or the sequence could not be reliably determined from the available records.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was final amputation. Secondary outcomes included 30-day amputation, in-hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, and perioperative complications, with particular emphasis on AKI. These outcomes were analyzed in relation to the exposure variable of postoperative CS.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate according to their distribution, and were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To evaluate the association between postoperative CS and clinical outcomes, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed for final amputation and AKI. CS was entered into the models as the primary exposure variable. Candidate covariates were selected based on clinical relevance, biological plausibility, and prior literature, with particular consideration given to ischemic severity, metabolic disturbance, and baseline renal risk. Despite efforts to carefully select candidate covariates, the limited number of outcome events, particularly for amputation, inherently constrains the number of variables the multivariable models can reliably support, increasing the risk of overfitting. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed before model fitting. Because of the relatively small sample size and event number, the multivariable analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. All statistical analyses were performed using two-sided tests, and a P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In particular, analyses for amputation were considered exploratory due to the limited number of events. Given its ordinal nature, the Fontaine classification was entered into the regression models as an ordinal variable to assess the linear trend across increasing severity. Given the limited number of outcome events, the number of covariates included in the multivariable models was restricted to minimize the risk of overfitting. All analyses were conducted using complete-case data. Events-per-variable (EPV) ratios were calculated to assess model stability. Because of the limited number of events and the large effect estimate for AKI, sensitivity analyses were performed. First, metabolic injury markers, including creatine kinase, serum potassium, pH, lactate, ischemia duration, and technical success, were added to assess residual confounding by ischemia–reperfusion severity. Second, Firth penalized logistic regression was used to reduce sparse-data bias. Rhabdomyolysis was not included in the primary model because it may represent an intermediate manifestation within the ischemia–reperfusion injury pathway linking CS and AKI. Crude odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were also calculated for outcome comparisons between the CS and non-CS groups.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 153 patients were included in the study, and 29 (19.0%) of them developed CS. Compared to the non-CS group, the CS group had a higher rate of males (89.7 vs. 60.5%, p = 0.006), a longer duration of ischemia (13.2 ± 4.2 vs. 9.6 ± 3.0 h, p < 0.001), and a higher degree of Fontaine classification (p = 0.001). Among the laboratory parameters, the CS group showed higher preoperative levels of lactate, creatinine, and serum potassium, and lower pH levels (all p < 0.05). In addition, there was a lower rate of surgical success in the CS group (89.7 vs. 99.2%, p = 0.022). The detailed baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Clinical outcomes

In terms of clinical outcomes, the CS group showed a trend toward a higher rate of amputation (final amputation: 17.2 vs. 5.6%, p = 0.052). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance in unadjusted analysis. In subsequent multivariable analysis, the association was further attenuated after adjustment for ischemic severity and metabolic factors. In contrast, the incidence of perioperative complications was significantly higher in the CS group, including the occurrence of AKI (69.0 vs. 12.1%, p < 0.001), CRRT, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, ICU admission, reoperation, and infection rates (all p < 0.05). Among the 29 patients who developed CS, the median time from revascularisation to CS diagnosis was 5.6 h (IQR 3.5–8.3). The median measured compartment pressure was 40.0 mmHg (IQR 36.3–44.8), and 28 of 29 patients (96.6%) had a measured compartment pressure ≥30 mmHg. Fasciotomy was performed in 24 of 29 patients (82.8%), with a median time from CS diagnosis to fasciotomy of 2.2 h (IQR 1.2–3.4). No patient with available fasciotomy timing had delayed decompression beyond 6 h.

AKI occurred in 35 of 153 patients, including 20 of 29 patients in the CS group and 15 of 124 patients in the non-CS group. Among the 20 patients who developed both CS and AKI, manual chart review showed that CS was documented before the onset of AKI in all cases (20/20, 100.0%). Because post­operative creatinine measurements were not performed at uniform time intervals, the exact median time from revascularisation to AKI could not be reliably calculated. Nevertheless, the observed temporal sequence supports interpreting postoperative CS as a clinically recognizable marker of severe ischemia–reperfusion injury preceding AKI, while causality cannot be definitively established in this retrospective observational study. Among the 35 patients with AKI, 7 (20.0%) had KDIGO stage 1 AKI, 12 (34.3%) had stage 2 AKI, and 16 (45.7%) had stage 3 AKI. CRRT was required in 5 patients, accounting for 14.3% of AKI cases.

Patients in the CS group also had longer ICU stay and hospital stay than those in the non-CS group. The median ICU stay was 3.0 days (IQR 0.0–6.0) in the CS group and 0.0 days (IQR 0.0–0.0) in the non-CS group. The median hospital stay was 19.0 days (IQR 15.0–22.0) in the CS group and 7.0 days (IQR 5.0–10.0) in the non-CS group.

Multivariable analysis for final amputation

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis for final amputation, no statistically significant association was observed between CS and final amputation (OR 1.56, 95% CI 0.27–8.97, p = 0.618). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because only 12 final amputation events occurred, yielding an EPV of approximately 2.4 for the five-variable model. Therefore, the amputation analysis was considered underpowered and exploratory, and the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no association. Technical success was associated with a lower odds of final amputation (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.01–0.91, p = 0.041). Details are presented in Table 3 and illustrated in Figure 2.

Multivariable analysis for AKI

In the primary multivariable model for AKI, CS remained strongly associated with AKI (OR 20.94, 95% CI 5.62–78.10, p < 0.001). The AKI model included 35 AKI events and six variables, corresponding to an EPV of approximately 5.8. Given the limited number of events and the large effect estimate, additional sensitivity analyses were performed. In the metabolic injury model, CS remained associated with AKI (OR 14.39, 95% CI 2.80–73.92, p = 0.001), and the association persisted after additional adjustment for technical success (OR 11.35, 95% CI 2.06–62.56, p = 0.005). Firth penalized logistic regression yielded similar findings in the primary model (OR 16.73, 95% CI 4.70–59.54, p < 0.001) and metabolic injury model (OR 11.66, 95% CI 2.40–56.63, p = 0.002), suggesting that the observed association was not solely explained by sparse-data bias. Nevertheless, although CS was documented before AKI among patients with both events, these results should still be interpreted as associative rather than definitively causal because of the retrospective observational design and the potential for residual confounding by ischemia–reperfusion severity. Discussion

This study evaluated the association between postoperative compartment syndrome (CS) and limb-related as well as systemic outcomes in patients with LEAOD undergoing revascularisation. The main findings were as follows. First, although patients with CS had a higher crude rate of final amputation, no statistically significant association was observed after adjustment; however, this analysis was likely underpowered because only 12 final amputation events occurred. Second, CS was strongly associated with AKI and other systemic complications, but this association should be interpreted in the context of shared ischemia–reperfusion injury, metabolic disturbance, and rhabdomyolysis rather than as evidence that CS directly causes AKI [17]. Importantly, among patients who developed both CS and AKI, CS was documented before the onset of AKI in all cases. Therefore, postoperative CS may be better understood as a clinically recognizable marker of severe ischemia–reperfusion injury and systemic injury burden preceding AKI, although causality cannot be definitively established.

The notable association between CS and AKI identified in this study has important clinical relevance and biological plausibility. However, the magnitude of the observed effect should be interpreted cautiously, as it may partly reflect shared underlying severity of ischemia–reperfusion injury rather than a purely causal relationship.

Severe limb ischemia and reperfusion can lead to widespread muscle cell necrosis, resulting in the release of large amounts of myoglobin, potassium ions, and other intracellular components, which triggers rhabdomyolysis [18–20]. The deposition of myoglobin in the renal tubules can directly cause tubular toxicity, while the accompanying hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis further exacerbate renal dysfunction. Moreover, the inflammatory response and oxidative stress activated by revascularisation will deteriorate the condition by endothelial dysfunction and microcirculatory disorders [14,21]. Accordingly, CS may serve as a clinical indicator of the pathological cascade of ischemia–reperfusion injury leading to systemic complications [9,22]. The results provide important insights for clinical practice. Commonly, CS is considered a complication affecting the prognosis of local tissues and limbs, and the clinical management is a prompt fascial release procedure [23]. It is even more appropriate to define CS as an early warning sign of the risk of systemic injury. For patients who have the possibility of developing CS, we should further strengthen the early identification and intervention of systemic complications, particularly an ongoing monitoring of renal function, based on local decompression. These findings highlight the importance of careful monitoring of renal function, metabolic status, and systemic complications in patients at risk of CS [24,25]. The shift from “Local Treatment” to “Holistic Management” will improve patients’ overall prognosis [26]. To date, most previous studies have focused on limb outcomes, and some have reported that CS is associated with an increased risk of amputation [22,27,28]. The downside is that they often fail to adequately adjust for key confounding factors such as the severity of ischemia and metabolic status. After multivariable adjustment, our findings did not confirm a significant independent association between CS and amputation, which may be partly explained by confounding factors and limited statistical power. In fact, the lack of research about CS connected with systemic complications makes it difficult for us to accurately understand the role of CS in healthcare. To address this issue, we sought to explore the connection between CS and AKI, offering updated clinical evidence and supporting the assumption about CS’s important role in systemic inflammation and organ damage [29].

Several additional limitations should be noted. First, although we examined the temporal relationship between CS and AKI where data were available, the retrospective design limited the precision of event timing in some patients. Second, the number of amputation events was small, resulting in a low EPV and limited statistical power for the amputation model. Third, WIfI classification could not be reliably reconstructed because complete wound and foot infection information was not consistently available in the medical records. Fourth, fasciotomy timing was analyzed descriptively because most decompressions were performed early and the number of delayed cases was insufficient for reliable comparative modeling.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort of patients with LEAOD undergoing revascularisation, postoperative CS was associated with a higher incidence of AKI and other systemic complications. Among patients who developed both CS and AKI, CS was documented before the onset of AKI in all cases. This temporal sequence supports the interpretation that postoperative CS may represent a clinically recognizable marker of severe ischemia–reperfusion injury preceding AKI. However, given the retrospective observational design and potential residual confounding, the association should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causality. No statistically significant association was observed between CS and final amputation after adjustment, but this analysis was likely underpowered because of the limited number of amputation events. These findings support close monitoring of renal function, metabolic status, and systemic complications in patients who develop CS after revascularisation.
