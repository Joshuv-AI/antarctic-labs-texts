Engineering the oxygen microenvironment for pancreatic islet transplants
Authors: Samantha Kruzshak, Flannery G Williams, Gulden Camci-Unal, Emmanuel S Tzanakakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic β-cell dysfunction causes diabetes in over 400 million worldwide. Islet transplantation promises but faces donor scarcity, rejection, and hypoxic failure. β cells are vulnerable to oxygen deprivation, affecting survival and function. Strategies include pre-implantation, prevascularization, genetic modifications to improve survival; biomaterial encapsulation for immune protection and nutrients; and oxygen delivery after implantation to sustain viability, advancing islet transplantation for diabetes.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Cell biology, Medical research, Stem cells

Introduction

Importance of oxygen in the islet

The hormonal interplay among endocrine cells within the islets of Langerhans is key to the maintenance of blood glucose homeostasis. The viability and functional performance of these secretory cells are highly dependent on a well-oxygenated vascular bed given their high metabolic activity based on aerobic respiration. Islets thus require significant amounts of oxygen and nutrients to function properly, yet an intrinsic shortage of antioxidant defenses makes the β-cell susceptible to oxidative stress and harmful byproducts such as reactive oxygen species (ROS)1–3. Insufficient oxygen supply to islets can lead to islet necrosis or prevent the maturation of pluripotent stem cell (PSC)-derived islet-like organoids. These challenges hold significant translational weight in diabetes research, as islet transplantation is advantageous over exogenous insulin administration for autonomously correcting β-cell dysfunction- the hallmark of most diabetes variants. Immediately following transplantation, the islets remain in a state of oxygen deprivation until host revascularization restores their ischemic tolerance resulting in up to 60–70% loss of cells1,4. Even islet-like clusters from the directed differentiation of PSCs lack internal microvasculature, compounding issues of cell viability with the absence of native, developmentally cell-cell interactions and uniform exposure to oxygen-rich conditions that augment their function as selective responders to glucose exposure5.

Islet transplant motivation

Type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are autoimmune or metabolic pathological manifestations, respectively, of pancreatic β-cell impairment and insulin dysregulation6. Diabetes has a significant global impact, with approximately 432 million diagnoses7. Almost 80% of them represent T2D, characterized by insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency, with primarily lifestyle-related risk factors such as obesity driving its prevalence6. Diabetes impacts over 300 million people worldwide8 and is the seventh leading cause of death in the United States alone9. The aberrant regulation of glucose in the bloodstream results in debilitating health effects, including neuropathy, kidney disease, and increased likelihood of cardiovascular episodes and stroke10. Pancreatic transplantation is currently the only long-term avenue of reconstituting endocrine output9, despite inherent surgical risk factors, subsequent immunosuppressive requirements, and the short-term nature of imparted insulin independence. Lifelong insulin administration has been modernized from multiple daily injections to insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors10. Nonetheless, current technologies impose a permanent burden on patients, as no available treatment is curative10. The widespread application of transplantation is hindered by the scarcity of high-quality islets and a significant reduction in graft survival.

Islet composition and cell sourcing

Islets can be sourced as allogeneic organ products from cadaveric donors (Edmonton protocol), living donors (Kyoto islet isolation method, among others)11, or, for a virtually inexhaustible supply, generated through directed differentiation of PSCs12. Islet transplantation by intraportal vein infusion results in substantially reduced glycemic variability and elimination of severe hypoglycemia, achieving insulin independence for up to five years. As with pancreas transplants, the scalability of this treatment is constrained by the scarcity and quality of donor islets13,14. In mitigation, platforms for the large-scale production of PSC-derived islet-like clusters have demonstrated success in vivo8, and several have undergone clinical trials15. Both primary and PSC-derived islets can be cultured ex vivo for downstream engineering and scale-up, then ultimately used to restore endogenous insulin secretion and maintain blood glucose control in diabetic patients. The importance of transplanting whole islets instead of purified β-cells cannot be overstated, as β-cells interact with other islet cells, especially α- and δ-cells, to maintain normal blood sugar levels. The α-cells secrete glucagon, which sensitizes β-cells under hyperglycemic conditions, thereby triggering insulin production that ultimately halts glucagon secretion in a feedback loop16,17. The δ-cells secrete somatostatin, which inhibits both glucagon and insulin release18.

This review discusses engineering approaches for the oxygenation of islet transplants (i) during pre-implantation, (ii) in the cell delivery device, and (iii) in the implantation site (Fig. 1). Pre-implantation strategies aim at optimizing the function of cellular products in vitro prior to clinical manipulation. Genetically engineering cells with hypoxic defenses and pre-vascularizing islet cultures both fall under this classification. Prevascularization establishes a circulatory network to prevent core necrosis and exposes cells to developmentally relevant signals that enhance secretory performance, thereby reducing cellular demand for an implant to be therapeutically effective. The design of the implantable device further determines oxygen supplementation to the islets once they are in vivo. Islet encapsulation methods to reduce immune attack on implanted cells and prevent worsening of the peri-implant immune response should also encourage the formation of new blood vessels. Finally, post-implant oxygenation techniques help maintain an oxygen-rich microenvironment inside and around the cells delivered, which is particularly important during the initial avascular phase. Oxygen can be supplemented to the implanted cells via the direct delivery of oxygen, the transportation of oxygen to the site with molecular carriers, or the in situ generation of oxygen by taking advantage of controlled gas release kinetics.

Strategies for enhancing oxygen availability and promoting vascularization in islet implantation.

Approaches encompass: (1) pre-implantation oxygenation of islet cells employing genetic engineering, or pro-vascularization approaches, (2) use of biocompatible materials for oxygenation, and (3) oxygen supplementation via engineering of the implantable devices to support the maintenance of oxygen supply to the implant or graft site.

Pre-implantation oxygenation strategies

In vitro vascularization of stem cell-derived cultures

Protocols documenting the directed evolution of PSCs to secretory islet cells drive cellular aggregates through a stepwise recapitulation of early pancreatic development by manipulating specific soluble hormones and growth factors19–23. However, the orchestration of biochemical signals used in vitro to direct PSCs towards pancreatic endocrine fates does not necessarily pertain to cells that construct internal islet vasculature, including vascular endothelial cells (ECs) that derive from the mesoderm. Engineering PSC-derived islets with the proper vasculature is a significant limitation to overcome for their transplantation. Fully developed islets isolated from the pancreas face similar complications once harvested for in vitro culture, as the rapid loss of vasculature contributes directly to impaired β-cell performance24.

Receiving 5–15% of total pancreatic blood supply25,26, native islets are a highly vascularized compartment relying on blood vessels for oxygen delivery, nutrient sensing, paracrine communication, and hormone secretion27. Within each islet, 1–5 arterioles and an extensive capillary net collectively comprise a microcirculatory network within islets of 50–250 μm25,27,28. The dense vascular network of endothelial cells (ECs), stromal cells, neurons, and immune cells surrounds and penetrates each islet, where vessels sustain a high blood flow rate (estimated in rats to be 70–125 μL/min/pancreas29) that is directly coupled to the needs for proper insulin release. Blood flow is selectively enhanced to promote rapid exchange of blood glucose and islet hormones26, allowing β-cells to sense circulating glucose and prompting the secretion of insulin and glucagon30,31. There is no anatomical segregation of endocrine cells amidst blood vessels, but rather a random patterning with no clear partiality of one cell type over another for nutrient and oxygen access28. Blood vessels and capillaries emerge concomitantly with islet morphogenesis, and their presence contributes to the structural environment, mechanical forces (i.e., fluid and blood flow), distribution of oxygen and nutrients, and paracrine interactions from supporting cell types (ex. stromal cells; vascular ECs). Key to the native islet microenvironment is the interface between individual β-cells and the dense capillary network of ECs, initiating β-cell maturation, including glucose sensitization and priming for insulin secretion32. Disruption of nutrient or oxygen supply throughout the fetal pancreas during pregnancy or after birth limits the glucose-sensitive insulin secretion of the β-cell, potentially underlying cases of congenital diabetes33,34. Likewise, failure to replicate the complex interplay of biological, chemical, and mechanical signals required to produce fully functional cells in vitro often leads to the emergence of immature phenotypes5,30,35–37. Consequently, utilizing vasculature in culture to encourage maturation of islet cells for transplantation would enhance glucose responsiveness and hormone secretion, ultimately prolonging graft survival and reducing the number of cells required for transplantation.

The 1–2 capillaries in contact with each β-cell derive a basement matrix that activates integrins to determine cell orientation for insulin granule fusion, thus specializing the cell for secretion36,38,39. Supplementation of select basement proteins in the form of a matrix-coated surface has been shown in monolayer cultures of dissociated hPSC-derived β-cell spheroids to promote local integrin activation and polarize cells, resulting in a more accurate structural assembly with up to a 3.9-fold increase in stimulated insulin secretion36. However, recreation of this aspect of the 3D microenvironment would most likely require the presence of a physical capillary network capable of branching throughout the aggregate.

In addition to basement proteins, islet vasculature provides inductive signals such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) for pancreatic development and informs the early specification of pancreatic endoderm, observed through the direct patterning of PDX1-expressing cells. The surrounding endothelium promotes the maturation of downstream endocrine derivatives in a stage-dependent manner40–43. Specific factors emerging from this crosstalk include transcription factor PTF1A, which is activated by signals from aortic endothelial cells and plays a pivotal role in the expression of insulin and glucagon44. Adding vasculature is thus an important step towards mimicking non-endocrine aspects of the native millieu for ensuring the maturation of engineered tissues during in vitro formation of islet-like tissue30,45,46.

Endothelial and fibroblast co-cultures

Efforts to optimize engineered 3D islets with vasculature have become the focus of several campaigns aiming to generate improved transplantable organoids. The most prevalent strategy has been to co-culture stem cells with ECs and/or fibroblasts, often by incorporating ECs in later-stage assembly to induce maturation. When in co-cultured with rat heart microvascular endothelial cells (RHMVECs), hPSC-derived pancreatic progenitors undergo maturation to islet cells exhibiting strong upregulation of insulin transcripts by qRT-PCR as opposed to cells that do not receive endothelial cell signaling during final-stage maturation37. EC-mediated islet cell maturation has also been studied by co-culture quantifying intracellular C-peptide as a biomarker of endogenous insulin production. Glucose responsiveness was assessed by exposing PSC-derived cells to 2.5 mM (low) or 25 mM (high) glucose for 4 h, resulting in intracellular C-peptide levels of 2.5 pg/mg in low glucose and a nearly 7-fold increase in high glucose at over 16 pg/mg. PLSR (partial least square regression) analysis revealed several genes correlated with insulin expression, including WISP1, which supports β-cell regeneration, while negative correlations with FABP1 and EPO reinforce that hypoxia impairs β-cell maturation, with normoxia restoration by endothelial cells favoring β-cell differentiation and suppressing hypoxia signaling47. With the intention of establishing multifaceted disease models of improved diagnostic potential, others used the post-differentiation assembly method to engineer islet organoids with blood vessels for enhanced maturation of β-cell function30. These experiments comprised embedding ECs with fibroblasts in a 3D fibrin gel, allowing vascular network formation, then incorporating SC-derived islets, engulfed by the vasculature. The availability of a vascular bed-like configuration of ECs to PSC-derived β-cells was shown through scRNA-seq to augment maturation-important signaling pathways, specifically BMP2/4-BMPR. While significant, such configuration has not been leveraged in protocols lacking EC vascularization. In murine studies certain voltage channel-regulating proteins, such as ELKS, are enriched at vascular-facing junctions in β-cells. Consequently, β-cells in vascularized islets display improved stimulus-dependent responsiveness to calcium, a primary cue for insulin secretion48. BMP4 signaling arose as the connecting factor between vasculature-induced pathway enrichment and improved β-cell calcium sensitivity. However, vasculature was not efficient in penetrating through SC islets30.

Co-culture with ECs improves the viability and functionality of PSC-derived islets, and can help preserve primary donor islets, which lose vascular density between donor removal and host integration24,49. Various attempts have been reported to optimize this step by culturing islet-EC composites49–51. Song et al. mixed isolated islets from the pancreas and ECs from the thoracic aorta of rats in non-adherent petri dishes at 1000–3000 islets per 0.3–0.5 million aortic EC for 2–7 days. Diatrizoate staining and electron microscopy showed that co-cultured islets maintained structural integrity in contrast to controls lacking ECs. A 14-day static incubation assay showed double insulin secretion compared to EC-absent controls (7.49 ± 0.66 mU/L insulin from controls compared to 18.84 ± 0.87 66 mU/L from co-cultures at day 14 under high glucose stimulation)49. These data, among other studies, suggest that co-culturing freshly isolated islets with ECs may enhance survival and function during the critical avascular period between tissue harvest and transplantation.

Oxygen and nutrient delivery by capillaries are vital for maturing PSC-derived pancreatic β-cells and their glucose responsiveness. Adding vasculature via co-culture with ECs or fibroblasts boosts cell maturation and insulin production. This is essential for creating quality cell products and reducing cell numbers needed for transplantation. Co-culturing with endothelial cells benefits both PSC-derived and human donor islets by improving structural integrity, delaying apoptosis, and boosting insulin secretion during isolation from vasculature.

Taken together, β-cell maturation depends on direct vascular contact, providing oxygen, ECM–integrin signals, and endothelial factors. In the absence of these cues in vitro, cells remain immature, with poor glucose responsiveness. Endothelial co-culture, with or without fibroblasts, improves insulin expression, C-peptide secretion, and glucose response, and activates maturation pathways. Thus, including endothelial cells in culture enhances islet structure, survival, and insulin secretion prior to transplantation.

Gene editing for enhancing the islet defense

Effects of hypoxia on the islet

Hypoxia is a major factor limiting the successful clinical application for both tissue-engineered and primary islets. Studies in nonhuman models suggest that endoderm commitment occurs under hypoxic conditions (1.5–5.3%) during embryogenesis52 and later, the islet becomes highly vascularized and increasingly susceptible to oxygen deficiency. Lack of oxygen blocks mitochondrial respiration and forces the β-cell from glucose catabolism into anaerobic metabolism, leading to the depletion of NAD+ and ATP, the two staples of cellular energy53. This triggers oxidative stress and disrupts critical processes such as insulin secretion, ultimately leading to cell loss. Indeed, in rat pancreatic islets, the pO2 levels are 31–40 mm Hg, whereas in the surrounding exocrine pancreas, the pO2 is between 20 and 23 mm Hg54. After intraportal transplantation, the mean islet pO2 drops below 10 mm Hg because transplanted islets rely on passive diffusion until vascularization, which takes weeks and is typically incomplete. Hypoxia impairs insulin secretion substantially55 as it is also shown by mathematical modeling, for instance, employing Michaelis-Menten kinetics56. In a low oxygen milieu, tissue damage is largely irreversible, and post-hypoxic reoxygenation leads to the production of oxidants that can activate apoptosis57. Both hypoxia2 and hyperglycemia58,59 can drive the overproduction of ROS that inflict cellular injury associated with disease states in β-cells, including T1D and T2D. Efficient vascularization in the native islet is a key determinant of endocrine cell viability through the prevention of hypoxia, which induces redox imbalance and metabolic turnover to toxin-inducing modes of energy supply. Unfortunately, methods of integrating functional vasculature into differentiating PSCs are not well-established, and the isolation of mature islets from primary tissues disrupts vascular connections, identifying inadequate oxygenation as a rudimentary flaw in the in vitro expansion of islet cell products and a leading cause of cell loss after transplantation.

Although physical vascularization of in vitro islets is a work in progress, increasing islet cells’ ability to withstand hypoxic conditions can be activated by transducing islet cells with genes for hypoxia resistance. The delivery of antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, or angiogenic genes enhances β-cell defense against hypoxic injury57,60.

Angiogenic genes

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-A is highly expressed under hypoxic conditions to induce tissue vascularization57,61. Equipping cells to overexpress VEGF-A during the initial avascular period after islet transplantation is particularly advantageous, as failure to integrate with the host vasculature compromises the graft62,63. The VEGF-A gene can be delivered both to vascular ECs or islet cells preceding transplantation to accelerate the vessel formation upon engraftment (see below), but overexpression during normoxia poses the risk of pathogenic angiogenesis, and therefore, it must be transient or modulable64. Adenovirus-based gene delivery of the VEGF-A gene to human islets has shown promise in increasing the survival of transplanted islets65,66, but limitations linked to the use of viral vectors have prompted more diverse gene delivery methods. For instance, polyethylenimine gene carriers have been engineered with hypoxia-responsive promoters. Analysis by ELISA indicated that VEGF-A is selectively induced under hypoxia following delivery to rat and pig islets, although viability was not assessed64. With the same goals of avoiding VEGF-A overexpression and use of viral vector, VEGF-A was delivered to primary mouse and human cells through liposomal transfection. By evaluating the ratio of graft vessel to total graft area for 7-day and 30-day retrieved islets, an immediate over twofold increase in graft revascularization was observed during the early post-transplantation window in transfected islets compared to non-transfected controls. Yet, an immune response was noted, necessitating modifications in the mRNA formulation for clinical application63.

Vascular ECs are another target for VEGF-A transfection. From studies in diabetic rats, the transient VEGF-A expression through viral delivery into vascular ECs normalizes blood glucose and stimulates angiogenesis after co-transplantation with islets. Islets lacking ECs or un-transfected ECs display hyperglycemia and lower microvessel density67. Upregulation of VEGF is also identified as a drug target for islet transplantation. Langlois et al. found that the iron chelator deferoxamine increases VEGF mRNA expression and secretion, identifying a possible solution for delayed graft revascularization68. While VEGF is the primary inducer of EC proliferation and vascular formation in the developing embryo69, other angiogenesis factors, including fibroblast growth factors (FGFs)70, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)71, angiopoietins71, HGF72,73, and ephrins74 accelerate vascularization in conjunction with VEGF.

Antiapoptotic genes

Antiapoptotic genes such as neuroglobin (Ngb), cytoglobin (Cygb), and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) have been successfully delivered to β-cells for defense against hypoxia. Ngb and Cygb are efficient intracellular oxygen scavengers binding oxygen and other gaseous ligands and transport them into tissues. During hypoxia, Ngb and Cygb upregulate and activate anti-apoptotic signaling to prevent cell loss75. Mendoza 2005 introduced Ngb to cultured human islets using a recombinant fusion protein with a peptide from the HIV transcriptional transactivator (TAT) protein, showing that TAT-Ngb protected the islets in culture for up to 96 h. Engineered islets maintained their functional response to glucose compared to untreated islets, indicating that TAT-Ngb may help prepare higher-quality islets for transplantation75. Others utilized the Cygb to maintain aerobic metabolism and reduce islet cell loss due to chronic hypoxia. Transfecting Cygb into rat islets reduces central islet necrosis and maintains normoglycemia for at least 90-days post-transplantation76. Following up on these results, Cygb was found to reduces hypoxic β-cell death while increasing insulin secretion in rat islets cultured under 10% or 20% oxygen for 15 days vs. untreated and vehicle-treated controls77. Adjacent results were reported in rat islet transplanted within a polyacrylonitrile-polyvinylchloride hollow fiber to extend the use of augmented Cygb into more clinically-relevant applications with immunoisolated islets78.

In addition to activating insulin under hyperglycemic conditions, GLP-1 and its analog/receptor agonist, exendin-4, support cytoprotection and improve β-cell mass. Exendin-4 significantly extends the survival of rat islets (~18 days from ~10 days) and reduces the islet quantity needed to induce euglycemia (50 vs. 150 islets) when transduced with lentivirus and transplanted under the kidney capsule of diabetic mice79. The antiapoptotic mechanisms of exendin-4 have also been evaluated in tandem with elements such as CREB, a transcription factor closely associated with glucose metabolism that is activated by exendin-4 through multiple signaling pathways. In diabetic mouse models, exendin-4 treatment of hypoxia-exposed and hypoxia-reperfused cadaveric human islets can be compounded by overexpression of CREB after adenoviral delivery to repress proapoptotic genes while significantly amplifying those critical for β-cell survival and improved glycemic (i.e., insulin receptor substrate-2, and baculoviral IAP repeat-containing 3)4. Not mentioned but of note are the anti-apoptotic genes Bcl-280, X-linked IAP81.

Antioxidant genes

ROS produced in response to oxygen deprivation damage cells by oxidizing lipids, proteins, and DNA, disrupting membrane integrity, causing protein dysfunction and DNA mutations, thus activating signaling that induces inflammation or apoptosis. Enrichment in intracellular antioxidants such as the intracellular metallothionein (MT), NAC, Taurine, and vitamins can mitigate free radical damage in β-cells. Transgenic overexpression of MT bolsters islet cytoprotection against oxidative stress by scavenging various ROS and reducing nitric oxide-induced cell death. When transplanted into diabetic mice, MT-expressing islets maintain greater insulin content and extend the duration of normoglycemia by 2-fold, amounting to an increase of 8 days compared to non-transgenic islets. This is corroborated by nitrotyrosine staining showing significantly lower free radical damage in transgenic cells82. Additionally, vitamins are antioxidants modulating protective pathways in islet cells to prevent oxygen deprivation-induced death. Vitamin E, a lipid-soluble antioxidant responsible for the neutralization of lipid radicals, defends β-cells against the membrane-damaging consequences of oxidative stress57,83. Specifically, islets treated with a combination regimen of Vitamins E and C show improved β-cell defense84. A more comprehensive catalog of antioxidants contributing to islet cell defense can be found elsewhere83.

Hence, hypoxia leads to graft failure by impairing β-cell metabolism, causing ATP depletion, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Strategies such as VEGF gene delivery aim to restore oxygenation during the post-transplant phase but require regulation to prevent abnormal vascular growth. Additional cytoprotective gene approaches targeting anti-apoptotic and antioxidant pathways improve β-cell survival and function, extending graft viability and insulin production.

Mitigating hypoxia through material advances for islet transplantation

Encapsulation strategies for local oxygen control

Encapsulated islet transplantation promises insulin independence without the need for immunosuppression, yet hypoxic graft loss limits its clinical translation. Conventional islet transplantation, implementing the Edmonton protocol, results in the loss of nearly half of the islet mass immediately post-operation due to hypoxia85,86. Stem cell-derived islets face unique vulnerabilities, and despite lower oxygen consumption rates than primary human islets87, similar graft losses are reported in preclinical models88. Wang et al. demonstrated that the population of β-cells in a stem cell-derived islet declines rapidly in 2% oxygen culture, with an 82% loss of C-peptide+/NKX6.1+ cells after 6 weeks89.

Multiple companies have pursued encapsulation platforms, including Beta-O290, ViaCyte91–97, and Sernova98,99, but none have achieved long-term function, primarily attributed to hypoxic cell death100,101. The opposing outcomes in Vertex’s clinical program bear the hallmarks of this challenge. VX-264102, utilizing encapsulated stem cell-derived islets, was terminated in March 2025 due to inadequate efficacy, while VX-880103, employing identical cells without encapsulation has yielded promising results. The persistent challenge of achieving clinical success with encapsulated cells has motivated research on device designs with improved oxygen transport in two directions, namely optimizing encapsulation designs to enhance oxygen transport while protecting against immune cells, and directly supplementing oxygen via integrated delivery systems.

A successful encapsulation design must balance the competing needs of maximizing oxygen and nutrient transport while ensuring immunoisolation through selective permeability. The foreign body response (FBR) introduces additional challenges, as fibrotic overgrowth can quickly isolate implants from host vasculature, leading to severe hypoxia within the encapsulated tissue104–106. Biocompatible materials can develop fibrotic capsules exceeding 100 μm in thickness in less than a month104, severely restricting oxygen and insulin transport106,107. The damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) resulting from hypoxia108 (e.g., HMGB1109) increase graft injury by activating the innate immune system and recruiting macrophages, creating a self-sustaining cycle of oxygen deficiency110. This cascade, where fibrosis causes hypoxic stress, leading to DAMP molecule and antigen release, recruitment of more immune cells, and additional fibrotic buildup, hampers the long-term graft survival105,111. Strategies to address these oxygenation issues include adjusting the device design and structure, choosing materials with high oxygen permeability, applying surface modifications to reduce fibrosis, and encouraging angiogenesis to bring the host’s blood vessels closer to the graft.

Device geometry and its role in diffusion and fibrosis

Islet encapsulation strategies broadly fall into two categories, microencapsulation and macroencapsulation. Microencapsulation involves the enclosure of individual or small groups of islets within spherical capsules, typically ranging from 0.2 to 2 mm in diameter, offering advantageous surface area-to-volume ratios that provide each islet with enhanced access to diffusing oxygen and nutrients112. However, achieving therapeutic doses with microencapsulation requires injecting hundreds to thousands of microspheres, creating significant challenges for complete retrieval113. When delivered into the intraperitoneal cavity in large animal and non-human primate models, microcapsules tend to sediment and aggregate, drastically reducing exposed surface area and creating severe hypoxic conditions114. Moreover, microencapsulated islets are susceptible to pericapsular fibrotic overgrowth, which has been observed in grafts retrieved from human recipients even when immunosuppression is used115.

In contrast, macroencapsulation contains therapeutic doses of islets within a single retrievable device, offering significant safety advantages, especially for stem cell-derived products where concerns about cell escape, off-target differentiation, and teratoma formation require reliable retrieval strategies116,117. Despite these safety benefits, the large size and high cell density of macrodevices make them particularly vulnerable to hypoxic graft loss116,118, to the point that graft viability is limited by insufficient oxygen supply even without fibrosis119,120. Since the maximum diffusion distance of oxygen from native capillaries is only 100–200 μm121 and oxygen solubility in bodily fluids is inherently low, encapsulation devices face significant mass transport challenges that require minimized material thickness and maximum diffusivity. Oxygen limitations at all encapsulation scales have led to the development of strategies to improve its transport, from optimizing device geometry to reduce fibrotic responses, to engineering active transport mechanisms, to encouraging vascular integration at the transplant site.

Fibrotic capsule formation is affected by the size and shape of the implant, especially for microencapsulation devices, where fibrotic overgrowth is often cited as the cause of failure114,122. Veiseh et al. showed that larger spherical devices cause less fibrotic overgrowth than smaller ones across various materials, including alginate, stainless steel, glass, polycaprolactone, and polystyrene104. When comparing 0.5 mm and 1.5 mm diameter microspheres in immunocompetent mice, the latter showed significantly less fibrotic deposition across all materials tested. They confirmed the impact of fibrosis on islet function by implanting 0.5-mm and 1.5-mm alginate microspheres containing rat islets into diabetic immunocompetent mice. The results showed that the 0.5-mm microspheres reversed diabetes for an average of 30 days, while the 1.5-mm microspheres maintained normal blood glucose levels for 175 days104. Vegas et al. corroborated these findings using human embryonic stem cell-derived β cells, observing both extended glycemic control and reduced macrophage adhesion with larger microspheres123. Ozawa et al. reported similar findings when comparing cylindrical alginate hydrogels with diameters of 1 mm and 6 mm, which developed fibrotic capsules of 400 µm and 20 µm, respectively124. Additionally, surfaces with less curvature have been shown to resist protein adhesion and cell attachment by promoting planar orientations and altering cell signaling and protein structure125,126. The variability in these outcomes shows that mitigating hypoxia requires combining mass transport engineering with biological factors to optimize diffusion efficiency and minimize the FBR.

The larger volume of macrodevices allows for more complex engineering strategies to improve oxygen transport through convection-enhancing structures. Early implementations of this approach used intravascular macrodevices, which utilize the circulating blood flow to move oxygen and nutrients through an arteriovenous shunt within the device127. Although these devices exhibited blood glucose control for more than 5 months in diabetic dogs127,128, clinical translation was inhibited by incidents of thrombosis and shunt rupture112,129. Recent re-exploration using improved surgical techniques demonstrated preserved islet viability without thrombosis in short-term (up to 30 days) swine transplantations130–133. Extravascular approaches utilizing external pumps or syringes for fluid perfusion134,135 similarly improved islet viability compared to non-perfused controls. Still, they failed to reverse diabetes in xenogeneic models of rats134 and nonhuman primates135. Alternative strategies to enhance mass transfer employ surface area-optimized geometries and porous networks to facilitate oxygen diffusion throughout the device. Ernst et al. developed interconnected toroidal hydrogels with three times the surface area and one-sixth the diffusion distance of volume-equivalent spheres, achieving normoglycemia within 2 days and sustaining blood glucose control for 84 days in diabetic immunocompetent mice136. Although increased surface area accelerates diffusion, fundamental limitations persist due to low diffusivity and solubility of oxygen. To overcome these constraints, Wang et al. exploited the 10,000-fold greater diffusivity of oxygen in the gas phase by building interconnected, water-impermeable porous structures encapsulated in alginate hydrogels137. With the scaffold fixed in the hydrogel’s core, oxygen diffuses radially outward to encapsulated islets, providing a fivefold reduction in necrotic tissue formation compared to the control. This design achieved a 180-day reversal of diabetes when implanted into the intraperitoneal cavity of mice137. Liu et al. combined these approaches in the SHIELD device, incorporating a hollow cylindrical hydrogel within a nanofibrous pore network138. Optimizing nanofiber pore size prevented cell migration, while zwitterionic alginate modification reduced cell attachment, thereby improving the device’s oxygen permeability and immunoprotective capabilities. In this setup, rat islets transplanted intraperitoneally into diabetic mice achieved normoglycemia for 399 days. Additionally, human stem cell-derived β cells reversed diabetes in mice for up to 238 days, demonstrating a clinically relevant model of safe stem cell transplantation138. Although architectural optimization greatly enhances results, the most effective long-term strategies merge these geometric principles with improved materials.

Material design for balancing permeability and biocompatibility

The physical and chemical properties of encapsulation materials, including oxygen permeability, mechanical characteristics, and biological interactions, fundamentally determine oxygen availability to encapsulated islets. Material porosity presents an immediate challenge, as pores should be small for immunoisolation yet large enough for vascular integration. For exclusion of immune cells and smaller effector molecules, pores must be below 0.8 μm, preventing macrophage and T-cell infiltration139. However, small pore structures can prevent neovascularization, which is critical for resolving hypoxia and functional blood glucose control. Brauker et al. demonstrated that polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes with five μm pores supported 80–125 times more vascular integration and exhibited minimal fibrotic overgrowth compared to 0.02 μm PTFE membranes, which developed fibrotic capsules 30–100 μm thick140. This trade-off has profound consequences for oxygen transport. Modeling studies determined that 100 μm of fibrotic overgrowth on 0.5 mm alginate microspheres reduces the oxygen available to encapsulated islets to 3.4 mmHg. In contrast, direct vascularization at the alginate surface, facilitated by an outer membrane with larger pores, increases the available oxygen to a functional level of 27.4 mm Hg141. The severity of this constraint has a direct impact on the therapeutic capacity of an implant. Suszynski et al. modeled that 100 μm of fibrosis is sufficient to reduce the secretory capacity of a 150 μm islet by 42% and induce necrosis. This reaches to a 98% loss in secretory capacity for islets larger than 300 μm142.

The choice of base polymer also dictates the maximum rate of oxygen transport, a property governed by the oxygen permeability of the material, defined as the product of its oxygen diffusivity and solubility. Alginate remains the most extensively studied material, owing to its mild polymerization conditions, low cytotoxicity, and non-degradability in mammals113,143. As its matrix consists primarily of water, alginate exhibits an oxygen diffusivity near that of water itself, ~1.5 × 10−5 cm2/s to 2.7 × 10−5 cm2/s144–146, allowing for relatively efficient oxygen transport. This property has enabled long-term normoglycemia in preclinical rodent models using unmodified alginate spheres104,147. However, alginate elicits fibrotic overgrowth in humans, restricting oxygen delivery to encapsulated islets. Moreover, analysis of clinical grafts revealed viable islets only in regions without amyloid deposits, which comprise fibrils inducing cytotoxicity148. Other naturally derived hydrogels, such as agarose149, cellulose150,151, gelatin152,153, hyaluronic acid154,155, and collagen156–158, have been used to encapsulate pancreatic islets. Synthetic polymers, such as hydrogels or porous scaffolds, offer distinct advantages due to their tunable characteristics but also have drawbacks, including a lack of bioactivity and a tendency to activate the immune response159. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is commonly used in hydrogel scaffolds and as a coating material because its hydrophilicity mitigates fibrosis and slows down hydrolytic degradation160. Compared with alginate161 and hyaluronic acid162, PEG it exhibits superior vascularization and increased functional duration, although direct comparisons have yielded conflicting outcomes163. Silicone-based materials are also frequently used in islet tissue engineering, primarily due to the high oxygen permeability of their siloxane backbones, which provides them with an oxygen diffusivity greater than that of water. Exploiting this improved oxygen permeability, a silicone-polycarbonate-urethane nanofibrous device has been reported that maintains normoglycemia for 200 days with allogeneic islets and over 100 days with xenogeneic rat islets in immunocompetent mice164. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is also a silicon-based material commonly used in macrodevice construction, as its diffusion coefficient of ~3.5⋅10−5–5⋅10−5 cm2/s165–167 promotes oxygen transport through diffusion-limited scaffolds139,168.

In addition to permeability, the mechanical properties of an encapsulating material can dynamically influence oxygen availability through biological and physical mechanisms. Material stiffness is a key cellular microenvironment regulator, influencing islet function and immune response169,170. Schmitz et al. demonstrated that cells encapsulated in softer, lower-concentration gelatin-methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels experienced more severe hypoxia than those in stiffer gels at the same density. This was not caused by lower material permeability but by the softer matrix encouraging greater cell spreading and metabolic activity, which increased the local oxygen consumption rate. This illustrates how mechanical cues can directly influence the oxygen demand of the encapsulated graft171. The material’s viscoelastic properties also influence its intrinsic oxygen diffusivity. Computational modeling of poroelastic scaffolds has shown that highly elastic materials with a low Young’s Modulus, such as soft alginates, enhance convective transport by deforming under flow, which can raise local oxygen levels by up to 30% compared to rigid scaffolds172.

Surface modification to mitigate the foreign body response

A primary challenge, regardless of device geometry or bulk material properties, is the FBR, since even highly biocompatible materials can trigger the formation of a fibrotic capsule that greatly hinders oxygen and nutrient flow. Designing the material surface to interrupt the FBR process is an effective way to maintain the device-host interface. These modifications aim to either make the surface “invisible” to macrophages or to release factors that locally reprogram the immune environment to be more tolerant of the implant111,159.

The most common approach is to develop anti-fouling surfaces that stop the initial protein adsorption, which triggers macrophage activation and subsequent fibrosis111,173. In this context, zwitterionic coatings like sulfobetaine can be grafted onto materials such as alginate174, GelMA175, or polyurethane176, where they form a tightly bound hydration layer that mitigates protein adhesion. This strategy significantly reduces pericapsular fibrosis, resulting in a 100-day increase in diabetes reversal as compared to unmodified implants174. The addition of hydrophobic groups has also shown significant benefits to fibrosis mitigation and implant function, such as triazole-based chemistries, which were first identified from a combinatorial library of fibrosis-mitigating alginates177. Stem cell-derived β- cells encapsulated within triazole-functionalized alginates maintained normoglycemia in immunocompetent mice for over 170 days with virtually no fibrosis123. When applied to PDMS-based macrodevices, the same modification reduced fibrotic capsule thickness from over 150 µm to less than 50 µm139, demonstrating the versatility of the chemistry across different material platforms.

An alternative to passive anti-fouling involves releasing immunomodulatory agents from the device. By incorporating a colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor, which targets macrophage proliferation, an impressive 460-day reversal of diabetes was reported in immunocompetent mice with alginate microcapsules178. A similar effect was seen in alginate macrodevices, where the CSF1R inhibitor decreased capsule thickness by 64% in a subcutaneously implanted xenogeneic model179. Other strategies release bioactive factors that reprogram the local immune response. For example, covalent attachment of the peptide LL-37 to PTFE membranes has been shown to promote a shift toward anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, increasing vascular density 13-fold and reducing fibrotic encapsulation from over 1200 µm to 115 µm180. Similarly, co-encapsulation of islets with materials that release the chemokine CXCL12 can recruit regulatory T-cells to the graft site, creating a localized tolerant microenvironment that supports graft function for more than 150 days181. While surface modifications improve the transport of existing oxygen supply, promoting angiogenesis boosts local oxygen availability by recruiting host vasculature to the implant site.

Promoting angiogenesis for improved oxygen delivery

A significant challenge with avascular implants is their reliance on passive diffusion from distant host capillaries182. Vascularization addresses this issue by reducing the diffusion distance, which improves oxygen delivery, supports glucose sensing, and enhances insulin secretion for physiological glycemic control183,184. Procedures to induce sufficiently high vasculature can potentially compromise immunoisolation unless the device is designed to keep an efficient immune barrier140. A range of pro-angiogenic approaches can overcome this challenge by pre-conditioning the implant site and forming vasculature before delivering cells, which release pro-angiogenic factors.

One way for site prevascularization is to leverage the FBR by using a sterile implant to create a highly vascularized tissue pocket that can incorporate the therapeutic cell payload185. Permanent and open-pored devices such as the Theracyte device (San Clemente, CA)186 or the Sernova Corp’s Cell Pouch (London, ON, Canada)187 can be utilized for this purpose. These devices are typically implanted for several weeks to facilitate host vessel infiltration before islets are introduced into the system. In preclinical models, this strategy is effective with reversal of diabetes for periods ranging from 100 days to 6 months at subcutaneous sites187–189. However, translating such success to a clinical setting has been challenging. For instance, graft function was not maintained using the Sernova Cell Pouch and ViaCyte’s (San Diego, California) vascularizing devices190–192. C-peptide levels appeared briefly within 24 h and then disappeared190, which is indicative of early engraftment failure. ViaCyte’s encapsulated stem cell product VC-02™ resulted in only 4% of the initial cell mass compared to the original amount at six months. The significant gap between preclinical and clinical results indicates a crucial mismatch in scale and metabolic demands. A vascular bed that is sufficient for a mouse islet dose can be inadequate to meet the significant oxygen consumption associated with a full clinical dose of human islets, leading to acute hypoxic failure.

To address the challenges of using permanent implants, temporary cell delivery devices, such as catheters, can be employed to create a pre-vascularized subcutaneous site. The islets are introduced after removing the implant. This approach was shown to aid in the reversal of diabetes in mice for over 100 days with a significant increase in graft vascular density at a site where unmodified subcutaneous transplants previously failed193. This method also promoted the maturation and function of stem cell-derived islets, enabling the maintenance of normoglycemia approximately 100 days after transplant87. Similarly, the subcutaneous host-enabled alginate thread system utilized a pre-implanted nylon catheter to induce formation of a vascularized pocket194, resulting in the amelioration of diabetes for over 190 days in syngeneic and allogeneic mouse models. However, the device did not support the implantation of human islets into non-immunosuppressed diabetic mice. The induced vasculature supported rodent islets, but it was inadequate for a therapeutic dose of human islets because of their higher oxygen consumption rate.

Another approach for vascularization involves incorporating pro-angiogenic factors into implanted devices to recruit the host vasculature. Biomaterials can be engineered to deliver sustained and localized release of growth factors and matrix signaling molecules, promoting faster neovascularization and reducing the risk of hypoxic graft loss. This can be achieved by conjugation of the vascularizing factor to the material or physical encapsulation to control its delivery for a sufficiently long time until vasculature is formed185. For example, Yin et al. covalently conjugated VEGF to an alginate hydrogel, which resulted in an almost eightfold increase in blood vessel density compared to alginate gels containing physically encapsulated VEGF, and approximately 30-fold increase compared to pristine alginate. Implantation in diabetic mice supported sustained normoglycemia, but the free VEGF and unmodified groups were unable to achieve this outcome195. The alginate gels that contained free VEGF released 90% of their payload in 24 h, while the VEGF-conjugated alginate still contained 70% after 1 month, with a stable and long-term gradient for forming a functional vascular network. Controlled release of vasculogenic factors has been achieved using different engineering strategies, such as attaching peptides or growth factors, including RGD, VEGF, or PDGF, to biodegradable hydrogels168,196,197. These factors were released as the hydrogels degraded and were remodeled by infiltrating host cells, matching the release rate with the speed of tissue formation. An efficient approach would be to engineer a two-component device with a non-degradable core to host the islets, surrounded by a degradable and vasculogenic outer layer with the pro-angiogenic factors. This configuration can enhance the vascular density while providing immune protection.

The mechanisms of angiogenesis are more complex than simply delivering growth factors because these molecules also influence the local immune environment. Pro-angiogenic signals can enhance the recruitment of healing-associated immune cells, including M2-like macrophages. These cells then secrete more angiogenic factors, which provide a microenvironment that supports blood vessel growth and tissue integration198,199. Additional improvements can be achieved by delivering small molecules that stimulate the tissue to produce its own growth factors. For example, releasing the adenosine agonist 5’-N-ethylcarboxamidoadenosine (NECA) locally from a polymeric sheet was shown to increase VEGF expression in the graft. This approach enhanced endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation, leading to improved neovascularization and superior islet engraftment (Fig. 2)200. NECA treatment enhanced islet viability as provided by live/dead and CCK-8 assays (Fig. 2 A, B), maintained insulin secretion in normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 2C, D), preserved functional responsiveness based on stimulation index (SI) (Fig. 2 E), and upregulated VEGF expression as indicated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 2 F, G). Another strategy is to modify the biomaterial through the incorporation of survival signals that islets lose during isolation. Polymers such as alginate or PEG are bio-inert materials lacking the extracellular matrix (ECM) motifs for cell adhesion. ECM components, such as RGD peptides, can be used to restore vital cell-matrix interactions by providing islet integrin binding sites198. This addition augments islet cell adhesion and survival, and reduces macrophage-driven inflammation and fibrotic response to the implant199,201. Kuwabara et al. reported that alginate microcapsules with collagen IV and the RGD peptide significantly enhanced allograft survival in an immunocompetent mouse model compared to other peptide modifications. The functional benefit was shown by a 5.8-fold decrease in the number of capsules affected by fibrosis and a 14-fold increase in the oxygen consumption rate of the islets. These results indicate that restoring native cell-matrix signaling can substantially improve islet metabolism and protect the graft from pericapsular overgrowth202.

A more advanced approach is to deliver endothelial cells (ECs) along with mesenchymal or stromal cells to build a vascular network. This way, the delays and variability of host vessel recruitment can be avoided by engineering a functional microvasculature before implantation or by promoting rapid self-assembly in the body. For example, endothelial cells can be seeded on biocompatible scaffolds, such as decellularized lung matrix203 or fibrin204, where they form perfusable networks. Islets are then added to these vascular beds, enabling immediate integration and rapid diabetes reversal in animal models203,204. This approach has been advanced using hiPSC-derived ECs to form vascular networks204. Bioprinting methods have further improved this approach by spatially patterning stem cell-derived islets and ECs into islet-like structures. This design promoted islet maturation and formation of interconnected capillaries in vitro205. Islets were also co-transplanted with microvessel fragments, which rapidly anastomosed with the host circulation206,207. This method was crucial for stem cell-derived pancreatic progenitors, which could not reverse diabetes on their own due to hypoxia. Co-transplanted microvessel fragments enabled long-term function206. Neovascularized implantable cell homing and encapsulation (NICHE) devices combine prevascularization with mesenchymal stromal cells (Fig. 3). The device is shown by optical and SEM imaging of the 2-layer mesh and nanoporous membrane (Fig. 3A). Histological analysis demonstrates the host response, with Masson’s trichrome staining and quantification of fibrotic capsule thickness and implant reactivity scores revealing good biocompatibility of the materials (Fig. 3B-E). Cross-sectional optical images indicate differences in subcutaneous integration between materials, with improved tissue ingrowth occurring in polyamide (PA) devices (Fig. 3F, G). Surface characterization reveals differences in roughness and wettability between materials, which can influence their biointegration behavior (Fig. 3H, I). Vascular density in the device is shown by blood vessel staining and vessel density analysis (Fig. 3J, K). Sustained release profile of immunosuppressive agents from the NICHE reservoir is shown in (Fig. 3L). Functional assays demonstrate that exposure to these agents does not impair glucose-responsive insulin secretion (Fig. 3M-O). The fractional blood volume at the subcutaneous site increased from 4% to 13%, and vascular density exceeded that of the native pancreas, which enabled long-term allograft function208,209. These vascularization techniques can be combined with immunoprotective strategies. For example, islets enclosed in protective devices can be used with engineered microvascular meshes to balance vascular access and immunoisolation204. Despite improved vascularization, oxygen delivery to islets remains limited by the low partial pressure of oxygen in the microvasculature, which may be inadequate for optimal islet function210. This challenge has motivated the development of active oxygen supplementation strategies.

In summary, material-based approaches to mitigate hypoxia in islet transplantation focus on improving passive oxygen transport, limiting fibrosis, and preserving graft integration. Device geometry and architecture can decrease diffusion distance and influence the extent of the FBR, while material selection determines oxygen permeability, mechanical behavior, and host compatibility. Surface modifications and engineering enhance graft performance by reducing protein adsorption, macrophage activation, and fibrotic capsule formation, while angiogenic and prevascularization strategies decrease the distance between the graft and host vasculature. Overall, these approaches enhance oxygen availability indirectly, but their long-term success is limited by oxygen solubility and the delayed formation of a fully functional vascular network.

Post-implant oxygen supplementation

Direct delivery of gaseous oxygen

Islet encapsulation devices have demonstrated inadequate islet survival in clinical trials even with optimization of materials, vascularization, and device geometry190–192,211,212. Despite an enhanced vascular network, passive diffusion cannot satisfy the high metabolic demand of the graft until the new vasculature becomes functional and perfused213–215. This oxygen shortage leads to large-scale cell death, posing a major obstacle to successful cell encapsulation-based therapies. To bridge this important gap, proactive strategies such as active oxygen supplementation are needed. These approaches create an artificial and oxygen-rich microenvironment in the device to maintain a sufficiently high local partial oxygen pressure (PO₂) supporting islet survival and function during the initial avascular phase. A major challenge in islet transplantation is balancing the need to prevent hypoxia and avoid hyperoxia, since too much oxygen can produce damaging reactive oxygen species and block the native angiogenic signals needed for host integration216,217. Three main strategies have been developed to deliver supplemental oxygen: (1) direct delivery of gaseous oxygen from a reservoir, (2) the use of chemical or biological carriers to enable transport, and (3) in situ generation of oxygen through controlled chemical or electrochemical reactions. Similarly, approaches including perfusion devices218 have been suggested to prolong islet survival and preserve their function during storage prior to transplantation. In addition, in vitro microfluidic and organ-on-chip platforms incorporating controlled oxygen delivery have shown improved islet viability and glucose responsiveness under hypoxic conditions, showing the critical role of oxygen in maintaining islet health prior to implantation219–222.

The most direct way to supplement oxygen involves implanting a device with a refillable gas chamber, maintaining high levels of oxygen near the encapsulated islets. This method provides close control of oxygen and avoids cytotoxicity from chemical reactions or byproducts112. The first oxygen-supplemented islet implant tested in a clinical trial used this approach, demonstrating significant improvements in cell survival and creating a foundation for future innovations182. Efficient oxygen transport to the encapsulated cells is achieved through the concentration gradient-driven diffusion mechanism. Continuous oxygen replenishment then provides sustained and long-term support of cellular oxygen needs.

Beta-O2 Technologies (Rosh Haayin, Israel) demonstrated a clinical application of this technology with their βAir device, which supported transplanted islets in humans without immunosuppression. The device features a gas chamber between islet-laden alginate hydrogels, enclosed within a PTFE immunoisolation membrane223,224. Barkai et al. showed that the βAir device maintained allogeneic islet function for up to 180 days without immunosuppression in rats223. Later, Ludwig et al. reported a human pilot study, in which the oxygenating and immunoisolating βAir device supported islet viability for 10 months in a T1D patient without immunosuppressive therapy. Although the islet dose was subtherapeutic (2100 IEQ/kg body weight), the patient exhibited enhanced basal and glucose-responsive C-peptide levels with daily insulin needs dropping from 52 ± 5.8 IU/day to 43 ± 4.9 IU/day225. A follow-up clinical trial showed that the device prevented rejection without immunosuppression; however, therapeutic efficacy was limited. PET/CT imaging indicated delayed permeation across the membrane, suggesting impaired diffusion of glucose (180 Da), and even greater delay for insulin (5800 Da), which may have limited glucose-responsive function226. Other practical limitations include the device’s large size and the need for daily transcutaneous refills. Permanent immunoisolation strategy requires long-term in vivo stability without degradation, which prevents vascularization and requires continuous maintenance. While a recent study reported promising results using a smaller device in a diabetic rat model to address some of these limitations227, the practical challenges of the βAir device motivated the development of alternative system designs.

Another way for direct oxygenation is to use the atmosphere as an oxygen source. A transcutaneous device exposes the graft to ambient air (~160 mmHg PO₂) and connects it to the subcutaneous space for nutrient exchange. Komatsu et al. developed a microelectromechanical systems device using a transcutaneous cannula attached to a silicone diffuser that contained the islet graft. The system exhibited a three-fold increase in islet survival in diabetic rats228 and supported twice the islet density of control devices in a refined design229. More recently, An et al. engineered a biphasic “atmosphere-breathing” device to overcome the main challenges of transcutaneous implantation230. This device uses a perfluorocarbon (PFC)-infused nanomembrane at the air interface to prevent dehydration and bacterial growth. The device is modular, allowing the cell-laden component to be refilled non-surgically. The system maintained islet viability and reversed diabetes in a rat-to-mouse xenograft model, and showed successful cell refilling in a canine model230. Although promising, significant challenges for atmosphere-breathing devices include their scalability to clinically relevant sizes and the need for long-term maintenance of a permanent transcutaneous interface229,230.

Chemical and biological oxygen carriers

Instead of bulk gas storage, chemical and biological oxygen carriers can be used to increase oxygen availability through the incorporation of molecules that reversibly bind and transport oxygen in the encapsulation material. Examples include hemoglobin-based molecules and PFC emulsions. They act as local oxygen buffers and release the stored oxygen in response to the metabolic demand of the graft121. Unlike oxygen generators, these carriers rely on the surrounding oxygen levels, only transporting it from high to low-concentration regions.

Hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers are appealing for islet oxygenation because, like in the blood, they regulate oxygen and support cell signaling and inflammatory response231. Native hemoglobin (Hb) releases oxygen as local PO₂ decreases, with P50 representing 50% saturation. This property is preserved in stabilized and polymerized therapeutic formulations of Hb231,232. Avila et al. reported an early application of polymerized human Hb in islet isolation. They showed that Hb-treated islets had higher viability and glucose response compared to controls, establishing the proof-of-concept for Hb-assisted islet transplantation233. Later, Espes et al. applied polymerized bovine Hb for in situ oxygenation of intramuscular islet grafts, reducing apoptosis and significantly improving insulin secretion by 50%234. However, human hemoglobin can carry four oxygen molecules per tetramer and has a P50 of 27 mmHg. Because pancreatic tissue physiologically has a higher PO2 (~40 mmHg), Hb remains saturated, which may limit its effectiveness for islet oxygenation232,235. A recent innovative study explored marine worm-derived Hbs, which have a significantly higher oxygen-binding capacity (156 O2 binding sites) and a P50 of 37 mmHg, which is well-matched to native pancreatic tissue235. These Hbs maintain higher islet survival under hypoxia and mitigate hypoxic stress marker expression compared to the untreated controls235. When combined with metal peroxide oxygen-generating reagents, marine worm Hbs exhibit superoxide dismutase-like activity by modulating reactive oxygen species-induced islet cell death236 and upregulating pro-angiogenic VEGF secretion237. This suggests they can fine-tune the local environment to match islet metabolic needs. The therapeutic potential of this approach is supported by successful early clinical trials using marine worm Hb for kidney preservation238.

PFCs are oxygen carriers that are chemically inert and dissolve oxygen in proportion to their partial pressure, enabling them to hold significantly higher amounts of oxygen compared to blood232,239,240. PFCs have successfully increased islet yields two- to three-fold in whole pancreas preservation241,242, however, their clinical application has been limited due to unpredictable distribution and toxicity issues. The findings from islet encapsulation have been mixed. Johnson et al. found that PFC emulsions provided little benefit to islet survival except under severe hypoxia and in some cases induced unwanted immune responses in vivo unless alginate and PLL coatings were applied243. Similarly, other studies have linked the failure of PFC-treated islets in vivo to fibrin-triggered inflammation244. Recent studies have focused on addressing these hurdles with improved formulations. For example, Domingo-Lopez et al. developed a PFC emulsion in hyaluronic acid hydrogels that improved islet survival and function in vitro154,245. An innovative approach by Gattas-Asfura et al. covalently linked PFCs to alginate, producing a water-soluble hydrogel with enhanced oxygen transport that significantly increased islet survival in encapsulated microbeads246. While these results are promising, PFCs rely on external oxygen sources and have biocompatibility concerns, limiting their use for short-term therapies.

Oxygen generation through controlled reactions

Oxygen can also be generated in situ chemically, for example, by oxidation with peroxides and electrolytic splitting of water. These strategies provide controlled oxygen release kinetics with minimal harmful byproducts and can be adjusted with material barriers or electrochemical stimuli to maintain oxygen at optimal levels at the transplant site.

Oxidation strategies involve the encapsulation of a peroxide compound in a polymeric matrix or distributed within a larger construct112,247–252. While calcium peroxide (CaO2) has been the most widely studied oxygen-generating substrate for islet transplantation156,217,253–258, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)259, sodium percarbonate (2Na2CO3·3H2O2)260,261, and lithium peroxide (Li2O2)262 have also been explored as oxygen sources for β-cells. Upon contacting water, solid peroxides decompose into H2O2, which further dissociates into water and O2. The generation of intermediate H2O2 poses a challenge since early burst-release post-transplantation can induce accumulation of reactive oxygen species, leading to oxidative stress and cell death. A polymeric barrier helps control oxygen release kinetics247. The application of this method to improve islet function was demonstrated by Pedraza et al. using solid CaO₂ encapsulated in a PDMS disk. The 3D construct released adequate oxygen to adjust the locally hypoxic environment to physiological levels for two weeks and continued to release oxygen for over 40 days. When the disk was placed in a 3D agarose construct hosting MIN6 β-cells under hypoxia, a 93% cell viability was noted after three days, which was statistically similar to normoxic controls. The results demonstrated the cytocompatibility and therapeutic benefits of the disk when used with insulin-secreting cells217. In a follow-up study by Coronel et al., MIN6 β-cells were encapsulated in a CaO2-laden PDMS disk called OxySite (Fig. 4). The device achieved physiological glucose levels in diabetic mice over 30 days post-transplantation. This is shown by a higher number of animals reaching normoglycemia (Fig. 4A) and improved non-fasting blood glucose levels compared to controls (Fig. 4B). Metabolic assessment indicated improved glycemic clearance in OxySite-recipient animals during intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) (Fig. 4C). Ex vivo analysis of explanted grafts showed increased total insulin content and improved stimulation index (Fig. 4D). Live/dead staining confirmed enhanced cell viability in the device (Fig. 4E). In addition, human islets encapsulated in OxySite showed a 3.7-fold higher glucose stimulation index than the non-oxygenated controls, indicating the compatibility of the construct with primary human islets254. Liang et al. refined this approach by developing macroporous scaffolds containing CaO2-laden PDMS microbeads for transplantation in diabetic rats. The system rapidly reversed diabetes at a clinically relevant islet purity of ~80% without limiting vascular integration. The results demonstrated that localized oxygen generation can support cell viability and tissue engraftment255.

Recently, Li2O2 was explored as an alternative oxygen-generating material. Wang et al. developed an inverse-breathing encapsulation device (iBED) that generates oxygen from a reaction between Li2O2 and carbon dioxide (CO2). The device contained Li₂O₂ and PFC phases in a chamber separated by a silicone membrane, placed near an alginate hydrogel laden with islets. CO₂ from the islets diffused into the chamber, reacted with Li2O2, and the released oxygen diffused to the islets. The optimized device restored normoglycemia in immunocompetent diabetic mice for over 3 months262. The iBED system represents a significant advancement in oxygen-generating technologies, establishing a feedback loop where increased cellular metabolic activity generates more oxygen, thereby enabling a dynamic response.

Peroxide-based systems offer advantages for short-term oxygen supplementation; however, their finite supply limits their long-term application. Electrolytic oxygen generation provides a renewable alternative to support long-term transplant viability by splitting water into oxygen and hydrogen. This method was first investigated by Wu et al. in 1999 and involves applying a low voltage across two electrodes to split water. The electrochemical reaction takes place at the anode, where water is oxidized to generate molecular oxygen, and water molecules are reduced at the cathode to form hydrogen263. Krishnan et al. recently reported a major improvement with a battery-free and wireless electrochemical oxygen generator supporting therapeutic islet transplantation. The device included islets in an oxygen-permeable macrodevice, which, upon subcutaneous transplantation with rat islets in diabetic mice, maintained normoglycemia for over a month at high islet densities (~1000 IEQ/cm2)264. Lee et al. further advanced this technology by developing an electrocatalytic oxygen generator (ecO2) that works in contact with biofluids, eliminating the need for an external water source. The device enhanced the viability of high-density cell clusters by approximately threefold after 10 days of subcutaneous implantation in rats265. In contrast to chemical oxygen-generating systems, electrolytic devices can produce oxygen over the long term, potentially supporting permanent immunoisolation strategies that suppress vascularization. However, challenges remain in power supply, electrode stability and lifetime, biocompatibility, and scalability for clinical translation.

In summary, post-implant oxygen supplementation strategies focus on overcoming the limitations of passive diffusion during the early avascular phase. Direct delivery of gaseous oxygen provides the most controlled oxygen source, however, it usually requires refilling or transcutaneous access. Chemical and biological oxygen carriers, such as hemoglobin-based materials and perfluorocarbons, can help maintain local oxygen levels, but their efficiency depends on the availability of surrounding oxygen and the biocompatibility of the formulation. In situ oxygen-generating systems offer a self-sustained oxygen supply, but challenges remain in the formation of non-cytocompatible byproducts, controlling release kinetics, design and engineering complexity, and clinical translation. Overall, direct oxygenation strategies demonstrate strong promise, especially when combined with biomaterials and vascularization approaches that support long-term graft survival and function.

Conclusions and future directions

Oxygen is vital for islet function, dependent on a highly vascularized microenvironment and aerobic respiration. Hypoxia negatively impacts islets, leading to necrosis and impaired stem cell maturation. Efficient vascularization is essential for viability, glucose responsiveness, and graft survival. Understanding the role of oxygen is paramount to addressing beta-cell dysfunction.

Diabetes causes insulin dysregulation, and while external insulin is common, islet transplantation offers a more physiological solution. However, post-transplant hypoxia leads to extensive cell death and limits graft survival, hindering clinical use. Future therapies will incorporate personalized treatments tailored to patient factors such as age, immune status, and existing medical conditions. Refining stem cell differentiation protocols for islet production and utilizing gene delivery of antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, or angiogenic genes can enhance hypoxia resistance and immune protection. Pre-implant oxygenation, delivery device modification, and site optimization can be implemented synergistically to prevent hypoxia, promote angiogenesis, and sustain oxygen after transplantation. To this end, future efforts to develop devices that are scalable with real-time oxygen monitoring are highly desirable. Effective encapsulation and delivery strongly influence oxygen transport to transplanted cells and are critical for oxygenation success.

Encapsulation is an effective way to protect islets while maintaining their function. Microencapsulation (<2 mm capsules) has practical limitations, such as difficulty in achieving a therapeutic dose, aggregation, and fibrotic overgrowth, whereas macroencapsulation allows the delivery of more islets but can be associated with hypoxia at high densities. This necessitates the improvement of device geometry and materials to enhance diffusion and vascularization. Computational modeling will play an important role in optimizing oxygen diffusion and prevent fibrotic encapsulation, and hybrid micro/macroencapsulation systems are expected to improve performance. Continued progress in materials science will be crucial for advancing encapsulation efficiency and oxygenation design. Relevant materials must prevent immune cell infiltration and reduce immune reaction while allowing insulin and vascular exchange. Material stiffness and viscoelasticity also influence oxygen transport, suggesting that future material designs should strategically modulate these properties. Smart, dynamic biomaterials that adapt stiffness and permeability to local oxygenation and immune cues, with improved durability, biocompatibility, and vascular integration, will be essential for graft success in the long term.

Balancing angiogenesis and immunoisolation remains critical in encapsulation devices, which can induce prevascularization and reverse diabetes. Alternatively, incorporating angiogenic factors or endothelial cells can foster vascular networks. Future vascularization strategies include controlled release of growth factors, biofabricated prevascularized constructs, and co-culture systems to accelerate vascular integration. Additional oxygenation technologies may be needed if vascularization is insufficient.

Oxygen delivery options include gaseous systems, oxygen carriers, and in situ generation methods. Gaseous approaches offer limited scalability, while carriers rely on external oxygen sources and raise biocompatibility concerns. Chemical generation via peroxide decomposition or electrolytic water splitting can sustain oxygen availability at the transplant site. Peroxide-based systems are suitable for short-term use, whereas electrolytic systems offer continuous supply but face challenges in power requirements, electrode stability, and scalability. Future designs should focus on self-sustaining oxygen platforms that operate without external power, combining chemical and electrochemical methods for dynamic control.

Collectively, these biological and engineering strategies demonstrate the important role of oxygen in maintaining islet function. Future studies that integrate gene editing, vascularization, and biomaterials advances will be essential to achieve durable, oxygenated, and functional islet grafts for diabetes therapy.
