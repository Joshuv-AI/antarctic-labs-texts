The Aarhus Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale (A-PGDs): identification of a clinically validated cut-off score for identifying bereaved at risk of PGD
Authors: Enya Redican, Maja O'Connor, Nicolai Ladegaard, Maria Louison Vang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) is a new stress-related disorder included in both the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR. It is characterised by persistent longing for and preoccupation with the deceased, accompanied by associated emotional symptoms. The Aarhus Prolonged Grief Disorder scale (A-PGDs) is a self-report measure based on these diagnostic criteria. A clinically validated straightforward scoring method may increase its clinical utility.Objective: This study aimed to establish a scoring procedure for identifying probable ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD cases using the A-PGDs. Specifically, we sought to develop a two-step approach requiring endorsement of core symptoms and exceeding a cut-off score on associated symptoms.Method: Participants included bereaved adults (n = 122; 89.9% female) experiencing grief-related difficulties more than six months post loss. Optimal item thresholds for core symptoms were identified, and receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analyses determined the optimal cut-off score for associated symptoms. The Aarhus Structured Clinical Interview (A-PGDi) served as the gold standard.Results: Endorsing core symptoms at a threshold of ≥3 identified 81.1% (n = 99) of participants. For these individuals, a cut-off score of ≥27 on the associated symptom items produced sensitivity and specificity of 80% and 61%, respectively, for ICD-11 PGD, and a cut-off score of ≥29 produced sensitivity and specificity of 81% and 66%, respectively, for DSM-5-TR PGD. Using the full two-step method, sensitivity and specificity were 80% and 70% for ICD-11 PGD, and 78% and 74% for DSM-5-TR PGD. Prevalence estimates were 34.4% for probable ICD-11 PGD and 36.0% for DSM-5-TR PGD.Conclusion: This two-step scoring method provides a simple, easy-to-use approach for clinicians to identify probable PGD cases.KEYWORDS: Prolonged grief disorder, ROC analysis, ICD-11, DSM-5-TR, prevalencePALABRAS CLAVE: Trastorno de duelo prolongado, análisis ROC, CIE-11, DSM-5-TR, prevalencia

The ICD-11 (World Health Organisation [WHO], 2018) and DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022) include Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) as a distinct psychiatric disorder. Both systems characterise PGD as involving intense longing for and/or preoccupation with the deceased in addition to associated symptoms such as sadness, emotional numbness, identity disruption, anger, and social withdrawal. These symptoms must be accompanied with significant functional impairment, persist for a defined period of time after the loss, and exceed what is typically considered within the bounds of normal grieving as per cultural or religious norms (APA, 2022; WHO, 2018). Although the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD criteria are mostly comparable, there are a few differences regarding the time criterion (duration of 6 months of symptoms in ICD-11 and 12 months since the loss in DSM-5-TR), the minimum number of associated symptoms required (a clinical evaluation of the presence of associated symptoms in ICD-11, often quantified as at least one in research, and at least 3 out of 8 in DSM-5-TR), and the specific types of associated symptoms included (such as blame in ICD-11 and loneliness in DSM-5-TR) (Eisma et al., 2022).

The Aarhus PGD-scale (A-PGDs; O'Connor et al., 2023) is one of the few validated self-report measures available which evaluates PGD symptoms in accordance with both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR guidelines. Although the A-PGDs can assess symptom severity via a composite score or probable caseness through a diagnostic algorithm, the absence of a clinically meaningful and easily applied scoring approach hinders clinicians from efficiently identifying patients with clinically relevant symptomatology. To address similar challenges, some PGD measures have introduced cut-off scores. However, these measures were either not developed in alignment with ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR definitions of PGD (Treml et al., 2024) or were validated without gold standard clinical references such as clinician-administered interviews (e.g. Lenferink et al., 2024). While cut-off scores can enhance efficiency for both researchers and clinicians to identify probable cases of PGD, they risk overestimating prevalence rates and increasing false positives (Stroebe et al., 2024), partly because they fail to capture the nuance of diagnostic criteria. For example, a PGD diagnosis requires the presence of core symptoms (longing and preoccupation), which a cut-off score may fail to capture if the total score reaches the cut-off based solely on associated symptoms. This limitation is particularly concerning given that PGD has been the subject of considerable criticism for potentially pathologizing normative grief responses (e.g. Cacciatore & Frances, 2022). Therefore, an alternative approach is needed – one that recognises the diagnostic nuances of PGD while maintaining the utility of cut-off scores – to improve clinical efficiency, estimate population prevalence, track symptom progression, and identify risk and protective factors (O'Connor et al., 2020).

In the present study, this will take the form of a novel two-step scoring method where the presence of core PGD symptoms is first established, followed by a cut-off score on associated symptoms. Determining the presence of core symptoms is relatively straightforward through the application of a threshold for symptom endorsement; however, there remains a lack of consensus regarding what constitutes symptom endorsement. The A-PGDs was originally designed such that item endorsement was considered as a score of ≥4 (‘very much’) on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (e.g. Redican et al., 2024). Nevertheless, it has been argued that PGD should be evaluated using the same criteria as other stress-related disorders, such as posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), complex PTSD, and adjustment disorder, where item endorsement is typically regarded as the midpoint of a five-point Likert scale (Hyland et al., 2024). Moreover, establishing a cut-off score for the associated symptoms requires the application of diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) analysis, which evaluates a test’s ability to reliably distinguish between individuals with and without clinically significant levels of symptoms (Leeflang et al., 2009). DTA analysis is typically based on the analysis of a test’s sensitivity (the proportion of those with the target condition who have a positive test result), specificity (the proportion of those without the target condition who have a negative test result), and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Campbell et al., 2015). To identify a cut-off score for the associated symptoms that strikes a balance between accurately identifying those who have clinically meaningful symptoms and those that do not, the results of an index test must be compared with those of a gold-standard clinical measure (Leeflang et al., 2009).

While acknowledging the lack of consensus surrounding what should be considered a gold standard measure within the psychiatric field (Hyland & Shevlin, 2024), structured clinical interviews are generally considered the de facto method for identifying the presence of a psychological disorder (O'Connor et al., 2020). Determining a clinically significant cut-off score for the A-PGDs was hampered since, until recently, there was no formal clinical interview for PGD diagnosis which mapped directly onto the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR descriptions of PGD. However, the recent development of the Aarhus Structured Clinical Interview for Prolonged Grief Disorder in ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR (A-PGDi; O’Connor et al., 2025) now makes this possible. The A-PGDi is the first structured clinical interview that can be used to formally diagnose PGD in accordance with the diagnostic requirements of the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR and has been shown to demonstrate good validity and reliability (O’Connor et al., 2025).

The aim of this study was to determine a scoring procedure for identifying probable ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD cases using the A-PGDs. Specifically, we sought to first identify an optimal item threshold on the core symptoms that would capture potential cases at least as well as the clinical interview and then apply ROC analyses to the ten associated symptoms relevant to ICD-11 PGD and those relevant to DSM-5-TR PGD. In keeping with the notion that the A-PGDs functions as a screening tool designed to identify individuals who may require a more comprehensive clinical evaluation, we aimed to determine an optimal threshold on the core symptoms that would capture potential PGD cases at least as well as the clinical interview and identify a cut-off score that achieved satisfactory sensitivity and specificity, with a greater focus on sensitivity than specificity.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were bereaved people recruited through Facebook groups and non-profit organizations e.g. bereavement support groups, churches, and the Danish Cancer Organization, as well as a pool of participants from previous studies at the Unit for Bereavement Research at Aarhus University. Participants had difficulties coping with their grief for a minimum of six months after the death of a loved one and experienced a negative influence on their daily life and/or functioning. Functional impairment was not formally assessed as a criterion for study entry. The study's inclusion criteria included fulfilling the aforementioned description, being at least 18 years old, having experienced a close relative's death at least six months prior, and having adequate Danish to successfully complete the interview. Being unable to engage in the interview, because of a cognitive disability or low level of literacy, was an exclusion criterion. Ethical approval for the current study was obtained from the local ethics committee at Aarhus University (ID:2021-0231525; Approval number: 2021-39). The final analytic sample comprised of 124 bereaved adults. Because one participant reported a bereavement below the six-month requirement for inclusion and another reported an invalid time since loss (>150 years), these cases were deleted. This resulted in a final sample of 122 participants, with a mean age of 43.36 years (SD = 17.12, Range = 19–83 years). Most of the sample were female (85.2%; n = 104). More than a third of the sample had lost a parent (38.5%; n = 47), 31.5% (n = 37) had lost a partner, 18.9% (n = 23) lost a child, 9.0% (n = 11) lost a sibling, and 2.4% (n = 3) reported other types of losses. Almost half of the sample lost a loved one from cancer (41.8%; n = 51), 17.2% (n = 21) lost a loved one from suicide, 10.7% (n = 13) from other illness, 17.3% (n = 21) from other/unknown causes (due to some categories having low responses, we have coded these as other to avoid risk of disclosure), 9.0% (n = 11) from accident, and 4.1% (n = 5) from cardiovascular disease. The average months since loss for the sample was 56.09 (SD = 74.51, Range = 7–588.0).

Measures

Aarhus Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale (A-PGDs; O'Connor et al., 2023): The A-PGDs is a sixteen-item scale which assesses for symptoms of PGD as described in both the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR. Two items assess the core symptoms of PGD (i.e. ‘longed for ______ during the past month?’ ‘Preoccupied with thoughts of _____ even when you did not want to be thinking about them?’), while the remaining items assess associated symptoms (see Table 1 for associated symptoms applicable to the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR descriptions). Participants rate symptom intensity using a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Not at all’ (1) to ‘Overwhelmingly’ (5). Moreover, there is one item which measures functional impairment, and two items that measure the extent to which participants have had concerns regarding their grief reaction, one measuring concerns regarding their own grief reaction as compared to others surrounding them or their own perceptions of what constitutes ‘normal' grief and the other measuring whether acquaintances have expressed concerns. A final item also assesses whether the grief symptoms have been present for at least six months (ICD-11 only). These questions include response options of ‘Yes’ (1) or ‘No' (0). For the cultural deviation items, if a respondent selects ‘yes' for either of the two questions, cultural deviance is positively endorsed. Symptom severity for the associated symptoms was computed by summing responses to the relevant symptoms for ICD-11 PGD (i.e. items 3-12) and DSM-5-TR PGD (i.e. items 3, 5-6, 9, 11-16). There were no missingness for these items. Cronbach’s alpha estimates indicated excellent internal consistency for the ICD-11 items (α = .91) and the DSM-5 items (α = .91).

The Aarhus Structured Clinical Interview for Prolonged Grief (A-PGDi; O’Connor et al., 2025): The A-PGDi is a structured clinical interview designed to facilitate the diagnostic assessment of PGD in accordance with the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR diagnostic requirements. The A-PGDi was developed by clinicians, researchers, and bereaved people, and a detailed description of development of the A-PGDi is contained within the initial validation study (O’Connor et al., 2025). Briefly, interviewers were clinicians and master student of psychology experienced in working clinically with bereaved people. They were trained by experienced experts in structured clinical interviews. The training involved an extensive full day of supervised roleplays focused on the administration of the A-PGDi. Pilot interviews were performed by the interviewers and closely supervised in group supervisions. Following completion of the training, interviewers began conducting structured clinical interviews using the A-PGDi and associated measures, all while attending monthly group supervision on video recordings of interviews with the experienced experts. Prior to commencement of the interview, the interviewer provided participants with a brief explanation of the interview's format, including that for each question, the interviewer will inquire until they are able to evaluate if a symptom is present or not, and if so, inquire the participant regarding the intensity of the symptoms. For the first part of the interview, participants were asked to indicate whether a loved one had died and their relationship to the deceased (Criterion A) and the number of months since the loss occurred (Criterion B). Participants were required to indicate ‘yes’ to these items to be able to move onto the next part of the interview. Next, participants were interviewed to clarify the presence of the core symptoms of PGD (Criterion C). The interviewer indicated a ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘9’ (not possible to evaluate) for the presence of each symptom. If a symptom was endorsed the severity using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘to a very high extent’) was evaluated. If the participant positively endorsed at least one Criterion C symptom, the interview continued by the same procedure to evaluate endorsement of the 14 symptoms measuring associated emotional symptoms (Criterion D), some of which were exclusive to ICD-11 (D2, D3, D6, D8) and some of which were exclusive to DSM-5 (D11, D12, D13, D14). The response format for the Criterion D items was the same as for Criterion C. If at least one item from Criterion D was positively endorsed, the interviewer moved onto Criterion E (functional impairment), Criterion F (cultural deviance), and Criterion G (duration of symptoms; ICD-11 only). These items were all evaluated by the interviewer using ‘yes’ or ‘no’ responses. The interviewer then used the scoring sheet (see Appendix) to determine whether their client met criteria for ICD-11 PGD and DSM-5 PGD. See O’Connor et al (2025) for detailed description of A-PGDi and free training materials.

The Center for the Epidemiological Studies of Depression Short Form (CES-D; Andresen et al., 1994): The CES-D is a ten-item self-report scale used to assess depression. Participants rate symptom frequency during the past week using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Rarely or none of the time) to 3 (All of the time). The recommended cut-off point of ≥10 was used to identify possible depression cases. In the present study, the internal reliability of the CES-D was adequate (α = .71).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006): The GAD-7 is a seven-item self-report scale used to assess GAD as described in the fourth edition of the DSM (DSM-IV; APA, 1994). Participants rate symptom frequency during the past two weeks using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). The recommended cut-off point of ≥10 was used to identify possible GAD cases; this cut-off point has been shown to have good sensitivity (0.89) and specificity (0.82) (Spitzer et al., 2006). In the present study, the internal reliability of the GAD-7 was excellent (α = .87).

Analytic plan

Analyses were conducted in four phases. In the first phase, we report descriptive statistics for the A-PGDs items and the endorsement rates of the A-PGDs items. McNemar’s tests were used to assess whether observed differences in marginal endorsement rates between the clinical interview and self-report thresholds were statistically significant. McNemar’s tests were reported as asymptotic Z statistics with two-sided p-values. In keeping with the screening purpose of the A-PGDs, thresholds that did not result in significantly lower endorsement rates than the clinical interview were prioritised for Phase 2 analyses. Chi-square tests of association were used to examine whether there were statistically significant differences in endorsement of the core symptom items based on those differing thresholds compared to the A-PGDi. The item threshold that yielded significantly higher endorsement rates than the clinical interview, as indicated by McNemar’s test, was selected for Phase 2 analyses. In the second phase, a number of preparatory steps were adopted to ensure the adequacy of the data for DTA. Specifically, logistic regression analysis was used to determine whether total ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR scores were associated with diagnostic status using the A-PGDi. Next, the DTA for each cut-off score on the A-PGDs associated symptoms (see #Tables A1 and A2 for associated symptoms relevant to ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR formulations of PGD) was examined, using the A-PGDi as the gold standard. These analyses were conducted using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis for only those participants who met the core symptoms requirement. Area under the curve (AUC), a metric for a diagnostic test's accuracy, was one of the many factors taken into consideration while choosing the best cut-off point. The AUC must be larger than 0.5 and, preferably, greater than 0.8 for the diagnostic tool to be meaningful (Nahm, 2022; Zhu et al., 2010). Moreover, Youden's J (Youden, 1950) statistic was considered when selecting the optimal cutoff point. Youden's J values, which range from – 1 to 1, represent the diagnostic test's discriminatory ability. Youden's J is computed using the formula J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1, where Sensitivity (True Positive Rate) and Specificity (True Negative Rate) are defined as the proportions of accurate positive and negative identifications, respectively. The ideal threshold is usually identified when J reaches its peak value. Nevertheless, we considered the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, together with informed judgement, when determining the final cut-off score.

In the next phase sensitivity and specificity were calculated for the overall two-step method. Participants who failed to meet Step 1 criteria were classified as screening negative and did not proceed to Step 2. Sensitivity and specificity of the two-step algorithm were then computed by cross-tabulating the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR classifications on the A-PGDi with the corresponding ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR cut-off scores. Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of true positives (participants meeting criteria on both the A-PGDi and the two-step classification) divided by the sum of true positives and false negatives (meeting A-PGDi criteria but not identified by the two-step procedure). Specificity was defined as the proportion of true negatives (participants not meeting criteria on either the A-PGDi or the two-step classification) divided by the sum of true negatives and false positives (not meeting A-PGDi criteria but identified by the two-step procedure).

In the final phase, the level of agreement between meeting caseness for probable ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD according to the APGDs based on the two-step method (i.e. meeting the core symptom requirement, exceeding the cut-off score, and meeting time criterion – 6 months for ICD-11 and 12 months since the loss for DSM-5-TR) and the clinical interview were examined using the kappa statistic (Cohen, 1960). Kappa values were interpreted using conventional guidelines, with values ≤ 0 indicating no agreement, 0.01–0.20 slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement, and 0.81–1.00 almost perfect agreement. The same analyses were also conducted to examine the association between satisfying caseness for probable ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD based on the cut-off score (i.e. core symptoms along with cut-off score and time criterion) and satisfying caseness for probable anxiety and depression. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Version 28 (IBM Corp, 2021), and the code for these analyses is available in Supplementary Materials.

Results

Phase one

Mean item and scale scores for the A-PGDs for the total sample are presented in Table 1. The observed range of scores on the A-PGDs based on the ICD-11 description was 12 to 54, with a mean score of 30.28 (SD = 10.52, Median = 30.00). The observed range of scores on the A-PGDs based on the DSM-5 description was 12 to 54, with a mean score of 31.30 (SD = 10.58, Median = 32.00). Moreover, when considering the associated emotional symptoms only, the observed range of associated emotional symptoms scores on the A-PGDs based on the ICD-11 description was 10 to 44, with a mean score of 23.70 (SD = 8.75, Median = 23.50 across 10 symptoms). The observed range of associated emotional symptom scores on the A-PGDs based on the DSM-5-TR description was 10 to 44, with a mean score of 24.72 (SD = 8.85, Median = 25.00 across 10 symptoms). The most commonly endorsed items were item 1 ‘Have you longed for ____ during the past month’ (M = 3.62, SD = 1.10; ICD-11 & DSM-5-TR), item 3 ‘Have you had feelings of sadness or sorrow during the past month’ (M = 3.11, SD = 1.20; ICD-11 & DSM-5-TR) and item 9 ‘During the past month, have you felt that you have lost a part of yourself’ (M = 3.07, SD = 1.37; ICD-11 & DSM-5-TR). As demonstrated in Table 2, McNemar’s tests indicated statistically significant differences in endorsement rates between the longing item including in the clinical interview and the self-report threshold at both ≥3 and ≥4. In contrast, no statistically significant differences in endorsement rates were observed for the preoccupation item at either threshold. Despite the absence of significant differences for preoccupation item, the threshold of ≥3 was deemed most appropriate for both core symptom items due to producing higher endorsement rates, thereby possessing greater sensitivity.

For the total sample, 20.5% (n = 25; 95% CI: 13.0%, 28.0%) met diagnostic requirements for ICD-11 PGD using the clinical interview and 22.1% (n = 27; 95% CI: 15.0%, 30.0%) of the sample met diagnostic requirements for DSM-5-TR PGD using the clinical interview. Phase two

Any participants who did not meet the Step 1 core symptoms requirement (n = 23) were excluded from the following analyses. Using diagnostic status derived from the clinical interview to determine the optimal cut-off score based on the ten associated symptoms relevant to ICD-11 PGD using the A-PGDs provided a satisfactory accuracy (AUC = .76; 95% CI = .65, .88). A score of ≥ 27 on associated symptoms was determined to be the optimal cut-off, with 80% of cases being correctly identified as having ICD-11 PGD and 39% being incorrectly identified as having ICD-11 PGD (J = 0.45). Similarly, using diagnostic status derived from the clinical interview to determine the optimal cut-off score based on the ten associated symptoms relevant to DSM-5-TR PGD using the A-PGDs provided a satisfactory accuracy (AUC = .75; 95% CI = .65, .86). A score of ≥29 on associated symptoms was determined to be the optimal cut-off point, with 81.0% of cases being correctly identified as having DSM-5-TR PGD and 34% being incorrectly identified as having DSM-5-TR PGD (J = 0.47). For the overall two-step procedure, the sensitivity was 80% and 78% for ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD, respectively. Moreover, the specificity was 70% and 74% for ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD, respectively (Tables 3 and 4).

Phase three

Of the total sample, all of whom were bereaved at least six months (ICD-11 requirement), the majority (86.9%; n = 106) were bereaved twelve months or longer (DSM-5-TR requirement). When applying the time criterion alongside the two-step method for the A-PGDS, the prevalence rate of probable ICD-11 PGD was 34.4% (n = 42; 95% CI: 25.9%, 43.0%) and probable DSM-5-TR PGD was 36.0% (n = 36; 95% CI: 21.3%, 37.7%). There was moderate agreement between diagnostic status using the A-PGDi and the two-step method using the APGDs for ICD-11 PGD (K = .42; 95% CI: 0.40, 0.29) and DSM-5-TR PGD (K = .49; 95% CI: 0.13, 0.48).

There was also a significant association between meeting diagnostic requirements for APGDs ICD-11 PGD using the two-step method and having clinically significant symptoms of anxiety, (K = .35; 95% CI = 0.40, 0.71) and depression (K = .41; 95% CI = 0.28, 0.54). Similarly, there was a significant association between meeting diagnostic requirements using the APGDs for DSM-5-TR PGD using the two-step method and having clinically significant symptoms of anxiety (K = .35; 95% CI = 0.18, 0.52) and depression (K = .37; 95% CI = 0.23, 0.51).

Discussion

This present study aimed to identify a way of scoring the Aarhus Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale-self-report (APGDs) that may be used to identify potential ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD cases. To accomplish this, we opted for a two-step method: (1) endorsement of one core symptom (yearning or preoccupation), and (2) a cut-off for associated symptoms across ICD-11 and DSM-5. A score of ≥3 on the core symptom items was deemed the optimal threshold for symptom endorsement. Among those endorsing the core symptoms, a cut-off score of 27 or higher on the ten associated symptoms in ICD-11 provided a sensitivity and specificity score of 80% and 61%, respectively. Moreover, a cut-off score of 29 or higher on the ten associated symptoms in DSM-5-TR provided sensitivity and specificity of 81% and 66%, respectively. For the overall two-step method, sensitivity was 80% and 78% for ICD-11 PGD and DSM-5-TR PGD, respectively, and specificity was 70% and 74% for ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD, respectively. The slightly lower specificity scores imply that the cutoff's capability to recognize non-PGD cases is not as strong as its ability to accurately detect probable PGD cases. Nevertheless, we consider this an optimal balance as the purpose of the cut-off is to identify participants that would benefit from further clinical assessment for PGD. Hence, the chosen cut-off demonstrates acceptable levels of accuracy and may be used by both researchers and clinicians to identify potential ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD cases of clinical relevance.

The choice of threshold for indicating symptom endorsement on the core symptoms item was informed by our findings, which showed that endorsement rates based on scores ≥3 were lower than those obtained using the clinical interview, whereas rates based on ≥4 were notably higher. Because the lower threshold (i.e. scores ≥3) aligns with the criteria used to indicate probable caseness in other stress-related self-report measures such as the International Trauma Questionnaire (Cloitre et al., 2018) and the International Grief Questionnaire (IGQ; Hyland et al., 2024), and reflects the screening aim of identifying individuals who may benefit from further clinical assessment, it will serve as the new benchmark for symptom endorsement on the A-PGDs. With respect to determining the most appropriate cut-off score for the associated symptoms, several alternatives were considered. Ultimately, the selected cut-off score of 27 for the ten associated symptoms relevant to ICD-11 PGD and the cut-off score of 29 for the ten associated symptoms relevant to DSM-5-TR PGD were chosen because, following the initial core symptom step (which was designed to avoid misclassifying individuals who do not exhibit the disorder’s distinguishing characteristics), it was important to preserve the screening nature of the measure – that is, minimize the risk of overlooking potentially relevant cases. The selected cut-offs for ICD-11 PGD and DSM-5-TR PGD represented one of the lower thresholds that demonstrated acceptable discriminatory ability and achieved satisfactory sensitivity and specificity, with the former slightly higher. Although having a comparable cut-off for PGD across the two diagnostic systems would be ideal, the differing constellations of associated symptoms specified by each system – together with variation in endorsement patterns across these symptoms – likely necessitated different thresholds to achieve acceptable sensitivity and specificity.

The sensitivity and the specificity of psychological measures are influenced by numerous factors, including the prevalence of the target condition and the characteristics of the sample (Trevethan, 2017). In the present study, although the sensitivity and specificity values for the associated symptom scores and the overall two-step method were satisfactory, they may be considered modest. The relatively high prevalence of PGD observed in this sample, as well as the nature of the sample itself, may partly account for this finding. Based on estimates by Bujang and Adnan (2016), a disorder with a prevalence of approximately 20% – such as PGD in the present study – would require a sample of around 225–250 participants to detect good diagnostic accuracy with adequate power. As the present sample was considerably smaller than these recommendations, the resulting estimates of sensitivity and specificity may be less precise and more unstable. Further research with larger samples is needed to replicate these findings and obtain more precise estimates of diagnostic accuracy. Nevertheless, the lower specificity is not necessarily problematic for screening tools, which primarily serve as a foundation for clinicians to estimate the intensity of a clients PGD symptoms and determine whether a full clinical examination is required.

Results showed that 34.4% of the sample fulfilled probable caseness for ICD-11 PGD and 36.0% met probable caseness for DSM-5-TR PGD using the two-step method for the A-PGDs. These rates exceeded those derived from the A-PGDi, where 21% and 21.8% could be diagnosed with ICD-11 PGD and DSM-5-TR PGD, respectively (O’Connor et al., 2025), and also those reported in other studies using diagnostic algorithms where the average prevalence of probable PGD has ranged from 5% to 16% (Comtesse et al., 2024). As previously highlighted, cut-off scores can overestimate prevalence rates and increase the proportion of false positives (Stroebe et al., 2024), which may partly explain the higher rates in the present study. Alternatively, the characteristics of the sample may have contributed to this finding. Specifically, participants were bereaved individuals who self-identified as having difficulties in coping with their grief, and a substantial proportion of these participants reported the death of a child, the death of a partner, and the loss of a loved one from suicide, all of which are recognised risk factors for PGD (Boelens & Lenferink, 2022; Buur et al., 2024; Doering et al., 2022). Thus, further research will be required to determine the prevalence of probable PGD using this two-step approach in more representative bereaved samples. It should be noted that the two-step method does not include assessment of functional impairment or cultural deviance as we judged these to be better suited for a full clinical assessment due to challenges in reliable estimates (Redican et al., 2024).

Limitations and restraints on generality

This study has several limitations. First, as previously highlighted, the relatively small sample size may have affected the reliability and precision of the estimates of diagnostic accuracy (e.g. sensitivity, specificity, AUC) (Bujang & Adnan, 2016). Second, evidence suggests that variations in sensitivity and specificity with disease prevalence may be caused by clinical processes and deficiencies in the design and implementation of research (Leeflang et al., 2009). Therefore, the self-selection of participants, along with the majority being female and having experienced the death of a child or partner and a suicide death, may have impacted the sensitivity and specificity of the two-step method in the present study. Third, Youden’s J values were somewhat lower, however, this is not inherently problematic as Youden's index generally presumes that sensitivity and specificity have equal weight, and thus, the specificity can be compromised when sensitivity is favoured (Gabriel et al., 2021). Fourth, data from the present study are not publicly available due to ethical reasons, however, the syntax used to generate the results is provided in Supplementary Materials. Finally, since the present study focuses on establishing an alternative scoring procedure for the A-PGDs, it is important to acknowledge limitations of self-report measures. Structured clinical interviews and judgement are fundamental for achieving a more accurate diagnosis and a more holistic understanding of the bereaved person’s experience (Stroebe et al., 2024). Consequently, it is likely that the prevalence rates produced by the A-PGDs and other self-report scales represent an overestimation of true prevalence.

Conclusion

Overall, the current study has identified that along with meeting core symptoms requirements, a cut-off score of ≥27 on the ten associated symptoms for ICD-11 PGD and a cut-off score of ≥29 on the ten associated symptoms for DSM-5-TR PGD on the APGDs can be used to identify people with probable PGD. While the two-step method is not intended to replace diagnostic algorithms, if offers a practical alternative given that algorithms are often complex and time consuming to apply.
