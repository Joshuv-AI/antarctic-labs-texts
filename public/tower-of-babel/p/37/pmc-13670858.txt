Knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of organ donation among medical university students in Armenia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Daniel Panossian, Rawad Affan, Georges Jarrouge, Sahag Panossian, Shafika Assaad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrgan donation is essential for patients with end-stage organ failure, yet transplantation systems remain limited by organ shortages, public awareness, ethical concerns, and trust in healthcare systems.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of organ donation among medical university students in Armenia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 700 medical university students in Armenia during the 2025–2026 academic year. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire covering demographics, knowledge, attitudes and beliefs, perceptions, and donation-related practices. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27 and presented as frequencies, percentages, and mean ± standard deviation.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 21.55 ± 2.279 years, and 66.9% were female. Most students had heard about organ donation in Armenia (76.9%) and knew that transplantation is performed in the country (79.0%). Although 89.9% knew about brain death, only 64.4% considered it irreversible death. Knowledge of legal and organizational aspects was limited: 32.3% knew that organ donation after brain death is legal, 29.4% knew about the organ donation registry, and 19.1% knew where or how donation is performed. Only 1.7% had an organ donation card, while 58.1% agreed to donate their organs after death.ConclusionMedical university students in Armenia generally show positive perceptions toward organ donation; however, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding legal and organizational aspects, donor registration remains exceptionally low. Targeted education, public awareness, and transparent communication are needed.

Introduction

Organ transplantation is an established therapy for end-stage organ failure and, for many patients, the best or only life-saving treatment available [1]. Despite major advances in transplantation medicine, the supply of transplantable organs remains far below demand. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that, in 2022, more than 150,000 solid organ transplants were performed worldwide, representing no more than 10% of global need [2]. Access to transplantation also remains highly unequal across countries because of differences in legislation, governance, financing, infrastructure, workforce capacity, and public awareness [1].

Because of this persistent gap, organ donation must be understood not only as a clinical issue, but also as an ethical, legal, and social one. The current WHO policy framework urges Member States to integrate donation and transplantation into health-care systems, protect living donors, strengthen governance and regulatory oversight, encourage donation as an altruistic, voluntary, and non-remunerated act, and expand deceased donation where appropriate [3]. Public understanding, trust in the health system, and awareness of donation procedures are therefore essential for the development of effective and ethical transplantation systems [1, 3].

Medical university students constitute a particularly important population in this context. As future physicians, they will be involved in identifying potential donors, discussing organ donation with patients and families, and shaping public attitudes toward donation through their professional practice [4, 5]. International evidence suggests that medical students are often favorably disposed toward organ donation, but important knowledge gaps and sociocultural differences remain. A meta-analysis of 57 studies found that 69.2% of medical students were willing to donate their organs after death, with substantial variation across geographic and cultural settings [4]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Hong Kong found that 99.5% of medical students expressed a positive attitude toward organ donation, yet only 28.1% had formally registered as organ donors, highlighting a gap between positive attitudes and actual commitment [5].

A worldwide survey of 1,541 medical students from 104 countries found that 94.4% supported organ donation, yet only 12.4% had already consented to donate, highlighting a substantial gap between general support and actual commitment. The same study also showed that prior education was associated with better knowledge, higher self-donation rates, and more positive attitudes [6]. Similar findings have been reported in Europe. In Poland, 86.6% of medical students declared willingness to donate their organs after death, but only 31.71% possessed an organ donor card; place of residence and religion significantly influenced donation attitudes [7].

This issue is especially relevant in Armenia. Armenia’s legal framework for transplantation, first adopted in 2002, defines brain death as equivalent to death, prohibits commercialization of organs and tissues, establishes a donor-recipient registry, and permits organ retrieval from deceased individuals in the absence of a registered refusal, subject to consent from relatives in a legally defined order [8]. The law also requires the competent authority to provide the population with information about transplantation, donation conditions, and related issues [8].

However, transplant activity in Armenia remains largely dependent on living donation. According to the International Registry in Organ Donation and Transplantation (IRODaT), Armenia reported no deceased donors in either 2023 or 2024. In 2023, the country recorded 26 living kidney transplants and 5 living liver transplants, while in 2024 it recorded 23 living kidney transplants [9, 10].

Available Armenian evidence indicates that the challenge is not merely legislative. A qualitative study conducted in Armenia identified lack of knowledge about brain death, deep mistrust toward the health-care system, the influential role of the donor’s family in decision-making, and misinterpretation of religious positions as major barriers to acceptance of donation after death [11]. These findings underline the need for targeted educational assessment among future health professionals, particularly medical university students, whose knowledge and attitudes may influence both clinical practice and broader community perceptions [4, 5, 11].

Knowledge on organ donation among students in Armenia remains an understudied domain. Therefore, to fill this gap, this cross-sectional study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of organ donation among medical university students in Armenia. Specifically, the study objectives are to: (1) evaluate students' awareness of organ donation and transplantation practices in Armenia; (2) assess their understanding of brain death and the legal framework governing organ donation; (3) examine attitudes, beliefs, and perceptions toward donation; and (4) identify gaps and inform targeted educational strategies and long-term policy efforts to strengthen ethically grounded organ donation and transplantation in Armenia.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This is a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study conducted among medical university students in Armenia in the 2025–2026 academic year.

Participants were medical students who were currently enrolled during the study period and provided informed consent prior to questionnaire completion. The online questionnaire was designed with required-response fields, meaning participants could not proceed without completing all items, and duplicate submissions were not feasible due to the platform settings; therefore, incomplete or duplicate responses were not encountered.

A total of 700 participants were included in the study. Participants were recruited through institutional email, student groups, and social media platforms. A convenience sample of eligible students who agreed to participate during the study period was used.

Study instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire prepared for this study. The instrument began with a participant information and consent section explaining the study purpose, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality of responses, non-collection of names, and the right to withdraw.

The questionnaire consisted of demographic items and four substantive domains. Demographic variables included age, gender, and year of study. Section A assessed knowledge using 12 items on awareness of organ donation and transplantation in Armenia, knowledge of brain death, the legal status of organ donation after brain death, awareness of the organ donation registry, and perceived limitations to transplantation.

Section B assessed attitudes and beliefs using 8 items addressing previous donation, future willingness to donate, preferred recipients, ethical views on donation for money, willingness to encourage family or friends to donate after brain death, perceived religious support, trust in the Armenian healthcare system, and the inclusion of organ donation in the educational curriculum. This section also included follow-up multiple-response items exploring reasons for willingness or unwillingness to donate.

Section C assessed perceptions using 12 statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. These items explored humanitarian values, social acceptance, taboo, religious support, public knowledge, trust in physicians, promotion of donation, family approval, trust in donor registration, recipient preference, healthcare image, and emotional discomfort.

Section D assessed practice and intentions using 6 items on possession of an organ donation card, willingness to sign one, prior family discussion, willingness to accept a deceased-donor transplant, willingness to donate organs after death, and suggested strategies to increase organ donation in Armenia.

The questionnaire was adapted from previously validated surveys on organ donation conducted among university students and healthcare professionals, and the english version of the questionnaire was used. Following a comprehensive review of these studies, relevant items were selected and modified to fit the armenian context [12–15]. The original questionnaires demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with reported Cronbach's alpha coefficients exceeding the accepted threshold of 0.70, supporting the reliability of the instrument.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median depending on data distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Yerevan State Medical University after Mkhitar Heratsi under approval number N 8–7/26. The research followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [16]. Prior to questionnaire completion, participants were presented with a detailed online participant information sheet explaining the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any time without penalty or loss of benefits, the confidentiality of their responses, the anonymous nature of data collection, and the exclusive use of data for research purposes. Informed consent was obtained electronically, whereby participants were required to actively confirm their agreement before proceeding to the questionnaire. Access to the data was restricted to the research team, and may be requested upon reasonable request.

Attitudes and beliefs toward organ donation

Regarding donation preferences, more than half of participants preferred donating their organs to family members (383, 54.7%). In terms of ethical views, 420 participants (60.0%) considered donating an organ for money to be ethical.

Participants showed mixed views regarding family encouragement, religion, and trust in the healthcare system. A total of 322 participants (46.0%) would encourage family or friends to donate after brain death. Only 124 participants (17.7%) believed that religion in Armenia encourages organ donation. Trust in the Armenian healthcare system to manage organ donation and transplantation fairly was also limited, with 191 respondents (27.3%) expressing trust, compared with 234 (33.4%) who did not. Finally, most participants (483, 69.0%) believed that organ donation and transplantation should be included in the school or university curriculum.

High neutral responses were seen for whether religion supports organ donation (61.6%), whether signing an organ donation card is trustworthy and safe (65.7%), and whether organ donation is respected by society (48.7%). Discussion

Most Armenian medical students had heard about organ donation in Armenia and knew that transplantation is performed in the country. However, awareness of the legal and organizational aspects was limited. Only 32.3% knew that organ donation after brain death is legal in Armenia, 29.4% knew about the national organ donation registry, and 19.1% knew where or how organ donation is performed. This is important because the current Armenian legal framework regulates transplantation through defined legal procedures, prohibits organ and tissue trade or profit, provides for a donor-recipient registry, and permits procurement from cadaveric donors after confirmation of brain or biological death [8]. Knowledge of brain death was also incomplete. Although 89.9% had heard of brain death, only 64.4% believed that it represents irreversible death, while 23.7% were unsure. Since deceased organ donation depends on public and family understanding of brain death, this uncertainty may represent a major barrier. Recent evidence similarly shows that limited knowledge of brain death and unclear donation procedures can reduce willingness and affect decisions about deceased donation [17, 18]. Previous Armenian research also identified limited understanding of brain death and deep mistrust in the healthcare system as key obstacles to deceased donation [11].

Most medical students participants viewed organ donation as an act of humanity and solidarity, and the main reason for willingness to donate was saving lives. However, willingness did not translate into action. Only 1.7% had an organ donation card, and only 15.9% of those without one were willing to sign one. This attitude and behavior gap is consistent with recent studies among university and health-science students, where positive attitudes were higher than actual donor registration or donor card possession [18, 19]. Additionally, a recent scoping review found that lack of knowledge, religious concerns, fear of body damage, and mistrust in healthcare professionals or the donation process remain frequent reasons for opposition or refusal [17].

Moreover, only 27.3% trusted the Armenian healthcare system to manage organ donation and transplantation fairly, while 33.4% did not. Distrust was also one of the main reasons for unwillingness to donate. This supports previous findings from Armenia showing that mistrust in the healthcare system is a major barrier to deceased organ donation, and it is also consistent with recent international evidence identifying distrust in professionals or the organ donation process as a recurring reason for refusal [11, 17].

Family influence also appears important, as 54.7% of medical students preferred donating their organs to family members. Since family approval may influence donation decisions, especially after death, future awareness efforts should encourage open family discussions about organ donation preferences. Recent evidence shows that family consent remains a decisive factor in deceased organ donation and that uncertainty, emotional distress, unclear knowledge of the deceased person's wishes, and mistrust can contribute to family refusal [17, 20]. Religious uncertainty was also evident, as 56.6% of participants were unsure whether religion in Armenia encourages organ donation. This suggests a need for clearer public messaging, possibly involving religious and community leaders, to address misconceptions and reduce uncertainty. Recent studies continue to show that religious beliefs and interpretation of religious teachings can influence attitudes, intentions, and registration decisions related to organ donation [21–23].

An important ethical concern was that 60.0% of medical students in Armenia considered organ donation for money to be ethical. This may reflect confusion between distinct concepts: legitimate reimbursement of expenses incurred by living donors, incentive-based systems such as funeral benefits or waiting list priority, and outright commercial organ sale for profit. Educational interventions should clearly delineate acceptable forms of donor support from unethical commercialization [3, 8].

The findings highlight the need for structured educational interventions. 69% of participants supported to include organ donation and transplantation in school or university curricula. Public education campaigns, hospital-based education, university campaigns, and media outreach were also identified as key strategies. This is consistent with recent evidence showing that educational interventions, structured communication, and digital or social media approaches among university students can improve knowledge, willingness to donate, family communication, and positive attitudes toward organ donation and transplantation [18, 24, 25].

Comparison with regional and international studies

Comparison of the present findings with studies from other countries provides important context for understanding organ donation attitudes among Armenian medical students. The proportion of participants willing to donate their organs after death in the current study (58.1%) was lower than the pooled global estimate of 69.2% reported in a meta-analysis of medical students from multiple countries by Iniesta-Sepúlveda et al. [4]. This suggests that Armenian medical students may demonstrate lower donation willingness than the international average despite generally positive perceptions toward organ donation [4].

The gap between favorable attitudes and actual donor registration was particularly pronounced in Armenia. Only 1.7% of participants possessed an organ donation card despite 58.1% expressing willingness to donate after death. Similar discrepancies have been reported internationally, although the magnitude observed in Armenia appears substantially greater. In Hong Kong, 99.5% of medical students expressed positive attitudes toward organ donation while 28.1% had formally registered as organ donors [5]. Likewise, a multicenter study in Poland reported that 86.6% of medical students were willing to donate their organs after death and 31.7% possessed donor cards [7]. In Turkey, a research similarly identified positive attitudes toward donation but higher registration-related indicators than those observed in Armenia [26]. These findings suggest that barriers in Armenia may extend beyond personal attitudes and include structural, educational, legal, or trust-related factors affecting the transition from intention to formal donor registration.

Family communication regarding organ donation also appeared considerably lower in Armenia. Only 23.0% of participants reported discussing organ donation with their families. This proportion is lower than rates reported in studies from Turkey, Poland, and Spain, where family discussion and communication regarding donation were substantially more common [7, 26, 27]. This finding is particularly important because Armenian legislation permits organ procurement from deceased donors only within a framework that involves relatives and family consultation. Consequently, limited family discussion may represent a modifiable barrier that could negatively influence donation decisions at the time of death.

The present findings also support observations from international literature that trust in healthcare systems and understanding of brain death remain critical determinants of donation willingness. Similar concerns have been reported in Armenia [11] and Turkey [26]. Collectively, these studies suggest that educational interventions focusing on brain death, legal procedures, donor registration processes, and transparency within healthcare systems may improve donation intentions and actual registration rates.

Strengths and limitations

This study focuses on an underexplored topic in Armenia, assessing knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions toward organ donation among medical university students, and to our knowledge, based on a search of available literature databases, this is the first such study conducted in Armenia among medical university students. The relatively large sample size included medical students from different years of study in Armenia, which strengthens the descriptive value of the findings. In addition, the use of a structured questionnaire adapted from previously validated surveys allowed the study to assess multiple important domains, including awareness of brain death, legal knowledge, trust in the healthcare system, religious perceptions, and willingness to donate.

This study was a convenience-based cross-sectional survey of medical university students in Armenia; therefore, causal relationships cannot be inferred, and the findings may reflect students’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions at a specific point in time. Although the sample size was relatively large, the use of online and group-based recruitment may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the results. Since the study was based on anonymous self-reported responses, the findings may be influenced by recall bias. In addition, comparisons with international studies should be interpreted with caution because differences in study instruments, participant characteristics, cultural contexts, and years of data collection may influence reported estimates.

Conclusion

This study shows that medical university students in Armenia generally view organ donation positively, but important gaps remain in legal knowledge, donor registration, and understanding of donation after brain death. Despite recognizing organ donation as a humanitarian act, few participants had an organ donation card or had discussed donation with their families. Limited trust in the healthcare system, uncertainty about religious views, and lack of practical information may be associated with lower donation willingness.

Targeted education, public awareness campaigns, family discussions, and clearer communication about ethical and legal safeguards are needed to promote informed organ donation decisions in Armenia.
