Risk factors for new vertebral compression fracture after vertebroplasty and efficacy of osteoporosis treatment: A STROBE-compliant retrospective study
Authors: Seongjoo Park, Sang Sik Choi, Heezoo Kim, Sang Yoon Byun, Chung Hun Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Vertebroplasty (VP) effectively treats vertebral compression fractures (VCFs). However, the issue of secondary new VCFs (SNVCFs) after VP is yet to be addressed. Therefore, identification of risk factors for SNVCFs after VP may aid the development of strategies to minimize SNVCF risk. This study aimed to retrospectively evaluate risk factors for SNVCFs after VP, including those associated with the type of anti-osteoporotic treatment administered after VP. Data from 128 patients who underwent single-level VP were collected and reviewed. Patients were divided into 2 groups: those with (n = 28) and without (n = 100) SNVCF within 1 year of VP. We collected the following patient data: age, sex, site of compression fracture, medical history, bone mineral density (BMD), history of long-term steroid use, history of osteoporosis drug use, duration between fracture and VP, VP implementation method (unilateral or bilateral), cement usage in VP, cement leakage into the disc, compression ratio before VP, pre- and postoperative recovery ratio of the lowest vertebral body height, and kyphotic angle of fractured vertebrae. These data were analyzed to identify factors associated with SNVCFs after VP and to investigate the effects of the type of anti-osteoporotic treatment administered for SNVCFs. SNVCFs occurred in 28 patients (21.9%) within 1 year of VP. Logistic regression analysis identified BMD, cement leakage into the disc, and long-term steroid use to be significantly associated with the occurrence of SNVCFs. The group treated with zoledronate after VP had a significantly reduced SNVCF incidence compared with the group treated with calcium (P < .001). In addition, the zoledronate group had a lower SNVCF incidence compared with the groups treated with alendronate (P = .05), selective estrogen receptor modulators (P = .26), or risedronate (P = .22). This study showed that low BMD, presence of an intradiscal cement leak, and long-term steroid use were risk factors for developing SNVCFs following VP. Additionally, among osteoporosis treatments prescribed for VP, zoledronate may be the preferred choice to reduce the risk of SNVCFs.

1. Introduction

As the population ages, the incidence of osteoporosis, the most common metabolic bone disease worldwide, has also increased in recent decades.[1] Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) is considered the most common complication of osteoporosis in the elderly.[2] Reducing mobilization due to OVCF, especially in elderly patients, can lead to various complications such as social isolation, muscle loss, and decreased lung vitality.[3] Because of these characteristics, OVCF-induced pain has been reported to increase morbidity and mortality in elderly patients.[4,5]

Percutaneous vertebroplasty (VP) is a minimally invasive procedure that provides rapid pain relief and stability for OVCFs.[6,7] Therefore, VP is widely used for OVCF treatment, and > 80% of patients who have undergone VP experience immediate and significant pain relief.[6,7]

Although VP has positive effects such as immediate pain relief and rapid functional rehabilitation, adverse effects associated with this procedure have been reported.[8,9] Among these complications, secondary new vertebral compression fractures (SNVCFs) have become particularly concerning, as patients experience a recurrence of severe pain and may be unable to perform normal daily activities again and hence require an additional VP. Recently, several studies have analyzed the risk factors—decreased bone mineral density (BMD), cement leakage into the disc, sagittal imbalance, the presence of an intravertebral cleft, endplate cortical disruption, and the degree of restoration of the vertebral height—associated with SNVCFs after VP.[10–15] However, the results are contradictory and remain inconclusive. Furthermore, whether SNVCFs are caused by VP or by spontaneous progression of osteoporosis is yet to be confirmed.[10]

Among the proposed mechanisms, ongoing osteoporosis plays an important role in inducing SNVCFs.[8,16–18] Therefore, various osteoporosis treatments, such as calcium, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), and bisphosphonates, have been used to increase BMD before and after VP. SNVCFs can also occur following OVCF treatment with balloon kyphoplasty (KP), a procedure in which a balloon is inserted into the fracture and then inflated. Treatment with bisphosphonate zoledronate significantly lowers SNVCF incidence after balloon KP.[19–21] However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has compared the protective effects of different osteoporosis treatments against SNVCFs after VP.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the primary risk factors for SNVCFs after VP and the effects of osteoporosis treatment on SNVCF incidence.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Study design and patient selection

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (approval number: 2020GR0416). This manuscript adheres to the applicable STROBE (S1) checklist [Supplemental Digital Content (STROBE checklist, http://links.lww.com/MD/K739)].

Data were collected from electronic medical records and radiographic images of patients who underwent VP for OVCFs in the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae (T4–L5) at our institution between March 2011 and January 2020.

Patients were selected according to the following inclusion criteria: a pain score of ≥ 4 on a numerical rating scale despite 2 weeks of conservative treatment (including opioids) after an acute or subacute fracture with bone marrow edema confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), diagnosed with MRI-confirmed Kummell disease, underwent a single-level VP, diagnosed with osteoporosis with a BMD ≤ –2.5, and completed a follow-up period of 12 months after VP. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: edema not confirmed by MRI, pathological compression fractures due to tumors, BMD > –2.5, underwent VP at multiple levels owing to multiple fractures, neurological deficits or systemic or localized spinal infections, lost to follow-up within 12 months of VP, and incomplete medical records.

All patients had a continuous epidural catheter placed near the fracture site 1–2 days prior to VP to relieve the pain caused by the fracture and the procedure. In addition, 200,000 IU of vitamin D was administered intramuscularly every 3 months from the day of VP. Treatment for osteoporosis, such as calcium, SERMs, alendronate, risedronate, and zoledronate, was administered to all patients at the initiation of the conservative treatment. VP was performed by a single physician.

2.2. Technique for vertebroplasty

VP was performed according to established methods. The patient was asked to lie on a table in a prone position with a pad placed under the chest or abdomen to correct the patient kyphosis as much as possible. After measuring the vital signs, 8 mL of 0.20% ropivacaine was administered through the epidural catheter. Thereafter, an aseptic dressing was applied to the treatment site, and 1% lidocaine was administered to induce local anesthesia. The fracture level was observed fluoroscopically, and a trocar was inserted and advanced through the pedicle into the fractured vertebral body using a guidewire. After the guidewire was placed inside, a cannula was inserted such that the tip of the cannula was positioned on the lateral side of the posterior third of the vertebral body. Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement was injected after proper placement of the cannula on one or both sides. PMMA was injected until it reached the superior and inferior endplates or the anterior and posterior cortical margins as observed by fluoroscopy. The procedure was terminated immediately if PMMA reached the posterior quarter of the spine or if a major leak was suspected.

2.3. Follow-up and secondary new vertebral compression fractures

Patient follow-up data from the first year after the initial procedure were analyzed. In cases of new-onset severe back pain, physical and radiographic examinations were conducted to evaluate the presence of an SNVCF. Symptomatic SNVCF was diagnosed in the following cases: recurrence of back pain after a period of apparent pain relief post initial VP; evidence of SNVCF with bone marrow edema on MRI at levels higher or lower than previously treated levels; and requirement of additional medication or VP to relieve pain from subsequent fractures. Patients included in this study were divided into 2 groups: those with and without SNVCF within 1 year of VP.

2.4. Collection of data

Possible risk factors that could affect SNVCF were reviewed from multiple angles: age, sex, site of compression fracture (thoracic, T-L junction [T10–L2], lumbar), medical history, history of long-term steroid use (>3 months), type of anti-osteoporotic treatment after VP (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronate, SERMs, or calcium), duration from fracture to VP, BMD, VP implementation (unilateral or bilateral), cement usage in VP, cement leakage into the disc, compression ratio before VP (Fig. 1), preoperative kyphotic angle (KA) (Fig. 2) and postoperative recovery rate of KA, preoperative vertebral body height of the fractured vertebra, and postoperative recovery rate of vertebral body height (Fig. 3). The recovery rates of KA and vertebral body height were expressed as (value after 1 day of VP–baseline value/baseline value) × 100 (%). The types of anti-osteoporotic treatment were based on the medication or injection that the patient received after VP.

The vertebral compression ratio is calculated using the following formula: ([A + C]/2—B)/([A + C]/2). (A) anterior vertebral height of the upper vertebra, (B) anterior vertebral height of the fractured vertebra, and (C) anterior vertebral height of the lower vertebra.

The kyphotic angle is calculated based on the intersection angle of the lines running parallel to the upper and lower endplates of the fractured vertebrae.

The level of spinal deformity is evaluated using the lowest vertebral height before and after surgery to evaluate the recovery of the vertebral height at the same location after surgery. (A and B) Lowest vertebral height and recovery of the vertebral height at the same location after surgery are indicated by arrows.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Among the demographic and clinical variables compared in patients with and without SNVCF within 1 year of VP, categorical and continuous variables were analyzed using Fisher exact test or chi-square test and Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test, respectively.

Significant risk factors were identified using univariate logistic regression analysis by considering each possible risk factor as an independent variable and the occurrence of SNVCF as a dependent variable. These potential risk factors for SNVCF were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression analysis by backward stepwise elimination for risk factors found to be significantly related to SNVCF in the univariate logistic regression analysis (P < .05 was considered significant).

We evaluated whether the selected osteoporosis treatment had a significant effect on SNVCF incidence using Fisher exact test for each treatment option used after VP.

3. Results

This study reviewed the medical records of 216 patients who underwent VP. Among them, 48 patients who received multiple-level VP owing to multilevel OVCFs, 25 patients whose fractures were due to primary cancer or metastasis, 2 patients with no edema in the fractured vertebral body on MRI, 1 patient with a post-traumatic compression fracture and a BMD > –2.5, 4 patients who died from other diseases within the follow-up period, and 8 patients who were lost to follow-up or had missing data were excluded. Therefore, data from 128 patients were included in the analysis. Of these patients, 28 (21.9%) sustained an SNVCF at another spinal level within 12 months (Fig. 4).

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1 [Supplemental Digital Content (Supplemental datasets, http://links.lww.com/MD/K740)]. Significant differences were found in the duration from fracture to VP, site of compression fracture, BMD, cement leakage into the disc, and type of anti-osteoporotic treatment between the no fracture and new fracture groups. The results of univariate logistic regression analysis showed that duration from fracture to VP, BMD, long-term steroid use, and cement leakage into the disc were significantly associated with SNVCF (Table 2). Multivariate logistic regression analysis of the variables identified by the univariate logistic regression showed that long-term steroid use, BMD, and cement leakage into the disc significantly correlated with SNVCF (Table 3). The type of anti-osteoporotic treatment administered after VP did not significantly correlate with SNVCF in the univariate logistic regression analysis. However, in the anti-osteoporotic treatment subgroup analysis (Table 4), the group treated with zoledronate after VP showed a significantly lower SNVCF incidence compared with the calcium group (P < .001). The zoledronate group also had a lower SNVCF frequency compared with the groups treated with alendronate (P = .05), risedronate (P = .22), and SERMs (P = .26).

4. Discussion

This study aimed to determine the risk factors associated with the occurrence of SNVCFs after VP and assess whether SNVCF incidence differed according to the type of anti-osteoporotic treatment used. The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified BMD, cement leakage into the disc, and history of long-term steroid use as risk factors for SNVCF occurrence. In the comparison between different anti-osteoporotic treatments, the zoledronate group showed a significantly lower SNVCF incidence compared with the calcium group and had a lower SNVCF frequency compared with the alendronate, risedronate, and SERM groups.

Several studies have reported the occurrence of SNVCF after VP.[22,23] However, the reported incidence of SNVCF after VP varies from 7.4% to 48%, possibly owing to factors including differences in patient selection criteria, study design, skill of the operator, postoperative treatment, follow-up period, and sample size.[22,23] Lindsay et al[24] reported that a population with initial vertebral compression fracture (VCF) had a 5-fold higher risk of developing additional VCF than a population without VCF. The same study reported a VCF incidence of 19.2% within 1 year of an initial fracture treated conservatively without surgical intervention. Therefore, whether the occurrence of SNVCF after VP is due to osteoporosis progression or surgical factors remains unclear.

These results highlight the need to minimize the risk of further fractures in patients with VCF, irrespective of the treatment method selected for the initial VCF. It is important to identify factors that increase the risk of SNVCF so that this risk can be minimized. In the following sections, we will discuss the potential risk factors for SNVCF as identified in this study.

4.1. Bone mineral density

BMD is an important indicator of bone mass and can reflect the degree of osteoporosis. Previous studies have identified low BMD as a risk factor for SNVCF following VP,10–12 which is consistent with our univariate and multivariate analysis results showing a significant correlation between SNVCF occurrence and low BMD. Low BMD is indicative of a weakened spine, and the vertebrae adjacent to the initial VCF are therefore likely to fail even if VP has not been performed. After VP, rigid cement augmentation increases the disc pressure and inward bulge of the endplates adjacent to the augmented vertebrae. This mechanical change may result in structural failure in patients with osteoporosis, in whom the normal bone remodeling cycle is compromised.[25] Kaufman et al[26] reported that BMD was strongly associated with SNVCF following VP, with a 3% reduction in SNVCF risk for every 1% increase in BMD. Therefore, anti-osteoporotic treatment before and after VP, especially in patients with OVCF, is strongly recommended to reduce SNVCF incidence.[27,28]

4.2. Cement leakage into the disc

When bone cement is injected into the compressed vertebrae during VP, the bone cement may leak into the intradiscal space through the fissure of the fractured spine.[29] Leakage of bone cement into the intervertebral space mechanically irritates the endplates of the vertebral bodies and accelerates disc degeneration, which adds to the stress on adjacent vertebral bodies, increasing the risk of fractures.[18]

Cement leakage into the disc has been identified as a risk factor for SNVCF in several studies[15,18,21]; however, this finding remains controversial. The present study also identified cement leakage into the disc as a risk factor for SNVCF through univariate and multivariate analyses. Therefore, efforts to minimize cement leakage into the disc should be made before and during VP. Hong et al[30] found that endplate cortical disruption and abnormal T2 hyperintensity in adjacent discs, as observed by MRI, are risk factors for cement leakage into the disc. Zhang et al[31] showed that high-viscosity cement injection reduced the incidence of computed tomography-identified cement leakage compared with low-viscosity cement (29.4% vs 68.2%). For severe central collapsed compression fractures, a more lateral placement of the needle tip, to keep it farther away from the fracture site of the median endplate, is also a recommended method of reducing the frequency of cement leakage within the disc.[32]

4.3. Type of anti-osteoporotic treatment

Zoledronate has the greatest potential among the bisphosphonate class of therapeutics. It differs from previous bisphosphonates, as it causes apoptosis of osteoclasts, reduction of osteolysis and bone turnover, and activation of osteoblastic activity, which in turn increases bone mass and replaces existing resorption pits.[33,34] It is also effective in lowering the risk of VCF in men and postmenopausal women,[35] which may account for its use after VP.

Wu et al[36] suggested that perioperative administration of zoledronate improved BMD and pain scores in patients who underwent VP or KP. Hu et al[37] reported increased BMD and reduced refracture incidence in a group receiving zoledronate compared with a control group (1.7% vs 10.7%, respectively). However, there is limited evidence regarding the superiority of bisphosphonates in preventing SNVCF after VP. In our study, the logistic regression analysis did not confirm that the type of anti-osteoporotic treatment is a risk factor for SNVCF after VP. However, comparisons between zoledronate and other osteoporosis treatments showed that the group that received zoledronate after VP had a lower incidence of SNVCF after VP compared with the groups that received alendronate, risedronate, or SERMs and had a significantly reduced frequency of SNVCF compared with the group that received calcium.

In our previous study, the zoledronate group showed a significantly lower SNVCF incidence after KP compared with the risedronate group.[21] This finding, in conjunction with the results of the present study, suggests that zoledronate use after VP may be more effective in reducing SNVCF occurrence compared with other bisphosphonates, calcium, or SERMs.

4.4. History of long-term steroid use

Glucocorticoids alter the cellular composition of bone by affecting the activity of osteoclasts and osteoblasts, resulting in impaired bone formation, increased bone resorption, and bone microarchitecture deterioration.[38] Long-term use of glucocorticoids, for example, by patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or autoimmune diseases, may cause glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis.[39] In the present study, long-term use of steroids was identified as a risk factor for SNVCF by both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. This result is consistent with studies by Hiwatashi and Westesson[40] and Harrop et al,[41] which showed that patients with steroid-induced (secondary) osteoporosis were more likely to develop SNVCFs after VP than patients not receiving steroid therapy.

Anti-absorbents, such as bisphosphonates, are recommended as first-line treatment for glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis in guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology and Royal College of Physicians.[42,43] The results of the present study, in conjunction with these guidelines, indicate that bisphosphonates, particularly zoledronate, should be used for the treatment of osteoporosis in preference to calcium or SERMs, especially in patients receiving long-term steroid treatment.

4.5. Early surgical intervention

Following the initial VCF, movement of the fracture site during activity can cause additional bone loss at the fracture site approaching the endplate over time, which can further reduce the height of the fractured vertebral body.

Delayed surgery increases the risk of cement leakage into the disc. This is because hematoma absorption and fracture line hardening occur during bone healing, which acts as a barrier enabling leakage of cement in the low-pressure direction. In addition, these intravertebral fissures and sclerotic changes can create a barrier between the cement and trabecular bone, hindering their solid integration and, subsequently, an increased SNVCF incidence.[44]

In this study, VP was performed earlier in the non-SNVCF group than in the SNVCF group (22.1 ± 10.4 vs 28.1 ± 10.2 days). The duration between the incidence of fracture and VP was a significant risk factor in the univariate analysis but did not show a significant difference in the multivariate analysis. However, Yang et al[45] suggested that VP within 30 days of the fracture reduced the subsequent development of SNVCFs in the thoracolumbar spine at 1-year follow-up. He et al[46] also showed that the rate of SNVCF was significantly lower in an early-VP group (21 days) compared with that in a late-VP group during a 4–6-year follow-up period. Therefore, VP should be performed not only within 1 month after the fracture but as early as possible.

This study has some limitations. First, data on multi-segmental compression fractures, an important risk factor for SNVCF development, were lacking.[10] However, we considered that the inclusion of these fractures might result in possible bias in the analysis of variables, including cement volume, cement leakage into the disc, and site of compression fracture. Therefore, only data from patients who received a single-level VP were analyzed, and the application of this study is limited to these patients. Second, this was a retrospective, single-center study. Future prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trials are needed to overcome these limitations and provide more reliable clinical data.

5. Conclusion

This study showed that low BMD, long-term steroid use, and cement leakage into the disc are possible risk factors for SNVCF occurrence after single-level VP. Importantly, zoledronate may reduce SNVCF incidence after VP compared with other osteoporotic medications. These data provide evidence for the development of optimal prophylaxis for SNVCFs after VP. To avoid complications, current surgical methods and treatment strategies may need to be adjusted based on patient-specific risk factors, and clinicians should ensure strict medical management of patients during the postoperative period.
