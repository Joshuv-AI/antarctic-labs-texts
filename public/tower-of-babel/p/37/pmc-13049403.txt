Vanishing bone metastasis in pediatric mediastinal lymphoma: A diagnostic pitfall on contrast-enhanced computed tomography
Authors: Bouanane Rania, Belkouchi Lina, Taibi Ouiam, Allali Nazik, Chat Latifa, El haddad Sihame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVanishing bone metastasis (VBM), a reversible pseudopathologic vertebral enhancement related to vertebral venous congestion, is an imaging pitfall that can mimic true osseous metastases in patients with thoracic venous obstruction. We report a rare pediatric case involving a 15-year-old boy with mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma who presented with superior vena cava obstruction. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed multiple vertebral enhancements suggestive of metastases, which were visible exclusively on post-contrast images and completely resolved following recanalization of the thrombosed veins. This case emphasizes the pathophysiologic mechanism of this reversible phenomenon, resulting from vertebral venous congestion, and underlines the importance of recognizing vanishing metastasis to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary oncologic treatment. Awareness of vanishing bone metastasis is essential to prevent misinterpretation as true bone metastases.Keywords: Vanishing bone metastasis (VBM), Vertebral venous plexus, Superior vena cava obstruction, Pseudopathologic enhancement, Lymphoma, Pediatric radiology

Introduction

Vanishing bone metastasis (VBM) is a rare imaging phenomenon that may simulate bone metastases on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT). It occurs when contrast medium refluxes into the vertebral venous plexus secondary to venous obstruction, most commonly of the superior vena cava (SVC) or brachiocephalic veins [1,2]. This venous congestion leads to a transient increase in enhancement within vertebral bodies, creating pseudometastatic images. The condition is benign and reversible once the venous obstruction resolves [3,4].

Although VBM has been described in adults with mediastinal malignancies and SVC thrombosis [5,6], pediatric cases appear to be extremely rare, and, to our knowledge based on available literature, no similar case associated with mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma has been clearly documented. The case presented here highlights this underrecognized radiologic entity and contributes new insights into its pathophysiology, imaging characteristics, and differential diagnosis.

Case description

A previously healthy 15-year-old boy was admitted for treatment of a biopsy-confirmed mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma. Clinical findings included swelling of the face and neck, distended cervical veins, and dyspnea, consistent with SVC syndrome.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated a large anterior mediastinal mass circumferentially encasing the SVC, with intraluminal hypodense material consistent with thrombus within the right brachiocephalic and internal jugular veins (Fig. 1).

Axial contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) images of a 15-year-old male with mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma. (A) Showing a bulky anterior mediastinal mass associated with superior vena cava extrinsic compression (blue arrow). (B) Demonstrates additional thrombosis of the right brachiocephalic vein (green arrow) and prominent collateral venous pathways, including external paravertebral venous plexuses (orange arrow).

In the same acquisition, several small, nodular, and homogeneously enhancing foci were observed along the posterior borders of the vertebral bodies and pedicles from C7 to T5 (Fig. 2). These lesions, sharply marginated and visible on contrast-enhanced images, raised suspicion for osseous metastases or lymphomatous infiltration.

Sagittal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) images of the cervicothoracic spine. (A) Sagittal post-contrast CT image in bone window showing multiple nodular enhancing lesions involving the vertebral bodies from C7 to T5 (arrowheads), corresponding to vertebral pseudolesions related to venous contrast reflux rather than true osseous infiltration. (B) Sagittal maximum intensity projection (MIP) reconstruction demonstrating marked prominence of the azygos–hemiazygos venous system (arrows). Collateral venous pathways converging toward the vertebral venous plexus explain the posterior vertebral body enhancement (arrowheads), consistent with intraosseous venous congestion secondary to superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction, typical of vanishing bone metastasis (VBM).

However, maximum intensity projection reconstructions (Fig. 2) demonstrated that these nodular enhancements aligned with collateral venous pathways converging toward the vertebral venous plexus, supporting a vascular origin. The absence of cortical destruction or soft-tissue extension further argued against true metastases.

No unenhanced CT phase was acquired because the examination was performed as a follow-up study using a pediatric dose-reduction protocol aimed at minimizing radiation exposure. The vertebral findings were incidental and their unexpected appearance contributed to the initial diagnostic uncertainty. Follow-up CT performed three months later using the same protocol showed complete recanalization of the SVC and right brachiocephalic vein, along with total resolution of the vertebral enhancements (Fig. 3). The vertebral bodies appeared entirely normal, confirming the diagnosis of VBM, secondary to vertebral venous congestion related to SVC obstruction.

Contrast-enhanced sagittal computed tomography (CT) angiography images of the cervico-thoracic spine. (A) Sagittal CT angiography in bone window showing nodular enhancement of the vertebral bodies from C7 to D5 (red arrows), representing vertebral pseudolesions caused by venous contrast reflux, associated with enhancement of the external paravertebral venous plexuses (yellow arrows). (B) Follow-up unenhanced CT demonstrating complete disappearance of previous vertebral and paravertebral enhancements, confirming the transient vascular nature of vanishing bone metastasis (VBM).

This chronological evolution illustrates the reversible and benign nature of pseudopathologic vertebral enhancement in the context of thoracic venous obstruction and highlights the importance of correlation with venous anatomy to prevent diagnostic pitfalls.

Discussion

The thoracic venous system drains the upper body via the SVC, brachiocephalic, and internal jugular veins. When venous return is impaired by thrombosis or external compression, as commonly occurs in malignancies, blood is redirected through collateral pathways, notably the vertebral venous plexus (Fig. 4) [7,8]. This valveless network allows bidirectional flow and comprises internal, external, and basivertebral veins, all of which communicate with thoracic and azygos–hemiazygos veins [7]. The absence of valves explains why contrast medium injected intravenously may reflux into these channels under conditions of increased venous pressure [5].

Sagittal view of the vertebral venous plexus, illustrating the anterior and posterior external plexuses, the internal vertebral plexus within the spinal canal, and basivertebral veins connecting the networks through the vertebral bodies.

SVC obstruction causes elevated venous pressure and engorgement of the vertebral venous plexus, particularly within the basivertebral veins that traverse the vertebral bodies. When contrast is injected into the arm ipsilateral to the obstruction, contrast reflux becomes accentuated, resulting in transient vertebral marrow hyperattenuation [5,6].

Kara et al. [2] analyzed nine such cases, all showing vertebral enhancement mimicking metastases in patients with proven SVC thrombosis, whereas Beck and Han [4] reported a similar pattern in a patient with extrinsic venous compression without thrombosis. Anatomically, posterior vertebral body involvement is more frequently observed, likely reflecting the predominance and larger caliber of the anterior internal vertebral venous plexus compared with posterior channels, whereas vertebral pedicle involvement is less commonly described.

The physiologic explanation of VBM is thus a localized venous congestion phenomenon. The refluxed contrast pools within engorged intraosseous venous channels, generating focal or diffuse enhancement within the vertebral body, which disappears once normal flow is re-established [1,6,9]. The pseudolesions are typically located in the lower cervical or upper thoracic spine regions most affected by SVC obstruction, although involvement of other levels may occur depending on collateralization patterns [8].

Imaging features are distinctive: The pseudolesions are well-defined areas of hyperattenuation visible only on post-contrast images, without structural bone change, lysis, or sclerosis. Their shape varies from oval to irregular and may correspond to multiple vertebral levels. Associated findings of venous obstruction, such as enlarged azygos and internal thoracic veins, support the diagnosis [3,7]. In chronic cases, identification of ectatic vessels within or adjacent to the vertebrae increases diagnostic confidence [2].

The main differential diagnosis includes true osseous metastasis, osteoblastic lesions, osteomyelitis, and other infiltrative marrow diseases. However, metastases persist across phases, frequently involve the pedicles asymmetrically, and may display cortical destruction or soft-tissue extension. The reversible nature of VBM, best demonstrated through interval resolution on follow-up imaging, remains the key diagnostic criterion [1,9,10].

MRI may further help by showing normal marrow signal without enhancement or restricted diffusion. In uncertain cases, comparison between enhanced and unenhanced CT acquisitions is useful to differentiate vascular pseudolesions from true neoplastic infiltration [4]. However, in pediatric imaging, unenhanced phases may be omitted to limit radiation exposure, as in our case, highlighting the importance of correlating vertebral enhancement with venous findings such as intraluminal filling defects in the SVC or brachiocephalic veins for accurate interpretation.

To the best of our knowledge and based on currently available literature, pediatric presentations remain exceptionally uncommon, and no similar case associated with mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma has been clearly described. Previous reports have involved adults, usually with bronchogenic carcinoma or mediastinal tumors compressing or thrombosing the SVC [1,2,6]. The pediatric context, combined with an aggressive but curable lymphoma, makes recognition of this benign imaging finding even more critical to prevent overstaging and unnecessary modification of therapy.

In pediatric patients, additional considerations include dose-reduction imaging strategies that may limit acquisition phases and increase diagnostic challenges, as well as a higher risk of overstaging when vertebral enhancement is misinterpreted as marrow involvement in lymphoma. Therefore, recognizing VBM is particularly important in the pediatric oncologic setting to avoid unnecessary treatment escalation.

This phenomenon is often underrecognized, as radiologists may focus on apparent vertebral hyperattenuation without considering venous reflux or the underlying venous component.

As Fukamizu et al. [10] emphasized in their pictorial essay, the correlation between osseous findings and thoracic venous circulation is the cornerstone of diagnosis. Identifying thrombosis or extrinsic venous compression on CT, particularly in patients with mediastinal masses, should prompt a careful reassessment of vertebral images before concluding to metastasis.

Clinically, failure to recognize VBM can lead to significant consequences, including misclassification of disease stage, unjustified biopsies, and unnecessary intensification of chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Awareness of the venous origin of these transient lesions ensures proper management and avoids invasive procedures.

Conclusion

VBM is a benign, reversible manifestation of vertebral venous congestion caused by thoracic venous obstruction, typically involving the SVC or brachiocephalic veins. In our case, a 15-year-old boy with mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma developed striking vertebral enhancements that disappeared after recanalization of thrombosed veins. This observation broadens the existing knowledge base by documenting a rare pediatric presentation. Recognition of its imaging characteristics, correlation with venous findings, and verification of reversibility are essential steps to distinguish pseudopathologic enhancement from true metastasis and prevent overtreatment.
