Does social media addiction disrupt mindful attention of students in health colleges at a university in Saudi Arabia?
Authors: Noor H. AlYousef, Fatimah M. Alhakeem, Kawthar A. AlNasser, Zainab H. AlSinan, Zainab K. Alessa, Fatemah M. AlDawood, Marwa M. Shafey, Wejdan M. Aljohni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Attention, a fundamental cognitive process that is crucial for achieving personal and academic goals, can be significantly impacted by the use of social media. With the rise of platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and WhatsApp, social media addiction (SMA) has become a growing concern, especially among students. Studies show that excessive engagement with social media can impair attention. This study’s aim was to explore the impact of SMA on the mindful attention (MA) of undergraduate students in health colleges.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted in health colleges at a University in Saudi Arabia. An online questionnaire was distributed to 380 undergraduate students at health colleges. MA was assessed by the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) and Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) assessed SMA. Data were analysed using SPSS; categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while mean, standard deviation, and median were used to summarize continuous variables. Pearson correlation was employed to assess the relationship between SMA and MA. Multiple linear regression model employed to identify independent predictors of MAAS scores.RESULTS:A total of 335 students participated in the study; the mean MAAS score was 57.99 (SD= 14.17) and a mean SMA score of 16.07 (SD=4.78). A significant negative correlation was found between SMA and mindfulness (R = -0.519, P < 0.001); the higher SMA was associated with lower MAAS scores. In multiple regression model, higher SMA remained an independent significant predictor of lower MAAS (B = -1.14, P < 0.001, 95% CI: -1.43 to -0.85).CONCLUSION:The study highlights that higher social media use is linked to lower attention. Positive lifestyle factors such as academic satisfaction, social support, good sleep, and diet were associated with better attention. Self-perceived stress, consumption of caffeine, and watching short videos were negatively associated with MA. These findings highlight the need to promote healthy lifestyle habits.

Introduction

Attention is defined by the American Psychological Association as “a state in which cognitive resources are focused on certain aspects of the environment rather than others, and state of readiness of the central nervous system to respond to stimuli.”[1] It is a fundamental cognitive process of executive functioning. Attention is essential for achieving personal goals and practical outcomes such as academic success and mental well-being.[23] Mindfulness, which is defined as intentionally directing one’s attention toward the present moment and consciously observing one’s thoughts, feelings, and experiences,[45] has been proposed as a potential method for improving attentional control.

Social media, largely driven by the rapid growth of internet-based platforms such as WhatsApp, Snapchat, TikTok, and Instagram has become an indispensable part of daily life. In Saudi Arabia, approximately 94.9% of the population actively used social media platforms in the year 2024.[6] A recent study published in 2025 on Saudi young adults across the kingdom, found that almost 36% of the participants were addicted to social media.[7] Furthermore, a study of medical students in 2023 in the Riyadh region found that 55% of the participants were addicted to social media.[8]

In recent years, short-form videos (e.g., reels) have become a prominent type of social media content. These videos are recognized for their brief, engaging, personalized, and fragmented nature, effectively serving the present fast-paced lifestyle. While moderate social media use can yield benefits, uncontrolled and disproportionate engagement with these platforms, namely social media addiction (SMA), has raised growing concerns of detrimental results, such as declining academic performance and deteriorating mental and physical health, including attention.[8]

The quality and capacity of attention vary in individuals as a result of multiple internal and external factors such as personal motivation, task difficulty, mindfulness practices, dietary habits, physical activity, and media exposure.[239] Of these factors, exposure to social media has emerged as an increasingly influential determinant of attentional capacity and cognitive performance, especially students of the health sciences, who often experience psychological distress owing to demanding academic requirements.[89]

Numerous studies have highlighted the association between mindful attention (MA) and SMA, and the negative impact of SMA has been consistently reported.[81011] A study conducted at Qassim University found that more than half of the participants perceived that SMA negatively affected their daily functioning and sleep. Furthermore, they found that SMA was correlated with lower grade point average (GPA) and higher anxiety levels.[12] Similarly, a study conducted on Saudi pharmacy students reported that mindfulness was positively associated with resilience and academic achievement, suggesting that higher MA may mitigate the adverse effects of SMA.[13]

Given the growing recognition of this problem and its adverse consequences, there is increased interest in understanding how it influences attentional capacity. However, studies on the link between SMA and decreased MA in healthcare college students are still limited, which underlines a considerable gap and the need for further investigations. The study aimed to assess SMA, MA, and examine their association in students of health colleges in a Saudi University and identify factors related to the MA.

Materials and Methods

The 6-month study was conducted from December 2024 to May 2025. Sample size was calculated using the Epi-Info application, considering the prevalence of SMA at 55.2%, with a margin of error of 5% and P ≤ 0.05, which yielded a required minimum sample size of 380 students. A convenience sampling approach was used, and all eligible undergraduate students were invited to participate. An online questionnaire was generated by QuestionPro software and distributed to 380 undergraduate students at health colleges (included were the health track, colleges of medicine, dentistry, nursing, applied medical sciences, clinical pharmacy, and public health). Criteria for inclusion were: All undergraduate students enrolled in these colleges and who consented to participate. Exclusion criteria: Students who self-reported a diagnosis of cognitive impairment, such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and schizophrenia. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) vide letter no. IRB-UGS-2024-01-708 dated 10/10/2024, and written informed consent was taken from all participants in the study. Data were anonymized and accessible only to the research team.

The questionnaire consisted of three parts: Participants characteristics and two validated questionnaires. Participants’ characteristics: Age, gender, nationality, marital status, monthly income, self-reported GPA, failing a subject/year, diagnosed medical conditions, academic experience satisfaction, self-perceived rating of stress level, family atmosphere, relationships quality, sleep, and diet quality, caffeine consumption, physical activity, using phone before sleeping, checking notifications while studying, watching short videos over the day, and the use of playback speed.

The Bergen SMA Scale (BSMAS) is used to assess SMA has 6 items reflecting salience, tolerance, mood modification, conflict, withdrawal, and relapse. Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very rarely to 5 = very often). Total score ranged from 6 to 30. A score of 16 or higher on the BSMAS indicates SMA.[814]

Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) is used to assess individuals’ dispositional mindfulness, with an emphasis on their level of attention to the present moment has 15 items about experiences such as, doing things automatically, forgetting someone’s name, not noticing physical discomfort, or losing focus during conversations. It also includes being easily distracted, daydreaming while doing tasks, and rushing through activities without awareness. Other items reflect forgetting whether a task was completed, eating without appreciation of the taste, and difficulty recalling recent actions. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = almost always to 6 = almost never). The total score ranged from 15 to 90, scores from 15 to 40 indicate low mindfulness, 41–65 for moderate mindfulness, and 66–90 are high mindfulness levels.[1516]

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 27.0 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY: USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is used to assess the internal consistency of each scale, 0.92 for BSMAS and 0.78 for MAAS. Categorical data were presented in frequencies and percentages, while mean, standard deviation, and median were used to summarize continuous variables. A test of normality was performed, and accordingly, t-tests and ANOVA were used. Pearson correlation for BSMAS and MAAS scores. Univariate linear regression was performed to assess individual relationships between independent variables and MAAS scores. Significant variables in the univariate linear regression were subsequently included in a multiple linear regression model to adjust for potential confounders and to identify independent predictors of MAAS scores.

Results

A total of 335 participants were included in the study with a response rate of 88% and a mean age of 20.59 ± 1.83 years (range: 16–26, median = 20). Nearly 80% of the students were female, Saudi (99.4%), single (93.4%), and 12.8% reported a chronic medical condition. The higher responses were from the college of medicine (34.9%). About 88.4% had a self-reported GPA between 3.6 and 5.0. Academic failure of a year or subject was reported by 6.9% of students. About half (52.2%) of the participants were satisfied with their university experience. Stress was highly perceived by 6.7%. Positive family atmosphere was reported by 55.5%, and 68.1% rated their relationships as good. Sleep quality was poor for 63.6%, and 69.6% perceived their diet as poor. Age had a weak, nonsignificant negative correlation with MAAS (R = −0.071, P = 0.198). No significant differences in MAAS scores were found for gender, nationality, marital status, income, chronic illness, or type of college. Students with GPAs 3.6–5.0 had significantly higher MAAS scores (P = 0.04). Satisfaction with university experience, positive family atmosphere and good relationships, good sleep and diet quality correlated positively with mindfulness (P = 0.001). Higher perceived stress was linked to lower MAAS (P = 0.001) [Table 1].

In BSMAS, about 35.5% reported that they sometimes felt a strong urge to use the social media, and 31.9% had unsuccessfully tried to reduce use. A considerable proportion (33.7% sometimes, 19.7% often, 14.6% very often) used social media to escape personal problems. Notably, 54.7% felt distressed when restricted. One-third (33.4%) said that sometimes excessive use affected academics or work. The mean raw score was 16.07 ± 4.78 (range: 6–30); with 54% (n = 181) having SMA tendencies [Figure 1].

In MAAS, the total mean raw score was 57.99 ± 14.17 (range: 17–90), based on scores, 11.6% had low mindfulness, 57.3% moderate, and 31% high [Figure 2]. A percentage of 37 reported they became aware of their emotions only sometimes after they had experienced them. Twenty-nine percent very frequently had difficulty focusing on the present. About 26% almost never paid attention to their surroundings; 18.2% frequently walked quickly with no awareness. 26.9% felt they were “running on automatic” often, and 24.2% drove their cars without recalling the purpose. A percentage of 30.1 frequently did tasks inattentively, and 25.1% rushed through activities. Around 31.9% were frequently preoccupied with the past or future. Attention lapses included forgetting names soon after introduction (23.9%) and partial listening while multitasking (29%). About 27.8% had snacks without being aware of what they ate. Notably, a significant negative correlation between SMA and mindfulness was found among participants (R = −0.519, P < 0.001), the higher SMA is associated with lower MAAS scores [Figure 3].

Discussion

This study’s aim was to explore the relationship between SMA and MA in students at health colleges, where they demonstrated moderate levels of mindfulness, as reflected by MAAS score of 57.99.[1516] Consistent with our findings, an interventional study of medical students in Saudi Arabia reported a similar baseline MAAS score. Notably, their mindfulness levels improved following a mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) program.[17] Similarly, a study of nursing students in China reported a MAAS score of 56.57, which closely aligns with our average score.[18] This resemblance may reflect the demanding academic environments of health-related fields, in which multitasking and elevated stress levels can negatively impact mindfulness. In contrast, a study conducted in India reported a lower MAAS score of 52.68 by students from various academic backgrounds.[15] This difference may be attributable to variations in target populations, educational demands, and cultural or spiritual conceptualizations of mindfulness.

Social media use is pervasive among young adults worldwide, and Saudi students are no exception. The average BSMAS score for SMA in this study was 16.07, and 54% showed SMA tendencies. Similar scores were reported by Saudi medical students in 2023, highlighting the vulnerability of this group.[8] In addition, comparable BSMAS scores were reported in China (16.67) and Russia (14.15), whereas the UK and Germany had lower averages (10.24 and 10.62), respectively.[19] Differences may reflect academic stress, cultural attitudes, and post-COVID digital reliance. Notably, 87.5% of participants used social media mainly for entertainment, possibly exceeding usage in regions where usage is more academic or professional. While SMA tendencies were evident, participants remained below the threshold for clinical diagnosis of social media disorder.[20] This highlights the need for early screening and preventive strategies.

Regarding the factors affecting MA, this study indicates that students with higher academic performance, lower perceived stress, stronger social support, better sleep, and healthier diets had higher MA. This aligns with findings on medical students in Jordan that showed that mindfulness positively influenced academic performance.[13] Similarly, a recent study conducted on nursing students in Jazan, Saudi Arabia, revealed a positive impact of mindfulness on academic achievement.[21] On the other hand, a previous study of pharmacy students in the southern region of Saudi Arabia found a weak correlation.[22] High mindfulness consistently correlates with lower stress levels, and MBSR has shown stress-reducing benefits in medical students at King Saud University.[16] In addition, a study of Chinese students found that higher social support positively correlated with mindfulness.[23] Moreover, a study among Brazilian medical students showed that poor sleep quality was associated with reduced attention.[24] Poor dietary habits, likewise, were negatively correlated with mindfulness as found by a study conducted in the United States.[25] These findings are all in accord with the findings of this study.

Though this study didn’t find any significant association with phone use before sleep or while studying, a prior study reported sleep disturbances from bedtime use of the social media.[2627] Watching videos at rapid speed can reduce mind-wandering, but beyond that may impair comprehension.[28] Frequent fast playback use in our study was linked to lower MA. Platforms such as TikTok and Instagram, with short video formats, were associated with fragmented attention. Consistent with a previous study, watching short videos for over 4 h/day was negatively linked to MA.[29]

A central finding of this study was the significant inverse association between SMA and MA, even after adjustment. This mirrors findings from studies conducted in China and Pakistan, which found that higher mindfulness was associated with better attentional control, less mind-wandering, and lower SMA levels.[10303132]

Variations in the strength of significance reported in the literature may be attributed to differences in study population characteristics, research design, contextual settings, and the tools used to measure mindfulness and SMA. Taken together, these findings provide compelling evidence that constant exposure to social media creates a highly distracting environment, making it challenging for students to sustain attention and remain fully present in their offline academic and personal lives.

Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality. The self-administered survey format may be subject to response and recall biases. Data derived from a single institution may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other settings.

Conclusion

Key findings highlight a significant negative association between the use of social media and attention. Higher academic performance, self-perceived academic satisfaction, greater social support, good diet, and sleep quality were all positively correlated with attention. Conversely, self-perceived stress, caffeine consumption, watching short videos, and frequent use of playback speed had negative associations.

The findings of the present study emphasize the importance of promoting healthy lifestyle habits and digital media awareness to enhance attention of health college students. The results add to existing evidence linking SMA with reduced MA, underscoring the cognitive impact of excessive digital engagement and the value of mindfulness-based approaches for strengthening attentional control. Future interventions may include the integration of MBSR programs into health college curricula and the offer of simple practices such as guided meditation sessions and digital detox strategies. Institutions can also adopt screening tools to identify students at higher risk of SMA and low attention levels, enabling timely counseling and support. In addition, screen-time monitoring apps may provide personalized notifications of excessive use and encourage short mindfulness breaks. Such strategies not only support academic success but may also promote psychological well-being, reduce stress and burnout, and enhance the resilience of future healthcare professionals.
