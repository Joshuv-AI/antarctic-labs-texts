A tandem recruitment site in the pseudokinase scaffold PEAK3 is subject to phosphorylation-dependent regulation and cancer-associated mutations
Authors: Tianyue Zhao, Jianmei Hou, Changyuan Hu, Thomas R. Cotton, Roger J. Daly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The PEAK protein family, comprising PEAK1-3, are pseudokinase scaffolds that regulate cell proliferation and motility via recruitment of specific effectors. For PEAK3, the latter include the adaptor proteins Grb2 and CrkII and the Arf GTPase-activating protein (ArfGAP) ASAP1. PEAK3 exhibits a tandem site spanning a CrkII SH3 domain binding sequence and phosphorylation-dependent 14-3-3 recruitment motif at serine 69 (S69), with 14-3-3 binding mediating a negative control ‘switch’ on PEAK3 signaling. However, whether this control switch is subject to (patho)physiological regulation has remained unclear. Here, using MCF-10A breast epithelial cells as a model system, we demonstrate that S69 phosphorylation occurs predominantly in the cytoplasm and is subject to growth factor regulation, being enhanced by EGF and insulin stimulation but with distinct temporal dynamics. We identify Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) and protein kinase C (PKC) as key mediators of this phosphorylation event. Disruption of the pS69/14-3-3 interaction in the PEAK3 S69A mutant leads to elevated basal Erk phosphorylation, altered EGF-induced Erk and Akt activation kinetics, partial epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and increased Arf1 activation. Interrogation of the COSMIC database identified cancer-associated mutations in the tandem recruitment site, and functional characterization revealed a subset that confer enhanced Grb2/ASAP1 binding and migration potential compared with wild-type PEAK3. Interestingly, amongst the cancer mutations, PEAK3 R66P and R66Q lost 14-3-3 binding in vivo, but L55P retained it. Together, our study reveals that the tandem PEAK3 regulatory site is subject to physiological control and is also mutated in cancer.

Protein kinase-catalyzed phosphorylation of protein substrates plays a critical role in regulating cell signalling pathways and controls a wide variety of biological processes, including cell growth, proliferation, and metabolism. Nearly 10% of human protein kinases, however, lack one or more conserved catalytic residues in the kinase domain and are classified as pseudokinases (1, 2). Lacking catalytic function, pseudokinases function as allosteric modulators of active kinases or scaffolding proteins (1).

The PEAK family of pseudokinases in humans comprises PEAK1 (pseudopodium-enriched atypical kinase one or Sugen kinase 269/SgK269) (3), PEAK2 (SgK223, the human orthologue of rat Pragmin) (4) and the recently identified PEAK3 (5). The PEAKs share a conserved molecular structure, with a C-terminal pseudokinase (PsK) domain flanked by regulatory N- and C-terminal α-helices, which form a split helical dimerization (SHED) domain required for homo and heterodimerization (6, 7, 8, 9). The PEAKs also contain N-terminal intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) punctuated with binding motifs that mediate selective protein-protein interactions, enabling the PEAKs to function as signalling scaffolds. These motifs include: SH2 domain binding sites for Grb2 (present in PEAK1 and PEAK3), tensin3 (PEAK1), and Csk (PEAK2); a phosphotyrosine binding (PTB) domain binding site that recruits the scaffold Shc1 (PEAK1); a src homology (SH)3 binding motif for recruiting the adaptor protein CrkII (conserved in PEAK1-3); and a 14-3-3 binding motif characterized in PEAK3 (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17). Importantly, dimerization of PEAK proteins is critical to their signalling potential, with heterodimerization mediating diversification of signal outputs (6, 10, 13, 18).

PEAK1 localizes to the actin cytoskeleton and focal adhesions where it signals downstream of specific integrins and the epidermal growth factor receptor, thereby promoting cell spreading, migration, invasion and proliferation (3, 14, 19, 20). Reflecting its potent signaling role, PEAK1 is implicated in several human malignancies, including colon (3), pancreatic (21), lung (22) and breast cancer (14). Similar to PEAK1, PEAK2 has reported roles in pancreatic and lung cancer, together with esophageal adenocarcinoma (23, 24, 25). PEAK3 also functions as an oncogenic scaffold with a pro-migratory and -invasive role (11, 12, 13). Important downstream effectors of PEAK3 are Grb2, which is recruited following Src family kinase-mediated phosphorylation of Y24 and in turn couples to the Arf GTPase activating protein (GAP) ASAP1, and CrkII (13) (Fig. 1, A and B). Interestingly, the PEAK3 interactome exhibits dramatic dynamics in response to EGF stimulation, with a significant reduction in binding to Grb2, ASAP1 and CrkII early after EGF treatment, reflecting PTPN12-mediated dephosphorylation of PEAK3 (13). An additional mechanism for regulation of PEAK3 signalling is via binding of 14-3-3 proteins. This is mediated via a 14-3-3 binding motif that encompasses phosphorylated serine (S) 69 and is juxtapositioned next to the conserved CrkII binding site (11, 26). Together the CrkII and 14-3-3 binding motifs form a tandem site that plays a critical role in regulating PEAK3 signaling. Recruitment of 14-3-3 proteins negatively regulates the PEAK3/CrkII interaction via steric interference and also reduces Grb2 binding, which may reflect an indirect role for CrkII in promoting PEAK3 tyrosine phosphorylation (11). While most work on PEAK signaling has focused on tyrosine phosphorylation, the recent functional characterization of PEAK3 S69 highlights an emerging and important role for serine/threonine phosphorylation. For example, PEAK1 binds and activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII), which in turn mediates PEAK1 serine and threonine phosphorylation to enhance PEAK1 oncogenic signaling (10). In contrast, phosphorylation of PEAK3 S69 plays a negative regulatory role, similar to phosphorylation of specific serine/threonine residues on the Gab1 and Gab2 scaffolds (27, 28, 29). Importantly, such negative regulatory sites, or residues in close proximity to them, can be targeted by oncogenic mutations, as exemplified by Gab1 T387 (29).

In this study, we further characterize the role of the PEAK3 tandem site and the associated S69-dependent regulatory switch. In particular, through generation of a corresponding site-selective phosphospecific antibody, we determine the kinetics and localization of PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation and identify the kinases responsible. Furthermore, we identify and functionally characterize cancer-associated mutations in the tandem site, demonstrating that this region is targeted by oncogenic alterations during malignant progression.

Results

Regulation of PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation

The N-terminal IDR of PEAK3 contains a tandem site that recruits 14-3-3 proteins upon phosphorylation of S69 (11, 26) (Fig. 1 A and B), with 14-3-3 binding representing a negative regulatory switch for PEAK3 signaling (11). However, whether S69 phosphorylation is regulated by physiological stimuli or PEAK3 structural organization has remained unclear. To address this issue, we generated a custom antibody specifically recognizing S69-phosphorylated (pS69) PEAK3. This antibody recognized wild-type (WT) PEAK3 expressed in MCF-10A immortalized breast epithelial cells (MCF-10A/PEAK3) (13) but not the S69A mutant (Fig. 1C). Western blotting of cell lysates from serum-starved and EGF-stimulated cells revealed that S69 is phosphorylated in response to stimulation with this growth factor (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the basal and EGF-stimulated pS69 signal was abolished upon lambda protein phosphatase treatment of cell lysates (Fig. 1D), confirming the antibody’s requirement for PEAK3 phosphorylation. Characterization of the dynamics of EGF-induced PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation determined that the pS69 signal increased significantly within 5 min of EGF stimulation, peaked at 20 min, and then declined to basal levels by 180 min (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, EGF-induced phosphorylation of endogenous PEAK3 S69 could be detected in A172 glioblastoma cells, but this required PEAK3 immunoprecipitation before Western blotting due to the low levels of PEAK3 expression (Fig. S1A). In contrast to the EGF response in MCF-10A cells, insulin induced a progressive increase in S69 phosphorylation over time which reached statistical significance at 180 min (Fig. 1F). We also determined whether PEAK3 dimerization is required for S69 phosphorylation, an important question since PEAK3 dimerization is critical to its signaling and biological activity (13) and required for its interaction with 14-3-3 (26). However, phosphorylation of S69 was similar to WT in three dimerization-impaired PEAK3 mutants expressed in PEAK1/2 double knockout MCF-10A cells (13) (Fig. 1G), indicating that dimerization potential is not required for this modification. Overall, these data indicate that S69 phosphorylation is regulated by growth factor and hormonal stimulation and, given its known functional role, mediates negative regulation of PEAK3 signaling via 14-3-3 binding.

Subcellular localization of S69-phosphorylated PEAK3

A previous study reported that 14-3-3 interaction regulates PEAK3 intracellular localization in COS-7 cells (26). To determine whether this also applies to MCF-10A cells, we applied confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to cells transiently expressing GFP-PEAK3 or GFP-PEAK3 S69A at similar levels (Fig. S1B). Compared to the GFP control, GFP-PEAK3 predominantly localized to the cytoplasm, whereas GFP-PEAK3 S69A was enriched in the nucleus (Fig. 2A), as indicated by the quantified nuclear to cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio (Fn/c) (Fig. 2B). To characterize the subcellular distribution of S69-phosphorylated PEAK3, we undertook indirect immunofluorescence on MCF-10A cells transiently expressing GFP-PEAK3 using the custom pS69-specific antibody and CLSM. Consistent with Western blotting data (Fig. 1D), the EGF-induced pS69 signal was greatly diminished following lambda protein phosphatase treatment or in cells expressing the PEAK3 S69A mutant (Fig. S1C). Upon EGF stimulation of serum-starved cells, the pS69 signal rapidly increased (Fig. 2C). Notably, the pS69 signal was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm (Fig. 2C) and quantitative analysis of the fold change in relative cytoplasmic fluorescence intensity (Fc) of pS69 (normalized to total GFP-PEAK3 signal within the same cell) confirmed a significant enhancement peaking at 5 to 20 min compared to the 0 min time point (Fig. 2D). Characterization of the nuclear to cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio of the GFP signal indicated a cytoplasmic enrichment beginning at 5 min following EGF stimulation (Fig. 2E).

Identification of kinases regulating PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation

PEAK3 S69 is characterized by R at the −3 position, indicating that it is a strong candidate for phosphorylation by a variety of basophilic kinases, including protein kinase D (PKD) (Fig. 3A). Indeed, a previous study reported that treatment with the pan-PKD inhibitor CRT0066101 reduced PEAK3/14-3-3 interaction in HEK293 cells, and that recombinant PKD2 phosphorylated a PEAK3 peptide harboring S69 in vitro (26). However, treatment of MCF-10A/PEAK3 cells with CRT0066101 did not significantly reduce S69 phosphorylation (Fig. S2A). In addition, knockdown of the three PKD isoforms, either individually or in combination, did not result in an obvious decrease in pS69 levels (Fig. S2B). Consequently, PKD does not play a major role in mediating S69 phosphorylation in MCF-10A cells. We then turned to other candidate basophilic kinases (Fig. 3A). While treatment of cells with selective inhibitors for protein kinase A (PKA) or Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) did not impact S69 phosphorylation (Fig. S2C), significant decreases were observed upon administration of Go6850, which targets protein kinase C (PKC)α, PKCβI, PKCβII, and PKCγ) and Go6976 (PKCα and PKCβ1) (Fig. 3B). Additionally, treatment with RA306, a second-generation CaMKII inhibitor that preferentially targets CaMKIID and CaMKIIG (31), significantly reduced S69 phosphorylation (Fig. 3C), a result validated by siRNA-mediated knockdown of CaMKIID and CaMKIIG (Fig. 3D). Of note, although EGF simulation enhanced S69 phosphorylation, inhibition of MEK (with trametinib), PI3K (BKM120) and Akt (MK2206) did not cause a significant reduction in S69 phosphorylation, indicating that the MEK/Erk and PI3K/Akt pathways downstream of the EGFR do not regulate pS69 levels (Fig. S2D). Impact of the negative regulatory switch imposed by pS69 on cell biology and signaling

To further characterize the functional role of pS69, we utilized MCF-10A cells stably expressing either PEAK3 WT or the S69A mutant. Cells expressing PEAK3 WT displayed a cobblestone-like morphology in monolayer cell culture similar to vector controls, with well-defined colony boundaries and tightly packed cell islands (Fig. 4A). In contrast, MCF-10A/PEAK3 S69A cells exhibited a more loosely scattered distribution rather than compact cell islands (Fig. 4A). Since this phenotype was suggestive of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), we undertook an EGF stimulation time course experiment that allowed us to determine basal levels of E-Cadherin as an epithelial marker as well as dynamic changes in Erk and Akt activation, two known kinase pathways known to regulate EMT (32, 33) (Fig. 4, B–E). E-cadherin levels were comparable between PEAK3 WT and vector control cells but were significantly reduced in cells expressing the S69 mutant (Fig. 4, B and D). In addition, basal levels of phosphorylated Erk (pErk) were significantly increased in cells overexpressing PEAK3 WT and elevated further in those expressing PEAK3 S69A (Fig. 4, C and E). However, basal activation of phosphorylated Akt (pAkt) was not altered upon introduction of PEAK3 WT or S69A (Fig. 4B). Upon EGF stimulation, pAkt in MCF-10A/PEAK3 cells exhibited a response pattern comparable to that of the control, with a gradual increase peaking at 20 min, followed by a return to near baseline levels at 180 min (Fig. 4, B and F). In contrast, S69A-expressing cells displayed a more rapid pAkt activation that peaked at 5 min post-stimulation, followed by a decline over the remaining time course (Fig. 4, B and F). In terms of pErk regulation, control-, PEAK3 WT- and PEAK3 S69A-expressing cells displayed maximum activation at 5 min, but the latter exhibited a more rapid decay in pErk, with a significant decrease at 20 min that returned pErk to near base line levels, whereas this was not achieved until 180 min for control and MCF-10A/PEAK3 cells (Fig. 4, C and G). PEAK3 S69A enhances activation of the small G protein Arf1

PEAK3 interacts with ASAP1, a GAP for Arf1, Arf5 and Arf6 in vitro (34), via the adaptor Grb2, but rapidly dissociates from ASAP1 following EGF stimulation of cells (13). ASAP1 is involved in several biological processes relevant to PEAK3 function, including cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesion dynamics and cell migration (35, 36, 37). However, the effect of PEAK3 on Arf activation has yet to be characterized. To test this, we performed a pulldown assay with a fragment of GGA3 (Golgi-associated, γ-adaptin ear-containing, ARF-Binding Protein 3), which specifically binds to active (GTP-bound) Arfs (38). Under basal conditions, expression of PEAK3 WT or S69A modestly enhanced Arf1 activation compared to vector controls, with the effect of S69A significant compared to vector but not WT (Fig. 5, A and B). However, following 5 min of EGF stimulation, Arf1 activation in vector, PEAK3- and PEAK3 S69A-expressing cells were similar (Fig. 5, A and B). Arf5 exhibited a non-significant trend for increased activation by PEAK3 S69A under basal conditions, which was not observed with PEAK3 WT or following EGF treatment (Figs. 5A and S3A). PEAK3 WT or S69A did not affect activation of Arf6 (Figs. 5A and S3B). Cancer-associated mutations in the PEAK3 tandem site exhibit altered signaling potential

PEAK3 exhibits oncogenic properties in a variety of cell models and enhanced expression of PEAK3 occurs in acute myeloid leukemia (12, 13). To determine whether particular regions of PEAK3 are mutated in human cancers, we interrogated the COSMIC database (39). Missense mutations leading to amino acid substitutions occurred at low frequency and were relatively evenly distributed over the PEAK3 open reading frame, although a R147H mutation exhibited markedly increased occurrence relative to other sites (Fig. S4). Interestingly, R147 resides in the secondary PEAK3/14-3-3 binding interface, and a R147A substitution reduces 14-3-3 binding (26). To characterize the functional effect of PEAK3 R147H, we expressed this mutant, as well as PEAK3 S69A and R147A, in HEK293 cells and undertook co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) studies for 14-3-3 and specific PEAK3 effectors (Fig. S5). As previously reported (11), the S69A mutant lost 14-3-3 binding and exhibited significantly enhanced recruitment of CrkII and Grb2 compared to PEAK3 WT (Fig. S5, A–D). Interestingly, R147A exhibited a trend for decreased 14-3-3 association while R147H displayed a significant reduction. However, binding of Grb2 and ASAP1 were both significantly reduced for the R147A and R147H mutants and association of CrkII was significantly decreased for R147H (Fig. S5, A–E). Consequently, although these data support a role for R147 in mediating 14-3-3 association, how the cancer-associated PEAK3 R147H substitution contributes to oncogenic signaling remains unclear.

We then turned to several substitutions in the tandem site region that alter the CrkII and 14-3-3 binding motifs (Figs. 6A and S4). While we accept that some may be 'passenger' events rather than oncogenic changes, the important role of this region in adaptor protein recruitment and negative signal modulation (11, 13, 26) justified selection of these mutations for further functional characterization. HEK293 cells were transiently transfected to express HA-tagged PEAK3 WT or selected mutants (L55P, P56L, R66P, R66Q, P71H and T72N) (Fig. 6B). Upon Co-IP analysis, recruitment of CrkII, 14-3-3, Grb2 and ASAP1 to PEAK3 WT was detected, as reported previously (11, 13, 26) (Fig. 6B). In the CrkII motif, the L55P and P56L mutants exhibited modest but nonsignificant increases in 14-3-3 binding but contrasting alterations in adaptor protein recruitment. PEAK3 L55P displayed significantly enhanced interaction with Grb2 and a trend for increased ASAP1 association, with no change to CrkII recruitment, while the P56L mutant exhibited significantly reduced CrkII and Grb2 binding (Fig. 6, B–F). Strikingly, R66P and R66Q in the 14-3-3 binding motif abolished 14-3-3 interaction, significantly enhanced association with Grb2 and ASAP1 and interestingly, reduced CrkII interaction. The other two mutants analyzed, P71H and T72N, exhibited similar binding properties to PEAK3 WT. These data indicate that cancer-associated mutations can have both positive and negative effects on the binding properties of PEAK3. Furthermore, while two positively-acting mutants are associated with loss of the 14-3-3 'brake' on signaling, another (L55P) maintains 14-3-3 recruitment. Our previous work demonstrated that PEAK3 enhances random cell motility, an effect further enhanced with PEAK3 S69A, which was determined by live cell tracking (11). To determine whether cancer-associated mutations in the vicinity of S69 impact biological endpoints, we extended this analysis to PEAK3 R66P and R66Q, which reside in the 14-3-3 binding region. Determination of the mechanism of action of cancer-associated PEAK3 mutations

Reduced 14-3-3 binding by PEAK3 mutants could involve two mechanisms, which are not mutually exclusive. First, they could lead to reduced phosphorylation of S69 by altering the substrate recognition sequence for upstream kinases. This would negatively impact 14-3-3 binding because phosphorylated S69 is a critical component of the 14-3-3 binding motif. Second, they could alter the affinity of 14-3-3 for the phosphorylated motif because they alter other residues in the motif outside of S69. We addressed these mechanisms for PEAK3 R66P and R66Q, which both lose 14-3-3 binding, and included L55P for comparison, as this mutant localizes outside the tandem site and does not significantly impact 14-3-3 recruitment, but enhances association of Grb2 with PEAK3 (Fig. 6, A and B).

To address the first mechanism, we developed a custom mass spectrometry workflow, since we were unable to use the pS69 phosphospecific antibody as the R66P and R66Q mutations altered the antigen sequence. This workflow took into account both the altered protein sequence and loss of trypsin cleavage site for particular mutants (Fig. 7, A and B). HA-tagged PEAK3 WT and mutant proteins were expressed in HEK293 cells, immunoprecipitated and subjected to the MS workflow. For each mutant, we searched for both the phosphorylated and unphosphorylated form of the modified (mutant) peptide, quantified both, and then determined the relative phosphorylation of the peptide (phosphorylated/unphosphorylated). This revealed that the L55P mutation did not affect S69 phosphorylation, but R66P and R66Q led to a marked and significant reduction (Fig. 7C and Table S1). Overall, these experiments indicate that for both PEAK3 R66P and R66Q, the overriding factor underpinning their gain of signaling activity is the reduction of S69 phosphorylation due to loss of a basic residue at the −3 position relative to S69, a critical requirement for phosphorylation by PKC and CaMKII (Fig. 3A). If S69 is not phosphorylated, then 14-3-3 will not bind, and the negative control ‘switch’ is not engaged (11, 40). In the case of PEAK3 R66P, if S69 does become phosphorylated by a kinase with corresponding substrate selectivity, then the mutant protein exhibits a very low affinity for 14-3-3 and the negative control mechanism is still impaired. However, phosphorylated PEAK3 R66Q exhibits significant 14-3-3 binding activity, indicating that the control switch is at least partially functional. Consequently, the failure of R66Q to become phosphorylated by upstream kinases appears to be the key factor underpinning its gain of downstream signaling activity.

Discussion

In this study, we build upon previous reports of a pS69/14-3-3-mediated control switch on PEAK3 (11, 26) by demonstrating that phosphorylation of PEAK3 S69 is a physiological negative control mechanism in response to growth factors and hormones, with PKCs and CaMKII identified as upstream S69 kinases. Phosphorylation of S69 also promotes cytoplasmic retention of PEAK3. While disruption of the control switch in PEAK3 S69A leads to increased CrkII and Grb2 association (11), we now demonstrate that it also promotes enhanced signaling by Erk and Arf1 and promotes partial EMT. Moreover, the impact of the control switch is emphasized by our characterization of cancer-associated PEAK3 mutants that feature abrogated 14-3-3 binding.

Tyrosine phosphorylation plays a fundamental role in promoting signaling by the PEAK family, creating binding sites for a variety of SH2 and PTB-containing effectors (6). However, a growing body of evidence indicates that serine/threonine phosphorylation also regulates PEAK scaffolding functions. In particular, PEAK1 activates and sustains CaMKII activation through direct interaction with the CaMKII kinase domain. In turn, activated CaMKII promotes PEAK1 serine/threonine phosphorylation, positively regulating its heterotypic association with PEAK2 (10). In contrast, our study reveals that S69 phosphorylation of PEAK3 functions as a negative regulatory mechanism, similar to that observed in other scaffolds. For example, phosphorylation of Gab2 at S210 and T391 mediates 14-3-3 recruitment, which negatively regulates Gab2 downstream signaling by disrupting its interaction with Grb2 (28). Here, an approximately 2-fold increase in phosphorylation of these sites is observed upon EGF treatment, an effect similar in magnitude to the EGF-induced phosphorylation of PEAK3 S69. Notably, PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation reaches a maximum at 5 min, coinciding with dephosphorylation of PEAK3 at Y24, mediated by PTPN12, and dissociation of Grb2/ASAP1 (13). Consequently, the S69-mediated control switch in PEAK3 co-operates with PTPN12 to switch off PEAK3 signaling shortly after growth factor stimulation.

Results from pharmacological inhibition and knockdown experiments indicated that specific PKC and CaMKII isoforms contribute to PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation, consistent with the motif surrounding this site, which matches the substrate selectivity of these kinases. In addition, PKD has also been reported as a PEAK3 S69 kinase, although we were unable to demonstrate this role in MCF-10A cells. These data indicate that there is significant redundancy in the kinases that phosphorylate this site, which may reflect the role of the PEAKs as signal integrators that receive signals from multiple upstream pathways. We also note that individual knockdown of CaMKIID and G, as well as treatment with the CaMKII inhibitor RA306, all resulted in a similar (∼2-fold) reduction in S69 phosphorylation. Since one would expect a greater inhibition by RA306, it is possible that the broader effect of this kinase inhibitor leads to network rewiring and compensatory phosphorylation by other kinases (41).

Our finding that CaMKII is a key mediator of PEAK3 S69 phosphorylation highlights important differences between PEAK1 and PEAK3 in their interactions with the CaMKII family. Both PEAK1 and PEAK3 harbor a consensus motif for CaMKII binding R/K–X–X–ɸ–X–R/K–X–X–S/T–ɸ (where ɸ represents a hydrophobic residue) (42) (Fig. S7). However, unlike PEAK1, which has an alanine at position 0 and an aspartic acid at +2, PEAK3 contains a serine at position 0 (S69) and a proline at +2 (Fig. S7). Once S69 is phosphorylated, this region forms a canonical 14-3-3 binding motif (RXXpSXP) (40). Notably, no known CaMKII binding partners have a proline at the +2 position (10, 42). Moreover, the region downstream of the PEAK1 CaMKII interaction motif is enriched with negatively charged residues (D308, D310, D311, and D314), predicted to form salt bridges and electrostatic interactions across the surface of CaMKII (10) (Fig. S7). In contrast, the corresponding region in PEAK3 lacks such acidic residues, and the residues corresponding to PEAK1 D310 and D311 are basic. Consequently, PEAK1 is a pseudo-substrate that binds with high affinity and activates CaMKII, while PEAK3 is a bona fide substrate of CaMKII, likely has a lower affinity for CaMKII association, and once S69 is phosphorylated binds 14-3-3, which will compete with CaMKII for binding. This raises the possibility of crosstalk between PEAK1 and PEAK3 signaling, where PEAK1 promotes CaMKII-mediated negative regulation of PEAK3.

Recruitment of the Arf GAP ASAP1 to PEAK3 is well-documented (12, 13), and ASAP1 knockdown reduces PEAK3-mediated cell proliferation and invasion in 3D culture (13). However, the impact of PEAK3 on Arf activity has not been characterized. This is an important question, since Arf proteins regulate critical cellular processes such as cell-cell adhesion, focal adhesion turnover, integrin cycling and actin cytoskeleton remodeling (43, 44). Although ASAP1 exhibits GAP activity towards Arf1, Arf5, and to a lesser extent Arf6 in vitro (34), the most convincing effects of PEAK3 were on Arf1, where there was a modest but significant enhancement of GTP-loaded Arf1 upon expression of PEAK3 S69A. Given the known role of ASAP1 as an Arf GAP, this was an unexpected result. There are two potential explanations, which are not mutually exclusive. First, since PEAK3 recruits PYK2 as well as ASAP1 (12, 13), and PYK2 is known to tyrosine phosphorylate ASAP1, resulting in a reduction in Arf GAP activity (45), ASAP1 may be negatively regulated by binding to the PEAK3 complex. Second, PEAK3 sequesters ASAP1 away from Arf1. Both models are consistent with the loss of PEAK3 S69A-mediated Arf1 activation following 5 min of EGF stimulation, since at this time point the Grb2/ASAP1 complex has dissociated from PEAK3 (13). Of note, while the effect of PEAK3 S69A on ARF1 in the pulldown assay is modest, the assay only provides an averaged estimation of ARF activity, and the enhancement of activity at specific subcellular compartments may be more marked.

MCF-10A/PEAK3 S69A cells exhibited a partial EMT, characterized by altered monolayer organization, decreased E-Cadherin expression and markedly enhanced cell motility. The increased activation of Erk likely contributes to this phenotype, since activated Erk translocates to the nucleus where it can upregulate EMT-inducing transcription factors such as Snail, Slug, ZEB1/2, and Twist (46, 47, 48). This raises the question of how Erk is activated by PEAK3, given that PEAK3-bound Grb2 appears to couple to ASAP1 rather than the Ras GEF Sos1 (13). A likely candidate is provided by Arf1, since Arf1 overexpression promotes activation of Ras and Erk (49, 50), as well as EMT (49). The enhanced binding of Crk by PEAK3 S69A (11) may also contribute to pro-EMT signaling, since Crk positively regulates this process (51). Consequently, the ability of PEAK3 S69A to promote EMT likely reflects its coupling to multiple downstream effector pathways, which may also synergize in enhancing this phenotype.

The presence of cancer-associated mutations in the tandem motif, some with properties consistent with an oncogenic role, underscores the critical regulatory role of this region. We accept that some of these alterations are passenger mutations but consider it likely that those with potent functional impact represent oncogenic changes. We also acknowledge that our characterization of these mutants (as well as PEAK3 S69A in Figs. 4 and 5) involved overexpression studies, which may affect the relative stoichiometry of specific binding partners and subcellular localization. Noting these caveats, our work leads to some interesting observations and insights. First, mutation of S69 itself is not observed, with changes limited to flanking residues. The reason for this is not clear, since a breast cancer-associated mutation in Gab1, T387N, abolishes negative feedback regulation of this scaffold by blocking T387 phosphorylation, leading to amplified Gab1 signaling (29). Second, while two mutations, R66P and R66Q, disrupt the 14-3-3 binding motif, they differ in their functional impact to S69A, which also resides in this motif. Specifically, S69A enhances both Grb2 and CrkII binding (11), while the two R66 substitutions increase binding of Grb2/ASAP1 but decrease CrkII association. The most likely explanation is that R66 contributes to CrkII SH3 domain binding. R147 resides in a secondary PEAK3/14-3-3 interface distal to the tandem motif (26) and the R147H mutation results in significantly reduced 14-3-3 binding. However, rather than enhanced effector recruitment, the R147H mutant exhibits decreased association with CrkII, Grb2 and ASAP1. The mechanistic basis for these effects is unclear at present, and whether this mutant recruits alternative effectors remains to be determined.

Third, for R66P and R66Q, the predominant factor underpinning their gain of signaling activity is the reduction of S69 phosphorylation due to loss of the substrate recognition site for PKC and CaMKII, although the R66P mutation also decreases 14-3-3 binding to the cognate binding motif once the motif is phosphorylated. Fourth, R66P, R66Q and L55P all enhance Grb2/ASAP1 association while either decreasing, or having no effect on, CrkII binding. This highlights the importance of Grb2/ASAP1 signaling to the oncogenic role of PEAK3, as we previously demonstrated in the MCF-10A model system (13). Fifth, alterations in the tandem motif outside of the 14-3-3 binding region can also enhance scaffolding function, as seen with L55P. Here, the mechanism underpinning increased Grb2 binding is unclear, but one possibility is that this substitution increases SH3 domain-mediated recruitment of this adaptor.

Overall, the data presented here indicate that the PEAK3 tandem motif is subject to both physiological modulation and dysregulation in cancer, underscoring its critical role in gating PEAK3 signal output.

Cell lysis and immunoprecipitation

Cells were harvested with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing Tris (50 mM, pH 8.0), NaCl (150 mM), Sodium Deoxycholate (0.5%, w/v), NP40 (1%, v/v), SDS (0.1%, w/v), glycerol (10%, v/v), EDTA (5 mM) and NaF (20 mM), and normal lysis buffer (NLB) containing HEPES (50 mM, pH 7.4), NaCl (150 mM), Triton X-100 (1%, v/v), glycerol (10%, v/v), MgCl2 (1.5 mM), EGTA (1 mM), pyrophosphate (10 mM) and NaF (20 mM) (55) for immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation (IP), respectively, with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Merck, catalog no. 4693132001), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate) added freshly before use. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C), and cell lysate supernatant was transferred to a pre-cooled new tube. For active Arf pulldowns and IP of HA-tagged proteins, cell lysates were incubated with either GGA3 protein-binding domain (PBD) agarose beads (Abcam, ab211180) or red anti-HA affinity gel (Sigma, E6779), respectively, at 4 °C for 2 to 4 h with end-to-end rotation. After the incubation, the supernatant (unbound fraction) was removed, and beads were washed three times with ice-cold NLB. Thereafter, bound proteins were eluted with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subjected to Western blotting analysis. To immunoprecipitate endogenous PEAK3, the custom PEAK3 and control antibody were covalently crosslinked to Protein G-Sepharose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 101,242) using crosslinker BS3 (bis(sulfosuccinimidyl)suberate, ThermoFisher Scientific, A39266) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This cross-linking strategy minimizes antibody heavy-chain contamination during antigen elution.

Western blotting

Protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, A55864) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Proteins were subsequently separated on 8, 10 or 15% SDS-PAGE gels before transfer to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, Bovogen, BSAS-AU) in tris-buffered saline (pH 7.5) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) and then incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight followed by HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Western blots were imaged using the ChemiDoc Touch Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and analyzed by Image Lab (version 6.0.0).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were prepared for immunofluorescence 48 h post seeding onto μ-Slide 8 Well chambers (ibidi, 80,807) by fixation with 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher Scientific, AA43368). Cells were then permeabilized using 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 (westlab, TL125) for 5 min at RT and blocked with PBS containing 1% (w/v) BSA, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 1 h at RT, followed by labelling with PEAK3 phospho-Ser69 primary antibody for 1 to 2 h at RT and washed thoroughly with PBS. Thereafter, cells were incubated in the dark for 1 h at RT with goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 555. For the lambda protein phosphatase treatment assay, the dephosphorylation step was performed after cell permeabilization according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C before blocking. Lambda protein phosphatase was added at 400 units per well in a total reaction volume of 100 μl.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy and image analysis

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used a Leica SP8 microscope (Monash Micro Imaging Facility, Monash University) with 63× oil immersion objective. The obtained images were analyzed using ImageJ software. For fluorescence analysis, the mean nuclear fluorescence intensity (Fn) and mean cytoplasmic fluorescence intensity (Fc) of individual cells were collected in the appropriate channels, as well as the mean background fluorescence intensity (Fb). These data were used to calculate the ratios of Fn to Fc (via equation Fn/c = (Fn - Fb)/(Fc - Fb)) to reflect nuclear enrichment. Mean whole-cell fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified to reflect the expression levels of GFP-PEAK3 wild-type (WT) and S69A constructs. For quantification of relative cytoplasmic pS69 fluorescence intensity, total GFP–PEAK3 fluorescence was used as an internal normalization control. In any single assay, ≥ 20 cells were analyzed per sample/condition to calculate the mean ratio values or averaged cytoplasmic fluorescence. Data were then expressed relative to either GFP control or basal condition, which were arbitrarily set at 1, and represented the averaged relative values ± S.E.M. (standard error of the mean) of three independent biological experiments. Scale bars indicate 10 μm. Statistical analysis used ratio paired t test.

Cell motility assay

MCF-10A cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. The following day, cells were cultured in MCF-10A complete growth medium containing mitomycin C (1 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, M4287) to inhibit cell division. For each condition, images were captured at eight random positions every 20 min over a 24-h period using a Leica DFC9000 camera with Leica Application Suite X software. Approximately 40 cells were analyzed per condition. Cell motility was quantified using ImageJ software (version 2.0.0).

Off-beads proteolytic digestion of IP samples for mass spectrometry (MS)

All samples were prepared in biological triplicate. After washing, proteins were eluted from the beads using 5% SDS buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Kawasaki, Kanagawa, Japan) and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was then subjected to simultaneous reduction and alkylation in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (Merck) and 400 mM 2-chloroacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by incubation at 55 °C for 30 min. Protein lysates were acidified with phosphoric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and diluted with a 7:1 volume of S-Trap binding buffer (90% methanol (Merck)/100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) (Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.1). The mixture was loaded onto S-Trap micro columns (PROTIFI) and centrifuged to retain proteins within the matrix. Trypsin (enzyme-to-protein ratio ∼1:1) (Promega) in 50 mM TEAB (Sigma-Aldrich) was added, and digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 18 h. Peptides were sequentially eluted with 50 mM TEAB (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.2% formic acid (FA) (Merck), and 50% acetonitrile (ACN) (Sigma-Aldrich). Eluates were pooled, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 8 μL MS loading buffer (2% ACN (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.3% trifluoroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich)) containing HRM peptide mix (Biognosys, Schlieren, Switzerland) before MS analysis.

Mass spectrometry-based proteome analysis

The MS samples were analyzed using an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano-LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) fitted with a PepMap100 trap column, 100 μm × 2 cm nanoviper and a PepMap100 RSLC analytical column, 75 μm × 50 cm, nanoViper, C18 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were loaded in solvent A (0.1% FA (Merck)) and eluted using a 150 min gradient at a flow rate of 250 nl/min. The gradient for peptides was as follows: 2.5% solvent B (0.1% FA (Merck), 80% ACN (Sigma-Aldrich)) for 2 min, 2.5 to 7.5% solvent B for 1 min, 7.5 to 37.5% solvent B for 120 min, 37.5 to 42.5% solvent B for 3 min, 42.5 to 99% solvent B for 5 min, 99% solvent B for 5 min, 99-2.5% solvent B for 1 min, and finally the system equilibrated with 2.5% solvent B for 14 min. MS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode. The electrospray voltage was set at 2100 V, and the ion transfer tube temperature was set to 300 °C. The full scan MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 within the m/z range of 350 to 1200 using an automated gain control target of 1.2 × 106. The radio frequency (RF) lens was set to 40%, and the dynamic exclusion time was set to 45 s. High-Field Asymmetric Waveform Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) was applied with a compensation voltage of −45 during MS1 scans. In the MS2 setting, high-energy collision dissociation was performed with 28% normalized collision energy at an Orbitrap resolution of 15,000. Multiply charged precursor ions starting from m/z 120 were selected for further fragmentation. The automated gain control target was set to 1.2 × 106 and a 1.6 m/z isolation window was used for fragmentation. FAIMS was applied with a compensation voltage of −65.

Data processing

All raw MS files were searched using MaxQuant software version 2.0.1.0 (56) against an in silico tryptic digest of Homo sapiens proteins from the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot sequence database (https://www.uniprot.org/, release March 2020). In addition, the PEAK3 mutant protein sequences (S69A, R66P, R66Q, and L55P) were appended to the database to enable correct identification of mutation-derived peptides. Database searching was performed with the following settings: carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification; oxidation, N-terminal acetylation, and phosphorylation on serine, threonine, and tyrosine as variable modifications; up to three missed tryptic cleavages permitted; and a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at the peptide-spectrum match level. After peptide quantification, all peptides containing the PEAK3 sites S69, S69A, R66P and R66Q, regardless of phosphorylation status, were extracted. For each type of peptide and variant, the intensity-based ratio of phosphorylated to non-phosphorylated peptides was calculated and compared across samples.

Synthetic PEAK3 peptides

Synthetic PEAK3 (phospho)peptides were produced by Mimotopes (Mulgrave, Victoria, Australia) (95% purity, N-term acetylation, C-term amidation). The following peptide sequences were used in this study: PEAK351-74 (S69 unphosphorylated): Ac-NPEPLPPPLPKKILTRTQSLPTRR-NH2, PEAK351-74 (pS69): Ac-NPEPLPPPLPKKILTRTQpSLPTRR-NH2, PEAK351-74 (S69A): Ac-NPEPLPPPLPKKILTRTQALPTRR-NH2, PEAK351-74 (pS69, L55P): Ac-NPEPPPPPLPKKILTRTQpSLPTRR-NH2, PEAK351-74 (pS69, R66P): Ac-NPEPLPPPLPKKILTPTQpSLPTRR-NH2 and PEAK351-74 (pS69, R66Q): Ac-NPEPLPPPLPKKILTQTQpSLPTRR-NH2.

Recombinant 14-3-3γ protein production

The pCOLD_IV vector (Takara) encoding full length human 14-3-3γ (UniProt ID: P61981) with N-terminal 8xHis tag and TEV cleavage site was kindly provided by Prof Isabelle Lucet (Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, Melbourne). Chemically competent C41(DE3) E. coli were transformed, plated on LB agar + ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single colony was used to inoculate a 50 ml starter culture (2xYT media + ampicillin) which was cultured overnight at 37 °C. The starter culture was used to inoculate large scale cultures (10 ml starter culture per 1L 2xYT + ampicillin), which were grown at 37 °C until OD600 ∼0.8, before induction with 0.5 mM IPTG overnight at 18 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and cell pellets resuspended in 15 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM imidazole, 5 mM beta-mercaptoethanol) per 1 L culture pellet. Cells were lysed by sonication and lysates clarified by centrifugation at 40,000g for 30 min. The supernatant was filtered (0.2 μm syringe filter) and loaded onto HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 88,221) equilibrated with lysis buffer. The bound resin was washed with lysis buffer, and protein eluted in lysis buffer supplemented with 250 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was diluted 10-fold with lysis buffer before incubating overnight with recombinant His-tagged TEV protease at a 1:50 (v/v) ratio for His-tag cleavage. A subtractive nickel affinity step was used to remove the cleaved tag and TEV protease, and untagged 14-3-3γ collected in the flow-through. The protein was concentrated to <1 ml (10 kDa MWCO, Amicon) and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300, Cytiva) in SEC buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)). Fractions containing homogenous 14-3-3γ were pooled and concentrated, before aliquoting and flash freezing in liquid nitrogen (LN2) and stored at −80 °C. The expected molecular mass of the purified protein was confirmed by intact mass-spectrometry.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments for quantification of PEAK3 peptide binding to 14-3-3γ were performed using a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). Purified 14-3-3γ (untagged) was diluted to 10 μg/ml in 10 mM Na acetate pH 5.0 and amine coupled to a Xantec CMD200M sensor chip (channels 2–4) at 25 °C to final immobilization level of 5400 to 6900 RU followed by surface deactivation with 1 M ethanolamine pH 8.5. A blank activation/deactivation was used for the reference surface (channel 1). Peptide binding was performed at 25 °C in SPR running buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 0.005%(v/v) Tween-20). Synthetic PEAK3 peptides (Mimotopes) were first prepared as 10 mM stocks in water: PEAK351-74 (S69 unphosphorylated), PEAK351-74 (pS69), PEAK351-74 (S69A), PEAK351-74 (pS69, L55P), PEAK351-74 (pS69, R66P) and PEAK351-74 (pS69, R66Q). Peptide stocks were further diluted in running buffer to 20 μM and prepared as an eight-point concentration series (3-fold serial dilution, 20 μM–0 μM). Samples were injected across channels 2,3 and four in a duplicate (n = 2) multi-cycle run (flow rate 30 μl min−1, contact time of 60 s, dissociation 180 s) without regeneration. Sensorgrams were double referenced, and data fitted using a steady-state affinity model.

L55P and R66P peptides displayed aggregation at the highest concentration tested (20 μM), and as a result the 20 μM data points were excluded from analysis for these peptides. The entire experiment was repeated (n = 2) with freshly-prepared peptide dilution series. Representative steady-state binding curves are shown in Fig. S6. Fitted dissociation constant (KD) values, reported as mean ± S.E.M. (n = 6 technical replicates), for both experiments are provided in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 10.0. Comparisons between two means were evaluated for differences using ratio t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA as appropriate. Quantitative data were reported as means ± S.E.M. Most experiments were repeated at least three times, and numbers of biological replicates are indicated in each legend. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.
