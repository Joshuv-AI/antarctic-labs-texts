Nutritional contents, nutritional quality and manufacturer of ready foods in China: a cross-sectional study of 2087 food products
Authors: Tengyi Wang, Yuze Xin, Xinyan Liu, Pengpeng Ye, Shuangjie Peng, Shiwenqian Yin, Jinyu Liang, Leon Booth, Kristy Law, Kathy K Trieu, Simone Pettigrew, Jason HY Wu, Maoyi Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina’s rapid economic development and urbanisation have led to significant shifts in dietary habits, moving from home-cooked meals to ready foods. This transition may exacerbate the burden of non-communicable diseases.ObjectiveThis study aimed to (i) assess the overall nutritional quality and quantities of specific nutrients of ready foods available from Chinese online stores, (ii) evaluate whether these foods comply with national dietary recommendations, and (iii) determine whether the nutritional quality of ready foods differs across manufacturers.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed ready foods from four major Chinese online stores: Taobao, JD.com, Suning, and Vipshop. Ready foods include pre-packaged, partially processed foods requiring minimal cooking or reheating before consumption. The nutritional quality was calculated based on the amount of dietary fibre, saturated fat, protein, sugar, sodium, and energy of the product by using the Health Star Rating (HSR) scoring algorithm. Continuous and categorical variables are described using means (standard deviation [SD]) or median (inter-quartile range [IQR]) and proportions respectively.ResultsWe identified 109 419 products from the four major online stores, with 2 087 products included in analyses after conducting eligibility checks and removing duplicates. Median nutritional values for all ready foods were as follows: energy content 2203.3 kJ (IQR 2055.6–3222.5), protein content 29.2 g (IQR 14.9–51.5), fat content 35.5 g (IQR 17.0–57.4), carbohydrate content 15.5 g (IQR 5.8–45.6), and sodium content 1733.6 mg (IQR 995.7–3287.1). Only 25.3% of products met the estimated energy recommended range according to the Chinese dietary reference intakes and more than 85.6% exceeded the recommended sodium intake according to Chinese dietary guidelines. The mean HSR was 2.7 (SD = 0.9), and less than half (32.9%) were classified as healthy products (HSR ≥ 3.5).ConclusionsMany of the assessed Chinese ready foods failed to meet national dietary recommendations for nutrient composition and exhibited overall low overall nutritional quality. Potential strategies to combat the growing burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases include (i) enhancing consumer awareness about the nutritional aspects of ready foods, (ii) encouraging the food industry to reformulate products, and (iii) supporting consumers to choose healthier options.Subject terms: Risk factors, Nutrition

Introduction

The global disease burden is transitioning from communicable diseases to non-communicable diseases (NCDs), primarily due to shifts in lifestyle and dietary habits [1, 2]. Unhealthy dietary habits, marked by high intake of energy-dense foods that are low in nutrients and rich in fats, sugars, and salt, are major contributors to NCDs [1, 3]. In China, economic advancement and urbanisation have significantly altered dietary habits, resulting in a transition away from traditional home-cooked meals to more convenient options, such as takeaway foods and ready foods, many of which are now widely available through online stores [3–6]. The rise of e-commerce has further accelerated this shift by making these ready food options more accessible. This expansion of online stores offers consumers a broader selection of ready foods than traditional physical stores [7]. According to industry reports, the ready foods market size has maintained an annual growth rate of nearly 20% since 2020, reaching 516.5 billion Chinese yuan (approximately 73 billion U.S. dollars) in 2023 [8]. Moreover, the ready foods industry has recently received endorsement from the State Council, the highest administrative authority in China, to cultivate and develop the industry as part of ongoing efforts to revitalise rural areas [9].

This study uses the term “ready foods” as a functional and context-specific translation of the Chinese concept “Yu Zhi Cai (prepared food)”, which has been formally defined by Chinese governments and Chinese Cuisine Association [10, 11]. “Yu Zhi Cai” refers to pre-packaged, partially processed food products that are designed for convenient consumption with minimal cooking or reheating. These products include both complete meals and side dishes, as well as partially prepared ingredients and intermediate products. These products typically require minimal cooking or reheating before consumption, which distinguishes them from fully cooked or ready-to-eat meals. Ready foods sold in high-income countries are typically unhealthy due to being high in sodium, saturated fats, and/or added sugars [12–17]. These nutritional components are associated with elevated risk of NCDs such as hypertension, type-2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [18–22]. Moreover, ready foods often contain a low proportion of fruits and vegetables, which may result in digestive system disorders and increased risk of cancer [22, 23].

While studies have extensively evaluated the nutritional components and potential health risks of ready foods in high-income countries [11, 15, 24–27], there remains a lack of evidence regarding the nutritional quality of ready foods available in the Chinese market. This study aims to (i) assess the overall nutritional quality and quantities of specific nutrients of ready foods available from Chinese online stores, (ii) evaluate whether these foods comply with national dietary recommendations, and (iii) determine whether the nutritional quality of ready foods differs across major manufacturers.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional study that assessed ready foods available on major Chinese online stores.

Data sources

According to Statista, China’s business-to-consumer online retail market, Taobao holds the largest market share at 63.1%, followed by JD.com at 31.5%, Vipshop at 2.7%, and Suning at 1.1%, with others (including Xiaomi Youpin and Gome) making up the remaining 1.6% [28]. In this study, we selected the four biggest online retailers (Taobao, JD.com, Vipshop, and Suning) representing 98.4% of the total online market sales as the data sources for the product search.

Definition of ready food, ready dish, and ready meal

We followed the definition of ready foods provided by the China Cuisine Association, to guide the conduct of the study (Box 1) [11, 29]. We further categorised ready foods into ready dishes and ready meals, based on recently released guidelines from six Chinese ministries (Box 1) [10, 30].

Definition of ready foods:

These are pre-packaged food products made from one or more edible agricultural ingredients, processed through standardized industrial methods. Designed to simplify meal preparation and replace traditional cooking, ready foods do not include plain staple foods sold without accompanying dishes [10, 11, 29, 30].

Definition of ready dishes:

Side dishes that do not constitute a full meal on their own. They are typically served with staple foods and require minimal preparation or simple reheating. Examples include braised beef slices, stir-fried shredded potatoes or Mapo tofu.

Definition of ready meals:

Complete meals that combine staple and main components in a single package. They require minimal preparation and can be consumed after heating, offering a convenient alternative to cooking. Examples include rice boxes with braised pork or microwaveable curry with rice.

Search strategy and data collection

The search for ready foods was conducted in line with the China Cuisine Association classification of ready foods, which comprises four categories: ready-to-eat, ready-to-heat, ready-to-cook, and ready-prepared [11, 29]. The search was performed on the four major Chinese online stores noted above. The search was conducted using a set of pre-defined keywords derived from the official classification of ready foods. The primary keywords used were four categories by China Cuisine Association. In addition, related terms such as “convenience foods” (Fang Bian Shi Pin), “pre-cooked foods” (Yu Zhi Shi Pin), and “frozen ready meals” (Su Dong Shi Pin) were included to ensure a comprehensive search. All keyword searches were done using Chinese characters, with Chinese Pinyin provided in Box 2. Search results for each keyword were sorted by sales volume, and data for the top 2000 products were collected, including brand names, product names, and URLs. If the search resulted in fewer than 2 000 results, all search results were collected.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible food products that met the following criteria were included: (i) the product was classified as ready food (based on the definition above); (ii) the product was sold in the included online stores between March and April 2023; and (iii) complete nutritional information was available for the product, including energy, protein, carbohydrate, sodium, and total fat content. A product was excluded if it (i) lacked clear front or back packaging images; (ii) had an invalid URL, which prevented verification of product information; or (iii) was discontinued.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed in four phases. In phase one, one researcher (TW) removed duplicated products within each online store using the unique product identification code, assigned by the online store. In phase two, two researchers (TW and SJP) independently accessed each product’s URL and extracted data including brand name, product name, weight, price, claim information, ingredients, and nutritional composition. To ensure consistency in data extraction, the first 100 products were extracted by both researchers independently as a training session. Any discrepancies between the data extractors were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting the senior investigator (MT) to reach consensus. After the training session, the two researchers conducted the data extraction separately. All extracted data were entered into Epidata (version 3.1, Epidata Association, Denmark) and exported to Microsoft Excel (version 2023, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). In phase three, since not all products had complete barcode information, one researcher (TW) conducted further deduplication by cross-referencing brand names, product names, and nutritional information to identify and remove duplicated products across different online stores and packaging sizes. In phase four, one researcher (SJP) reviewed the online shops of the top 10 ready meals and ready dishes manufacturers to verify the representativeness of manufacturers included in the database. Any missing products identified during this phase were added to the database through purchases from the online stores.

Measurement of nutritional quality

In light of the absence of an official nutritional evaluation system for ready foods in China and the lack of a universal standard globally, the Health Star Rating (HSR) system introduced by Australia and New Zealand in 2014 was employed in this study to measure the nutritional quality of the included products. As a spectrum rating framework, the HSR system measures the overall nutritional quality of packaged foods [31]. It aims to assist consumers in making healthier choices and encourage the industry to improve product formulations. The system rates the nutritional content of foods, assigning an HSR score ranging from 0.5 (unhealthy) to 5 stars (healthiest), with increments of 0.5 stars. Previous research has applied this methodology to evaluate the nutritional quality of pre-packaged foods in China [32].

The process began with categorising each product based on the HSR system’s six packaged food categories: category 1, non-dairy beverages; category 1D, dairy beverages; category 2, foods; category 2D, dairy foods; category 3, fat oil; and category 3D high-fat cheeses [33]. In our study, all ready foods were assigned as category 2 and their HSR scores were calculated as per the HSR algorithm as follows. First, baseline points were calculated using the scoring table, taking into account the product’s energy content, saturated fat, total sugars, and sodium per 100 grams. Second, additional points were adjusted for the presence percentage of fruits, vegetables, nuts, and legumes, as well as for protein and fibre content per 100 grams (Fig. S1). Lastly, the final HSR score was determined by subtracting the modifying points from the baseline points (Fig. S1). Because key nutrients required for HSR calculation (saturated fat, total sugars, dietary fibre, and the proportion of fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes) are missing from Chinese ready food labels, we adopted a proxy-based estimation approach from previous studies [32, 34]. This approach uses nutrient values from comparable products in the Australian Food Composition Database and was applied only to products with at least two of four HSR key nutrients (saturated fat, sugars, sodium, protein) available [32]. These proxy values were utilised along with available data on energy, protein, and sodium to compute the HSR for ready-food products. An HSR score of 3.5 was used as a threshold for healthiness, with foods scoring 3.5 or above considered healthy, and vice-versa [35].

Data analysis

Quantities of specific nutrients were calculated per package. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was conducted to assess the distribution of nutrient variables. In case of non-normally distribution (p < 0.05), descriptive analyses will be conducted using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). HSR scores were summarised using means and standard deviations (SDs) for descriptive purposes, in line with reporting approaches used in previous studies employing the HSR. To evaluate whether nutrient levels met dietary recommendations, Chinese dietary reference intakes for adults aged 18–65 years (both sexes) were applied to estimate energy, total fat, carbohydrate, and protein, while the China Dietary Guidelines were used for sodium. All daily reference values were converted to per-meal equivalents based on three meals per day [36–39]. To further explore differences in nutritional quality by manufacturers, manufacturers were categorised based on the number of products included in this study. Mann–Whitney U tests were performed to compare the HSR scores between manufacturers having the leading ten number of included products and the rest of manufacturers. All analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (version 2023, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

Results

In total, 109,419 ready food products were sourced from four major online stores, including 36,085 products from Taobao, 29,230 from JD.com, 9662 from Suning, and 34,442 from Vipshop. A total of 49,438 products were removed within the same online store due to being duplicates (identified using the store-assigned unique product identification code). In addition, 27,297 products were excluded with unrecognised images of front and/or back of packages, 7282 were excluded because they were discontinued on the online stores, and 2382 did not meet the definition of ready foods. A further 20,999 products were removed as duplicates by cross-referencing brand names, product names, and nutritional information across different online stores and package sizes. Furthermore, 66 products were added from offline purchase. Ultimately, 2087 products were included in the analyses (Fig. 1).

Nutritional contents

The median weight of ready foods was 272.0 g (IQR 185.0–420.0) per package. The median energy content was 2203.3 kJ (IQR 1236.0–3553.1), the median protein content was 29.2 g (IQR 14.9–51.5), the median total fat content was 35.5 g (IQR 17.0–57.4), the median carbohydrate content was 15.5 g (IQR 5.8–45.6), the median sodium content was 1 733.6 mg (IQR 995.7–3 287.1) (Table 1).

The median weight of ready dishes and ready meals was 256.0 g (IQR 180.0–450.0) and 280.0 g (IQR 245.0–367.0) per package, respectively. Compared to ready dishes, ready meals containing staple foods and main courses had higher median energy (2 617.5 kJ [IQR 2055.6.3–3222.5]) versus 2099.9 kJ [IQR 1114.6–3 637.6]) and carbohydrate (91.9 g [IQR 72.2–109.6]) versus 12.8 g [IQR 4.8–28.2]), and a lower median protein (13.8 g [IQR 9.9–21.2] versus 33.3 g [IQR 17.5–55.7]), total fat (21.4 g [IQR 11.7–33.4] versus 38.5 g [IQR 18.4–61.2]), and sodium (1627.3 mg [IQR 1053.4–3530.0] versus 1 750.0 mg [IQR 977.1–3 260.4]).

Only 28.2% of the ready food products met the Chinese dietary reference minimum energy content recommendation. More than 85% of the ready foods exceeded the Chinese dietary guideline for sodium intake and more than 93% exceeded the protein intake recommendation according to Chinese dietary reference intakes. For energy, protein, and sodium, the proportion of ready dishes and ready meals that met the recommendations for any of these nutrients did not exceed 50%.

Nutritional quality

The average HSR for the sampled Chinese ready foods was 2.7 (SD = 0.9), with notable variation between ready meals and ready dishes. Ready meals had an average HSR of 2.7 (SD = 0.9), while ready dishes had an average HSR of 2.4 (SD = 0.9) (Fig. 2 and Table S1). Only 32.9% of all ready foods were classified as healthy using the 3.5 HSR cut-off. Compared to ready dishes, ready meals were less healthy (13.3% healthy products versus 35.7%) (Fig. 2 and Table S1).

The leading 10 manufacturers of ready dishes produced 144 products, representing over half of all included ready meals (54.6%). The average HSR of ready meals across the manufacturers spanned from 1.9 (SD = 1.1) for manufacturer A1 to 3.3 (SD = 0.3) for manufacturer A6. Discussion

This study assessed the nutritional contents and healthiness of over 2 000 ready foods sold in major Chinese online stores. The findings indicate that Chinese ready foods are not compliant with the recommended levels for sodium, energy, protein, and carbohydrates. Additionally, the HSR scores of these foods were typically low, with more than two-thirds classified as unhealthy. Further analyses revealed that products produced by the leading 10 manufacturers in the ready meals and ready dishes categories were less healthy on average compared to products produced by smaller manufacturers.

Consistent with prior similar studies assessing ready-to-eat foods conducted in high-income countries [12, 15, 26, 40], this study found that Chinese ready foods were high in sodium. This may be attributed to the widespread use of high-sodium condiments, such as soy sauce, and a cultural preference for savoury, umami flavours, as well as the use of salt as a natural preservative [41, 42]. However, unlike findings from USA, the average energy content in these foods was lower [43]. This could be due to three reasons. First, excluding high-energy staple foods from the analyses such as pizza and hamburgers (as per the definition of ready foods in China) likely resulted in lower median energy values than similar products in the USA [43]. Second, the primary cooking methods of Chinese ready foods, such as steaming and boiling, require less added fat and oil [44, 45]. In addition to these differences, there appear to be differences in nutrient content across product types within the Chinese ready food market. Ready meals tend to be higher in carbohydrate and lower in fat, with a structure broadly consistent with traditional dietary patterns [2]. However, their protein content is generally low, which may limit their nutritional adequacy if consumed as meal replacements. In contrast, ready dishes contain more fat and protein but much less carbohydrate. While these products are not usually consumed in isolation, they are often combined within meals. In the context of Chinese dietary habits, where multiple dishes are commonly served together in a meal, the simultaneous intake of several high-fat ready dishes may lead to cumulative fat consumption [45]. Over time, such patterns might contribute to a dietary shift toward higher fat intake, with potential implications for obesity, dyslipidaemia, and other chronic health conditions [46].

The HSR scores and the proportions of healthy products identified in the present study were much lower than those of similar foods reported in an Australian study where these types of products had an average HSR of 3.4 (vs 2.7 in China) and 84.6% of the products were categorised as healthy (vs 32.9% in China) [47]. This is also evident from an evaluation of the nutritional quality of packaged foods globally, showing that Chinese ready foods ranked the seventh among 12 countries [32]. One explanation for the low HSR scores of Chinese ready foods is that manufacturers often prioritise taste, market acceptance, and extended shelf life over improving nutritional quality. Although studies on Chinese ready foods are scarce, it is likely that, similar to global trends, ready foods in China often contain added sugar, salt, and fat to meet consumer demand for convenience and flavour, leading to low nutritional profile [48, 49]. Additionally, market competition may push manufacturers to reduce production costs by using cheaper, less nutritious raw materials, as has been observed in other countries [50]. While similar dynamics are plausible in China, direct empirical evidence remains scarce and warrants further investigation [51]. While these measures help reduce production expenses, they directly impact the nutritional quality of the products. However, more research is needed to confirm whether these global trends are applicable to the Chinese context and to fully understand the factors contributing to the lower nutritional quality of Chinese ready foods.

HSR scores across China’s ready food market indicate consistently low nutritional quality, pointing to broader structural issues across the industry. This may reflect a prioritisation of taste preferences over product healthfulness and limited incentives for reformulation. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated multi-layered policy responses. Global experience suggests two major strategies. Mandatory nutrition labelling requires declarations of saturated fat, added sugars, and sodium to improve transparency. China’s forthcoming standard (GB 28050–2025) is a step forward, mandating saturated fat and total sugars from 2027 [52]. Building on this progress, future revisions could enhance its effectiveness by including dietary fibre, added sugars, and the proportion of fruits, vegetables, nuts, and legumes, thereby providing consumers with comprehensive information to support healthier food choices. However, the effectiveness of back-of-pack information alone may be limited by low nutrition literacy among Chinese consumers [53]. In response, front-of-pack labelling (FoPL) has been widely implemented in many countries as a complementary strategy to enhance accessibility and clarity of nutrition information [54, 55]. Interpretive systems such as Australia’s Health Star Rating and Chile’s warning labels have helped consumers identify healthier options while also incentivising manufacturers to reformulate products [56, 57]. Although industry resistance was noted in Australia, China’s context may differ [58]. Large-scale manufacturers, particularly state-owned enterprises, are more likely to align with public health priorities and can serve as early adopters [59]. Their engagement could help establish product norms and accelerate wider improvements in ready food reformulation.

The primary strength of this study lies in its comprehensive assessment of the nutritional quality of ready foods sold in major Chinese online stores. There are several limitations that could be addressed in future research. First, the cross-sectional design resulted in a single timepoint sample that could not capture dynamic market changes. Second, despite efforts to cover a wide range of products, the complexity and diversity of China’s ready foods market means that some products with unclear front and back packaging could not be included. Third, although the included nutrition information was extracted directly from packaging images and confirmed by two independent reviewers, the assessment of the accuracy and timeliness of the nutritional information was out of the scope of the study, such information highly depends on manufacturer-declared values. Fourth, many products only reported aggregated macronutrients, e.g., total fat, carbohydrates, and protein, without detailed information related to saturated fat, added sugars or dietary fibre. This limited the opportunity to conduct robust assessment of nutritional quality. Thus, the use of proxy values to impute missing information may lead to under- or overestimation. Nevertheless, comparison with Australian products remains meaningful, as the estimation reflects overall differences in nutritional quality based on currently available nutrients in Chinese products, while comparisons with other countries should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, due to the lack of sales data, the number of distinct products was used as a proxy for market presence. While this does not reflect actual market share, manufacturers with more listed products are more likely to appear repeatedly in online searches, increasing their exposure and potential influence on consumer purchasing decisions. Finally, there is currently no unified and clearly established definition of “ready foods” across countries, which may bring certain challenges to the comparability of product nutritional characteristics between countries. Future research could consider providing more precise and standardized definitions to facilitate cross-country comparisons.

Conclusion and recommendations

Chinese ready foods, produced by a wide range of manufacturers, generally have low HSR scores and fail to meet the national dietary recommendations for key nutrients. The increased popularity of ready foods in China has sparked growing concerns over their health impacts. Despite these concerns, the fast pace of modern life makes it difficult for many to avoid such products. As addressing these challenges requires coordinated efforts at multiple levels, we recommend empowering consumers by increasing their awareness of healthy diet choices and their ability to understand nutrition panels. This will enable them to make more informed food choices. In addition, policy makers should consider the mandatary inclusion of clear information on added sugars, saturated fats, and dietary fibre, and the implementation of front-of-pack labels to guide consumers toward healthier options. Moreover, the food industry, particularly the leading manufacturers, needs to take greater corporate social responsibility by adopting industry-wide standards that focus on reducing unhealthy ingredients, and increasing transparency about nutrients and ingredients.
