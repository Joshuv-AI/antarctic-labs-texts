Effects of Pd/CeO2 on the Abatement of Unburnt Hydrocarbon Emissions from Gasoline Vehicles during the Cold-Start Period
Authors: Eunwon Lee, Jongchan Bu, Sungha Hwang, Jinu Choi, Hyeongdong Jung, Yongwoo Kim, Hyokyoung Lee, Chang Hwan Kim, Jong Suk Yoo, Do Heui Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Unburnt hydrocarbon emissions from gasoline vehicles contribute to air pollution and pose health risks, necessitating effective removal strategies. Conventional three-way catalysts (TWCs) effectively oxidize hydrocarbons to CO2 at temperatures above 250 °C. However, the increasing adoption of turbocharged gasoline engines, which emit exhaust gases at significantly lower temperatures (∼300 °C versus ∼ 800 °C in conventional vehicles), has renewed concerns over hydrocarbon emissions. The primary challenge is the prolonged warm-up time of catalytic converters, during which TWCs remain largely ineffective. To address this, we developed a dual-function Pd/CeO2 catalyst capable of retaining unburnt hydrocarbons through adsorption during cold start and promoting their oxidation at elevated temperatures. Propylene (C3H6), a representative short-chain olefin in gasoline exhaust, was used as a probe molecule to evaluate the catalyst’s hydrocarbon abatement performance under realistic conditions. Periodic density functional theory calculations were conducted to investigate and compare C3H6 oxidation pathways on pure Pd and the Pd–CeO2 interface. O2 dissociation was identified as the most kinetically hindered step on pure Pd, whereas OH* formation was the rate-limiting step at the Pd–CeO2 interface. Consequently, the Pd surface exhibits sluggish oxidation activity, whereas the interface is prone to O-poisoning, which suppresses catalytic turnover. Interestingly, the coexistence of pure Pd and Pd–CeO2 sites induces a synergistic effect mediated by intermediate spillover, which significantly enhances C3H6 oxidation. Considering the simultaneous emission of C3H6 and NO in gasoline exhaust, we also investigated their interaction and found a mutual inhibitory effect, with NO more strongly suppressing C3H6 oxidation. This suppression is linked to high NO x * coverage at the Pd–CeO2 interface, which hinders O* diffusion to the Pd surface. Our work reveals the mechanistic basis of Pd/CeO2’s dual functionality and offers a framework for designing TWCs effective at hydrocarbon abatement during cold-start in modern gasoline engines.

Introduction

Three-way catalyst (TWC) technology effectively reduces nitrogen oxides (NO x ), carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrocarbons in the exhaust gas at temperatures above 300 °C. The development of TWCs represents one of the most significant advances in automotive emission control, enabling substantial reductions in harmful pollutants over the past several decades. Despite these advancements, the mitigation of cold-start emissions continues to pose a major challenge. During vehicle start-up, catalytic converters remain at room temperature, which is insufficient for effective operation. As a result, pollutants are released without adequate after-treatment, contributing significantly to overall emissions. − Previous reports have shown that 60–80% of total hydrocarbon emissions occur during the cold-start period, while our prior work revealed that 75.9% of NO emissions are also released before the TWC reaches its light-off temperature. The challenge is further intensified by the adoption of high-efficiency engine technologies designed to lower exhaust temperatures to improve fuel economy, placing additional strain on emission control systems during start-up. While TWCs can effectively oxidize hydrocarbons above 200 °C, their activity is significantly reduced at lower temperatures. ,

To mitigate cold-start hydrocarbon emissions, hydrocarbon traps have been widely studied for diesel engines, where they capture unburnt hydrocarbons at low temperatures and release them for oxidation by DOCs once the system reaches its activation temperature. − These traps have been successfully implemented in diesel applications due to their ability to adsorb large-molecular-weight hydrocarbons, operate at low aging temperatures, and function effectively under lean conditions. ,,, Various metal ion-exchanged zeolites (e.g., MFI, BEA, FAU, SAPO-41) have been investigated as traps for light hydrocarbons. In particular, Beta and ZSM-5 have demonstrated high effectiveness for trapping hydrocarbons with six or more carbon atoms. ,

While these zeolite-based hydrocarbon traps have been successfully implemented in diesel applications, they face significant challenges in gasoline environments. Positioned upstream of the TWC and directly after the engine, zeolites encounter stability issues due to the high exhaust temperatures of gasoline engines. In addition, the lower oxygen concentration in gasoline exhaust may hinder the complete oxidation of released hydrocarbons, and the release temperature of stored hydrocarbons may be lower than the TWC light-off temperature, reducing overall efficiency. Despite these limitations, efforts to utilize zeolite-based traps for hydrocarbon management in gasoline engines have continued in recent studies, reflecting ongoing interest in their potential application during the cold-start period. −

The hydrocarbon adsorption capacities of different zeolites were simulated based on Monte Carlo (MC) method. Distinct structural characteristics of zeolitesincluding differences in topology, pore architecture, and surface areaplay a key role in determining hydrocarbon adsorption performance under cold-start conditions. For instance, due to the one-dimensional pore structure of MOR, it exhibited higher diffusivity than the three-dimensional zeolite Beta, resulting in a 0.56 mmol/g lower maximum adsorption capacity for C3H6. Moreover, a closer match between pore size and molecular dynamic diameter was found to enhance hydrocarbon adsorption capacity. Finally, multicomponent adsorption results indicated that molecules with larger masses led to higher adsorption capacities, which can be attributed to stronger intermolecular interactions with the zeolites, such as van der Waals forces. ,

However, the complex interactions among different exhaust gas pollutants (H2O, NO, CO and C3H6) further complicate the development of emission control strategies. In Pd/BEA catalysts, the presence of NO has been shown to strongly inhibit the ability of C3H6 trap, which is attributed to the competitive adsorption between NO and C3H6 on Brønsted acid sites. Another study reported that NO and C3H6 compete for Pd sites, with C3H6 exhibiting a stronger adsorption in Pd/BEA. In contrast, recent findings in Pd/SSZ-13 catalysts revealed that the coadsorption of NO and C3H6 can enhance NO storage capacity via the formation of a Pd2+(NO)­(C3H6) complex. Moreover, other gas compositions such as CO and H2O can significantly influence the adsorption and oxidation properties of Pd/BEA, leading to a loss in C2H4 uptake of 90% compared to dry conditions without CO. This is further supported by MC simulation results, which show that Fe2+-modified BEA increases C2H4 adsorption but also enhances hydrophilicity, leading to a reduced hydrocarbon adsorption capacity in the presence of H2O. , Collectively, these observations emphasize the necessity for a comprehensive understanding of competitive abatement in exhaust gas mixtures to enable the rational design of more effective emission control catalysts.

Upcoming emission regulations target the combined levels of NO x and nonmethane organic gases (NMOG), including unburnt hydrocarbons, requiring manufacturers to comply with fleet average NMOG + NO x limits of 0.30 mg/mi by 2025. , This integrated approach to emission control necessitates solutions that simultaneously address both NO x and hydrocarbon emissions, particularly during cold-start. This requires a comprehensive understanding of their interactions when designing hydrocarbon traps. Herein, C3H6 (propene, CH2CHCH3) was selected as a model hydrocarbon compound due to its prevalence as an unburnt hydrocarbon in gasoline exhaust.

Collectively, a new system capable of overcoming the shortcomings of zeolite-based hydrocarbon traps and performing effectively in gasoline environments is required. In this context, CeO2, well-known for its exceptional oxygen storage capacity in TWCs, was investigated as a promising support for cold-start emission control in gasoline vehicles. Particularly, the incorporation of platinum group metals (PGMs) has been shown to markedly enhance the surface reducibility of CeO2, potentially facilitating the formation of oxygen vacancies at lower temperatures, which may interact with target species such as NO and hydrocarbons. − Herein, Pd/CeO2 emerges as a strategic candidate for cold-start emission control in gasoline vehicles; however, despite extensive studies on its performance in NO abatement, its potential as a hydrocarbon trap under such conditions remains largely unexplored.

Our findings reveal that both Pd surface sites and Pd–CeO2 interfacial sites play crucial roles in C3H6 adsorption, while oxygen vacancies at the interface also serve as key sites for NO storage. Given the simultaneous emission of C3H6 and NO in real gasoline exhaust, we paid particular attention to their interaction, revealing a mutual inhibitory effect between C3H6 and NO abatement, with NO exerting a stronger suppressive impact on C3H6 oxidation. Interestingly, while NO inhibited the oxidation of C3H6, it did not prevent its adsorption at low temperatures, reinforcing the viability of the proposed trapping strategy. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the complete oxidation of C3H6 to CO2 and H2O is mediated by the diffusion of intermediates between Pd surface sites and Pd–CeO2 interfacial sites, highlighting the importance of their interaction. Further analysis suggested that poisoning of the interface by NO* and NO2* inhibits the diffusion of O* to the Pd surface, which is a key step to complete the catalytic cycle, thereby significantly suppressing C3H6 oxidation in the presence of NO. By elucidating the mechanism governing the complete oxidation of C3H6 and demonstrating the dual functionality of Pd/CeO2 for both hydrocarbon and NO x abatement during cold-start, this study addresses key challenges associated with increasingly stringent emission regulations for gasoline vehicles. This work offers a basis for the rational design of cold-start emission control systems capable of simultaneously mitigating multiple pollutants while maintaining thermal stability under gasoline engine conditions.

Experimental Section

Catalyst Preparation

Catalysts were prepared by impregnating PGMs on CeO2 (Rhodia) and γ-Al2O3 (Sasol) using the incipient wetness impregnation method. Aqueous solutions of Pd­(NO3)2·2H2O (Merck), Pt­(NH3)4(NO3)2 (Alfa Aesar) and Rh­(NO3)3 (10 wt % Rh in solution, Merck) were used to impregnate the desired amount of Pd, Pt and Rh, respectively. The impregnated catalyst powders were dried overnight at 105 °C and then calcined at 800 °C for 2 h under a 100 mL/min flow of 15% O2/N2. The prepared catalysts are CeO2, Pd(2)/CeO2, Pt(2)/CeO2, Pt(1)­Pd(1)/CeO2, Rh(2)/CeO2, Pd(2)/Al2O3, Pt(2)/Al2O3, and Rh(2)/Al2O3, which the number in the parentheses indicates the loading of the metal. Pd/CeO2 catalysts with various loadings (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 10 wt %) were also synthesized. Two wt % of Pd was loaded onto several Ce–Zr–O type supports (CeZrO2 mixed oxides; S1–S3), which were denoted as Pd(2)/S1–S3. Pd(2)/CeO2 and Pd(2)/S1–S3 catalysts were further aged at 800 °C for 2 h under a 100 mL/min flow of 5% H2/N2 to investigate their stability after reductive aging.

Catalyst Characterization

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas were evaluated by N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms using a BELSORP-mini II (MicrotracBEL, Japan) instrument. Prior to the measurements, the samples were pretreated at 200 °C for 4 h under vacuum conditions to remove impurities.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were taken on a powder X-ray diffractometer (Smartlab, Rigaku, Japan) with a current of 30 mA and voltage of 40 kV in the 2θ range from 10 to 90°.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) analysis was performed using an OPTIMA 8300 (PerkinElmer, USA) spectrometer.

Pulsed CO chemisorption was conducted using the BELCAT II apparatus (MicrotracBEL, Japan). In a typical procedure, 0.05 g of the sample was pretreated at 400 °C under a He flow, then cooled to 120 °C. The samples were subsequently exposed to a 5% H2/Ar flow at 120 °C for 30 min, followed by purging with He and cooling to −78 °C using a liquid nitrogen spray. CO pulses were then dosed in a He stream until the catalyst was saturated with CO. Pulsed CO chemisorption was performed at −78 °C to suppress CO adsorption on the CeO2 surface and prevent the overestimation of Pd dispersion. The stoichiometric factor, the ratio of Pd atoms to the chemisorbed CO molecules, was assumed to be 1. The number of Pd atoms exposed on the surface was considered equal to the number of chemisorbed CO molecules.

In situ diffuse reflective infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted using an online Fourier transform-infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a diffuse reflectance cell (Praying Mantis, Harrick Scientific Products Inc.) and a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The diffuse reflectance reaction chamber was mounted inside a diffuse reflectance cell and fully packed with catalyst powder. The amount of catalyst required to completely fill the chamber depended on the catalyst’s density; for Pd/CeO2, approximately 0.1 g was needed. Spectra were then collected in the range of 4000–600 cm–1 with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm–1. A total flow rate of 50 mL/min was maintained during the DRIFTS experiments. The pretreatment was performed under N2 at 400 °C for 30 min, followed by cooling to 120 °C and subsequent treatment with an O2/H2 (8:1) gas mixture for 30 min. After the pretreatment, the samples were purged with N2 for 30 min and then cooled to the desired temperature.

Evaluation of Catalytic Performance

The hydrocarbon abatement abilities of the catalysts were evaluated using a continuous-flow quartz tubular reactor. First, 0.1 g of catalyst (80–100 mesh) was loaded in the reactor with 0.1 g of α-Al2O3 (80–100 mesh) to dissipate the heat produced during the experiments. The total flow rate was maintained at 200 mL/min (GHSV = 120,000 mL/h•gcat) under atmospheric pressure, with various gas compositions (H2O, CO2, O2, CO, H2, C3H6, NO, and N2) to simulate the gasoline exhaust.

The air-to-fuel ratio (AFR) is the mass ratio of air and fuel during engine combustion. AFR can be manipulated by engine control and determines several factors, such as the flammability of the fuel mixture, amount of energy released, and composition of pollutants emitted during the combustion reaction. Typically, the stoichiometric AFR that can burn all fuel mixtures without leaving excess air is about 14.7:1 for gasoline fuel. The air-to-fuel equivalence ratio, λ, is the ratio of actual AFR to stoichiometry for a given air and fuel mixture (λ = AFR/AFRstoichiometric), determined by the composition of the exhaust gas from the engine outlet. There are several models to compare the idealized and actual AFR by calculating the molar ratio of oxygen molecules to carbon and hydrogen molecules in the exhaust gas components. A simple expression using the partial pressure of each gas used in this study is as follows: λ=[O2]+0.5[CO]+0.5[H2O]+[CO2]+0.5[NO][CO]+0.5[H2]+4.5[C3H6]+0.5[H2O]+[CO2]

The composition of gasoline exhaust was provided by Hyundai Motor Group. Typically, 5% H2O, 5% CO2, 6,000 ppm of CO, 2,000 ppm of H2, 1,000 ppm of C3H6, and N2 (as the balance) were introduced, with variations in the O2 concentration of 0.5 and 1.35%; this changed the value of λ to 0.95 and 1.05. A value of λ greater than 1 indicates a fuel-lean condition, while a value of λ lower than 1 indicates a fuel-rich condition. The concentrations of the outlet gas components passing through the gas cell were analyzed using an FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an MCT detector.

Prior to the catalytic performance evaluation, pretreatment was performed at 700 °C for 30 min with 5% O2, 5% H2O, and 5% CO2 (balanced with N2), then cooled to 120 °C. O2 was switched off and the catalyst was stabilized under the flow of H2O, CO2, and balance N2. The gas composition including 5% H2O, 5% CO2, 6,000 ppm of CO, 2,000 ppm of H2, the desired concentration of O2 for each λ, and balance N2 was introduced. After 20 min, 1000 ppm of C3H6 was injected to evaluate the C3H6 abatement ability of the catalysts. Various concentrations of NO (100, 260, 380, 500, 1000 ppm) were simultaneously introduced to investigate the relationship between NO and C3H6 abatement. 260 ppm of NO was introduced either 20 min before or after the injection of C3H6, depending on the experimental requirements. For the C3H6 oxidation light-off, the temperature was raised from 120 to 400 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min, while maintaining the gas composition identical to that of the abatement test. The schematic illustration of the experimental protocol including the pretreatment, abatement and reaction is shown in Figure S1. In addition to the C3H6 abatement test, other hydrocarbons such as 1000 ppm of CH4, 900 ppm of C3H8, 560 ppm iso-C5H12, or 500 ppm toluene (C7H8) were injected to reveal their abatement ability. The gas composition at the reactor outlet was measured, and the number of hydrocarbons abated by the catalyst was calculated over time by subtracting the hydrocarbon concentration profiles from the blank test conducted in the absence of a catalyst.

Computational Methods and Models

The Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) was used to calculate and compare the reaction energetics of Pd(111), CeO2(111), and the Pd–CeO2 interface in C3H6 and NO oxidation reactions. The Pd(111) surface was modeled with a (4 × 4) unit cell and four atomic layers, while the CeO2(111) surface used a (4 × 2) unit cell with three layers. For the Pd–CeO2 interface, a quasi-one-dimensional Pd nanowire on CeO2(111) was used as a simplified model to approximate the complex metal-oxide interface. Note that this model avoids quantum size effects and better represents larger Pd nanoparticles (≥2 nm), consistent with our experimental characterizations. Previous works have shown that the nanowire models accurately capture key experimental kinetics at metal–support interfaces. ,−

Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the projector augmented wave (PAW) , method to describe the inner cores of atoms. Electron exchange and correlation were modeled using the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA). , To account for van der Waals interactions in large molecules such as C3H6, DFT+D3 corrections were applied. For electron correlation effects in CeO2 and the Pd–CeO2 interface, the DFT+U approach was used, with a Ueff of 4.5 eV applied to cerium in CeO2. ,, A kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV and Gaussian smearing of 0.1 eV were used. A Monkhorst–Pack k-points grid of 4 × 4 × 1 was employed for Pd(111), while 2 × 4 × 1 was used for both CeO2 and the Pd–CeO2 interface. A vacuum spacing of more than 20 Å along the z-direction was maintained to avoid interactions between periodic images on all surfaces. The total energy and atomic positions were optimized with an energy convergence threshold of 10–4 eV and a force convergence threshold of 0.03 eV Å–1

All adsorbates, along with the two topmost layers of Pd(111), CeO2(111) and the Pd–CeO2 interface were relaxed, while the remaining layers were fixed to preserve bulk properties. Adsorbates on the catalyst surface were denoted by an asterisk (*). The adsorption-free energies (ΔG) of all the intermediates were referenced against gas-phase H2, C3H6, NO, and O2. ΔG was calculated as shown below: ΔG=ΔEads+ΔEZPE−TΔS⁡(T=393.15⁡K) where ΔE ads, ΔEZPE, and ΔS indicate the changes in adsorption energy, zero-point energy (ZPE), and entropy (S), respectively. The vibrational frequencies for determining ZPE and S were evaluated using the harmonic normal mode approximation. Gas entropies were either calculated with the ORCA program or obtained from the NIST database. Transition-state (TS) geometries were determined using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.

Results and Discussion

C3H6 Abatement Strategy during the Cold-Start Period

Controlling hydrocarbon emissions at low temperatures is essential for meeting the emission standards, as the majority of tailpipe hydrocarbon emissions are released within the first few minutes following an engine cold start. A recent study highlighted the importance of using laboratory-scale testing protocols that replicate the realistic conditions for gasoline TWC cold-start. In previous work, we evaluated the oxidation reactions of hydrocarbons (C3H8, iso-C5H12, and C3H6) under lean and rich gasoline engine conditions following lean-rich cycling aging. However, hydrocarbon oxidation primarily takes place at elevated temperatures, and achieving high conversion rates below 150 °C remains challenging. In this study, we propose a strategy to effectively remove hydrocarbons during the cold-start period, using C3H6 as a representative molecule to simulate the complex and dynamic mixtures of hydrocarbons in exhaust streams.

Figure demonstrates the C3H6 abatement performance of various PGM-supported catalysts (Pd/CeO2, Pt/CeO2, Pt–Pd/CeO2, Rh/CeO2, Pd/Al2O3, Pt/Al2O3, and Rh/Al2O3) under operating conditions of λ = 1.05 and 120 °C. The catalysts were exposed to full feed gas mixture containing H2O, CO2, O2, CO, H2, C3H6, and N2. At this low temperature of 120 °C, the removal of C3H6 via oxidation was inefficient, with steady-state conversion below 20%. Therefore, temporary adsorption of C3H6 on the catalyst emerges as an effective strategy for cold-start hydrocarbon removal. Although the cold-start period in gasoline vehicles is relatively short (∼20 s), C3H6 was exposed for an extended duration to evaluate the catalysts’ abatement capabilities. The initial C3H6 removal profile over the first 20 min reflects both oxidation and adsorption of C3H6 (Figure ). Total C3H6 abatement was quantified by comparing the integrated areas of C3H6 concentration over time with and without the catalyst. Adsorption capacity was estimated using the steady-state C3H6 concentration at 120 °C as a baseline (indicated by the red shaded area in Figure ).

In general, CeO2–based catalysts showed superior C3H6 abatement compared to their Al2O3–based counterparts (Figure ). For reference, the C3H6 abatement performance of pristine CeO2 was also evaluated. The enhanced performance of CeO2-based catalysts suggests that CeO2‘s remarkable oxygen storage capacity contributes to both C3H6 oxidation and adsorption. Among the tested catalysts, Pd/CeO2 exhibited the highest C3H6 abatement ability (418.7 μmol/g), whereas Pt/Al2O3 showed the lowest (9.99 μmol/g). Accordingly, Pd/CeO2 was selected for further investigation as a model catalyst. The exceptional performance of Pd/CeO2 can be attributed to the strong Pd–CeO2 interaction, − which aligns with our previous findings highlighting its critical role in NO storage (discussed further in Section ). , Upon increasing the temperature, C3H6 desorption and oxidation occurred simultaneously, resulting in a concave-upward light-off profile (Figure S2). Complete oxidation was achieved above 250 °C, confirming that C3H6 can be temporarily adsorbed during the cold-start period and subsequently removed through oxidation at elevated temperatures.

Advancements in gasoline vehicle technology have enabled precise engine control via a powertrain control module, allowing seamless adjustment of λ in exhaust gas. , Optimizing λ, which is dictated by oxygen concentration, is crucial for maximizing catalyst performance and fine-tuning engine operation. For example, integrating Pd/CeO2 catalyst with engine control has decreased NO emissions by 67.6% throughout the cold-start temperature range of gasoline vehicles. The C3H6 abatement abilities of the prepared catalysts at λ = 0.95 were evaluated over the first 20 min (Figure S3), and a quantitative comparison of abatement performance at λ = 1.05 and 0.95, representing the typical exhaustive conditions, is shown in Figure S4. Under slightly fuel-rich conditions (λ = 0.95, O2 concentration = 0.5%), the abatement abilities of most catalysts decreased compared to that at λ = 1.05 (O2 concentration = 1.35%). For instance, Pd/CeO2 achieved an abatement capacity of 272.1 μmol/g under these fuel-rich conditions. This suggests that increased O2 availability enhances C3H6 oxidation, consistent with the positive reaction order of O2 in C3H6 oxidation. , Notably, to bridge the gap between conventional lab-scale testing and actual engine conditions, we further verified the C3H6 abatement ability of the Pd/CeO2 catalyst under a more realistic cold-start condition (20 s) with a ramp rate of 45 °C/min, where λ = 1.05 showed superior performance (Figure S5).

The adsorption and oxidation of C3H6 on Pd/CeO2 were further examined by IR spectroscopy. Figure a,b display the DRIFT spectra in the C–H stretching region (3000–2700 cm–1) and fingerprint region (1800–800 cm–1) for Pd/CeO2, respectively. The spectra were obtained after exposure to 1000 ppm of C3H6 (black line), followed by the injection of 2% O2 flow (red line) at 120 °C. In the C–H stretching region (Figure a), peaks at 2846, 2882, 2934, and 2979 cm– 1 were observed, corresponding to νs(CH2), νs(CH3), νas(CH2), and νas(CH3), respectively (where νs and νas denote symmetric and asymmetric stretching modes). , When O2 was injected after C3H6 adsorption, C–H vibrational peak intensities increased, indicating the facile activation of intermediates. Additional infrared absorbance bands appeared in the fingerprint region owing to the surface adsorbed intermediates generated by C3H6 oxidation. Distinct bands observed in the range of 1566–1548 cm–1 and at 1431 cm–1 under these conditions can be assigned to νas(COO) and νs(COO) associated with carboxylate species, respectively. , The band observed near 1300 cm–1 is attributed to a C–H deformation mode (δ­(CH2)), while the feature at approximately 1372 cm–1 can be attributed to νs(C = O). , It should be noted that IR signals observed for CeO2 and Pd/Al2O3 were significantly lower than those for Pd/CeO2, consistent with the C3H6 abatement abilities presented in Figure (Figure S6).

FT-IR measurements confirmed the absence of gas-phase CO2 or CO during C3H6 exposure without O2 at 120 °C (Figure c). While some studies suggest that C3H6 oxidation on metal oxide-supported catalysts follows the Mars–van Krevelen (MvK) mechanism via lattice oxygen, , the lack of CO2 formation at low temperatures indicates that Pd/CeO2 does not follow this pathway under these conditions, in agreement with our DFT calculations (Figure S7). As shown in Figure S8, no steady consumption of C3H6 is observed beyond the first few minutes of transient adsorption, indicating that neither total oxidation to CO2 nor partial oxidation of C3H6 takes place at 120 °C without O2. However, above 200 °C, CO2 generation suggests lattice oxygen involvement in oxidation, with CO formation above 300 °C likely due to incomplete oxidation from lattice oxygen depletion. Elucidating the Active Sites for C3H6 Adsorption

As shown in Figure , both oxidation and adsorption play crucial roles in C3H6 abatement at low temperatures. Given the extremely short cold-start period in gasoline vehicles, the contribution of C3H6 adsorption becomes even more significant. To gain deeper insight into C3H6 abatement, the active sites for C3H6 adsorption on Pd/CeO2 were investigated. Pd/CeO2 catalysts with various Pd loadings of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 10 wt % were prepared, and their C3H6 abatement profiles under λ of 1.05 and 0.95 are shown in Figure S9. Regardless of Pd loading, superior C3H6 abatement was observed at λ = 1.05. As Pd loading increased, both C3H6 oxidation and adsorption improved, with abatement efficiency progressively increasing up to 5 wt %. The highest C3H6 abatement was achieved at 5 wt % Pd/CeO2, while a slight decline was observed at 10 wt %, resulting in performance comparable to that of the 3 wt % catalyst. This deviation may be attributed to the formation of larger Pd clusters at higher loadings. Figure S10 shows the total C3H6 abatement for Pd/CeO2 catalysts with different Pd loadings, along with values normalized to the total Pd content and surface Pd (estimated via low-temperature CO chemisorption and ICP-AES, detailed in Table S1). While increasing Pd loading led to a slight decrease in C3H6 abatement when normalized by total Pd, the abatement efficiency improved when normalized by surface Pd. Although Pd(5)/CeO2 exhibited the highest C3H6 abatement, optimizing noble metal utilization for practical applications requires careful consideration of factors such as cost efficiency.

The amount of C3H6 storage is plotted in Figure a for Pd/CeO2 catalysts with varying Pd loadings (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 wt %). For comparison, the C3H6 adsorption capacity of pristine CeO2 is also included. The number of exposed Pd sites, estimated from low-temperature CO chemisorption, is represented as a shaded region indicating the error range and is quantitatively compared with the amount of C3H6 adsorbed. The observed proportional increase in C3H6 adsorption with the number of surface Pd sites suggests that Pd plays a crucial role in facilitating C3H6 adsorption. Notably, at all Pd loadings, the total amount of C3H6 adsorption significantly exceeded the number of surface Pd sites, underscoring the substantial contribution of the CeO2 support. However, pristine CeO2 exhibited only minimal C3H6 adsorption (11.3 μmol/g), which aligns with the weak adsorption energy of C3H6 on CeO2. This suggests that Pd–CeO2 interactions play a pivotal role in enhancing C3H6 adsorption and abatement.

Given the significant role of Pd–CeO2 interactions in C3H6 adsorption, we investigated the correlation between the Pd–CeO2 interfacial area and C3H6 abatement efficiency. The Pd–CeO2 interfacial area was quantitatively assessed using the method described in our previous study. The strong interaction between Pd and CeO2 enhances the thermal stability of Pd/CeO2. , For instance, after calcination at 500 °C, the BET surface areas of CeO2 and Pd(2)/CeO2 were 145 and 125 m2/g, respectively. However, after calcination at 800 °C, the BET surface area of CeO2 decreases significantly to 67 m2/g, whereas Pd/CeO2 retains a higher surface area of 108 m2/g (Figure S11). As Pd loading increases from 0.5 to 5 wt %, the BET surface areas of Pd/CeO2 increase (Figure S12 and Table S1). This enhancement in thermal stability is attributed to the formation of more Pd–O–Ce bondslarger Pd–CeO2 interfacial areaat higher Pd loadings, leading to higher BET surface area retention after calcination at 800 °C. This suggests that the Pd–CeO2 interface effectively inhibits CeO2 thermal sintering. Consequently, the Pd–CeO2 interfacial area can be estimated from the difference in BET surface area before and after calcination at 800 °C. The correlation between C3H6 adsorption capacity and the Pd–CeO2 interfacial area is depicted in Figure b (black circles). The observed linear correlation strongly supports that the Pd–CeO2 interface plays a crucial role in C3H6 abatement.

We developed a strategy for C3H6 abatement during the cold-start period and validated its effectiveness under controlled conditions, where C3H6 was introduced in the absence of NO. In our recent work on Pd/CeO2-based catalysts for achieving zero NO emissions via the λ-switching strategy, we identified the Pd–CeO2 interface as the primary adsorption site for NO. Consistently, the correlation between C3H6 adsorption capacity and BET surface area followed a similar trend to that observed for NO adsorption (blue triangles in Figure b). Addressing both NO and C3H6 abatement is crucial, as these pollutants are simultaneously emitted under realistic gasoline combustion conditions. Furthermore, the anticipated implementation of combined NMOG + NO x regulations underscores the importance of tackling both emissions concurrently. However, given that NO and C3H6 share the Pd–CeO2 interface as an adsorption site, their simultaneous presence in gasoline vehicle emissions necessitates an investigation into the potential impact of NO on C3H6 abatement.

Mutual Inhibitory Effect between C3H6 And NO Abatement

To explore the relationship between the abatement of C3H6 and NO over Pd/CeO2, further study was carried out using 2 wt % Pd/CeO2 as a model catalyst. The abatement behavior was observed based on the presence or absence of each gas component or by controlling the injection sequence of C3H6 and NO under simulated full feed conditions (H2O, CO2, CO, H2, O2, and N2 balance).

In Figure a, the “without NO” condition represents the C3H6 abatement profile when 1000 ppm of C3H6 is introduced in the absence of NO, identical to the profile in Figure . In contrast, when C3H6 and NO were cointroduced (“C3H6 + NO”), the C3H6 abatement ability was significantly reduced, with the inhibitory effect becoming more pronounced as NO concentration increased. Even with the addition of a relatively small amount of NO (100 ppm), C3H6 abatement decreased substantially from 418.7 to 128.6 μmol/g, indicating that the majority of C3H6 removal was suppressed. When 1000 ppm of NO was introduced alongside C3H6, abatement further decreased to just 18.9 μmol/g, effectively eliminating C3H6 removal. Notably, under these conditions, the presence of NO in the feed completely suppressed the steady-state C3H6 abatement, suggesting that C3H6 oxidation was largely inhibited, leaving adsorption as the primary mechanism for C3H6 removal at low temperatures in the presence of NO. Given that NO and C3H6 are simultaneously emitted in gasoline vehicle exhaust and that the cold-start period is brief (∼20 s), these findings emphasize the critical role of transient C3H6 adsorption as an effective strategy for its abatement in the initial minutes of operation.

Variations in NO abatement were also examined when NO and C3H6 were introduced simultaneously. The “without C3H6” condition in Figure b represents the NO abatement profile measured under simulated full feed conditions with 260 ppm of NO in the absence of C3H6. While the presence of C3H6 (denoted as “NO + C3H6”) did lead to a slight diminishment in the NO abatement from 158.8 to 134.8 μmol/g, the inhibitory effect was not deemed significant. This confirms that the NO abatement strategy devised in our previous study remains effective regardless of the presence or absence of C3H6. Although the extent of the mutual inhibitory effect between C3H6 and NO varies, the results reinforce the notion that they share the same adsorption sites (Figure ), with competitive adsorption being more detrimental to C3H6 than to NO.

To further investigate the abatement behaviors of NO and C3H6, control experiments were conducted by preadsorbing each gas component for 20 min, followed by the introduction of the other gas component (denoted as “NO preadsorbed” and “C3H6 preadsorbed” in Figure c,d, respectively). Under these conditions, the mutual inhibitory effect between NO and C3H6 was significantly more pronounced than when both gas components were introduced simultaneously. Compared to the independent presence of each gas, NO abatement decreased from 158.8 to 100.9 μmol/g, while C3H6 completely lost its abatement ability following the preadsorption of NO. This suggests that NO preadsorption shifts the light-off temperature of C3H6 oxidation to higher values, thereby suppressing oxidation at 120 °C. This observation aligns well with previous studies showing the inhibitory effect of NO preadsorption on the light-off of CO and C3H6 oxidation under diesel exhaust conditions. It should be noted that, in addition to inhibiting C3H6 oxidation, NO preadsorption entirely suppressed its adsorption, further emphasizing the competitive interaction between the two species at the Pd–CeO2 interface.

Investigation of the C3H6 Abatement Mechanism

Previous studies have shown that Pd and CeO2 exist in a reduced state under working conditions (λ = 1.05) with both H2 and O2 present. Metallic Pd promotes CeO2 surface reduction by supplying H* via H2 activation, forming oxygen vacancies (Vos) at the Pd–CeO2 interface. While the oxygen vacancy formation is found to be highly feasible at the Pd–CeO2 interface and formation of the first vacancy is thermodynamically favorable, DFT calculations indicate that the Pd–CeO2 interface may contain up to 50% Vo concentration at T = 393 K, due to the increasing difficulty of H spillover from Pd to CeO2 with higher Vo concentration, as shown in Figure S13. Based on these findings, we adopted a slab model representing Pd–CeO2 interface with 50% Vo concentration, denoted as “Interface 50”, to predict the C3H6 abatement activity of Pd/CeO2.

Three different catalyst regions were considered to identify the active sites of Pd/CeO2 for C3H6 abatement: Pd(111), CeO2(111), and Interface 50. At T = 393 K, C3H6 adsorption is thermodynamically favorable on Pd(111) and Interface 50, with ΔGads = – 0.53 and – 0.61 eV, respectively, but unfavorable on CeO2(111) (ΔGads = 0.86 eV), as shown in Table and S2. This suggests that CeO2 contributes marginally to C3H6 abatement via transient adsorption due to its moderately high reaction energy and negligible activation barrier (Supplementary Discussion II). However, its adsorption activity is considerably lower than that of the Pd surface and the Pd–CeO2 interface, consistent with our experiments in Figure . To better understand the complex NO and C3H6 abatement over Pd/CeO2 beyond adsorption behavior, DFT calculations were performed to investigate the C3H6 oxidation mechanism on Pd(111) and Interface 50.

We present the calculated energetics of C3H6 oxidation to CO2 and H2O (C3H6 + 9/2O2 → 3 CO2 + 3H2O), divided into four stages: C3H6 to acrylate (Figure a), acrylate to CO* (Figure b), CO* to CO2 (Figure c), and H2O formation (Figure d). On Pd(111), the reaction is limited by O2 dissociation with a high free-energy barrier of 1.21 eV, resulting in low oxidation activity. C3H6 abatement can still occur via dehydrogenation (CH2CHCH3 → CH2CHCH3* → CH2CHCH2* + H*), without involving O*. In contrast, Interface 50 has a lower O2 dissociation barrier of 0.58 eV, and the reaction is likely limited by O* insertion into CH2CHCO* to form acrylate, with a barrier of 1.04 eV, leading to slightly enhanced oxidation activity. However, Interface 50 is prone to O*-poisoning, as the barrier for OH* formation from O* is 1.11 eV.

Although O* is readily formed and tends to accumulate at the interface, the adsorption energy of O* on Pd(111) is 0.33 eV lower than at Interface 50, facilitating the diffusion of O* from the interface to the Pd surface (Table S2). This indicates that when Pd(111) and Interface 50 coexist, C3H6 oxidation can be more efficient, not only on the Pd surface, where O* is easily supplied from the interface, but also at the interface, where O*-poisoning is alleviated. In contrast, the adsorption energy of OH* at Interface 50 is 1.32 eV more stable than on Pd(111), promoting the diffusion of OH* from the Pd surface to the interface (Table S2). However, OH* accumulation at the interface does not pose a problem, as the barrier for H2O formation from OH* is relatively low, with a value of 0.61 eV.

Based on the calculated reaction energetics for Pd(111) and Interface 50, the energetics for Pd/CeO2, where both surfaces coexist, can be approximated by assuming free diffusion of shared intermediates between the two surfaces, as shown by the green dashed lines in Figure . Consequently, different stages of C3H6 oxidation occur on distinct parts of the Pd/CeO2 catalyst: O2 dissociation occurs at the Pd–CeO2 interface, C3H6 is converted to acrolein on the Pd surface, while acrolein undergoes further conversion to acrylate, which subsequently decomposes into CO* at the interface. This CO* reacts with O* (rather than lattice oxygen via the MvK–mechanism, as supported by Figure c) to form CO2 at the interface. OH* forms on Pd, while H2O is produced at the interface. In summary, the coexistence of a pure Pd-surface and a Pd–CeO2 interface plays a crucial role in inducing a synergistic effect, significantly enhancing C3H6 abatement through oxidation reactions.

Discussion

As shown in the free-energy diagrams for NO oxidation in Figure S17, NO is readily oxidized to NO2* at Interface 50 (NO* + O* → NO2*). At Interface 50, the O2 dissociation and NO2* formation barriers are energetically favorable by 0.63 and 0.56 eV, respectively, compared to the Pd(111) surface (Figure S17). The desorption energy of NO2* at Interface 50 is 0.56 eV higher than on Pd(111), which is attributed to the difference in the adsorption configurations of NO2* on the two surfaces. On Pd(111), NO2* adsorbs in a monodentate configuration via the N atom, while on Interface 50, it adopts a bidentate structure by binding to two oxygen atoms (Figure S14). Consequently, NO is expected to remain as NO2* on the Pd–CeO2 interface, rather than desorbing as NO2. This is consistent with our experimental results, where NO2 was not detected as a final product. Thus, it is anticipated that NO* and NO2* may accumulate at the interface when NO is present in the feed.

Table compares the adsorption energies of various adsorbates on Pd(111) and Interface 50. The adsorption energies of NO* and NO2* at Interface 50 are −1.97 and −1.30 eV, respectively, indicating that they adsorb more strongly to the Pd–CeO2 interface than C3H6*0.61 eV). This suggests that NO* will likely cover the interface more extensively than C3H6* under conditions where both NO and C3H6 are present. This poisoning effect likely suppresses the diffusion of O* from the interface to the Pd(111) surface, inhibiting the full oxidation of C3H6. As a result, C3H6 can only adsorb in small amounts on the Pd surface or interface, suppressing steady oxidation. This mechanism accounts for the mutual inhibitory effect between C3H6 and NO, which is particularly detrimental to C3H6, as shown in Figure . The DRIFTS spectra measured under simple feed conditions further support the model that NO accumulated on Pd/CeO2 suppresses C3H6 abatement (Figure S18). The behavior of C3H6 abatement on Pd/CeO2, both with and without NO, is illustrated in Scheme . When C3H6 is preadsorbed (Figure d), C3H6 oxidation intermediates may partially occupy the interface, hindering NO adsorption. However, when NO is preadsorbed (Figure c), its strong adsorption not only poisons the interface but also preoccupies the Pd(111) surface, completely inhibiting the adsorption and oxidation of C3H6.

The ability of the Pd/CeO2 catalyst to abate other unburnt hydrocarbons, including CH4, C3H8, iso-C5H12 and toluene (C7H8), is evaluated and presented in Figure S19. The oxygen concentration was fixed at 1.35% (λ = 1.05), with hydrocarbon concentrations adjusted to maintain consistent λ values under slightly lean conditions (900 ppm of C3H8, 560 ppm iso-C5H12, 500 ppm toluene, and 1000 ppm of CH4). The Pd/CeO2 catalyst exhibited no abatement for CH4 and C3H8, a minor abatement effect for iso-C5H12, and significant transient adsorption, rather than oxidation, for toluene (60.4 μmol/g, Table S4) at 120 °C. These results indicate that Pd/CeO2 is more effective in adsorbing unsaturated hydrocarbons, due to the involvement of Π-bonding. However, a notable difference was observed in the simultaneous abatement of NO and hydrocarbons: while C3H6 oxidation was largely suppressed, adsorption of toluene was significantly inhibited under the same conditions.

Finally, due to the nature of trapping catalysts, which are typically positioned directly after the engine, thermal stability is a critical factor for their practical application. In this regard, while CeO2 supports exhibit excellent oxygen storage capacity and pollutant abatement capabilities, their practical use is limited. We observed that the introduction of Ce–Zr–O mixed oxides (supports S1–S3) improved both the physical properties and C3H6 oxidation activity compared to CeO2. Pd/Ce–Zr–O catalysts, particularly Pd/S1, showed stronger resistance to reductive aging at 800 °C and enhanced C3H6 abatement activity (Supplementary Discussion III, Figure S20). Nevertheless, Pd/CeO2 was selected as a model catalyst in this study to gain mechanistic insights into C3H6 abatement at the Pd–CeO2 interface, which can then be reasonably extended to more thermally stable Pd/Ce–Zr–O formulations under practical operating conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of Pd/CeO2 as an effective hydrocarbon trap for mitigating cold-start emissions in gasoline vehicles. By combining hydrocarbon adsorption at low temperatures (120 °C) with catalytic oxidation at higher temperatures, we propose a strategy to overcome the limitations of conventional three-way catalysts, which are ineffective during early engine operation. Our findings reveal that both Pd and the Pd–CeO2 interface play key roles in C3H6 adsorption and oxidation, with oxygen vacancies at the interface contributing to NO storage. The concurrent presence of NO and C3H6 caused mutual inhibition, with NO suppressing C3H6 oxidation. Density functional theory calculations show that this suppression results from NO* and NO2*-induced poisoning of the interface, hindering O* diffusion, and disrupting the catalytic cycle. These insights into low-temperature hydrocarbon abatement highlight the importance of interfacial interactions in emission control. With growing regulatory demands for combined NO x and hydrocarbon reduction, the Pd/CeO2 system offers a promising alternative to traditional hydrocarbon traps, improving performance under realistic gasoline exhaust conditions. This work advances emission control strategies and lays the groundwork for catalysts capable of simultaneously addressing NO x and hydrocarbon emissions during the cold-start phase.
