Re-engineering a machine learning phenotype to adapt to the changing COVID-19 landscape: a machine learning modelling study from the N3C and RECOVER consortia
Authors: Miles Crosskey, Tomas McIntee, Sandy Preiss, Daniel Brannock, John M Baratta, Yun Jae Yoo, Emily Hadley, Frank Blanceró, Robert Chew, Johanna Loomba, Abhishek Bhatia, Christopher G Chute, Melissa Haendel, Richard Moffitt, Emily R Pfaff

ABSTRACT

Introduction

The electronic health record is a rich source of data to study post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection, or long COVID, resulting in findings around common symptoms,1 risk factors,2 subphenotypes,3,4 and longitudinal trajectories.5 However, there are challenges in using electronic health records to study this condition including missing data, idiosyncratic coding practices, and selection bias.6 These challenges should not prevent use of electronic health records as a tool for long COVID research, but require investigators to be thoughtful about methods and caveats to ensure utility, rigour, and reproducibility.

Much has changed during the past 3 years of COVID-19. With widely available home testing, electronic health records miss many cases; the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention mandate to collect and report counts of national COVID-19 cases has ended7; and a high number of US residents with at least one (and often multiple) SARS-CoV-2 infection causes difficulty in identifying control populations within clinical data.8 For these reasons, the same analytical methods that worked earlier in the pandemic cannot be relied on to research COVID-19 or long COVID, and instead these methods need to adapt to the changing landscape.

Accurate identification of long COVID cases in electronic health records remains challenging. In 2021, we used the National COVID Cohort Collaborative (N3C)9 as part of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) RECOVER Initiative to develop a machine learning pipeline (LCM 1·0) to identify patients with a high probability of having long COVID.10 The machine learning model uses information from electronic health record data to predict missing long COVID labels, thus serving as a computable phenotype for long COVID. The study showed that the model was performant and generalisable,11 but it heavily relied on the existence and timing of an index date for a patient’s acute COVID-19 infection. Moreover, the model only considered a patient’s first SARS-CoV-2 infection, as we did not anticipate the now common occurrence of SARS-CoV-2 reinfections.12

Here, we describe the substantial re-engineering of our machine learning-driven long COVID phenotype to be more inclusive and attuned to the years of the COVID-19 pandemic after 2022. By eschewing the COVID-19 index date as an anchor point for analysis, we aimed to assess the probability of long COVID among patients who might have tested at home, or with suspected (but untested) cases of COVID-19, or multiple SARS-CoV-2 reinfections. This modelling study was an exercise for maintaining and updating any machine learning pipeline used for clinical research and operations. As clinical knowledge and circumstances change over time, so should our methods, even when major re-engineering is required.

Methods

Study design

This machine learning modelling study was performed using the N3C data repository. N3C is a data and analytical environment sponsored by the NIH National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS), which compiles and harmonises longitudinal electronic health record data from more than 80 sites in the USA and over 22 million patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 infection or who are demographically matched controls who tested negative for SARS-CoV-2 infection (and have never tested positive) to support comparative studies. The N3C infrastructure,9 data quality procedures,13 and governance processes14 have been described in previous publications. This study used N3C data, version 141, which was released in September, 2023. This study was declared exempt by the Institutional Review Board at University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (IRB number 21–0309).

Training data selection

In our original model, without a gold standard to use as a training set, we defined a silver standard of patients who had visited a long COVID specialty clinic at least once. In October, 2021, the U09·9 ICD-10-CM code (post-COVID-19 condition, unspecified) was made available for clinical use, and we updated our silver standard to include patients who had at least one U09·9 code in their electronic health record. This inclusion increased the size of our training set, but inconsistent and idiosyncratic use of that code in practice6 probably introduced some noise.

For our new model (LCM 2·0), we leveraged the increased amount of available longitudinal data to require two or more U09·9 codes or two or more long COVID specialty clinic visits to qualify for the training set. Although we also tested the requirement of one code or visit, because the two-code requirement resulted in more than 30 000 patients for training and testing, we chose the more stringent criteria to decrease the risk of labelling a patient with the long COVID code due to error or rule-out. Patients included in the training set were selected from 5 357 122 patients aged 18–99 years who tested positive for COVID-19. The training set contained 36 238 patients with a long COVID label (per the aforementioned definition) and 36 507 patients without a long COVID label who were randomly selected from the population of patients in the N3C cohort (see Running the model section). There was no selection process for patients with a label between the training and validation sets; all patients with a long COVID label in the N3C repository were used. To sample patients without a label, we began with the full population set of patient windows in which the patient was aged 18–99 years in the given window, removed from this set all entries with a patient identification matching patients in the training set (ie, patients with a label), and randomly sampled the set of remaining patient windows for unlabelled patient–window pairs. The labelled set was approximately three orders of magnitude smaller than the unlabelled set before selection.

The validation set contained 1890 patients with a long COVID label and 1899 without. No patients from the training set appeared in the validation set. Each patient who was potentially eligible for the training set had a 5% chance of being randomly assigned to the validation set instead. Summary statistics for the training set, validation set, and full population are available in the appendix (pp 4–5).

Time windows

LCM 1·0 used each patient’s first acute COVID-19 infection date as an anchor point, basing its predictions on data sourced from a specified number of days before and after that date. The 90 days surrounding the acute infection (45 days before and 45 days after) were censored from data gathering. Reinfections were not considered. LCM 2·0 does not require an acute COVID-19 date. Therefore, we developed a new anchoring method using set time windows that applied to all patients, not only those with COVID-19 index dates. LCM 2·0 gathers data for each patient in overlapping 100-day periods that progress through time and issues a probability of long COVID for each 100-day period. If a patient had known acute COVID-19 during any 100-day window (including reinfections), we censored the data from 7 days before the diagnosis or positive test date to 28 days after. This censoring prevents the model from confusing the symptoms of acute COVID-19 for long COVID. However, because the time windows overlap, we captured patients’ evolving health status (and changing model score) over time. A low probability in each window does not invalidate a high probability in an earlier window. Once a patient passed the probability threshold, they were categorised as having long COVID going forward.

We explored censoring a period of 12 weeks; however, 43% of the patients with at least two U09·9 codes in the N3C data had that code applied earlier than 12 weeks from their evidence of having acute COVID-19. Within this group, the median time from acute COVID-19 to first U09·9 was 26 days. As such, a 12-week blackout period would essentially invalidate nearly half of the long COVID cases as diagnosed by clinicians. Although this does not align with the standard 12-week guidance for when long COVID begins, there was more benefit in aligning with how the code is used in clinical practice.

Patients with no new data in each window were still given a score for that window. This window was predicted to have a very low score, as it would only be based on static features like binned age and sex. A comparison between the anchoring methods of LCM 1·0 and 2·0 is illustrated in figure 1.

Feature selection

N3C uses the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) clinical data model that uses Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED) as a standard vocabulary for diagnoses. However, using every unique SNOMED concept as a model feature would result in thousands of features and major computational inefficiency, and increased granularity that could obscure important patterns. For these reasons, we rolled up more granular SNOMED concepts to parent concepts, cutting down the initial number of possible diagnosis features from 50 349 (ie, the number of unique codes across the population described in the Running the model section) to 9623. As SNOMED is multi-hierarchical (ie, a child concept can have many parent concepts) and the depth of its leaf nodes is highly variable, we needed to develop an algorithm to select optimal parent concepts using consistent logic (appendix pp 1–2).

To improve efficiency and avoid overfitting, we further pruned this list of features by assessing the covariance of each selected parent concept with the label from the training set: 1 for long COVID and 0 for not long COVID. We selected the top 200 concepts with the highest covariance to serve as the model’s diagnosis features. We excluded three features that were essentially COVID-19 and long COVID labels (ie, the SNOMED equivalents of the ICD-10 codes U07·1 [COVID-19], U09·9 [post-COVID-19 condition], and B94·8 [sequelae of infectious disease, used as a proxy for U09·9 before its release]).

Additional features included patient age, number of unique outpatient days, number of unique inpatient days, and patient sex. Continuous variables were binned to reduce overfitting. Age was binned into 10-year bins, whereas the number of visits was binned into categories of 0–2, 3–10, and ≥11.

Running the model

The model was constructed using the XGBoost Python package. After training the model, we ran the model on a subset of patients in the N3C cohort (n=5 875 065) who met at least one of the following criteria from Jan 1, 2020, to 22 June, 2023: a U07·1 (COVID-19) diagnosis code; a positive SARS-CoV-2 test; a U09·9 (post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection) diagnosis code; a prescription for nirmatrelvir–ritonavir or remdesivir; or an M35·81 (multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children [MIS-C]) diagnosis code. Each patient was given a model score that predicted post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection status for each 100-day window in which they were aged ≥18 years.

Although the model was no longer anchored to a COVID-19 index date, to limit false positives in the N3C use case, we ran the model on patients who had some electronic health record evidence of COVID-19 (ie, met one or more of the listed criteria).

We aimed to not only determine whether a patient was likely to have long COVID, but also whether a patient was likely to have long COVID in a specific time window. We defined the correct window for a patient with a label as the latest window that contained a patient’s first U09·9 code or long COVID clinic visit. We randomly selected correct windows for patients without labels. We considered the model to be correct when a patient with a label had a score above the 0·9 threshold in this window, or a patient without a label had a score below the threshold (see Threshold selection).

Threshold selection

Choosing an appropriate threshold is crucial for effective use of algorithms, particularly when the objective is to obtain a binary decision rather than a continuous confidence score. The threshold serves as the mechanism through which false positive errors are weighed against false negative errors.

Several approaches for selecting a threshold are available, and there is no single method that is generalisable for all use cases.15 One common way to select a threshold is to maximise the Youden Index, which maximises the true positive rate minus the false positive rate to achieve the percentage correct. This analysis was done on the validation set.

Historical controls and false positives

Long COVID has many overlapping symptoms with other conditions such as myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome,16 other post-viral syndromes (eg, sequelae triggered by influenza),17 dysautonomia,18,19 and various respiratory illnesses. In many cases, the data available are not detailed enough to distinguish between these conditions and long COVID. There are probably many undiagnosed patients in the training set who have long COVID, making it difficult to measure a true false positive rate.

To better understand the false positive rate, we leveraged the same RECOVER analysis cohort described in the Running the model section but swapped in the cohort’s electronic health record data from 2018 to 2019, before the existence of COVID-19. 5 572 579 patients in the RECOVER analysis cohort had data available for this purpose and met the minimum age criterion during the earlier period. We compared the distribution of model scores for 2018–19 to those of 2020–23, theorising that the proportion of patients with high scores in 2019 could serve as a rough estimate of the false positive rate in the COVID period. This historical control experiment, in which pre-2020 patients serve as controls for patients in 2020 and later, is a richer source of evidence of false positivity than the default option of declaring all patients with high scores but no long COVID label as false positives. Patients without labels might have a false negative result with undiagnosed long COVID—a known challenge in assessing performance of diagnostic machine learning models.20

Results

For LCM 2·0, the maximisation of percentage correct is achieved at a threshold of 0·9, resulting in a false positive rate of 0·149 and a true positive rate of 0·843 in the validation set (figure 2).

The distribution of model scores across the full RECOVER cohort (n=5 875 065) is shown in figure 3. Using the 0·9 threshold, the prevalence of long COVID was 17·9% in this COVID-enriched population. However, using the historical control experiment, we estimated the proportion of these cases that could have been false positives and change our prevalence estimate. Using the 0·9 threshold with historical controls, we calculated the false positive rate of 7·5%. This was calculated using the difference between 0 (the long COVID prevalence in the pre-COVID era) and 7·5% (the proportion of historical controls with a model score ≥0·9). Subtracting from 17·9% resulted in an estimated prevalence of patients with long COVID of 10·4% (n=1 051 045).

The receiver operating characteristic curve for the model was calculated on a person basis and window basis (figure 4). The person basis used a patient’s maximum model score over all time to classify them (ie, has this patient ever had long COVID?). The 0·9 threshold was selected to purposefully maximise true positives and minimise false positives. For this reason, we sacrificed recall to maximise precision. Alternative thresholds resulted in different balances between precision and recall and could be adjusted as desired for a specific use case.

We also performed a chart review of 50 patients sourced from a single site to further assess model accuracy. A description of the chart review and its results is available in the appendix (pp 2–3, 5). Given the described limitations of this review, the results cannot be considered true performance metrics but are still informative.

Discussion

Our updated long COVID machine learning computable phenotype re-engineered previous work to adapt to the changing landscape of COVID-19. LCM 2·0 performed similarly to LCM 1·0 while removing dependency on the date of acute COVID-19 infection as a feature and censoring data from known reinfections.

Ideal uses for this model include identifying potential participants for clinical trials and defining cohorts for secondary data analyses. Defining cohorts for trial target emulations are of particular interest in long COVID research as the search for potential treatments and preventive measures continues. Target trial emulations that have used our model defined the trial cohort using the model and ran a sensitivity analysis using patients identified by a U09·9 diagnosis code, regardless of model score.21 We believe this method results in robust reproducible findings and is a template for future work.

From an informatics perspective, these findings exemplify the benefits of continually re-evaluating analytical artifacts (eg, data pipelines, predictive models, and risk-scoring tools) as time passes and scientific knowledge evolves.

A key test for the generalisability of any machine learning model is its explainability, or whether the features it relies on the most pass the common sense test. Models that rely on unusual features to make decisions might latch onto idiosyncratic patterns in the training data, which would not necessarily translate to external datasets. Our top model features include pulmonary, cardiac, and neurological symptoms that are well supported by long COVID literature. Non-symptom-related, continuous variables such as rates of health-care use are binned to avoid overfitting and over-reliance on patterns unique to the N3C population. As was true of the previous version,11 we believe this new model version will be translatable to other sites and consortia with OMOP implementations to promote reuse and reproducibility.

As home COVID-19 testing is (and will continue to be) commonplace, we thought it was important to re-engineer our long COVID model to work without specific electronic health record documentation of acute COVID-19. This change also allows us to account for patients without a COVID-19 label in early-to-mid 2020, when tests were scarce and the U07·1 diagnosis code had not yet been released, and patients who had COVID had not sought care for it. In general, this inclusivity is desirable. Leaving these populations out of data-driven COVID-19 and long COVID research might result in selection bias towards patients who have worse symptoms, have greater access to specialty care, or had more severe COVID-19.

However, there is higher risk of false positives with increased sensitivity. Our 0·9 threshold was selected to minimise these false positives, although, at the expense of high recall. Regardless, as shown in our historical control experiment, there is a population of patients who match the long COVID symptom profile but do not have long COVID. Long COVID symptoms are numerous and affect multiple body systems, leading to unavoidable overlap with many other diseases. For this reason, downstream researchers using these model results might wish to consider imposing additional inclusion criteria as desired (eg, to require documentation of a positive SARS-CoV-2 test in addition to a model score above the threshold), particularly for research use cases requiring higher specificity. Applying additional criteria downstream of the model, as opposed to before running the model, enables maximal flexibility.

With no consensus on a clinical definition of long COVID,22,23 assessment of the true accuracy of algorithms like ours is challenging. The existence of long COVID-like post-viral effects from other viruses might mean our model is capturing not only long COVID, but also other post-viral sequelae. Moreover, the absence of a consistent estimate for the prevalence of long COVID23,24 makes it difficult to determine an appropriate model threshold using real-world evidence. Assessing performance using the U09·9 label as ground truth is the most readily available option, but the inconsistency of the code’s use by providers and its late availability4 mean that many patients with long COVID do not have a long COVID label. Our training set might also have inherent biases, as patients with the long COVID label might be more likely to have greater access to health care than those without the label. As with any machine learning phenotype, model output should be used thoughtfully, and additional selection criteria should be imposed for use cases where specificity is paramount.

Many long COVID symptoms (eg, post-exertional malaise and brain fog) are not well represented in the electronic health record because they do not have a discrete ICD-10-CM code or they are more commonly referenced in free-text clinical notes than in structured data. Therefore, these features do not have the opportunity to contribute to the model. However, this should not be interpreted to mean that these symptoms are unreported or that patients do not have these symptoms.

Missingness can also be difficult to quantify in electronic health record data. Because electronic health record data are real-world data rather than collected for research, there is no way of knowing whether there should have been an observation when one did not occur. As such, we probably cannot quantify or describe the degree of missing information or know how missingness affects model performance.

As machine learning is increasingly used as a computable phenotyping tool, it is incumbent on its users to ensure that models are not only created but maintained and updated over time. As an example of this principle, the landscape of COVID-19 and long COVID has changed substantially over the course of the pandemic, and so too should our model. We re-engineered our machine learning long COVID phenotype to respond to these changes, solving some challenges from the previous model (not accounting for reinfection, requiring an acute COVID-19 index date), and encountered new ones (increased risk of false positives). We believe this new model is generalisable for use as a foundation of data-driven long COVID research.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

There have been several efforts in the past 4 years to create and use computable phenotypes for long COVID. These phenotypes enable researchers to identify cohorts of patients with long COVID in electronic health records or claims data for data-driven research or trial recruitment. The existing phenotypes are heterogeneous; there is no consensus definition for long COVID and thus no consensus computable phenotyping method. Some phenotypes use individual symptoms or symptom clusters in the post-COVID-19 period to characterise long COVID, whereas others, including our previous work, use various machine learning methods to assess patients’ similarity to known patients with long COVID. As all such phenotypes depend entirely on the data available to create and use them, there is a need for phenotypes created early in the pandemic (ie, 2020–21) to adapt to the data environment of the latter pandemic (ie, 2024–25). No formal literature review was done.

Added value of this study

Electronic health record data are an important resource supporting COVID-19 and long COVID research. However, the context and availability of those data have changed over the course of the pandemic, with (1) more missingness in later years, due in large part to home testing; (2) the appearance of multiple reinfections for some patients, over long periods of time; and (3) increasing uptake and use of the U09·9 ICD-10 code (ie, post-COVID-19 condition, unspecified). These changes necessitate re-engineering computable phenotypes for long COVID developed earlier in the pandemic to maintain accuracy and generalisability.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our updated long COVID machine learning computable phenotype re-engineers previous work to adapt to this changing data landscape. The new version performs similarly to its predecessor while removing the dependency on the date of acute COVID-19 infection as a feature and censoring data from known reinfections. Increased use of U09·9 resulted in a much larger training set than the previous model and improved the alignment of the model’s most important features with commonly reported symptoms and manifestations of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection. We believe this new model is generalisable for use as a foundation of data-driven long COVID research in the current era of the pandemic.

Data sharing

The National COVID Cohort Collaborative (N3C) data transfer to National Institutes of Health (NIH) National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) is performed under a Johns Hopkins University reliance protocol (IRB00249128) or individual site agreements with the NIH. The N3C Data Enclave is managed under the authority of the NIH; more information can be found at ncats.nih.gov/n3c/resources. Enclave data is protected and can be accessed for COVID-19-related research with an institutional review board-approved protocol and data use request. The Data Use Request identification for this study is RP-5677B5. Enclave and data access instructions can be found at https://covid.cd2h.org/for-researchers. All code used to produce the analyses in this manuscript, including the machine learning model discussed, is available within the N3C Data Enclave to users with valid login credentials to support reproducibility.
