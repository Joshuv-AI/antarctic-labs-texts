Two-Stage Regulation of trans-Pterostilbene Biosynthesis in Blueberry Leaf-Derived Cell Cultures
Authors: Tae Eun Kim, Jaeho Pyee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Trans-pterostilbene (t-Pt), a methylated derivative of trans-resveratrol (t-R), exhibits potent pharmacological properties but accumulates minimally in plants, limiting its practical applications. This study establishes a proof-of-concept in vitro system for t-Pt production using blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) leaf-derived callus cultures. Calli were induced on a solid medium containing 0.5 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.2 mg/L BA, with red calli (RC) demonstrating superior stilbenoid accumulation. Suspension cultures of RC in liquid medium supplemented with 0.2 mg/L 2,4-D yielded about 89-fold higher t-Pt levels than control leaves without UV treatment. Furthermore, UV-A and UV-C treatments (600 J/m2, followed by a 7-day recovery) produced modest increases in ROMT1 expression: in leaves, ∼1.06-fold with UV-A and ∼1.10-fold with UV-C; in calli, ∼1.17-fold with UV-A and ∼1.26-fold with UV-C, with only UV-C reaching statistical significance. These transcriptional changes at a stabilized 7-day time point are consistent with a role for resveratrol O-methyltransferase 1 (ROMT1) in supporting t-Pt biosynthesis. Although conducted as separate experiments, the combined results from RC suspension cultures and UV-C elicitation demonstrate a two-stage proof-of-concept processRC induction followed by UV-C treatment, as a residue-free, controlled in vitro approach for enhancing t-Pt biosynthetic capacity.

Introduction

Stilbenoids are phytoalexins found in a limited number of edible plant species, including blueberries (Vaccinium spp.), grapes (Vitis spp.), and peanuts (Arachis spp.), and are distributed across various plant tissues, such as fruits, leaves, and roots. , They are synthesized via a specialized branch of the phenylpropanoid pathway that competes with flavonoid biosynthesis for precursors. −

Although trans-resveratrol (t-R) has been widely studied for its biological benefits, including antitumor, antiviral, and neuroprotective effects, its poor bioavailability and rapid metabolism limit practical applications. , In contrast, trans-pterostilbene (t-Pt), a dimethylated analogue of t-R, shows superior metabolic stability and pharmacokinetics, enhancing its therapeutic potential. These advantages have drawn increasing interest to t-Pt as a nutraceutical and pharmaceutical candidate; yet, its natural abundance in plants is extremely low, necessitating alternative production strategies.

Previous studies have used diverse plant systems to explore stilbenoid biosynthesis. In grapevine, resveratrol induction is classically observed in excised leaves and canes exposed to ultraviolet (UV)-C or pathogen infection, but this approach relies on excised tissues and is less amenable to long-term scalability. , In peanuts (Arachis hypogaea), hairy root cultures have been developed that produce high levels of prenylated stilbenoids (arachidins) upon elicitation with chemical inducers, rather than t-Pt. , Reports in other Vaccinium species, including cranberry (V. macrocarpon) and bilberry (V. myrtillus), describe phenolic metabolism under callus/cell or tissue culture conditions; however, pterostilbene has been reported only in select species such as V. ashei and V. stamineum, and not in cranberry or bilberry. To the best of our knowledge, UV-elicited activation of the t-Pt pathway has not been demonstrated in blueberry callus or suspension cultures.

Plant cell cultures offer a controlled platform for secondary metabolite production, providing an environmentally favorable alternative to traditional extraction methods. Notably, UV light has been shown to act as a powerful elicitor in grape and peanut cultures, enhancing stilbenoid biosynthesis by upregulating pathway genes such as STS (stilbene synthase) and ROMT (resveratrol O-methyltransferase). However, the potential of blueberry-derived cultures for t-Pt production and their responsiveness to UV elicitation remain largely unexplored.

Previous work in intact blueberry leaves reported UV-induced increases in phenolic metabolism. However, the applicability of UV elicitation to in vitro cell-based systems, particularly blueberry leaf-derived callus and suspension cultures, remains underexplored. To address this gap, this study establishes an in vitro platform using blueberry leaf-derived callus and suspension cultures. We hypothesized that UV irradiation, particularly UV-C, could act as a residue-free physical elicitor of ROMT1 expression in in vitro materials (plantlets and yellow calli), thereby supporting t-Pt biosynthetic potential, while stilbenoid accumulation was evaluated separately in suspension cultures within a proof-of-concept framework. Our approach integrates two components: (i) generation of callus lines and suspension cultures with distinct metabolic profiles and (ii) UV elicitation experiments to assess ROMT1 induction in in vitro tissues. This proof-of-concept framework provides a basis for the sustainable production of high-value stilbenoids.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials and Reagents

This study was conducted at the Korea Food Research Institute (Republic of Korea). In vitro-cultured blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L., cv. O’Neal) plantlets were obtained from Dankook University and maintained under standard growth chamber conditions (25 °C; 16 h light/8 h dark, white fluorescent lamps at ∼1500 lx). Leaves were used for subsequent UV irradiation experiments. Culture media were prepared using plant cell culture chemicals, including Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium with B5 vitamins (M0231), sucrose (S0809), gelrite (G1101), 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) (D0911), and 6-benzylaminopurine (BA) (B0904), all purchased from Duchefa Biochemie (Haarlem, The Netherlands).

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade reagents were obtained from JT Baker (Phillipsburg, NJ) and Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Standard materials, including t-resveratrol (t-R) (R5010), trans-pterostilbene (t-Pt) (P1499), and trans-pinosylvin (t-Pn) (56297), were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, while t-ε-viniferin (t-Vf) (FV30429) was purchased from Carbosynth (Berkshire, U.K.). All remaining chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

Callus Culture

The basal medium used for callus induction was MS medium supplemented with B5 vitamins, 2,4-D (0.2, 0.5, or 1.0 mg/L), BA (0, 0.05, 0.1, or 0.2 mg/L), 3% (w/v) sucrose, and 0.4% (w/v) gelrite. The pH was adjusted to 5.8 before being autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. Blueberry plantlet leaves were cut into 50 mm2 sections and placed in the medium. Callus induction was performed under dark conditions by wrapping culture vessels in aluminum foil in a growth chamber maintained at 25 °C with 60% relative humidity. After 6–8 weeks, leaf-derived calli were isolated from the explants and subcultured every 4 weeks in the same medium. Pigmentation first appeared during the third transfer; red calli (RC) harvested at the third and fourth transfers were operationally defined as primary calli for subsequent experiments, as pigmentation later faded with prolonged subculture. At each subculture step, brown calli were discarded due to loss of pigmentation and viability, while soft yellow or red calli were selected for continued culture. Calli were classified according to the method of Matkowski based on induction and growth status: (−), weak initiation and poor growth; (+), good induction but poor growth; (++), good induction and moderate growth; and (+++), excellent induction and vigorous growth. Each experiment was performed in five replicates.

Suspension Culture

Suspension cultures were established using subcultured calli sorted by color, and primary calli sorted by age. Fresh callus cells (400 mg) were inoculated into 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 40 mL of liquid medium. The cultures were maintained under dark conditions in a shaking incubator set at 100 rpm and 25 °C for 7 weeks to ensure optimal cell proliferation.

The suspension culture medium consisted of MS medium supplemented with B5 vitamins, 2,4-D (0 or 0.2 mg/L), BA (0, 0.1, 0.2, or 0.4 mg/L), 3% (w/v) sucrose, and 30 mM CaCl2, with the pH adjusted to 5.7 before sterilization.

Ultraviolet (UV) Irradiation Treatment

Ultraviolet (UV) irradiation was applied to evaluate effects on ROMT1 expression in in vitro-cultured blueberry plantlets and derived yellow calli (YC). Whole plantlets were irradiated with UV-A (365 nm) or UV-C (254 nm), and leaves were harvested after a 7-day recovery for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR); YC was irradiated as intact tissues and incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 7 days before sampling. Preliminary dose–response tests in plantlet leaves at 100, 300, and 600 J/m2 indicated that only 600 J/m2 increased ROMT1; therefore, 600 J/m2 was used for the main experiments in plantlet leaves and YC. Irradiances were 0.338 mW·cm–2 (UV-A) and 0.482 mW·cm–2 (UV-C); to deliver 600 J/m2, exposure times were approximately 178 s (UV-A) and 125 s (UV-C).

Determination of Callus Induction and Growth

Callus induction efficiency was assessed using the method described by Liu et al. The induction coefficient (%) was calculated as follows: Induction coefficient=(total number of induced callinumber of cultured explants)×100

To evaluate the growth ratio, suspension cultures of 7-week-old calli were utilized. Calli were initially obtained from blueberry leaf explants and suspended in liquid medium for 4 weeks. After 7 weeks of culture, they were separated from the liquid medium by filtration, and their fresh weight was measured. The initial fresh weight refers to the callus weight at the time of suspension culture initiation.

The growth index (GI) was calculated using the following formula: GI=final fresh weight(g)−initial fresh weight(g)initial fresh weight(g)

Stilbenoid Analysis

Stilbenoid extraction and HPLC analysis were conducted according to the method described by Kim et al. One gram portion of freeze-dried, pulverized material was extracted with acidified methanol (0.1% HCl) 20 times for 30 min each at 27 °C. The extract was centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min (Centrikon T-324, Kontron, Milan, Italy), filtered through a Whatman No. 4 filter (Whatman International Ltd., Maidstone, U.K.), and evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure.

Afterward, 25 mL of distilled water was added; the mixture was vigorously vortexed and mixed with an equal volume of ethyl acetate. The mixture was incubated for 10 min at 4 °C. The ethyl acetate layer was then evaporated under a vacuum at 40 °C. The concentrate was dissolved in 1 mL of methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was conducted using an Agilent 1260 series system (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm2, 5 μm, Agilent Technologies). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (phase B). The flow rate was maintained at 1.0 mL/min, and the column temperature was set at 25 °C. The injection volume was 20 μL. The elution profile was as follows: from 0 to 30 min, 10–80% B; from 30 to 35 min, 80–10% B; and from 35 to 45 min, 10% B. Detection was performed at 300 nm. Standard samples, including t-R, t-Vf, t-Pn, and t-Pt, were used. All tests were repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Total RNA Extraction

Total RNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method. Callus samples (100 mg) were ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle, then suspended in 500 μL of prewarmed CTAB extraction buffer (2% CTAB; 2.5% PVP-40; 100 mM NaCl; 2 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 25 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; and 2% β-mercaptoethanol), freshly prepared before use.

The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min. An equal volume of chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v) was added. The solution was vigorously inverted and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, and an additional chloroform/isoamyl alcohol extraction was performed.

To precipitate RNA, LiCl (3 M final concentration) was added to the supernatant, which was incubated at 4 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting RNA pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of prewarmed SSTE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 1% SDS; 1 M NaCl). An equal volume of chloroform/isoamyl alcohol was added, followed by centrifugation.

The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, and cold isopropyl alcohol (0.7× supernatant volume) was added. The mixture was incubated overnight at −20 °C, then centrifuged, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in 30 μL of 0.1% (v/v) diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated water. The extracted RNA was stored at a temperature of −80 °C until use.

The quality of RNA was assessed by 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis, and the RNA concentration was quantified using a DeNovix DS-11+ Spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE).

Complementary DNA (cDNA) Synthesis

First-strand cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (M170A, Promega) in a 20 μL reaction.

A 12.5 μL mixture containing 4 μg of RNA, 1 μL of oligo (dT) primer (500 ng/μL), and DEPC-treated water was incubated at 65 °C for 5 min and rapidly cooled on ice. Next, 1 μL of RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (200 U/μL; Thermo Scientific), 2 μL of dNTPs (10 mM each of dATP, dGTP, dCTP, and dTTP), 0.5 μL of recombinant RNasin RNase Inhibitor (20 U/μL), and 4 μL of 5× reaction buffer were added.

The reaction mixture was incubated at 42 °C for 60 min. Enzyme inactivation was performed at 70 °C for 10 min. The synthesized cDNA was diluted to 25 ng/μL with distilled water and stored at 4 °C.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using a SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (Tli RNase H Plus; Takara, Dalian, China). Three biological replicates (n = 3) were analyzed. Relative quantification used the comparative Ct (2–ΔΔCt) method, normalizing ROMT1 to GAPDH and using the untreated control as the calibrator. When no single condition served as a calibrator (e.g., cross-sectional comparisons among callus and suspension culture types), expression values were summarized as 2–ΔCt normalized to GAPDH. Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 6 μL of nuclease-free water, 10 μL of SYBR Premix Ex TaqII (2×), 0.8 μL of forward primer (10 μM), 0.8 μL of reverse primer (10 μM), 0.4 μL of ROX Reference Dye II (50×), and 2 μL of cDNA. Amplification conditions were an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 56 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s.

Statistical Analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (SAS version 9.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). All data were tested for normality prior to ANOVA. Exact P-values were reported where relevant, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Gene expression experiments were performed with three biological replicates (n = 3) and are reported as means ± SE. Stilbenoid quantification was performed with five biological replicates (n = 5) and is reported as means ± SD. For figure panels using letter annotations, groups sharing a letter do not differ significantly by Duncan’s test.

Under optimized conditions, fully proliferated YC were periodically subcultured. As defined in the Materials and Methods, pigmentation emerged at the third transfer, and RC collected at the third and fourth transfers were used as primary calli for subsequent work; beyond the fourth transfer, RC progressively lost pigmentation (turning white/brown), indicating instability that may constrain scaling. At this stage, calli were separated by pigmentation; YC and RC were clearly distinguished and subsequently used to establish independent suspension cultures (Figure C).

Similar findings have been reported in other berry-derived tissues, such as strawberry calli cultured in darkness, which exhibited anthocyanin levels up to 3.5 times higher than those grown in light. , These results indicate that dark culture conditions effectively support anthocyanin production in RC while enabling the establishment of both YC and RC as distinct callus types for further suspension culture studies.

Anthocyanin biosynthesis is generally promoted by light. − Mechanistically, the MYB–bHLH–WD40 (MBW) complex operates downstream of photoreceptors (PHY/CRY) via the COP1–HY5 cascade and integrates hormonal/metabolic cues such as jasmonic acid, abscisic acid, and sucrose. , In our system, however, RC pigmentation emerged only under dark incubation, suggesting a noncanonical regulatory mode. One possibility is that darkness enhances COP1 activity and promotes HY5 degradation, reshaping upstream signaling/post-transcriptional control and thereby altering MBW composition or activity to activate late anthocyanin biosynthetic genes without strong light input. Because flavonoid and stilbenoid metabolism share precursors within the phenylpropanoid pathway, such rewiring could also redistribute the flux between chalcone synthase (CHS) and stilbene synthase (STS), directed branches. In Vitis vinifera, STS transcripts are induced by MYB and WRKY transcription factors under abiotic stress, supporting the plausibility that altered flavonoid regulation in dark-grown RC may influence stilbenoid biosynthesis as well. Although transcription factor expression was not examined here, this framework motivates targeted time-course profiling of MBW components, COP1 and HY5, and key phenylpropanoid genes in future work. Future efforts focusing on stabilization strategies (e.g., culture-medium optimization, elicitor supplementation, and genetic approaches) will be needed to improve the industrial scalability of RC-based systems.

Cell Growth in Different Callus Types and Suspension Culture Conditions

After 6–7 months of culture, YC and RC calli were established and transferred to suspension cultures. The cultures were maintained for 7 weeks in media supplemented with varying PGR concentrations.

In media containing 4 mg/L BA, callus cells became completely necrotic after approximately 3 weeks. Similarly, excessive PGR concentrations caused cells to lose pigmentation, turn black, and ultimately undergo necrosis. These observations suggest that while PGRs are essential for cell proliferation, excessive BA can be toxic to suspension cultures (Figure D–F).

Growth index (GI) was influenced more by the callus type than by specific suspension conditions. Across all treatments, YC exhibited 1.2–1.5-fold higher GI than RC (Table ; P < 0.05), indicating that proliferation efficiency does not necessarily predict secondary metabolite output.

Despite its lower GI, RC accumulated substantially more stilbenoids than YC. Although competition for the common precursor phenylalanine could imply a trade-off between the flavonoid and stilbenoid branches, prior studies indicate that parallel activation can occur via coordinated transcriptional programs, , spatial compartmentalization/metabolic channeling that minimizes direct substrate competition, and increased upstream flux at the PAL step. , Together, these mechanisms plausibly explain the concurrent enhancement of anthocyanins and stilbenoids observed in the RC.

Red calli (RC) selection therefore plays a critical role in targeted metabolite production, and optimizing both the callus type and culture conditions will be required to balance growth and biosynthesis. While RC shows strong potential for producing high-value stilbenoids under controlled suspension conditions, sustaining this performance at scale will require strategies to mitigate pigmentation loss and phenotypic instability during prolonged subculture.

Stilbenoid Content in Different Callus Types and Suspension Culture Conditions

Four stilbenoids, t-R, t-Vf, t-Pn, and t-Pt, were identified and quantified in extracts from blueberry leaf-derived suspension cultures (Table ). Their accumulation depended on the callus type (YC vs RC) and culture conditions.

Red calli (RC) suspension cultures consistently accumulated more stilbenoids than YC. Notably, t-Vf and t-Pt levels were markedly elevated in RC cultured in medium supplemented with 0.2 mg/L 2,4-D, showing approximately 18-fold and 89.1-fold increases, respectively, compared with the untreated leaf control. Similarly, t-Pn increased significantly in RC grown in medium containing 0.2 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.2 mg/L BA. In contrast, t-R was present only in trace amounts across all conditions, suggesting preferential metabolic flux toward its downstream derivatives within the phenylpropanoid pathway.

The observed RC–YC differences likely reflect a coordinated adjustment of phenylpropanoid metabolism rather than strict precursor competition. The red pigmentation of RC indicates active flavonoid biosynthesis; however, the concurrent rise in stilbenoids implies a dynamic balance between the flavonoid and stilbenoid branches, consistent with reports that culture conditions and elicitation can redirect pathway flux to targeted metabolites. , In addition, media containing 2,4-D and BA have been shown to modulate key enzymes and shift metabolism toward secondary products, which aligns with the enhanced accumulation of both anthocyanins and stilbenoids observed in RC cultures.

Notably, the 89.1-fold increase in t-Pt refers to RC suspension cultures under non-UV conditions. In UV-treated samples, we measured ROMT1 transcripts only; compound-level stability, degradation, and isomeric profiling were not performed. Accordingly, metabolite quantification and UV assays were conducted separately, providing complementary insight into biosynthetic capacity (suspensions) versus UV-responsive regulation (plantlets/YC).

Looking ahead, targeted studies on compound stability under UV, long-term culture performance, RC phenotypic stabilization, and process parameters for scale-up will be important to translate RC-based production to industrial settings.

Consistent with Kim et al., which examined phenolics in intact leaves, our data extend UV responsiveness to in vitro materials by showing stronger ROMT1 induction under UV-C than UV-A after a 7-day recovery. Mechanistically, prior work in blueberry and grape indicates a sequential induction of stilbenoid metabolism under abiotic stress, with UV-A favoring earlier products (e.g., resveratrol) and UV-C associated with downstream derivatives. , Consistent with this model, our data show that UV-C more strongly enhances ROMT1, which encodes the O-methyltransferase catalyzing the terminal O-methylation step, than UV-A. Moreover, time-resolved studies in grape canes report that PAL/STS transcripts rise before resveratrol accumulation, supporting a stepwise activation along the pathway, and regulation of phenylpropanoid/stilbene genes is known to be nonsynchronous across steps. Although consistent with prior reports, we did not quantify PAL or STS in this study; thus, our interpretation should be viewed as a plausible mechanistic framework rather than a direct demonstration of the present materials.

The modest increase in ROMT1 under UV-A suggests that its effect may be dose-dependent or time-sensitive. Although only the 600 J/m2 dose was used in the main experiment based on preliminary dose–response trials in which lower doses failed to induce significant gene expression, further studies are warranted to establish a full dose–response curve and to optimize UV-A treatment parameters.

ROMT1 Gene Expression in Cultured Cells of Blueberry Leaves

Expression of ROMT1 was analyzed in YC, RC, and their respective suspension cultures grown in medium supplemented with 0.2 mg/L 2,4-D (Figure B). Expression of ROMT1 was significantly higher in suspension cultures (YCS and RCS) than in their respective callus forms (YC and RC), as confirmed by Duncan’s test (P < 0.0001). This increase may result from physical stressors, such as agitation and increased cell-to-cell interactions, which are known to upregulate secondary metabolism-related genes during the transition from solid to liquid culture.

In particular, RCS showed markedly elevated ROMT1 expression compared to YC, RC, and YCS (P < 0.001), paralleling the highest t-Pt accumulation observed among all groups (Table ). These findings indicate that RC suspension cultures offer a more favorable metabolic environment for stilbenoid biosynthesis, highlighting the combined effects of the callus type and suspension conditions in optimizing secondary metabolite production.

Effect of UV on ROMT1 Gene Expression in Calli Obtained from Blueberry Leaf Explants

Yellow calli (YC) were irradiated with UV-A or UV-C (600 J/m2), and ROMT1 expression was analyzed relative to that of untreated controls. Both treatments resulted in an increase in ROMT1 expression; however, only UV–C treatment led to a statistically significant upregulation compared to that of the control (P = 0.016; Figure C).

Despite the lack of statistical significance, UV-A treatment showed a modest increase in ROMT1 expression, suggesting a potential regulatory effect that may be enhanced under optimized conditions. In contrast, UV-C treatment markedly induced ROMT1 expression, highlighting its stronger elicitation effect and its capacity to activate secondary metabolism-related genes. These findings underscore the need to optimize UV-A parameters (dose and exposure duration) and motivate testing of combined/sequential UV-A–UV-C regimens for potential synergy.

Continued research should determine how enzymes upstream of ROMT1 (e.g., PAL, STS) contribute to the regulation of stilbenoid biosynthesis under UV-induced stress. In addition, the timing of sample collection following UV treatment is an important factor influencing the observed responses. Previous studies have shown that UV-elicited transcriptional and metabolic changes can be detected at different temporal windows ranging from short-term (1–2 days) in callus systems , to midterm (6–7 days) in grape and strawberry tissues , and even longer durations of several weeks to one month in Fagonia indica callus and preharvest strawberries. , Based on this spectrum, we adopted a 7-day recovery period after UV treatment for both in vitro-cultured plantlets (under light conditions) and YC (under dark incubation), aiming to capture stabilized and sustained induction of ROMT1 rather than transient early peaks. We acknowledge, however, that earlier time points (e.g., 1–24 h) may display distinct induction kinetics, which should be addressed in future time-course studies.

From a scale-up perspective, RC-based production will hinge on addressing density-dependent limits in stirred systems (oxygen transfer, nutrient supply, and shear). For UV elicitation specifically, photoreactor geometry and effective UV penetration are critical, as light attenuation in dense suspensions can reduce elicitor effectiveness. Although we did not conduct yield projections or techno-economic analyses, integrating these engineering variables with metabolic optimization will be essential to evaluate the feasibility of UV-elicited t-Pt production.

Conclusion

This study establishes a two-stage proof-of-concept strategy for in vitro t-Pt production in blueberry leaf-derived cultures consisting of RC induction and UV-C elicitation. RC suspension cultures accumulated markedly higher stilbenoid levels than control leaves under non-UV conditions, whereas UV experiments were confined to transcriptional responses: UV-C consistently induced ROMT1 in plantlets and YC. Together, these findings support UV-C as a residue-free physical elicitor of pathway activation, while clarifying that compound quantification and UV-responsive gene expression were evaluated in separate experiments.

Within this controlled in vitro platform, we outline practical next steps rather than claims of immediate scalability: assessing UV photostability and potential isomerization of t-Pt, stabilizing RC pigmentation and growth over extended passages, and benchmarking UV-C against chemical or biotic elicitors. For future translation, bioreactor design and operation will be critical, particularly oxygen transfer at higher cell densities, shear management, and UV penetration/photoreactor configuration, and should be integrated with metabolic readouts before any techno-economic conclusions are drawn.
