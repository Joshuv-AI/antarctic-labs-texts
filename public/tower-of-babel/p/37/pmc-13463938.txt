Sleep disturbances and risk of Alzheimer’s disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies
Authors: Qu Li, Fenqin Chen, Rui Zhan, Wenqiang Cong, Siyu Mu, Shanshan Zhong, Xin Liu, Defu Zhao, Yueren Wang, Chuansheng Zhao, Kexin Kang, Zhike Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep is essential for healthy bodily functioning in many aspects, including physiology, psychology and cognition. Due to the reverse causation, it remains inconclusive whether sleep disturbances contribute to Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The purpose of this meta-analysis was to investigate the association between sleep disturbances and AD risk.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched for longitudinal cohort studies until May 2025. Random-effects models were employed to pool risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsOf the 2106 records, 31 studies from East Asia (23%), Europe (32%), and North America (45%) were eligible for inclusion, involving 13,109,323 participants. The pooled effect size showed that sleep disturbances were linked to higher risk of AD (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.51), with the magnitude attenuated but still significant through multivariate adjustment (adj-RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.42). Similar effects persisted even after reducing effects of reverse causation, as implemented by stratified analysis on mid-life cohorts and longer follow-ups (5–15: RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.81; ≥ 15 years: RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.57). Dose–response analysis revealed a U-shaped correlation, with sleep duration < 6 or > 8 h associated with AD risk elevation. Neither baseline age, follow-up length, nor study quality significantly diluted the observed association.ConclusionsThis study adds to evidence that sleep disturbances are relevant to incident AD, even in mid-life cohorts and longer follow-ups, supporting sleep disturbances as a mid-life risk factor of AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02102-8.Keywords: Sleep disturbances, Alzheimer’s disease, Meta-analysis, Risk factor

Background

Globally, dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) remains a substantial public health threat by the World Health Organization (WHO) [76]. More than 44 million people are suffering from AD, with 112 new cases per 10,000 person-years in adults over 65 years old [1, 17]. As the demographic expands and ages, the prevalence is estimated to reach 132 million by 2050, accounting for annual hospitalization and caregiving expenditures of US $1.89 trillion [10, 68]. In stark contrast, long-term and high-investment foundational research have yet yielded a revolutionary pharmacotherapy; namely, none of the currently available drugs could reverse, halt, or even delay the onset and development of AD. Given this, the modifiable risk factors for AD prevention are currently receiving unprecedented attention. As of 2024, the Lancet Commission on Dementia has identified 14 life-course modifiable risk factors, except for sleep disturbances [43–45]. This is due at least in part to the lack of convincing epidemiological evidence base, a gap that is precisely the motivation for systematic review and meta-analysis.

Any abnormality in the sleep–wake homeostasis can be defined as sleep disturbances, ranging from prolonged sleep-onset latency, sleep fragmentation, early morning waking, to excessive sleepiness [6, 52]. In general, sleep disturbances are categorized into sleep duration problems (e.g., insomnia, excessive sleep duration, and excessive daytime sleepiness) and sleep quality problems (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, sleep fragmentation, and low rapid eye movement sleep). Alongside with these phenotypes is the secondary overactivation of integrated stress response and hippocampal retrieval, which pose individuals at a higher risk of subnormal cognition involving attention, alertness, execution, learning and memory [14, 51, 85]. Converging lines of empirical evidence have shown that sleep disturbances over the life course may be associated with AD risk. A 40-year follow-up study revealed that after adjustment for age, men with self-reported sleep disturbances had a 51% higher risk of developing AD compared to those with normal sleep [3]. Other supportive evidence comes from higher prevalence of AD and sleep disturbances in women [23, 65]. This phenomenon is likely relevant to the life-course fluctuations of estrogen levels, a sex-specific hormone susceptibly tuned by sleep and stress [19]. For type-specific sleep characteristics, the community-based survey lasting at least 15 years demonstrated a relationship of insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea in mid-life with subsequent AD risk [49]. To our knowledge, several postulated theories linking sleep disturbances with AD have been proposed as follows: (i) misaligned rhythms of hormones and neurotransmitters [70, 86]; (ii) deposit of Aβ [29] and release or spread of tau [26] (also known as the core pathological hallmarks of AD); (iii) impaired clearance of metabolic waste [78]; and (iv) indirect processes via modulating vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, etc.) [55, 62].

Earlier meta-analyses have investigated sleep association with the incidence of all-cause dementia [39, 77, 80]. Not surprisingly, the reliability of pooled outcome is subject to debate given the diverse etiologies of dementia subtypes. Such conclusions are also limited by absent quality assessment of included studies, wherein the non-significant or null association could be yielded if sleep is represented as a continuous variable (e.g., apnea–hypopnea index, sleep fragmentation index) [37, 38], adjustment for maximal confounders [37], or subjects span a wide range of baseline age and/or follow-up length [64, 74, 80]. Indeed, there is possibly a 20-year or longer lag between the emergence of AD pathologies and overt cognitive symptoms [16]. Therefore, clinical trials with older cohorts and shorter follow-ups are prone to introducing reverse causation, as they might have recruited patients with prodromal AD.

In view of this, we sought to evaluate and summarize sleep data from longitudinal cohort studies, taking into account the effects of reverse causation and thus to disclose the temporality-dependent association of sleep disturbances with subsequent AD risk.

Methods

Search strategy

This overview was operationalized with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [53], and registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), CRD42024538836. Following the predefined protocol, we performed a retrieval of electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science) from the inception to February 24, 2025, with the last update on May 24, 2025. The key terminologies were refined as follows: ((sleep) OR (insomnia) OR (sleepiness) OR (sleep–wake disruption)) AND (Alzheimer*) AND ((longitudinal) OR (follow-up) OR (cohort)). A full description of search strategy was listed in Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, we also manually checked bibliographies of included articles and relevant reviews to minimize the omission of eligible publications.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) population-based cohort studies; (2) reporting a link between sleep disturbances and incident AD; (3) the risk estimate could be extracted or calculated by utilization of raw data; and (4) full-text articles in English language. Exclusion criteria were: (1) case–control or cross-sectional designs; (2) no cognitive assessment at baseline when including participants aged ≥ 65 years; (3) individuals developed into AD less than 3-year follow-up; (4) all-cause, mixed, or non-AD dementias; and (5) abstracts, editorials, sectional studies, or comments. Two independent investigators meticulously censored the identification and selection of literatures. Any discrepancies were first resolved via negotiation, if failed, with the arbitration conducted by a third experienced researcher.

Data extraction

Study characteristics were collected by two reviewers based on prespecified standard templates, including first author, publication year, study design, region, follow-up length, ascertainment of exposure, traits of sleep disturbances, total sample size, number of AD cases, mean age, male proportion, body mass index (BMI), education, apolipoprotein E (ApoE) ε4 carriers, adjusted confounders, risk estimates, and multivariable-adjusted estimates. Depending on the diversity of sleep disturbance characteristics, an original study might conduct multiple comparative analyses of the exposure-outcome pair, yielding more than one estimates. All effect estimates of the comparisons in the study were included in our meta-analysis. Adjusted factors from approved articles also involved variables such as study center, ethnicity, marriage, wealth, depressive status, etc. We sought to contact the corresponding authors of original studies for the missing requisite data or clarification.

Quality assessment and publication bias

Using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [67], the quality of longitudinal cohort studies was independently assessed by two reviewers. The sum of NOS score is 9 points including study selection (4 points), group comparability (2 points), and outcome ascertainment (3 points). In the last item, adequate follow-up is defined as > 5 years, and < 20% of withdraw rate is set as follow-up integrity that does not introduce risk bias. The score less than 3 points is regarded as low quality, 4–6 points as moderate quality, and 7–9 points as high quality.

Publication bias was qualitatively evaluated through the visual inspection of funnel-plot symmetry; further, a trim-and-fill method by the enhanced contour funnel plot was employed to conclude whether there was publication bias due to underreporting results [54].

Statistical analysis

Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were utilized as the summary statistics of effect sizes. Hazard ratios (HRs) reported in original studies should be converted into RRs according to the following algorithm: RR = (1-e^(HR*ln(1-r)))/r. Here, r is the incidence rate of endpoint events in non-exposed or reference group [63]. We used random-effects models in meta-analyses, and evaluated the heterogeneity of pooled results by I-squared statistic (I2). An I2 > 75% with P < 0.05 was considered as substantial between-study heterogeneity.

Through subgroup meta-analyses, meta-regressions, and supplementary analyses, sensitivity tests based on study design, traits of sleep disturbances, sample size, baseline age, follow-up length, quality assessment, gender, education, BMI, and number of effect modifiers were performed to examine the robustness of pooled results. To reduce the influences of reverse causation, we conducted additional secondary analysis on high-quality studies with baseline age and follow-up length. Using meta, dplyr, and ggplot2 packages in R software, dose–response analysis was executed to investigate the relationship of sleep duration per night with AD risk. We used two-sided tests with significance set at P < 0.05. All analyses were done in STATA 15.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) and R 3.6.2 (https://www.r-project.org).

Results

Literature search

Figure 1 depicted the flow diagram of literature selection. Preliminary search of electric databases and bibliographies yielded 2106 records, leaving 338 potential eligible studies by skimming titles and abstracts. After full-text review, 307 articles unsatisfying the inclusion criteria were excluded (Supplementary Table S2). Finally, 31 eligible studies from East Asia (23%), Europe (32%), and North America (45%) were incorporated into the meta-analysis [2–4, 7, 9, 11–13, 20, 21, 25, 27, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 42, 48–50, 57, 59–61, 72, 75, 79, 81, 82].

Demographic characteristics

General characteristics of included studies reporting an association between sleep disturbances and AD risk were given in Supplementary Table S3. The incidence of AD was 3.9% (n = 13,109,323), with the mean baseline age 67.5 (SD 7.4, range 49.6–84.1) years, male proportion 50.3%, BMI 27.5 (SD 2.6), high education attainment 21.5%, and ApoE ε4 allele 15.4%.

Main meta-analysis

Among all 54 comparisons (31 studies), there were 29 comparisons (14 studies) pertaining to sleep duration problems, 16 comparisons (13 studies) regarding sleep quality problems, and 9 comparisons (8 studies) belonging to unclassified type. For subtypes of sleep quality problems, 9 comparisons (7 studies) involved obstructive sleep apnea, 2 comparisons (2 studies) were related to sleep fragmentation, and 2 comparisons (2 studies) were linked to excessive daytime sleepiness. The pooled estimate showed that all sleep disturbances were positively associated with AD risk (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.51) (Fig. 2). The multivariable-adjusted RR (1.29, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.42) for 53 comparisons (n = 30 studies) were statistically significant, but slightly lower than the primitive RR (Table 1).

Subgroup meta-analyses, meta-regressions, and supplementary analyses

According to the substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98%) across study characteristics, the robustness of risk estimate was detected by subgroup meta-analyses and meta-regressions (Table 1). Neither baseline age, follow-up length, nor study quality modified the pooled outcome. Among high-quality studies, the RRs in mid-life cohorts (mean baseline age < 65, range 49.6–64.3 years) and stratified follow-ups (< 5: RR 1.24, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.44; 5–15: RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.81; ≥ 15 years: RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.57) exhibited an association of sleep disturbances with AD risk.

In meta-regressions and supplementary analyses (Table 2), we found that except for sleep fragmentation (RR 1.23, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.83), obstructive sleep apnea (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.59), excessive daytime sleepiness (RR 1.68, 95% CI 1.07 to 2.65), study design, the largest sample or weight, sample size, modifiers (gender, education, BMI, and ApoE ε4 status) served as covariate or non-covariate, and number of modifiers did not interfere with the observed association.

Forest plots of supplementary analyses on sleep duration (Fig. 3A) showed that insomnia (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.52) and excessive sleep duration (RR 1.68, 95% CI 1.44 to 1.96) were significantly associated with AD risk. Dose–response analysis (Fig. 3B) delineated a U-shaped trajectory of sleep duration per night in relation to AD incidence. More specifically, the pooled RRs showed significantly positive sleep-AD associations in sleep duration < 6 h or > 8 h, whereas there was null association within 6–8 h.

Study quality and publication bias

Quality scoring of NOS was conducted for individual studies, details of which were given in Supplementary Table S4. In brief, 14 out of 31 studies were rated as high quality, 14 as moderate quality, and 3 as low quality. The funnel plot presented a certain degree of asymmetry; after the trim-and-fill approach, we found the statistically significant results underreported in enhanced contour funnel plot, suggesting that the source of heterogeneity was improbably derived from publication bias (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

This systematic overview, which embraced meta-analyses of sleep data from more than 13 million participants, is to date the largest synthesis on the sleep-AD association. We provided a pooled estimation of how sleep disturbances give rise to incident AD, exploring the potential causality related to temporality factors as well as dose–response aspects of this relationship. Collectively, the pooled results emphasized a consistent correlation of sleep disturbances with AD risk. Neither baseline age, follow-up length, nor study quality significantly moderated the robustness of risk estimates, suggesting sleep disturbances to be a potential life-course risk factor of AD.

There have been prior meta-analyses exploring the effects of sleep disturbances on AD risk through unique focus and methodology [8, 71, 74]. Nonetheless, the significant statistical validity is mostly restricted to older cohorts and shorter follow-ups (< 10 years) [8, 18, 71]. Interpretation of these results should be cautious owing to small sample size, less-stringent eligibility criteria, and non-adjusted confounders. Another key point to note is that the observed link could be mutually causal; that is, sleep disturbances might not only be a risk factor but also a consequence of AD [20, 34]. This is especially evident among the elderly, where sleep disturbances may overlap with incipient symptoms of AD [22, 46]. On the other hand, sleep traits can alter with advancing age, a process that largely depends on health condition and environmental sleep issues [15, 24]. Apart from those temporality factors, the quality of source studies is also an important modifier in the meta-analysis that cannot be ignored. Herein, we excluded old-aged cohorts who did not undergo the cognitive assessment, as well as those who developed AD within the first 3-year follow-up. Although these criteria limited the number of comparisons and enlarged the CIs, the bias of pooled results could be somewhat alleviated by harmonizing the raw data. Among only high-quality studies, we found the sleep-AD association persistent in mid-life cohorts and longer follow-ups. Most importantly, this procedure drastically reduced the possibility of reverse causation, thus lending strong support for sleep disturbances as a mid-life risk factor of AD.

The dose–response analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and AD risk, with pooled estimate significant in sleep duration < 6 or > 8 h rather than 6–8 h. As supported by in vivo animal experiments using two-photon imaging, the clearance of metabolic waste (including Aβ peptides) was impeded in response to sleep deprivation and wakefulness, which supported the role of inadequate sleep in the pathological aggregation of AD [78]. However, the mechanism by which excessive sleep leads to AD remains elusive. A plausible explanation is that obstructive sleep apnea often alongside with excessive sleep can elicit cerebral hypoxia and cortical atrophy, resulting in both cognitive decline and a greater demand for sleep [66]. Noteworthily, the model of dose–response analysis only included seven comparisons, and our protocol did not obtain precise numerical values for sleep duration, such as 6–7, 6–8, and 7–8 h. Further large investigation of sleep duration is needed to conclusively ascertain the threshold points at which an increased risk of AD occurs, or the optimal range with substantial benefits in the prevention of AD.

Findings emerging from supplementary stratified analyses demonstrated that except for sleep fragmentation, any other subtype of sleep disturbance (insomnia, excessive sleep duration, excessive daytime sleepiness, and obstructive sleep apnea) increased AD risk. These were generally consistent with the most recent meta-analyses [5, 73, 84], thereby lending support for the reliability of our auxiliary findings. The pooled estimates (RR 1.68) were numerically highest in both excessive sleep duration and excessive daytime sleepiness, indicating the strong correlation of excessive sleep with AD risk. Despite non-significant results, data on sleep fragmentation should be interpreted with caution, as only two studies were included in this analysis. Few original studies focused on various modalities of sleep quality when investigating sleep quantity, and vice versa. Given the diversities of sleep phenotypes, future in-depth research should excavate the shared pathogenic pathways of AD due to abnormalities in sleep quantity, quality, or both. Previous studies have reported the influences of gender [28], BMI [47], genetic risk [30, 56], and education [31, 55] in sleep-AD association. For example, the ApoE ε4 allele has been shown to be involved in slow-wave sleep loss and nocturnal sleep apnea, which synergistically aggravate the processing and formation of AD pathologies as well as brain atrophy [29, 40, 41, 58, 78]. In regards to education, albeit that virtually all studies have adjusted for this factor, individuals could differ in cognitive reserve even with equivalent educational attainment [31, 69]. None of the aforementioned confounders significantly interfered with pooled effect sizes according to sensitivity tests, providing support for the robustness of conclusions drawn from the present analysis.

Strengths and limitations

The major strengths of this meta-analysis include the use of large-scale epidemiological data, which enable us to perform an additional secondary analysis on high-quality studies with mid-life cohorts and longer follow-ups. This procedure, by stratifying temporality factors, could not only reduce reverse causation and generate a more authentic result, but also add evidence for a link between mid-life sleep disturbances and long-term risk of AD. Using multi-level sensitivity tests, we examined the influences of various covariates on the sleep-AD relationship, gaining a nuanced insight into how AD risk was modified by sleep disturbances. Further, we investigated dose–response correlation of sleep duration with AD, establishing a rough range of sleep duration (6–8 h per night) conducive to AD prevention. These results should be considered more precise and reliable than that obtained by Zhang et al. through empirically manual stratifications (non-significant interval: 7–8 h per night) [84]. Although the visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed a slight asymmetry, the enhanced contour funnel plot did not show definitive evidence of publication bias.

There were several limitations to be noted. First, the sources of heterogeneity might originate from study designs (prospective or retrospective), methodological errors (selection bias, misclassification of exposures or outcomes, and loss-to-follow-up bias), and English-language restriction. Second, original studies that were excluded owing to missing data weakened the generalizability and interpretability of the conclusions. Third, some studies employed dichotomous questionnaires to define sleep disturbances, which affected the sensitivity to sleep variations and thus to ceiling effects [83]. Fourth, the imprecise description of sleep duration led to deviations in the statistical validity of dose–response analytic results. Finally, few studies have taken into account the influence of sedative-hypnotics, daytime dozing, or napping on the observed relationship. Further work wishes to adopt overnight polysomnography or 24-h actigraphy to gain objective and detailed parameters of sleep phenotypes, with their healthcare data (e.g., medication usage, daily schedule) accessible publicly.

Conclusions

The present meta-analysis highlights a positive association of sleep disturbances with subsequent AD risk, even after multivariable adjustment and reduction of reverse causation. Our findings support sleep disturbances as a potential mid-life risk factor for AD, and sleep duration of 6–8 h may be a recommended lifestyle against incident AD. However, this overview remains a sententious synthesis of sleep data from previous epidemiological investigations. Large longitudinal surveys with prospective design, younger age at entry, longer follow-ups, and objective measures of sleep are warranted to confirm these results. Lastly, future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials of non-pharmacological sleep interventions (e.g., heliotherapy, physical activity) to identify the effectiveness of improved sleep in preventing AD, which may help alleviate the global burden of dementia and yield long-term social, economic, and health benefits.
