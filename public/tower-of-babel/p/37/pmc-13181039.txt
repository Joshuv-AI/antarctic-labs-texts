A novel deep intronic COL5A1 variant in an Ehlers-Danlos syndrome family: functional characterization by minigene assay
Authors: Jie Zhao, Jingjing Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify the genetic cause of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) in a Chinese family and to evaluate the functional impact of a deep intronic COL5A1 variant using a minigene assay. The genetic analysis was performed using medical whole-exome sequencing, covering 5,398 genes associated with human genetic diseases. A candidate variant was prioritized based on its segregation and potential effect on splicing. To confirm functional effects, wild-type and mutant minigene constructs were created and transfected into HEK293T cells. RT-PCR and sequencing were used to analyze splicing products. A heterozygous intronic COL5A1 variant (NM_000093.5: c.1774–95 C > T) was found in both the proband and her father, but not in the mother. Clinically, affected individuals showed features typical of classic Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (cEDS), including hyperextensible skin, atrophic scars, easy bruising, and joint hypermobility. Functional minigene assays demonstrated that the variant causes abnormal splicing, leading to pseudoexon inclusion, a frameshift (p.Gly592CysfsTer2), and the production of a truncated protein. Our findings provide functional evidence that the deep intronic COL5A1 variant c.1774–95 C > T disrupts normal splicing and is likely pathogenic in cEDS. Introduction

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a group of connective tissue disorders that are clinically and genetically diverse, affecting the skin, joints, cardiovascular system, eyes, and other organs in different ways1,2. In 2017, the International EDS Consortium reclassified EDS into 13 distinct subtypes, with the most common including classical (cEDS), vascular (vEDS), and hypermobile (hEDS)3,4. Classical EDS represents one of the major subtypes and is characterized by skin hyperextensibility, generalized joint hypermobility, and atrophic scarring. The estimated prevalence of cEDS is approximately 1 in 20,000–40,000 people5. Molecuarly, cEDS is mainly linked to variants in COL5A1, COL5A2, and, less frequently, COL1A16–8. Among these, variants in COL5A1 make up roughly 75–78% of all pathogenic mutations identified. Advances in molecular genetics have shown that most causative variants are found in collagen-encoding genes or genes crucial for collagen biosynthesis and modification9.

The COL5A1 gene encodes the pro-α1(V) chain, an essential part of type V collagen, which forms heterotypic fibrils with type I collagen in skin and other tissues10. Type V collagen regulates fibrillogenesis by affecting fibril diameter and organization. Therefore, disruption of COL5A1 function weakens collagen structure and causes the tissue fragility characteristic of cEDS11.

Most reported pathogenic variants in COL5A1 are missense, nonsense, or frameshift mutations located within coding regions or canonical splice sites12. In contrast, deep intronic variants, situated far from canonical exon–intron boundaries, are rarely documented. However, such variants can activate cryptic splice sites, leading to abnormal RNA processing and pseudoexon inclusion. These mechanisms reveal an underrecognized layer of pathogenicity in hereditary disorders, especially when standard exome sequencing does not detect coding mutations.

Here, we report a novel deep intronic COL5A1 variant (c.1774–95 C > T) in a Chinese family with classical EDS. Using a minigene splicing assay, we demonstrate that this variant causes pseudoexon inclusion, frameshift, and premature termination, thereby confirming its pathogenicity. This study broadens the mutational spectrum of COL5A1 and highlights the importance of functional assays in interpreting intronic variants in hereditary diseases.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and ethical compliance

The proband was a 30-year-old Chinese woman who visited the Department of Dermatology at Qilu Hospital, Shandong University, with symptoms indicating cEDS. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for genetic testing and publication of clinical photographs. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical evaluation

A detailed family history was collected, followed by a thorough clinical examination of the proband and her parents. Skin elasticity, joint range of motion, scars, and bruising tendency were systematically recorded. Standardized photographs were taken to document phenotypic features. Cardiovascular assessment, including echocardiography, was conducted to rule out vascular complications.

Genetic testing and variant interpretation

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from the proband and her parents. Genomic DNA was extracted using standard protocols. The genetic analysis was performed using medical whole-exome sequencing, covering 5,398 genes associated with human genetic diseases. Bioinformatic analysis included alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), variant calling, and annotation. Variants were filtered based on their frequency in population databases (gnomAD, ExAC, 1000 Genomes), predicted functional impact, and inheritance pattern. Variants were filtered based on a minor allele frequency threshold of < 0.01 in population databases, including gnomAD v4.1.0 and other public datasets.

Candidate variants were interpreted in accordance with the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Segregation analysis was conducted through Sanger sequencing in the family.

Minigene splicing assay

To assess the functional impact of the identified intronic variant, we used a minigene assay. Both wild-type and mutant COL5A1 genomic fragments, including exon 15, exon 16, and flanking intronic sequences, were cloned into the pMini-CopGFP vector (Beijing Hitrobio BiotechnologyCo, Ltd.). The constructs were verified through sequencing.

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher). After 48 h, cells were harvested. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by DNase treatment to remove genomic DNA contamination. Reverse transcription was carried out using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara).

RT-PCR amplification was carried out with the following primers:

MiniRT-F: GGCTAACTAGAGAACCCACTGCTTA.

COL5A1-RT-R: CTTGGGGCCAGTTTGTCCAG.

RT-PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis and recovered. The wild-type products were directly sequenced, while the mutant products underwent cloning and sequencing, followed by Sanger sequencing at Biosune (Shanghai, China).

Results

Clinical findings

The proband exhibited multiple atrophic scars, widespread bruising, soft and hyperextensible skin, and hypermobile finger joints (Fig. 1a-d). She reported having fragile skin since childhood, with frequent injuries and slow wound healing. During examination, the skin showed a velvety texture and could be stretched beyond normal limits but recoiled when released.

Clinical manifestations of the proband and her father. (a) Atrophic scar on the skin; (b) hyperextensibility of the finger joints; (c) multiple ecchymoses; (d) hyperextensible skin on the limbs; (e) craniofacial features of the proband’s father.

Her father exhibited skin laxity and mild dysmorphic features, including hypertelorism and a broad nasal bridge (Fig. 1e). Her mother showed no abnormal features.

Collectively, these findings were consistent with the classical subtype of cEDS.

Genetic findings

We identified a heterozygous intronic variant in COL5A1 (NM_000093.5: c.1774–95 C > T), predicted to result in a frameshift at the protein level (NP_000084.3: p.Gly592CysfsTer2).

Among all variants identified by whole-exome sequencing, the COL5A1 c.1774–95 C > T variant was prioritized based on the strong concordance between the patient’s clinical phenotype and classical Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, the segregation pattern observed in the family, and the established role of COL5A1 in cEDS. Based on ACMG criteria13, the variant was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Bioinformatic prediction indicated a possible splicing alteration, which prompted further functional investigation. Following functional validation demonstrating aberrant RNA splicing, the variant was subsequently reclassified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria.

Minigene assay results

The wild-type COL5A1 construct produced the expected transcript containing exons 15–17. In contrast, the mutant construct generated two transcripts: one that is identical to the wild-type and another that contains a 139-bp insertion from intron 15 (Fig. 2, original gel is presented in Supplementary Figure S1).

Minigene splicing assay of the COL5A1 variant (NM_000093.5:c.1774–95 C > T). (a) Schematic representation of the COL5A1 variant and its splicing consequence; (b) Agarose gel electrophoresis showing the corresponding RT-PCR products. WT, wild type; MT, mutant type.

Minigene functional analysis demonstrated that the intronic COL5A1 variant (NM_000093.5: c.1774–95 C > T) resulted in aberrant mRNA splicing. Translation of the aberrantly spliced transcript is predicted to result in a frameshift mutation (NP_000084.3:p.Gly592CysfsTer2), introducing a premature termination codon shortly downstream of the pseudoexon. This truncation is expected to cause loss of normal COL5A1 function, consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism underlying classical Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a novel deep intronic variant in COL5A1, c.1774–95 C > T, as the genetic cause of cEDS in a Chinese family. Although located far from canonical splice sites, this variant activates a cryptic splice donor site, causing pseudoexon inclusion and a frameshift. Our findings emphasize the important role of RNA-level assays in diagnosing variants outside the exome.

Type V collagen is an essential regulatory fibril-forming collagen that co-assembles with type I collagen14. Disruption of the COL5A1 gene causes haploinsufficiency and defective fibrillogenesis, leading to clinical features such as skin fragility, hyperextensibility, and joint hypermobility15,16. The truncated protein predicted from c.1774–95 C > T is expected to undergo nonsense-mediated decay or produce a nonfunctional peptide, both of which are consistent with a loss of function.

Most pathogenic COL5A1 variants reported in cEDS are nonsense or frameshift mutations in coding exons or canonical splice site changes17,18. Reports of deep intronic variants are rare, yet several studies in other collagen-related disorders have highlighted similar mechanisms of pseudoexon activation19–21. Our findings expand this pathogenic paradigm to COL5A1 and classical EDS.

Based on ACMG criteria, with consideration of current ACGS and ClinGen recommendations22, the COL5A1 c.1774–95 C > T variant is best classified as likely pathogenic, supported by functional evidence demonstrating aberrant splicing, absence from population databases, and a clinical phenotype consistent with classical Ehlers–Danlos syndrome. This information is important for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis, helping to provide accurate risk assessment for family members.

Our study highlights the diagnostic blind spot of standard exome sequencing, which usually excludes deep intronic regions. In patients with strong clinical suspicion of hereditary disorders but negative exome results, functional splicing assays should be considered.

Although the minigene splicing assay provides functional evidence supporting the likely pathogenic role of the COL5A1 c.1774–95 C > T variant, it represents an in vitro surrogate system and may not fully recapitulate endogenous, tissue-specific splicing regulation. Alternative splicing is known to be tissue-dependent, and the lack of patient-derived RNA or protein samples limits direct confirmation of the aberrant splicing event in vivo. Unfortunately, disease-relevant tissues such as skin fibroblasts were not available for RNA or protein analysis in this family. Future studies incorporating RNA sequencing or protein-level validation from patient-derived tissues will be important to further substantiate the pathogenic mechanism.

Emerging therapies like antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) show promise for fixing abnormal splicing caused by pseudoexon activation19,23,24. In principle, ASOs designed against the cryptic splice site triggered by c.1774–95 C > T could restore normal splicing, providing a personalized treatment option. CRISPR-based RNA editing methods are also in development and may eventually be used for EDS.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that COL5A1 c.1774–95 C > T is a likely pathogenic splicing variant associated with cEDS. This deep intronic mutation leads to the inclusion of a pseudoexon and the production of a truncated protein, resulting in functional haploinsufficiency of COL5A1. Our findings expand the mutational landscape of COL5A1, underscore the clinical significance of intronic variants, and emphasize the need for functional RNA-level validation in unresolved genetic cases. Importantly, this variant could serve as a potential target for splice-modulating therapies.
