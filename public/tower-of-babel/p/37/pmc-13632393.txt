Educational quality and viewer engagement of Youtube videos on ceramic implants: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mehmet Zahit Baş, Halil İbrahim Durmuş, Tevfik Kızılseki, Nuran Dağ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCeramic implants are increasingly used as metal-free alternatives to titanium implants because of their favorable esthetic properties and biocompatibility. As patients frequently seek dental information online, YouTube has become an important source of patient-oriented health information. This study aimed to evaluate the educational quality and ceramic implant-specific content coverage of YouTube videos on ceramic implants and to examine whether viewer engagement metrics were associated with video quality scores.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted on 63 English-language YouTube videos identified through six predefined keyword searches. Videos were screened independently by two calibrated evaluators according to predefined eligibility criteria. During scoring, evaluators were blinded to viewer engagement metrics. Video quality was assessed using the Video Information and Quality Index, Global Quality Score, and an exploratory, non-validated dentistry-specific Specific Content Checklist developed to evaluate ceramic implant-specific content coverage. Viewer engagement metrics, including views, likes, comments, and subscriber counts, were recorded. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients, and relationships between variables were analyzed using Pearson correlation tests.ResultsThe mean Video Information and Quality Index, Global Quality Score, and Specific Content Checklist scores were 3.27 ± 0.56, 3.34 ± 0.57, and 3.29 ± 1.68, respectively. Inter-rater reliability showed good agreement between evaluators, with average-measure intraclass correlation coefficients (two-way mixed-effects, consistency) ranging from 0.779 to 0.870. No statistically significant correlations were found between video quality scores and viewer engagement metrics; exact p values for these correlations ranged from .129 to .921. The wider dispersion of Specific Content Checklist scores indicated variability in ceramic implant-specific content coverage across videos.ConclusionsViewer engagement metrics should not be interpreted as reliable indicators of educational quality or ceramic implant-specific content coverage in YouTube videos on ceramic implants. Because the Specific Content Checklist was exploratory and non-validated, findings should be interpreted as indicators of content coverage rather than definitive measures of factual accuracy or clinical adequacy. Clinician-guided digital health literacy remains essential for helping patients interpret online ceramic implant information.Keywords: Ceramic implants, Zirconia implants, YouTube, Implant dentistry, Patient education, Digital health

Introductıon

Currently, the most commonly preferred materials in dental implant rehabilitation are commercial pure titanium and titanium alloys. Thanks to their high biocompatibility and mechanical strength, these materials have significantly increased long-term success rates in the treatment of tooth loss [1]. However, despite these advantages, the most notable disadvantage of titanium implants is their metallic gray color, which can be reflected through the peri-implant mucosa. This can negatively affect esthetic outcomes, particularly in patients with thin mucosal biotypes [2, 3]. Furthermore, the potential for immunological reactions to metallic materials is also a subject of debate. Current studies report that titanium particles may rarely cause hypersensitivity reactions in some individuals [4, 5].

These esthetic and biological concerns have paved the way for the development of metal-free alternative materials [6, 7]. In this context, zirconia ceramics stand out as a strong alternative in dental implantology due to their tooth-like color properties, high fracture toughness, and superior soft-tissue integration [1, 7–9]. Recent mapping reviews indicate encouraging short- to mid-term survival rates for zirconia implants; however, concerns remain regarding fracture risk and limited prosthetic flexibility in certain clinical scenarios. Furthermore, recent clinical studies highlight that zirconia surfaces exhibit lower bacterial plaque affinity compared to titanium, potentially reducing the risk of peri-implant mucosal inflammation. However, the surgical placement and prosthetic rehabilitation of ceramic implants—especially one-piece systems—require strict case selection, precise three-dimensional (3D) positioning, and careful occlusal load management [10].

The widespread adoption of digital technologies and smartphone usage has fundamentally altered patients' habits regarding access to health information [11, 12]. Individuals now conduct extensive online research about treatment options before consulting their dentists.

Digital misinformation has become a significant problem in the healthcare sector, as patients may encounter incomplete, misleading, commercially driven, or scientifically inconsistent information online [13]. YouTube, which offers both visual and audio content, has become one of the most frequently consulted sources of information in the fields of medicine and dentistry [14–17]. However, the fact that content on YouTube is uploaded without undergoing any peer review raises concerns about the accuracy and reliability of the information shared [14, 18]. The visibility of videos on the platform may be influenced by engagement-based factors such as views, likes, comments, and algorithmic ranking rather than educational completeness. This may lead to algorithm-driven visibility bias [19, 20]. Therefore, digital health literacy is important for enabling patients to critically evaluate online health information and interpret it appropriately within real-world patient–clinician communication [21, 22]. In this context, popularity should not be equated with educational value, scientific reliability, or clinical accuracy. This is because viewer interactions—such as views, likes, and comments—do not necessarily indicate the adequacy of evidence-based clinical content [23, 24].

Broad online health information and general social media exposure may shape patients’ expectations, perceived treatment benefits, and pre-consultation information-seeking behavior [17, 25]. In this context, visually engaging platforms such as YouTube may influence the specific questions and concerns patients bring to clinical consultations and shared decision-making discussions [26, 27].

Although there are studies in the literature [28–30] examining the quality of YouTube videos on various dental implant modalities, to the best of our knowledge, as of our comprehensive literature search in February 2026, no study has yet been conducted that specifically evaluates content on ceramic implants on this platform. This distinction is extremely important because, unlike conventional titanium implants, ceramic implants are highlighted in the clinical literature for entirely patient-centered motivations, such as high esthetic expectations, a preference for metal-free treatment, and concerns about overall health [2, 7, 10]. As demonstrated in YouTube content related to general dental implants and medical topics, the fact that videos are often incomplete, superficial, or commercially biased [20, 29, 30] makes the independent evaluation of ceramic implant content a critical necessity; because it is known that this type of digital information pollution may influence patients’ pre-treatment expectations and the dynamics of patient-dentist communication [11, 17].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the educational quality and ceramic implant-specific content coverage of the most-watched YouTube videos on ceramic dental implants using VIQI, GQS, and SCC, and to analyze the relationship between these scores and viewer engagement metrics. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was that there would be no positive correlation between the educational quality and ceramic implant-specific content coverage of ceramic implant videos on YouTube and viewer engagement metrics.

Materials and methods

Research design and ethical considerations

This cross-sectional study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. This study analyzed publicly available content on the YouTube platform and did not involve human participants, patient records, animal subjects, or any intervention. No identifiable private information was collected. Therefore, institutional ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required.

Search strategy and pre-selection process

To assess the feasibility of the search process, a preliminary search was conducted by two independent research assistants specializing in the surgical field. To minimize temporal variability and personalization bias, all searches were conducted on February 6, 2026, in a single session. Searches were performed using newly created YouTube accounts in private/incognito browsing mode. Before the search session and between keyword searches, browser history, cache, and cookies were cleared, and no previous search or viewing history was associated with the accounts. The account location settings were set to “worldwide.” Following this standardized search process, the retrieved videos were screened according to the predefined eligibility criteria.

Six different keywords were used in English during the data collection process: “ceramic implants,” “zirconia implants,” “zirconia dental implants,” “metal-free dental implants,” “ceramic vs titanium implants,” and “zirconia implant placement.” The initial general scan without any filters applied yielded hundreds of thousands of results (e.g., 58,500 results for “zirconia implants” and 23,400 results for “ceramic implants”). To narrow down the search results to the targeted content, YouTube's advanced search option filter “exact phrase” was used.

The results obtained were filtered using both the “sort by relevance” and “sort by view count” filters. Then, in line with the study criteria, videos published between January 1, 2021, and February 6, 2026, in English, and with a duration of 4–20 min were included to exclude extremely short or excessively long content and to ensure comparability across videos. The 4–20 min duration range was selected because it corresponds to YouTube’s built-in “medium-length video” search filter. The use of this platform-specific filter provided a repeatable and standardized screening criterion. Medium-length videos were considered more likely to contain structured, patient-focused educational information compared to very short clips, while distinguishing themselves from excessively long lecture or webinar-style content. This approach is consistent with previous analyses of dental YouTube and implant-related videos [19, 20, 31] that used duration-based eligibility criteria to obtain more comparable educational content. As a result of these filters, a total of 700 videos were found from six keyword pools (“ceramic implants”: 191, “zirconia implants”: 229, “zirconia dental implants”: 268, “metal-free dental implants”: 3, “ceramic vs titanium implants”: 6, “zirconia implant placement”: 3). Because this was an exploratory cross-sectional content analysis, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Instead, all eligible videos identified within the predefined search range and meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All 700 videos were pooled and independently screened by two calibrated evaluators. First, duplicate videos from different keyword searches were identified and removed from the list. Then, all remaining videos were watched from start to finish by two evaluators and subjected to detailed content analysis.

Videos containing commercial terms, lacking standardization, brand-focused, or purely promotional (lacking educational value) were excluded from the study based on the joint decision of the two evaluators to prevent their inclusion. Promotional content was defined as content primarily focused on brand advertising, product promotion, clinic marketing, price information, or testimonial-based persuasion without structured educational explanation. Additionally, videos not directly related to the clinical topic under review were also excluded. Any potential disagreements between evaluators regarding the inclusion or exclusion of videos were resolved through a consensus meeting. Based on typical user search behavior, videos appearing within the most relevant search results and fulfilling all inclusion criteria were considered for analysis. Ultimately, 63 videos met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Data collection and parameters

Uploader source was classified as dentist, dental clinic, or dental community. Dental community was defined as a dental education group, professional dental organization, implant-related educational platform, or multi-author dental information channel that could not be classified as an individual dentist or a single dental clinic.

The following descriptive data were recorded for each included video: video title and URL address, video duration (minutes), time elapsed since the upload date (days), and uploader source (classified as dentist/dental clinic/dental community). Additionally, the view count, number of likes, number of comments, and video type (distinguished as real case or animation) were documented.

Evaluator calibration and blinding

To reduce measurement bias and standardize the scoring process, the two evaluators completed a calibration session using a randomly selected pilot subset of 10 videos before formal assessment. During this process, discrepancies were resolved by consensus and the eligibility criteria and interpretation of VIQI, GQS, and SCC scoring items were standardized before formal evaluation. After calibration, all eligible videos were scored independently by the two evaluators. During VIQI, GQS, and SCC scoring, the evaluators were blinded to viewer engagement metrics, including views, likes, comments, and subscriber counts. These engagement metrics were recorded separately only after completion of the quality and content scoring. Inter-rater reliability was then assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Quality assessment scales

The educational quality and content quality of the videos were scored using three different scales:

Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI): Consists of 4 sub-parameters: information flow, presentation clarity, video quality, and title-content consistency. Each item is scored between 1 (worst) and 5 (best) (Total score: 5–20) [30, 32].

Global Quality Scale (GQS): A single 5-point scale measuring the video's overall educational value and the benefit it provides to the viewer (1: Poor quality/Useless, 5: Excellent quality/Very useful) [30].

Specific Content Checklist (SCC): Due to the absence of a validated and standardized scale to evaluate ceramic implant-specific YouTube content, an exploratory SCC was developed within the scope of this study. The checklist was adapted from content-domain approaches established in prior implant-related YouTube studies [28, 31, 33] and was tailored to incorporate the unique clinical characteristics of ceramic implants. The SCC comprised five primary domains: indications, contraindications/limitations, surgical technique principles, prosthetic/rehabilitation-related information, and complication/risk disclosures. Each domain was scored using a binary system, where a score of 1 was assigned if the relevant information was explicitly presented in the video, and 0 if it was absent, yielding a cumulative SCC score ranging from 0 to 5 (Table 1).

Nevertheless, the SCC was not designed to evaluate the actual clinical accuracy, clinical superiority, or the relative clinical weight of each domain. Consequently, the SCC served as an exploratory content-coverage tool to assess whether core educational topics associated with ceramic implants were addressed in each video.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For continuous variables, mean (M), standard deviation (SD), minimum, and maximum values were calculated; for categorical variables, frequency and percentage distributions were reported. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values, Shapiro–Wilk/Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. For the SCC variable, although the skewness value (− 1.62) approached the acceptable ± 2 boundary, the combined distributional assessment did not indicate a serious violation of normality. Overall, because the skewness and kurtosis values for all indices remained within the acceptable ± 2 limits and visual inspections (histograms and Q–Q plots) revealed no serious distributional violations, parametric statistical tests were applied where appropriate.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between continuous variables. Multiple linear regression was not included as a primary analysis because this study was designed as an exploratory cross-sectional content analysis rather than a predictive modeling study. In addition, given the modest final sample size (N = 63), several viewer engagement metrics, including views, likes, comments, and subscriber counts, were expected to be interrelated, which could increase the risk of multicollinearity and unstable model estimates. Therefore, the relationships between video quality scores and viewer engagement metrics were evaluated primarily using correlation analyses, with corresponding effect sizes and exact p values reported to support transparent interpretation. Comparisons of VIQI, GQS, and SCC scores according to uploader source were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. When significant differences were detected, Bonferroni post-hoc tests were applied. Eta-squared (η2) values were reported as effect sizes.

Inter-rater reliability between the two independent evaluators was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with consistency type. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Descriptive characteristics and engagement metrics

A total of 63 videos met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. Verbal narration was present in 88.9% (n = 56) of the videos. Regarding the uploader source, 52.4% (n = 33) were uploaded by dentists, 34.9% (n = 22) by dental clinics, and 12.7% (n = 8) by dental communities. Almost all content used real clinical footage (96.8%, n = 61) rather than animation (3.2%, n = 2).

Viewer engagement metrics showed high heterogeneity. The mean view count was 6598.08 ± 16,827.91, mean likes were 182.89 ± 529.85, and the mean subscriber count was 15,919.00 ± 37,171.94. For the 51 videos with enabled comments, the mean comment count was 24.80 ± 62.64. Detailed descriptive data are summarized in Table 2.

A two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated to assess agreement between the two independent evaluators for VIQI, GQS, and SCC scores. Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that viewer engagement metrics were not significantly associated with either educational quality scores or ceramic implant-specific content coverage. In other words, higher view, like, comment, or subscriber counts did not necessarily correspond to higher VIQI, GQS, or SCC scores. These findings indicate that platform popularity indicators should not be interpreted as direct proxies for educational adequacy, scientific reliability, or content completeness. However, because this study did not directly evaluate patient comprehension, treatment choices, or behavioral outcomes, the findings should be interpreted as associations between video characteristics rather than evidence of direct clinical impact on patients.

Although various studies in the literature have examined the quality of YouTube content related to dental implants [30, 31, 34], no YouTube evaluation study specific to ceramic implants has been identified despite the growing clinical and esthetic interest in this topic. Therefore, the present study fills a current gap in both patient education and the clinical evaluation of digital content. As patients frequently access health-related videos before clinical consultation, YouTube may shape their expectations, perceived treatment benefits and risks, and the questions they bring to clinicians [17, 26, 27]. However, because YouTube content is not subject to standardized academic or editorial review, the educational completeness and evidence-based balance of such videos may vary considerably [27, 35]. This concern is particularly relevant for ceramic implants, where esthetic expectations, material-related concerns, and technique-sensitive clinical protocols require careful individualized explanation [10, 36].

The predominance of dentist-generated content is consistent with previous implant-related YouTube studies, which have shown that professional sources constitute a substantial proportion of videos in this field [30, 37]. This pattern may reflect both the technical nature of implant dentistry and the role of clinicians in producing patient-oriented educational content. In the present study, videos uploaded by dentists demonstrated significantly higher overall educational value, as measured by GQS, than those uploaded by dental clinics. However, uploader source alone did not guarantee greater ceramic implant-specific content coverage (SCC) or better presentation quality (VIQI). Thus, although professional origin may contribute to general educational impression, it should not be regarded as a stand-alone indicator of specific content completeness.

The divergence observed between viewer engagement and educational quality in the present study is consistent with prior infodemiological literature in medical and dental fields. Hegarty et al. reported that online health videos with lower informational quality may achieve high view counts, whereas videos with higher informational value may not always receive a similar level of public interest [23]. Similarly, Desai et al. demonstrated that the popularity of medical videos may be independent of their scientific quality [20]. This suggests that platform visibility metrics do not necessarily guarantee evidence-based reliability. Consistent findings have also been documented in recent dental YouTube studies, which showed that user engagement parameters such as views and likes are weakly associated with content usefulness or quality [24, 38]. Taken together, these findings indicate that patients may encounter highly visible content that does not necessarily provide optimal educational completeness or clinical balance.

This discrepancy should also be evaluated in the context of YouTube’s recommendation and ranking systems, alongside commercial factors that may influence platform visibility. Search and discovery systems may prioritize user retention, click-through behavior, watch time, and engagement metrics over pedagogical integrity or evidence-based balance. Although the broader pattern of engagement-driven visibility bias was identified in earlier infodemiological studies [19, 20], YouTube’s platform features and recommendation systems have evolved substantially since 2013. Recent literature nevertheless suggests that, despite this ongoing algorithmic evolution, commercial factors and popularity metrics continue to influence the visibility of digital health content [34, 35]. Consequently, professionally produced or commercially oriented videos may gain enhanced visibility through visual appeal, search optimization, persuasive narratives, patient testimonials, or esthetically favorable clinical outcomes [20, 27]. These features may contribute to cognitive bias in health-information consumption, as viewers may perceive visually well-structured or frequently watched content as more credible [23, 35]. Therefore, production quality and visual attractiveness should be viewed as potential drivers of viewer engagement rather than as direct indicators of scientific reliability, educational completeness, or clinical safety [20, 23].

The SCC findings suggest that although many videos visually present ceramic implant procedures, the coverage of core content domains specific to ceramic implantology remains variable. Key subject areas such as indications, contraindications or limitations, prosthetic planning, and complication/risk explanation were not consistently addressed across the analyzed videos. Consequently, visual presentation alone should not be equated with educational completeness or clinically balanced information. Similar observations have been documented in YouTube analyses across different dental disciplines [39, 40]. The relatively wide dispersion in SCC scores in the present study should be interpreted with caution, as the SCC is an exploratory, non-validated checklist using a dichotomous scoring system. Therefore, this variability should not be viewed as a definitive measure of factual clinical accuracy, educational depth, or clinical safety, but rather as a reflection of differences in the number of ceramic implant-specific content domains addressed across the sampled videos.

From a patient-education perspective, YouTube videos may serve as useful tools for introducing broad concepts such as metal-free alternatives, esthetic expectations, basic procedural steps, and general comparisons with titanium systems. However, these digital platforms should not be considered substitutes for individualized clinical consultation [11, 29]. Because viewer engagement metrics are not reliable indicators of educational quality or specific content coverage, patients may easily overvalue visually appealing but clinically incomplete videos. Therefore, guided digital health literacy—an individual’s ability to access, understand, and critically evaluate online health information—is essential for patient-centered implant care [21, 41]. To prevent patients from being misled, clinicians must take an active role. Rather than expecting patients to independently evaluate complex clinical parameters, clinicians should actively discuss online information during consultations, clarify misconceptions, and direct patients toward balanced, evidence-informed resources that explicitly address specific indications, limitations, risks, alternative treatment options, and long-term clinical evidence [10, 22]. This clinician-guided approach is particularly crucial when patients present with expectations shaped by commercially oriented or non-individualized digital content [22].

Several limitations should be noted. First, because YouTube algorithms and content availability are dynamic, complete search reproducibility cannot be guaranteed; therefore, this cross-sectional design captures only a static snapshot of video visibility and content characteristics. Second, the inclusion of only English-language videos restricts the generalizability of the findings to non-English-speaking populations. Third, the use of professional and material-specific keywords, such as “ceramic” and “zirconia,” improved search precision but may have excluded colloquial lay terms such as “white implants,” introducing potential keyword-selection bias. Fourth, the exclusion of purely promotional videos, which constitute a substantial part of the real-world digital information environment, may have influenced the overall quality profile of the analyzed sample. Fifth, the exploratory SCC used dichotomous coding and should therefore be interpreted as a measure of content coverage rather than factual accuracy, educational depth, or clinical safety. Finally, this study focused exclusively on YouTube, excluding rapidly growing short-form platforms such as TikTok and Instagram Reels, and did not validate video quality against patient-centered outcomes such as comprehension, recall, perception, or behavioral outcomes.

Future studies should employ longitudinal cross-platform designs, multilingual video cohorts, patient-centered validation methods, and AI-assisted misinformation detection to more accurately capture the evolving landscape of digital patient education in implant dentistry.

Conclusions

In conclusion, YouTube videos on ceramic implants are accessible and visually engaging patient-oriented information sources; however, viewer engagement metrics should not be interpreted as reliable indicators of educational quality or ceramic implant-specific content coverage. Because the SCC used in this study was exploratory and non-validated, the findings should be interpreted as indicators of content coverage rather than definitive measures of factual accuracy, educational depth, or clinical adequacy. Clinicians should support patients by promoting guided digital health literacy and directing them toward balanced educational resources that discuss indications, limitations, risks, alternative treatment options, and long-term clinical evidence. Future studies should include longitudinal and multilingual video analyses, patient comprehension assessments, formal validation of dentistry-specific content evaluation tools, AI-assisted misinformation analysis, and comparative evaluations across multiple social-media platforms.
