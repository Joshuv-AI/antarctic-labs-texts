Interpretable machine learning model for cardiovascular disease risk prediction: a feature decomposition-based study
Authors: Liliang Yu, Jiancheng Wu, Xin Wu, Chenxi Chen, Yuanjun Chen, Li Fang, Daikun Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading public health issue worldwide. The key to preventing CVD is the early prediction and identification of CVD risk factors. The aim of this study is to construct and validate CVD prediction models using machine learning (ML).MethodsThis study utilized 11 features from 68,205 CVD respondents in the Kaggle dataset. Experiments were conducted using a feature decomposition-based deep learning model (FDDL) to predict CVD incidence in this dataset. The proposed model was compared with six other machine learning models. Moreover, the SHAP method was employed to interpret the model in this study.ResultsThe FDDL model demonstrated superior predictive capability, achieving benchmark metrics of 75.52% accuracy, 78.14% precision, 71.68% recall, an F1 score of 0.7522, and an AUC-ROC value of 0.7643. In contrast, the LR model exhibited the weakest predictive ability among the compared methods. SHAP value-based feature importance ranking identified diastolic blood pressure, cholesterol level, systolic blood pressure, and age as the most critical predictors for cardiovascular disease risk assessment in our dataset.ConclusionWe have developed an ML model for predicting the risk of CVDs. This model shows potential to assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and providing a theoretical basis for personalized preventive healthcare measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24921-4.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Machine learning, Attention mechanism, Decomposition model

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major global public health issue and one of the leading causes of death [1]. According to the American Heart Association, CVD caused over 19.9 million deaths globally in 2021, with projections exceeding 23.6 million by 2030, imposing a significant burden on health care systems [2]. CVD is characterized by a prolonged asymptomatic phase with multiple risk factors, complicating clinical diagnosis and population health management. Early identification of high-risk individuals is therefore critical.

Clinical prevention guidelines suggest that risk stratification can benefit asymptomatic patients [3]. Personalized risk assessment enables early CVD detection, and the European Association of Preventive Cardiology (EAPC) emphasizes the utility of prediction models in population-level prevention and health promotion [4, 5]. Currently, many risk prediction models have been recommended in different guidelines. For example, the Framingham Cardiovascular Risk Score (FGCRS) [3, 6], Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE) [7, 8], and the QRISK score are recommended by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines, respectively [9–11]. However, these models rely primarily on statistical methods (e.g., logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards) with limited risk factors, and their accuracy is often compromised by data distribution biases or multicollinearity [12].

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence that uses large datasets to discover novel risk predictors and model their complex interactions, thereby increasing prediction accuracy. The initial phase leverages traditional machine learning with limited yet critical clinical variables (e.g., age, blood pressure, ECG features) for diagnostic support. Titapiccolo et al. pioneered the integration of LASSO regression’s feature sparsity with random forest’s ensemble advantages to develop a dynamic 6-month risk prediction framework, enhancing early intervention efficacy [13]. Notably, deep learning (DL) based on artificial neural networks is a subset of machine learning. With the rapid development of DL, its powerful feature learning and large-scale data processing abilities have attracted the attention of CVD researchers. However, DL models are often opaque black boxes that lack interpretability in decision-making processes [14, 15]. Kumar et al. further fused KNN with linear SVM metrics, achieving 96% critical-case decision accuracy with only 3.10% missed diagnoses [16]. Their follow-up employed the Hawks Optimizer (HO) to coordinate stacked deep forests, overcoming class imbalance, and introduced the binary krill herd algorithm (B-KHA) to achieve an accuracy rate of 95% [17, 18]. Pujadas et al. integrated vascular risk factors (VRFs), cardiac MRI (CMR), and radiomic features into a multimodal fusion model, achieving precise discrimination of atrial fibrillation (AUC = 0.93), heart failure (AUC = 0.89), myocardial infarction (AUC = 0.91), and stroke (AUC = 0.88) [19].

In this study, our research aims to develop a feature decomposition-based deep learning (FDDL) model for CVD risk prediction. Using medical records of 68,205 patients from the Kaggle database (11 features per patient), we performed hierarchical decomposition of risk factors (e.g., physiological indicators) and integrated an attention mechanism to prioritize key predictors, thereby optimizing disease probability estimation.

Decomposition network

For patients’ physiological data, a decomposition network is first employed to perform feature extraction. The residual decomposition architecture isolates and disentangles raw physiological features, which captures key information in the data more efficiently. Additionally, physiological features often exhibit mixed effects due to mutual interference between different characteristics. The decomposition network can disentangle these interactions, rendering each feature independent and clearly defined. Through this feature decomposition process, causal relationships and latent structures within the data become more interpretable, thereby enhancing the model’s overall explainability.

Data Source

The dataset used in the experiment is an open-source CVD dataset from Kaggle, comprising medical records of 68,205 patients. Each patient’s record includes 11 features, categorized into three groups: (1) factual information (e.g., age, weight, height), (2) examination measurements (e.g., systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, blood glucose levels), and (3) subjective indicators (e.g., smoking status, alcohol consumption, and exercise habits). The dataset can be accessed at https://www.kaggle.com/code/sulianova/eda-cardiovascular-data.

Data preprocessing

Before training the model, the data underwent a thorough preprocessing process. First, the numerical features were standardized using StandardScaler, which transforms all the input features into a standard normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, thereby eliminating the impact of varying feature scales on model performance. Categorical variables were encoded as numeric values using LabelEncoder to ensure that they could be used as inputs to the neural network. Additionally, missing values in the dataset were identified and handled using mean imputation for numerical features, ensuring the integrity of the data. Finally, the data were split into training and testing sets using train_test_split, with an 80–20 split. Stratified sampling (stratify parameter) was applied to maintain the class distribution (i.e., the presence or absence of cardiovascular disease) across both the training and testing sets, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize.

To solve the problem of imbalanced data categories, we adopted the following methods: (1) Weighted loss function: A class-weighted cross-entropy loss was used to penalize misclassification of the minority class more heavily. The loss weights were empirically set to [1.0, 1.1], emphasizing positive (cardio = 1) cases. (2) Synthetic oversampling: We employed the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) to artificially increase the presence of minority class samples in the training set. SMOTE was applied only after the train–test split to avoid data leakage. To improve model generalizability further, Gaussian noise N(0, 0.01) was added to numeric features such as systolic/diastolic blood pressure, age, and cholesterol. Additionally, feature permutation within the same class was performed on a small percentage (5%) of training samples to simulate plausible physiological variability. These steps were implemented during the training pipeline and combined with normalization, encoding, and stratified sampling.

Model explanation

The interpretation of the prediction model is performed by SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP), which precisely calculates the contribution and influence of each feature on the final prediction. In addition, each observation in the dataset can be explained by a specific set of SHAP values.

Statistical analysis

Six machine learning models, including decision tree (DT), logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), backpropagation neural network (BPNN), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), were compared with our proposed predictive model. To ensure the repeatability and clarity of the proposed FDDL model, its key architecture and training parameters are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The performance evaluation metrics included the classification accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). The dataset was partitioned into an 80–20 split for the training and validation sets, with model training conducted on the former and performance validation on the latter.

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used for continuous variables. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the means ± standard deviations and were compared using t tests. Continuous variables with skewed distributions are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared with the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentages and were compared with the chi-square test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model construction and evaluation

For the training dataset, we constructed the FDDL model and compared it with the DT, LR, SVM, RF, BP, and LightGBM models. As summarized in Table 2, the FDDL model demonstrated superior predictive capability, achieving benchmark metrics of 75.52% accuracy, 78.14% precision, 71.68% recall, an F1 score of 0.7522, and an AUC-ROC value of 0.7643. In contrast, LR exhibited the lowest performance among the compared models. The model convergence analysis revealed a progressive improvement in predictive efficacy with increasing training epochs. As illustrated in (Fig. 2A), the classification accuracy stabilized at approximately 75% by the 40th epoch, ultimately reaching peak performance (75.52%) at the 100th training iteration. Additionally, the model achieved a high true-positive rate (TPR) and a low false-positive rate (FPR), further confirming its superior predictive accuracy (Fig. 2B).

Explanation of the FDDL model with the SHAP method

The SHAP method was employed to evaluate the importance of each predictive variable in the FDDL model’s predictions. This study modeled the relationships and relative importance between features using a decomposition structure, and the feature importance analysis chart ranked the most significant variables in descending order. As shown in (Fig. 3), the most important feature was age, followed by systolic blood pressure, weight, and cholesterol. Additionally, to investigate the positive and negative relationships between predictors and the target outcome, SHAP values were utilized to identify disease risk factors. As illustrated in (Fig. 4), negative values on the horizontal axis indicate negative correlations between the input features and the outcome, whereas positive values indicate positive correlations; the magnitude of the SHAP value reflects the strength of the correlation. The size of the feature points is represented by a color gradient ranging from blue (small) to red (large). The results demonstrated that increases in diastolic blood pressure, cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, and age were significantly positively correlated with the occurrence of the disease, thereby promoting its progression.

Discussion

Artificial intelligence and machine learning are driving medical advancements, offering novel perspectives for the prediction and diagnosis of CVDs [20–22]. In this study, we developed an FDDL (feature decomposition deep learning) model and applied it to the prediction of CVDs. Compared with traditional machine learning models, the proposed FDDL model exhibited superior predictive performance (accuracy: 75.52%, AUC = 0.7643), indicating that the FDDL model can capture the key information in patients’ health records more accurately.

Although FDDL models demonstrate significant advantages in terms of prediction accuracy, traditional machine learning models (e.g., DT, RF) maintain superior interpretability [23, 24]. To address this limitation and explore the interpretability of FDDL models, we employed SHAP analysis to elucidate the model’s decision-making logic. The SHAP results identified age, systolic blood pressure, and cholesterol as key predictors of CVDs, which is consistent with the FGCRS, thereby validating both the rationality and reliability of the model’s predictions [3, 6]. This alignment reinforces the clinical validity of our model and its compatibility with existing clinical reasoning pathways. Additionally, in the dataset, the overall class distribution of the target variable (cardio) was nearly balanced (0: 50.03%, 1: 49.97%), and several predictor variables exhibited severe internal class imbalance. For example, 90.4% of the samples had normal cholesterol levels, and over 90% were nonsmokers. These skewed distributions of key risk factors can lead the model to underrepresent rare subgroups during training. Therefore, we adopted a method including a weighted loss function and synthetic oversampling to address these challenges.

During model training, we observed that network decomposition effectively reduced feature aliasing effects, thereby clarifying causal relationships between individual features, which supports the potential of this model in clinical applications. However, this study has the following limitations. First, our data were derived from the Kaggle dataset; thus, it was very difficult to completely avoid bias in the data, resulting in insufficient representativeness of the data. Second, owing to the inherent limitations of the database, we evaluated 11 available clinical feature predictors. These variables are all routine items that are easily accessible in clinical practice, which may have led to the omission of some key variables in our analysis. To improve the accuracy of these predictive models, other parameters, such as details of drug use, dosage, quantification of smoking frequency, and other lifestyle indicators, need to be considered. Molecular markers related to CVDs and other medical indicators also need to be included. Third, due to the limitations of space and computing resources in this study, the complete ablation experiment was not included in the current version. However, we still believe that the good performance of the model has not been masked. But we also recognize that external verification is needed to ensure universality. In future research, we will conduct a complete ablation study. Fourth, although the dataset did not permit explicit subgroup analysis (e.g., by gender or ethnicity), the SHAP dependence plots reveal varying risk associations across feature values, suggesting that the model captures heterogeneous risk patterns. In future multicenter studies, we will further clarify subgroup-specific risk patterns. Fifth, the data came from a single center. Further large-scale multicenter studies are needed to verify and adjust the accuracy and practicability of our model, and external validation should be conducted on datasets from different institutions or populations. In the future, we will conduct multicenter research to obtain larger-scale and more representative data and improve the generalizability of the model. Meanwhile, herein, various feature extraction techniques were evaluated to determine the best method for data representation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed an ML model for predicting the risk of CVDs. This model shows potential to assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and providing a theoretical basis for personalized preventive healthcare measures.
