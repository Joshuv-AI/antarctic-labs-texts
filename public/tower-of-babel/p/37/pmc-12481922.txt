Suture Button Fixation for Regan-Morrey Type II Coronoid Fracture of Anterior and Posterior Monteggia Fracture: A Report of Two Cases
Authors: Shiro Yoshida, Mitsuhiro Matsuura, Yusuke Ogrura, Taiki Nishimura, Koji Hiraoka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This report details two cases of Monteggia fracture-dislocations that were successfully managed using the TightRope suture button technique via the posterior approach. The first case involved a 26-year-old man with type IID posterior Monteggia fracture-dislocation. Treatments included coronoid stabilization, radial head replacement, and variable-angle elbow plate placement. Seventeen months after the injury, his elbow motion ranged from 15° to 115°, with 50° supination and 10° pronation. In the second case, a 50-year-old woman with type IID anterior olecranon fracture-dislocation achieved elbow motion from 10° to 125° at 12 months after injury, with 80° supination and 70° pronation following similar fixation. Both patients underwent surgery using a global posterior approach. TightRope fixation was used for coronoid tip fragments, whereas locking plates were used for proximal ulnar and olecranon fractures. This method eliminates the need for an anterior incision and allows open reduction internal fixation through a single incision.

Monteggia fracture-dislocations are complex injuries that typically involve proximal ulnar fractures with associated coronoid fractures, leading to disruption of the elbow joint and dislocation. The coronoid process of the proximal ulna acts as a crucial bony stabilizer, serving as a buttress to resist posterior axial displacement of the ulna.1 Biomechanical studies have suggested that fractures involving >50% of the olecranon process significantly destabilize the elbow.2 Furthermore, olecranon fractures in complex proximal ulnar fractures are often classified as O’Driscoll basal type and frequently involve tip fragments, leading to a debate regarding the optimal fixation method and approach.3 This report details two cases where reduction of anterior and posterior Monteggia fracture-dislocations and internal fixation of coronoid tip fragments were successfully performed using a suture button.

Case Report

Suture button fixation

Surgery was performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, with exposure beginning at the olecranon fracture site. For simple fractures of the radial head and neck, internal fixation was performed, and in cases of comminution, replacement with a prosthetic radial head was performed. The coronoid tip fragments were reduced from the posterior approach using TightRope (Arthrex Inc) (Fig. 1) and secured by fixation to the proximal ulna or olecranon. Case presentation

Case 1

A 26-year-old man sustained a right-type IID Monteggia fracture-dislocation after falling from a height of 3 m (Figure 2, Figure 3). One week later, definitive fixation was performed using a variable-angle elbow plate, mini-fragment screws and plates, and radial head replacement (Fig. 4). Intraoperative reduction of the anterolateral and anteromedial facets of the coronoid was confirmed using standard anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views. A 2.7 mm cannulated drill hole was created after a Kirschner guide wire was inserted from the fracture surface to the anterior aspect of the coronoid tip under direct visualization. A blunt elevator was placed against the anterior surface of the coronoid tip to provide counterpressure, allowing safe intra-articular drilling of the fragment.

A second transosseous tunnel was then created from the coronoid fracture site to the dorsal aspect of the olecranon. A TightRope suture button was passed through the coronoid tip fragment and flipped intra-articularly to prepare for posterior pullout fixation. In this case, as the fracture pattern resembled a Monteggia variant, coronoid tip fixation was performed after olecranon fixation. In addition, the round button was removed to allow retrieval of the sutures through the dorsal exit point of the olecranon.

Following anatomical reduction and fixation of the olecranon using a variable-angle plate, the TightRope suture was dorsally tightened with the olecranon plate to reduce and stabilize the coronoid tip fragment. During final tightening, the trochlea served as a counterforce, facilitating secure anatomic reduction of the coronoid fragment (Fig. 5).Figure 5Radiographic lateral view showing stable elbow joint A extension and B flexion.

After surgery, the arm was immobilized in a long-arm splint for 1 week, after which gentle range-of-motion exercises were initiated. At the latest follow-up, 17 months after injury, the patient demonstrated elbow motion from 15° to 115°, with 50° of supination. Only 10° of pronation was achieved because the patient had a neurological issue (Fig. 6). Case 2

A 50-year-old woman fell from a height of 1.5 m, sustaining a right-type IID anterior olecranon fracture-dislocation (Fig. 7). Definitive fixation was completed using TightRope and a variable-angle elbow plate, along with ulnar nerve exploration and decompression through a global posterior approach. The olecranon fracture was located more proximally in this case than in case 1, allowing the olecranon to be flipped and the coronoid fragment to be reduced to the ulnar metaphysis under direct visualization. A guide wire was passed from the dorsal aspect of the ulna to the coronoid tip fragment to create a transosseous tunnel with a 2.7-mm cannulated drill. The coronoid fragment was then reduced under direct visualization while the TightRope was pulled out posteriorly. The end button was positioned over the center of the coronoid process and fastened with a TightRope (Figure 8, Figure 9). A variable-angle plate was used to stabilize the olecranon fractures (Fig. 10). The patient’s arm was placed in a long-arm splint for wound rest. The splint was removed 1 week later, and gentle range-of-motion exercises were initiated. At the most recent follow-up, 12 months after injury, her elbow motion ranged from 10° to 125°, with 80° supination and 70° pronation (Fig. 11).Figure 7Case 2. Discussion

After reduction of posterior fractures, such as posterior fracture-dislocation of the olecranon or posterior Monteggia fractures, through the posterior approach, a fragment-specific approach involving repositioning of the patient to address anterior coronoid fragments via an anterior approach is associated with significant invasiveness and extended operative time. Coronoid process fractures of the ulna can be treated using several approaches. First, regarding the anterior approach, the median nerve and brachial artery should be retracted medially to improve visualization of the coronoid fracture; furthermore, ligation of any arteries or venous branches crossing the site and obstructing exposure may be required.4 Second, the medial approach is recommended for the treatment of comminuted or anteromedial coronoid fractures.5 However, for fractures involving the sublime tubercle, such as O’Driscoll anteromedial subtype 3, we additionally perform medial exposure using the Taylor and Scham approach to apply a buttress plate, as illustrated in case 1. Therefore, our technique enables surgeons to repair the coronoid process with minimal dissection, potentially lowering the risk of heterotopic ossification.

Various techniques are available for fixation of coronoid process fractures, including lag screw fixation, miniplate fixation, anchor screw fixation, and the suture lasso method.6 For common small coronoid process tip fractures or comminuted tip fractures, screw fixation can easily cause iatrogenic fractures and is difficult to perform accurately. The suture button construct used in our cases functions biomechanically as a tension band, providing dynamic resistance against anterior translation of the coronoid fragment. This principle is supported by a recent biomechanical time-zero study that demonstrated that suture button fixation provides comparable resistance to the displacement provided by traditional screw fixation for Regan-Morrey type II coronoid fractures.7 The suture button system allows for secure fragment capture while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic fracture propagation, especially in small coronoid tip fragments.

The TightRope system offers several advantages: it is technically easy to use and enables bone-preserving fixation, making it particularly advantageous for small fracture fragments. The use of TightRope in fracture management typically allows for fragment manipulation and reduction without anterior exposure, thereby preserving the biological integrity of the fragments and minimizing the risk of iatrogenic damage to the neurovascular structures.8 Another advantage of the TightRope system is that it does not rely on anterior compression using a clamp. Instead, compression is achieved by tensioning between the two buttons, which provides stable fixation without requiring direct access to the anterior ulna. This technique also helps avoid the risk of fragment displacement that may occur when placing screws from posterior to anterior—particularly when a screw inserted from the dorsal proximal ulna pushes the coronoid fragment away during insertion.

In this study, we addressed Regan-Morrey type II coronoid fractures in the context of anterior and posterior Monteggia-type injuries. Based on the recent systematic review by Nieboer et al,9 these injuries are more precisely classified as transulnar fracture-dislocations, subtype C, which involve proximal ulna disruption with associated coronoid and often radial head fractures.

The Wrightington three-column classification further supports surgical planning in these complex injuries.10 In both of our cases, the posterior and medial columns were disrupted, with the coronoid acting as a medial stabilizer. Although traditional fixation of coronoid fragments—particularly involving the anteromedial facet—often requires anterior or medial exposure, the TightRope pullout technique is most appropriately applied to bifacet coronoid fractures without a sublime tubercle fragment. This targeted approach enables minimally invasive yet stable fixation using a single posterior incision.

Despite these promising results, this study has several limitations. The TightRope system is associated with higher implant costs than other techniques, such as the suture lasso method using FiberWire (Arthrex), which may impact its accessibility in certain settings. Furthermore, not all fracture patterns are amenable to this technique; it is best suited for larger coronoid tip fragments without severe comminution. In highly comminuted fractures, secure fixation using a suture button construct may be technically challenging or insufficient. Suture tensioning pulls the fragment against the trochlea with bony guidance. However, rotational instability correction is limited without direct access. Nonetheless, the TightRope technique remains a valuable, minimally invasive option for achieving anatomical reduction and stable fixation in select cases of olecranon fracture-dislocations.

In summary, the TightRope suture button technique is a minimally invasive and effective method for fixing coronoid fractures in complex Monteggia fracture-dislocations. This method may offer a valuable option for surgeons to manage these challenging cases.
