Factors associated with intentions to pursue pediatric subspecialties: a cross-sectional study among medical residents of the Federal District, Brazil
Authors: Renata Orlandi Rubim, Sérgio Eduardo Soares Fernandes, Nathalia Paredes Rodrigues, Adriana de Rezende Dias, Alexandre Peixoto Serafim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe choice of a medical specialty is multifactorial, influenced by personal, professional, and institutional factors. In pediatrics, understanding the factors associated with attitudes and intentions toward pursuing subspecialty training is crucial for addressing workforce planning and educational strategies. This study aimed to identify the factors associated with pediatric residents' attitudes and intentions toward pursuing subspecialty training in the Federal District, Brazil.MethodsA cross-sectional, survey-based study was conducted among pediatric residents from programs affiliated with the Federal District Health Department. Data were collected via a structured electronic questionnaire. The instrument included sociodemographic variables and items assessing attitudes toward subspecialization using a Likert-scale. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, cluster analysis, mediation analysis, and longitudinal evaluation.ResultsAmong 73 respondents (response rate 49.6%), the majority were female (83.6%), with a median age of 28 years. About 74.5% expressed interest in pursuing a pediatric subspecialty. Background

The selection of a medical specialty is a critical decision shaped by a complex interplay of personal inclinations, professional aspirations, and institutional influences [Bland et al. 1995] [3]. In pediatrics, understanding the determinants behind the decision to pursue subspecialty training is crucial, which can significantly impact career trajectory, clinical focus, and workforce distribution [Pelley & Carnes 2020] [14].

Currently, the medical residency program in Pediatrics lasts three years in Brazil. Following this, pediatricians may pursue subspecialty training, which typically requires an additional two to three years, a structure comparable to many North American systems but distinct in its public healthcare system context and distribution of training positions [6]. Despite this evolution in the level of training, there is a scarcity of pediatricians and subspecialists working in the Unified Health System (SUS) in the country, which creates difficulties in the care of children and adolescents, especially in more vulnerable regions [Araujo, 2018] [1].

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of role models, clinical experiences during training, lifestyle considerations, and gender dynamics in specialty selection [Heiligers 2012, Kahvo et al. 2023, Khan 2023] [9–11, 17]. However, most research has been conducted in high-income countries, and there is a scarcity of data from middle-income settings like Brazil, where healthcare systems and training structures differ substantially.

This study aimed to bridge this gap by investigating the factors associated with pediatric residents' attitudes and intentions toward pursuing subspecialty training in the Federal District of Brazil. By identifying these determinants, we hope to inform educational policies and support strategies that align with residents’ aspirations and societal needs.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional, survey-based analytical study was conducted between September and October 2024 among pediatric residents affiliated with the Federal District Health Department.

Participants

The study was conducted in the Federal District. While a precise national figure is fluid, according to the Brazilian Ministry of Education, there are 2.918 pediatric residency positions nationwide annually. All the 147 residents in the District were invited, with 73 participants (response rate 49.6%). All professionals who had already completed the pediatrics residency program, resident physicians who were currently undertaking a pediatric subspecialty residency. Resident physicians on prolonged leaves of absence of more than four months were excluded because many could not be reached in time to meet the survey deadline. Furthermore, we considered that their prolonged absence from the clinical and academic environment could significantly alter their perspectives and exposure to factors influencing career choice, making them non-representative of the actively training resident population.

The tool used for data collection was REDCap, provided by the Health Sciences Education and Research Foundation (FEPECS), with control and access to electronic questionnaires and databases restricted to the researchers. For participation, the informed consent form (ICF) was applied. The questionnaire was sent by the supervisors of each residency program between September and November 2024, with two intermittent reminders.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described with medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables with absolute and relative frequencies of the categories and their respective confidence intervals.

Analyses were performed using Python 3.9 and the SciPy, scikit-learn, and statsmodels libraries.

The results were presented in tables and stacked percentage bar charts. Missing data were excluded from specific analyses.

Scale construction and validation

The three items underwent internal consistency analysis using Cronbach's Alpha coefficient (α = 0.724), demonstrating adequate reliability for composing a unidimensional scale in exploratory research [Taber 2018] [18]. The final score ranged from −6 to + 6, where higher values indicate a more favorable attitude toward subspecialization.

Mediation analysis

A simple mediation model was tested according to the Baron and Kenny (1986) approach [2], using linear regression to estimate direct and indirect paths, bootstrapping with 5,000 samples for confidence interval calculation.

Cross-sectional comparison by residency year

Cross-sectional comparison by residency year was analyzed using one-way ANOVA with residency year as a factor (R1, R2, R3), Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons, analysis of cluster distribution by residency year.

Additional analyses

Pearson correlation was used for continuous variables, and Student's t-test was used for comparison between sexes, adopting a significance level of p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

The sample was predominantly female (83.6%), with a median age of 28. Most were single (58.9%), without children (82.2%), and graduated from private institutions (57.5%), as presented in Table 1.

Attitudes toward subspecialization

Seventy-four point five percent intended to pursue a subspecialty. The most frequently cited preferred subspecialties were Neurology, Endocrinology, and Neonatology (each selected by 20.9% of respondents). Work profile and free time were the most weighted factors (40.7% and 39%, respectively). Mediation analysis

Perception of employability partially mediated the effect of belief in quality of life in general pediatrics on attitude toward subspecialization (23.5% of total effect, Additional File 1).

Comparison across residency years

No statistically significant difference in overall mean attitude scores was found across residency years (F[2,99] = 0.48; p = 0.622) (Additional File 2 and 3). Post-hoc Tukey tests confirmed no pairwise differences between any residency years (all p > 0.05), indicating that attitudes toward subspecialization remain relatively stable during pediatric residency training. While overall mean attitude scores did not change significantly, the distribution of attitudinal profiles showed a descriptive pattern: Enthusiasts decreased from R1 to R3, while Satisfied increased.

Discussion

This study identified multiple factors associated with pediatric residents' attitudes toward subspecialization, aligning with international literature [Stahn & Harendza 2014] [9–11, 17]. The predominance of women in the sample reflects global trends in pediatrics [Fraher et al. 2024] [7], and the influence of role models and clinical exposure echoes findings from Stahn & Harendza [17] and Kahvo et al. [10].

Gender-specific factors, though not deeply explored here, are critical. Khan’s review [11] emphasizes how gendered experiences and discrimination can steer career choices. The sample was predominantly female (83%), reflecting the gender composition of the specialty in Brazil, according to data from the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics (SBP) [Sociedade Brasileira de Pediatria, 2024].

Our findings reveal three distinct attitudinal profiles among pediatric residents regarding subspecialization, each with unique guidance needs. This segmentation underscores the importance of tailored career counseling approaches rather than a one-size-fits-all strategy. The importance of work-life balance and employability resonates with studies highlighting lifestyle as a key determinant in specialty selection [9, 10]. However, we identified that the perception of employability acts as a partial mediator (accounting for 23.5% of the effect) in the relationship between beliefs about quality of life in general pediatrics and the overall attitude towards pursuing further specialization. This highlights that the decision is not solely based on the anticipated lifestyle but is significantly influenced by the resident's assessment of how specialization will impact their future job prospects and career stability. The mediating role of employability perceptions underscores the need for clear career pathways and financial security in subspecialty training [7]. While international studies often highlight financial incentives as a major driver, there is a more nuanced reality in the Brazilian public healthcare context. Concerns about job security and the public health system’s capacity to absorb subspecialists are significant, rather than high income alone.

A notable finding was the difference in attitudinal profiles across residency years. Residents in earlier years (R1) were more frequently classified as 'Enthusiasts', while those in later years (R3) were more frequently classified as 'Satisfied with General Pediatrics'. This cross-sectional pattern suggests that the factors influencing attitudes may differ by stage of training. It is important to note that our cross-sectional design did not measure intra-individual change over time. However, the difference in attitudinal profiles across residency years aligns with findings from longitudinal studies, such as that by Macy et al. (2018), which tracked the evolution of fellowship intentions within individuals during pediatric residency [Macy et al. 2018] [12]. This suggests that the residency experience itself, potentially due to exposure to workload realities, burnout, or a lack of sustained inspiration, can dampen the early ambition for specialized training and reinforces the need for structured mentorship and career guidance programs throughout residency, not only at its inception.

Also, the culture of residency programs, including the proportion of interns interested in fellowship and the size of the program, affects the likelihood of pursuing subspecialty training. Decisions are made earlier in programs with a strong subspecialty culture and later in those with less emphasis on fellowship [12].

Another possibility is that the additional training fosters greater confidence and a sense of preparedness among residents, which is particularly relevant given that Brazilian pediatric residency programs last three years [Campos et al. 2019, Vaz & Campos Junior 2015] [5, 19].

Regarding employability, the current context of pediatrics in Brazil's Federal District and nationwide must be considered. Pediatricians were traditionally responsible for well-child care in a non-territorialized population. This role has now largely shifted to family health teams providing territorialized care, mirroring systems in countries like Canada and the UK. However, despite constitutional guarantees of universal healthcare access, coverage in Brazil is not complete. Pediatric care is not a prioritized model, and there is an increasing burden of chronic conditions alongside persistent infectious/parasitic diseases and external causes, even with a significant reduction in preventable infant mortality.

Low productivity and resolutivity of primary care for pediatric patients, coupled with overcrowded emergency departments (especially during respiratory illness seasonality) and increased referrals to subspecialists with long wait times, create a challenging and potentially unattractive work environment for new professionals. This has led to questions about the pediatrician's role in this modern context [Brandão 2019, Norman 2019, Prentice 2017, Souza et al. 2019] [4, 13, 15, 16].

A study on healthcare access and quality performance by age group showed improvement across all evaluated countries, including Brazil. While scores varied by sociodemographic index (lower in low- and middle-income countries), the youngest age group (0–14 years) showed the highest estimated convergence [GBD 2019 Healthcare Access and Quality Collaborators 2022] [8]. This encouraging result points towards reduced inequalities in healthcare access and quality. It also suggests a pivotal role for pediatricians, working in articulation with primary care teams to provide not only clinical care but also health promotion and prevention services.

Practical suggestions informed by the factors associated with subspecialization intentions and the noted scarcity of subspecialists to consider are based on the patterns identified in our data. These suggestions are directly informed by our key findings: the existence of distinct attitudinal profiles, the partial mediating role of employability perceptions, and the observed distribution of these profiles across training years. This should be mindful of the need for empirical support and feasibility.

- Educational and residency programs should explore opportunities to ensure residents and students receive high-quality, consistent exposure to pediatric subspecialty role models and dedicated mentors.

- This engagement may prioritize models who demonstrate enthusiasm and articulate the benefits of their career, as positive attitude and perceived value are associated with an increased intention to pursue subspecialization.

- Recognizing the influence of work environment and concerns related to burnout, institutional leaders could consider and pilot strategies to improve the sustainability of a subspecialty career.

- Efforts to optimize workload, ensure adequate support, and address challenges to work-life balance may help increase the appeal of subspecialty training.

- To better align career aspirations with the needs of the community and the SUS, educational authorities could review and integrate information about regional and national subspecialty workforce shortages into career counseling.

Professional development should be informed by and responsive to public health requirements, thereby supporting policy development aimed at aligning resident career decisions with societal needs [8]. Further study is warranted to test the efficacy of specific programmatic changes (e.g., mandatory early rotations, increased compensation for in-demand subspecialists) to ensure that practical recommendations are robustly supported by evidence.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of attitudes at a single point in time and precludes any inference of causality or the assessment of true temporal evolution within individuals. The differences observed between residency years represent comparisons between distinct cohorts, not longitudinal changes. Second, and fundamentally, this study assessed attitudes, beliefs, and intentions toward subspecialization, not actual career choices or decisions. Measured intentions, especially in early training, are not definitive predictors of future career pathways. Third, while the response rate was acceptable, the potential for selection bias remains, as respondents may have had a stronger pre-existing interest in the topic than non-respondents. Fourth, the sample was drawn from a single region and a specific network of residency programs within Brazil's Federal District. Although the demographic profile aligns with national trends in pediatrics, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other regions or countries with different healthcare systems and training structures. Fifth, residents' attitudes are dynamic and can be substantially influenced by later clinical exposures, mentorship, personal circumstances, and external constraints—such as the limited availability of subspecialty training positions and variable job markets—which this study could not capture. Finally, while the attitudinal scale demonstrated adequate reliability, the use of a limited set of items may not encompass all relevant dimensions influencing this complex decision. Future multi-center longitudinal studies, incorporating qualitative components, are needed to track how intentions translate into actual career choices and to explore the contextual and systemic barriers involved.

Implications for medical education

Early clinical exposure and positive role models are essential.

Mentorship programs should be formalized.

Transparency in subspecialty training outcomes (employment, satisfaction) may aid decision-making.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study identified key factors associated with pediatric residents' attitudes and intentions toward pursuing subspecialty training within a Brazilian public health training network. We found that perceptions of future employability, beliefs about the quality of life in general pediatrics, and early clinical experiences are significantly linked to residents' favorability toward further specialization. The identification of three distinct attitudinal profiles—Enthusiasts, Neutral/Pragmatists, and those Satisfied with General Pediatrics—highlights the heterogeneity in resident perspectives. Furthermore, the perception of improved employability emerged as a partial mediator, connecting lifestyle beliefs to overall attitude. These findings, which reflect measured intentions rather than final career outcomes, contribute to a nuanced understanding of the factors that may shape career deliberations during residency. They may inform the design of more tailored, evidence-based career guidance discussions within pediatric training programs.
