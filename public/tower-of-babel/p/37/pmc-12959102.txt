Bilateral Pulmonary Embolism With Bi-atrial Cardiac Thrombi Entrapped in a Patent Foramen Ovale
Authors: Denise Mourad, Fahd E Mohamed, Yashi Ballal, Elie Mrad, Natasa Petreska, Sachin Singh, Paritharsh Ghantasala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBi-atrial thrombi with bilateral pulmonary embolism (PE) through a patent foramen ovale (PFO) is rare and life-threatening, requiring urgent management due to embolization risk. We present a 53-year-old female with left lower extremity extensive deep vein thrombosis (DVT), bilateral PE, and bi-atrial thrombi straddling a PFO. She underwent thrombectomy, PFO closure, and pulmonary embolectomy but later experienced recurrent DVT and PE. Anticoagulation was limited due to bleeding risk given a recent neurosurgery. Literature lacks clear guidelines for managing such cases necessitating individualized treatment. This case highlights the complexity of bi-atrial thrombi with PFO and the need for further research to optimize management strategies.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Patent foramen ovale, embolectomy, anticoagulation, Biatrial cardiac thrombi

Introduction

Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) in the lower extremities forms occlusive thrombi that, if they migrate to the vena cava and right heart, can lead to pulmonary embolism (PE), potentially causing a massive PE and right heart strain. For venous thromboembolism to enter the arterial circulation, communication between the right and left atria, such as in atrial septal defect (ASD) or patent foramen ovale (PFO), is required.1 Venous thrombi can also travel from the left atrium to the left ventricle, entering systemic circulation and causing infarcts in multiple organs, including the brain, intestines, kidneys, and eyes. This phenomenon is called paradoxical embolism, which can be life-threatening.2

Case report

We present a case of a woman in her 50s with a recent craniotomy for a right frontal brain abscess due to an oral abscess, requiring multiple washout procedures. She presented for postoperative follow-up with unilateral left lower extremity (LE) edema and no prior DVT history. Her vital signs were unremarkable. On examination, she had residual left LE weakness, new onset left LE swelling, and tenderness in the left calf. Cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were normal, and she denied chest pain or shortness of breath. Venous duplex ultrasound revealed extensive DVT in the left LE along the peroneal, posterior tibial, popliteal, and femoral veins. A chest computed tomography angiogram (CTA) showed PE in the bilateral lobar, segmental, and subsegmental branches with moderate clot burden and no evidence of right heart strain. She was started on IV heparin and underwent a mechanical thrombectomy of the left LE for symptomatic DVT.

Due to stable vital signs with no need for supplemental oxygen, the patient did not require PE thrombectomy. A 2D echocardiogram showed mobile echo-densities in both atria (Supplemental figure 1, figure 2 (https://scholarlycommons.gbmc.org/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1529&window=additional_files&context=jchimp)). Given the institution’s limitations to do mechanical thrombus retrieval and her contraindication for systemic thrombolysis due to recent brain surgery, she was transferred to a larger center. There, a repeat 2D echocardiogram revealed a 4 cm thrombus originating in the left interatrial septum on one end and freely mobile on the other end, crossing the mitral valve. Additionally, a right atrial thrombus with a PFO was identified. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was moderately elevated at 51.4 mmHg (normal: 15–30 mmHg). The patient was taken to the operating room. After midline sternotomy and under cardiopulmonary bypass, the mass was found to be densely attached to the septum. She had a right atriotomy, mass excision, septal tissue removal, PFO closure, and bilateral pulmonary artery embolectomy. Pathology and cultures of the mass were negative, and the mass was consistent with clotted aggregated blood.

The next day, her course was complicated by left-sided weakness. Computed Tomography (CT) head revealed worsening brain edema within the right frontal lobe, a progressive leftward midline shift and sub-falcine herniation. This led to an urgent craniotomy for resection and removal of residual brain abscess and craniectomy. Following surgery, she showed rapid neurological improvement. Due to recent brain surgery and anemia, anticoagulation was withheld, an inferior vena cava filter (IVCF) at the infrarenal level was placed, and she was discharged stable after five days.

Seven days later, she presented with left LE swelling. CTA revealed the unresolved thrombi in the pulmonary arteries, and a new partially occlusive thrombus in the inferior vena cava extending from the IVCF distally to the bifurcation, and bilateral LE DVT. She was started on heparin, transitioned to apixaban, and discharged on lifetime oral anticoagulation (Supplemental Figure 3 (https://scholarlycommons.gbmc.org/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1529&window=additional_files&context=jchimp)). Inpatient investigations were positive only for lupus anticoagulant, but repeat testing was infeasible due to Eliquis use, which required a 1–2 week hold, increasing thrombosis risk. A follow-up echocardiogram two months later showed complete thrombus resolution. Neurologically, she remained intact, with residual left LE weakness; however, she was fully ambulatory and did not develop any cardiovascular complications, including heart failure.

Discussion

PE is a common and fatal cardiovascular disorder, with 600,000 cases and 100,000 deaths annually in the U.S.3 It typically results from a dislodged embolus, most often from the lower extremities.4 Our patient developed bilateral pulmonary embolism with bi-atrial thrombi. Despite no inherent risk factors, her recent surgery significantly increased clot risk, as major surgeries like craniotomy raise venous thromboembolism odds tenfold.5

Intra-atrial thrombi in the left atrium are rare and typically occur in the context of left atrial enlargement, often associated with conditions such as restrictive cardiomyopathy and atrial fibrillation. Right atrial thrombi are most commonly associated with DVT, and pulmonary embolism.6 Bi-atrial thrombi are rare, and when combined with a PFO, the risk of paradoxical embolus increases.2 Furthermore, PFOs are typically hemodynamically silent, and do not produce an audible murmur in the absence of significant shunting. A murmur may occasionally be detected when associated conditions are present—such as an atrial septal aneurysm or elevated right-sided pressures—that promote right-to-left shunting and turbulent flow. In rare cases, a faint systolic murmur may be heard due to subtle flow turbulence across the foramen.7

Although the blood and thrombus cultures were negative, and there were no signs of systemic infection at presentation, the presence of a brain abscess raised the theoretical concern for septic embolism. Previous reports have described cases of brain abscesses in the setting of a “silent” PFO.8,9 These cases typically involved patients presenting with systemic illness and, in some instances, they also had atrial vegetations presumed to be septic in nature.10 In contrast, our patient’s clinical presentation and the histopathology of the atrial masses were not consistent with septic embolism. Moreover, thrombus resolution following anticoagulation therapy, that aided with the patient’s overall clinical improvement, further supported this conclusion.

According to randomized prospective studies like the Assessment of Cardioversion Using Transesophageal Echocardiography (ACUTE) trial, more than 88.2 % of thrombi in the heart arise in the left atrial appendage.11 The rarity of right atrial thrombi, seen in only 11.8 % of cases in the ACUTE trial, highlights the uniqueness of thrombi straddling a PFO, which significantly increases mortality risk by 27 %–45 % even with treatment, and 100 % in untreated cases.11 The rarity of recurrent bilateral PE after surgical thrombectomy, pulmonary artery embolectomy, and PFO closure with IVCF placement further complicates management. Early identification and treatment are crucial due to the high risk of systemic embolization.

While no clear guidelines exist for managing biatrial thrombi with straddling PFO, treatments like anticoagulation, thrombolysis, and surgical thrombectomy are common.12 Some studies suggest surgical thrombectomy is superior to medical treatment,13 while others report comparable outcomes. 12,14 Recent advancements in aspiration catheter devices, such as AngioVac, offer an alternative to surgery but were not used in this case due to the risk of embolus dislodgement.15

Anticoagulation has advantages in certain cases due to its quick onset and monitoring capability using the activated partial thromboplastin time ratio.12,14 However, in our patient, her recent brain surgery and high bleeding risk made anticoagulation and thrombolysis higher-risk options and surgical intervention the preferred approach.

Similarly, Nakamura et al. reported recurrent PE despite anticoagulation and IVCF placement, with a thrombus trapped in a PFO, managed with surgical embolectomy and PFO closure.16 This highlights the need for vigilant monitoring in similar cases. Ghaffar et al. and Myers et al. also favored surgery for bi-atrial thrombi straddling a PFO.4,17 These cases, including ours, suggest surgery is preferred to mitigate systemic embolization and bleeding risks of thrombolytics or anticoagulants.

Despite successful surgical removal of the thrombus, our patient developed extensive DVT and unresolved PE, likely due to the thrombogenic nature of the surgical site and contraindications to anticoagulation.18 Unfortunately, further hypercoagulability workup could not be pursued because continued anticoagulation was necessary.

Considering patient medical histories and identifying pertaining risk factors are pivotal steps in management, especially when dealing with rare cases without any established guidelines. The patient’s recent surgery, PFO, and components of Virchow’s triad added significant complexity, highlighting the need for personalized care. The absence of definitive guidelines stresses the importance of ongoing research to develop evidence-based approaches for managing complex cardiovascular cases. Until then, management should be individualized for each patient, based on the comorbidities and hemodynamic status of the patient.

In conclusion, this case highlights the complex decision-making required for managing rare conditions like bi-atrial thrombi with straddling PFO and bilateral PE. It emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary, patient-centered approach that considers both medical and surgical options, particularly after major surgeries. This case underscores the importance of developing evidence-based, tailored strategies and balancing treatment risks to improve patient outcomes.
