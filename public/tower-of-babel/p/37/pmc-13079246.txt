Fiber Track Length Gradients in the Avian Sound Localization Circuit Require Conduction Velocity Gradients to Maintain Isochronicity
Authors: David M. Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe chicken brainstem sound localization neural network exhibits a case where fiber length varies continuously across the fiber arrays connecting n. magnocellularis and n. laminaris. Detailed morphometric measurements were taken to quantify these length gradients. For both ipsilateral and contralateral projections, the path lengths connecting the caudal low‐frequency segments are longer than those at the rostral high‐frequency end, with a linear path length gradient between them. The length and the length gradient of the contralateral projection increase from hatchling to 2–4 weeks. Synchronous transmission of information is achieved if there is a conduction velocity gradient across both the ipsilateral and contralateral fiber tracts. Adjustments of conduction velocity are required at both the cellular and population levels. A model is proposed that balances conduction distances and velocities by varying the number of nodes of Ranvier along a gradient to achieve isochronicity across the entire network. Continued differential growth between fiber arrays requires a continuous timing adjustment during development and maturation. Control is assigned to the oligodendrocytes that produce and maintain myelin.

Introduction

An emerging concept in neuroscience is isochronicity in neural networks (Kimura and Itami 2009; Pajevic et al. 2013). There are many locations in the brain where information from various sources must travel along pathways of different lengths and arrive synchronously at a postsynaptic site. In these circumstances, there is a need to adjust conduction velocities to achieve isochronicity. Myelination provides dynamic regulation of the timing of communication among various neuronal connections with different lengths and is a candidate for controlling timing in the auditory pathway (Cramer and Rubel 2016).

As an example, topographic information from sensory systems needs to be faithfully transmitted for central processing without distortion caused by unequal path lengths within the topographic projection. In the visual system, retinal ganglion X‐cells provide precise spatiotemporal mapping information to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the cat. The distal portion of the axon is unmyelinated as it crosses the retina and conducts at about 1–2 m/s. When the fiber reaches the optic disk, it gains myelination, and the proximal portion propagates at 20–40 m/s. Stanford (1987a, 1987b) observed temporal compensation in measured conduction times of individual HRP‐labeled cells. Despite large distance disparities in retinal location relative to the optic disk, the velocities in each distal and proximal segment were balanced to produce a constant conduction latency from the soma to the terminal in the lateral geniculate nucleus.

The ability of animals to identify the location of a sound source and recognize an auditory space is established in the brainstem, where signals from both ears are first analyzed in second‐order auditory nuclei. In the gerbil, a complex circuit of bilateral excitatory and inhibitory elements converges on bipolar cells in the medial superior olivary nucleus that compare bilateral inputs for differences in the time of arrival at the two ears. Since these differences are less than a millisecond, there is a need for precise temporal synchrony. Isochronicity is maintained, despite differences in path lengths for ipsilateral and contralateral inputs and additional synaptic delay through interneurons (Grothe et al. 2010), by the adjustment of conduction velocities to synchronize delay times (Seidl et al. 2014).

A similar length disparity exists in the avian brainstem auditory system (C. E. Carr and Konishi 1990; Overholt et al. 1992). Nucleus magnocellularis (NM) and nucleus laminaris (NL), bilateral avian brainstem auditory nuclei, are interconnected in a neural network that compares interaural sound delays and spatially codes the sound source location in the medial/lateral dimension of NL (Overholt et al. 1992; Jeffress 1948; Joseph and Hyson 1993). Ipsilateral and contralateral signals need to be synchronized bilaterally to compare the time of arrival at each ear (C. Carr et al. 2016). However, ipsilateral and contralateral branch path lengths are significantly different and require different conduction velocities to maintain isochronicity. C. E. Carr and Konishi (1990) suggest that timing in this network is controlled by the distance between nodes of Ranvier in myelinated segments (Arancibia‐Cárcamo et al. 2017; Waxman and Melker 1971). Seidl et al. (2010) discovered greater internode distances (INDs) between nodes in the longer contralateral branches than in the shorter ipsilateral branches, which compensates for differences in path length. The timing and spatial relationships of the circuit have been studied extensively at the cellular level. The specific aim of this study was to perform a detailed 3D reconstruction and morphometric analysis of the NM and NL shape and location, and of the interconnecting path lengths, to better understand the spatial and temporal characteristics of the entire sound source localization circuit at the population level.

A morphometric analysis of this network predicts that there is a gradient in conduction velocities mediated by axon diameter and IND in the rostral/caudal dimension. This gradient arises from spatial constraints on path length imposed by circuit morphology and the surrounding neuroanatomy. In Section 4, we estimate the number of nodes in the ipsilateral, contralateral, rostral, and caudal pathways. The results suggest two different mechanisms controlling conduction velocity (CV). At the cellular level, the conduction delay of ipsilateral and contralateral projections is synchronized with a similar number of nodes in the two pathways. At the population level, a gradient in the number of nodes compensates for a length gradient across the fiber array.

Materials and Methods

White leghorn chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus, RRID:NCBITaxon_9031), aged 0–27 days posthatch, were used in this study. Specimens from an electrophysiology study were routinely processed for electrode track reconstruction using the photomicrotome system described below. Six image stacks from this group were used for morphometric analysis.

Photomicrotome and 3D Reconstructions

This system provides accurate spatial reconstructions for morphometric analyses (Seidl et al. 2010; Harris et al. 1990) and for the identification of electrode tracks. Subjects are anesthetized (chickens: urethane 1 mg/kg, ketamine 20 mg/kg) and perfused intracardiacally with 0.9% saline plus 0.4% heparin, followed by a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde and 1% methylene blue. The methylene blue provides high contrast for in situ imaging (Figure 2). The entire head is further fixed in paraformaldehyde for 24 h, rapidly decalcified in RDO (Apex Engineering, Aurora, IL) for 3–4 h, and passed through sucrose gradients (10%, 20%, and 30%) over 48 h for cryoprotection, then blocked in the coronal plane. Heads are then put in embedding medium, Tissue‐Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura, Torrance, CA), under vacuum to remove air from the middle ear cavities.

A digitized image stack of aligned and vital‐stained serial sections (Figure 2) is made with a photomicrotome, which is basically a frozen‐section microtome mounted on a photographic copy stand with the cut face of the specimen in the focal plane of a digital camera (Nikon D200, 10.5 Mpix). A bellows and Nikkor macro lens provide 1–50x magnification. The specimen is placed on the stage, covered with embedding medium, and packed in dry ice. Precise X–Y calibration is achieved with an image of a microscope reticule at the top of the image stack (see graphical abstract). The Z‐axis was calibrated to produce sections at 22‐µm intervals. Frozen sections are acquired, and before each section is taken, a digital image of the cut surface is captured with a footswitch. That tissue section is paired with that image during analysis. The images are uploaded automatically via Nikon Capture 4 V4.4 software (Nikon Corp., Melville, NY). This process yields a stack of high‐contrast, perfectly aligned images, undistorted by histological manipulation, with about 10% shrinkage from fixation. The entire brain and head are imaged in situ (Figure 2). Histological sections containing the auditory nerve (AN), n. angularis (NA), NM, and NL are saved, mounted on microscope slides, and stained with Cresyl violet to aid in identifying recording locations, cells, nuclear boundaries, and other structures during reconstructions. Individual neurons (red and yellow dots in Figure 1) can be identified in the digital images by referring to the paired histology slide that is viewed side by side with a light microscope.

For this study, the image stacks were rotated, cropped to remove unnecessary image information, and contrast‐enhanced using the batch processing feature in Photoshop (Adobe, San Jose, CA; Adobe Photoshop RRID:SCR_014199). Three‐dimensional models were saved as tiff files and measured using ImageJ (National Institute of Mental Health, Bethesda, MD; ImageJ RRID:SCR_003070).

Several previous studies have measured the trajectory and length of single‐NM‐neuron collaterals (Harris et al. 2008; Young and Rubel 1986) (Figure 3). These data helped guide our estimate of the fiber tract trajectory. Fiber track lengths were measured from sequential digital images using ImageJ (Figure 4, right panel). The XDCT path length was measured from the approximate location of the first bifurcation ventral to the NM (BP‐1) to the medial edge of the contralateral NL. These measurements were straightforward and reliable. Also measured was the length of the “ipsi‐loop.” The ipsilateral branch of a single NM cell is usually not a smooth loop but may have several bends and sharp angles. Path length was estimated with an average trajectory drawn through the dorsal fiber tract from BP‐1 to the medial edge of the ipsilateral NL. An example is shown in Figure 4 for a P15 chick.

Statistical Analysis

Branch length versus caudal–rostral distance functions were fit with linear regressions, and the slopes were compared using t‐tests. A second analysis used analysis of covariance, and the slopes were compared by assessing the caudal–rostral distance by group interaction.

Results

NL is a single layer of bipolar cells aligned in a saddle‐shaped plane. NM is positioned in the concave curvature of NL, with the ventral aspect of NM separated from dorsal NL by about 300 µm of neuropil (see Supporting Information S3). Primary afferents from the AN innervate NM. The axons of NM cells exit the ventral aspect of the nucleus, bifurcate, and send one branch to the dorsal ipsilateral NL via the ipsilateral branch, the ipsi‐loop (Young and Rubel 1983), and the other branch crosses the midline via the XDCT to innervate the ventral dendrites of the contralateral NL.

The NM is shifted caudally relative to NL, the nuclei are slanted in the brainstem from ventral–caudal to dorsal–rostral, and the rostral end is angled medially. The 3D construction shown in Figure 1 is rotated in the videos posted in the Supporting Information. The XDCT is a band of fibers running in opposite directions that originate in bilateral NM, pass under the VIth ventricle, and terminate in the ventral aspect of the contralateral NL. The XDCT is about 300 µm thick at the midline and extends about 1 mm from ventral–caudal to dorsal–rostral (Figure 1). The distance between the bilateral rostral high‐characteristic frequency (CF) regions of NL is shorter than the distance between the caudal low‐CF regions. Caudal XCDT fiber path lengths are further lengthened to circumvent the ventral aspect of the IVth ventricle (Figure 4, left). The midline of the XDCT is approximately at a 45° angle relative to the coronal plane (see Supporting Information S2). The abscissa in Figures 4 and 5, caudal to rostral distance, is based on the spacing of the histological sections and is relative to the actual width of the XDCT fiber array by a factor of approximately 2 due to foreshortening. The amount of foreshortening of the ipsi‐loop was indeterminate. Comparison with single‐cell data (see Section 4) indicates that the ordinate is accurate.

Average path length of ipsilateral and contralateral branches of NM projections in two age groups in chickens. There is a difference in path lengths between ipsilateral and contralateral branches. There is also a linear change in path length from caudal to rostral in both branches. The path length gradient of the XDCT increases from 1.7 in P0–P1 hatchlings to 2.3 in older P14–P27 animals (p < 0.001).

The XDCT path length was measured from aligned serial sections from the approximate location of the first bifurcation (BP‐1) to the medial edge of the contralateral NL in six chicks aged P0–P27. Also measured was the length of the ipsi‐loop from BP‐1 to the medial edge of the ipsilateral NL. An example is shown in Figure 4 for a P15 chick, and summary data are shown in Figure 5. The range of average lengths for the contralateral branch was 2022–3328 µm in three P14–P27 chicks and 1846–2812 µm in three P0–P1 chicks. The ipsilateral branch range was 886–1764 µm in both age groups. At each location, the contralateral path length is significantly longer than the ipsilateral path length. This length disparity is partially compensated for by the ipsilateral branch following a dorsal loop. There is clearly a linear gradient (caudal to rostral) in length for both the contralateral and ipsilateral branches. Furthermore, ipsilateral and contralateral branches co‐vary in length along this gradient, maintaining an approximately constant 1500 µm difference at each location. The XDCT length gradient appears to flatten out at the rostral end. This coincides with a flat area that can be appreciated in Figure 1 below the word “rostral.” This region is where the IVth ventricle transitions into the cerebral aqueduct.

Length data are averaged across three chicks aged P0–P1 and across three chicks aged P14–P27 in Figure 5. There is about a 10% increase in the length of the XDCT across the array in the 2 weeks posthatching, and possibly an increase in the caudal–rostral dimension as well. However, this trend is not apparent in the ipsilateral branch. Branch length versus caudal–rostral distance data are well fit with linear regressions (R 2 = 0.95–0.98).

Left and right path lengths were measured over distance for three chickens each in two age groups for contralateral and ipsilateral branches. Gradients (slopes) of path length by caudal–rostral distance were evaluated by branch and age. Due to differences in distances measured for each chicken, the summary analysis used only those distances from 0 to 528 µm. The analysis included distances for three chickens per age group and branch. Left and right sides were compared over distance using analysis of covariance and showed no differences, so the average of the right and left values was used as the primary variable of analysis.

Slopes between age groups were compared by branch separately using the standard errors of the slopes. For both analyses, contralateral branch slopes were significantly greater for the older age group (p < 0.001), but for the ipsilateral branch, no differences between age groups were found (p = 0.701). The change in both slope and length indicates differential elongation posthatching of caudal versus rostral as well as ipsilateral versus contralateral projections.

Discussion

The Avian Sound Localization Network

The ipsilateral branch of an NM cell innervates the dorsal dendrites of a row of NL cells that is orthogonal to the tonotopic axis, an iso‐frequency contour (Overholt et al. 1992; Young and Rubel 1983; Köppl and Carr 2008). In the chicken, activation of dorsal dendrites is simultaneous along the row. The contralateral branch of the NM cell terminates in a medial‐to‐lateral “delay line” innervating the ventral dendrites of a row of NL cells along the same iso‐frequency contour on the opposite side. Activation of ventral dendrites is sequential along the row (Figure 6A). The barn owl (Tyto alba) has a different circuitry, with the ipsilateral projection establishing a lateral‐to‐medial delay line. In both cases, the bipolar NL cells act as a row of coincidence detectors that encode interaural timing differences (ITD, also referred to as interaural phase differences [Moiseff and Konishi 1981]). The rostral‐to‐caudal tonotopic organization and the medial‐to‐lateral ITD distribution of the postsynaptic NL cells establish a 2D map of ITD versus CF (Köppl and Carr 2008). This geometry spatially separates the neural representation of spectra specific to sound sources at different locations based on the difference in interaural timing delays.

Axons originating from the nucleus magnocellularis (NM) bifurcate and send collaterals to both the ipsilateral (IPSI) and contralateral (CONTRA) nucleus laminaris (NL). (B) Three‐dimensional diagram showing the gradients in path lengths. In order to maintain isochronicity, the differences in conduction distances require velocity gradients across both fiber arrays. (C) Estimates of collateral lengths (µm) and number of nodes of Ranvier derived from Seidl et al. (2010). The number of nodes in the conduction path determines conduction velocity and compensates for differences in conduction length. Path Lengths Versus Single‐Cell Data

All six animals showed similar results. There was a length gradient that increased linearly from rostral to caudal across the XDCT as well as the ipsilateral fiber array. There was also an increase in the XCDT length from hatching (P0–P1) to 3–4 weeks posthatch (P14–P27) that was not observed in the ipsilateral projection. We observed a linear gradient in track length and assume the same trend for the individual cells. The length of the contralateral fiber pathway ranged from 2022 to 3328 µm. This is very close to the measurements of individual NM cells. Figure 4B (axon lengths BP‐1 to terminal) in Seidl et al. (2010) shows that the contralateral branch length of NM cells ranged from 2300 to 3000 µm. The length gradient of the ipsilateral fiber array was 886–1764 µm compared to a range of 800–1500 µm for the ipsilateral branches of NM cells. Thus, a rostral NM cell would have an 800 µm ipsilateral collateral and a 2300 µm contralateral collateral, a difference of 1500 µm, and a caudal NM cell would have a 1500 µm and a 3000 µm branch, also a difference of 1500 µm (Figure 6C). This compares well with the Seidl et al. (2010) estimate of the average difference of 1600 µm.

C. E. Carr and Konishi (1990) measured in barn owls the same NM to NL conduction times for all CF locations. In order to preserve this signal coherence in the frequency domain, the time of arrival to different tonotopic locations must be synchronized to accommodate differences in path length. Seidl et al. (2010) reported a difference in IND and axon diameter to compensate for the difference in lengths of ipsilateral and contralateral projections. They present a mathematical model to demonstrate how these factors control CV to produce equal conduction delays. The current data indicate that this difference in path length actually exists along two length gradients, and an additional timing adjustment is required to maintain isochronicity.

The traveling wave of the inner ear introduces a progressive transduction delay for high‐ to low‐frequency components of a stimulus. This stimulus feature is preserved throughout the auditory pathway. Because the caudal low‐frequency end of the XDCT is longer than the high‐frequency end, equal conduction velocities would add additional delays and distort this stimulus feature. To preserve fidelity in transmission from NM to NL, it is necessary to predict a CV gradient to compensate for a gradient of length differences, and we would also expect to detect a gradient in INDs and axon diameters that is controlling CV.

Control of CV

Early studies have identified numerous physical characteristics that can influence the CV of myelinated axons. Hursh (1939) and Gasser and Grunfest (1939) showed that CV was linearly related to axon diameter, and in 1947, Hess and Young (Hess and Young 1949) established a linear relation between IND and axon diameter. C. E. Carr and Konishi (1990) hypothesized that axon diameter and INDs in the barn owl might provide a means to regulate conduction times in the sound localization network. Seidl et al. (2010) verified that hypothesis.

Does the Number of Nodes of Ranvier Establish the Velocity Gradient?

To examine the circuit at an overall network level, we estimate the number of nodes and their gradients in the transmission pathways from published single‐cell data. It is established that there is a gradient in length, and in the following, we infer a concomitant gradient in INDs. Figure 10C in Seidl et al. (2010) shows that XDCT INDs ranged from 100 to 225 µm, and the ipsi‐loop INDs ranged from 30 to 150 µm. A valid conclusion from their data is that longer collaterals are associated with larger INDs; therefore, it is assumed within the XDCT that the ipsi‐loop large INDs are paired with long collaterals and short INDs are paired with short lengths. Given the length of a conduction path and the IND for a myelinated axon, one can estimate the number of nodes in that path. The contralateral branch length range of NM cells was 2300–3000 µm, and the ipsilateral branch range was 800–1500 µm. We estimate the number of nodes (length/IND) in a contralateral collateral at the rostral end as 2300/100 or 23 nodes and at the caudal end as 3000/225 or 13 nodes. The estimated number of nodes in an ipsilateral collateral is 26 nodes rostrally (800/30) and 10 nodes caudally (1500/150) (Figure 6C). These rough estimates indicate that both ipsilateral and contralateral branches show a similar rostral‐to‐caudal decrease in the number of nodes to compensate for increased distance, as expected (Seidl 2014; Seidl and Rubel 2016). Unexpectedly, ipsilateral and contralateral branches of the same cell appear to have a similar number of nodes despite differences in length.

There are several different mechanisms regulating conduction delays. In this case, at the cellular level, conduction delays are equalized to the ipsilateral and contralateral terminals of the same cell (Grothe et al. 2010; Seidl 2014). The estimate above indicates that in chicken, both NM collaterals have similar, if not the same, number of nodes, while the IND and the axon diameter increase to maintain the same conduction delay. This suggests that a similar number of nodes, regardless of the length, results in synchronous time of arrival of a signal at both terminals. If the CF‐paired contralateral NM cell also synchronizes collaterals and the two cells innervate dorsal and ventral dendrites of the same NL neurons, then it provides the microcircuitry necessary for coincidence detection at that CF location (Figure 6A).

In addition to the current understanding of the sound source localization microcircuit at a cellular level, the 3D structure of the circuit has provided an additional perspective of circuit timing across neuronal ensembles. This leads to the prediction of two distinct mechanisms that control conduction delays at the cellular and population levels. A second timing adjustment equalizes conduction delays across populations of cells. Since the contralateral and ipsilateral path lengths both vary rostral to caudal, isochronicity needs to be maintained across both fiber pathways. It seems plausible that both arrays require a similar velocity gradient to maintain isochronicity in the entire network (Figure 6B). A gradient in the number of nodes will produce a gradient in conduction velocities across the fiber array. In the case of the XDCT and the ipsi‐loop, a rostral/caudal velocity gradient may be established by adding or removing nodes. The farther the distance between nodes, the faster the CV (Waxman and Melker 1971; Waxman 1980). Conversely, adding nodes slows down the signal like adding stops to the Metro line. The short high‐CF fibers at the rostral end apparently have significantly more nodes than the longer caudal, low‐CF fibers (Figure 6C).

This example may be one of many possibilities for timing adjustments (Kimura and Itami 2009). For example, there is variance in the spacing of INDs on any collateral, and the gradation of node and internode properties can also tune conduction speed (Arancibia‐Cárcamo et al. 2017; Ford et al. 2015). CV is related to axon diameter (Gasser and Grundfest 1939; Ford et al. 2015). Seidl et al. (2010) measured an average difference in fiber diameter between ipsilateral and contralateral projections. Contralateral axon diameters averaged about 1.9 µm and ipsilateral diameters about 1.1 µm. A closer look should reveal a rostral‐to‐caudal gradient in diameters.

Similar gradients have been measured. There is a well‐established gradient in cell morphology along the tonotopic axis of NL with a gradient in associated biochemical factors (Korn et al. 2011; Smith and Rubel 1979). NL neurons have long dendrites in the low‐CF region and short dendrites in the high‐CF region. This specialization is proposed to enhance coincidence detection for each frequency (Agmon‐Snir et al. 1998). Coincidentally, longer collaterals of presynaptic NM cells make contact with longer dendrites on postsynaptic NL cells at the caudal end of NL, and shorter collaterals pair with shorter dendrites at the rostral end.

Oligodendrocytes Control CV

There is a growing realization that glia are intimately involved in system‐wide network function (Munyeshyaka and Fields 2022). Kimura and Itami (2009) contend that this timing maintenance function for glia is ubiquitous with examples from thalamocortical connections (Salami et al. 2003), interhemispheric pathways in cerebral cortex (Innocenti 2009), amygdala projections to the perirhinal cortex (Pelletier and Paré 2002), and inferior olive projections to cerebellar cortex (Lang and Rosenbluth 2003; Sugihara et al. 1993). Myelination is regulated by activity‐dependent communication between oligodendrocytes and neurons (Barres and Raff 1993; Lin and Bergles 2004; Gibson et al. 2014; Fields 2015; Hines et al. 2015; Chorghay et al. 2018; Kato and Wake 2019; Dutta et al. 2018). This complex neural/glial network sustains an active balance among path lengths and INDs controlled by an association of contiguous oligodendrocytes that establish and maintain “fine tuning” (Cramer and Rubel 2016). In the avian embryo, myelination of NM axons takes place around E18, soon after the onset of neuronal activity, and continues for several weeks posthatch (Korn et al. 2011; Korn and Cramer 2008; Cheng and Carr 2007). It is during this period that the adjustment of conduction delays among fibers is somehow orchestrated by a colony of newly arrived glial cells (Cramer and Rubel 2016). Once established, this is a dynamic and continuous process (Xin and Chan 2020; Pajevic et al. 2023; C. Carr et al. 2023). The age‐related increase in XDCT dimensions indicates the need for continued regulation of CV as the animal matures and relative path lengths change (Chuang et al. 2023; Sugio et al. 2022).

The chicken XCDT provides a unique opportunity to study a continuous gradient in the concomitant cellular and biochemical processes that control CV and isochronicity in neural networks. A rostral‐to‐caudal CV gradient is proposed to compensate for a continuous change in fiber length imposed by circuit morphology and the surrounding anatomy. This predicts a gradient in IND, axon diameter, and associated biochemical markers across both ipsilateral and contralateral fiber arrays.
