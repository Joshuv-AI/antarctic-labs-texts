AI interactivity and human-technology engagement: psychological mechanisms underlying learners’ intention to use AI tools in language learning contexts
Authors: Chenming Lyu, Shi Tang, Shaopeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into education has transformed how learners interact with technology. Understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying this interaction is essential for optimizing human-technology engagement. Grounded in models of technology acceptance and human-AI interaction, this study examines how perceived AI interactivity influences learners’ behavioral intention to use AI tools, with perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) as mediators.MethodsA total of 740 Chinese university EFL learners completed a validated questionnaire measuring four dimensions of AI interactivity (responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement) along with PU, PEOU, and behavioral intention. Data were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM) with bootstrapping to test direct and indirect pathways.ResultsAll four dimensions of AI interactivity significantly predicted behavioral intention both directly and indirectly through PU and PEOU. Responsiveness exerted the strongest direct effect, whereas, learning engagement showed the highest indirect influence via PU and PEOU. The final model demonstrated good fit (χ2/df = 2.216, RMSEA = 0.041, CFI = 0.964). The results reveal key psychological pathways through which human-AI interactivity shapes motivation and technology acceptance.ConclusionsAI interactivity plays a central role in shaping human-technology engagement. Immediate feedback and interactive design enhance motivation and perceived competence, thus offering practical implications for psychologically informed AI-based learning systems.

Introduction

The rapid development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has profoundly reshaped education and transformed how learners interact with their knowledge and technology. AI has evolved beyond instructional automation to enable personalized learning, adaptive feedback, and learner-centered engagement [30, 42]. In the field of English as a Foreign Language (EFL) education, AI tools, ranging from conversational chatbots to generative language models, offer learners interactive opportunities to practice communication, receive immediate feedback, and co-construct meaning through real-time dialogue [54]. These affordances underscore AI’s unique potential to enrich both the cognitive and affective dimensions of language learning by fostering motivation, engagement, and self-regulated learning.

A central pedagogical and psychological affordance of AI lies in its interactivity, which encompasses responsiveness, personalization, and the degree of control learners experience when engaging in AI systems. Interactivity is not merely a technological feature; it is a psychological condition that shapes learners’ engagement, motivation, and sustained behavioral intention to use technology [49, 50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that higher levels of interactivity are associated with improved learning performance, stronger motivation, and enhanced satisfaction [12, 46]. In the context of EFL education, this interactive experience is particularly valuable in promoting continuous practice and meaningful feedback. However, despite growing enthusiasm for AI-assisted language learning, empirical research remains limited on how AI interactivity influences students’ behavioral intention to use AI, especially within Chinese universities, where large class sizes and restricted instructional hours constrain opportunities for individualized instruction [9, 36].

To address this research gap, the present study employs the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [15] as a theoretical foundation. TAM provides a robust framework for examining users’ perceptions of new technologies, focusing on Perceived Usefulness (PU), Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU), and Behavioral Intention (BI) as key determinants of acceptance and adoption. While TAM effectively explains the cognitive determinants of technology acceptance [6], it does not fully capture the experiential and pedagogical dimensions, such as interactivity and engagement, that drive learners’ motivation in educational contexts. Therefore, the present study extends TAM by introducing AI interactivity as an antecedent variable, exploring how responsive, personalized, and engaging AI experiences shape learners’ perceptions of usefulness and ease of use, and consequently, their intention to adopt AI in EFL learning.

Literature review

AI in EFL contexts

AI has emerged as a transformative force in education, redefining how learners interact cognitively, socially, and emotionally with their learning environments. Its rapid integration into instructional practice has created new opportunities for personalized learning, adaptive assessment, and interactive engagement, fostering not only improved academic outcomes but also enhanced learner motivation and agency [7, 42]. These developments are particularly significant in the field of EFL education, where AI technologies align closely with the communicative and cognitive demands of language learning [26, 30, 32]. Through natural language processing and generative algorithms, AI can analyze linguistic input, provide individualized feedback, and generate contextually appropriate responses: creating dynamic, psychologically engaging environments that simulate authentic communication and social interaction [37, 54, 55].

Empirical studies have consistently demonstrated that AI integration in language learning promotes both linguistic performance and non-linguistic psychological outcomes. Linguistically, AI-based systems enhance writing, speaking, and listening proficiency through immediate data-driven adaptive feedback [4, 53]. For example, AI chatbots have been shown to outperform traditional oral practice activities in improving pronunciation accuracy, fluency, and overall communicative competence [2, 16]. Beyond language proficiency, AI-assisted learning contributes to learners’ self-regulated learning, intrinsic motivation, and reduction of communication anxiety [17, 56]. These findings suggest that AI tools not only enhance academic performance but also engage key affective and motivational mechanisms essential for sustained learning engagement.

The psychological and pedagogical affordances of AI are particularly valuable in Chinese higher education. Large class sizes and limited teacher-student interactions often restrict opportunities for individualized guidance [9, 36]. Simultaneously, university students’ relatively higher autonomy [28] and technological readiness [20] provide a favorable foundation for self-directed, technology-mediated learning. These contextual characteristics make Chinese EFL learners a critical population for examining how AI interactivity influences their perceptions, motivation, and behavioral intention to engage with AI tools. Understanding these mechanisms is not only pedagogically meaningful but also essential for clarifying the psychological processes underlying human-AI interaction in language learning.

The technology acceptance model in EFL contexts

The TAM, developed by Davis [15] and based on the Theory of Reasoned Action [19], remains one of the most influential frameworks for explaining user behavior toward emerging technologies. The model posits that two key cognitive appraisals, PU and PEOU, jointly determine users’ BI to adopt a system. PU refers to the degree to which an individual believes that using a specific technology enhances task performance, whereas PEOU reflects the perceived effort required to operate it. These perceptions influence users’ attitudes and, in turn, predict behavioral intention, which is considered the strongest psychological precursor to actual technology use [1, 33, 57].

TAM has been widely employed in educational technology research, including studies on EFL learning and AI-assisted instruction [24, 34, 48]. For instance, Liu and Ma [34] demonstrated that PU and PEOU significantly influenced students’ intentions to adopt ChatGPT for informal digital learning, whereas Hu and Gong [24] found similar relationships in L2 writing tasks supported by generative AI tools. These findings highlight TAM’s enduring relevance in examining learners’ psychological acceptance of AI technologies in language learning contexts.

However, although TAM offers a robust cognitive framework for understanding how users evaluate technologies, it does not fully account for the pedagogical, experiential, or affective factors that often shape technology adoption in educational settings. Recent extensions of TAM have introduced additional variables, such as technology anxiety [58], curiosity [14], and learning attitude [47], to capture domain-specific motivational and emotional determinants. Despite these efforts, within AI-enhanced EFL education, the role of AI interactivity as an antecedent variable influencing PU, PEOU, and BI remains underexplored. Addressing this theoretical gap is essential for linking technology acceptance to learners’ psychological experiences of interaction, thereby enriching the model’s explanatory power in contexts where cognitive, motivational, and pedagogical processes converge. The present study introduces AI interactivity as a pedagogically grounded and system-oriented construct that captures the experiential quality of learner-AI engagement. This approach shifts the analytical focus from static user traits to the dynamic process of interaction, thereby aligning TAM more closely with the principles of human–computer interaction and learner-centered pedagogy.

AI interactivity in EFL learning

The concept of interactivity originates in communication theory, which describes reciprocal and responsive exchanges between communicators [39]. With the advent of intelligent learning systems and conversational AI, interactivity has evolved into a defining characteristic of contemporary educational environments [10, 31]. In AI-mediated learning, perceived interactivity, the learner’s subjective sense of meaningful, responsive, and adaptive exchange, has been shown to enhance engagement, motivation, and learning satisfaction [8, 11, 40]. In this sense, interactivity represents not merely a technological feature but a psychological and pedagogical catalyst, promoting sustained learner attention, emotional involvement, and personalized learning experiences that strengthen motivation and persistence.

From the perspective of Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [41], interactivity satisfies the key psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Responsive AI feedback enhances learners’ perceived competence by confirming progress and clarifying errors, personalization fosters relatedness by making learners feel “understood” by the system; and learner control promotes autonomy by enabling self-directed pacing and topic selection. Together, these elements foster intrinsic motivation, which has been shown to drive continued technology engagement. [43, 45]. Drawing on Wang et al. [50], AI interactivity can be conceptualized through four interrelated dimensions: responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement [52],personalization, the extent to which AI-generated responses are tailored to learners’ specific needs and contexts [25],learner control, representing perceived autonomy over pacing, sequencing, and content navigation [38, 44],and learning engagement, reflecting the learner’s emotional, behavioral, and cognitive investment in the interaction [46, 51]. This multidimensional conceptualization offers a comprehensive framework for evaluating learners’ interactive experiences with AI tools. However, it also has certain contextual limitations. The scale primarily measures perceived rather than actual interactivity, which may be influenced by users’ digital literacy or cultural learning norms. Moreover, its dimensions were developed within general online learning environments and thus require validation in AI-assisted EFL contexts where language interaction and feedback are central. Recognizing these boundaries, this study adapts the framework to examine how the four interactivity dimensions function as psychological antecedents influencing PU, PEOU, and BI within the extended TAM.

A growing body of research in online and AI-enhanced learning contexts demonstrates that interactivity fosters behavioral intention by strengthening intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and engagement [11, 40]. These dimensions are particularly critical in the domain of EFL learning, where feedback, interaction, and communication are fundamental to language acquisition. However, few empirical studies have systematically examined how AI interactivity operates within the TAM framework to shape technology adoption in pedagogical context.

To address this gap, the present study integrates AI interactivity into TAM as a key antecedent of PU, PEOU, and BI. The conceptual model (Fig. 1) posits that the four dimensions of AI interactivity, responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and engagement, influence BI both directly and indirectly through PU and PEOU. This integration enables a more comprehensive understanding of learners’ psychological acceptance processes by connecting cognitive appraisals (usefulness and ease of use) to experiential engagement (interactivity and responsiveness).Fig. 1Conceptual model of AI interactivity and technology acceptance

Methodology

Participants

Using convenience sampling method [18], this study recruited 740 Chinese EFL learners from a comprehensive university in eastern China. As shown in Table 1, the sample represents a demographically diverse cohort. Among the participants, 211 were male (28.5%) and 529 were female (71.5%). Regarding academic level, 379 were freshmen (51.2%) and 361 were sophomores (48.8%). In terms of disciplinary background, 282 participants (38.1%) majored in science and 458 (61.9%) in liberal arts. The relatively high proportion of female and liberal arts students reflects the institutional orientation of the participating university, which is traditionally strong in the humanities and social sciences, and aligns with gender patterns typically observed in similar institutions. Data collection

The questionnaire comprised five sections: (1) demographic information, (2) perceived AI interactivity, (3) PU, (4) PEOU, and (5) BI. All scales were adapted from validated instruments and measured using a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). A 6-point scale was chosen to reduce central tendency bias and encourage more decisive responses.

AI interactivity was measured using Wang et al.’s [50] four-dimensional scale, encompassing responsiveness (5 items, e.g., “AI processes my responses quickly”), personalization (3 items, e.g., “AI satisfies my specific learning needs”), learner control (6 items, e.g., “I can freely choose what I want to learn using AI”), and learning engagement (3 items, e.g., “AI keeps my attention during learning”). PU and PEOU were adapted from Davis [15] and operationalized with 5 items each (e.g., “Using AI enhances my learning performance” [PU]; “Learning to use AI is easy for me” [PEOU]). BI was measured using 6 items adapted from Liu and Ma [34] and Sun and Mei [47], such as “I intend to use AI tools in my future learning.”

The final questionnaire (Appendix A) contained 33 items. The survey was administered online via Wenjuanxing (www.wjx.cn), a secure and commonly used Chinese academic survey platform. The participants were informed of the study’s purpose, assured of anonymity, and voluntarily consented to participate. To ensure linguistic and cultural accessibility, the instrument was provided in Mandarin Chinese following a rigorous forward–backward translation process. This procedure aimed to achieve cross-cultural semantic equivalence and was facilitated by a panel of three bilingual experts specializing in applied linguistics, translation studies, and educational technology. Their collective review ensured that the scale items were not only linguistically precise but also contextually attuned to the Chinese EFL learning environment. All the responses were complete and valid, yielding a 100% usable dataset.

Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was translated and contextually adapted for Chinese EFL learners using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure semantic and cultural equivalence. The preliminary version was reviewed by three experts in applied linguistics and educational psychology, and subsequently pilot-tested with 35 students to assess clarity, readability, and item relevance. Based on their feedback, minor linguistic refinements were made to improve item clarity. Results of the confirmatory factor analysis confirmed that all items demonstrated strong loadings (> 0.70), satisfactory Composite Reliability (CR > 0.80), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE > 0.50), indicating good convergent and discriminant validity within the Chinese EFL context.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 27 and AMOS 24, following the two-step approach recommended by Anderson and Gerbing [5].

First, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and CR, whereas convergent validity was assessed using AVE. Discriminant validity was verified by comparing the square roots of AVE values with inter-construct correlations [29]. Model fit was assessed using multiple indices, including χ2/df, RMSEA, NFI, RFI, IFI, TLI, and CFI.

In the second step, structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to test the hypothesized relationships among constructs, specifically, the direct and indirect effects of AI interactivity (responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement) on behavioral intention via PU and PEOU.

Bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) with bias-corrected confidence intervals was used to test the mediating effects of PU and PEOU, as recommended by Hayes [22] and MacKinnon et al. [35]. The explanatory power of the model was further evaluated using the R2 values for the endogenous constructs (PU, PEOU, BI).

Given that all data were collected through self-report measures, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess the potential common method bias. The unrotated exploratory factor analysis revealed that the first factor accounted for 27.6% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. This result suggests that the common method variance is unlikely to pose a serious threat to the validity of the findings. To further verify this, a marker variable test was also performed, which yielded nonsignificant correlations with the main constructs, confirming that common method bias was minimal.

Moderation analysis of grade level

To further explore the potential moderating role of grade level (freshman vs. sophomore), moderation analyses were conducted. The results revealed no statistically significant interaction effects between grade level and any of the four AI interactivity dimensions on behavioral intention (all p > 0.05). This indicates that the influence of AI interactivity on behavioral intention remains consistent across both cohorts, underscoring that the model’s structural pathways do not vary significantly by academic year.

Discussion

Direct effects of AI interactivity on BI

The SEM results revealed that AI interactivity exerted a strong direct influence on students’ behavioral intention to use AI in EFL learning. This finding is consistent with Rahim et al. [40], who argued that interactivity strengthens behavioral intention by enhancing users’ trust and emotional engagement with AI tools. Similarly, Kim and Kwon [27] demonstrated that a higher perceived interactivity increases learners’ willingness to adopt AI applications, lending further support to the present findings.

Among the four interactivity dimensions, responsiveness exhibited the strongest direct effect on behavioral intention (β = 0.252, p < 0.01). This highlights the psychological salience of instant, adaptive feedback, particularly in Chinese EFL classrooms where delayed teacher feedback is common due to large class sizes [9]. The dominance of responsiveness in predicting behavioral intention suggests that learners’ motivation to adopt AI is highly sensitive to the immediacy and accuracy of feedback. In the context of EFL learning, where timely correction and clarification are pedagogically crucial, fast and contextually appropriate responses satisfy learners’ immediate needs for competence and reduce cognitive uncertainty. This aligns with SDT [41], which emphasizes that responsive feedback enhances perceived competence and intrinsic motivation, directly fostering technology acceptance. This finding echoes Ali and Sheikh [3], who found that rapid and contextually relevant AI responses foster sustained usage intention. In other words, the immediacy of feedback fulfills learners’ desire for competence and control, reinforcing motivation through real-time cognitive validation. However, the current result differs from that of Wang et al. [50], who identified learning engagement as the most significant predictor of behavioral intention. This discrepancy may stem from contextual variation: in the EFL domain, where communication is feedback-driven, responsiveness becomes a primary motivator. Moreover, the prominence of generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) that emphasize responsiveness may further amplify this effect. Future studies could investigate whether these dynamics hold in other learning domains or across different AI platforms.

In contrast, learner control exhibited a weaker direct effect on behavioral intention (β = 0.131, p < 0.01). This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that autonomy is culturally moderated [13]. In Chinese educational contexts, where learning tends to be teacher-centered and structured, excessive autonomy may induce uncertainty rather than empowerment. Thus, greater learner control may not necessarily enhance intention unless it is supported by clear guidance. This underscores the importance of cultural congruence in AI design. While Western learners might value flexibility, Chinese students may respond more positively to structured interactivity that combines control with scaffolding.

Bootstrap mediation analysis (Table 6) indicated that the direct effects of AI interactivity (73.8–86.1%) outweighed the indirect effects via PU and PEOU (13.9–26.2%). This suggests that for EFL learners, the interactional experience itself, rather than the cognitive appraisal of usefulness or ease, drives technology adoption. SDT [41] helps explain this pattern: interacting with AI satisfies the psychological needs for competence (through responsive feedback), autonomy (through selective control), and relatedness (through simulated dialogue). When these needs are met, intrinsic motivation emerges, leading to sustained behavioral intention independent of the cognitive evaluations of utility.

Furthermore, as AI interfaces become increasingly intuitive, ease of use may function as a “hygiene factor” [23], which is a basic expectation rather than a motivator. Learners may no longer perceive ease of use as a distinct benefit, but its absence may hinder adoption. Thus, emotional and motivational satisfaction derived from interactivity, particularly responsiveness and engagement, now appears to be a more powerful predictor of behavioral intention than cognitive judgments alone.

The strong direct effects of AI interactivity on behavioral intention may be partly attributed to contextual and motivational factors not explicitly modeled in this study. In the Chinese EFL context, learners operate within a culturally structured, teacher-centered system that often limits opportunities for spontaneous feedback and self-directed learning [13]. As such, interactive AI experiences, characterized by immediacy, responsiveness, and perceived autonomy, may trigger a stronger motivational response, directly enhancing learners’ willingness to adopt AI. This interpretation aligns with SDT [41], which posits that satisfaction of competence and autonomy needs promotes intrinsic motivation, and with cross-cultural research suggesting that collectivist learning orientations may amplify the motivational salience of interactive feedback.

Regarding the role of grade level, our analysis suggests that the impact of AI interactivity on behavioral intention is relatively consistent between first-year and second-year EFL learners. The absence of a significant moderating effect may stem from the students’ similar educational paths. Having transitioned through comparable K-12 school systems and now following a standardized university curriculum, learners in both cohorts likely share similar perceptions of AI interactivity and comparable patterns of AI usage. Additionally, it is possible that the single-institution nature of our sample and the specific sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect more nuanced inter-group variations. Future research could involve a broader range of institutions and larger cohorts to further validate whether grade-based differences emerge in diverse educational contexts.

Mediating roles of PU and PEOU

Despite the dominance of direct effects, the mediation analysis confirmed that PU and PEOU play significant but partial mediating roles, supporting TAM’s core assumptions [15]. Specifically, AI interactivity shapes behavioral intention both directly and indirectly through these two perceptual variables. This finding aligns with Hu and Gong [24], who demonstrated the pivotal role of perceived usefulness and ease of use in promoting AI adoption among Chinese EFL learners.

Interestingly, PEOU showed a slightly stronger mediating effect than PU, particularly in the pathways from responsiveness, personalization, and learning engagement to behavioral intention. This suggests that ease and comfort during interactions are psychologically more immediate determinants of adoption than long-term perceptions of usefulness. The process-oriented nature of language learning may explain this asymmetry: EFL learners experience motivation through moment-to-moment fluency and flow, rather than delayed perceptions of achievement. When AI systems provide seamless and adaptive exchanges, learners experience reduced cognitive load and increased satisfaction, which in turn enhances their willingness to continue using AI tools.

The finding that PEOU can outweigh PU also implies a temporal dimension of technology acceptance. Ease of use may initially encourage experimentation and engagement, whereas usefulness becomes more salient as learners accumulate successful experiences. Future longitudinal research could explore how these perceptions evolve over time, revealing how short-term usability translates into sustained adoption.

Furthermore, learning engagement emerged as the most influential determinant of PU (β = 0.341, p < 0.01) and PEOU (β = 0.350, p < 0.01). This underscores engagement as a psychological bridge connecting interactivity and technology acceptance. When learners are emotionally and cognitively absorbed, they perceive tasks to be more enjoyable (higher PEOU) and outcomes to be more valuable (higher PU). Engagement reflects sustained attention, emotional involvement, and cognitive investment, which shape how learners perceive the system’s long-term benefits rather than its immediate utility. Consequently, engagement primarily enhances behavioral intention indirectly, by elevating perceptions of usefulness and usability over time. Thus, designing AI systems that sustain engagement through adaptive feedback, gamified interaction, or collaborative dialogue can enhance both cognitive and affective dimensions of technology acceptance.

Overall, the results suggest that AI interactivity activates both experiential and cognitive pathways in shaping learners’ behavioral intentions. While interactivity directly fulfills motivational needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness), it simultaneously enhances cognitive evaluations (usefulness and ease), creating a dual-route mechanism that strengthens technology adoption. This integrative understanding enriches TAM’s explanatory scope and highlights the psychological depth of human-AI interaction in educational settings.

Implications

Theoretical implications

This study extends the TAM by introducing AI interactivity as a theoretically grounded antecedent that influences behavioral intention both directly and indirectly through PU and PEOU. The findings reveal that interaction quality, including responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement, functions as both an experiential and a cognitive determinant of technology adoption. This integration moves beyond the instrumental and utilitarian assumptions of the original TAM, highlighting that affective, motivational, and interactive experiences are integral to technology acceptance, particularly in AI-mediated educational contexts.

Moreover, situating this research within the Chinese EFL context contributes to the contextual diversification of TAM applications. The results suggest that cultural and pedagogical factors, such as structured learning traditions and teacher-centered instructional norms, shape learners’ perceptions of interactivity and moderate its influence on behavioral intention. This context-sensitive perspective demonstrates that technology acceptance is not purely cognitive but also socially and culturally situated, offering a more nuanced understanding of how learners from collectivist educational systems engage with AI-based learning tools.

Practical implications

The findings provide practical guidance for both AI developers and EFL educators. For AI developers, these findings underscore the importance of designing systems that actively sustain learners’ motivation and self-efficacy. Responsiveness should remain a key design priority, as immediate and contextually relevant feedback reinforces learners’ sense of competence and control, the core components of self-efficacy. Similarly, personalization and adaptive learning pathways can help maintain motivation by aligning content difficulty with learners’ evolving skill levels. AI systems that can track progress, visualize improvements, and provide positive reinforcement may further promote sustained engagement.

For EFL educators, the results highlight that pedagogical integration plays a decisive role in transforming short-term interactions into long-term motivational benefits. Teachers can scaffold AI-based learning by setting incremental goals, modeling effective AI use, and encouraging reflection on learning progress, thereby enhancing both perceived usefulness and self-efficacy. Designing tasks that promote collaboration between learners and AI, such as dialogic writing practice or real-time pronunciation feedback, can nurture autonomous engagement and sustained motivation over time.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged, which also suggest meaningful directions for future research. First, the data were collected from a single Chinese university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The sample also showed an overrepresentation of female and liberal arts students, potentially biasing the results toward certain learning styles and technology perceptions. These demographic characteristics may influence how students interpret and respond to AI interactivity, as gender and disciplinary background are known to shape digital literacy, communication preferences, and attitudes toward technology use. Future studies should include cross-regional, cross-disciplinary, and more gender-balanced samples to verify the robustness of the proposed model across diverse contexts. Comparative analyses across STEM and non-STEM disciplines, as well as between different types of higher education institutions, would provide deeper insight into how contextual and demographic variables moderate the relationships among interactivity, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and behavioral intention. Second, the cross-sectional design only captures learners’ perceptions at a single point in time. Longitudinal or experimental designs could more effectively reveal how AI interactivity influences behavioral intention and sustained use over time. Third, all variables were measured using self-reported data, the possibility of common method bias cannot be entirely excluded, despite the results of Harman’s single-factor and marker variable tests suggesting minimal influence. Future research could reduce this risk by employing multi-source data, longitudinal tracking, or experimental designs that combine behavioral and perceptual measures. Finally, future studies could expand the model by incorporating additional psychological factors, such as trust, emotional engagement, or ethical perceptions, to capture the multidimensional nature of human–AI interaction in education. These factors could deepen our understanding of how learners adopt, sustain and regulate their engagement with AI-based learning environments.

Conclusion

This study investigated how AI interactivity shapes Chinese university EFL learners’ behavioral intention to use AI, drawing on the TAM. The results revealed that all four dimensions of AI interactivity, responsiveness, personalization, learner control, and learning engagement, significantly and positively predicted behavioral intention, with responsiveness exerting the strongest direct influence. Both PU and PEOU served as partial mediators, confirming their continuing relevance in AI-supported educational environments.

By integrating AI interactivity into the TAM, this study extends the model beyond its traditional cognitive scope, demonstrating that experiential and motivational factors play a vital role in shaping technology acceptance. The findings emphasize that the quality of human, AI interaction, particularly the immediacy, adaptability, and emotional resonance of feedback, is central to sustaining learners’ motivation and engagement. Overall, this study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of human-technology interaction in language learning, offering both theoretical enrichment and practical guidance for designing and implementing AI tools that foster responsiveness, engagement, and long-term adoption in higher education contexts.
