Reliable Change of Blood-Based Biomarkers Following Acute Sport-Related Concussion: A CARE Consortium Study
Authors: Anna E. Croghan, Rachel Churchill, Benjamin L. Brett, Daniel L. Huber, Jaroslaw Harezlak, Jessica M. Gill, Paul Pasquina, Steven P. Broglio, Thomas W. McAllister, Michael A. McCrea, Timothy B. Meier, Joshua T. Goldman, Joshua T. Goldman, Jason P. Mihalik, M. Alison Brooks, Steve Rowson, Kenneth L. Cameron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence supports the potential of blood-based biomarkers for aiding in concussion diagnosis, though most work has focused on biomarker changes at the group level. Here, reliable change indices (RCI) identify individual athletes exhibiting meaningful elevations or decreases in biomarkers from baseline to multiple acute post-concussion timepoints.MethodsBaseline and post-injury blood (0–12, 12–36, and 36–60 h) was prospectively collected from collegiate athletes in the Concussion Assessment, Research and Education Consortium. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1), tau, neurofilament light (NfL), and phosphorylated-tau181 (p-tau181) were measured in plasma; c-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-receptor antagonist (RA), tumor necrosis factor, and vascular endothelial growth factor were measured in serum. Reliable change was calculated by dividing the difference between baseline and post-injury visits by the standard error of the difference from uninjured controls. Athletes with concussion with RCI > 1.65 (upper 5%) were classified as having ‘elevated’ biomarkers; those RCI < − 1.65 (lower 5%) were classified as ‘decreased’. Binomial tests determined if the proportion of athletes with elevated/decreased biomarkers was different than 5%. Fisher’s exact tests determined if presence of either loss of consciousness (LOC) and/or post-traumatic amnesia (PTA) impacted proportions of elevated or decreased biomarkers.ResultsA total of 521 participants (198 controls, 323 athletes with concussion) were included in this study. A significant proportion of athletes with concussion had elevated GFAP (16.0%), IL-1RA (16.4%), and IL-6 (15.9%) at 0–12 h; a significant proportion had elevated GFAP at 12–36 (19.0%) and 36–60 h (14.6%). Subsets of athletes with concussion had no elevations in any marker at 0–12 (37%), 12–36 (45%), and 36–60-h visits (63%). For decreased biomarkers (RCI < − 1.65), fewer participants with concussion had decreased NfL (0%) and GFAP (0%) at 12–36 h, while a greater proportion had decreased p-tau181, NfL, UCH-L1, tau, IL-1RA, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and IL-10 at 36–60 h (10.5–21.2%). Presence of LOC and/or PTA was associated with elevated GFAP at 12–36 h (45% versus 15%; p = 0.004).DiscussionElevations in biomarkers were primarily observed in the early-acute window (0–12 h); with only GFAP showing elevations at later visits and sensitivity to altered consciousness. Only a subset of athletes with concussion had meaningful elevations of any single blood-based biomarker, with elevations seen in single markers rather than a global increase across multiple markers. Any single marker has limited ability to capture the heterogenous physiological response to concussion and ultimately aid in concussion diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02479-4.

Introduction

Over 500,000 collegiate athletes participated in the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) in 2021–2022 [1]. While there are many benefits to participation in sports, athletes are vulnerable to sports-related injuries, including mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) or concussion. Notably, research has found that the incidence of sport-related concussion (SRC) has increased in recent decades [2], which may in part be due to increased awareness and improved monitoring/detection. Presently, the diagnosis of concussion is based on clinical signs and features (e.g., the presence of altered consciousness) and subjective symptoms. Prior studies have shown that it is common for SRC to go undetected/undiagnosed [3] and that playing through an undiagnosed injury can result in longer recovery from injury [4, 5]. As a result, there is great interest in identifying methods to aid in the characterization and diagnosis of concussion.

Blood-based biomarkers are one such tool that has shown promise in the detection of concussion. For example, blood-based biomarkers that are thought to correspond with the neurophysiological effects of concussion, such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin c-terminal hydrolase-L1 (UCHL-1), tau, neurofilament light (NF-L), and phosphorylated tau 181 (p-tau181) have been found to be elevated after SRC [6–13]. Recent research has additionally supported the potential utility of peripheral inflammatory blood-based biomarkers for the assessment of concussion/mTBI. Elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) were associated with poorer outcomes in hospital patients [14]. Further, Edwards et al. (2020) found that interleukin (IL)-6 was acutely elevated following concussion in military personnel [15]. In recent studies conducted by our group, IL-6 and IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) were shown to be elevated at early-acute time points in participants with concussion relative to baseline levels and compared with non-injured controls [16–18]. There is also evidence for sensitivity of additional markers to concussion, including IL-8, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [19–21].

With growing interest in the utility of biomarkers for informing the physiological effects and clinical management of concussion, it is important to establish meaningful thresholds of biomarker elevations following injury. Currently, most existing research has focused on biomarker changes at the group-level; creating a pressing need to examine what biomarker changes are meaningful at the individual level, particularly at lower levels of brain injury severity (i.e., SRC). One method to explore meaningful changes in measures over time at the individual level is reliable change indices (RCI) [22]. One widely accepted approach is the equation for RCI developed by Jacobson and Truax which divides the difference of values between two timepoints by the standard error of the difference (SED) [23]. This statistical approach allows for determination of whether an individual’s change score is greater than would be expected to occur due to normal variability driven by measurement error alone. In the context of blood biomarkers for concussion, RCI can thus be used to identify thresholds of elevations (or decreases) in biomarker changes from a pre-injury timepoint to various post-injury timepoints at an individual level, extending interpretation to reliable within-person change, which is arguably more aligned with clinical decision making post-concussion than group differences. This is important since traditional group-level analyses can show significant effects across the sample even if this is not reflected for all individuals within the sample [24].

The primary aims of the current study were to develop and use RCI thresholds to identify individual athletes exhibiting elevations or decreases in brain injury and inflammatory biomarkers at multiple acute timepoints following concussion, and to determine if results using this subject-level approach mirror findings observed in previous group-level analyses. Biomarkers were selected based on prior work demonstrating elevations in these markers following concussion or mTBI [6–21]. Because these biomarkers have been shown to have different temporal profiles following concussion, particularly within the acute timeframe, analyses focused on multiple acute timepoints within 0–60 h post-injury. A secondary aim was to qualitatively describe patterns of elevations/decreases across participants to determine if concussion is associated with global changes of relevant biomarkers at the individual level or if distinct groups of participants show changes in various specific biomarkers. Finally, additional analyses were conducted to determine the effects of the presence of loss of consciousness (LOC) and/or post-traumatic amnesia (PTA) on individual-level changes in blood biomarkers. Based on previous findings from our group [9, 12, 16, 18], it was hypothesized that a significant proportion of collegiate athletes with SRC would show elevations in blood-based biomarkers across multiple acute timepoints (ranging from 0 to 60 h) post-injury, with a larger proportion of elevations observed in athletes reporting LOC and/or PTA.

Methods

Participants

The current work focuses on athletes (aged 17–23 years) participating in the Advanced Research Core of the prospective Concussion Assessment, Research and Education (CARE) Consortium study occurring from January 2015 to March 2020 [18, 25]. Athletes from six NCAA sites were enrolled and completed a preseason baseline. Due to slight differences in procedures in military service academies (MSA; 2 of 6 sites), MSA and non-MSA sites are described separately; however, analyses of the current study focused on athletes combined from across all sites. Enrollment—MSA Athletes: Baseline enrollment criteria included participation in an NCAA-recognized contact or collision sport; a club sport; or rugby, wrestling, boxing and combat training. Cadets that sustained a concussion consistent with the study definition of concussion completed multiple follow-up visits, described below. Uninjured military service academy cadets, selected from cadet-athletes enrolled at baseline, were matched to injured cadets by institution, sport, varsity status, and sex at a 1 control to 4 injuries ratio. The exclusion criteria for follow-up visits in both controls and participants with concussion was any contraindication to study procedures. Enrollment—Non-MSA Athletes: Baseline enrollment criteria included participation in an NCAA-recognized contact or collision sport. Athletes that sustained concussion meeting the study definition completed follow-up visits. Contact sport controls, selected from baselined athletes, completed similar visits and were matched to participants with concussion based on institution, sport, and sex, and algorithmically matched on race/ethnicity, baseline scores on the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading, primary position, years of position in primary sport, concussion history, and starter/non-starter status. Exclusion criteria for follow-up visits included any contraindication to study procedures.

Concussions were diagnosed by local staff at all sites using a common definition and criteria for concussion based on Department of Defense (DOD) evidence-based guidelines, which define concussion as “a change in brain function following a force to the head, which may be accompanied by temporary loss of consciousness, but is identified in awake individuals with measures of neurologic and cognitive dysfunction” [26]. Individuals with multiple concussions over the study period were considered as independent cases. All aspects of this study were approved by the institutional review board at the Medical College of Wisconsin and the US Army Medical Research and Development Command Human Research Protection office; all participants provided written informed consent. Study Timepoints

Participants in the CARE Consortium completed baseline visits with blood collection and follow-up visits occurring acutely post-injury, 24–48 h post-injury, at the initiation of their graduated return-to-play protocol (i.e., asymptomatic), and 7 days following their unrestricted return to play. As in our prior work, timepoints were reclassified for current analyses based on the time from injury for each blood draw to allow more granular consideration of the temporal dynamics of the effects of concussion on biomarkers [18, 27]. The time of the first non-baseline blood collection was considered as 0 h post-injury for controls. Based on the resultant distribution (see Supplementary Fig. 1 in the electronic supplementary material [ESM]), datapoints were binned into three post-injury timepoints (in addition to baseline): 0–12 h, 12–36 h, and 36–60 h post-injury. Not all participants provided blood samples at every identified time point. Participants self-reported “current biological sex” (“male” or “female”) and the presence of injury characteristics including LOC and PTA.

Biomarker Collection and Analysis

Non-fasting venous blood was collected via 10-mL red-top serum and 10-mL EDTA plasma tubes and processed following recommended guidelines based on TBI common data elements [28]. GFAP, UCH-L1, tau, and NfL were measured in plasma using multiplexed single protein array technology and p-tau181 was measured in plasma using the single molecule array p-tau181 V2 Advantage kit (Simoa; Quanterix Corp., Billerica, MA, USA). CRP, IL-1RA, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF, and VEGF were measured in serum on the Meso Scale Discovery platform using V-PLEX assays. All biomarkers were processed in duplicate, blinded to diagnosis, using research platforms. Biomarkers below the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced by the lower LOD divided by the square root of 2. Batch effects were observed in tau; therefore, tau values across the two batches were harmonized using quantile normalization. Specifically, a quantile normalization equation was derived from data restricted to control participants in the parent study (contact and non-contact sport controls). The estimated slope of the fitted line and intercept were determined based on the Q-Q plot of the data from the two batches using 100 evenly spaced quantiles and subsequently used to harmonize the tau data across the two batches. Finally, for all markers, duplicates with coefficient of variation > 25% were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

All biomarkers were natural log transformed to decrease skewness of their distributions prior to analysis. The equation outlined by Jacobson and Truax was used to compute RCI values [23]. The baseline biomarker value was subtracted from the post-injury biomarker value for every participant and subsequently divided by the standard error of the difference \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({\mathrm{SED}} = \sqrt {2S_{1}^{2} (1 - r_{12} )} )$$\end{document}(SED=2S12(1-r12)), which was computed using only data from the control group. This process was repeated for all biomarkers using baseline to post-injury timepoint differences for each of the three timepoints in the participants with concussion. Biomarkers exceeding the 95th percentile (RCI > 1.65) were classified as being ‘elevated’, while biomarkers below the 5th percentile (RCI < − 1.65) were classified as ‘decreased’. Binomial tests were used to determine if the distribution of participants with concussion with elevated (or decreased, run separately) biomarker levels was different from 5% (corresponding to either the upper 95% or lower 5% confidence interval [CI] for positive and negative RCI calculations, respectively) for each biomarker at each timepoint. The overlap of individuals with patterns of elevations (or decreases) of different biomarkers was qualitatively investigated using frequencies and UpSet plots. Finally, Fisher’s exact tests were used to determine if the proportion of athletes with concussion that had elevated (RCI > 1.65) or decreased (RCI < − 1.65) biomarkers differed based on the presence of LOC and/or PTA. To account for multiple comparisons across 12 biomarkers and reduce the risk of type I errors, a Bonferroni corrected significance level of p < 0.0042 (0.05/12) was considered for all analyses. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 29. Plots were created in R version 4.3.3.

Discussion

Prior studies have demonstrated changes in blood-based biomarkers in relation to concussion, generating interest in enhancing understanding and classification of concussion through use of biomarkers. Here, we extend upon prior work by utilizing RCI to identify longitudinal elevations in biomarkers at an individual subject level. We observed that the proportion of athletes with concussion with acute elevations in GFAP, IL-1RA, and IL-6 relative to baseline significantly exceeded the expected rate of 5% (i.e., RCI > 1.65). These elevations were mostly observed at the early acute stage (0–12 h) post-injury, except for GFAP which showed greater than expected rates of elevation that persisted into the later sub-acute post-injury timepoints (12–36 h and 36–60 h). Further, elevations in GFAP at the later acute windows were more common in athletes reporting PTA and/or LOC following injury. Conversely, greater than expected rates of reduced biomarker levels were observed at the 36–60-h timepoint (p-tau181, NfL, UCH-L1, tau, IL-1RA, IL-10, and VEGF), demonstrating the dynamic nature of biomarker changes following concussion. Finally, only a relatively small percentage of concussed athletes had elevations in any single marker at any single acute visit (i.e., < 20%), with different sets of athletes showing elevations in different markers and a large percentage of concussed athletes having no elevations in any biomarker.

Overall, current findings regarding biomarker elevations post-concussion are consistent with previous reports of group-level elevations. A significant proportion of athletes with concussion had elevated GFAP, IL-6, and IL-1RA at the 0–12-h timepoint, all of which have previously been shown to be elevated at the group level in CARE Consortium data and independent cohorts [7–9, 13, 16–18]. Interestingly, < 20% of athletes with concussion had an elevation in any single biomarker at any visit, suggesting that a single marker has limited sensitivity to identify the presence of diagnosed concussion in isolation. This also raises the possibility that the previously reported group differences in biomarkers following concussion may be driven by subsets of athletes rather than by consistent elevations across all individuals with concussion. For example, it is possible that not all concussions are associated with a physiological response robust enough to be measured by current methods for any single marker (see further discussion below).

In contrast, multiple biomarkers which have previously been reported as elevated following concussion did not show significant elevations at the individual level in the current work, such as UCH-L1, p-tau-181, tau, and NfL [6–12]. This could potentially reflect increased variability of these markers across visits in controls (e.g., the SED of UCH-L1 across visits was more than two times the SED of GFAP), which is considered in the calculation of RCI cut-offs. It is noteworthy, however, that several of these markers did show > 5% of injured athletes having elevations at the 0–12-h visit (e.g., 15% UCH-L1, 12% tau, 11% p-tau181), though the effect was not significant with our conservative multiple comparison correction.

GFAP was the only marker in the current study to show significant elevations at the individual level beyond the 0–12-h timepoint, which is also consistent with previous group-level findings [9, 13, 16]. The fact that GFAP is elevated beyond the early-acute window makes it a promising potential biomarker for aiding in the diagnosis of concussion, allowing eventual implementation in multiple clinical contexts and timeframes (e.g., sideline to later emergency department assessment). Moreover, it is noteworthy that in addition to the greater proportion of athletes with concussion that had elevated GFAP, no athletes with concussion had GFAP below the lower RCI threshold (versus the expected 5%) at the 0–12-h or 12–36-h visits. This raises the possibility that GFAP could also ultimately be used to rule-out concussion or potentially track neurological recovery for athletes with lower-than-expected levels following a suspected concussion event, analogous to the current clinical utility of GFAP for ruling out the need for head CT imaging for TBI in the emergency department setting.

GFAP also demonstrated a severity-related dose response, with a greater proportion of elevated levels observed in athletes with either loss of consciousness and/or post-traumatic amnesia following concussion relative to those without (45% vs 15% at the 12–36-h timepoint). This is consistent with prior studies that demonstrated the sensitivity of GFAP to acute injury characteristics [9, 13, 16]. Again, though they did not reach significance, nearly twice as many athletes with loss of consciousness and/or post-traumatic amnesia had elevations at the 0–12-h timepoint in all biomarkers except IL-1RA and TNF. Overall, these data make an argument for the additive utility of GFAP as a marker of injury-severity post-concussion, particularly as report of LOC and PTA is often reliant on participant report.

Interestingly, there were multiple biomarkers (p-tau181, NfL, UCH-L1, tau, IL-1RA, IL-10, and VEGF) for which a greater proportion of athletes with concussion had lower levels compared with baseline than expected (i.e., < 5%) at the 36–60-h timepoint. Similar post-injury undershoots have been previously observed [7, 9, 29, 30]. The physiological mechanism and clinical significance of this phenomenon remain unclear, and additional work is needed. Nevertheless, these results highlight the importance of timing in the assessment of blood-based biomarkers following concussion and illustrate the dynamic nature of these biomarkers.

Finally, qualitative assessment of biomarker changes following concussion on an individual basis illustrated two main findings. First, a relatively large percentage of athletes with a diagnosed concussion had no elevations of any biomarkers at the 0–12-h timepoint (37%), the 12–36-h timepoint (45%), or the 36–60-h timepoint (63%). Thus, while biomarkers have great promise as diagnostic aids to detect (or rule out) a potential concussion, current results demonstrate that they lack the sensitivity to identify concussion in isolation. Second, for those individuals showing elevated biomarkers, elevations were most commonly observed in a single marker rather than a global increase across multiple markers. This likely reflects the complex, heterogenous physiological response to concussion, and suggests that a single marker may be insufficient to capture the physiological effects of concussion at a single-subject level. Additional studies are needed to determine what combination of biomarkers best captures the physiological consequences of concussion, what combination of biomarkers is most useful for improving the clinical management of concussion, and whether the absence of elevated biomarkers at the single-subject level reflects less severe injuries (e.g., injuries without measurable physiological injury) and/or non-concussive events.

Study Strengths and Weaknesses

This study expands upon the previous literature by applying RCI to measure meaningful change from baseline in a wide array of biomarkers at the individual level in a cohort of athletes at multiple sub-acute timepoints post-SRC using both qualitative and quantitative methods. Although a novel approach, the current analysis has no direct application to patient care at this time. It is conceivable that such an approach could be applied in future scenarios where the collection of blood during preseason baseline testing becomes more widely adopted; however, it was not the intent of this study to identify biomarker cut-offs for use across other studies and future work in the development of SRC-specific thresholds is needed. Moreover, this study focused on collegiate athletes, and it is unclear if findings generalize to other athlete populations. In addition, although both male and female athletes were included, female athletes were underrepresented and future studies with a greater proportion of female athletes are needed. Detailed information regarding number of games or other exposures experienced through sport or military training was not available. Although controls were matched to injured participants on multiple factors, including sport and position, head impact exposures were not directly measured, and it is possible that these exposures may impact blood-biomarker values. Post-injury visits were classified based on the number of hours post-injury to attempt to account for the dynamic temporal profile of investigated biomarkers. It is possible that the selected time bins may not capture the exact temporal profiles of all investigated biomarkers (e.g., they could possibly prevent the identification of relevant changes in biomarkers that are rapidly released and cleared following concussion) and thus results should be interpreted with caution. In addition, no athlete had baseline and all three post-injury visits and there was variability in the number and times of visits completed by participants; thus the calculation of RCI was conducted on different subsets of athletes at each visit. Finally, it is possible that additional biomarkers not measured in the current cohort are sensitive to concussion; additional biomarker discovery for the acute effects of concussion is merited.

Conclusion

Current analyses of biomarkers at the individual-subject level demonstrate that only a subset of athletes with concussion (< 20%) have a meaningful elevation of any single blood-based biomarker acutely following concussion. Elevation of GFAP emerged as a more robust and enduring potential indicator of injury severity than other markers. The most common pattern was an early-acute elevation of a single marker rather than multiple markers, while a subset of athletes with concussion had no elevations in any biomarker. Although current results need to be interpreted within the context of the study limitations, they suggest that the use of biomarker change in isolation using the RCI approach for the diagnosis of concussion is not currently supported.
