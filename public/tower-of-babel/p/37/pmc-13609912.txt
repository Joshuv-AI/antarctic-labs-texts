Comparison of the Clinical and Biochemical Parameters Between Cystic and Non-Cystic Pheochromocytoma
Authors: Somdatta Giri, Aravind Prasad, Bandna Kumari, Kodakkattil S. Sreerag, Sidhartha Kalra, G. Ramkumar, Nandini Pandit, Jayaprakash Sahoo, Sadish K. Kamalanathan, Dukhabandhu Naik, Samim A. Mondal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:There are limited literature studies and awareness regarding the clinical and biochemical characteristics specific to cystic pheochromocytomas. This study aims to comprehensively assess clinical and biochemical characteristics of cystic pheochromocytomas and compare them with non-cystic counterparts.Methods:Based on pre-operative computed tomography (CT) imaging, patients were divided into two groups. Group 1 comprised patients with predominantly cystic pheochromocytoma, defined by the presence of >50% cystic component within the tumour volume. Group 2 included patients with non-cystic pheochromocytoma.Results:A total of 46 patients with histopathologically confirmed pheochromocytoma were analysed, of which 8 (17.4%) were cystic and 38 (82.6%) were non-cystic. There were no differences in demographic and clinical parameters between the two groups. However, patients with cystic tumours had significantly lower prevalence of elevated plasma normetanephrine levels (37.5% vs. 85.7%, P = 0.005), suggesting a lower catecholamine secretory burden. Cystic pheochromocytoma more frequently occurred on the left side (85.7% vs. 29.4%, P = 0.03) and had lower baseline CT attenuation than non-cystic pheochromocytoma (31.3 ± 8.2 vs. 39.25 ± 7.6, P = 0.03).Conclusion:Cystic pheochromocytomas may represent a distinct subtype with lower biochemical activity. Their identification requires careful radiological assessment, especially in biochemically negative patients. Enhanced awareness of their characteristics may improve diagnosis and optimise surgical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Pheochromocytomas are rare neuroendocrine tumours that often present a wide range of clinical and radiological manifestations. Cystic pheochromocytomas are a rare presentation that can pose diagnostic and management challenges.[1] Some pheochromocytomas do not produce symptoms and are discovered incidentally. In this instance, the cystic appearance and occasional peripheral calcification may lead to the erroneous diagnosis of adrenal cysts and prompt a potentially dangerous cyst aspiration. Adrenal cysts are classified as pseudocysts, endothelial cysts, epithelial cysts, and parasitic cysts.[2] In a study by Erickson et al.,[1] 41 cases of adrenal cystic lesions were reported, of which three were associated with pheochromocytomas.

The pathogenesis of cystic pheochromocytoma has largely been speculated, and the inciting event could be the tumour outgrowing its vascular supply. Subsequent intralesional haemorrhage, necrosis, and liquefaction within the mass eventually give the tumour a cystic appearance. There are limited literature studies and awareness regarding the clinical, imaging, and biochemical characteristics specific to cystic pheochromocytomas. Although a few case reports from India exist, large series remain scarce.[34] Most reported cases are detected retrospectively following surgical intervention, resulting in a paucity of comprehensive data on tumour behaviour.[56] Cystic pheochromocytomas can often be overlooked due to misleading diagnostic findings. Recently, the Mayo Clinic compiled a series of 21 cases, providing valuable insights into the diagnosis and clinical presentation of cystic pheochromocytomas.[7] This retrospective study aims to comprehensively assess these characteristics, including intra-operative haemodynamic fluctuations, and compare them with non-cystic counterparts in a single-centre cohort from the Indian demographic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective observational analysis conducted in the Department of Endocrinology at an academic institute of India. Patients who were admitted to the Endocrinology ward and underwent surgical excision for pheochromocytoma between 2015 and 2025 were evaluated for inclusion.

Study population

The study included adult patients aged 18 years and above who were diagnosed with pheochromocytoma based on histopathological confirmation. Patients were eligible if they had complete clinical, biochemical, and imaging data available at presentation. Based on pre-operative computed tomography (CT) imaging, patients were subsequently divided into two groups. Group 1 comprised patients with ‘predominantly cystic’ pheochromocytoma, defined by the presence of > 50% cystic component within the tumour volume as assessed by CT scan.[8] Group 2 included remaining patients with pheochromocytomas (predominantly non-cystic). The objective of the study was to compare the clinical and biochemical parameters between the groups. Adrenergic tumours are those that secrete either epinephrine (or metanephrine) alone or both epinephrine and norepinephrine (or metanephrine and normetanephrine). Noradrenergic tumours are those that secrete only norepinephrine (or normetanephrine).

Data collection

Patient data were retrospectively retrieved from electronic medical records and the hospital’s imaging database. Demographic details such as age, gender, and anthropometric measurements were collected. Clinical parameters recorded included symptoms attributable to catecholamine excess and signs of mass effect. Biochemical data comprised pre-operative plasma fractionated-free metanephrine. For plasma-free metanephrine (metanephrine and normetanephrine), liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was used. The laboratory range for plasma-free metanephrine is 7.9–88.7 pg/ml; plasma-free normetanephrine is 20.1–135.4 pg/ml; the values >2 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) were considered biochemically positive. Cardiac data included electrocardiograms and two-dimensional echocardiograms. All radiological examinations were performed with a commercially available CT scanner (SOMATOM sensation 64, Siemens medical solution, Forchheim, Germany) with the following imaging parameters: FOV 360, slice thickness 1 mm, 1:1 table pitch, 120 kVp, variable tube current depending on patient size (150–250 mas). Radiological parameters included tumour location (right, left, or bilateral), size (largest diameter in cm), total volume (calculated from imaging), presence of calcification, and CT attenuation values in both unenhanced and contrast-enhanced phases [arterial phase (20 s), early venous phase (60 s), and delayed venous phase (15 min)]. The presence of the “ring sign” was assessed, and both the absolute and relative percentage washout values of the lesions were calculated. Special attention was paid to quantify the cystic and solid volumes. Volumetric analysis was performed using the Syngo. Via application integrated into the institute’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). The imaging data were reviewed by an experienced radiologist. Attenuation values were measured using a region of interest (ROI) of at least 1 cm² placed over the solid, maximally enhancing component of the lesion in the arterial phase. The ROI was carefully positioned to avoid cystic/necrotic areas. In heterogeneous lesions, the ROI was drawn within the most homogeneous enhancing area (in the arterial phase) and replicated at the same location across other phases. Measurements were obtained from three separate sections of the tumour, and the mean value was used for analysis. 131I-Meta-Iodo-Benzyl-Guanidine (131I-MIBG) scan data were collected, if available. Haemodynamic variables included the number of episodes of intra-operative hypertension (SBP ≥160 mmHg) and hypotension (MAP <60 mmHg) and maximum and minimum SBP, DBP, and HR.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was calculated via SPSS statistics for Windows, version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the distribution of all dependent and independent continuous variables was checked by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-parametric variables were summarised as median (IQR), while parametric variables were presented as mean ± SD. Comparative analyses between patients with cystic and non-cystic pheochromocytomas were performed using appropriate statistical tests based on data distribution: independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

The study procedures adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent revisions. This study was performed after obtaining the Institute Ethics Committee’s approval (Institutional Ethics Committee for observational studies, JIPMER, Puducherry). Approval letter number: JIP/IEC-OS/2024/444 dated 14/02/2025. There was waiver of the consent as this is a retrospective study.

RESULTS

A total of 46 patients with histopathologically confirmed pheochromocytomas were analysed, of which eight (17.4%) were cystic and 38 (82.6%) were non-cystic. The demographic, clinical, biochemical, and radiological characteristics were compared between the two groups. There were no statistically significant differences in age, gender, and anthropometric parameters [Table 1].

Clinical features and biochemical profiles

Overall symptomatology between cystic and non-cystic pheochromocytomas was similar, as shown in Table 1. Mass effect symptoms were seen in 43% of cystic lesions and 29% of non-cystic lesions (P = 0.66). Pain abdomen was the chief complaint in 37.5% (3/8) cystic pheochromocytoma patients. Typical spells of pheochromocytomas, hypertension, and the complications were noticed similarly in both the groups. However, patients with predominantly cystic tumours had significantly lower prevalence of elevated plasma normetanephrine levels (37.5% vs. 85.7%, P = 0.005) [Table 1]. Adrenergic tumours were observed in 9 (31%) patients in the non-cystic group, whereas none were identified in the predominantly cystic group. Anaemia was present in half of cystic pheochromocytoma patients and haemoglobin levels tended to lower in the cystic group, albeit not reaching significance. Intra-operative parameters, including maximum and minimum blood pressure and blood pressure fluctuations, were comparable between groups. Cystic tumours showed a trend towards fewer hypertensive spikes during surgery, though this was not statistically significant [Table 2]. The incidence of intra-operative hypertensive crises was lower in the predominantly cystic lesions than in those with non-cystic lesions. Post-operative complications included end-stage renal disease in one patient and dilated cardiomyopathy in another, both requiring ongoing follow-up.

Radiological characteristics

Left-sided occurrence was significantly more frequent in cystic tumours (P = 0.005). Four in the non-cystic group and one in the cystic group had bilateral lesions. Three von Hippel Lindau (VHL) patients were there in cystic group, out of which one had bilateral lesions. All nine cystic tumours (in eight patients) had predominant cystic components as shown in Figure 1. Apparently non-cystic looking pheochromocytomas had necrosed segment, and the median cystic component was measured to be 5.5% (0–30%). In this group, five patients had VHL, one had MEN 2B, and three had NF1. Although statistically insignificant, predominantly cystic pheochromocytomas had larger tumour sizes (5.8 ± 2.6 cm vs. 7.2 + 3.4 cm, P = 0.31) and volumes (48.5 ± 33.8 vs 105.1 ± 75.5 cm3, P = 0.07) compared to non-cystic lesions. CT scan attenuation patterns of the solid component of cystic pheochromocytomas were measured. At the baseline, the cystic group had lower attenuation compared to the non-cystic counterpart (39.25 ± 7.6 vs 31.3 ± 8.2, P = 0.033). However, in the arterial phase, both showed similar enhancement patterns of a typical pheochromocytoma [Table 3]. The ‘ring sign’ was observed in 37.5% of the predominantly cystic lesions. The 131I-MIBG scan was able to pick up 71.4% (5/7) of the cystic pheochromocytomas, which was similar to the non-cystic ones. No metastatic lesion was detected.

DISCUSSION

Adrenal cysts are classified into four main types: pseudocysts, endothelial cysts, epithelial cysts, and parasitic cysts.[2] Pseudocysts are the most encountered subtype in surgical series. Endothelial cysts are further sub-divided into lymphangiomatous and angiomatous types. Epithelial cysts are rare and include true glandular and embryonal cysts; some authors also include cystic adrenal tumours within this category. The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the clinical, biochemical, and radiological distinctions between cystic and non-cystic pheochromocytomas. In our cohort, the peak incidence of cystic pheochromocytomas was observed during the fourth decade of life, with a slight female predominance. The observed age of onset in our cohort was earlier than that reported in Western populations, where cystic lesions are more commonly diagnosed in the sixth decade.[7] A total of eight patients had cystic pheochromocytomas, with one patient having bilateral adrenal involvement, accounting for nine cystic lesions overall. Notably, cystic tumours in this series predominantly involved the left adrenal gland (87.5%), contrasting with the right-sided predominance seen in non-cystic tumours (70.6%). This lateralisation pattern differs from the Mayo Clinic series, which reported a right-sided predominance for cystic lesions.[7] Although they found only 21 (3.2%) cystic lesions of 638 pheochromocytomas, in other series, the prevalence of cystic pheochromocytomas ranges from 19% to 55%.[7891011] Our study documented a notable prevalence of cystic pheochromocytomas (17.4%), highlighting the potential under recognition of this subtype in clinical practice. The observed disparity in prevalence in different studies may reflect ethnic differences, modality of imaging, and the definition of cystic pheochromocytoma.

All adrenal masses in our cohort were referred to endocrinology, and pre-operative plasma metanephrine was routinely assessed as per institutional protocol. Only three patients in the predominantly cystic group were found to have normetanephrine-secreting tumours, reinforcing the notion of lower secretory capacity in cystic pheochromocytomas. However, none of the cystic pheochromocytomas was secreting metanephrine in our study, in contrast to the study by Ito et al. and Dogra et al., where cystic lesion was more common among epinephrine secreting pheochromocytomas.[79] Therefore, many cystic pheochromocytomas were biochemically silent, with diagnosis often made incidentally during resection due to haemodynamic fluctuations.[5612] Our findings differ from the Mayo Clinic series, which reported that most cystic pheochromocytomas were biochemically active.[7] This discrepancy may be attributed to the inclusion of partially cystic lesions in their study, with approximately 60% solid component, potentially influencing the overall secretory profile. In contrast, our cohort comprised relatively more cystic lesions, likely accounting for the observed lower biochemical activity. Interestingly, despite literature suggesting that adrenaline-secreting tumours are more prone to degeneration, none of the cystic lesions were adrenergic in our series.

The prevalence of hypertension and its complications was comparable between cystic and non-cystic groups. The incidence of intra-operative hypertensive crises was notably lower in the cystic group. This finding, while not statistically significant, may indicate a lower catecholamine secretion profile in cystic tumours, reducing intra-operative haemodynamic instability. Nonetheless, pre-operative optimisation remains crucial due to the potential for intermittent catecholamine release, particularly in lesions with residual solid components. Post-operative complications included development of end-stage renal disease in one and dilated cardiomyopathy in another, both requiring ongoing follow-up. Overall, the diagnostic challenges posed by cystic pheochromocytomas, particularly in normetanephrine-negative cases, underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with large adrenal masses and atypical imaging features.

Cystic lesions exhibited significantly larger tumour volumes and diameters than their non-cystic counterparts (105.1 vs. 48.5 cm3; 7.2 vs. 5.8 cm). This finding is consistent with Ito et al.,[9] who reported that cystic degeneration correlates with larger tumour sizes, particularly in lesions exceeding 6 cm in diameter. The larger size at presentation likely contributed to mass effect which was predominant symptoms in our series. The lower haemoglobin levels observed in the cystic group could be due to extensive necrosis causing more inflammation and release of interleukin 6.[13]

Radiologically, the ‘ring sign’ described by Velasquez et al.[14] is characterised by a hyper enhancing rim surrounding a necrotic core. It was observed in 37.5% of our cystic lesions. We also found that had the solid components in cystic lesions on CT scans a mean baseline attenuation more than 10 HU consistent with pheochromocytoma diagnosis.[15] The arterial phase enhancement of the cyst wall is a striking feature that distinguishes it from other benign lesions. A Brazilian study also emphasised the significance of rim enhancement as a key diagnostic feature of cystic pheochromocytomas.[10] Mayo Clinic also recommended biochemical testing for pheochromocytoma in all cystic adrenal lesions with baseline attenuation more than 10 HU.[7] However, two cystic pheochromocytoma lesions had baseline attenuation less than 10 as reported in the study by Raja et al.[8] Additionally, the clinical utility of 131I-MIBG scan warrants discussion. The 131I-MIBG scan was performed in seven of the eight cystic pheochromocytoma patients, successfully diagnosing five cases with a detection rate comparable to that of non-cystic counterparts. This underscores the role of radiological and nuclear imaging in distinguishing cystic pheochromocytomas from other cystic adrenal masses.

This study is limited by the small sample size in the cystic group, which may reduce the power to detect some statistically significant differences. However, all CT images were evaluated by a single radiologist, which limits assessment of interobserver variability. Additionally, retrospective data collection and single-centre design may introduce selection bias. The presence of hereditary syndromes, particularly VHL in the cystic group, may have influenced tumour biology and secretory profile. The extent of cystic degeneration was assessed by imaging and not histologically, which could affect accuracy. Further multicentric studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these observations.

CONCLUSION

Predominantly cystic pheochromocytomas are frequently left-sided, exhibit less frequent catecholamine secretion, and tend to be larger with a lower baseline but comparable attenuation in the arterial phase of solid components on CT; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. Their identification requires careful radiological assessment, especially in biochemically negative patients. Enhanced awareness of their characteristics may improve diagnosis and optimise surgical outcomes.
