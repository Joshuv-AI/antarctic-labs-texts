Effectiveness of an Educational Intervention on Medical Device‐Related Pressure Injury Prevention for Registered Nurses: A Single‐Group Quasi‐Experimental Pre–Post Intervention Trial
Authors: Mousa Y Asiri, Sahar Abdulkarim Al‐Ghareeb, Homoud Alanazi, Badr Ayed Alenazy, Abdullah Musaed Alharbi, Tariq Mohammed Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMedical device‐related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) are a patient safety concern in acute and critical care settings. Registered nurses must implement preventive strategies, yet gaps remain in their knowledge, attitudes, and clinical practices related to MDRPI prevention. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured educational intervention in improving registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to MDRPI prevention. A quasi‐experimental pre–post intervention study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital in Saudi Arabia, with 311 registered nurses participating. Data were collected using a questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to MDRPI prevention. Participants received a one‐day structured educational program based on the knowledge, attitude, practice (KAP) framework and the evidence‐based SKINCARE bundle. The educational intervention resulted in significant improvements. Mean scores increased from 14.17 (SD = 2.38) pre‐intervention to 16.25 (SD = 1.97) post‐intervention (t = −11.81, p < 0.001). The intervention demonstrated a moderate‐to‐large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.67), indicating meaningful improvement in nurses' preventive competencies. Structured educational programs can enhance registered nurses' knowledge and practices in MDRPI prevention. Integrating evidence‐based training programs into routine hospital education may strengthen pressure injury prevention guidelines and improve patient safety outcomes.Keywords: knowledge, attitude and practice; medical device‐related pressure injury; nursing education; patient safety; pressure injury prevention; SKINCARE bundle

Introduction

Guided by post‐positivist philosophy, this study objectively measured changes in registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and practices following a structured educational program [1]. The study is grounded in the knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) framework, which posits that increasing knowledge modifies attitudes, which in turn improve clinical practice [2, 3], and is informed by the health belief model, which holds that perceived susceptibility, severity, and self‐efficacy jointly determine preventive behaviour [4]. The integration of these frameworks provides a theoretically coherent basis for designing and evaluating educational interventions targeting complex nursing preventive behaviours [1].

The hypothesis tested was that registered nurses who received comprehensive MDRPI prevention education would demonstrate significantly improved prevention protocols at post‐test than at pre‐test [5, 6]. Although growing global attention has been directed towards MDRPI prevention, few interventional studies in Saudi Arabia have assessed registered nurses' SKINCARE bundles using frameworks such as the KAP model [2, 7]. The present study addresses this gap by combining a validated behavioural measurement tool with a structured, evidence‐based educational program delivered in a Saudi Arabian hospital setting [6].

Background

Pressure injuries (PIs) occur over bony prominences when prolonged pressure, friction, or shearing forces compromise skin integrity [7]. The National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) defines medical device‐related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) as localised damage to the skin or underlying tissue resulting from devices designed for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes [8]. The resultant PI generally conforms to the device's pattern or shape [9]. The PI staging system for medical devices is used to describe PIs in clinical practice [10]. Since the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, the use of medical devices in both intensive care and general wards has increased due to the greater need for respiratory support and continuous patient monitoring. The prolonged use of these devices may increase the risk of medical device‐related pressure injuries, contributing to a higher incidence of MDRPIs [11]. As PIs are widely recognised as nursing‐sensitive indicators, their prevention has become a key focus in healthcare quality improvement efforts [11].

The global prevalence of MDRPIs varies significantly across healthcare settings and patient populations [12]. Nearly one‐third of all PIs are associated with medical devices [7, 13]. In SA, Amirah et al. [14] reported a 26.7% prevalence of MDRPIs in ICUs, with 83% linked to devices such as endotracheal tubes and urinary catheters [15]. In the United States, MDRPIs impose an annual economic burden of approximately $2.73 to $3.48 billion [16].

Preventing MDRPIs is complex, particularly when devices are essential to patient treatment. Studies have shown that patients with medical devices are two to four times more likely to develop PIs [17]. MDRPIs can result in severe consequences such as infection, visible scarring, pain, extended hospitalisation, and reduced quality of life [7, 18]. Given registered nurses' pivotal role in patient care, their awareness of and training in MDRPI prevention are essential [2]. However, device‐related challenges often hinder routine skin assessment and care, as registered nurses may hesitate to reposition medical devices for fear of dislodgement [19]. Inconsistent prevention protocols, often due to a lack of standardised training and time constraints, further exacerbate the issue [20].

Given their increasing prevalence across healthcare settings [21], MDRPIs have emerged as a critical indicator of nursing care quality and patient safety [19]. Recent epidemiological studies have demonstrated substantial variation in MDRPI incidence, reflecting both clinical and systemic disparities [12]. The prevalence of medical device–related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) varies across healthcare settings and countries. For example, Saleh and Ibrahim [22] reported that 5.01% of ICU patients in Jordan developed MDRPIs, while Dang et al. [22] identified a 13.1% prevalence among hospitalised patients in China. Similarly, De Assis et al. [23] reported that 34% of patients in Brazilian intensive care units experienced MDRPIs, highlighting the substantial variation in reported rates across different clinical contexts [23, 24]. Although these differences can be partly attributed to population characteristics and device types, they more critically point to inconsistencies in prevention protocols, staff training, and institutional policies [24].

Across studies, facial areas, especially those affected by endotracheal and nasal devices, have been consistently identified as high‐risk anatomical sites [22, 23]. While some studies have reported the occurrence of early‐stage injuries (e.g., Stage 1), which suggests the potential for prevention through early detection, this is undermined by frequent under‐documentation of preventive interventions [25]. For example, ***Saleh and Ibrahim [26] reported that only 177 preventive interventions were documented among 15 patients at risk of MDRPIs, indicating suboptimal adherence to recommended prevention practices. Consequently, registered nurse–related factors, including knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices, play a critical role in MDRPI prevention [19, 26, 27]. Despite their pivotal position in daily patient care, studies reveal insufficient knowledge and practice gaps [2]. ***Sönmez and Bahar [28] found that only 23% of registered nurses had received formal MDRPI education, while Sönmez et al. (2025) reported poor knowledge among ICU staff, regardless of clinical experience [29]. Fang et al. [27], in a systematic review involving 8847 registered nurses, echoed these concerns, highlighting the disconnect between generally positive attitudes towards MDRPI prevention and insufficient knowledge or practical application [27]. Notably, prior MDRPI training, higher education, ICU experience, and wound care certification were positively associated with higher knowledge scores and better clinical practices [26].

The implementation of structured educational interventions has shown promising outcomes in addressing these gaps [26, 30]. For instance, Tayyib et al. [7] demonstrated that a SKINCARE bundle intervention significantly enhanced compliance with MDRPI prevention guidelines among ICU registered nurses. Neil and Martin [31] found that combining training with practical tools, such as prophylactic dressings, scheduled device repositioning, and comprehensive skin assessments, substantially reduced the incidence of MDRPIs [31]. These interventions were most effective when they were multifaceted, evidence‐based, and supported by organisational leadership [32].

Despite these advances, gaps remain in integrating these programs into practice, particularly in regions such as Saudi Arabia, where standardised education on MDRPI prevention is limited [7, 14, 33]. Moreover, many existing studies rely on observational or retrospective designs, thus limiting causal inference [12]. Few have employed interventional designs specifically rooted in behavioural change frameworks, such as the KAP framework [2]. Collectively, these findings highlight inconsistent MDRPI prevention practices globally and underscore the need for theory‐driven educational interventions, particularly in Saudi Arabia military city [2, 7, 12]. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured one‐day educational intervention incorporating the SKINCARE bundle and guided by the KAP framework in improving registered nurses' MDRPI prevention protocols in a Saudi Arabian military city.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A single‐group quasi‐experimental pre–post intervention design was employed [5, 6]. Quasi‐experimental designs are appropriate for evaluating educational interventions in intact clinical populations when randomisation is ethically or logistically infeasible [6]. The study was conducted at a military hospital in Saudi Arabia. Following institutional ethical approval, an official authorisation letter from the nursing administration of the military city was distributed to all clinical directors and head nurses.

Sampling Technique, Sample Size, and Participants

Convenience sampling was used to recruit all full‐time registered nurses directly involved in patient care at the study hospital [6]. Administrative and nonclinical nursing staff were excluded [6]. Of approximately 2300 registered nurses in the workforce, 311 voluntarily completed the pre–post questionnaire (participation rate 13.5%) [34].

Sample size was determined a priori using power analysis, applying the following parameters: α = 0.05, power = 0.80, anticipated effect size d = 0.40 (consistent with comparable educational interventions) [27, 30], ICC = 0.03, and 10% attrition allowance [34]. The achieved sample of 311 exceeded the minimum required, providing adequate statistical power for the planned analyses [34].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (a) full‐time registered nurses currently employed at the study hospital, (b) directly engaged in hands‐on patient care [6, 35]. Exclusion criteria: (a) administrative or management role with no direct patient care responsibilities and (b) nonclinical nursing staff [6, 35]. These criteria ensured that all participants had professional relevance to MDRPI prevention practice [6, 35].

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary [36]. An informed consent form was distributed electronically, and completion of the online questionnaire constituted implied consent [36]. Participant anonymity was maintained throughout the process [36].

Educational Intervention

Participants received a structured one‐day educational session designed to enhance registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices regarding MDRPIs [30]. The intervention was developed based on the KAP framework and integrated the evidence‐based SKINCARE bundle proposed by Tayyib et al. [7] (see Table 1).

The session was delivered by certified wound care practitioners and included multiple teaching strategies such as lectures, case‐based discussions, skills demonstrations, and simulation exercises [37]. To accommodate nursing shift schedules, the sessions were repeated over several days to ensure that all eligible participants had the opportunity to attend [37]. The educational program was delivered as a training session based on international pressure injury prevention guidelines [38]. The curriculum included five modules covering pressure injury overview, risk assessment, medical device‐related pressure injury prevention strategies, implementation of the SKINCARE bundle, and case‐based simulation. Each module lasted approximately 45 min [7].

The KAP framework guided the educational design, emphasising that improving registered nurses' knowledge of MDRPI pathophysiology and prevention can positively influence attitudes and ultimately enhance clinical preventive practices [2, 3]. The SKINCARE mnemonic provided a structured framework for translating evidence‐based recommendations into practical clinical behaviours for MDRPI prevention [7].

Data Collection Instruments

Data were collected using a structured self‐administered questionnaire adapted from previously validated instruments assessing registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices related to pressure injury prevention and medical device‐related pressure injuries [7]. The questionnaire incorporated elements derived from the SKINCARE bundle framework [7]. The items were scored (0 = incorrect, 1 = correct), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 23 [7]. Higher scores indicated greater knowledge of evidence‐based strategies [7]. The instrument demonstrated overall good psychometric properties, with acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.87) and test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.85) [39].

The questionnaire was developed in English, which is the primary working language used for clinical communication and professional documentation within the hospital [7]. The questionnaire was pilot tested with 20 registered nurses to assess clarity and feasibility; minor revisions were made based on feedback, and pilot data were excluded from the final analysis.

Study Procedure and Data Collection

Participants received the structured educational intervention outlined above, integrating the KAP framework with the evidence‐based SKINCARE bundle (See Table 1) [7, 30]. The one‐day structured educational session was delivered by qualified wound care practitioners and comprised lectures, case‐based discussion, skills demonstration, and simulation [30, 40]. Sessions were delivered across multiple days to accommodate nurses working different shifts [40]. This study adopted the KAP framework as the conceptual framework guiding the educational intervention [2, 7, 37]. The KAP framework supports the idea that increasing registered nurses' knowledge will influence attitudes, which will in turn positively affect clinical practice [2, 3]. The post‐test was administered immediately upon completion of the educational session, providing a measure of immediate knowledge acquisition [41]. A follow‐up assessment was not conducted because the study was designed to evaluate the immediate effect of the educational intervention within the available study timeframe [41].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarise participants' sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, years of clinical experience, and prior training in pressure injury prevention [42, 43]. Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were reported to describe the sample distribution [6] (see Table 2). Before the inferential analysis, data were tested for normality using skewness > 2, kurtosis > 7 [29], and Q–Q plots to determine the suitability of parametric tests. The distributions of pre‐test and post‐test scores met the assumptions of normality, allowing the use of paired‐samples t‐tests for mean comparisons [44]. A paired‐samples t‐test was conducted to assess differences in registered nurses' knowledge and practice scores before and after the educational intervention [44] (see Tables 3 and 4). Pearson's correlations were performed to explore associations between registered nurses' demographic characteristics and pre‐ and post‐test scores [43]. Finally, the effect size was measured using Cohen's d to assess the magnitude of the intervention effect [45, 46, 47].

Results

The sample comprised 311 registered nurses (81.0% female; mean age 33.32 years, SD = 4.63). The majority held bachelor's degrees (94.9%) and had 2–4 years of experience (66.6%). Most were assigned to medical units (63.3%). Prior pressure injury training had been received by 79.1% of participants (see Table 2). Skewness and kurtosis values for all variables were within acceptable limits (Sk < 2, Ku < 7), indicating approximate normality. Therefore, the assumptions for parametric testing were met. Given the large sample size (N = 311), minor deviations from normality were unlikely to affect subsequent parametric analyses (see Table 3).

A paired‐samples t‐test was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the educational intervention. There was a statistically significant improvement from the pre‐test (M = 14.17, SD = 2.38) to the post‐test (M = 16.25, SD = 1.97), t(311) = −11.81, p < 0.001. The mean increase of 2.08 points (95% CI [−2.43, −1.73]) corresponds to a moderate‐to‐large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.67; 95% CI [0.55, 0.79]). The Hedges' correction yielded a similar value (d = 0.67). Considering the questionnaire range of 0–23 points, the observed increase of 2.08 points represents an improvement of approximately 9% of the total scale, indicating a meaningful educational impact on registered nurses' competencies related to MDRPI prevention (see Table 4).

A Pearson product moment correlation was conducted to examine the relationships among age, pre‐test, and post‐test scores. The results indicated no significant correlation between pre‐test and post‐test scores, r(311) = −0.01, p = 0.863. A weak, negative correlation was observed between age and pre‐test scores, r(311) = −0.11, p = 0.049, suggesting slightly lower pre‐test scores among older registered nurses. However, age was not significantly correlated with post‐test scores, r(311) = −0.04, p = 0.494. These findings indicate that the educational intervention improved scores across all age groups (see Table 5, Figures 1 and 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured educational intervention designed to improve registered nurses' competencies in preventing MDRPIs [23]. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in competency scores between pre‐ and post‐intervention assessments, indicating that structured education can meaningfully enhance registered nurses' readiness to implement evidence‐based prevention practices [42]. These findings contribute to the expanding body of literature highlighting the importance of targeted education as a key strategy for strengthening pressure injury prevention practices in clinical settings [48, 49]. Educational interventions remain central to international prevention strategies because registered nurses play a critical role in the early identification of device‐related risk factors and the implementation of preventive care measures [28].

The observed improvement in competency scores supports the growing evidence that structured educational interventions can enhance registered nurses' knowledge and clinical practices related to pressure injury prevention [24, 31, 47]. Previous studies have demonstrated similar improvements following structured educational programs focusing on device‐related pressure injury prevention. For example, Tayyib et al. [7] reported significant improvements in ICU registered nurses' knowledge and preventive practices following implementation of the SKINCARE bundle training program [7]. Likewise, Fang et al. [7, 27]. These findings align with the theoretical principles of the KAP framework, which suggests that improving knowledge can positively influence clinical attitudes and ultimately lead to improved preventive practices [27]. Educational interventions that combine theoretical instruction with practical case‐based learning may therefore strengthen registered nurses' ability to translate evidence‐based guidelines into routine clinical care [27].

The moderate‐to‐large effect size observed that the educational intervention produced a meaningful improvement in registered nurses' competencies, in line with a systematic review conducted by Alshahrani [33]. Effect sizes of this magnitude are considered substantial in educational and clinical research and suggest that the intervention may have practical relevance for improving prevention practices in healthcare settings [33]. Comparable improvements have been reported in previous studies examining pressure injury prevention education programs across critical care environments [30].

Improved nursing competencies have previously been associated with enhanced pressure injury prevention guidelines and improved patient safety outcomes [50]. Research by Tan et al. [51] emphasised that registered nurses' clinical knowledge and perceptions regarding pressure injury preventability are critical determinants of prevention strategies. Nevertheless, although improved competencies represent an important intermediate outcome, the present study did not directly measure clinical outcomes such as MDRPI incidence rates [12, 21, 51].

An important finding of the present study is that demographic characteristics such as age, years of clinical experience, and educational level were not significantly associated with post‐intervention competency scores [2]. Although minor differences were observed in the baseline scores, these differences did not persist following the educational intervention. This is consistent with research by Yoo et al. [11], who found that well‐designed educational programs can benefit registered nurses across diverse demographic groups and clinical backgrounds.

Previous cross‐sectional studies by Li et al. [52] and ALFadhalah et al. [53] reported that registered nurses with greater professional experience or prior education demonstrated higher baseline knowledge related to pressure injury prevention. However, a systematic review conducted by Carmo et al. suggested that structured training programs can reduce such disparities by providing standardised evidence‐based instruction [54]. These findings support the implementation of universal educational programs rather than restricting training opportunities to specific demographic groups.

Prior studies by Tobiano et al. [55], Kharoosheh [28], and Lyu et al. [56] found that educational interventions demonstrated consistent effectiveness across a range of clinical environments, including medical wards, surgical units, intensive care units, emergency departments, and operating rooms [28, 55, 56]. While earlier research on MDRPI prevention has primarily focused on intensive care settings due to the high prevalence of medical devices in these units, the increasing use of devices in general hospital wards has expanded the relevance of MDRPI prevention beyond critical care environments [19].

Klaas and Serebro [57] reported that registered nurses across different clinical departments frequently exhibit gaps in knowledge related to pressure injury prevention practices. This evidence supports the need for institution‐wide educational initiatives to strengthen nurses' competencies and ensure consistent implementation of prevention strategies across healthcare settings.

The results of this study align closely with recommendations provided by international pressure injury prevention guidelines [38]. Guidelines published by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel and the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel emphasise that ongoing staff education is a fundamental component of comprehensive pressure injury prevention programs [38, 57, 58]. These guidelines recommend regular competency‐based education focusing on risk assessment, device‐related injury prevention, skin inspection, and the implementation of preventive interventions [38].

The educational intervention implemented in the present study incorporated these core guideline components, including device‐specific risk identification and prevention strategies [59]. The alignment between the intervention content and internationally recognised prevention guidelines strengthens the external validity of the study and supports the integration of similar educational programs into institutional prevention policies [60].

Strengths and Future Directions

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, a validated psychometrically robust instrument enabled reliable quantification of change [5, 6]. Second, the sample of 311 provided adequate statistical power and supported subgroup analyses across diverse demographic and clinical variables [42]. Third, integration of the KAP framework and the health belief model provided a coherent theoretical basis for both intervention design and outcome measurement [2, 4]. Fourth, multi‐shift delivery reduced selection bias from shift‐based exclusion. Fifth, the study contributes evidence from Saudi Arabia, a context underrepresented in the international MDRPI education literature [2, 33].

Future Directions

Several research directions emerged from this work. First, follow‐up assessments at prespecified time points (4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months) are needed to determine whether immediate post‐test gains are retained and translate into observable reductions in MDRPI incidence. Second, randomised or stepped wedge‐controlled designs should be employed where feasible to enable stronger causal inference. Third, multi‐site trials across diverse Saudi Arabian hospital types, academic medical centres, community hospitals, and private facilities would assess generalisability and identify contextual moderators of effectiveness. Fourth, direct observational measures of clinical practice (e.g., skin assessment audits and device repositioning compliance checks) should supplement self‐reports to address the gap between reported knowledge and actual behaviour. Fifth, economic analysis of the intervention's cost‐effectiveness relative to the established financial burden of MDRPIs [9] would strengthen the case for institutional adoption. Sixth, program adaptation for undergraduate nursing curricula would establish preventive competencies before registered nurses enter clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that structured educational interventions can significantly enhance registered nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to the prevention of MDRPIs. The results emphasise the critical role of ongoing professional development programs in strengthening clinical guidelines and improving patient safety standards. Healthcare organisations should prioritise mandatory MDRPI prevention education as part of orientation and annual competency training for all nursing personnel, particularly those working in high‐risk units such as intensive care and surgical and medical wards. In addition, the findings highlight the value of standardising educational frameworks aligned with international best practices to ensure consistent preventive care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Quasi‐experimental designs without randomisation limit the ability to draw firm causal conclusions, as unmeasured factors such as motivation, prior training, and workload may have affected outcomes [31]. Direct behavioural adherence to MDRPI prevention protocols in clinical practice was not assessed; the outcome measured reflects self‐reported questionnaire performance only. Reliance on self‐reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability bias, while the short follow‐up period captures only immediate post‐intervention effects rather than long‐term knowledge retention [31]. Moreover, the single institution sample limits generalisability, and contextual factors such as staffing levels or organisational support were not explored [31]. The participant distribution was heavily weighted towards medical units (63.3%), with minimal representation from the ICU (1.9%), the clinical setting where MDRPIs are most prevalent. This imbalance limits the generalisability of findings to high‐risk critical care contexts and should be considered when interpreting the results [31]. Despite these limitations, the findings provide evidence that structured educational programs can effectively enhance registered nurses' knowledge and preventive practice scores related to MDRPI prevention.
