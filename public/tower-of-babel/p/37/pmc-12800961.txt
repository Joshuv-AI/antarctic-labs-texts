A 3-Dimensional-Printed Shoulder Simulator for Rotator Cuff Repair Training
Authors: Patrick A. Massey, Payton Yerke Hansen, Robert Rutz, Wayne Scalisi, Arjun Verma, Giovanni F. Solitro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With advances in medical technology, the use and indications for arthroscopic procedures have expanded. Simulation training has been shown to directly translate to operative skills; however, high costs and limited funding impede access for many orthopaedic surgery resident physicians. This article introduces a low-cost, high-fidelity, highly adaptable, and anatomically accurate 3-dimensional-printed arthroscopic shoulder simulator that can improve access to arthroscopic training opportunities. This simulator was developed from computed tomography imaging of a cadaveric shoulder and designed with easily assembled, modular components that can simulate multiple shoulder pathologies. The authors show a double-row rotator cuff repair using the simulator. This procedure and others can be simulated with a rubber band, a glove, or a commercially available bone block.

Surgical training traditionally comprised the apprenticeship model, in which resident physicians gained intraoperative training under the guidance of clinical faculty. Despite the increasing prevalence and use of arthroscopy, training is limited by rising health care costs, concerns for patient safety, work hour restrictions, and a lack of objective competency measures.1, 2, 3 Trainees face significant difficulty with the neurocognitive requirements of arthroscopy, and many residents report inadequate time for arthroscopic training.4 Surgical simulation enables trainees to safely develop arthroscopic skills outside of the operating room and has been shown to improve their intraoperative arthroscopic performance.5, 6, 7, 8 However, access to simulation training is limited by funding at many orthopaedic surgery residency programs.9

A variety of relatively low-cost surgical simulators are available, but these options are often limited by face and transfer validity.10 Simulators have previously been produced via 3-dimensional (3D) printing for patient- and procedure-specific preoperative planning and training, such as in anterior stabilization and the Latarjet procedure.2,11,12 Because these simulators were designed for specific surgical techniques and/or patient anatomy, they may be limited in adaptability. Despite the increasing use of simulation training, there remains a need for low-cost, high-fidelity arthroscopic shoulder simulators.13 The authors present a double-row rotator cuff repair technique using an anatomically accurate, 3D-printed arthroscopic shoulder simulator that is low cost, high fidelity, and procedurally adaptable.

Surgical Technique

Simulator Development

The simulator was developed from a 3D model of a cadaveric left shoulder, which included the scapula, clavicle, and proximal humerus. The 3D model was previously obtained in an institutional review board-exempt study that utilized computed tomography images of a 67-year-old female cadaver. The scapula, clavicle, and proximal humerus were isolated, exported as a 3D model, and fitted with support structures and baseplates to be used in assembly (3D Slicer).14 Each component was printed individually with a rectilinear infill pattern and 90% density, and this modularity may facilitate both the future replacement of components and the simulation of multiple surgical techniques (Table 1). All simulator components, excluding the optional materials, were 3D printed in polylactic acid, a biodegradable plant-based polyester, on a Prusa MK4S 3D fused deposition modeling printer (Prusa Research a.s.). Optional components, including bone blocks, rotator cuff simulation material, elastic bands, and rubber grommets, may be added after printing and assembly of the simulator components. Pearls and Pitfalls of the 3D-Printed Arthroscopic Shoulder Simulator

Simulator Assembly and Procedural Adaptability

After printing all components and removing support material, the shoulder simulator is assembled by inserting components on the assembly plate, attaching the clavicle and shoulder hood via a locking pin, wrapping an elastic band around the baseplate hook and clavicular hook, and inserting the locking key in the lateral shoulder (Fig 1 A and B). The simulator is easily modified to simulate a variety of shoulder pathologies. Rubber grommets may be added to portal sites to simulate the size and tactile sensation of arthroscopic portal sites in vivo. The rotator cuff is simulated by taping a piece of neoprene, glove material, or fabric to the scapula. Labral repair, Bankart repair, and SLAP repair may be simulated by adding a bone block in the glenoid cavity. A bone block sheet can be added to the distal clavicle sockets to simulate acromioclavicular or coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction. Single- and double-row rotator cuff repairs of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus are simulated by replacing the lateral aspect of the proximal humerus with a 3-cm × 3-cm × 3-cm bone block (Sawbones) (Fig 1A). Simulation Training Technique: Double-Row Rotator Cuff Repair

In Video 1, the authors show a double-row rotator cuff repair using the simulator. A artificial tendon, fashioned from surgical gloves, is used to simulate the rotator cuff. A 30° arthroscope is inserted into the lateral portal, and the tear size is assessed using a probe in the posterolateral portal. Next, the superior portal is used to tap the anterior anchor site of the medial row, and the anterior suture anchor is then placed. Through the lateral portal, a suture passer with a top capture jaw is used to pass both sutures individually through the rotator cuff. After pulling these sutures anteriorly, the posterior suture anchor is tapped and placed. The posterior sutures are passed through the rotator cuff and then pulled through the posterior portal. The posterior and anterior sutures are then tied using alternating half-hitches.

Next, the lateral row is addressed. Knotless anchors are used for the lateral row, starting with the posterior anchor. One suture from both the anterior and posterior medial anchors is pulled through the lateral portal. These 2 sutures are passed though the knotless anchor. An awl is used to prepare the anchor site, and the anchor is driven into place. The suture ends are cut flush to the bone at the lateral anchor site. These steps are repeated for the anterior anchor in the lateral row. This completes the simulated double-row rotator cuff repair using the shoulder model.

Discussion

Simulation training has been shown to improve surgical proficiency and safety among orthopaedic surgery residents.5,6 Many arthroscopic simulators exist, including cadaveric models, virtual reality simulators, and benchtop simulators, such as the Alex II Shoulder Professor (Sawbones).15 However, access to high-fidelity simulators is often limited by high costs. In this article, the authors present a double-row rotator cuff repair technique using an anatomically accurate, 3D-printed arthroscopic shoulder simulator that is low cost, high fidelity, and procedurally adaptable. These features enable this simulator to be deployed globally. While 3D-printed surgical simulators have been described previously, this simulator is the first to facilitate high-fidelity and low-cost training of multiple surgical techniques.2,16 Moreover, the simulator is made of biodegradable materials and is environmentally friendly (Table 1).

Potential limitations of this simulator include the need for a 3D printer. Additionally, there may be limited face validity. However, an overreliance on an “anatomically accurate” model may deprive the trainee of the nuances of anatomic variation present in cadaveric specimens.17 Further, the proposed simulator is a dry model with no fluid in the joint. Despite these limitations, the simulator enables trainees to become more familiar with arthroscopic equipment and develop the neurocognitive coordination required for successful arthroscopy in a low-risk setting.
