Malignant esophageal perforation with pleural empyema: a successful robotic esophagectomy with primary reconstruction – a case report
Authors: Salome Breidenbach, Philipp Rhode, Robert Nowotny, Jürgen Feisthammel, Kien Vu Trung, Stefan Niebisch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Esophageal perforation, particularly when associated with malignancy, is a rare and life-threatening condition with a high mortality rate. Evidence regarding optimal management remains limited, with treatment options ranging from conservative measures to surgery, including staged repair or primary anastomosis. Further data are needed to guide the best practices for these complex cases.Case presentation:A 69-year-old man developed a malignant esophageal perforation following stent placement during neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The course was complicated by sepsis, pleural empyema, and mediastinitis. A single-stage robotic-assisted esophagectomy with primary reconstruction and simultaneous decortication, and lavage of the empyema was performed. The anastomosis was protected using EsoSPONGE®. The patient’s recovery was uneventful, allowing early oral intake. At follow-up, the patient remained cancer-free and was fully orally nourished.Clinical discussion:The optimal treatment strategy for malignant esophageal perforations remains unclear. In this case, resection with a direct anastomosis was feasible and prevented the patient from requiring further surgery. A high-volume center with specialized expertise and an interdisciplinary approach, involving intensive care, endoscopy, oncology, nutritional medicine, and surgery, was essential in achieving the best possible outcome.Conclusion:Primary anastomosis following resection in malignant esophageal perforations, complicated by sepsis and pleural empyema, is a feasible treatment option.

Introduction

Esophageal perforation is a life-threatening condition with a high mortality rate, which depends on early diagnosis and precision of treatment[1]. The etiology of perforation is diverse and includes iatrogenic, spontaneous, traumatic, infectious, and malignant causes[2]. Management strategies range from endoscopic interventions to non-operative or operative approaches[3], with the choice of treatment determined by the cause, location, and extent of perforation, as well as the patient’s general condition[4]. Endoscopic interventions, including stent placement, are established options for managing early-detected spontaneous or iatrogenic perforations and advanced esophageal cancer[5]. However, stenting is associated with significant risks, particularly stent migration and perforation[6]. Larger, more complex, and especially malignant perforations are usually managed surgically via primary repair or esophagectomy[4].

Treatment remains controversial in patients with risk factors for high morbidity and mortality, such as advanced age, malignant perforation, sepsis, and pleural contamination. Surgical therapy can address perforation, eliminating the source of infection while simultaneously managing associated complications, such as pleural empyema or mediastinitis[7,8]. Although esophagectomy is recommended for malignant perforations[9], there is a lack of consensus regarding the use of direct versus delayed anastomosis[7,10], which complicates treatment planning and necessitates an individualized, interdisciplinary approach.

We present a case of esophageal perforation with pleural empyema in a patient with adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) following chemotherapy and stent placement. Esophagectomy with primary repair via gastric sleeve reconstruction and pleural empyema resection were performed in a single procedure.

This case was reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines for reporting surgical cases[11].

Case report

A 69-year-old man was referred from a peripheral hospital with sepsis, right-sided pleural empyema, and mediastinitis. Four months prior to the event, the patient was diagnosed with an AEG II tumor (cT1a N1 Mx G2). During neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which consisted of four cycles of fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel (FLOT), dysphagia worsened. A fully covered metal stent was placed for symptomatic relief.

The patient was brought to a peripheral hospital by emergency medical services because of acute upper abdominal pain. Sepsis with pleural empyema and mediastinitis was diagnosed using computed tomography (Fig. 1), and antibiotic therapy was initiated. On arrival at our intensive care unit, the patient was alert, oriented, and hemodynamically stable, with respiratory stability under four liters of oxygen via a nasal cannula. Endoscopy revealed a perforation of the lower third of the esophagus, just below the metal stent. The stent did not cover the tumor. It was removed, and a vacuum-assisted closure stent (VACStent)[12] was implanted. A chest drain was inserted into the right thoracic cavity to manage pleural empyema, and antibiotic therapy was initiated. Following a thorough interdisciplinary discussion, two treatment options were presented to the patient and his family, along with the associated risks and potential outcomes. The first option, single-stage resection and reconstruction, would address the empyema, the septic source, and the carcinoma in a single procedure but carried a higher risk of anastomotic insufficiency. The second option, a staged approach consisting of drainage followed by delayed tumor resection and reconstruction, was considered the safer alternative. However, given that the patient had recently completed neoadjuvant chemotherapy, a staged approach posed a significant risk of forfeiting the oncological benefit of the neoadjuvant treatment. With full awareness of both options and their respective implications, the patient elected to proceed with the single-stage approach. The planned procedure, therefore, comprised esophagectomy with immediate reconstruction via gastric conduit, combined with empyema debridement, thereby addressing both the septic focus and the underlying malignancy.

The abdominal phase was performed robotically (Da Vinci system), reflecting our institutional standard for esophagectomy, as this approach provides excellent visualization while avoiding laparotomy. Additionally, the robotic system enables extensive mediastinal dissection from the abdominal approach, thereby reducing the operative time required during the thoracic phase and minimizing the duration of one-lung ventilation. Trocar positioning for the robotic abdominal phase is demonstrated in Figure 2. No evidence of peritoneal carcinomatosis was found. The esophagus was mobilized, a gastric conduit was fashioned, and abdominal lymphadenectomy was completed. Given the increased risk of anastomotic insufficiency, a jejunal feeding tube was placed 30 cm aborally to the ligament of Treitz. The thoracic phase was performed using a lateral muscle-sparing thoracotomy. Extensive pleural empyema was debrided. Clean conditions were achieved, enabling direct anastomosis. The esophagus and surrounding lymph nodes were mobilized. Transection of the esophagus was performed at the level of the azygos vein to ensure a safe distance from the tumor and to leave sufficient esophageal length for anastomosis. The gastric sleeve was translocated into the thoracic cavity; perfusion was assessed using hyperspectral imaging (HSI), and end-to-side anastomosis was performed using a circular stapler (EEA 25). The anvil head was secured within the esophageal lumen using a purse-string suture. A second inverting suture was placed circumferentially to ensure sufficient tissue was captured within the stapler prior to firing. The stapler was introduced through the gastric conduit, and the anastomosis was created under direct vision. Both tissue donuts, the esophageal and the gastric conduit rings, were inspected and confirmed to be intact and uninterrupted, indicating a technically complete anastomosis. The anastomotic line was subsequently reinforced with an additional oversewing suture. The gastrotomy through which the circular stapler had been introduced was closed using a linear stapler. An omentum flap was interposed between the anastomosis and the trachea, and a prophylactic Eso-SPONGE® was placed endoscopically at the level of the anastomosis. Two drains were inserted: one at the location of the anastomosis and another within the thoracic cavity connected to a portable suction system (Thopaz). Over the following days, the patient’s condition stabilized, allowing cessation of catecholamines and weaning from ventilatory support. While treating pneumonia with antibiotic therapy, respiratory function improved under non-invasive ventilation (NIV) therapy. Nutrition was maintained via a jejunal feeding tube. Eight days post-surgery, the Eso-SPONGE® was removed. Good wound healing and an intact anastomosis were observed, allowing the initiation of oral nutrition. Bilateral pleural effusion necessitated the placement of additional interventional thoracic drains. The patient exhibited gradual recovery of respiratory function and was transferred to the regular ward on the 26th day post-surgery. Both the right pleural empyema and left-sided effusion resolved, allowing the removal of both interventional drains. Experiencing subjective dyspnea, the patient underwent respiratory training. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed normal oxygen saturation. Dietary progress was made. Histopathological examination revealed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma of the esophagus. Following neoadjuvant therapy, staging according to the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, 8th edition, was ypT2 ypN0 (0/22) M0 R0 L0 V0 Pn0 (ypStage I)[13,14]. Tumor regression was graded as TRG 1b (subtotal regression, < 10% residual tumor cells) according to the Becker classification[15]. Considering the patient’s overall condition, we decided against postoperative chemotherapy. The patient was discharged for neurological rehabilitation 51 days after surgery with one drain for irrigation.

During follow-up at our outpatient clinic after rehabilitation, the remaining drain and jejunal feeding tube were removed after a successful transition to full oral nutrition. At 6 months postoperatively, the patient was mobilizing with a rollator and no longer required supplemental oxygen. As no further surgical issues were present, follow-up was subsequently continued in the oncological outpatient setting only.

Discussion

Esophageal perforations caused by tumors and stents are relatively uncommon. Rare cases of perforations have been reported during neoadjuvant chemotherapy[16]. The use of stents in the neoadjuvant setting for esophageal cancer in patients scheduled for esophagectomy remains a topic of debate. Although stent placement in patients with a stenosis-causing tumor can improve dysphagia, it is not associated with improvement in body weight or serum albumin[17]. Furthermore, stents are related to stent migration, can cause perforation, and are suspected to contribute to a higher mortality rate compared to patients who do not receive a stent[18]. Therefore, stent placement should be approached cautiously in patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy. Alternative strategies to support nutrition should be considered in such cases.

Esophageal perforations often present with nonspecific symptoms, making diagnosis challenging. Nevertheless, early diagnosis and treatment are crucial, as mortality rates can reach up to 40% when treatment is initiated later than 24 hours after the perforation event[4].

The management of malignant esophageal perforations, especially when complicated by sepsis and pleural empyema, remains controversial due to the limited number of cases and the lack of standardized guidelines. The current literature suggests an individualized treatment approach, as additional oncological needs impose unique requirements[19]. Severe inflammation within the thoracic cavity may hinder the proper healing of direct anastomosis after surgery. Primary reconstruction of the passage with direct anastomosis is performed only in exceptional cases[8].

In this case, we demonstrated that decortication and lavage of the pleural empyema, combined with a direct anastomosis, is a feasible approach. This strategy spared the patient from additional surgery and facilitated an earlier reintroduction of oral nutrition. Additionally, cervical diversion remains possible if the anastomosis fails to heal properly. The interdisciplinary approach, including the use of an EsoSPONGE® to protect the anastomosis, contributed to improved outcomes. To date, no clear evidence supports the use of sponge therapy for anastomosis after esophagectomy. Given the high risk of anastomotic insufficiency in this case and the favorable technical results of sponge therapy in the treatment of anastomotic leakages, a prophylactic sponge was placed[20]. Additionally, the placement of a jejunal feeding tube ensured adequate nutritional support, promoting the healing process.

We recommend a flexible approach that considers the patient’s overall condition, tumor stage, intraoperative findings, and patient preference.

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexity of managing malignant esophageal perforations, particularly when complicated by sepsis and pleural empyema. The management of such cases requires a high-volume center with specialized expertise. Therefore, early transfer is essential, as time is a critical factor in optimizing the outcome.

Our approach, which combines decortication and lavage of the pleural empyema with direct anastomosis, proved to be a feasible strategy, avoiding the need for additional surgery and allowing for an early return to oral nutrition.

A flexible and interdisciplinary approach, including intensive care, endoscopy, oncology, nutritional medicine, and surgery, is key to achieving the best overall and oncological outcomes. Given the lack of standardized guidelines for such cases, we emphasize the importance of an individualized treatment strategy that considers the patient’s overall condition, tumor stage, intraoperative findings, and patient will.
