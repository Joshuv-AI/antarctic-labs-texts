Network-targeted TMS modulates nicotine craving and default mode network connectivity in psychotic disorders

Introduction

Tobacco use is the top preventable cause of early mortality in schizophrenia, leading to a 20-year decreased life expectancy compared to the general population [1,2]. This is because the prevalence of tobacco use in schizophrenia is upwards of 60%, three-times the prev alence in the general population. Moreover, current smoking cessation treatments are significantly less effective for people with schizophrenia [3].

Despite decades of research, the underlying pathophysiology to explain the high prevalence of tobacco use in schizophrenia remains unknown. To date, investigators have largely focused on differences in reward circuitry between schizophrenia and healthy smoking populations [4,5]. Unfortunately, this has not led to more effective smoking cessation treatments in schizophrenia. What if, in addition to reward circuits well-established in addiction, there was another brain circuit driving nicotine use in schizophrenia? If we could treat this schizophrenia-specific circuit pathology, could we develop more effective smoking cessation treatments for this population?

To look beyond reward circuitry, in previous work, we used an agnostic, data-driven connectome-wide analysis that identified novel schizophrenia-specific brain circuits linked to nicotine use [6]. We observed that default mode network (DMN) organization was linked to daily cigarette use in schizophrenia, but not in controls. Specifically, in the schizophrenia group, we observed expansion of DMN spatial extent in parietal cortex that was related to daily cigarette use. While promising, this finding was observed in 35 people and requires replication in a larger, heterogeneous sample.

Therefore, in this manuscript, we first use a large psychosis-spectrum sample and healthy controls (Bipolar-Schizophrenia Network of Intermediate Phenotypes 2, B-SNIP2, n = 596, Fig. 1A) to validate the relationship between DMN organization and tobacco use in psychosis. Only in the psychosis group, we observe that 1) current smokers have lower DMN connectivity than former and never smokers and 2) that higher daily cigarette use is associated with lower DMN connectivity.

In previous work, we used an agnostic, data-driven connectome-wide analysis that identified novel schizophrenia-specific brain circuits linked to nicotine use [6]. We observed that the organization of the default mode network (DMN) was linked to daily cigarette use in schizophrenia, but not in controls. In this manuscript, we conducted a series of three experiments to extend and validate this relationship. First, we used a large cross-sectional sample of people with psychosis-spectrum disorders and healthy controls (Bipolar-Schizophrenia Network of Intermediate Phenotypes 2, B-SNIP2, n = 596) to validate a psychosis-specific relationship between DMN organization and tobacco use (Fig. 1A). We then tested this network model of nicotine use in psychosis in two mechanistic repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies. First, we applied single sessions of DMN-targeted iTBS, cTBS, and sham to determine their effects on nicotine craving (Single-Session DMN-Targeted TMS, Fig. 1B). We observed that single sessions of iTBS targeted to the DMN acutely increased craving compared to cTBS and sham. Given the hypothesized opposing effects of iTBS and cTBS, we increased the stimulation intensity of cTBS and delivered multiple sessions in an accelerated design (Accelerated, Multi-Session DMN-Targeted cTBS, Fig. 1C). With a higher stimulation intensity, we subsequently observed that cTBS reliably reduced craving both within- and between-participants. We also observed reduced DMN connectivity following multiple sessions of cTBS, thereby demonstrating evidence of target engagement. Created with BioRender.com.

These findings linking current cigarette use with reduced DMN connectivity are consistent with our previous work showing that acute nicotine administration normalizes DMN hyperconnectivity – but only in people with psychosis, not in controls [6]. DMN hyperconnectivity is a well-established observation in psychosis [7]. These results suggest that nicotine normalizes DMN dysfunction in psychosis, so modulating DMN connectivity may be a novel intervention for nicotine craving in psychosis.

To test this network model of nicotine use in psychosis, we used transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) targeted to the DMN [8]. Intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) has been associated with excitatory effects (i.e., increased connectivity), while continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) has been associated with inhibitory effects [9], but these effects have been inconsistent. We identified a reliable, personalized left parietal DMN target and applied single sessions of iTBS, cTBS, and sham to determine their effects on nicotine craving (Single-Session DMN-Targeted TMS, Fig. 1B) [10]. We hypothesized that iTBS would increase DMN connectivity and craving while cTBS would decrease connectivity and craving.

We observed that single sessions of iTBS targeted to the left parietal DMN acutely increased craving compared to cTBS, which supported our hypothesis. Given the hypothesized opposing effects of iTBS and cTBS (i.e., that cTBS should decrease connectivity and decrease craving), we increased cTBS stimulation intensity and delivered multiple sessions in an accelerated design (Accelerated, Multi-Session DMN-Targeted cTBS, Fig. 1C). With higher stimulation intensity, we observed cTBS reliably reduced craving and reduced DMN connectivity, thereby demonstrating evidence of target engagement.

Our findings provide first evidence for a potential mechanism of nicotine use in psychosis that suggests a novel target for smoking cessation interventions.

The final sample (Table 1) included 276 healthy and 320 psychosis-spectrum individuals (schizophrenia = 129, schizoaffective disorder = 124, bipolar disorder with psychotic features = 67).

Nicotine Assessments:

Nicotine dependence was assessed using the Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) [11]. Participants also provided tobacco use history.

MRI Acquisition & Processing:

Subjects underwent T1-weighted structural scan and 8-min resting-state fMRI scan (rs-fMRI) on a 3T scanner. Preprocessing was performed using the advanced DPARSF module within DPABI V6.0_210501 (Supplemental Table 1). Volume slices were corrected for different signal acquisition times across sites. The time series of images for each subject were realigned. Individual structural images (T1-weighted) were co-registered to the mean functional image after realignment. Transformed structural images were then segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [12]. To remove nuisance signals, the Friston 24-parameter model [13] was utilized to regress out head motion effects. To better control for physiological noise, a combined WM/CSF mask was generated from which the top 5 principal components were extracted and signal adjusted using CompCor [14]. Linear trends were adjusted since the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal exhibits low-frequency drifts. The DARTEL tool [15] was used to transform the functional data from individual native space to MNI space. Following this, temporal filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was performed. Details in supplement.

Neuroimaging Analysis - Calculation of Whole-Network Connectivity Values:

We calculated average DMN connectivity as previously by extracting time courses from standard DMN nodes and averaging connectivity between nodes for each participant [6]. We calculated individual values of average DMN functional connectivity by placing 6 mm spheres at coordinates corresponding to standard DMN nodes ([16] for coordinates). The BOLD signal time courses from the regions of interest (ROIs) were correlated with each other and z-transformed to generate an ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrix. A mean connectivity from this matrix was generated for each participant by averaging connectivity values for the entire DMN. Details in supplement.

Single-session DMN-targeted TMS in schizophrenia

Participants:

Fifteen individuals with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder aged 18–65 who use nicotine (confirmed for cigarette smokers by expired carbon monoxide ≥ 5 ppm) were enrolled in this randomized, sham-controlled, crossover TMS-fMRI study (Table 1, Fig. 1B, Supplemental Figs. 1–2, NCT07190352). Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center IRB. Details in supplement.

Nicotine Assessments:

Nicotine use was assessed at each of five visits. Nicotine dependence was assessed using the FTND [11] and Penn State Electronic Cigarette Dependence Index (PSECDI) [17]. Nicotine craving was assessed two ways: 1) 0–10 visual analogue scale (VAS) immediately before and after each TMS session; and 2) Tiffany Questionnaire of Smoking Urges (QSU) [18] at the beginning of each visit. Withdrawal was assessed using the Wisconsin Smoking Withdrawal Scale (WSWS) [19].

MRI Acquisition & Processing:

Imaging data were collected on a Siemens 3.0-T MRI system (Munich, Germany). Participants each completed 7 MRI scans: baseline and immediately before and after each TMS session (Fig. 1B). A total of 270 min of imaging data was collected per participant, including 140 min of resting-state imaging. Briefly, 1-mm3 T1-weighted anatomical scans and multiple 10-min functional runs were acquired (TR 650 ms, TE 34.80 ms, flip angle 50°, multiband acceleration factor 8, field of view = 207 mm, 64 slices, 3-mm3 voxels, anterior to posterior phase-encoded). All resting-state scans went through a quality assurance procedure that included calculating framewise displacement (FD) and temporal signal to noise ratio (tSNR). Scans with mean FD > 0.5 or tSNR<5th percentile of the sample distribution were excluded. After quality control, there were pre- and post-TMS scans for 8 cTBS, 9 iTBS, and 6 sham sessions across 9 subjects. Details in supplement.

TMS Protocol:

Individuals received single sessions of theta-burst stimulation (Fig. 1B & Supplemental Fig. 1) applied to an individualized left parietal DMN target (see Individualized DMN Target below and Supplemental Fig. 3) with neuroimaging collected immediately before and after each session. Individuals received one session of iTBS (600 pulses, 100% AMT), cTBS (600 pulses, 80% AMT as per [20]), and sham (coil flipped 180° using 100% AMT iTBS protocol, 600 pulses) on separate days, separated by at least 2 days to avoid carryover effect (median 6.5 days, mean 13.8 days (SD 22.2), range 2–96 days). TMS was applied using a MagPro X100 stimulator and an active figure-of-8 coil (Cool B65, MagVenture, Denmark) held tangentially to the scalp with the handle at 45°. TMS was applied in the standard theta-burst pattern (3 pulses at 50-Hz repeated at a rate of 5-Hz) [9]. Order was randomized, and participants were blinded.

Multi-session DMN-targeted cTBS in schizophrenia

Participants:

Twelve individuals with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder aged 18–65 who use nicotine (confirmed by expired carbon monoxide ≥ 5 ppm or urine cotinine >200 ng/ml) were enrolled in this TMS-fMRI study (Fig. 1C–Table 1, NCT07155096). Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Vanderbilt University Medical Center IRB. Details in supplement.

Nicotine Assessments:

As in the Single-Session TMS study, nicotine use was assessed at each of three visits. Nicotine dependence was assessed using the FTND [11] and PSECDI [17]. Nicotine craving was assessed two ways: 1) 0–10 VAS immediately before and after each TMS session and at each MRI visit; and 2) Tiffany QSU at each MRI visit [18]. Withdrawal was assessed using the WSWS [19] at each MRI.

MRI Acquisition & Processing:

Imaging data were collected on 3.0-T Philips Intera Achieva MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA). Participants completed MRI scans the week before and after the TMS intervention. As the durability of 5 TMS sessions is unknown, post-TMS scans were scheduled 3 to 7 days after TMS per protocol (mean 4.91 (SD 1.38) days), but due to repeated scheduling conflicts, one participant completed the post-TMS scan after 12 days (analyses in supplement). Briefly, 1-mm3 T1-weighted anatomical scans and multiple 10-min functional runs were acquired (TR 2000 ms, TE 28.0 ms, flip angle 90°, field of view = 240 mm, 38 slices, 3-mm3 voxels, anterior to posterior phase-encoded). Scans were processed according to the same procedure described above for the Single-Session DMN-Targeted TMS study. After quality control, there were pre-/post-TMS scans for 10 participants. Details in supplement.

Multi-Session TMS Protocol:

Individuals received 5 sessions of cTBS (600 pulses, 100% AMT, Fig. 1C) applied to an individualized left parietal DMN target (see Individualized DMN Target below) in a one-day accelerated protocol (30-min inter-session interval) with pre-/post-TMS neuroimaging. TMS was applied using a MagPro X100 stimulator and an active figure-of-8 coil (Cool B65, MagVenture, Denmark) held tangentially to the scalp with the handle at 45°. TMS was applied in the standard theta-burst pattern (3 pulses at 50-Hz repeated at a rate of 5-Hz) [9]. Details in supplement.

Individualized DMN Targeting:

To identify an individualized DMN map for TMS targeting, a standard DMN template [21] was warped into native space and applied to the participant’s pre-TMS scan. In each participant, the resultant connectivity maps yielded a correlation cluster in the left posterior inferior parietal lobule (IPL, Supplemental Fig. 3). A target was placed in the averaged center of the left posterior IPL correlation cluster (formed from the overlay of the left posterior IPL clusters derived from connectivity maps) on the cortical surface using Brainsight neuronavigation software (Rogue Research, Inc.). This individualized left parietal DMN region served as the TMS target for all sessions for both Single-Session DMN-Targeted TMS and Accelerated, Multi-Session DMN-Targeted cTBS.

TMS Neuroimaging Analysis - Calculation of TMS Target DMN Connectivity Values:

For the Single-Session TMS and Accelerated, Multi-Session cTBS analyses, we calculated average connectivity to the DMN from the left parietal DMN TMS target by extracting time courses from eight standard DMN nodes and averaging connectivity between the TMS target and the eight other DMN nodes for each participant [6]. We calculated individual values of DMN functional connectivity by placing 6 mm spheres at coordinates corresponding to standard DMN nodes ([16] for coordinates). The BOLD signal time courses from the left parietal DMN TMS target and the other DMN regions of interest (ROIs) were correlated with each other and z-transformed to generate ROI-to-ROI connectivity values. The mean DMN connectivity from the left parietal DMN TMS target was generated for each participant by averaging connectivity values of these eight network edges. Details in supplement.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables and Welch T-tests were used to compare continuous outcomes between groups (Table 1). For B-SNIP2, a linear mixed effects model with robust standard errors was used to model DMN connectivity by age, sex, site, group, tobacco use history, and their interaction.

For Single Session DMN-targeted TMS, a linear mixed effects model with robust standard errors was used to model craving by treatment time (pre- and post-stimulation), TMS condition (iTBS, cTBS, sham) and their interaction, including a random effect for subject. Tests of treatment effect for each stimulation condition were adjusted across groups using Tukey correction.

For Accelerated, Multi-Session DMN-targeted cTBS, we used a linear mixed effects model to fit craving by treatment time and visit number (modeled categorically) including a random intercept for subject. ANOVAs with robust standard errors were used to test the main effect of treatment time across all sessions. Paired t-tests were used to compare connectivity and behavioral measures at pre- and post-TMS MRI visits. All analyses were conducted in RStudio (Version 2023.03.1 + 446).

Results

B-SNIP2

History of tobacco use is related to DMN connectivity in psychosis

In B-SNIP2, individuals with psychosis were more likely to currently use tobacco and to have ever-used tobacco (Supplemental Results and Supplemental Fig. 4). In the combined sample, there was no significant interaction between tobacco use history and group (F = 0.9256, df1 = 2, df2 = 586, p = .40). In the psychosis group, we observed significant differences in DMN connectivity by tobacco use history (F = 3.8003, df1 = 2, df2 = 308, Cohen’s d = 0.222, p = .023, Fig. 2A) such that current smokers had lower DMN connectivity than former smokers (Estimate = −0.046, SE = 0.021, t(308) = −2.162, p = .031) and never smokers (Estimate = −0.044, SE = 0.018, t(308) = −2.403, d = 0.058, p = .017). There was no relationship between DMN connectivity and tobacco use in controls (F = 0.2252, df1 = 2, df2 = 266, d = p = .799, Fig. 2B). DMN connectivity was lower in current smokers with psychosis compared to controls (t(254.48) = 2.55, p = .011). See supplement additional analyses.

Current Tobacco Use is Related to DMN Connectivity in Psychosis.

Using a large, multisite study of individuals with psychotic disorders and controls (B-SNIP2), we observed significant associations between history of tobacco use and DMN connectivity in the psychosis group (p = .023, Fig. 2A) but not in controls (p = .66, Fig. 2B). In the psychosis group, current smokers had significantly lower DMN connectivity than former (p = .017) and never smokers (p = .021). Moreover, recent tobacco use was associated with lower DMN connectivity in the psychosis group, but not in controls. In the psychosis group, DMN connectivity was significantly lower in individuals who had used tobacco in the last 30 days (0.479 vs. 0.522, p = .038, Fig. 2C) and in the last 24 h (0.478 vs. 0.522, p = .006, Fig. 2D) than those who had not used.

In psychosis, recent tobacco use is associated with lower DMN connectivity

There was no significant interaction between tobacco use history and group (T = 0.3817, df = 1, p = .5373). In the psychosis group, DMN connectivity was lower in individuals who used tobacco in the last 30 days (Used in last 30 days: 0.479 vs. Not used: 0.522, T = 2.0979, df = 123.47, Cohen’s d = 0.38, p = .038, CI = [0.0024, 0.084]; Fig. 2C) and in the last 24 h (Used in last 24 h: 0.478 vs. Not used: 0.522, T = 2.7879, df = 251.9, d = 0.35, p = .006, CI = [0.013, 0.075]; Fig. 2D). In the control group, we did not observe differences in DMN connectivity based on tobacco use in the past 30 days (0.514 vs. 0.532, T = 0.51551, df = 37.023, d = 0.17, p = .609) or past 24 h (0.507 vs. 0.516, T = 0.26399, df = 16.153, d = 0.13, p = .795).

Higher cigarette use is associated with lower DMN connectivity

Daily cigarette use was quantified with the FTND, which provides a binned number of cigarettes smoked per day (≤10; 11–20; 21–30, ≥31). We observed a dose-response relationship where more cigarettes smoked per day was associated with lower DMN connectivity in the combined sample (Spearman, r = −0.21, p = .0095, CI = [−0.37, −0.054]). In a linear model, the interaction between cigarettes per day and group was not a significant predictor of DMN connectivity (Estimate = 0.006532, T = 0.079, p = .937). In the psychosis group, there was a relationship where more cigarettes smoked per day was associated with lower DMN connectivity (Spearman, r = − 0.22, p = .013). This relationship was nonsignificant in controls (Spearman, r = −0.026, p = .915).

Single-session DMN-targeted TMS in schizophrenia

Single session DMN-targeted TMS affects nicotine craving but not DMN connectivity

Ten participants with schizophrenia and daily nicotine use completed our randomized, controlled crossover study of individualized, left parietal DMN-targeted TMS (Fig. 1B and Supplemental Figs. 1–2, Table 1). Participants used various forms of nicotine: cigarettes (9/11), vape (2/11), and transdermal patch (1/11); were moderately nicotine dependent (mean FTND 5.33, SD 1.32); and smoked on average 14.89 cigarettes/day (SD 6.67). Ten participants completed all study visits and were included in the analysis (Table 1). TMS was well-tolerated, and there were no serious adverse events. Details in supplement.

Because we hypothesized iTBS and cTBS would have opposing effects on craving, we first directly compared these two interventions. There was a significant treatment by time interaction (X2 = 5.41, df = 1, p = .020) on nicotine craving. Adjusted tests of treatment effect showed iTBS significantly increased craving (Estimate = 2.5, t = 2.52, df = 27, padj = 0.018; Fig. 3) with insufficient evidence for cTBS (Estimate = −0.1, t = −0.14, df = 27, padj = 0.89). There were nonsignificant main effects of treatment (X2 = 0.38, df = 1, p = .54) and time (X2 = 1.27, df = 1, p = .26).

Single Session DMN-Targeted iTBS Increases Nicotine Craving.

In a randomized, controlled crossover study of personalized, DMN-targeted TMS, individuals received a single session of intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS), and sham stimulation (Fig. 3A). Nicotine craving was assessed immediately before and after each single TMS session using 0–10 visual analog scale. We hypothesized iTBS and cTBS would have opposing effects on craving. There was a significant treatment by time interaction (p = .020) on nicotine craving, such that iTBS significantly increased craving (padj = 0.018; Fig. 3B) compared to cTBS. Supplemental Fig. 5 includes error bars. Created with BioRender.com.

When we looked across all sessions, there was a trend-level treatment by time interaction (X2 = 5.6363, df = 2, p = .059) on nicotine craving. Consistent with earlier findings, iTBS significantly increased craving (Estimate = 2.5, t = 2.52, df = 45, padj = 0.015; Fig. 3), but no effect was observed with cTBS (Estimate = −0.1, t = −0.14, df = 45, padj = 0.88) or sham (Estimate = −0.4, t = − 0.37, df = 45, padj = 0.71).

Next, we directly compared the effects of iTBS and cTBS on left parietal DMN TMS Target connectivity to the DMN. There was no significant treatment by time interaction on connectivity (X2 = 0.082, df = 1, p = .78), suggesting a single session of TMS was insufficient to change connectivity across the network. Similarly, when we compared left parietal DMN TMS target connectivity to the DMN across all sessions, we did not observe a significant treatment by time interaction (X2 = 1.47, df = 2, p = .48).

Multi-session DMN-targeted cTBS in schizophrenia

Multiple sessions of DMN-targeted cTBS reduce nicotine craving

Twelve participants with schizophrenia and daily nicotine use were enrolled in an Accelerated Multi-Session study of individualized, left parietal DMN-targeted cTBS (Fig. 4A, Table 1, Supplemental Fig. 7). Participants used various forms of nicotine: cigarettes (4/12), vape (6/12), and non-combustible nicotine (2/12, transdermal patch, lozenge, or pouch), were moderately nicotine dependent (mean FTND 4.38, SD 2.77) and smoked on average 14.89 cigarettes/day (SD 6.67). TMS was well-tolerated, and there were no serious adverse events. Details in supplement.

Accelerated Multi-Session DMN-Targeted cTBS Reduces Nicotine Craving and DMN Connectivity.

In a study of Accelerated, Multi-Session DMN-targeted cTBS, individuals received five sessions of continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) applied to a personalized DMN target separated by 30 min with pre- and post-neuroimaging (Fig. 4A). Participants underwent resting-state neuroimaging prior to cTBS and 3–7 days after cTBS. Nicotine craving was assessed immediately before and after each single cTBS session using 0–10 visual analog scale. There were significant effects of time (i.e., pre vs. post each TMS session, p = .0001, Fig. 4B) and TMS session number (p = .0005) on craving, indicating that craving significantly decreased after each session of DMN-targeted cTBS. As evidence of target engagement, we observed a significant reduction in TMS target connectivity to the DMN (p = .047, Fig. 4C). Supplemental Fig. 8A and 8B include error bars. There were significant effects of time (i.e., post-pre = −0.40, X2 = 14.67, df = 1, p = .0001, CI = [−0.61, −0.19], Fig. 4B; Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Fig. 9) and TMS session number (X2 = 19.80, df = 4, p = .0005; Supplemental Tables 3–4) on craving, indicating that craving significantly decreased after each session of DMN-targeted cTBS. We observed a significant reduction in left parietal DMN TMS target connectivity to the DMN (t(8) = −2.34, CI = [−3.43, −0.025], p = .047, Fig. 4C & Supplemental Fig. 10). Exploratory analyses of individual DMN ROIs in supplement.

Discussion

Despite decades of research, nicotine dependence continues to pose a significant public health threat for individuals with schizophrenia due to the high prevalence of nicotine use and minimally effective smoking cessation treatments for this population. We used a large, multisite dataset of individuals with psychotic disorders to establish a novel relationship between DMN connectivity and nicotine use in psychosis-spectrum disorders. In people with psychosis, we observed a relationship where lower DMN connectivity was associated with higher daily cigarette use and more recent tobacco use, suggestive of a dose-response relationship. Our findings suggest a novel mechanism for the high prevalence of nicotine use in this population.

After establishing a relationship between DMN connectivity and nicotine use in psychosis, we targeted this network with single and multiple sessions of TMS applied to an individualized left parietal DMN target. A single session of iTBS increased craving compared to cTBS and sham. Given the hypothesized opposing effects of iTBS and cTBS, we concluded that a single session of cTBS (delivered at 80% AMT) was insufficient to reduce craving, so we next tested multiple sessions of cTBS at a higher stimulation intensity (100% AMT) delivered in an accelerated one-day protocol. Sure enough, with a higher stimulation intensity, we observed robust and reproducible effects where each session of cTBS reduced craving within- and between-participants. We observed demonstrable evidence of target engagement, as left parietal DMN TMS target connectivity to the DMN was reduced following cTBS.

In this study, we have identified a potential mechanism driving the high prevalence of nicotine use in schizophrenia and then used two mechanistic TMS studies to demonstrate target engagement. Our results are consistent with previous findings that acute nicotine administration normalizes DMN hyperconnectivity in people with psychosis [6]. Future research is necessary to understand the precise mechanism, but there is substantial evidence that modulation of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors leads to DMN deactivation during rest and task conditions [22, 23]. Our analysis advances the field in several ways. First, our findings suggest a relationship between nicotine use and DMN connectivity in psychosis. Although DMN connectivity has been linked to nicotine craving in non-psychosis populations [23–28], previous work targeting the left parietal DMN with TMS in the general population has not proven successful in reducing nicotine craving or withdrawal [29]. In contrast, we have now shown that targeting the left parietal DMN does affect craving in schizophrenia, providing evidence for a contributory mechanism driving nicotine use in people with psychosis, which may lead to more effective interventions for a population disproportionately at risk for the devastating consequences of tobacco use. While we did not observe a significant group*tobacco use interaction on DMN connectivity in B-SNIP2, the control population may have been underpowered. Our analyses show promising results of modulating the DMN in psychosis but did not test the effects of modulating DMN connectivity in controls who use nicotine. Therefore, it is challenging to determine if this relationship is unique to nicotine use or epiphenomena of illness, treatment, or other factors. To test if DMN modulation is more effective for nicotine craving in psychosis populations, future studies should compare DMN-targeted TMS in psychosis and control participants who use nicotine. DMN modulation may offer a more effective target for nicotine use in psychosis.

For decades, the field has sought to understand the high prevalence of nicotine use in schizophrenia by focusing on known overlapping abnormalities in reward circuitry in psychotic disorders and substance use [4]. However, this research has not led to more effective smoking cessation interventions in schizophrenia. Four-week continuous abstinence rates in schizophrenia following FDA-approved smoking cessation treatments lag below 20% – half the abstinence rate of the general population [3,30]. Our findings suggest that interventions targeting abnormal DMN connectivity may be more effective for smoking cessation in schizophrenia. Notably, our observed effects on craving were brief, suggesting that many more sessions of TMS are necessary to produce durable effects on craving and ultimately smoking cessation, consistent with prior studies [31–33]. Future studies should test if treatments modulating DMN connectivity improve smoking cessation rates in this population.

To our knowledge, this is the first study of single TMS sessions in schizophrenia with pre-/post-TMS neuroimaging. Single-session TMS studies provide critical information on mechanism but are difficult to execute [29,34], especially in symptomatic clinical populations, as effects of a single TMS session are fleeting, generally lasting less than 60 min [35]. Future studies should investigate test-retest reliability of single-session TMS studies.

Ours is also the first test of an accelerated TMS intervention for nicotine use in any population and the first for any substance use disorder in schizophrenia. Few studies have previously tested accelerated protocols for schizophrenia [36–39] and substance use disorders [40], but there is growing interest in developing accelerated protocols that may shorten time to response [41,42]. TMS was well-tolerated and has been shown to be safe in schizophrenia [43] and SUDs [44].

Our analyses have several limitations. Although the B-SNIP2 sample was large (n = 596), our two mechanistic TMS studies were relatively small (n = 10 and n = 12) and should be replicated in larger samples. It should be noted that although the Single-Session TMS study included neuroimaging data from 10 participants, we employed a within-subjects dense MRI scanning protocol where each participant underwent 7 scans, totaling 270 min/participant, including 140 min of resting-state imaging. Therefore, these participants provided a total of 70 separate scans for analysis, representing the first precision functional mapping study with TMS in schizophrenia. Importantly, our Accelerated Multi-Session cTBS study lacked a sham control, which is critical for future studies. Another limitation of our analysis was the assessment of craving using self-reported VAS. While VAS is brief and simple – both critical aspects for these mechanistic TMS studies – it lacks granularity. This may be why we did not observe an enduring effect of accelerated, multi-session cTBS on craving. Future studies should use more sophisticated assessments of craving, such as in-scanner cue-reactivity tasks, as correlates of target engagement.

In conclusion, these results provide evidence for a novel mechanism of nicotine use in psychosis that could lead to the first schizophrenia-specific treatment for nicotine dependence, which would combat nicotine’s devastating effects on morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population.
