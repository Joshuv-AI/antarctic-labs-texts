Integrated physiological response by four species of Rhodophyta to submarine groundwater discharge reveals complex patterns among closely-related species
Authors: Veronica L. Gibson, Angelene Dedloff, Lisa J. Miller, Celia M. Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Algal physiological ecology on submarine groundwater discharge (SGD) influenced reefs is likely shaped by intermittent, tidally-driven estuarine conditions that occur with SGD fluxes of fresh-to-brackish groundwater from the subterranean estuary to reef ecosystems. SGD is a common inconspicuous feature worldwide on reefs of basaltic high islands and continental margins. Yet, SGD-driven dynamics of algal physiology are not well understood. To understand how invasive species have physiologically outcompeted native species on many SGD-influenced reefs, physiology in tissue water potential (TWP) regulation, photosynthesis, nitrogen storage, and cellular anatomy were measured across a gradient of SGD-influence, for four Rhodophyte species. Compared with non-SGD conditions, SGD was associated with higher TWP, larger medulla cells with thinner walls, and thinner cortical cell walls for two invasives, Gracilaria salicornia and Acanthophora spicifera, higher photosynthetic rates in G. salicornia, greater nitrogen concentration for A. spicifera and G. salicornia, and increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}δ15N ratios for A. spicifera, G. salicornia, and native Laurencia dendroidea. Distinct physiological strategies were measured for the two invasive species across the gradient of SGD-influence, and for L. dendroidea and Gracilaria perplexa offshore. This study illuminates species-specific physiological response, and how introduced opportunistic species may outcompete native species under conditions of SGD.

Introduction

Diurnal oscillations in salinity and nutrient content occur within transitional water bodies, including submarine groundwater discharge (SGD) influenced nearshore reefs1,2. However, physiological mechanisms for macroalgal survival under oscillating conditions remain unclear. Salinity and nutrient oscillations determine ecosystem function by altering species’ ability to perform vital processes; shaping species distributions, structuring communities, and altering the transfer of energy within systems2. Changing salinity is a source of stress for most species, yet for halotolerant species, salinity oscillations act as a subsidy by allowing them to outcompete intolerant species3. One response to osmotic stress is the regulation of solute concentrations within the plant cells to maintain tissue water potential (TWP; solute concentration-driven osmotic potentials), a physiological response which is pivotal for turgor, photosynthesis, growth, and survival4. Where salinity oscillations are prominent, understanding algal physiological response to oscillations is critical to understanding ecosystem structure, function, and anthropogenic impacts5.

Naturally occurring SGD is an oscillating system that is prolific on nearshore reefs throughout Hawaiʻi6,7, tropical high islands, and continental margins8–10. SGD-influenced reefs are characterized by an environmental gradient of tidally-driven oscillations in water quality, including salinity and nutrient availability1,11–13. SGD likely influences benthic species distributions via species-specific salinity-nutrient thresholds5. The intensity of these oscillations declines with distance from the SGD source, giving way to stable marine conditions outside of the zone of SGD-influence1,11,13. Tidal oscillations cause benthic macroalgae to experience shifting conditions from marine to estuarine water quality, including a change in salinity from 0 to 35 ‰ and nutrient availability from ambient to three orders of magnitude higher5. The magnitude of these shifts varies with daily tidal variations and mixing1,5. Understanding how macroalgae respond physiologically to SGD conditions is necessary to explain the dynamics of SGD-influenced nearshore reef ecology.

Anthropogenic nutrient pollution, groundwater pumping, and alteration of groundwater flow paths alter SGD quantity and quality13,14, which impacts recipient ecosystems13,15,16 and ecosystem processes17–21. This likely alters species distributions and biomass, and contributes to increases in invasive species via nutrient loading5. For SGD adapted organisms, reduction in SGD flow reduces or redistributes habitat, while nutrient pollution may expand habitat for some weedy species, enabling them to outcompete others and cause subsequent losses in diversity5,9,12,16. The expansion of habitat for invasive algae can be linked to SGD-associated harmful algal blooms, which can lead to hypoxia, loss of native species and diversity, and phase shifts from coral to algal dominated systems20,21. However, definitively separating anthropogenic impacts from those of natural estuarine stressors can be challenging3, especially where current understanding of ecosystem functioning in oscillating systems is limited. Further, indirect impacts of SGD on herbivory may occur22. Significant gaps remain in our understanding of macroalgal physiological ecology in oscillating systems, and the causes of algal bloom conditions in SGD-influenced regions.

The magnitude of SGD-driven oscillations makes them key drivers of algal abundance and distribution, reflecting variability in tolerances evolved among species5,23. Few species have been tested for SGD tolerance in three studies, all in Hawaiian ecosystems. Invasive Gracilaria salicornia demonstrates tolerance and native Gracilaria coronopifolia exhibits partial tolerance to SGD-conditions24. Native Ulva lactuca demonstrates tolerance while the invasive Hypnea musciformis is intolerant of SGD conditions25. SGD is associated with positive impacts on growth for G. salicornia and native Bryopsis pennata, and with negative impacts on growth for invasive Acanthophora spicifera and three native species15.

To function under oscillating salinity, nutrient availability, and pH, macroalgae must maintain osmotic regulation, photosynthesis, and nutrient acquisition and allocation, all of which are intrinsically linked through multifunctionality and shared biochemical pathways. For example, in response to salinity-driven osmotic stress, regulation of solute concentrations within the plant cells to maintain TWP is pivotal for turgor, photosynthesis, growth, and survival4.

To better understand macroalgae present in SGD, and for a mechanistic understanding that can inform predictions about future distributions, characterization of the physiology and anatomy of these algae is needed; this has hardly been done in regard to oscillating systems (but see Amato et al.24). On SGD-influenced reefs and within systems where salinity-nutrient oscillations are prominent, understanding algal physiological response to these oscillations is critical to understanding ecosystem structure and functioning, and anthropogenic impacts on both.

While little is known about physiological response to oscillations in salinity, much is known about salinity stress tolerance linked to osmotic stress. Hypo- or hypersaline stress alters morphology and reduces growth, size and photosynthesis for some marine plants4. A biphasic feedback loop characterizes macroalgal response to moderate salinity changes. In the first minutes to hours, rapid changes in turgor pressure occur due to water fluxes via osmosis4,26,27. During this initial response, osmotic adjustment occurs based on the physico-chemical properties of the cell wall membrane complex4,28. In the second osmotic response phase, adjustment of TWP occurs as cellular concentrations of osmotically active solutes are altered to reach a new steady state, this occurs over multiple days in macroalgae4,26,27. In the Rhodophyta, floridoside, the major photosynthate, increases as external salinity increases, which decreases TWP, and prevents water loss via osmosis29. Since salinity changes often happen on time scales shorter than possible for complete TWP adjustment via osmolyte alteration, partial regulation has been proposed as an osmotic response mechanism4. Macroalgae appear to alter osmotic potential in response to hypo-osmotic stress with some combination of the following mechanisms: transfer of osmolytes into polymeric reserve products, inhibition of enzymes involved in osmolyte synthesis, stimulation of degradative pathways, and emergency release of organic osmolytes into the bathing medium4. None of these processes of osmotic response have previously been investigated in Hawai’i’s macroalgal species, nor in SGD-influenced reef systems.

Macroalgal ability to adjust TWP and thereby the osmotic potential gradients between the plant and bathing medium is likely critical to survival, and possibly nutrient acquisition, under salinity-nutrient oscillations. TWP regulation likely plays an important role in determining species distributions under oscillating SGD-conditions. Generally, marine plants maintain a TWP equal to or slightly more negative than the prevailing water potential of the surrounding bathing medium, such that there is little to no net movement of water across the cell membranes via osmosis, except for small potential gradients which could drive passive nutrient uptake (Fig. S.1). Without adaptations such as lowered TWP or adaptations in cell wall thickness, rapid exposure to fresher external salinities would drive water into macroalgal cells via osmosis and can cause cell lysis (Fig. S.2). Physiological ability to regulate TWP is likely characteristic to algal species and tied to their ecological success under conditions of oscillating salinity and nutrients. Direct measurement of macroalgal response in TWP is the first step to examining macroalgal response to oscillating salinity-nutrient regimes in SGD-influenced ecosystems, but has been hindered by a lack of convenient, rapid methodologies for measuring TWP in intact macroalgae. Waiʻalae ‘Iki is characterized by an onshore-offshore, tidally-driven SGD biochemical gradient typical of SGD-influenced nearshore reefs, with onshore conditions of strong oscillations in salinity, nutrients, and temperature and stable marine conditions offshore1,7,33. SGD here has a substantial flow rate of 13,000 m3/day, and an anthropogenically elevated nitrogen flux of 930 µm/day of NO3-, and a wastewater signal of 0.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document}%7. Biomass in the nearshore SGD-influenced region is dominated by two introduced invasive species, Gracilaria salicornia and Acanthophora spicifera5,24. SGD influences macroalgal ecology with species-specific changes in biomass and growth rates, and lowered diversity under onshore SGD conditions at Wai‘alae ‘Iki and other SGD-influenced sites15. Yet, the physiological strategies and anatomical plasticity by which macroalgae species tolerate SGD-driven environmental conditions are not well understood5.

This study focuses on ecologically relevant and relatively understudied species at the study site; two dominant invasives, G. salicornia and A. spicifera, prominent native Laurencia dendroidea, and commonly occurring presumed introduction Gracilaria perplexa. Gracilaria salicornia, a species widespread in warm waters of the Indian and Pacific Oceans34, has been the focus of previous studies which characterized physiology and ecology without TWP17,24 . The most well studied species, A. spicifera, is a cosmopolitan species found in tropical and warm temperate seas, which exhibits physiological tolerance to salinities from 19 to 40 ‰34–36. For L. dendroidea, a cosmopolitan species (regionally as L. majuscula)34,37, studies have characterized impacts of abiotic conditions and herbivory on chemical defenses38–40. Gracilaria perplexa was previously morphologically identified as G. coronopifolia in Hawaiʻi, until molecular analyses revealed the presence of G. perplexa, an Australian species also recorded in Asia41 (see also42). One previous study for this species examined growth regulators43.

Here we measure macroalgal integrated physiological response and water quality parameters across the gradient of SGD influence at Wai‘alae ‘Iki and examine relationships between SGD-influence, physiology, and water quality.

Discussion

On SGD-influenced nearshore reefs of oceanic high islands, tidally driven pulses of SGD expose macroalgae to semi-diurnal oscillations between ambient, oligotrophic marine conditions and estuarine conditions of lower-salinity, lower-temperature, and higher nutrient content5. How macroalgae physiologically tolerates oscillating conditions on SGD-influenced nearshore reefs remains unclear5, yet understanding SGD-influenced ecology is critical to stewardship of biocultural resources5,45,46. Within SGD-influenced ecosystems, macroalgae are important biological indicators and primary producers, yet published studies of macroalgal SGD ecology are limited5,45. Better understanding of these systems is pertinent as anthropogenic impacts of nutrient pollution, groundwater withdrawal, and alteration of coastal hydrology increase with growing human populations, and with climate change impacts of rising sea levels, ocean acidification, and ocean warming45,46. Further necessitating this work is recognition of SGD-influenced nearshore reefs as valued biocultural systems which provide critical habitat for valued species and ecological processes45–47.

In this work, invasives A. spicifera and G. salicornia demonstrate distinct strategies of invasiveness across this gradient of SGD-influence. Low abundance of G. salicornia in marine conditions and dominance in the nearshore SGD-influenced region24 suggests that G. salicornia is a halotolerant species that is likely able to outcompete intolerant species under SGD-conditions. In contrast, A. spicifera is present across the gradient and more abundant in the offshore-marine region15,24, and thus appears tolerant to SGD conditions yet more suited to marine conditions. While the native L. dendroidea and introduced G. perplexa demonstrate successful physiological strategies in offshore marine conditions, more work is needed to understand their absence in the nearshore and their physiology under SGD conditions. This may contribute to the widespread distributions of A. spicifera and G. salicornia and more narrow distribution of G. perplexa worldwide.

These results suggest that important links exist between the physiological parameters examined (TWP, photosynthesis, cell structure, and N storage) and the physiological and ecological parameters which change with SGD influence15,24. The ability to regulate TWP and influence transfer of nutrients across membranes, combined with thinner cell walls and increased cell surface area for rapid transfer of osmolytes, is likely key in driving high nutrient uptake and photosynthesis for G. salicornia under SGD conditions. Integrated physiological response to SGD is species-specific and likely linked to SGD-driven increases in growth rates and biomass of G. salicornia and native species, and decreased growth rates and biomass of A. spicifera and other native species15. This may be the key strategy of invasiveness which allows for high growth rates and branching of G. salicornia in SGD conditions, leading to dominance of nearshore regions, and lowered diversity and absence of other species15,24. Further, these dynamics likely impact the carbonate system, where SGD-driven chemistry and macroalgal biomass have been shown to lead to net dissolution and reduced net community production, compared to net calcification and production under offshore marine conditions7. This and past works highlight the need for more understanding of SGD-driven zonation on nearshore reefs5,7,15,24.

TWP regulation is an important yet understudied physiological trait that is intrinsically linked to nitrogen storage and growth4. This work confirms variation in TWP across an SGD-driven environmental gradient. By sampling during high SGD flow periods this study captured immediate macroalgal responses to SGD conditions, which reflect the initial, passive phase of osmotic response4. However, differences across the gradient of SGD-influence likely also reflect plastic responses to long-term conditions of intermittently estuarine conditions onshore versus stable marine conditions offshore. Partial TWP regulation has been proposed as a salinity tolerance mechanism since macroalgae are often exposed to changing external salinities on shorter time scales than allow for complete osmolyte response4. Some portion of our results may reflect partial TWP regulation under SGD conditions. Continued work will examine TWP regulation in controlled conditions, physiological linkages with other traits, species-specific differences, and ecological linkages. Of particular interest for future work is the role of macroalgal transmembrane channel proteins that transport water and ions across cellular membranes to create potential gradients48 in TWP regulation.

Anatomically, thin, flexible cell walls and subsequently high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in$$\end{document}∈ values increase plant’s sensitivity and decrease response time to changes in osmotic conditions and nutrient availability through osmosis4,27. Thus the thin walls found for A. spicifera and G. salicornia here suggest a plastic response to SGD conditions by these two species. Similarly to the thinner cell walls previously recorded for E. intestinalis under estuarine conditions27, in nearshore-SGD conditions both invasive species exhibited thinner cell walls. Cell size has also been related to salinity stress4. Gracilaria salicornia exhibited smaller medullary cells in SGD-influenced regions, which may aid in osmotic response by providing increased surface area through which osmolytes or ions can be exchanged via rapid transfer. This is contrary to the effects of salinity on cell size recorded in the literature, as larger cells in lowered salinities were found for Enteromorpha intestinalis27, and Porphyra purpurea49. This highlights species-specific anatomical responses to salinity regimes and the need for further work to understand the phylogenetic or evolutionary drivers of salinity tolerance in macroalgae.

This work further highlights the need to better understand relationships between SGD-borne N, nearshore primary producer physiology, and impacts on nearshore ecology. SGD is a known source of nutrients to nearshore ecosystems, yet the ecological impacts of SGD-driven N loading are not well understood11,13,16. Anthropogenically elevated nitrogen in SGD is linked to invasive algal blooms throughout Hawaiʻi17,18, and worldwide19,20,50, with significant negative economic impacts51. Better understanding of the physiological ecology described here will aid in understanding the dynamics of SGD-associated invasive algal blooms.

Future caged deployments (following Amato et al.24) of G. perplexa and L. dendroidea and other species will allow for examination of physiology in the nearshore SGD-influenced region in the absence of competition and herbivory. Their lack of nearshore distribution in itself suggests physiological intolerance towards SGD-influenced nearshore conditions. Intolerance of SGD conditions could also be related to competition with SGD associated species, changes in herbivory, SGD derived chemistry, or other stressors linked to oscillations. However, the possibility that species are excluded from the inshore region by non-SGD driven patterns of herbivory or lower growth rates cannot be ruled out in this study. Still further, laboratory simulation of SGD conditions (following25,32) will delineate species-specific physiological responses to individual and combined SGD-parameters.

We provide evidence of algal physiological response to SGD-driven oscillating conditions, which has implications for understanding ecological functioning on SGD-influenced nearshore reefs and in oscillating systems. This work necessitates better understanding of anthropogenic impacts on SGD and subsequent increased vulnerability of a system to invasive species, and losses of native diversity5. More work is needed to elucidate whether invasive species found on SGD-influenced reefs are particularly effective at invading SGD-influenced systems in general, or if anthropogenically created conditions render SGD-influenced systems particularly vulnerable to invasion. Further, if invasive algal dominance in the SGD-influenced nearshore region is anthropogenically driven, future work will examine if remediation can influence phase shifts back towards more diverse native ecologies.

Conclusions

This primary work demonstrates that TWP regulation occurs in SGD-influenced nearshore reef regions, which has implications for understanding competition and species-specific tolerance to oscillations in SGD and other oscillating systems. TWP regulation likely determines species presence, growth, and abundance in relation to water quality and oscillations therein. An important distinction in this work is the effectiveness of two distinct strategies of invasiveness which allow G. salicornia and A. spicifera to coexist and dominate the benthic cover in the nearshore SGD-influenced reef region.

Physiological response to SGD likely determines the distribution and abundance of species along the gradient of SGD influence. More work is needed to understand if native and non-invasive species are physiologically unable to cope with the SGD conditions found in the SGD-influenced nearshore region, or if they were present in this nearshore region under historically different SGD conditions or in the absence of invasive species. Native species’ physiological responses have historically been successful in SGD-influenced systems45, yet better understanding is needed as to how and why these strategies have become unsuccessful in locations where native species losses have been seen. Understanding the physiology of native SGD-associated species is critical to restoring SGD conditions required for native ecologies5. Likewise, to understand and mitigate anthropogenic and climate change impacts on nearshore ecosystems and native macroalgae species we must understand the ecological strategies of native species on SGD-influenced nearshore reefs. Further, understanding invasive macroalgal strategies under SGD conditions is critical to preventing invasions and ecosystem degradation, and for remediation of current blooms. This knowledge is critical to preventing the proliferation of invasive species and losses of valued SGD ecologies and biocultural systems45–47.

Methods

Study site

Wai‘alae ʻIki Beach (Fig. 1), within Maunalua Bay on the south shore of urban Oʻahu is a coastal site with SGD-driven onshore to offshore gradients of nutrients and salinity, similar to other SGD-influenced coastal Hawaiian reefs1,6,7,15,16,18,33. There are multiple SGD sources; large SGD springs occur 1–10 m from shore in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 1$$\end{document}<1 m of water (Fig. S.6), and abundant diffuse SGD seeps occur along the shoreline (Fig. S.7)1,7,33,52. An expansive reef flat extends to at least 370 m from shore, creating a zone of similarly shallow depths (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 2$$\end{document}<2 m) and reduced wave action24.

This study builds on previous work24, which used the same study site and experimental zones to deploy caged specimens of G. coronopifolia and G. salicornia, measure the physical conditions across Wai‘alae ‘Iki’s gradient of SGD-influence, monitor changes in chemistry across the site, and measure algal growth, photosynthesis, and branch development in these two Gracilaria species24. For this study, we mirrored the three previous study zones24 along the onshore-offshore gradient of SGD on the Wai‘alae ‘Iki reef flat (Fig. 1), with Zone A centered on the most prominent SGD spring in this region 15 m from shore, (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$21.27565^\circ$$\end{document}21.27565∘ N, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-157.7624990^\circ$$\end{document}-157.7624990∘ W). Many smaller seeps occur along the shoreline adjacent to Zone A1,5. Zone B is centered offshore of Zone A, 75 m from shore (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$21.27517^\circ$$\end{document}21.27517∘ N, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-157.762304^\circ$$\end{document}-157.762304∘ W). Zone B is characterized as a zone of intermediate SGD-influence, with some small springs throughout the region. Zone C (centered at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$21.27298^\circ$$\end{document}21.27298∘ N, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-157.751507^\circ$$\end{document}-157.751507∘ W) near the seaward edge of the Wai‘alae ‘Iki reef region, represents a control location outside of direct SGD-influence. Each zone consists of a rectangle which extends 100 m parallel to the shore and 10 m offshore. The previous study also characterized the benthic community across these zones, and found G. salicornia to be dominant in onshore, SGD-influenced Zones A and B compared to the offshore reef in Zone C, where greater diversity and more native species were found24. The intermediate zone of SGD-influence in Zone B contained the highest proportion of G. salicornia and the lowest diversity of the three regions24.

Wai‘alae ‘Iki has also been characterized by previous work which examined the effects of SGD on reef ecosystem metabolism and found a spatial progression from net photosynthesis and dissolution in the inshore SGD-influenced region to net photosynthesis and calcification with increasing oceanic conditions33. Another recent study examined the effects of SGD spatiotemporal dynamics on benthic community structure at Wai‘alae ‘Iki and found that zoanthid soft corals and turf algae are associated with high tidally-driven SGD variability, while A. spicifera, calcifying macroalgae, Pterocladiella sp., Lyngbya sp., and Avrainvillea lacerata are associated with less SGD variability15. Acanthophora spicifera and G. salicornia were also found to be the most common taxa at Wai‘alae ‘Iki53. Here, we build on previous studies by examining the algal physiology for four species, three of which are previously unexamined at this site, and by measuring TWP, which has not been examined for Hawaiian plant material nor across gradients of SGD-influence.

Statistical analyses

Linear mixed models from the lme4 package in R54,55 were used to explore relationships between algal physiology measurements and zone, between algae physiology measurements and salinity, and between water quality characteristics and zone. Linear mixed models are linear models (linear terms with a normally distributed response) that include a random effects term56. Mixed models are necessary in this study in order to handle the violation of the assumption of independently distributed data by conducting multiple measurements on the same organism, spatial blocking of the study site, multiple observations at each of many locations and timepoints, and multiple observations on each of many species56. Linear mixed models avoid pseudoreplication and are able to model multiple sources of variation occurring on different temporal and spatial scales56. Analysis of variance for all linear mixed models was conducted using type 2 ANOVAs from the Anova function within the car package in R54,57.

Since microscopy specimens were collected on a single day, the microscopy results were analyzed with simple linear regression models by using the lm function from the stats package in R54. Analysis of variance for all linear models was conducted using type 2 ANOVAs from the anova function within the stats package in R54.

Species selection

The species pool examined includes four prominent species at Wai‘alae ‘Iki5. The invasive species, G. salicornia and A. spicifera dominate the SGD-influenced zone at Wai‘alae ‘Iki5. These invasive species may be especially well adapted to SGD conditions physiologically, leading to dominance of benthic cover in SGD dominated systems. The native L. dendroidea and presumed introduction G. perplexa are common in Zones B and C, yet absent in the nearshore SGD-influenced Zone A of Wai‘alae ‘Iki. Of the four species examined, two belong to family Gracilariaceae, order Gracilariales; the introduced invasive G. salicornia and the presumed introduction G. perplexa. The remaining two species belong to family Rhodomelaceae, order Ceramiales, and include the introduced invasive Acanthophora spicifera and native Laurencia dendroidea. We predicted that physiological and anatomical traits differ between plants found nearest the SGD seep and plants growing in offshore marine conditions, and that these patterns vary among species.

Gracilaria salicornia and A. spicifera, found abundantly at Zones A, B, and C, were sampled in both 2019 and 2021. In 2021, two species were added: native Laurencia dendroidea and presumed introduction Gracilaria perplexa, an Australian endemic that was recently identified in Hawai‘i and separated from native G. coronopifolia through molecular analysis41,42. Laurencia dendroidea was found within Zones B and C, while G. perplexa was only found in Zone C. See Fig. 2 for study species.

Algal physiology and site characterization

To examine algal physiology across the zone of SGD-influence, 300 random points were generated within zones A, B, and C, where algal collection and water chemistry measurements were taken. Over the course of two field seasons during the summers of 2019 and 2021, sampling was conducted during low tides coinciding with the full and new moons. Sampling occurred each day during the sampling periods of 07/30/19–8/3/19, 5/26/21–5/29/21, 6/23/21–6/27/21, 7/9/21–7/11/21, and 7/21/21–7/25/21, for a total of 22 sampling days. Sampling days targeted times of low tide between 8 a.m. and 1 p.m. where the tide was below 0.15 m. Sampling spanned 3–5 consecutive days in which tides below 0.15 m occurred before 12 p.m.. Times of low tide were targeted in order to collect measurements during maximum times of SGD-influence, while day-times between 8 a.m. and 1 p.m. were targeted as ideal times for accurate photosynthetic measurements58. We sampled on as many days as possible that met this criteria and the field team was available. As many sampling points as possible were sampled during the sampling window. Due to daily changes in the duration of the sampling window, numbers of plants measured daily varied from 8 to 30 plants. A total of 155 points were sampled: 50 in Zone A, 54 in Zone B, and 51 in Zone C. Sampling was not conducted during 2020 due to beach closures during Covid shutdowns.

Collection points were randomly generated within each zone using R54. GPS coordinates for each collection point were loaded into a Garmin GPSMAP 78 GPS. A sampling collection team used the GPS to travel to each sampling point, where a visual search of the 1 m diameter area surrounding the point was conducted to confirm the presence or absence of the four target species. Where species were not found within the 1 m radius the radius was expanded to 5 m. If species were not located within twenty minutes within the allotted radius, they were recorded as not present.

Within Zone C, G. salicornia was not always present. In order to collect data for best comparison between A. spicifera and G. salicornia, collection points were adjusted when G. salicornia was not present at the point. To do so, the sampling team would move directly inland in a straight line until G. salicornia was found (in most cases this was within 10 m of the original point). In this case, a new GPS point was recorded for the samples. G. salicornia was targeted because A. spicifera was nearly always present, while L. dendroidea and G. perplexa were not always present within 20 m. Within Zones B and A points were not moved because G. salicornia and A. spicifera were nearly always present, while L. dendroidea was rare and G. perplexa was not present at all.

Once species presence and collection point were confirmed, measurements of water temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen were taken in situ using a multi-parameter sonde (YSI, model V24 6600). Anatomy

For anatomical analyses, three replicate plants of each species were collected from thirteen randomized points within Zone A and ten randomized points within Zone C on March 2, 2022. Where the species was not found within 5 m of the point within 20 min, the species was recorded as not present, and was not collected for that point. Plant collections included 9 A. spicifera plants from Zone A and 27 from Zone C, 33 G. salicornia plants from Zone A and 27 from Zone C, 21 G. perplexa plants from Zone C, and 17 L. dendroidea plants from Zone C.

Collected plants were transported to the lab where whole plants were photographed using a Fujifilm finepix XP80 digital camera and sectioned for microscopy. For each plant, cross sections were made of three plant parts: meristem, thallus, and holdfast. Meristems were classified as the growing tip of a primary thallus axis, not more than 10 mm from the tip. Thallus sections were taken 2 cm from the growing tip of the thallus, selecting for no visually reproductive, branching, or otherwise specialized tissues. Holdfast sections were taken at the basal most portion of the thallus, not more than 10 mm from the base of the thallus.

Thallus parts from sun-acclimated branches were selected for sectioning, and bleached or otherwise differentiated tissues were avoided. For each section, plant material was treated with 1% HCl and then dyed with aniline blue stain, and each slide made permanent using a sequence of 20–50% Karo and fresh seawater over subsequent days following61. Each slide was photographed using an Olympus BX51 microscope imaging system including a calibrated ocular micrometer. We selected anatomical traits based on past studies which demonstrated changes in cell wall thickness and cell size under lowered salinity27,49, and that cell wall rigidity plays a key role in initial osmotic response4,28. For each plant part we measured medullary cell diameter, medullary cell wall thickness, cortical cell diameter, and cortical cell wall thickness using Image J software62 (See Fig. 8 for cell types of all species, Fig. 8a. for medullary cell diameter measurement, Fig. S.4 for medullary cell wall measurement, and Fig. S.5 for cortical cell measurements).
