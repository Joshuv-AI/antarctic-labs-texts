The UL24 gene affects duck plague virus replication, and its deletion attenuates virulence, enabling use as a vaccine
Authors: Huijun Cao, Peilin Ruan, Hongying Chen, Mingshu Wang, Linjiang Yang, Anchun Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Duck plague virus (DPV) is the causative agent of duck plague (DP), which poses a significant threat to production safety in the duck industry. Little is known about the UL24 protein (pUL24) encoded by the DPV UL24 gene. In this study, pUL24, when transfected into duck embryo fibroblasts, localized to the nucleus, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence techniques. We discovered that UL24 contains two nuclear localization signals (NLS), with the first also serving as a nucleolar localization signal (NoLS). To further analyse the function of pUL24, two viral strains were constructed: a UL24 deletion strain and a UL24 NLS motif deletion strain. These deletion strains affected the replication of DPV to varying degrees. Notably, the virulence of the UL24 deletion strain (DPV-UL24null) was significantly reduced in ducks, indicating good safety, genetic stability, and immunogenicity. Ducks immunized with DPV-UL24null have a 100 % protective effect against challenge with a DPV Chinese virulent strain (DPV-CHv); thus this strain has the potential to be used as an attenuated vaccine. In summary, these findings provide new insights into the function and pathogenic mechanisms of the UL24 gene and present novel concepts for the development of a duck plague vaccine.

Introduction

Duck plague (DP), also known as duck viral enteritis (DVE), is a septicaemic infectious disease characterized by a high infection rate and significant morbidity (LIU et al., 2017; DHAMA et al., 2017). DP infects domestic ducks and wild ducks of all ages. Diseased ducks exhibit high fever that lasts for 3–5 days and symptoms such as loss of appetite, listlessness, and green watery diarrhoea. The pathogen of DP is Duck plague virus (DPV). The rapid replication and proliferation of DPV in tissues leads to pathological changes in various organs, manifesting as gastrointestinal mucosal haemorrhage, splenomegaly and congestion, thymic atrophy, etc. (LIU et al., 2017; DHAMA et al., 2017; KONG et al., 2022; LI et al., 2016). Vaccines are important means to control DP, but the existing inactivated vaccines do not have the ability to proliferate in ducks, the duration of stimulation to the body is short, and the neutralizing antibody titre is low (HUANG and JIA R, 2014; QI et al., 2009). The attenuated vaccine for ducklings simulates natural infection, but the genetic background of the attenuated DP vaccine on the market is unclear. After use, distinguishing between immunized ducks and naturally infected ducks is impossible, thus increasing the difficulty of DPV purification. Therefore, the development of a clearer and more effective vaccine to eradicate DPV has become an urgent task. The attenuated gene deletion vaccine has become an ideal candidate for DP vaccines because of its good safety and immunogenicity.

DPV is a member of the Herpesvirales family and the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae (LEFKOWITZ et al., 2018). The viral particles are composed of an envelope, a cortex, a capsid and a double-stranded DNA genome from outside to inside. The DPV genome is covalently connected by a unique long region (UL) and a unique short region (US) (WU et al., 2012; ZHANG et al., 2020). The UL24 homologous gene is usually referred to as the UL24 gene in most herpes viruses, but different names exist for some viruses (DAVISON, 2002; KNIZEWSKI et al., 2006; BUSSEY et al., 2018; KASEM et al., 2010; LIN et al., 2013). The UL24 homologous protein encoded by the UL24 gene is present in all members of the Herpesviridae family, and the members of the protein family are located mainly in the nucleus and nucleolus (LYMBEROPOULOS and PEARSON, 2007; BERTRAND and PEARSON, 2008; GAO and JIA R, 2017; ITO et al., 2005; SANDER et al., 2008; CAI et al., 2017). Because the replication of herpesvirus depends on the function of the host nucleus, this localization pattern is essential for the replication of the virus. In addition, the UL24 protein family plays a role in the innate immune escape of viruses (CHEN et al., 2021; XU et al., 2017; WANG et al., 2020), and other processes (BERTRAND et al., 2010; LYMBEROPOULOS et al., 2011; ORBAUM-HAREL et al., 2024; SANABRIA-SOLANO et al., 2016; NASCIMENTO and PARKHOUSE, 2007). DPV is an immunosuppressive virus characterized by extensive cytotropism, replication and proliferation in the host through immune escape. DPV UL24 is an immunosuppressive gene that can evade the immune surveillance of the host by inhibiting the immune factor interferon-β (IFN-β) (GAO and LIU R, 2022). UL24 protein (pUL24) targets the expression of multiple genes in the innate immune signalling pathway to promote DPV replication (RUAN et al., 2024). In theory, the DPV UL24 gene deletion strain can induce a faster immune response in ducks and has the potential to be used as an attenuated vaccine with immunogenicity.

In summary, the UL24 protein family has important biological importance in viral infection, but reports on the function of pUL24 are limited. Some characteristics of pUL24 have been studied in the laboratory. This study focused on the localization and immune efficacy of pUL24. First, pUL24 entered the nucleus using its own nuclear localization signal (NLS). NLS1 and NLS2 are necessary for nuclear translocation of heterologous proteins, and UL24 NLS1 functions as a nucleolar localization signal (NoLS). To further explore the characteristics of pUL24, two bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) platforms were used for DPV gene editing to construct and rescue DPV UL24 deletion viruses (DPV-UL24null) and UL24 NLS deletion viruses (DPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 and DPV-UL24ΔNLS2). Because the DPV UL24 gene is an immunosuppressive gene, its deletion may help to optimize the characteristics of the strain as a vaccine strain. Therefore, this study further evaluated the safety, immunogenicity, and genetic stability of the DPV-UL24 deletion strain in ducklings to clarify its potential as a candidate vaccine strain for DP. These findings highlight the UL24 gene as a potential target for vaccine development. Given the high conservation of the herpesvirus UL24 gene, this study offers valuable guidance for the prevention and control of DPV and other herpesviruses.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the Ethics and Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Protocol Permit Number: 20240485).

Test animals, cells, viruses and vaccines

DPV-negative ducklings for animal experiments and duck embryos for preparing duck embryo fibroblasts (DEFs) were purchased from Ya'an Farm of Sichuan Agricultural University. The experimental animal protocol was approved by the Ethics and Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University and carried out in accordance with the Chinese version of the Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching. DEFs were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10 % newborn bovine serum (NBS, Gibco, Grand Island, USA). Commercial duck plague vaccine (CVCCAV1222) was purchased from Harbin Pharmaceutical Group Biological. This is a duck plague chick embryonized vaccine. The virus content in one dose is not less than 103 50 % egg infective dose (EID50), which is used to prevent DP. Immunization began 3‒4 days after intramuscular injection of one dose.

The DPV Chinese virulent strain DPV-CHv was used (GenBank accession number: NO.JQ647509.1) (GUO et al., 2009); DPV-CHv50 was passaged in DEFs for 50 generations. The first infectious clone constructed with a BAC as the vector and EGFP as the reporter gene was pDPV-BAC-CHv50 (the rescued parent virus was named DPV-BAC-CHv50). The second infectious clone constructed by inserting DPV-CHv50 into the BAC vector was pDPV-CHv50 (the resulting parent virus was named DPV-CHv50). They were isolated, identified, constructed and preserved by the Institute of Animal Medicine and Immunology, School of Animal Medicine, Sichuan Agricultural University.

Sequence analysis of the DPV UL24 protein

The protein subcellular localization tool NLS Mapper (https://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp/cgi-bin/NLS_Mapper_form.cgi) was used to analyse and predict the UL24 coding sequence, and it revealed two potential NLSs with scores of more than 3 points in the sequence, namely, 44RRVAGSKRRTDANTSGSVARRLKA67 and 241IPTKGKRRKAKC252. These sequences were labelled UL24 NLS1 and UL24 NLS2, respectively. The online tool NoD (NOD: NucleOlar localization sequence Detector) could be used to identify NoLS candidate signal regions with a prediction score of 0.8. The scores for UL24 NLS1 and NLS2 were approximately 0.7 and 0.8, respectively.

Construction of the recombinant plasmid

The DH5α strain and eukaryotic expression plasmids pCAGGS, pEGFP-C2-GST, pCAGGS-3Flag, pCAGGS-3HA, pCAGGS-UL24-3Flag and pEP-Kan-S were preserved and provided by the Institute of Animal Medicine and Immunology, Animal Medical College, Sichuan Agricultural University. In addition, the DPV UL24, DPV UL24 NLS1 and DPV UL24 NLS2 fragments were inserted into the pEGFP-C2-GST vector to construct the pEGFP-C2-UL24-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24NLS1-GST and pEGFP-C2-UL24NLS2-GST plasmids used in this study. The B23 fragment was inserted into the pCAGGS-3HA vector to construct the pCAGGS-B23-3HA plasmid used in this study. DPV UL24ΔNLS1, DPV UL24ΔNLS2 and DPV UL24ΔNLS1+2 gene fragments were inserted into the pEGFP-C2-GST vector to construct the pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS1-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS2-GST and pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS1+2-GST plasmids used in this study. Immunofluorescence analysis

DEFs in 12-well plates were transfected with the pCAGGS-UL24-3Flag plasmid. According to the manufacturer's instructions, one μg of each plasmid DNA was mixed with two μL of transfection reagent. The plasmid was transfected into DEFs using Hieff Trans Liposomal Transfection Reagent (40802ES03, Yeasen, China). The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at 24 h and 36 h after transfection and then permeabilized with 0.25 % Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. The primary antibody used was anti-mouse-DDDDDDK-tag mAb (MBL, China), which was incubated overnight at 4°C. The secondary antibody used was Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor TM 568 (Thermo Fisher, USA), which was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole (DAPI, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and mounted with 70 % glycerol-PBS. Similarly, DEFs in 12-well plates were transfected with pEGFP-C2-UL24-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24NLS1-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24NLS2-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS1-GST, pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS2-GST or pEGFP-C2-UL24ΔNLS1+2-GST. The cells were fixed 24 h after transfection, and the subsequent steps were the same as above (without incubation with primary and secondary antibodies). In addition, EGFP-C2-UL24NLS1-GST and pCAGGS-B23-3HA were cotransfected into DEFs. The primary antibody used was a mouse anti-HA tag monoclonal antibody (MBL, China), and the secondary antibody used was the same as above. Images were observed and captured by using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) (RUAN et al., 2024).

Construction of recombinant virus

The first infectious clone was pDPV-BAC-CHv50. The BAC element and the reporter gene EGFP were selectively inserted into the UL23 region of DPV in pDPV-BAC. To ensure stable genome replication, the minimal fertility factor replicon (Mini-F) was retained in the BAC vector backbone to obtain the molecularly cloned parental virus (DPV-BAC-CHv50) (WU et al., 2017; BRUNE et al., 2000). The tyrosine kinase (TK) protein encoded by the UL23 gene is highly conserved in most herpesviruses (DONG et al., 2017); it is a nonessential gene for virus proliferation. This site in the DPV genome allows for the stable insertion and expression of heterologous genes, and its absence does not affect the proliferation of the parent virus in DEFs. However, the DPV genome is not detected in infected ducks, and animal tests cannot be performed. After analysis in our laboratory, this lack of detection was attributed to damage to the UL23 gene (WU et al., 2022). Therefore, the UL23 gene was restored, and the Mini-F cassette was removed to construct a second infectious clone, pDPV-CHv50. The parental virus (DPV-CHv50) rescued via this platform can be used for cell and animal experiments to more accurately define the pathogenicity of the DPV gene in ducks. Both infectious clones were genetically modified in E. coli GS1783 to achieve DPV gene deletion and heterologous gene insertion.

E. coli GS1783 was used to construct pDPV-UL24null, pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 and pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 infectious clones via two-step RED homologous recombination (TISCHER et al., 2010). The RED recombination system can perform homologous recombination using short homologous sequences. A Kan selection marker cassette (amplified from pEP-Kan-S) was introduced to replace the target region, and kanamycin was used for resistance screening. The I-SceI enzyme was then used to recognize restriction sites within the Kan sequence, ultimately completing the deletion of the target region. In short, the UL24 gene was replaced by a Kan cassette, which contains homologous arms, and the Kan cassette was removed by cutting the I-SceI site. A second recombination subsequently occurred in bacteria through 40 bp sequence replication. Through this method, the target gene region was successfully deleted. In addition, pDPV-UL24null Rev, pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 Rev and pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 Rev infectious clones were generated using the same principle. The primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Rescue and identification of the recombinant virus

The plasmid DNA of the recombinant pDPV-UL24null, pDPV-UL24null Rev, pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1, pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 Rev, pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 and pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 Rev strains was extracted using a Plasmid Medium Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Hieff Trans Liposomal Transfection Reagent (40802ES03, Yeasen, China) was used to transfect DEFs, and the green fluorescence in DEFs was observed within 10 days until many green fluorescent spots were generated for virus collection. The obtained virus was identified by PCR and sequencing and restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP).

The multistep growth curve of viruses

DEFs in 12-well plates were infected with three groups of viruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 (ZHOU et al., 2023). The first group included DPV-UL24null, DPV-UL24null Rev and DPV-CHv50; the second group included DPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1, DPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 Rev and DPV-BAC-CHv50; and the third group included DPV-UL24ΔNLS2, DPV-UL24ΔNLS2 Rev and DPV-CHv50. The total cell mixture of three parallel samples was subsequently collected at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h after infection, and less than 1 mL was supplemented with DMEM. The samples were subsequently frozen and thawed three times at −80°C. The virus titre in the sample was measured to evaluate the virus growth level, and the 50 % tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) was determined using DEFs in a 96-well plate.

Virus adsorption, invasion and replication

Adsorption. DEFs were prepared in 12-well plates. The cells were placed in a refrigerator at 4°C for 2 h (to permit binding but prevent viral internalization). Then, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and infected with 0.01 MOI virus (virus infection was the same as above). The infected cells were cultured in a refrigerator at 4°C for 2 h. After washing with precooled PBS 5 times, the cell samples were collected, and the virus copy number was determined (ZHOU et al., 2023).

Invasion. In accordance with the above adsorption procedure, the cells were infected with the virus for 2 h (to permit binding but prevent viral internalization), after which the supernatant was discarded and replaced with cell maintenance medium. The cells were incubated in a cell incubator at 37°C for 1 h (to allow entry of the virus) and then washed with precooled PBS 5 times, after which the cell samples were collected, and the virus copy number was determined (ZHOU et al., 2023).

Replication. Three groups of virus infections were performed at an MOI of 0.01. At 6 h after virus infection of DEFs, the supernatant was discarded and replaced with cell maintenance medium. The cells were collected at 12 and 24 h, and the virus copy number was measured.

The effect of pUL24 on DPV cell-to-cell spread in host cells

The cell-to-cell transmission of the recombinant virus was determined by plaque size. DEFs in 6-well plates were infected with viruses at MOIs of 10−3 or 10−4 (DPV-UL24null, DPV-UL24null Rev and DPV-CHv50) and incubated at 37°C for 2 h, after which 2 % methylcellulose (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to cover the DEFs. On the 5th day of infection, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min, washed with PBS and incubated with 0.5 % crystal violet (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS, the staining fluid was removed, and the plaques were observed and counted.

Morbidity and mortality of ducks infected with DPV-UL24null and DPV-UL24ΔNLS2 strains

In this study, 14-day-old ducklings were infected with the virus, and the injection volume was 1 mL. The injection method involved intramuscular injection of 500 μL each into the left and right legs. Ten ducklings each group (sixty total ducklings) were injected with 106 TCID50/mL of one of five viruses (DPV-UL24null, DPV-UL24ΔNLS2, DPV-UL24null Rev, DPV-UL24ΔNLS2 Rev and DPV-CHv50) or DMEM. The rectal temperature, and survival rate of the ducklings in the different groups were recorded daily.

Safety evaluation of ducks immunized with the DPV-UL24null strain

Sixty-four ducklings were randomly divided into four groups and injected with the DPV-UL24null strain (104‒106 TCID50/mL) or the vaccine (one dose per duck). First, the rectal temperature, and survival rate of the ducklings in the different groups were recorded daily. Second, the ducklings in the different slaughter groups were killed on the 5th and 9th days after infection with the virus, and a portion of each organ sample was collected to assess the distribution of each virus in the ducks by fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR).

Genetic stability of the DPV-UL24null strain

In vivo genetic stability. Twenty ducklings were randomly divided into 2 groups and injected with the DPV-UL24null strain (106 TCID50/mL) or DMEM. The clinical symptoms of each group were observed. On the 5th day, 3 ducks were randomly selected for necropsy and observation of pathological changes. The clinical symptoms of the remaining ducks were observed until they reached 28 days of age. In addition, 1 g of liver from infected ducks was collected to prepare a homogenate filtrate, which was intramuscularly injected into the next generation of ducklings. This method was used for 5 consecutive passages in ducks. The virus content in the liver of the first to fifth generations of the virus was detected, and DNA was extracted for PCR identification.

In vitro genetic stability. DEFs were infected with the DPV-UL24null strain, and cell samples (the first generation of virus) were collected after the appearance of cytopathic effects. In accordance with this method, DEFs were continuously passaged 20 times. The 5th, 10th, 15th and 20th generations were selected to determine the virus titre, and DNA was extracted for PCR identification.

Evaluation of the immune effect on ducks immunized with the DPV-UL24null strain

To study the immunogenicity of the DPV-UL24null strain and its protective effect against strong virus attacks, eighty ducklings were injected with the DPV-UL24null strain (104‒106 TCID50/mL), the vaccine or DMEM. After 14 days of immunization, the ducklings were challenged with lethal virulent DPV-CHv at a dose of 100 times the median lethal dose (LD50), and the rectal temperature and survival rate of the ducklings in the different observation groups were recorded daily. The low-dose DPV-UL24null strain (104 TCID50/mL) within a certain range has good immunogenicity. Considering vaccine loss and immune efficiency, the follow-up test focused on the DPV-UL24null strain (104 TCID50/mL). The ducklings were killed on the 6th and 10th days of virulent attack. Tissue samples were collected to assess the distribution of each virus in the ducks by fluorescence qPCR, and microscopic pathological sections were generated for pathological observation.

The clearance of DPV-CHv in ducks immunized with DPV-UL24null strain

The primers UL24-F and UL24-R were designed on the basis of the location of the UL24 deletion gene, and qPCR was used to detect changes in the viral load in the organs of ducks in the DPV-UL24null, vaccine and MOCK groups on the 6th and 10th days of DPV-CHv attack. For three groups of ducks, DPV-UL24null, vaccine and MOCK, cloacal secretions were collected using cotton swabs every other day after DPV-CHv exposure and placed in PBS until the end of the 9th day. The viral load in the cloacal swabs of ducks infected with DPV-CHv was detected, and the differences in viral load among the different groups were compared.

The effect of pUL24 on the DPV-induced immune response

Detection of the effect of pUL24 on the DPV-induced immune response in vitro. DEFs were infected with 0.01 MOI DPV-UL24null or DPV-CHv50. After 48 h of infection, RNA samples were collected to detect the immune signalling molecules cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), stimulator of interferon genes (STING), TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), and interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) and IFN-β, interleukin 6 (IL-6), myxovirus resistance (Mx) and 2′ −5′ -oligoadenylate synthetase like (OASL).

Detection of the effect of the UL24 protein on the DPV-induced immune response in vivo. Twenty-seven ducklings were randomly divided into 3 groups and injected with DPV-UL24null (102 TCID50/mL), DPV-CHv50 (102 TCID50/mL) or DMEM. The ducklings were sacrificed at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h after infection, and whole blood, thymus and spleen were collected to detect the mRNA levels of IFN-β, OASL and Mx.

Changes in cytokines in ducks immunized with the DPV-UL24null strain

On the 6th day after challenge with the DPV-UL24null strain (104 TCID50/mL), 0.1 g of the thymus and spleen were extracted from the vaccine or MOCK groups, and the mRNA levels of chemokine ligand 21 (CCL-21) and interleukin 4 (IL-4) were detected using qPCR.

Detection of neutralizing antibody levels in ducks immunized with the DPV-UL24null strain

Serum samples from ducks injected with DPV-UL24null (104 TCID50/mL), the vaccine or MOCK were collected at different time points, and the neutralizing antibody levels in immunized ducks were determined by the fixed virus dilution serum method. The neutralization antibody detection method was as follows: The serum to be tested was placed in a water bath at 56°C for 30 min to inactivate the complement. Then, a 2-fold serial dilution (2−1–2−7) was generated by mixing an equal volume of 200 TCID50/100 μL DPV-CHv50 virus solution, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The mixture was subsequently transferred to a 96-well plate, and a normal cell control and a virus titre control (10−1–102 TCID50/100 μL) were established. The cells were cultured in a 37°C incubator, and the cytopathic effect was observed daily. The 50 % serum neutralization titre was calculated by the Reed‒Muench method.

Histopathology

First, the collected tissues were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and then gradient dehydrated with 75 % alcohol, 85 % alcohol, 90 % alcohol, 95 % alcohol, anhydrous ethanol and alcohol benzene, followed by xylene transparency and finally paraffin embedding and slicing. The sections were sequentially dewaxed in xylene, anhydrous ethanol and 75 % alcohol. After rinsing, the sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin. Finally, the slices were dehydrated in anhydrous ethanol, cleared in xylene, sealed and examined by optical microscopy.

Construction and identification of UL24 and UL24 NLS deletion strains

The upstream and downstream regions of the DPV UL24 gene are the DPV UL23 and UL25 genes, respectively, and there is a 64-base overlap with the UL23 gene. The UL23 and UL25 gene promoter motifs (41‒89 AA and 281‒339 AA, respectively) on the UL24 gene were predicted by an online biological website (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/Promoter-2.0/), and the construction of UL24 gene-related infectious clones without affecting upstream and downstream genes was limited. The infectious clones of pDPV-UL24null, pDPV-UL24null Rev, pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 and pDPV-UL24ΔNLS2 Rev were constructed on the basis of the pDPV-CHv50 platform (UL24null represents the UL24 null protein with a deletion of 90‒280 AA), and cell and animal experiments were performed on the basis of the four rescued viruses. Because UL24 NLS1 (44‒67 AA) is in the UL23 gene prediction promoter motif, the NLS1 deletion mutant (pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1) and the revertant (pDPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 Rev) were constructed on pDPV-BAC-CHv50. The two viruses rescued on this basis can only be used for cell experiments. A schematic diagram of the UL24 gene upstream and downstream and genetic modifications is shown in Fig. 2A–C. First, the six infectious clones constructed in this study were sequenced using UL24 identification primers to amplify fragments containing the UL24 region. Owing to the significant viral modification resulting from UL24 gene deletion, the correctness of pDPV-UL24null and pDPV-UL24null Rev was verified through RFLP. RFLP analysis confirmed that the pDPV-UL24null and pDPV-UL24null Rev infectious clones were correctly constructed (Fig. 2D). Finally, the correctly sequenced plasmids were transfected into DEFs, and the green fluorescent spots were observed for 5–9 days. The viruses were collected and passaged (Fig. 3). Viral lifecycle of UL24 and UL24 NLS deletion strains

There was no significant difference in the number of DPV genome copies of each virus after infection of DEFs at 0.01 MOI for 2 h at 4°C, indicating that the UL24 and UL24 NLS proteins did not affect the adsorption of DPV (Fig. 4B). There was no significant difference in the number of DPV genome copies of each virus after the three groups of virus-infected DEFs at 0.01 MOI were adsorbed at 4°C for 2 h and cultured at 37°C for 1 h, indicating that the UL24 and UL24 NLS proteins did not affect DPV invasion (Fig. 4C). The genome copy numbers of the three groups of virus-infected DEFs at 0.01 MOI were lower than those of the parent virus and the recovered virus at 24 h after infection. The copy numbers of the parental virus were approximately 101.32, 101.18, and 100.52 higher than those of DPV-UL24null, DPV-BAC-UL24ΔNLS1 and DPV-UL24ΔNLS2, respectively, indicating that the deletion of the UL24 and UL24 NLS proteins reduces the replication ability of DPV in vitro (Fig. 4D).

The effect of UL24 on the efficiency of DPV cell-to-cell spread in host cells

The effect of the UL24 deletion virus on cell‒cell transmission was determined by plaque size. DPV-UL24null, DPV-UL24null Rev and DPV-CHv50 DEFs were infected, and the plaque size was observed 5–7 days after infection. Fig. 4E shows that, compared with the parental and revertant viruses, the DPV-UL24null strain formed smaller plaques in DEFs, indicating that the pUL24 influences viral cell-to-cell spread.

To further assess whether the DPV-UL24null strain has good safety in ducks, 104–106 TCID50 DPV-UL24null or a vaccine was used to immunize ducks. The effect of the UL24 protein on the DPV-induced immune response

The UL24 protein affects the DPV immune response in vitro. The UL24 protein affects the DPV immune response in vivo. The blood and immune organs of ducklings were collected at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h after DPV-UL24null and DPV-CHv50 infection to detect the expression of IFN-β and its downstream antiviral factors. Fig. 8B and C show that DPV-UL24null significantly increased the levels of IFN-β, Mx and OASL expression in ducks, indicating that the deletion of the UL24 protein enhanced the immune response of the host after DPV infection. In addition, the expression of the immune signalling molecules cGAS, STING, TBK1 and IRF7 in the blood of ducklings in each group was detected 72 h after infection. The results showed that DPV induced higher expression of immune signalling molecules after UL24 deletion.

Detection of cytokine changes and antibody levels in ducks immunized with the DPV-UL24null strain

Cytokines (interleukins, interferons, and chemokines) are involved in conservative innate immune responses and can effectively eliminate DPV infection. Changes in cytokines in the thymus and spleen of immunized ducks and nonimmunized ducks on the 6th day of virulent challenge were detected. Fig. 8D shows that the changes in the cytokines CCL-21 and IL-4 in immunized ducks were significantly greater than those in nonimmunized ducks. The neutralizing antibodies in the serum of immunized ducks and nonimmunized ducks were detected on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 35, 45, 60, 90 and 120. Fig. 8E shows that neutralizing antibodies were detected in the serum of immunized ducks beginning on day 7. The serum of DPV-UL24null-immunized and vaccine-immunized ducks peaked between 35 and 45 days after immunization. The highest average neutralizing titres of the two strains were 25.13 and 25.2, respectively, and decreased to 23.83 and 23.76 on day 60 after immunization, indicating that the DPV-UL24null strain-immunized ducks can produce neutralizing antibodies comparable to those of the commercial DP vaccine.

Discussion

The herpesvirus replication cycle generally includes adsorption, invasion, uncoating, replication, assembly and release (OWEN et al., 2015). The transcription of the viral genome, DNA replication, capsid assembly and packaging of linear double-stranded DNA occur in the nucleus (HEMING et al., 2017; MERCER et al., 2010). The entry and exit of proteins into and out of the nucleus require the use of specific NLSs and nuclear export signals (NESs) (FLORES and SEGER, 2013; LU et al., 2021; OKA and YONEDA, 2018). The UL24 protein family in herpesviruses comprises mainly nuclear proteins but is not limited to the nucleus. The herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) UL24 protein is associated with the nucleus and cytoplasm of HSV-1-infected cells, and its distribution and transport in cells are regulated by appropriate targeting signals (BERTRAND and PEARSON, 2008). DPV is a member of the α herpesvirus subfamily. Studies have shown that pUL24 is localized in the nucleus 12–24 h after the transfection of UL24-tagged plasmids in chicken fibroblasts (GAO and JIA R, 2017). However, the mechanism by which pUL24 enters the nucleus has not been reported. This study revealed that UL24 has two nuclear localization signals (NLS1 and NLS2) and that NLS1 is a NoLS (ZHAO and TIKOO, 2020). The influence of the NLSs of UL24 and UL24 on viral replication was confirmed via infection of DEFs. The analysis of pUL24 localization and the discovery of NoLS suggest that it may play important biological functions in the nucleus and nucleolus region. The nucleolus is involved in cell cycle regulation and the innate immune response (SIRRI et al., 2008; BELIN et al., 2010). However, whether the NoLS motif in the pUL24 is involved in mediating the escape of DPV from the host innate immune response still needs to be further verified and explored through subsequent experiments.

Existing studies have shown that the HSV-1 UL24 protein affects the replication of HSV-1 in cells. UL24-deficient viruses exhibit a decreased viral titre and a syncytial plaque phenotype (JACOBSON et al., 1998; LEIVA-TORRES et al., 2010). Deletion of the UL24 gene reduces the replication and transmission capacities of pseudorabies virus (PRV) in cells, but the UL24-deficient virus still maintains a certain degree of lethality in mice (YE et al., 2019). In other herpesviruses, such as Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), a termination mutation in UL24 is associated with significant reductions in viral DNA replication and the number of viral particles in offspring (ORBAUM-HAREL et al., 2024). This study demonstrated that the deletion of pUL24 significantly affected viral replication both in vitro and in vivo. DPV-UL24null exhibited reduced morbidity and mortality. Organ autopsies of ducklings infected with the DPV-UL24null strain did not show duck plague lesions. These results indicated that the UL24 deletion strain was not pathogenic to ducklings. This study revealed that the role of the conserved UL24 protein family during viral replication and pathogenesis does not seem to be exactly the same across viruses, but the deletion of the UL24 gene weakens the virulence of the virus, suggesting that specific inactivation of the UL24 gene could be considered a target for antiviral therapy for DPV or other herpesviruses. The DPV-UL24null strain did not have a complete deletion of the full-length UL24 gene; however, the deleted region encompassed the highly conserved PD-(D/E) XK motif (KNIZEWSKI et al., 2006). This motif has been proposed to confer potential endonuclease activity in various herpesviruses. Accordingly, a systematic structural characterization of UL24 family members is essential to elucidate their molecular functions and to assess their feasibility as antiviral drug targets.

UL24 proteins play key roles in antagonizing the host antiviral response. HSV-1 and PRV UL24 proteins inhibit IFN promoter activation by suppressing the cGAS-STING pathway and the NF-κB pathway (XU et al., 2017; WANG et al., 2020; LIU et al., 2021). The mRNA levels of IFN-β, Myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA) and Interferon-stimulated gene 20 kDa protein (ISG20) were elevated in cells infected with the UL24-ineffective virus (CHEN et al., 2021; CHEN et al., 2021). Further studies have demonstrated that UL24 is an inactivating gene in PRV strains with quadruple gene deletion. This recombinant virus effectively combines safety, immunogenicity and protective efficacy (LI et al., 2024). DPV UL24, a virulence gene and immunosuppressive gene, inhibits the type I immune response in innate immunity. This study revealed for the first time that immunization of ducklings with the DPV-UL24null strain has excellent safety and effectively stimulated the production of specific antibodies in ducklings to achieve protective effects similar to those of the live attenuated vaccine. The DPV-UL24null strain can be produced at a lower cost than the traditional chicken embryo passage-attenuated live vaccine. In addition, compared with traditional live attenuated vaccines, DPV-UL24null has a clear deletion region, which is more conducive to the prevention and eradication of duck plague. After infection with the DPV-UL24null strain, the mRNA levels of the cytokines IFN-β, Mx and OASL in DEFs and ducklings increased, further demonstrating the significant role of UL24 in host immunity. Given the stable genetic characteristics of DPV-UL24null, DPV-UL24null can also be used as a potential recombinant live vaccine vector. We anticipate that a multivalent combined vaccine based on this vector could be developed if the antigen proteins of avian influenza virus, Newcastle disease virus and other avian pathogens can be selectively expressed in this strain through genetic engineering.

In summary, this study presents the first report of the mechanism through which pUL24 is transported into the nucleus and indicates that the DPV-UL24null strain offers a good immune protection effect as a candidate vaccine for DP. This study lays a foundation for further analysis of UL24 protein functions in herpesvirus and provides new insights relevant to the application of DPV in the development of avian multivalent live vector vaccines. It also provides an important theoretical basis for the prevention and eradication of DP.
