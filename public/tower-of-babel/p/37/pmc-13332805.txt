Characterization of phylogroups of Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. quasipneumoniae and K. variicola) harbouring blaOXA-232, with special reference to ceftazidime/avibactam/aztreonam synergy and their genetic profile
Authors: Tuhina Banerjee, Kajal Mishra, Pue Rakshit, Ghanshyam Yadav, Ashok Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Klebsiella pneumoniae is a ‘critical priority’ pathogen responsible for several infections. The emerging evidence of carbapenem resistance in the phylogroups of K. pneumoniae (KpI), namely K. quasipneumoniae (KpII) and K. variicola (KpIII), has drawn attention.ObjectivesGiven the paucity of data, this study was conducted to characterize phylogroups harbouring blaOXA-232 with special reference to ceftazidime/avibactam/aztreonam (CAZ/AVI/ATM) synergy.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was performed on 150 previously characterized K. pneumoniae isolates (KpI, KpII, KpIII). Susceptibility against meropenem, ceftazidime/avibactam, aztreonam, polymyxin B, colistin and cefiderocol was determined using broth microdilution. CAZ/AVI/ATM synergy was assessed using gradient diffusion and disc diffusion methods and confirmed by time–kill assay. Detection of blaOXA-232 and class A, B and D carbapenemase genes was performed by PCR. Clonality of the ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant isolates was studied by ERIC-PCR. Susceptibility was compared with the genetic profile by chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsSeventy-two isolates (48%) carried blaOXA-232 of which 87.5% were carbapenem resistant; susceptibility to cefiderocol, colistin and polymyxin B was 80.95% (51), 82.5% (52) and 84.12% (53), respectively. Difference in cefiderocol resistance (P = 0.03) and colistin susceptibility (P = 0.03) was significant among phylogroups. CAZ/AVI/ATM synergy was observed in 92.3% (24) of the ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant isolates, with blaOXA-232 alone in 12 (50%), and blaOXA-232 in combination with blaNDM ± blaOXA-48 or other MBLs in 12 (50%). Multiple clones were detected.ConclusionsSynergy to the CAZ/AVI/ATM combination in the blaOXA-232-positive, ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae phylogroups was noted (92.3%). Cefiderocol and polymyxins were other therapeutic options.

Introduction

One of the most significant ‘critical priority’ category pathogens causing a range of infections, such as bloodstream infections, pneumonia, surgical site infections and urinary tract infections, is Klebsiella pneumoniae.1 The MDR shown by K. pneumoniae has been one of the biggest challenges in tackling infections caused by these bacteria. The recent emergence of phylogroups of K. pneumoniae, namely K. pneumoniae ssp. pneumoniae (KpI), K. quasipneumoniae ssp. quasipneumoniae (KpII), and K. variicola ssp. variicola (KpIII), causing human infections has been alarming.2–4 Moreover, data on the genetic content of these phylogroups have been extremely scarce.

In the context of carbapenem resistance, several carbapenemases in K. pneumoniae mediate the resistance.5 In the Indian scenario, MBLs and blaOXA-48 carbapenemases in K. pneumoniae have been very significant in mediating resistance in carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP).6 More than 50 blaOXA-48 variants have been identified. Among others, blaOXA-232, despite having weaker carbapenemase activity, has been drawing attention, due to increasing resistance shown by the CRKP harbouring the gene. The blaOXA-232 gene is endemic in China, India, South Korea and Thailand.7 While there have been several reports of CRKP carrying blaOXA-232,8,9 we recently characterized such isolates revealing that selective carbapenem pressure, often due to indiscriminate carbapenem usage, could lead to treatment failure due to overexpression of this gene.10

Among the newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor (BL/BLI) combinations, the majority of which are ineffective in the Indian context, ceftazidime/avibactam has been seen to have no activity against the MBLs. On the other hand, aztreonam/avibactam is active against MBL-producing organisms, as aztreonam alone is stable against MBLs but not against isolates co-producing MBLs and other β-lactamases.6 Against an emerging background of blaOXA-232, this study was performed to characterize the phylogroups of CRKP carrying blaOXA-232, with reference to their susceptibility to ceftazidime (CAZ)/avibactam (AVI)/aztreonam (ATM) synergy based on their genetic profile.

Materials and methods

Study site and ethics

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Microbiology and the associated 2500-bedded tertiary care hospital in Varanasi, India. The study was approved by the Institute’s Ethical Committee (letter no. IMS/IEC/2024/7333).

Bacterial strains

The study included 150 isolates of previously identified and characterized K. pneumoniae, comprising KpI (K. pneumoniae), 93 isolates (62%); KpII (K. quasipneumoniae), 36 isolates (24%); and KpIII (K. variicola), 21 isolates (14%). All the isolates were previously classified on the basis of the phylogeny groups.4 Briefly, biochemically confirmed isolates of K. pneumoniae were further tested by semi-multiplex PCR targeting the following chromosomal class A β-lactamase genes: blaSHV for KpI; blaOKP for KpII; and blaLEN for KpIII, and their flanking deoR gene. For validation of the phylogroups by PCR, Klebsiella ATCC 700603 (K. quasipneumoniae) and K. variicola ATCC 31488 were used as controls. The details of the classification have been described previously.4 These isolates were collected over 1 year (January to December 2023) from various clinical samples, including urine (66 samples), pus (50 samples) and blood (34 samples), from patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs), neonatal ICUs (NICUs) and general wards, with clinical suspicion of systemic infections.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Broth microdilution method

Determination of MICs against meropenem [resistant (R) ≥ 4 mg/L], ceftazidime/avibactam (R ≥ 16/4 mg/L), aztreonam (R ≥ 16 mg/L), polymyxin B (R ≥ 4 mg/L) (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals Pvt. Ltd, India) and cefiderocol (R ≥ 16 mg/L; MedChem Express Pvt Ltd, Allianz BioInnova, India) was performed using the broth microdilution (BMD) method.11 For cefiderocol, iron-depleted CAMHB (ID-CAMHB; Remel Inc., KS, USA, for research purpose only) was used. Colistin resistance was screened by the colistin broth disc elution (CBDE) method.12 The breakpoint values for all the antibiotics were interpreted according to the CLSI, 2023.13 Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC® 27853 and Escherichia coli ATCC® 25922 were used as quality control strains.

Susceptibility testing of aztreonam with ceftazidime/avibactam (synergy testing)

Synergy testing was done using gradient strips (Etest strip) and disc diffusion as per standards.14 In this method, a ceftazidime/avibactam Etest strip (Himedia Laboratories Pvt Ltd, India) containing a fixed concentration of avibactam (4 mg/L) was placed on the surface of the inoculated culture agar. Subsequently, an aztreonam disc (30 μg; Himedia Laboratories Pvt Ltd, India) was placed at 15 mm from the centre of the ceftazidime/avibactam Etest strip (Figure 1). The aztreonam disc was placed at the susceptibility breakpoint of ceftazidime/avibactam (8/4 mg/L in this case) for overnight incubation. The zone of inhibition on both sides of the aztreonam disc was measured for aztreonam alone as well as for ceftazidime/avibactam with aztreonam. The zone diameter was measured and compared with the aztreonam interpretive criteria (a zone diameter of 21 mm was considered susceptible). Restoration of aztreonam zone diameter to the susceptible range was considered as synergy. Also, a qualitative approach was taken, where an inverse D-shaped zone was considered as synergy.15

Synergy testing of CAZ/AVI/ATM combination using Etest strip and disc diffusion method.

Time–kill assay for detection of CAZ/AVI/ATM synergy

A time–kill assay was also performed for confirmation of synergy in all the isolates that were screened by the above method. Briefly, a single colony was grown in 2 mL Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) by overnight incubation in a shaker incubator (150 rpm) at 37°C. Following incubation, 20 µL of the growth medium was inoculated into 2 mL of fresh MHB and incubated for 1 h. The concentration of the growth medium was then adjusted to approximately 5 × 106 cfu/mL. Antibiotics at the following concentrations were added. Aztreonam was tested at concentrations of 16 and 32 mg/L, keeping the ceftazidime/avibactam concentration constant at 16/4 mg/L in each tube. Growth medium from the tubes was taken at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 24 h, serially diluted, and inoculated on nutrient agar. Following incubation at 37°C, bacterial colonies were counted after 24 h. Synergy was defined as a ≥2 log10 decrease in cfu/mL between the combination and aztreonam alone after 24 h.16

Molecular characterization of carbapenemase genes

All the isolates were tested for the following carbapenemase resistance–encoding genes. Multiplex PCR was performed for the amplification of class A (blaSME, blaNMC, blaGES, blaKPC), class B (blaIMP, blaVIM, blaNDM) and class D (blaOXA-48) carbapenemase genes using primers and reaction conditions as described elsewhere.17,18 Conventional PCR was performed for detection of blaOXA-232-carrying isolates. The reaction conditions for the PCR assay were 95°C for 2 min, 95°C for 20 s, 52°C for 40 s, 72°C for 1 min and 72°C for 5 min. The PCR was carried out for 35 cycles.10 A few representative amplicons of blaOXA-48 (10) and blaOXA-232 (5) were sequenced (Sanger sequencing; Barcode Biosciences, India) and a basic local alignment search tool for nucleotide (blastn) was performed in GenBank (NCBI) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) to determine the variants.

Molecular typing of isolates by ERIC-PCR

Clonality of the ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant isolates was determined by ERIC-PCR, using the universal primers as described elsewhere.19 A dendrogram was constructed by using NTSYS-pc version 2.0 software, based on the visualization of the amplified products.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test and chi-squared test were used to compare the resistance and susceptibility rates among the phylogroups KpI, KpII and KpIII for the tested antibiotics. Medcalc software (version 22.032) was used to analyse the data.

On analysing the genetic profile of the CRKP isolates, it was seen that 39 isolates (61.9%) harboured the blaOXA-232 gene alone (among the other β-lactamase genes tested; Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online), while 24 isolates co-harboured the blaOXA-232 gene with either MBLs or/and with the blaOXA-48 gene. Among the 26 ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant CRKP, 24 isolates that showed synergy, had blaOXA-232 gene in 12 (50%) isolates, and blaOXA-232 gene in combination with blaNDM gene, with or without blaOXA-48 or other MBLs in 12 (50%) of the isolates, as shown in Table 4. None of the isolates harboured a combination of the blaOXA-232 and blaOXA-48 genes only, other than in combination with the blaNDM gene.

ERIC-PCR for the ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant isolates identified approximately 10 different clonal groups (Figure S2), with two clones consisting of 10 and 5 isolates, respectively, comprising different sample types and isolates from different locations of the hospital.

Discussion

Our study revealed the therapeutic options in blaOXA-232-positive CRKP isolates with respect to their genetic profile based on the presence of carbapenemase-encoding genes. Additionally, the study also revealed the different phylogeny groups of K. pneumoniae, data for which are extremely scanty, especially against a background of emerging drug-resistant K. variicola. To the best of our knowledge, the study characterized K. variicola and K. quasipneumoniae for the first time as blaOXA-232-harbouring CRKP isolates.

Among the several carbapenemases, blaOXA-232 is endemic in China, India, South Korea and Thailand.7 Although having a weaker carbapenemase activity, K. pneumoniae isolates with blaOXA-232 are often carbapenem resistant, as seen in this study. This finding is in concordance with a previous study, wherein the sole presence of blaOXA-232 in K. pneumoniae could bring high-level resistance to carbapenems.10 Though there have been no prevalence-based surveillance data for blaOXA-232 in India, there have been several reports of blaOXA-232-harbouring K. pneumoniae isolates causing extensive outbreaks.8,9,20 The emergence of blaOXA-232 has been quite steady and its detection limited. The study is significant in that it provides first-hand knowledge about the widespread presence of blaOXA-232 in these isolates.

The study showed that cefiderocol and polymyxins could be potential therapeutic options against these CRKP isolates. Cefiderocol is a novel siderophore-based antibiotic recently marketed in India.21 However, the cost of treatment with this antibiotic in resource-limited contexts remains a hurdle. The efficacy of cefiderocol against MBL-harbouring Indian isolates has been limited with respect to CRKP, a fact also seen in this study.22,23 Polymyxins are the last therapeutic options for these isolates and are regarded as ‘reserve drugs’.6 Interestingly, susceptibility to ceftazidime/avibactam was seen in 58.73%, and among the ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant isolates, synergy with aztreonam combination was seen in 92.3% of the isolates. In this context, the Antimicrobial Resistance Research and Surveillance Network (AMRSN) data (2022) revealed ceftazidime/avibactam susceptibility of only 20% to 30% over a 5 year period, while among the CRKP, nearly 99% resistance has been reported.24 A lower rate of synergy has been reported (62.5%) in one study, with a similar genetic profile of K. pneumoniae isolates (MBLs).25 Greater synergy, however, has been seen in blaKPC- and ESBL-harbouring isolates of CRKP.26 Therefore, it was clearly revealed that in these CRKP isolates, synergy of CAZ/AVI/ATM was still a valid option for therapy among the other antibiotics studied.

Among the carbapenemases detected in this study, synergy in the presence of blaOXA-232 alone, among the studied carbapenemase genes, and those co-harbouring multiple carbapenemase-encoding genes (50%) was comparable. This is of interest in view of the scarcity of data on the effects of the presence of blaOXA-232 genes on CAZ/AVI/ATM synergy. The study also highlighted that lack of synergy could be seen in the blaOXA-232-positive K. quasipneumoniae CRKP isolates, emerging pathogens that have been recently reported from human infections.4 This study revealed the occurrence of blaOXA-232 in these phylogeny groups and its effects on the therapeutic options.

The strength of this study was that it revealed the probable therapeutic options among the emerging blaOXA-232-positive CRKP phylogroups with special emphasis on their susceptibility to the CAZ/AVI/ATM combination. However, the study was not without limitations. We could not study the detailed genetic context of these isolates with respect to the presence of other carbapenemase-encoding genes, which could have better revealed the actual genetic environment of these isolates. Further, STs of these isolates could not be detected due to resource limitations, though we studied the clonal relatedness of the isolates. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, these data are the first to reveal the therapeutic options in CRKP phylogeny groups harbouring class B and D carbapenemases.

The study showed 92.3% synergy to the CAZ/AVI/ATM combination in the blaOXA-232-positive ceftazidime/avibactam-resistant CRKP phylogroups, either alone or in combination with other class A and D carbapenemase genes. Newer antibiotics like cefiderocol and reserve drugs like polymyxins were other therapeutic options along with CAZ/AVI/ATM in these isolates.
