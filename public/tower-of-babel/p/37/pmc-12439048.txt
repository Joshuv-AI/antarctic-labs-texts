Inguinal hernia repair under crisis: a retrospective cohort study of surgical outcomes across the COVID-19 era in China
Authors: Dhierin Roman Jagdewsing, Wally Elijah, Ahmed A. Mohammedzeyn, Omane V. Kwakye, Yachin Singh, N M Motachim Mahmud, R. A. Geehari Mihinsa De Alwis, Masoumeh Rashidi, Hania Moharam, Fariya Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has affected healthcare systems worldwide, altering clinical practices and surgical outcomes. However, its specific impact on inguinal hernia repair (IHR) has not been extensively studied. This research investigates the effects of the pandemic on surgical outcomes and the evolution of surgical techniques during the COVID-19 era. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on surgical site infections (SSIs), secondary infections, postsurgical pain, edema, and the shift in surgical techniques in IHR patients.MethodsThis study is a retrospective cohort study involving 1,067 patients who underwent elective unilateral IHR from 2018 to 2024 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University, who were categorized into three groups based on the time of surgery: pre=pandemic (n = 239), pandemic (n = 592), and post-pandemic (n = 236).Clinical variables included surgical techniques, SSI classification, postsurgical pain, edema, hospitalization duration, and comorbid conditions.ResultsSignificant decrease in superficial and organ/space SSIs during the pandemic, particularly with laparoscopic surgery (odds ratio [OR], 0.13; p = 0.002). Post-pandemic, there was a significant reduction in mild, moderate, and severe pain (OR, 0.085, 0.127, and 0.029; all p < 0.001). Additionally, there was a marked increase in the use of laparoscopic surgery, from 9.6% pre-pandemic to 77.1% post-pandemic (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe post-pandemic group was associated with improved surgical outcomes, including reduced SSI rates, postsurgical pain, and shorter hospital stays, likely due to the increased use of laparoscopic surgery. These findings underscore the importance of adapting surgical approaches in response to crisis-driven changes.

INTRODUCTION

Inguinal hernia is a common condition characterized by the protrusion of intra-abdominal contents through weakened areas in the abdominal wall, often resulting in groin swelling and discomfort [1–3]. It is estimated that more than 20 million inguinal hernia surgeries are performed worldwide each year [4]. Risk factors include age, male sex, chronic cough, and increased intra-abdominal pressure [5–9]. Surgical repair is the only definitive treatment, performed either through open repair or laparoscopic techniques [10–12]. Laparoscopic repair, although requiring general anesthesia, has been associated with less postoperative pain, shorter recovery, and less infection risk compared to open surgery [13–19]. During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, healthcare systems adopted new safety protocols that led many hospitals to favor laparoscopic techniques to reduce hospital stays, minimize complications, and limit exposure risk [20–22]. However, it remained unclear whether these protocol-driven changes would result in improved surgical outcomes, especially given the ongoing strain on hospital resources and clinical services during the pandemic. It was therefore important to conduct this research to evaluate whether the increased use of laparoscopic surgery during and after the pandemic leads to better postoperative outcomes. This study investigates the impact of the COVID-19 era on inguinal hernia repair (IHR) outcomes across three time periods (pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic) with a focus on surgical site infections (SSIs), postoperative pain, edema, and hospital stay.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary care center and included patients who underwent IHR surgery between January 2018 and August 2024. A total of 1,480 admitted patients were screened for eligibility. Among these, 186 patients who underwent emergency surgeries were excluded, leaving 1,294 cases. An additional 227 patients were subsequently removed due to incomplete information, resulting in a final study population of 1,067 patients. These patients were categorized into three groups based on the timing of their surgery: 239 patients in the pre-pandemic phase, 592 during the pandemic, and 236 in the post-pandemic phase. The study period was divided into pre-pandemic (January 2018 to December 2019), pandemic (March 2020 to May 2023), and post-pandemic (May 2023 to August 2024) phases to enable an assessment of trends in surgical treatment and outcomes over time.

Patients undergoing emergency procedures or those with incomplete or missing records and bilateral inguinal hernias were excluded. Only verified medical records were included in the final analysis. All operations were performed by a consistent team of three experienced general surgeons, each with more than 10 years of experience in both open and laparoscopic hernia repair. One of the surgeons served in a senior leadership role, and the surgical team was supported by trainees and assistants. The choice of surgical technique was determined according to patient factors, team availability, and institutional clinical protocols. To minimize variability, standardized surgical procedures were applied throughout the study period.

Patients were followed for three months postoperatively. This follow-up duration was selected because data collection ended in November 2024, and therefore, a longer follow-up was not possible for those who underwent surgery after August 2024 due to incomplete data beyond this time point.

The primary independent variable of interest was the pandemic phase. Postoperative pain was classified according to the numeric rating scale, with scores of 1 to 3 considered mild, 4 to 6 moderate, and 7 to 10 severe. Postoperative edema was evaluated based on clinical notes and was classified as mild when limited to localized swelling, moderate when associated with some discomfort or limitation of mobility, and severe when there was marked swelling accompanied by pain or functional impairment requiring intervention. Body mass index (BMI) is categorized as underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). Clinical comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiac conditions (including ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, arrhythmias, and valvular heart disease), and lung conditions (such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, or other chronic respiratory diseases). Physical activity levels were classified as sedentary (primarily sitting or lying with minimal physical movement), light (occasional walking or household tasks), moderate (regular physical tasks or exercise 3–5 days per week), or heavy (physically demanding labor or daily vigorous exercise). Secondary infections documented during hospitalization were categorized as urinary tract infections (UTIs), pneumonia, or other infections (skin and soft tissue infections, bloodstream infections, gastrointestinal infections).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study groups. The chi-square tests were applied for comparisons of categorical variables, while the Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for nonparametric continuous data. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the effects of pandemic phase and surgical approach on postoperative outcomes. A priori power analysis was not performed because the study included all eligible cases available during the study period, providing a sufficiently large sample size for meaningful comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, which were performed using IBM SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

A total of 1,067 patients who underwent IHR were included in the analysis, comprising 239 in the pre-pandemic period, 592 during the pandemic, and 236 in the post-pandemic period (Table 1). There were no significant differences in sex distribution, age, BMI categories, or hernia type across groups. However, hypertension and lung conditions were significantly less prevalent in the post-pandemic group (p = 0.004 and p = 0.047, respectively). Physical activity levels changed substantially over time, with a greater proportion of patients reporting moderate activity in the pandemic and post-pandemic periods (p < 0.001). During these same periods, there was also a marked increase in the use of laparoscopic techniques and general anesthesia (p < 0.001 for both). Specifically, the proportion of laparoscopic procedures rose from 9.6% in the pre-pandemic period to 77.1% in the post-pandemic period, and the use of general anesthesia increased from 40.2% to 81.4% (Table 1). Surgical outcomes also improved over time (Table 2): median hospitalization days decreased from 5 to 2 (p < 0.001), and postsurgical pain severity shifted significantly, with severe pain dropping from 14.2% pre-pandemic to 1.7% post-pandemic (p < 0.001). SSI rates also declined significantly, particularly for superficial infections (p < 0.001). Multinomial logistic regression confirmed that laparoscopic surgery and the pandemic period were independently associated with lower odds of superficial SSI (odds ratio [OR], 0.13; p = 0.002) (Table 3). Similarly, the risk of secondary infections, such as UTIs and pneumonia, decreased significantly in both the pandemic and post-pandemic periods, with laparoscopic surgery also contributing to this reduction (Table 4). Edema severity decreased substantially post-pandemic; the odds of severe edema were significantly lower in post-pandemic and laparoscopic groups (OR, 0.02 and 0.08; both p < 0.001) (Table 5). Pain outcomes demonstrated a comparable trend, with markedly lower odds of moderate and severe pain in the post-pandemic group and in those receiving laparoscopic surgery (Table 6). Overall, the shift toward minimally invasive techniques during and after the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with significantly improved short-term surgical outcomes.

DISCUSSION

Our findings show that laparoscopic surgery significantly improved outcomes, including reductions in SSI, edema, and postoperative pain. This approach became increasingly common during and after the pandemic. The main reasons for this shift include institutional strategies aimed at minimizing hospital stays, infection control priorities, and surgeon preferences. While the pandemic accelerated the adoption of minimally invasive techniques, the improved outcomes observed in this study were primarily related to the use of these techniques. In addition to these technical benefits, institutional changes such as enhanced preoperative screening, stricter sterilization protocols, modified patient flow, and earlier discharge likely contributed to the overall reduction in postoperative complications.

Regarding SSI, multinomial logistic regression showed significantly lower odds of superficial SSI during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.11–0.53; p < 0.001). This finding is consistent with a World Journal of Surgery report that described a reduction in SSI rates during the pandemic, likely related to stricter infection control measures [23].

Laparoscopic surgery was also associated with a reduced risk of superficial SSI compared to open surgery (OR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.04–0.47; p = 0.002). In contrast, deep or organ/space SSI rates did not differ significantly across periods or surgical approaches. These results are in line with previous reports, including studies in Surgical Endoscopy and JAMA Network Open, showing that laparoscopic hernia repair is associated with lower wound infection and complication rates compared to open procedures [24–26].

For secondary infections such as UTIs and pneumonia, we found substantial reductions during and after the pandemic. Compared with the pre-pandemic period, the odds of UTI were significantly lower during the pandemic (OR, 0.10; p = 0.004) and post-pandemic (OR, 0.16; p < 0.001). Pneumonia incidence also decreased during these phases (OR, 0.17 [p < 0.001] and OR, 0.05 [p = 0.006], respectively). Laparoscopic surgery further reduced the risk of UTIs and other secondary infections compared with open surgery. These results support previous findings from BMC Infectious Diseases that infection control measures contributed to lower hospital-acquired infection rates during the pandemic [27,28].

Postoperative edema also improved significantly after the pandemic. Compared with the pre-pandemic period, the odds of mild, moderate, and severe edema were markedly reduced in the post-pandemic group (OR, 0.09 [p < 0.001]; OR, 0.05 [p < 0.001]; and OR, 0.02 [p < 0.001], respectively). Laparoscopic surgery was consistently associated with less edema across all severity categories. This reduction may be explained by the smaller incisions and reduced tissue trauma inherent to minimally invasive surgery, as well as improved perioperative protocols introduced during the pandemic [29,30].

In terms of pain and hospital stay, both factors improved markedly over time. The post-pandemic period was associated with significantly lower odds of moderate and severe postoperative pain, and laparoscopic surgery also provided a protective effect across all pain categories. Shorter hospital stays were related to both the adoption of minimally invasive approaches and modified discharge protocols introduced during the pandemic, including early mobilization, expedited discharge planning, and close outpatient follow-up. These changes aimed to minimize patient exposure and optimize hospital bed availability.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may limit the generalizability of these findings and may introduce selection and information bias. Second, although several potential confounders were adjusted for, unmeasured factors such as comorbidities or institutional differences across pandemic phases may have influenced the results. Emergency hernia repairs were excluded to ensure a more homogeneous elective surgery population, but this also limits the scope of the study. Finally, because this is an observational study, causal relationships cannot be established. Prospective multicenter studies will be necessary to confirm these findings and explore additional factors affecting outcomes.

In conclusion, this study examined the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on surgical outcomes, explicitly focusing on SSI, secondary infections, edema, and postoperative pain following IHRs. The findings suggested that both the pandemic and post-pandemic periods were associated with a reduction in the incidence of SSIs, edema, and pain compared to the pre-pandemic period, particularly evident in the post-pandemic group. Additionally, laparoscopic surgery consistently demonstrated a lower risk of complications, including SSIs, edema, and pain, compared to open surgery. Collectively, these results highlight the crucial role of infection control measures, the advantages of minimally invasive surgical techniques, and the enhancement of patient outcomes. Further studies are warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms driving these observed trends and validate these findings in larger multicenter cohorts.
