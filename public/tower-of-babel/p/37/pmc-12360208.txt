Novel ADAR2 variants in children with seizures, intellectual disability, and motor delay have reduced RNA editing
Authors: Qiupei Du, Anna Cherian, Raymond J. Louie, Giulia Barcia, Natasha L. Rudy, Rima Nabbout, Eugénie Sarda, Maelle Charpié, Wesley G. Patterson, Liam P. Keegan, Mary A. O'Connell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ADARB1 gene encodes the adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 2 (ADAR2) RNA editing enzyme, which edits the GRIA2 transcript Q/R editing site with almost 100% efficiency in the nervous system. The edited GRIA2R transcript encodes the GLUA2 R subunit isoform of tetrameric α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, which is essential to prevent seizures associated with aberrantly elevated AMPA receptor cation permeability. Rare biallelic variants in ADARB1 cause severe infant and childhood seizures and developmental delays in seven cases we previously described. Here, we report two new homozygous ADARB1 variants and study ADAR2 variant editing activities at the GRIA2 Q/R site and other editing sites in cell cultures. One new variant in the second double-stranded RNA binding domain (dsRBD II) retains up to 60% editing activity, whereas another, in the deaminase domain, eliminates RNA editing activity. Reduced GRIA2 Q/R site editing increases AMPA receptor permeability by upregulating the expression of the GLUA2 Q isoform and reducing overall GLUA2 subunit levels, resulting in AMPA receptors that lack GLUA2 and are calcium-permeable. Because failure to edit the GRIA2 Q/R site leads to failure of intron 11 splicing, we also examined the effects of ADAR2 variants on the splicing of a mouse Gria2-based reporter and concluded that ADAR2 variants affect splicing only through their effects on RNA editing activity. To expand the number of variants in ADARB1, some variants reported in ClinVar have also been analyzed by in silico methods to predict which are likely to be most deleterious and associated with seizures in patients.

INTRODUCTION

Enzymatic modification of RNA is very widespread and present in both coding and noncoding RNAs (Cappannini et al. 2024). The deamination of adenosine to inosine (A–I) by the adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR) family of enzymes is one of the most prevalent types of RNA modification and occurs on both coding and noncoding RNAs (for review, see Sinigaglia et al. 2019). This modification is termed RNA editing and can alter the coding potential of transcripts by modifying adenosines within double-stranded (ds) RNA, without the need for a strict consensus sequence. In mammals, there are three family members: ADAR1 and ADAR2, which are enzymatically active, and ADAR3, which is inactive but is a dsRNA binding protein expressed in the brain.

The ADAR gene encodes the ADAR1 enzyme, which is mainly associated with promiscuous editing of many adenosines in long dsRNAs. ADAR1 is ubiquitously expressed and plays a crucial role in innate immunity as it helps the cell to distinguish self from nonself dsRNA (for review, see Quin et al. 2021). Variants in the human ADAR gene can cause Aicardi–Goutières syndrome, an interferonopathy presenting with high levels of Type 1 interferon in children, which can result in brain calcifications and death (Rice et al. 2012) or in striatal neurodegeneration and dystonia in another set of patients (Livingston et al. 2014).

ADAR2 is encoded by the ADARB1 gene and is expressed in the vascular and nervous system (Tan et al. 2017). ADAR2 is responsible for editing the GRIA2 transcript encoding the glutamate receptor 2 (GLUR2) at the Q/R site, where a glutamate codon is converted into an arginine codon (Sommer et al. 1991). RNA editing at this position is normally ∼100% efficient. The arginine in the GLUR2 subunit is located in the ion channel pore of tetrameric α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors and prevents calcium from entering the neuron. The presence of glutamate at the Q/R site causes an influx of calcium and neuronal cell death (Sommer et al. 1991). AMPA receptors lacking the GLUR2 subunit are permeable to calcium, and reduced expression or editing of GLUR2 leads to increased calcium entry and excitotoxicity (for review, see Isaac et al. 2007). In a genetic mouse model, the Adarb1 mice die within 3 weeks of birth due to seizures. However, these mice are completely rescued by introducing the edited version of Gria2R whereby the arginine is gnomically encoded (Higuchi et al. 2000). Therefore, in a mouse model, the Q/R site in Gria2 transcript is the most important editing event catalyzed by Adar2.

It has previously been reported that the human GRIA2 p.(Gln607Glu) variant affecting the codon at the Q/R editing site changes a cytosine to guanosine, 5′ of the adenosine that is edited by ADAR2 so that it undergoes only 10% editing (Salpietro et al. 2019). This patient suffered from seizures as well as a global developmental delay and severe intellectual disabilities. Seven patients in total with variants in ADARB1 were also described (Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021). These variants in ADARB1 are very rare, as any significant decrease in RNA editing is likely fatal. These patients with variants had symptoms such as severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with global developmental delay and severe intellectual disabilities, similar to the patient with the GRIA2 p.(Gln607Glu) variant. Many of the variants in ADARB1 display a decrease in RNA editing activity, so it is likely that there is a decrease in editing at the Q/R site in GRIA2 underlies their symptoms.

In this study, we investigate two novel variants in human ADARB1 encoding ADAR2: p.(Arg244Gly) and p.(Thr375Arg) variants. These patients have symptoms similar to the other patients with variants in ADARB1. Both of the amino acids that are replaced in these novel variants and are highly conserved in ADAR2 and ADAR3. The p.(Arg244Gly) variant is in the dsRNA binding domain (dsRBD II) of ADAR2, whereas p.(Thr375Arg) is located within the catalytic deaminase domain. RNA editing activity of these variants was assayed in vitro in HEK293T cells and showed a decrease when transiently transfected plasmids expressing the protein and substrate were analyzed. As expected, the decrease in RNA editing was greater for p.(Thr375Arg) as it is located in the catalytic deaminase domain.

RNA editing can also affect splicing of GRIA2, and all the known variants in ADAR2 were analyzed to determine whether splicing of GRIA2 intron 11 immediately adjacent to the Q/R editing site is also affected (Schoft et al. 2007). Both ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) and p.(Arg603Glu) had a decrease in splicing of GRIA2 intron 11, which correlates with these two variants having the most severe effect on RNA editing activity. Variants in ADARB1 reported in the ClinVar website were also investigated in silico, with the focus on those that are in the coding region as they were likely to impact function. The analysis revealed that while some were predicted in silico to be pathogenic, others appear benign, whereas other variants gave in silico mixed results.

RESULTS

Patient 1 with a novel homozygous ADARB1 c.730C>G p.(Arg244Gly) variant

The patient presented for genetics evaluation at the age of 12 years due to developmental delay, autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, seizures, hip dysplasia, and flat feet (Table 1). He was born at term with a birth weight of 2.53 kg, length of 48.3 cm, and head circumference of 30.5 cm. The newborn period was complicated by cyanotic episodes and difficulty with latching. He had feeding difficulties throughout early childhood and failure to thrive but never required gastrostomy tube placement. He had his first seizure at age 12 months, initially during an illness. The patient's first and only brain MRI at age 12 months was normal. An electroencephalogram at age 4 years showed nonspecific cerebral disturbance. The patient has not taken antiepileptic medications since the age of 9 years without recurrence of seizures. His medical history is additionally significant for hip dysplasia and pes planus. Concerns about development first arose at 9 months, at which time developmental assessment showed gross motor skills commensurate with those of a 2–6 month old. He sat at 18 months and said his first words around age 4 years. He had episodes of developmental regression associated with seizures, resulting in loss of unsupported sitting and holding a bottle, though he was able to regain these skills. At age 12 years, he was functionally nonverbal and nonambulatory. Behaviorally, he had aggressive and self-injurious behaviors. On physical examination, he was found to have a distinctive facial appearance with a prominent nose and broad nasal tip. Family history was noncontributory. Biochemical testing completed at age 12 months, including plasma amino acids, organic acids, and an ammonia level, was normal. The patient underwent exome sequencing with copy number variant detection and was found to have a homozygous variant in ADARB1 c.730C>G p.(Arg244Gly). Patient 2 with a novel homozygous ADARB1 c.1124C>G p.(Thr375Arg) variant

This patient is the first child of healthy, consanguineous parents of Tunisian origin. She was born at term after a regular pregnancy via C-section due to fetal heart rhythm abnormalities. Birth growth parameters were normal (birth weight [BW]: 3820 g, birth length [BL]: 53 cm, birth head circumference [BHC]: 34.5 cm). The Apgar score was 9/10. At the age of 1 month, she began to present focal epileptic seizures characterized by eye deviation to the left, jaw clenching, and clonic movement of the left leg, lasting <2 sec (Table 1). The electroencephalogram (EEG) showed a lack of background organization with spikes predominating in the anterior regions. A tonic frontal seizure was recorded. The frequency of seizures increased, and she was initially treated with vigabatrin (VGB). At the age of 4 months, she started presenting spasms in clusters. At this time, the interictal EEG showed hypsarrhythmia with multifocal spikes predominantly in the frontal and temporal regions. Clusters of spasms were recorded. Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome was diagnosed. Hydrocortisone was added to VGB. With this treatment, the frequency of spasms reduced to one series/2 months. At neurological examination at 4 months, she presented a mild axial hypotonia. She had no dysmorphic features. Head circumference was normal. She presented with a psychomotor development arrest. Brain MRI was normal (1 month). Fundus oculi and electro-retinogram were normal. Her metabolic workup was normal (including aspartate aminotransferase [ASAT]/alanine aminotransferase [ALAT], ammonium, plasma and urine amino acids, urine organic acids, and lactate). Amino acids and neurotransmitters in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were also normal. At 10 months, she could smile, roll over, and try to handle objects, but she could not sit independently. She presented a bilateral pyramidal syndrome and started exhibiting erratic myoclonic movements. At the last observation, at the age of 3 years, she displayed psychomotor delay and a pyramidal syndrome. She had some social interaction and could smile but could not speak or sit autonomously.

Array comparative genomic hybridization (Array CGH) was normal. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) identified the c.1124C>G p.(Thr375Arg) homozygous variant in the ADARB1 gene, with both parents being heterozygous. This variant is predicted as pathogenic by in silico software and is not listed in gnomAD v4 (Table 2).

Summary of clinical features and comparison to previously reported patients

Seven previous patients have been published with ADARB1 variants, and major clinical features of those individuals included microcephaly, round face, feeding difficulties, short stature, developmental delays, seizures, and abnormal EEG (Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021). While clinical features varied among the two patients described in this study, both had feeding difficulties, motor delays, speech delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, seizures, and abnormal EEG (Table 1). Conversely, the two patients in this study lacked microcephaly, round face, thin upper lip, high-arched palate, short stature, peripheral hypertonia, and MRI abnormalities. These findings broaden the expressivity of the phenotype and indicate that some patients can be milder in the presentation.

The locations, conservations, and predicted impacts of ADARB1 variants

The domain structure of ADAR2 and the positions of the Arg244Gly and Thr375Arg mutations are shown in Figure 1A. These two mutations are located in dsRBD II and in the deaminase domain, respectively. The amino acid Arg244 is not conserved in other dsRNA binding proteins with the exception of human ILF3. PolyPhen-2 classified Thr375Arg as highly damaging, whereas Arg244Gly had lower scores, suggesting a more tolerated effect (Fig. 1B; Table 2; Supplemental Table S2; Adzhubei et al. 2010). SIFT predictions indicated that p.(Arg244Gly) is likely tolerated, while p.(Thr375Arg) is deleterious (Fig. 1C; Table 2; Supplemental Table S2; Ng and Henikoff 2003). FATHMM analysis further supported these findings, categorizing p.(Arg244Gly) as tolerated and p.(Thr375Arg) as damaging (Fig. 1D; Table 2; Supplemental Table S2; Shihab et al. 2013). MutPred2 analysis predicted that p.(Arg244Gly) may cause the loss of acetylation at K248 and minor structural alterations, while p.(Thr375Arg) is associated with significant structural disruptions, including altered nucleic acid binding (Fig. 1E; Supplemental Table S2; Pejaver et al. 2020). Overall, these results suggest that p.(Thr375Arg) may significantly impair ADAR2 function, whereas p.(Arg244Gly) is less likely to have a severe impact. Sequence alignment across multiple species (Fig. 1F) reveals that both Arg244 and Thr375 are highly conserved, emphasizing their functional importance for ADAR2 activity. This conservation underscores the potential detrimental impacts of these mutations on ADAR2 function.

The cocrystal structure of an ADAR2 deaminase domain dimer and one dsRBD II with a dsRNA substrate (PDB: 6VFF) indicates that the Arg244 and Thr375 residues play critical roles in RNA substrate binding and enzymatic activity (Supplemental Table S2). Specifically, Arg244 within the dsRBD II domain, at the C terminus of dsRBD II helix 1, is positioned close to the RNA backbone (Fig. 2A,B). The p.(Arg244Gly) substitution leads to the loss of a positively charged surface patch above the dsRNA (Fig. 2E). Structural modeling suggests that the p.(Arg244Gly) substitution may also destabilize dsRBD II α-helix 1 and potentially affect the fold of the dsRBD II domain by disrupting stabilizing hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2B; Pettersen et al. 2021).

ADAR2 Thr375 resides in the deaminase domain close to the active site and interacts with the ribose of the adenosine base to be edited, which is flipped out of the dsRNA into the deaminase active site (Fig. 2C). The ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) substitution introduces a bulky, positively charged residue near the catalytic site, partially closing the access to the active site and potentially hindering substrate accessibility. Structural modeling suggests that this mutation disrupts key hydrogen bonding interactions of Thr375 with the edited base, which may alter RNA positioning and reduce ADAR2's ability to recognize and efficiently edit its substrate (Fig. 2D; Thuy-Boun et al. 2020). Electrostatic surface representation analysis further highlights potential disruptions caused by ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) (Fig. 2E) and ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) (Fig. 2F; Pettersen et al. 2021).

The ADAR2 Arg244Gly and Thr375Arg variants show reduced ADAR2 editing activity

To test the hypothesis that these novel ADARB1 variants reduce ADAR2 RNA editing efficiency, we examined editing of the human pri-mi-376a2 substrate, which has a well-characterized A-to-I editing site at position +4 within the pri-mi-376a2 substrate predicted with RNAfold (Fig. 3A; Lorenz et al. 2011). The editing assays were performed in HEK293T cells, which exhibit minimal endogenous ADAR2 activity (5%–10%), providing a low background for accurate measurement of editing efficiency.

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with a plasmid expressing FLAG-tagged ADAR2 wild-type (WT) or P.(Arg244Gly) or p.(Thr375Arg) mutant proteins and another plasmid expressing pri-mir-376a2. After 72 h, total RNA was extracted, DNase I-treated, and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). Editing efficiency at the +4 site of pri-mir-376a2 was quantified using chromatogram peak heights [G/(A + G)] obtained by Sanger sequencing (Supplemental Fig. S1A). SnapGene analysis revealed significantly reduced editing activity in cells expressing ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) and even more reduced editing activity with the ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) mutant compared to ADAR2 WT (Fig. 3B; Supplemental Table S2). There are two isoforms of ADAR2 that either lack (ADAR2 short isoform [ADAR2S]) or contain (ADAR2 long isoform [ADAR2L]) a 40 amino acid, in-frame, insertion within the deaminase domain. The ADAR2S mutants showed a decrease in editing efficiency of 22% for ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) and complete loss of editing (0%) for ADAR2S p.(Thr375Arg) (Fig. 3B). The ADAR2L long isoform mutants displayed decreases in editing of 42% for ADAR2L p.(Arg244Gly) when compared with WT protein and 0% editing efficiency for ADAR2L p.(Thr375Arg) (Fig. 3B).

To further evaluate the effects of ADAR2 mutants, we used a dual-luciferase reporter assay. In this system, ADAR2 editing converts a UAG stop codon into a Trp codon (AGG), allowing for some level of nanoluciferase (NanoLuc) expression that can be compared to level of Firefly luciferase constitutively expressed for the same plasmid (Fig. 3C; Sinigaglia et al. 2024). The NanoLuc/Firefly ratios revealed no significant difference between ADAR2 WT and ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) with either ADAR2S or ADAR2L isoforms, while ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) exhibited a dramatic reduction in editing activity in both ADARS and ADARL isoforms, consistent with results from the pri-mir-376a2 editing assays (Fig. 3D). Immunoblotting of total cell lysates confirmed stable expression of both FLAG-tagged WT and mutant ADAR2 mutants, indicating that the observed functional impairments were not due to altered protein stability (Fig. 3E; Supplemental Fig. S1B).

To assess the editing activity of ADAR2 mutations, the plasmid containing Gria2 Q/R site was transfected into HEK293T cells. The editing efficiency at the Gria2 Q/R site was quantified using chromatogram peak heights [G/(A + G)] obtained by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 3F). Both ADAR2S and ADAR2L WT exhibited near-complete editing activity (Fig. 3F). In contrast, the ADAR2 Thr375Arg mutation in both ADAR2S and ADAR2L proteins completely abolished editing, yielding a G/(A + G) ratio of nearly zero, underscoring the essential role of Thr375 in ADAR2-mediated RNA editing (Fig. 3F). The ADAR2 P.(Arg244Gly) mutation exhibited isoform-specific effects. While ADAR2S p.(Arg244Gly) maintained editing efficiency comparable to WT, ADAR2L p.(Arg244Gly) showed a marked reduction in editing activity, suggesting that Arg244 is particularly critical for the catalytic function of the long isoform (Fig. 3F).

Together, these findings indicate that while the ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) mutant has a milder impact on ADAR2 editing efficiency, the p.(Thr375Arg) mutant severely compromises editing activity. The discrepancy between the pri-mir-376a2 and luciferase assay results for ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) suggests that the functional impact of this variant may vary depending on the editing substrate. Importantly, immunoblotting confirmed stable expression of all ADAR2 variants across assays, ruling out differential protein expression as a confounding factor (Fig. 3E).

The ADAR2 point mutants that impair editing activity impair splicing of Gria2 intron 11 to similar extents

Previous analysis of ADARB1 variants showed only modest decreases in RNA editing efficiency despite their association with severe clinical phenotypes (Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021). This leads us to investigate whether a threshold level of editing activity is required to support proper splicing of GRIA2 intron 11. The extensive dsRNA structure that supports editing at the exonic GRIA2 Q/R imposes a requirement for RNA editing at both the exonic Q/R site and at RNA editing hotspot 2 in the adjacent intron 11, to disrupt this dsRNA structure so that splicing of the GRIA2 transcript can occur (Higuchi et al. 1993; Schoft et al. 2007). The GRIA2 Q/R editing site is at base −25 from the 5′ splice site of GRIA2 intron 11, which lies within the exon–intron dsRNA structure required for editing (Higuchi et al. 1993; Schoft et al. 2007). If splicing at this position is less efficient than normal then reduced expression of GLUA2 subunits may also contributes to aberrantly increased calcium-permeable AMPA receptors by allowing more GLUA2-lacking, calcium-permeable AMPA receptors to form (Cull-Candy and Farrant 2021).

We assessed the effects of variants in the ADAR2S isoform that have been previously described; p.(Lys127Glu), p.(Lys367Asn), p.(Arg630Gln), and p.(Ala722Val) on splicing of a mouse Gria2 intron 11-containing transcript expressed in HeLa cells (Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021). We excluded ADAR2S p.(Arg603Gln) from splicing analysis, as we previously demonstrated that this mutation compromises the stability of the ADAR2S isoform (Tan et al. 2020). Thus, together with the novel variants described here, p.(Arg244Gly) and p.(Thr375Arg), a total of six ADAR2S variants were analyzed for their impact on splicing. To assess the effects of ADAR2 variants on splicing of mouse Gria2 intron 11, we used a dual splicing reporter, hereafter referred to as “11–12” (Fig. 4A; Schoft et al. 2007). In this construct, exons 11–12 together with the complete intervening intron 11 of the Gria2 gene are placed between sequences encoding an N-terminal red fluorescent protein (RFP) with a nuclear localization signal (NLS) and a C-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP). The construct is designed to measure splicing efficiency in transfected cells with fluorescent protein expression; however, we instead used RT-PCR to measure levels of the spliced and unspliced transcript (Fig. 4B). Quantification of the spliced/(spliced + unspliced) ratio confirmed that ADAR2S WT promotes robust splicing of 11–12, whereas both ADAR2S p.(Thr375Arg) and p.(Arg630Gln) mutants severely compromised this function (Fig. 4B,C; Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021). Other mutants ADAR2S p.(Arg244Gly), p.(Lys127Glu), p.(Lys367Asn), and p.(Ala722Val) that have less dramatic defects in RNA editing retained near-WT splicing activity (Fig. 4B,C). Immunoblotting analysis demonstrates that these differences were not due to reduced protein expression, as ADAR2 WT and ADAR2S mutant proteins were all detected at comparable levels (Fig. 4D). The ADAR2S p.(Arg244Gly) variant, which reduces editing efficiency at the pri-miR-376a2 + 4 site but not at the Gria2 Q/R site, does not significantly impact splicing of 11–12 (Fig. 4B,C). If ADAR2 mutant proteins were defective in recruiting an RNA helicase to melt the dsRNA structure or if they failed to depart from the dsRNA structure after editing then they might have greater defects in Gria2 intron 11 splicing than they have in RNA editing, but there is no indication of this for these mutants. This is consistent with previous findings indicating that it is the editing at the Q/R site per se, rather than ADAR2-dsRNA binding, that is required to allow efficient splicing of Gria2 intron 11 (Higuchi et al. 1993; Schoft et al. 2007).

(E) Immunofluorescence detection of expressed ADAR2 proteins in HeLa cells. Cells were stained with an anti-FLAG antibody (red channel) to detect the ADAR2 proteins, while DAPI (blue channel) was used as a nuclear stain. Representative cells displaying characteristic staining patterns were selected.

The two novel ADAR2 variants localize normally to the nucleoli

To determine whether mislocalization of ADAR2 mutants contributes to ADAR2 dysregulation, we analyzed the subcellular localization of two novel ADAR2 variants. Plasmids expressing FLAG-tagged ADAR2 WT or mutant proteins were transiently transfected into HeLa cells, followed by immunofluorescence (Schneider et al. 2012). The results showed that the two novel variants in both ADAR2S and ADAR2L proteins properly localized to the nucleus (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, consistent with previous reports on endogenous ADAR2, all mutants were enriched in the nucleolus (Fig. 4E; Desterro et al. 2003).

Clinical characteristics and potential pathogenicity of ADARB1 variants identified in ClinVar

Given our findings on the functional impacts of nine ADAR2 variants (Tan et al. 2020; Maroofian et al. 2021), we sought to expand our analysis by investigating additional variants reported in ClinVar to assess their potential pathogenicity. Of the 185 ADARB1 variants listed in ClinVar, 50 are single-nucleotide variants that result in amino acid substitutions. Among the variants analyzed in this study, only p.(Thr375Arg) is not currently recorded in the database. To reduce the list of variants in ADARB1, we prioritized those that alter buried residues, potentially affecting protein stability as well as those that may disrupt key functional interfaces, specifically, residues predicted to interact with dsRNA or to contribute to dimerization between ADAR2 monomers, based on the cocrystal structure of the ADAR2 dimer on dsRNA (Fig. 5A; Supplemental Table S2; Thuy-Boun et al. 2020).

Clinical information and computational impact predictions for further ADARB1 ClinVar variants. (A) Locations within ADAR2 domains of ADARB1 variants (in red) selected from ClinVar: p.(Gly276Val), p.(Pro345Leu), p.(Ile528Val), p.(Ser543Leu), p.(Thr616Asn), and p.(Arg674His). (B) Bar plot showing PolyPhen-2 scores for the ADARB1 variants, with HumDiv (sensitive to detecting functional effects) and HumVar (stringent for disease-related predictions) scores in blue and orange, respectively, and a threshold (red dashed line) indicating the cutoff. Mutations with scores below the threshold are considered potentially damaging. (C) Dual-axis plot of SIFT scores; mutations below the threshold (SIFT ≤ 0.05, red dashed line) are predicted to have a potentially damaging effect, and median sequence conservation (black dashed line) suggests evolutionary conservation and likeliness to affect protein function. (D) FATHMM scores predict the functional significance of mutations. Mutations with a negative FATHMM score (below <−2.0, green line) are damaging (in brown), indicating their potential to disrupt protein function. Tolerated mutation is in blue. (E) Results of the MutPred2 analysis predicted the potential molecular mechanisms affected by the mutations. The size of the bubbles represents the probability of the molecular mechanism being affected, with larger bubbles indicating higher confidence. Mutations p.(Arg674His) and p.(Ile528Val) were tolerated (<0.5), with no significant molecular mechanisms affected.

To further access the pathogenic potential of these variants, we assembled clinical data and performed in silico predictions with four complementary tools: PolyPhen-2, SIFT, FATHMM, and MutPred2 (Supplemental Table S2; Ng and Henikoff 2003; Adzhubei et al. 2010; Shihab et al. 2013; Pejaver et al. 2020). These tools evaluate the likelihood of functional disruption based on parameters such as structural integrity, evolutionary conservation, and predicted molecular interactions.

Among the ClinVar variants selected in our analysis, several have been reported in individuals with neurological disorders. One such variant, c.827G>T, p.(Gly276Val), was identified as biallelic in a female infant from a consanguineous union who presented with refractory seizures from day 4. Although parental testing was not performed, the consanguineous background suggests inheritance from both parents (Supplemental Table S2). This variant affects a dsRNA-contacting residue in dsRBDII and was consistently predicted to be deleterious across all in silico models. It was classified as probably damaging by both PolyPhen-2 and FATHMM and predicted to affect protein function by SIFT (Fig. 5B–D; Supplemental Table S2). Additionally, MutPred2 predicted a significant impact on RNA binding, loss of structure B-factor, and altered solvent accessibility, suggesting a substantial disruption of protein structure and function (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Table S2). These findings align with its clinical association with early-onset seizures, supporting its potential pathogenicity. However, no follow-up study was conducted on the patient, and the presence of additional mutations remains unknown.

Another notable variant, c.1034C>T, p.(Pro345Leu) was identified as monoallelic in a male neonate (<1 year) with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, seizures, and hypotonia. Imaging showed bilateral cerebral edema and hypoxic-ischemic injury; however, no second ADARB1 variant was detected, limiting the ability to establish causality (Supplemental Table S2). This variant affects a residue in the loop preceding the deaminase domain and showed discordant predictions across models. PolyPhen-2 classified it as “possibly damaging” but “benign,” while SIFT and FATHMM classified it as tolerated, indicating a lower likelihood of functional disruption (Fig. 5B–D; Supplemental Table S2). MutPred2 analysis suggested a loss of loop stability and a gain of N-linked glycosylation at Asn341, which could affect protein conformation and interactions, though the biological significance remains unclear (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Table S2).

Other variants show different levels of clinical significance. The variant c.1582A>G, p.(Ile528Val) appears to be a common polymorphism, found in over 10,800 monoallelic cases and 58 biallelic cases, suggesting that it is unlikely to be pathogenic (Supplemental Table S2). Accordingly, this variant was consistently classified as benign with all web-based tools, further supporting its nonpathogenic nature (Fig. 5B–E; Supplemental Table S2).

In contrast, c.1628C>T, p.(Ser543Leu) and c.1847C>A, p.(Thr616Asn) were reported in individuals with global developmental delay and hypotonia, but the possibility of additional variants in other genes is unknown, leaving their significance uncertain (Supplemental Table S2). Both these variants exhibit mixed computational predictions. The p.(Ser543Leu) variant was classified as “probably damaging” by PolyPhen-2 but tolerated by SIFT, while FATHMM predicted a damaging effect (Fig. 5B–D; Supplemental Table S2). MutPred2 predicted alterations in an ordered interface, loss of a structural strand, and changes in transmembrane protein interactions, indicating a potential disruption in structural stability (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Table S2). Similarly, p.(Thr616Asn) was classified as “probably damaging” by PolyPhen-2, tolerated by SIFT, and damaging by FATHMM (Fig. 5B–D; Supplemental Table S2). MutPred2 suggested a loss of structural integrity and loss of SUMOylation at Lys618, which could influence protein stability and function (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Table S2).

Similarly, c.2021G>A, p.(Arg674His) was detected in a compound heterozygous state in a 13-year-old female with encephalopathy, seizures, global developmental delay, behavioral issues, and dysmorphic features (Supplemental Table S2). Brain MRI revealed agenesis of the corpus callosum, mild colpocephaly, gray matter heterotopia in the right frontal lobe, and multiple small foci of T2 hyperintensity. This variant was maternally inherited, while a second variant, c.1066G>A, p.(Val356Ile), was paternally inherited. The p.(Arg674His) variant was classified as “possibly damaging” but “benign” by PolyPhen-2 and tolerated by SIFT (Fig. 5B,C; Supplemental Table S2). Despite FATHMM predicting it as damaging, MutPred2 did not indicate significant functional disruptions (Fig. 5D,E; Supplemental Table S2). These findings suggest that the pathogenicity of the p.(Arg674His) variant may be context-dependent, potentially influenced by compound heterozygosity rather than a direct loss of function effect.

By integrating clinical data with in silico predictions, we aimed to assess the potential pathogenicity of ADARB1 variants reported in patients with neurological phenotypes. Variants such as p.(Gly276Val) and p.(Thr616Asn) were supported by both damaging computational predictions and relevant clinical findings, suggesting a strong case for pathogenicity. In contrast, variants such as p.(Pro345Leu) and p.(Arg674His) showed discordant predictions across models and limited or inconclusive clinical information, complicating their interpretation. These cases underscore the challenge of variant classification in the absence of comprehensive genetic and phenotypic data. Although we did not proceed with functional studies for these variants, our findings highlight the diversity of ADARB1 variation that may influence neurological outcomes in both monoallelic and biallelic contexts. A more complete understanding of these variants, grounded in clinical, structural, and functional data, will be essential for refining their classification and determining their roles in disease.

DISCUSSION

We report the characterization of two novel variants of ADARB1, which increases the number of characterized patients with causative variants in this gene to nine. One of the new variants, ADAR2 p.(Arg244Gly) occurs in dsRBDII, whereas ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) is in the deaminase domain. Both of these variants have reduced RNA editing activity when expressed and assayed in HEK293T cells. The ADAR2 p.(Thr375Arg) variant has a greater reduction in RNA editing activity, and this is due to the altered amino acid being close to the active site and involved in hydrogen bonds to the edited adenosine base. We also assayed the ability of variants in ADAR2 to facilitate splicing of the GRIA2 transcript at the splice site near the Q/R site, which is embedded within an extensive dsRNA structure required for ADAR2 editing and must subsequently be efficiently melted to allow splicing. We wanted to ascertain whether the variants impacted splicing at this position as reduced overall expression of a GLUA2 can contribute to formation of GLUA2-lacking, aberrantly calcium-permeable AMPA receptors. Mouse Gria2 intron 11 forms a conserved dsRNA structure similar to that of human GRIA2 intron 11 and a reduction in splicing of it by ADAR2 mutants correlated only with reductions in RNA editing activity.

The question arises as to why relatively small decreases in RNA editing by ADAR2 mutant proteins can have such major clinical consequence. Greger et al. (2002, 2003) demonstrated that editing at the GRIA2 Q/R site regulates the trafficking of GLUR2 isoforms through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER); the unedited GLUR2 Q607 isoform is rapidly released from ER and travels to the cell surface of the neuron to give calcium-permeable AMPA receptors, whereas edited GLUA2 R607 transfers more slowly from ER, restraining AMPA receptor assembly and transport to the cell surface, as well as imposing calcium impermeability on AMPA receptors. In the experimental absence of GLUA2 subunits, GLUA2-lacking, calcium-permeable AMPA receptors may also form; until recently these were indistinguishable electrophysiologically from GLUA2 Q-containing calcium-permeable AMPA receptors. However, we find here that ADAR2 mutants with only slight reductions in RNA editing activity also allow near normal efficiency of Gria2 intron 11 splicing. Therefore, patients with weak ADAR2 mutants would not be expected to form GLUA2-lacking AMPA receptors either because there should not be a reduced overall level of GLUA2 subunits in these patients.

Studies in mice have demonstrated that a modest reduction in RNA editing can have dramatic consequences (Feldmeyer et al. 1999; Konen et al. 2020). GluA2ECS(G) transgenic mice were generated that were heterozygous for a base change in the intronic editing complementary sequence (ECS) in the Gria2 transcript intron 11 (Konen et al. 2020). Heterozygous GluA2+/ECS(G) mice had a mere 20% reduction in editing of the Q/R site in the Gria2 transcript. These mice had decreased body weights and premature mortality at ∼9 weeks, compared to 2–3 weeks for the Adarb1 mice. They also displayed reduced motor coordination, learning and memory impairment and hippocampal CA1-specific neuronal loss. When the transgenic mice were injected with IEM-1460, a specific inhibitor of calcium-permeable AMPA receptors, they had reduced seizure behavior. On the other hand, injection of an NMDA (N-methyl-d-aspartate) receptor antagonist, AP5, did not rescue the seizures. This demonstrates that the seizures in these GluA2+/ECS(G) mice are due to aberrant AMPA receptors and independent of NMDA receptors. To extrapolate from these studies in mice, it may be that a small proportion of unedited GLUR2 (Q) subunits can “poison” the AMPA receptors leading to calcium influx at the synapses. Therefore, specific inhibitors of AMPA calcium-permeable receptors such as IEM-1460 could potentially be beneficial.

We also investigated whether any variants in ADARB1 reported on the ClinVar website could be potentially pathogenic. The integration of clinical data and in silico analyses provides valuable insights into ADARB1 mutations and their potential roles in neurological disorders. Although PolyPhen-2, SIFT, FATHMM, and MutPred2 offer predictions about the functional consequences of missense mutations, their accuracy is limited by incomplete clinical data and computational biases. Our analysis highlights ADAR2 p.(Gly276Val) and p.(Thr616Asn), which are consistently classified as deleterious, supporting their pathogenic potential. In contrast, ADAR2 p.(Pro345Leu) and p.(Arg674His) showed discordant classifications, making their impact more difficult to assess. Additionally, MutPred2 suggested that some variants could disrupt post-translational modifications, such as SUMOylation p.(Thr616Asn) or ADP-ribosylation p.(Gly276Val), which may influence protein stability and function. Given these uncertainties, computational predictions alone cannot determine disease relevance, emphasizing the need for functional validation through RNA-editing assays, structural modeling, and patient-derived cellular studies. While we did not proceed with mutational studies, this analysis provides a framework for prioritizing ADARB1 variants for further investigation. Future studies integrating biochemical assays and patient phenotyping will be essential to clarify how these mutations contribute to neurological disorders and whether post-translational modifications play a regulatory role in ADAR2 function.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sequencing methods

For patient 1, The Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit was used to prepare genomic DNA for WGS. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System with 150 bp paired-end reads. Using Illumina DRAGEN Bio-IT Platform software, the DNA sequence was aligned and compared to the human genome build 19 (hg19/NCBI build 37). The Emedgene software was used to filter and analyze sequence variants identified within the patient's WGS data and to compare variants identified in the patient to the sequences of family members. The NxClinical software from BioDiscovery was used to filter and analyze copy number variants within the patient's whole WGS data.

For new patient 2, DNA was sequenced by WGS. The trio WGS was performed at the SeqOIA laboratory (https://laboratoire-seqoia.fr/). The library was prepared with the NEB Next Ultra II End repair/A-tailing DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) and sequenced in paired ends (2 × 150 bp) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The reads were aligned to the reference human genome (GRCh38.92) with the BWA-MEM 0.7.15 software package. The GATK haplotype caller (v4.1.7.0; Broad Institute) was used to call the SNVs, and CNVnator (v0.4.1; Mark B, Yale University) was used for copy number variation (CNV) calling. ClinSV (Minoche et al. 2021), a WGS-based bio-informatic framework that combined different methods based on read depth, was used to annotate and prioritize structural variants, including CNVs. The resulting variants were then annotated with AnnotSV v2.5.1 in an in-house developed workflow (SeqOIA-IT platform).

Cell culture

HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) with Earle's Salts (Biosera LM-E1141) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Merck F7524), 1× nonessential amino acids (Thermo Fisher 11140-050), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Biosera XCA4122). HeLa cells (ATCC CRM-CCL-2) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) High Glucose (Biosera LM-D1110) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Both cell lines were maintained as monolayers in sterile 10 cm culture dishes under standard culture conditions of 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

Site-directed PCR mutagenesis

Primers for site-directed mutagenesis were designed with NCBI Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Generi Biotech (Supplemental Table S1). WT ADAR2 plasmids (short and long isoforms) were previously generated (Tan et al. 2020). A 20 μL PCR reaction was performed with 10 ng of pCD3.1 ADAR2 WT plasmid as a template, 0.5 μM concentration of each primer, 1× Phusion plus buffer, 200 μM dNTPs, 0.2 μL of Phusion Plus DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher F-630L), and nuclease-free water to adjust the final volume. The PCR conditions followed the manufacturer's guidelines with a two-step protocol and number of cycles reduced to 20 to minimize errors.

To confirm successful amplification of the desired product, 10 μL of the PCR reaction was analyzed by electrophoresis on a 0.75% agarose gel and visualized under UV light. The remaining 10 μL of the PCR product was digested with 1 μL of 10 U/μL DpnI restriction enzyme (Thermo Fisher ER1702) at 37°C for 1 h to degrade the parental plasmid template. The entire digested product was transformed into 100 μL of chemically competent TOP10 Escherichia coli cells. The transformation mixture was incubated for 25 min on ice, heat-shocked for 90 sec at 42°C, immediately placed for 5 min back on ice, and then combined with 1 mL of prewarmed LB medium. The cells were incubated with gentle shaking at 230 rpm for 1 h at 37°C. After incubation, the cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 2 min, 800 μL of the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in the remaining medium. A 150 μL aliquot of the suspension was plated onto LB agar plates containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37°C.

Individual colonies were selected and cultured in 50 mL of LB medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin overnight at 37°C with shaking. Plasmids were isolated using the Exprep Plasmid SV Midi Kit (GeneAll 101-226) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The desired mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, performed by Eurofins Scientific.

A-to-I RNA editing activity assay

The A-to-I editing activity was initially assessed in HEK293T with pri-miR-376-a2 substrate. The plasmid expressing the human pri-miR-376a2 RNA editing substrate was previously described (Heale et al. 2009). HEK293T cells were seeded in 12 well plates at a density of 180,000 cells per well, 24 h before transfection. For cotransfection, 1 μg of the ADAR2-expressing plasmid and 1 μg of the pri-miR-376a2 editing substrate plasmid were mixed with 3.5 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher L3000015) in Opti-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher 31985070) as per the manufacturer's protocol. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and dividing the pellet into two aliquots. Total RNA was extracted from one aliquot and used for RT-PCR to analyze the editing efficiency at the +4 site of pri-miR-376a2. The second aliquot was used for immunoblotting to confirm ADAR2 expression levels. A dual-luciferase reporter assay was used to quantify A-to-I editing activity. The construction of the luciferase reporter plasmid and the detection method were performed as described previously (Sinigaglia et al. 2024). Editing efficiency was determined by the luminescence ratio of NucLuc (edited) to Firefly luciferase (total expression) with the Nano-Glo Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega N1610) and a Spark 10M multimode plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

The editing efficiency at the mouse Gria2 Q/R site was evaluated with a plasmid construct containing the mouse Gria2 sequence, spanning part of exon 11, intron 11, and exon 12, including the Q/R site, as the editing substrate (Tan et al. 2020). Cells were seeded at the densities described above and cotransfected with plasmids encoding Gria2 (harboring the Q/R site) and ADAR2S or ADAR2L WT. Cell collection and processing were performed as described above. Editing efficiency was assessed by determining the relative proportions of adenosine (A) and guanosine (G) at the Q/R site through Sanger sequencing.

Mouse Gria2 intron 11 splicing assay in HEK293T cells

The mouse Gria2 Q/R plasmid was described previously (Schoft et al. 2007). HEK293T cells were cotransfected as described above and were collected 48 h after transfection. The cell pellet from one tube was used for RNA extraction, and the other cell pellet was used for immunoblotting. RT-PCR was performed to detect spliced and unspliced transcripts, with the splicing efficiency was determined by calculating the ratio of spliced to unspliced transcripts.

RNA extraction and cDNA reverse transcription

Total RNA was isolated from cells with the Universal RNA Purification Kit (EURX E3598), following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA samples were treated with 2 units of TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher AM2238) per 10 μg of total RNA and then purified by phenol–chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation with sodium acetate. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher EP0442), with a specific primer.

PCR

PCR was performed for both the editing assays and splicing assays in a 25 μL reaction volumes containing 30 ng of template DNA, 12.5 μL of EliZyme FAST Taq MIX Red (Elisabeth Pharmacon EZ5120), 0.4 μM concentration of each forward and reverse primer, and nuclease-free water to adjust the final volume. The reaction was performed with a thermal cycler under the following conditions: initial denaturation for 1 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation for 15 sec at 95°C, annealing for 15 sec at the primer-specific temperature 55°C, and extension for 15 sec per kilobase of the expected product size at 72°C. A final extension step for 5 min at 72°C was included. Primers are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Following amplification, 5 μL of the PCR product was analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel and visualized under UV light to confirm the presence and size of the expected fragment. The bands corresponding to the spliced transcript (RFP-GFP) and the unspliced transcript (RFP-intron11) were quantified with ImageJ software. The primers are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Protein extraction

HEK293T cells were lysed in lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40, 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 5 mM EDTA, and 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 1× Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-Free (Thermo Fisher 78439). The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,500g for 20 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was quantified with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher 23225).

Immunoblotting

Protein lysates (20 μg per sample) were mixed with 5× Laemmli buffer, boiled for 5 min at 95°C, and loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. Electrophoresis was initiated at 90 V until the loading dye reached the gel's stacking–separating interface and the molecular weight marker bands were resolved. Subsequently, the voltage was increased to 120 V and maintained until complete separation of the samples was achieved, and proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes at 100 V for 1 h at 4°C.

Membranes were blocked with 5% dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies at the following dilutions: FLAG tag (rabbit anti-FLAG; 1:400; Sigma F7425) and Tubulin (rabbit anti-Tubulin; 1:8000; Sigma T6074 ).

The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in TBS for 1 h at room temperature: anti-mouse IgG (goat; 1:5000; Thermo Fisher 31430) and anti-rabbit IgG (1:80,000; Merck A0545). Detection was performed with Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad 1705061) and visualized with a UVITEC ChemiDoc imaging system.

Immunofluorescence

HeLa cells were seeded on coverslips in 24 well plates. Following treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Blocking was performed with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at 37°C. The coverslips were incubated with FLAG polyclonal primary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich F7425) in a humidified chamber overnight at 4°C. Afterward, the coverslips were washed thrice with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L): Thermo Fisher Scientific A-11072, 1:500) for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the coverslips were washed three times with PBS, once with distilled water, and mounted on slides (Superfrost Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher P36934) containing 1 mg/mL DAPI (Thermo Fisher D1306). The slides were left to solidify overnight in the dark. The samples were visualized with a Laser scanning confocal microscope Zeiss LSM 780 with Airyscan detector with a 63×/1.4 M27 oil DIC Plan-Apochromat objective. The raw images were processed in Fiji (Schneider et al. 2012).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism software. Figures showing editing and splicing efficiencies were generated, and statistical significance was evaluated with unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests.

Densitometric analyses of immunoblot bands and PCR gel electrophoresis results were performed with ImageJ software. Band intensities were normalized to their respective loading controls to determine relative expression or product levels.

All experiments were conducted in biological triplicates, and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
