Involvement of cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript neurons in glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile luteinizing hormone secretion in female rats
Authors: Sena MATSUZAKI, Safiullah HAZIM, Yuna TOYOHAMA, Koki YAMADA, Shunsuke SEKI, Kota INAMURA, Yuki OTSUKA, Marina TAKIZAWA, Chisato NODA, Naoko INOUE, Hiroko TSUKAMURA, Yoshihisa UENOYAMA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Malnutrition often suppresses reproductive function by inhibiting the pulsatile secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)/gonadotropins in mammals. This study aimed to determine whether cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART) neurons mediate the suppression of GnRH/gonadotropin pulses under malnutrition, since CART neurons have been reported to project to kisspeptin/neurokinin B/dynorphin A neurons (also known as the GnRH pulse generator) and GnRH neurons in rats. Wistar-Imamichi female rats were ovariectomized (OVX), and some of these rats were immediately implanted with subcutaneous Silastic tubing containing estradiol-17β (OVX + low E2) to maintain a diestrous level of estrogen. Free-moving conscious OVX and OVX + low E2 rats were subjected to 1-h frequent blood sampling followed by brain sampling to examine whether acute glucoprivation by peripheral administration of 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG), a glucose utilization inhibitor, suppressed the secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH) and activated hypothalamic CART neurons. The other cohort of animals was subjected to 3-h frequent blood sampling to examine whether central CART administration suppresses pulsatile LH secretion. Peripheral 2DG administration suppressed LH secretion and activated CART neurons in the hypothalamic supraoptic nucleus (SON) and parvocellular paraventricular nucleus (PVN), but not in the magnocellular PVN-zona incerta-dorsomedial nucleus continuum and arcuate nucleus, in OVX and OVX + low E2 rats. In addition, central CART administration suppressed pulsatile LH secretion in OVX and OVX + low E2 rats. These results suggest the possible involvement of CART neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN in malnutrition-induced suppression of pulsatile LH secretion in female rats.

In mammals, reproductive function is suppressed by malnutrition. In women, undernutrition often delays menarche [1] and weight loss often causes cessation of menstrual cycles [2]. In dairy cows, the timing of the first postpartum ovulation is often delayed by negative energy balance [3]. In addition, experimental fasting has been shown to prolong the length of the estrous cycle and reduce mating and pregnancy rates in female rats [4, 5]. The suppression is thought to be a strategic adaptation to avoid pregnancy, parturition, and lactation during malnutrition, and the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation to improve fertility in humans and livestock.

Mammalian reproductive function is regulated by the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal axis. Within this axis, kisspeptin/neurokinin B/dynorphin A neurons (also known as KNDy neurons) in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC) are the gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse generator, which is responsible for tonic gonadotropin secretion and the subsequent follicular development in female mammals [6,7,8]. KNDy neurons are possible targets of malnutrition-induced infertility and lactational anestrus because fasting and suckling stimuli downregulated ARC Kiss1 (encoding kisspeptin) expression and pulsatile luteinizing hormone (LH) secretion in female rats [5, 9,10,11,12] and mice [13, 14]. Our previous study showed that endogenous opioid peptides mediate malnutrition-induced suppression of GnRH/gonadotropin secretion, since antagonism against either the κ-opioid receptor, μ-opioid receptor, or δ-opioid receptor blocked glucoprivation-induced suppression of LH pulses in female rats treated with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG, an inhibitor of glucose utilization) [15,16,17]. In addition, these studies indicated that 2DG activated dynorphin A neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and supraoptic nucleus (SON) and enkephalin neurons in the PVN in female rats [15, 17].

Previous studies have demonstrated that cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART) fibers are in close proximity to KNDy and GnRH neurons in female and male rats [18, 19]. CART is considered an anorexigenic peptide, because CART gene (Cartpt)-knockout mice are predisposed to show hyperphagia and obesity under high-fat diet conditions in comparison with wild-type controls [20]. CART neurons are widely distributed throughout the hypothalamus, including the SON, PVN, zona incerta (ZI), dorsomedial nucleus (DMH), and ARC in rats [21]. Although the CART receptor has not yet been identified, a previous study suggests that the putative CART receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor that activates inhibitory Gi/o signaling because pertussis toxin, a Gi/o inhibitor, was shown to attenuate CART-induced reward behavior in male rats [22]. These findings suggest that CART neurons may contribute to malnutrition-induced suppression of pulsatile GnRH/LH secretion through inhibition of KNDy and/or GnRH neurons. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have examined the effects of central CART administration on LH secretion in vivo, although previous in vitro studies have reported that CART exposure stimulates mouse KNDy and rat GnRH neurons [19].

The present study aimed to explore whether CART is involved in the glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion in female rats. First, to determine whether CART neurons are activated under malnutritional conditions, we examined the effect of intravenous (iv) administration of 2DG on fos expression, a marker of neuronal activation, in CART neurons in the hypothalamic nuclei, including the SON, PVN, ZI, DMH and ARC, in ovariectomized (OVX) and estradiol-17β-implanted OVX (OVX + low E2) rats. Second, we investigated the effect of third ventricle (3V) administration of CART on pulsatile LH secretion in OVX and OVX + low E2 rats to test whether central CART signaling inhibits GnRH/LH pulse generation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Adult Wistar-Imamichi female rats (age, 9–12 weeks; weight, 230–280 g; Institute for Animal Reproduction, Kasumigaura, Japan) were housed in a controlled environment (14-h light, 10-h darkness, with lights on at 0500 h, 22°C ± 3°C) and had free access to food (CE-2; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and water unless otherwise stated. Bilateral ovariectomy was performed in female rats showing at least 2 consecutive estrous cycles. A subset of animals was maintained for 2 weeks to serve as OVX rats (n = 19). The other subset of OVX rats underwent immediate implantation of subcutaneous Silastic tubing (inner diameter, 1.57 mm; outer diameter, 3.18 mm; length, 25 mm; Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA) containing E2 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in peanut oil (Sigma-Aldrich) at 20 µg/ml; these rats were maintained for 1–2 weeks to serve as OVX + low E2 rats (n = 20). The surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (27 mg/kg, Fujita Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan)/xylazine (5.3 mg/kg, Elanco Japan, Tokyo, Japan) mixture and inhalant 1% to 2% isoflurane (Pfizer Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The present study was approved by the Committee on Animal Experiments of the Graduate School of Bioagricultural Sciences, Nagoya University.

Blood and brain sampling to determine the effects of peripheral 2DG injection on LH secretion and activation of CART neurons

Some of the OVX and OVX + low E2 rats underwent insertion of a silicone cannula (inner diameter, 0.5 mm; outer diameter, 1.0 mm; Shin-Etsu Polymer, Tokyo, Japan) into the right atrium through the jugular vein on the day before blood and brain sampling. Free-moving conscious OVX (n = 4) and OVX + low E2 (n = 3) rats received iv injection of 2DG (400 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) through the silicone cannula immediately after the first blood collection between 1300 and 1400 h. The doses of 2DG were chosen according to our previous study [23], which showed that iv injection of 2DG (400 mg/kg) suppressed LH pulses in both OVX and OVX + low E2 rats. An equimolar hypertonic concentration of xylose (366 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich), an indigestible sugar, was used for control in OVX (n = 4) and OVX + low E2 rats (n = 3). Animals were deprived of food 2–3 h before iv 2DG or xylose administration until the brain sampling to avoid the possible acute effects of feeding on 2DG-induced changes in expression of fos, a marker of neuronal activation, and Cartpt, as well as suppression of LH pulses. Blood samples (100 µl) were collected every 6 min for 1 h. Red blood cells—which were obtained from donor rats, washed, and diluted with heparinized saline—were replaced at each blood collection to maintain a constant hematocrit level. Blood samples were immediately cooled on ice and centrifuged, and plasma samples (50 µl) were collected, frozen at –80°C, and stored at –20°C until they were assayed for LH. At the end of 1-h blood sampling, the animals were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) and then perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.05 M phosphate buffer. The brain was fixed with the same fixative overnight at 4°C and then immersed in 30% sucrose in 0.05 M phosphate buffer at 4°C. Frozen frontal sections (50 µm thickness) of the hypothalamus, which included the SON, PVN, ZI, DMH, and ARC, were prepared using a cryostat (CM1800, Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and subjected to double in situ hybridization for Cartpt and fos.

Blood sampling to determine the effects of central CART injection on LH secretion

The other cohort of OVX (n = 11) and OVX + low E2 (n = 14) rats underwent stereotactic implantation of a stainless-steel guide cannula (22G; Plastics ONE, Roanoke, VA, USA) into the 3V on the basis of the rat brain atlas [24], on the day of ovariectomy. The tip of the guide cannula was located 0.8 mm posterior and 7.5 mm ventral to the bregma at midline. The OVX and OVX + low E2 rats underwent implantation of a silicone cannula (inner diameter, 0.5 mm; outer diameter, 1.0 mm; Shin-Etsu Polymer) into the right atrium through the jugular vein on the day before blood sampling.

Immediately after the first blood collection at approximately 1300 h, free-moving conscious OVX (n = 6) and OVX + low E2 (n = 8) rats received CART (0.1 nmol/2 µl; rat CART(55-102), an active fragment of the CART prepropeptide [25]; Peptide Institute, Ibaraki, Japan) into the 3V at a flow rate of 1 µl/min for 2 min using a microsyringe pump (EICOM, Kyoto, Japan) through an inner cannula (28G; Plastics ONE). The dose of CART was chosen on the basis of a previous study [26], wherein administration of 1, 2, or 3 µg (= 0.2, 0.4, or 0.6 nmol) of CART into the fourth cerebroventricle had inhibitory effects on feeding, and our preliminary study, wherein 3V injection of 0.4 nmol CART caused seizure-like behavior in OVX + low E2 rats. Ultrapure water (UPW) was injected into the 3V of OVX (n = 5) and OVX + low E2 (n = 6) control rats. Blood samples (100 µl) were collected every 6 min for 3 h. Red blood cells were replaced at each blood collection to ensure a constant hematocrit level, as described above. During the 3-h sampling period, the animals were deprived of food to avoid the influence of food intake on LH secretion. Blood samples were immediately cooled on ice and centrifuged, and plasma samples (50 µl) were collected, frozen at –80°C, and stored at –20°C until assayed for LH.

At the end of the blood sampling, the animals were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) and injected with 2 µl of 3% brilliant blue solution into the 3V at the same flow rate as the CART injection described above. The brain was removed, and the placement of the 3V cannula was verified by visual inspection of the brilliant blue solution. Correct cannula placement was confirmed in all animals.

Radioimmunoassay for plasma LH concentrations and LH pulse parameter analysis

Plasma LH concentrations were measured by a double-antibody radioimmunoassay (RIA) using a rat LH-RIA kit that included rabbit anti-rat LH antiserum (RRID: AB_2665533) provided by the National Hormone and Peptide Program (Harbor-UCLA Medical Center, Torrance, CA, USA), as described previously [27]. The concentrations were expressed in terms of rat LH-RP-3 levels. The least detectable level was 0.078 ng/ml in 50-µl plasma samples. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 8.1% and 3.8% at 1.02 ng/ml, respectively. LH pulses in 3-h plasma LH profiles were identified by the PULSAR computer program [28], as described previously [16]. The mean LH concentrations and the frequency, amplitude, and baseline of LH pulses were calculated for each individual and then for the groups.

Double in situ hybridization for Cartpt and fos in rat hypothalamus

Free-floating double in situ hybridization for Cartpt and fos messenger RNA (mRNA) was performed using frontal sections (including the SON, PVN, ZI, DMH, and ARC) obtained from OVX and OVX + low E2 rats treated with iv 2DG or xylose, as described previously [29]. The Cartpt-specific complementary RNA (cRNA) probe (position 208-722; GenBank accession number NM_017110.1) was designed and labeled using a digoxigenin (DIG)-labeling kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The fos-specific cRNA probe (position 573–1193; GenBank accession number NM_022197.2) [15, 29] was designed and labeled using a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeling kit (Roche Diagnostics). The brain sections were hybridized with 1 µg/ml DIG-labeled Cartpt and FITC-labeled fos cRNA probes at 60°C overnight. The hybridized FITC-labeled fos probe was detected by a peroxidase (POD)-conjugated anti-FITC antibody (Roche Diagnostics, RRID: AB_840257) and tyramide signal amplification Plus FITC Kit (1:100; Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). After inactivation of the POD on the anti-FITC antibody by incubating the sections in 0.1 N hydrochloric acid for 30 min, the hybridized DIG-labeled Cartpt probe was detected using a POD-conjugated anti-DIG antibody (Roche Diagnostics, RRID: AB_514500), tyramide signal amplification Plus Biotin Kit (1:100; Akoya Biosciences), and DyLight 594-conjugated streptavidin (1:500; Sigma-Aldrich). Fluorescence images were obtained using an ApoTome.2 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and the numbers of Cartpt-expressing cells and fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells were counted unilaterally in the SON (every 200 µm from 0.48 to 1.56 mm posterior to the bregma, 6 sections in total), parvocellular PVN (every 200 µm from 1.44 to 2.16 mm posterior to the bregma, 4 sections in total), magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum (every 200 µm from 1.56 to 2.64 mm posterior to the bregma, 6 sections in total), and ARC (every 200 µm from 1.72 to 4.36 mm posterior to the bregma, 13 sections in total) in the images on the basis of the rat brain atlas [24]. Cartpt-expressing cells with similar morphological features were continuously distributed across the region extending from the magnocellular part of the PVN through the ZI to the DMH; therefore, this region was defined as the “magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum” for cell-counting analysis. The percentage of fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells was also determined. No positive signals for Cartpt or fos mRNA were detected in the brain sections hybridized with the corresponding sense probe as a negative control.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance of differences in plasma LH concentrations between iv 2DG- and xylose-treated rats in the OVX and OVX + low E2 conditions was analyzed by three-way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA; main effects, 2DG treatment [xylose and 2DG], E2 treatment [OVX and OVX + low E2], and time after the 2DG/xylose injection). When a significant interaction was detected, simple main effects were analyzed. The statistical significance of differences in the numbers of Cartpt-expressing cells and fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells and the percentage of fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells between 2DG- and xylose-treated rats in the OVX and OVX + low E2 conditions was analyzed by two-way ANOVA (main effects, 2DG treatment [xylose and 2DG] and E2 treatment [OVX and OVX + low E2]). The statistical significance of differences in the LH pulse parameters, namely, the mean LH concentrations and the frequency, amplitude, and baseline of LH pulses, between CART- and UPW-treated rats in the OVX and OVX + low E2 conditions was analyzed by two-way ANOVA (main effects, CART treatment [UPW and CART] and E2 treatment [OVX and OVX + low E2]). When a significant interaction was detected, simple main effects were analyzed. All analyses were performed using SAS OnDemand for Academics (https://welcome.oda.sas.com).

In the parvocellular PVN, fos mRNA was expressed in a subset of Cartpt-expressing cells in OVX and OVX + low E2 rats treated with iv 2DG or xylose (Fig. 2A). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of 2DG treatment on the percentage of fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells in the parvocellular PVN. In the magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum, fos mRNA was expressed in a subset of Cartpt-expressing cells in both OVX and OVX + low E2 rats treated with iv 2DG or xylose (Fig. 2A). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of E2 treatment on the number of fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells in the continuum. No significant main effect of 2DG treatment or interaction was observed. Specifically, the number of fos-positive Cartpt-expressing cells in the continuum in the OVX + low E2 groups was significantly lower than that in the OVX groups (F[1,10] = 6.22, P = 0.032, Fig. 2B). Discussion

The present study suggests the possible involvement of CART neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN in glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion in female rats because systemic glucoprivation by iv 2DG administration activated SON and parvocellular PVN CART neurons, and 3V CART administration suppressed pulsatile LH secretion in both OVX and OVX + low E2 rats. In contrast, CART neurons in the magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum and ARC appear less important in the 2DG-induced suppression of LH pulses, since 2DG administration did not significantly increase the proportion of fos-expressing CART neurons in the magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum or ARC. This is the first report suggesting that hypothalamic CART neurons may be involved in the malnutrition-induced suppression of pulsatile LH secretion. Since CART fibers are in close proximity to approximately 65% of KNDy neurons and 60% of GnRH neurons in female rats [19], KNDy and/or GnRH neurons could be the action site(s) of 3V CART administration. These findings suggest the possibility that CART neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN that are activated by glucoprivation directly or indirectly inhibit KNDy and/or GnRH neurons and thereby limit LH secretion. Importantly, a majority of CART neurons in the SON and a subset of those in the parvocellular PVN co-expressed the dynorphin A gene in male rats [30]. In addition, our previous study showed that dynorphin A neurons in the SON and PVN were activated by glucoprivation in OVX + low E2 rats and that central κ-opioid receptor antagonism blocked glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion in OVX + low E2 rats [15]. Taken together, these findings may imply that, under malnutrition, CART/dynorphin A neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN may co-secrete CART and dynorphin A, and both neuropeptides mediate glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion through inhibition of KNDy and/or GnRH neurons in female rats.

The present study demonstrated that central administration of CART immediately suppressed pulsatile LH secretion in female rats, indicating that inhibitory CART receptor(s) may be involved in the inactivation of KNDy and/or GnRH neurons. Although CART receptor(s) have not been identified to date, previous studies have suggested that the putative CART receptor is an inhibitory G protein-coupled receptor because pertussis toxin, a Gi/o inhibitor, attenuated CART-induced reward behavior in male rats [22] and CART-stimulated activation of extracellular signal-related kinase (ERK) in AtT20 cells, a pituitary-derived cell line [31]. GPR160 has been proposed to be a candidate CART receptor because anti-GPR160 polyclonal antibodies blocked CART-induced neuropathic pain in mice [32] and CART-induced reduction of food and water intake in male rats [33]. However, Freitas-Lima et al. [34] reported that the same anti-GPR160 antibodies failed to compete with 125I-CART for specific binding in rat pheochromocytoma PC12 cells. Moreover, they also showed the absence of 125I-CART binding in the human monocyte THP-1 cell line, which exhibits high endogenous Gpr160 expression, as well as Gpr160-transfected human osteosarcoma U2OS and astrocytoma U-251 MG cell lines [34]. Thus, identification of the CART receptor(s) and development of appropriate antagonist(s) are essential to elucidate the role and action site of endogenous CART in malnutrition-induced suppression of pulsatile LH secretion.

Notably, a previous in vitro study demonstrated that CART (30 or 100 nM) exerted a stimulatory effect on neuronal firing and depolarization in the majority of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-labeled ARC KNDy neurons in OVX transgenic mouse brain slices, as well as in approximately 75% of GFP-labeled GnRH neurons in OVX + low E2 transgenic rat brain slices [19]. Thus, CART may not directly suppress KNDy and GnRH neurons. Therefore, the inhibitory effect of CART on LH secretion observed in the present study may be mediated by stimulatory interneurons, such as glutamatergic neurons, upstream of KNDy and/or GnRH neurons. This interpretation is supported by the findings of previous studies showing that glutamate stimulates KNDy neurons and subsequently enhances LH secretion in rats [35] and mice [36]. Under in vivo malnutritional conditions, such indirect inhibitory CART signaling may mask the direct stimulatory effects of CART on KNDy and GnRH neurons. Clarification of the co-expression of CART receptor(s) in neighboring glutamatergic neurons is essential to understand how endogenous CART suppresses pulsatile LH secretion.

Interestingly, our results indicated that diestrous levels of estrogen reduced Cartpt expression in the parvocellular PVN, magnocellular PVN-ZI-DMH continuum, and ARC. In contrast, estrogen did not alter Cartpt expression in CART neurons in the SON, which were predominantly activated by glucoprivation in the current study. Since CART suppressed LH secretion in both OVX and OVX + low E2 rats, the region-specific differences in estrogen responsiveness among CART neurons support the notion that SON CART neurons may contribute to glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion regardless of steroidal condition in female rats.

The present 2DG model should be interpreted as an acute glucoprivation model rather than a complete model of chronic undernutrition. Chronic undernutrition induces multiple metabolic changes, including altered availability of glucose, fatty acids, amino acids, and hormones. Among these factors, reduced glucose availability in the brain is considered an important signal linking negative energy balance to reproductive suppression. Thus, 2DG administration is useful for experimentally isolating the contribution of impaired glucose utilization to the suppression of the GnRH/LH pulse generating mechanism. This interpretation is supported by previous findings that chronic malnutrition reduces glucose availability and suppresses reproductive function, and that 2DG-induced glucoprivation suppresses LH secretion in rats [15,16,17, 23, 29, 37], goats [38], and sheep [39, 40]. Therefore, although 2DG does not fully recapitulate underfeeding, it provides a physiologically relevant model for examining how central glucoprivation contributes to reproductive dysfunction under negative energy balance in domestic animals and other mammalian species. Systemic glucoprivation by iv 2DG administration would be, at least partly, sensed by the fourth ventricle (4V), because our previous studies showed that 4V 2DG administration suppresses LH pulses in male and female rats [15, 37]. Notably, 4V 2DG administration activated PVN, but not SON, dynorphin neurons [15], which partly co-expresses CART [30]. Therefore, we envision that parvocellular PVN CART neurons may be activated by systemic glucoprivation sensed by 4V glucosensor via noradrenergic neurons originating from the nucleus tractus solitarius [41]. On the other hand, the neural pathway that activates SON CART neurons under the systemic glucoprivation remains unclear. Further studies are needed to clarify the neural pathway.

Our previous studies showed that iv 2DG administration rapidly increased plasma glucose concentrations, with hyperglycemia persisting for 3 h after administration [15,16,17]. Therefore, the most plausible interpretation is that reduced glucose availability/impaired glucose utilization leads to activation of SON and parvocellular PVN CART neurons and subsequent suppression of GnRH/LH release. Interestingly, 2DG-induced LH suppression was transient and was followed by recovery. This transient suppression may reflect loss of the 2DG effect, because circulating 2DG concentrations rapidly decreased after the end of 2DG infusion [40]. It remains unclear whether the recovery of LH pulses reflects compensatory increase in blood glucose and related signals such as pancreatic glucagon and/or adrenal glucocorticoid and adrenaline [42].

A major limitation of the present study is that the involvement of endogenous CART signaling in 2DG-induced suppression of LH secretion could not be directly examined using receptor antagonist(s), because the functional CART receptor(s) remain unidentified and specific CART receptor antagonists are not currently available. Future development of specific CART receptor antagonists or site-specific approaches to block CART signaling will be necessary to determine whether CART signaling mediates glucoprivation-induced suppression of LH secretion. Another limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample sizes with only three animals per group in some experiments. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the observed effects.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that glucoprivation activates CART neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN, and that central CART administration suppresses pulsatile LH secretion. These findings suggest the possible involvement of CART neurons in the SON and parvocellular PVN in glucoprivic suppression of pulsatile LH secretion in female rats. Thus, CART receptor(s) may represent potential therapeutic targets for overcoming malnutrition-associated suppression of reproductive function in humans and livestock.
