Real-world prescribing patterns of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in Type 2 diabetes with various ASCVD subtypes from a large tertiary referral center☆
Authors: Eleonora Avenatti, Archana Sadhu, Rakesh Gullapelli, Aditi Rao, Chetan Pavuluri, Khurram Nasir, Sangeeta R Kashyap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims/hypothesisTo evaluate prescription patterns of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2, inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) among, patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) within a contemporary healthcare system.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of the Houston Methodist Cardiovascular Learning Health System Registry, encompassing electronic medical records from June 2016 to August 2023. Adults with T2D and comorbid ASCVD were identified using validated algorithms based on ICD-10 codes, laboratory values, and medication records. Prescription rates of SGLT2i and GLP-1RAs were assessed, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with their use.ResultsAmong 791,873 adults seen in outpatient settings, 8096 met criteria for T2D with comorbid ASCVD. The mean age was 69.4 ± 9.7 years; 38.8% were women, and 56.1% were non-Hispanic White. Overall, 22% received SGLT2i prescriptions, and 25.3% received GLP-1RAs prescriptions. Patients prescribed SGLT2i were younger, more likely male, privately insured, and had higher HbA1c levels, as well as higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and heart failure (HF). GLP-1RAs recipients were younger, more often female, non-Hispanic White, privately insured, and had higher body mass index (BMI). In logistic regression analysis, men were more likely to be prescribed SGLT2i after adjusting for age, comorbidities and socioeconomic parameters for secondary prevention of ASCVD as a whole and across subtypes of ASCVD. Women were more likely to be prescribed GLP-1RAs after adjusting for age, comorbidities, obesity and socioeconomic parameters for secondary prevention of ASCVD, CAD and PAD but not significant in stroke.Conclusions/interpretationDespite guideline recommendations, prescription rates of SGLT2i and GLP-1RAs remain low in high-risk patients with T2D and ASCVD across CVD subtypes. Notable disparities exist based on gender, race, and socioeconomic status, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to address these gaps in care.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), Secondary prevention, Cardiovascular risk, Prescription patterns, Health disparities

Introduction

The advent of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1RAs) and Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitors (SGLT2i) has transformed type 2 diabetes (T2D) management, especially in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD). These agents improve glycemic control and offer significant cardiovascular and renal benefits demonstrated in large clinical trials [1]. Consequently, leading organizations now recommend their use irrespective of sex, age, race, HbA1c levels, or type of CVD [[2], [3], [4]]. Secondary prevention is critical to reduce recurrent cardiovascular events and improve long-term outcomes in this population.

Despite this, prescribing patterns of these therapies in clinical practice remain understudied, particularly regarding secondary prevention across various CVD subtypes including stroke and peripheral arterial disease (PAD). This study explored the prescription patterns of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is in patients with T2D and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the Houston Methodist Cardiovascular Learning Health System Registry, an electronic medical records (EMRs) linked database with clinical, lab, medication, and demographic data.

The Study Population included all adult (≥18 years) patients with a diagnosis of T2D and established ASCVD seen at least twice as outpatients within a single Institution between June 2016 and August 2023. T2D was identified using a validated algorithm [5] requiring at least two outpatient ICD-10 codes for T2D, along with supporting laboratory data (HbA1c > 6.5%, OR Fasting Glucose > 126 mg/dl, OR any glucose> 200 mg/dl) and/or prescriptions for glucose-lowering medications in both visits (suppl Fig. 1). ASCVD were identified by appropriate ICD-10 codes for coronary artery disease (CAD), PAD, or stroke (suppl Table 1). Patients on dialysis or who were pregnant were excluded.

Variables: Demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance, Area Deprivation Index [ADI]), clinical characteristics (Body mass index [BMI], HbA1c, eGFR, hypertension [HTN], dyslipidemia [HLD], chronic kidney disease [CKD], heart failure [HF]). Primary outcomes were active prescriptions of SGLT2i and GLP-1RAs.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Differences between groups were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to identify factors independently associated with SGLT2i and GLP-1RAs prescriptions Adjusted models were used to control for potential confounders . All analyses were conducted using STATA (STATACorp Version 14). P value<0.05 was considered significant in all instances.

Results

Among 791,873 adults seen in the outpatient setting in the defined timeframe, 8096 met inclusion criteria for T2D and established ASCVD [age 69.4 ± 9.7 years; 38.8% women, 56.1% non-Hispanic White] .Cardiometabolic comorbidities were highly prevalent - HTN: 94.7%, HLD: 63.1%,obesity: 47.3% CKD: 22%, HF:16.8%.

Overall, prescription rates were low (22% for SGLT2i, 25.3% for GLP-1RAs) even more so when compared to other I line treatment (eg 85.6% of patients had a prescription for statins and 78.1% had a prescription for glucose lowering medications). Patients prescribed SGLT2i were slightly younger (67.7 ± 9.4 vs. 69.9 ± 9.7 years), more often male (68.7%vs. 59%) with private insurance (28.8%vs. 21.9%), higher HbA1c levels (7.1 ±1.3% vs 6.9 ± 1.3%) and higher prevalence of CKD (23.9%vs. 20.8%) and HF (23.1%vs. 15%)- p < 0.005 for all comparisons.

Patients prescribed GLP-1RAs were younger (65.4 ± 9.4 vs. 70.9 ± 9.4 years), more often female (42%vs. 38%), and more frequently non-Hispanic White (58%vs. 56%) with private insurance (36.7%vs. 18.9%) and higher BMI (37.2 ± 6.8 vs 29.9 ± 6.2). They had higher prevalence of HTN (95.3%vs. 94.4%), HLD (70.2%vs. 60.7%), CKD (22.7%vs. 21.7%) and obesity (61%vs 42.6%) but lower prevalence of HF (15%vs. 17.4%)- p < 0.005 for all comparisons. Discussion

In this large, contemporary cohort of adults T2D and established ASCVD, prescription rates of SGLT2i (22%) and GLP-RAs (25%) remained low, despite well-documented cardiovascular and renal benefits. This aligns with prior reports showing a gap between guideline recommendations and real-world practice [6,7]. Our analysis adds nuance by assessing trends across CVD subtypes, sex and demographic and socioeconomic factors, highlighting disparities and barriers to care.

Underutilization of cardioprotective therapies

Under-prescription of GLP-1RAS and SGLT2i is concerning given their proven efficacy in reducing major cardiovascular events (MACE), heart failure hospitalizations, and progression of CKD [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. International guidelines—including the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [2], European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [3], and American College of Cardiology (ACC)4—now indeed recommend these agents for T2D patients with ASCVD, regardless of glycemic control. Yet our study reveals that fewer than one in four eligible patients are receiving these therapies. The high prevalence of statin (85.6%) and metformin (52.7%) prescriptions in our cohort suggests that the study population was actively engaged in cardiovascular care and that the low uptake of SGLT2i and GLP-1RAs does not appear to reflect a general failure in managing high-risk patients, but rather a class-specific implementation gap.

Several factors may contribute to this therapeutic inertia: clinical conservatism, cost-related concerns, lack of familiarity among some providers, limited formulary access in certain settings, and perceived complexities around initiation and titration. These issues may be further amplified in populations with limited health literacy, financial barriers, or reduced healthcare access.

Demographic and socioeconomic disparities in prescription patterns

Our findings suggest sex-specific prescribing trends, with men more likely to receive SGLT2i and women more likely to receive GLP-1RAs, even after adjusting for comorbidities and socioeconomic variables . The higher GLP-1RAs use among women, especially those with obesity and private insurance, may reflect the increased emphasis on weight loss benefits, or possible provider biases or differential marketing, with providers potentially associating GLP-1RAs more with weight-centric management in women rather than cardiovascular risk reduction.

SGLT2i use favored younger, male patients with private insurance and those with comorbid CKD or HF—conditions for which these medications are strongly indicated. Older adults and Medicare beneficiaries, who also benefit greatly, were underrepresented, possibly due to formulary restrictions, prior authorization hurdles, or concerns about side effects in frail individuals. This gap represents a missed opportunity for cardiovascular risk reduction.

Racial and ethnic differences

GLP-1RAs usage was marginally higher in non-Hispanic White, Black, and Hispanic patients compared to Asian patients. These patterns may stem from access differences, cultural attitudes, and implicit provider biases. These disparities warrant further qualitative and systems-level investigation, especially in light of growing concerns regarding equity in diabetes care [13]

Clinical implications and targeted interventions

Our data suggest that both GLP-1RAs and SGLT2i are prescribed reactively, in more complex or comorbid patients (e.g., those with existing CKD, HF, or poor glycemic control), rather than proactively for earlier secondary prevention. This reactive approach reduces the potential for preventing disease progression.

Additionally, the inverse relationship between insurance type and prescription rates (higher use among privately insured) highlights the economic barriers to accessing these medications. Policy reforms—such as price negotiations, generic options, and value-based formularies—are essential to promote equitable access [14].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design prevents establishing definitive temporality; thus, some therapies may have been initiated for primary prevention or glycemic control prior to a recorded ASCVD event. Second, the study period (2016–2023) was dictated by the inception of our institutional registry. Because this timeframe includes years prior to major guideline updates for HF and CKD, our results may underestimate the most recent prescribing trends. Third, while residual confounding by indication remains possible, the observed sex disparities likely represent entrenched prescribing habits, as HF, CKD, and obesity were not formal indications for these classes during the early years of the registry. Finally, as a single-center study, these findings reflect the specific formulary and provider practices of one institution and may not be fully generalizable.

Conclusion

Despite strong evidence and guideline support, use of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2i for secondary cardiovascular prevention in T2D remains low, with marked sex and socioeconomic disparities. Closing this gap will require coordinated action from clinicians, health systems, payers, and policymakers to ensure equitable access to these therapies.
