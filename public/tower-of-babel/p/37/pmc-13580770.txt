Molecular and Cell Biological Characterization of Patient‐Derived Head and Neck Squamous Carcinoma Cell Lines

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a major cancer among head and neck malignancies with a limited availability of effective molecularly targeted therapies. The underlying oncogenic mechanisms, however, remain poorly understood. To investigate patient‐specific molecular targets, we established patient‐derived cell (PDC) lines from surgically resected HNSCC tissue samples. We performed comprehensive analyses, including driver gene mutation profiling using a multigene panel, transcriptomic profiling, and karyotyping. Various cancer‐associated genomic alterations, primarily copy number gains, were identified, and these findings were consistent with those of previous studies. Notably, the amplification of EGFR, FGFR2, and CCND1 was associated with their overexpression, suggesting potential tumor driver roles. A PDC with a PIK3CA activating mutation was sensitive to the PI3K inhibitor Alpelisib. We also demonstrated that PDCs harboring chromosome segregation errors were vulnerable to KIF18A deletion and pharmacological inhibition. These results support the value of HNSCC‐derived PDCs as a platform for advances in precision medicine in oncology research.Keywords: chromosome segregation errors, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, patient‐derived cell, precision medicine, TCGA

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a major cancer in the region (approximately 90%) and more than 700,000 cases are newly diagnosed (International Agency for Research on Cancer. Global Cancer Observatory: Cancer Today. Lyon, France: IARC; 2022. Available from: https://gco.iarc.fr/today), with an estimated 355,000 disease‐related deaths per year (GLOBOCON2022), owing to the limited availability of effective molecularly targeted therapies. While the underlying oncogenic mechanisms are gradually being elucidated, our current understanding of the disease remains incomplete. The disease etiology can be deciphered based on the different HNSCC sites of origin observed in patients from various geographic regions. Oropharyngeal SCC, caused by human papillomavirus, is highly prevalent in Western Europe and North America [1]. In Japan, the hypopharyngeal region is the second most frequent site of origin for primary HNSCC, following the oral cavity, and East Asians suffer from hypopharyngeal SCC more frequently than others.

One of the critical problems in the therapeutics of HNSCC is the delay in the initial diagnosis; most cases (50%–60%) have already progressed to stage III or beyond at the time of diagnosis [2]. Following surgical removal and radiation therapy, more than 50% of patients experience recurrence, and salvage surgeries are usually not feasible. Another critical problem is the lack of targeted chemotherapy. The most effective and widely applied treatment for HNSCC is the PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade. The KEYNOTE‐048 study showed that the median overall survival was nearly five months longer for the “pembrolizumab alone” group as compared with the “cetuximab with chemotherapy” group [3]. Since then, many clinical trials have used a variety of augmentation methods for PD‐1/PD‐L1blockade or targeted alternative checkpoints for metastatic HNSCCs [4]. Another major molecular target in HNSCC is EGFR; however, cetuximab (an anti‐EGFR monoclonal antibody) is currently the only approved drug available for use in relapsed or metastatic HNSCC in Japan. The overall response rate to cetuximab in HNSCC treatment is approximately 10% [5].

Currently, several synthetic lethal reagents have been successfully used to treat various malignant neoplasms [6]. For example, poly ADP‐ribose polymerase inhibitors have shown significant effects on BRCA1/2 mutated cancers. Similarly, cancers showing a homologous deletion of the methylthioadenosine phosphorylase gene at chromosome 9p21 could be targeted using antagonists for arginine methyltransferase 5 [7]. Recently, Kinesin family member 18A (KIF18A) has become an attractive target for treating cancers with chromosome segregation errors, a common feature among a variety of cancers [8]. Kinesin is a molecular motor protein that is further segregated into subfamilies, each with different motor domain positions, and plays a major role in intracellular trafficking [9]. KIF18A belongs to the kinesin 8 family and, in lieu of intracellular trafficking, it regulates the length of microtubules, chromosomal congression, and other regulatory functions during mitosis [9]. KIF18A depletion causes hyperoscillations and segregation errors in mitotic chromosomes [9]. It is well known that anti‐tubulin therapies are effective against cancers with chromosome segregation errors. However, these therapeutic agents lead to intractable adverse effects such as neurotoxicity and bone marrow suppression, due to which anti‐tubulin therapy is frequently discontinued. Multiple preclinical studies have suggested that anti‐KIF18A therapy efficiently suppresses the proliferation of cancer cell lines with chromosome segregation errors [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Several anti‐KIF18A agents are currently being tested in clinical trials against HNSCC (NCT06799065, NCT06084416, NCT05902988, and NCT06772415, searched on October 20th, 2025, at https://clinicaltrials.gov/).

To identify an appropriate chemotherapeutic agent for a patient, patient‐derived xenografts are an attractive method for drug screening [14]. Here, we characterized two cell lines established in‐house and three American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) HNSCC cell lines for their genomic alterations, transcriptomes, and chromosomal instabilities. We then investigated the vulnerability of the in‐house established cell lines to the “actionable mutations” identified by the combination of comprehensive genomic profiling and RNA‐seq analyses. Finally, we tested the inhibition of KIF18A as a potential therapeutic strategy for HNSCC using the in‐house cell lines.

Materials and Methods

Samples and Cell Culture Conditions

Cell lines used in this study are listed in Table 1. HPCM1 and HPCM2 are squamous cell carcinoma cell lines derived from the xenografts and established in‐house [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. All cell lines (Table 1) were cultured in media, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Target Genomic Sequencing (ACTOnco Comprehensive Cancer Panel)

To comprehensively obtain driver mutations of cancer‐related genes, we outsourced the ACTOnco Comprehensive Cancer Panel [19] to Cannon Medical Systems (Otawara, Tochigi, Japan); 5 × 106 cells of each cell line were sent for continuous cell culture expansion. Extracted genomic DNA was used for library construction and next‐generation sequencing. The obtained sequencing data were analyzed by ACT Genomics (Taipei, Taiwan) as described by Chan YC et al. and Amarasinghe KC [19, 20]. We also delineated the mutational signatures of single‐nucleotide alterations using an R package, deconstructSigs [21].

Cytogenetics

We outsourced cytogenetics to H. U. Frontiers (Tokyo, Japan). We placed HPCM1 and HPCM2 in different 25 square centimeter flasks at 40% confluence at room temperature, following which they underwent continuous culture. After standard Giemsa staining and determination of the number of chromosomes in 50 mitotic cells, 10 cells were subjected to karyotyping.

Immunofluorescence Imaging

Immunofluorescence was performed as previously described [22]. We used mouse monoclonal anti‐α‐tubulin (B5‐1‐2, Cat. #T6074; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and an anti‐centromere antibody (CREST sera, Cat. #15–234‐0001; Antibodies Inc., Davis, CA, USA) for primary antibodies. The following secondary antibodies were used: Anti‐mouse Alexa‐Fluor‐633 and goat anti‐human IgG Alexa‐Fluor‐488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The cells were seeded in 12‐well plates as follows: First, we sterilized φ 15 mm cover glasses with 70% ethanol and dried them in the hood for 15 min. Cover glasses were put in 12 wells and coated with a 3 mg/mL collagen type I solution obtained from bovine skin (ASC‐1‐100; Nippi, Tokyo, Japan). The cover glasses were washed twice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and once with culture medium. Each cell line was plated on collagen‐coated cover glasses (1 × 105 cells/well) 2 days prior. Cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Permeabilization was performed in Dulbecco's PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 (PBST) for 5 min. After the permeabilization, the cells were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (WAKO #019–15,123) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, washed once with PBST, incubated with the primary antibody (1,000‐fold dilution for anti‐α‐tubulin and 200‐fold dilution for CREST) at room temperature for one hour, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for an additional hour. For double staining, the slides were washed with PBST and incubated with another primary antibody, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI: 340–07971, Wako, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured using a confocal laser fluorescence microscope (Nikon AI, Tokyo, JP). The images were then processed using ImageJ software (https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.2089) by cropping and counting the mitotic index (50–200 cells/experiment).

RNA‐Seq and Comparison With Copy Numbers of Oncogenes

We expanded the 5 cell lines as follows: HPCM1: 5.43 × 106 , HPCM2: 2.45 × 106, HSC‐3: 3.02 × 106, FaDu: 1.70 × 106, and BICR6: 3.60 × 106. The cells were trypsinized, and total RNA was extracted using Maxwell simplyRNA Cells Ki (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was quantified with Qubit RNA Broad Range (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 35–60 μg of total RNAs were obtained. Total RNA from each cell line (20 μg) was subjected to RNA‐seq analysis, which was outsourced to Rhelixa (Tokyo, Japan), including the mapping and calculation of transcripts per million (TPM) of mRNAs. To estimate the reproducibility of our data, we compared the transcriptomes of protein‐coding gene expression (above the average TPMs) of our RNA‐seq data of the three ATCC‐derived cell lines and those of corresponding cell line data in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia [23, 24], and the Pearson correlation coefficients of BIRC6, FaDu, and HSC ‐ 3 cell lines were 0.612, 0.642, and 0.621, respectively (see Doc S1). According to the previous report, most of the cell lines showed a moderate correlation coefficient (around 0.6) between different batches of identical cell lines [25] so that our RNA‐seq data can be compatible with the previous studies.

We compared the genomic copy numbers of oncogenes in the five cell lines with their expression. First, we extracted the TPM data for the target genes of the ACTOnco panel using a shell script. We then categorized copy number alterations as low (less than two copies per cell), neutral (two copies per cell), moderate (up to five copies per cell), and high (more than five copies per cell), and compared the TPMs of each category.

To compare the RNA‐seq data of our cell lines with The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) HNSCC cases, we downloaded the TPM dataset for head and neck carcinoma from the GDC Application Programming Interface by the use of Tange, S with modifications [26]. We separately downloaded the clinical features of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma samples from TCGA data and obtained the clinical data of 531 cases. The purity of TCGA tumors was estimated using the ESTIMATE algorithm [27]. To calculate the tumor purity score, we downloaded the ESTIMATE_score from https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/estimate/index.html and obtained the score for 522 HNSCC cases. For the comparison of transcriptomes with our cell lines, we used TCGA samples with a tumor purity score > 0.7. We then removed transcripts that were not expressed in three of the five cell lines. The merged TPM dataset was subjected to the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis using the uwot package (Melville J. uwot: The UMAP Method for Dimensionality Reduction. R package version 0.2.3, <https://CRAN.R‐project.org/package=uwot>, 2025) with default options. The scripts for these analyses were described in Doc S1.

siRNA Transfection

The siRNA designed for human KIF18A mRNA was provided by Japan Bio Services Co. Ltd. (Saitama, Japan), and double‐stranded short RNA was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. siRNA (100 nM) was applied using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in a 6 wells/plate in Opti‐MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cell Biological Analyses: Chromosome Counting and Cell Growth Analyses

To enrich the metaphase cells, we synchronized the cell cycle as described by Shindo et al. [22]. Before reaching confluence, proliferating cells were treated with 7.5 μM S‐Trityl‐L‐cysteine (an Eg5 inhibitor; Tocris Biosciences, Bristol, UK) for 4 h. Mitotic cells were collected, treated with water for hypotonic swelling, and subsequently fixed using freshly prepared Carnoy's fixative (methanol: acetic acid = 3:1). The fixation process was repeated thrice to ensure proper chromosome preservation. The fixed cells were then spread onto glass slides. After drying, the cells were subjected to Giemsa staining with 5% Giemsa solution (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan). Chromosomes of each cell line (20–50 cells of each cell line) were manually counted using ImageJ software.

We used the following molecular targeting drugs to test the efficacy of the cell lines for the selection of chemotherapeutic agents: Cetuximab for EGFR amplification, alpelisib for PIK3CA activation mutation, palbociclib for CCND1 amplification or overexpression, a KIF18A inhibitor, BTB1 for chromosome segregation errors, and paclitaxel as a positive control at various concentrations. For cell growth analysis, cells were plated onto 96‐well plates at a density of 3,000–5,000 cells/well. Relative cell numbers were determined by live‐cell imaging using IncuCyte ZOOM (Sartorius Göttingen, Germany) [28, 29].

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle was performed as previously described [30]. Briefly, bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU)‐pulsed cells were fixed in 70% ethanol and denatured with 2 N HCl and 0.5% TritonX‐100, followed by neutralization with 0.1 M sodium tetraborate (pH 8.5). The treated cells were stained with an anti‐BrdU antibody conjugated to FITC (364,104, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and 7‐AAD (42,403, BioLegend). The analysis was performed using a Spectral Cell Analyzer SA3800 (SONY, Tokyo, Japan). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.1 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses data and bar graphs of cell growth were generated using the GraphPad Prism software (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Cell growth was compared using Student's t‐test.

Results

Genetic Alterations of the Five HNC Cell Lines

The genetic alterations of the five cell lines are summarized in Figure 1 and Table S1. We defined amplification as five copies, as suggested by the manufacturer of ACTOnco, a comprehensive cancer gene panel. All five cell lines showed gene amplifications, but FGFR1 was only amplified in HPCM2 cells. The CCND1 gene was amplified in the four other cell lines. The three ATCC cell lines shared amplified genes (CCND1, FGF19, FGF3, NOTCH1, and RXRA), suggesting that these cell lines are more similar to each other than the two cell lines established in‐house. EGFR amplification was observed in HSC‐3 and HPCM1 cells. MRE11 was homozygously deleted in one cell line (HPCM2). In terms of short nucleotide changes, 57 possible driver mutants were detected in all five cell lines (Table S2). We observed a deep deletion of the tumor suppressor gene SMAD4 in HPCM2 and FaDu cells. Only one apparent activating oncogene short nucleotide alteration, p.Glu545Lys of the PIK3CA gene, was observed in HPCM2 cells. Regarding the inactivation of tumor suppressor genes, we observed truncation mutations in the TP53 gene in all five cell lines. In addition, at least one copy of the CDKN2A gene was inactivated by truncation mutations in HPCM2 and FaDu cells. A summary of the short variants identified in these cell lines is shown in Table 2.

We also obtained mutational signature data from short variants in cell lines [31] (Table S3). Interestingly, each cell line exhibited a unique mutational signature. Among the five cell lines, BICR6 and HSC‐3 were more similar to each other than to the other three. Both cell lines shared homologous recombination defects (SBS3), mismatch repair defects (SBSs 6 and 15 for BICR6; and SBSs 21 and 26 for HSC‐3), and base excision repair defects (SBS30). HPCM1 cells also exhibited mismatch repair defect‐related signatures (SBSs 6 and 15). Most (94%) of the mutational signature exhibited by FaDu cells was SBS1 (aging). Meanwhile, HPCM2 cells exclusively harbored SBS16, which is of unknown origin with respect to the mutational mechanism.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

We observed multiple gene amplifications in most HNSCC cell lines. To investigate the association between DNA copy numbers and mRNA expression, we obtained transcriptome data from the five cell lines and checked the correlation between the DNA copy numbers of the genes analyzed with the ACTOnco assay. We categorized the gene copy number data obtained from ACTOnco into four categories (see Methods) and compared the average TPMs among these categories. Figure 2A shows that the average TPMs correlated with the DNA copy number categories, suggesting that increasing DNA copy number caused an increase in gene expression, although several outliers were found in the moderate or neutral categories. It is also important to note that some of the “amplified” category genes showed very low expression (Figure 2A). To identify which amplified genes correlated with higher mRNA levels, we examined the relationship between DNA copy numbers and TPMs (Figure 2B). Strikingly, we found that most of the amplified genes were not overexpressed, and only a few genes, such as CCND1 and EGFR, showed higher expression than the other genes (Figure 2B). Interestingly, FGF19 was amplified similarly to its adjacent gene CCND1 but was not as highly expressed as CCND1 (Figure 2B).

To investigate whether the cell lines preserved the transcriptomic traits of the origins of primary organs, we compared the RNA‐seq data with those of the TCGA project. We obtained TPMs of 60,661 transcripts for 569 HNSCC cases and analyzed similarities among the clinical subsites of HNSCCs. We found that the cell lines aggregated in the UMAP scatter plot and the TCGA HNSCC samples were dispersed without any clustering, whereas the five cell lines gathered very close to each other, indicating that the huge difference of the complexity of mRNA sources of clinical samples, including stromal cells and immune cells as well as tumor propter, and culturing cells that consist of single cell clones adapted to the in vitro culturing condition (Figure S1A). To determine the correlation between the transcriptomes of the five cell lines and TCGA HNSCC subsite samples, we calculated Spearman's correlation coefficient. There were no statistically significant differences in the correlations among the TCGA HNSCC subtypes and the five cell lines' transcriptomes; however, we found that four out of five cell lines showed the strongest correlation of RNA‐seq data to the TCGA HNSCC cases of their original subsite, the hypopharynx, except for HSC‐3 (Figure S1B).

Specificity and Sensitivity to the Molecular Targeting Drugs of the Patient‐Derived Cell Lines

Testing chemotherapeutic agents is critical for the development of patient‐derived cell lines. We found that CDK4/6 is a potential actionable target for all cell lines because they showed either CCND1 amplification [32, 33] or CDKN2A loss‐of‐function mutations [34, 35, 36, 37]. Palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, significantly suppressed the proliferation of HPCM1 and HPCM2 cells (Figure 3A), as well as an ATCC‐derived cell line, BICR6 (Figure S3). The other ATCC‐derived cell lines, FaDu and HSC3, are also known to be vulnerable to the CCND1 suppression [38, 39]. The EGFR and PIK3CA are known as actionable targets for solid tumors, and we found those genetic alterations in our in‐house cell lines (amplification of EGFR for HPCM1 and mutation of PIK3CA for HPCM2). Consistent with these findings, we observed statistically significant growth suppression in HPCM1 by 400 μg/mL of cetuximab, an anti‐EGFR agent that is approved by the Japanese health insurance system [40, 41], compared with HPCM2 cells (Figure 3B). Similarly, HPCM2 cell growth was suppressed by alpelisib, a potent inhibitor of the PI‐3 kinase [42], by 1 μM compared with the non‐PIK3CA mutated cell line, HPCM1 (Figure 3C).

Chromosome Segregation Errors of the Five HNSCC Cell Lines

The variation in gene copy numbers detected by ActOnco prompted us to check the chromosomal stability of the five cell lines. First, we examined the karyotypes of the two HNSCC cell lines (Figure 4A). Both cell lines showed unstable chromosome numbers when expert cytologists counted 10 mitotic cells in each cell line. In HPCM1 cells, 50–55 chromosomes were observed (average = 53.1), and in HPCM2 cells, 54–95 chromosomes were observed (average = 86.8). We also observed several marker chromosomes in both karyotypes (Figure 4A). To estimate the extent of chromosome segregation errors of the five cell lines, we counted the number of chromosomes at the metaphase of the five cell lines (Figure 4B). All the cell lines showed a variety of chromosomal numbers, indicating chromosome segregation errors. Among these, FaDu and HPCM2 cells showed the highest coefficients of variation. Representative images of abnormal mitosis observed in these five cell lines, including multipolar spindles, chromosomal misalignment, and bridging, are shown in Figure S2.

KIF18A As a New Therapeutic Target for HNSCC and Its Effects on the Five Cell Lines

Recent reports suggest that KIF18A is an attractive target for treating malignancies with chromosome segregation errors, including HNSCC [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Because these five cell lines showed unstable chromosome numbers, we speculated that anti‐KIF18A therapy would be effective in these cell lines. We used siRNA to perturb KIF18A in the five cell lines and observed a substantial reduction in KIF18A expression in all cell lines (Figure S4A). Cell growth analysis indicated that four cell lines (BICR6, FaDu, HPCM1, and HSC‐3) were vulnerable to the perturbation of KIF18A expression (Figure S4B). Immunofluorescence analyses of mitotic morphology revealed various mitotic abnormalities (Figure S2A,B). All cell lines showed a decrease in normal mitotic events due to the perturbation of KIF18A expression, especially BICR6 and FaDu cells (Figure S4C,D). HPCM1, HPCM2, and HSC‐3 cells also showed moderately decreased normal mitosis (Figure S4C,D).

We tested whether commercially available KIF18A inhibitors had similar growth‐suppressive effects on the five HNSCC cell lines. Suppression of cell growth in all HNSCC cell lines, except for HPCM2, was induced by BTB1, a KIF18A inhibitor (Figure 5A). BTB1 induced slight growth suppression of HPCM1 cells comparable to the perturbation of KIF18A expression by siRNA (Figure 5A and Figure S3B). Similarly, another KIF18A inhibitor, sovilnesib, induced growth suppression in the three ATCC cell lines at a concentration of 100 nM, but it did not suppress (p < 0.05) cell growth of the HPCM1 and HPCM2 cell lines as strongly (Figure 5B). These results were consistent with the growth suppression due to the perturbation of KIF18A expression (Figure S4B). Paclitaxel, an inhibitor of mitotic cell division, was used as the positive control. Figure S5 shows that all five cell lines, including HPCM1 and HPCM2, showed suppressed cell growth after paclitaxel treatment, which corresponds to the fact that paclitaxel is commonly used for HNSCC chemotherapy.

Anti‐KIF18A Treatment Induced Mitotic Arrest of the HNSCC Cell Lines to Various Extents

Previous reports have indicated that anti‐KIF18A treatment induces programmed cell death and mitotic delays [10, 11, 13]. Fluorescence‐activated cell sorting analyses revealed that typical mitotic arrest was induced by the two KIF18A inhibitors, BTB1 and sovilnesib, in all HNSCC cell lines except HPCM2 (Figure 5C,D). The two KIF18A inhibitors showed relatively modest effects on another in‐house‐established cell line, HPCM1 (Figure 5C,D). In contrast, the cell cycle progression of the three ATCC cell lines was significantly affected by anti‐KIF18A treatment (Figure 5C). For example, BTB1 induced a 1.16 to 2.5 fold increase in the G2/M phase in BICR6, FaDu, and HSC‐3 cells, whereas only 0.9 and 1.05 fold increases were observed in the G2/M phase in HPCM1 and HPCM2 cells, respectively (Figure 5C,D, and Figure S5). Similarly, another KIF18A inhibitor, sovilnesib, induced mitotic arrest in BICR6, FaDu, and HSC‐3 cells, and HPCM1 cells were moderately affected by the drug; however, HPCM2 cells did not show mitotic arrest by sovilnesib (Figure 5C,D, and Figure S5). The sub‐G1 phase, which represents programmed cell death, was induced in most of the cell lines by KIF18A inhibitors (Figure S6).

Discussion

This study characterized the genomic, transcriptomic, and chromosomal instabilities of two in‐house established HNSCC cell lines, HPCM1 and HPCM2. We also identified one “actionable” genetic alteration of each cell line: EGFR amplification in HPCM1 and PIK3CA mutation in HPCM2. The molecular targeting agents for each cell line successfully suppressed their respective cell growth. Moreover, KIF18A is considered a candidate molecular target for cancers with chromosome segregation errors, and these cell lines also exhibited unstable numbers of chromosomes among cells. Perturbation of KIF18A expression and a specific inhibitor of KIF18A successfully suppressed the cell growth of HPCM1 and the other three HNSCC cell lines, but not of HPCM2 (Figure 6, Graphical Abstract).

Genetic changes in the two in‐house and three ATCC‐derived HNSCC cell lines showed similar patterns, although the two in‐house cell lines were developed through in vivo selection of stemness by the serial xenograft on heavily immunocompromised NOG mice [15, 18]. For example, all five cell lines had truncation mutations in the TP53 gene and dysregulation of the cell cycle, such as amplification of CCND1 or inactivation of CDKN2A. These genetic disorders may cause chromosome segregation errors in the five HNSCC cell lines, owing to a lack of coordinated cell cycle progression. These four cell lines exhibited multiple gene amplification. However, transcriptomic analyses revealed that not all amplified genes were overexpressed. Our results suggest that both genomic and transcriptome data are critical for identifying the true cancer‐driver amplification among genes showing an increased copy number.

It is a well‐known fact that mitotic errors cause chromosomal structural abnormalities in cancer cells [43]. It is a question why the HPCM2 did not show infrequent abnormalities of gene copy numbers compared with the other cell lines, whose chromosomal numbers are more stable than HPCM2, although there are many “marker chromosomes” in the HPCM2 (Figure 4A) that may be consequences of chromosomal breaks and gene amplifications or losses caused by mitotic errors. We speculate the reasons for this inconsistency as two factors. Firstly, the ACTOnco does not cover the amplified/lost genes in the HPCM2. The ACTOnco covers 409 genes as its target [19] but it may be possible that the other cancer‐related genes may show copy‐number alterations in the HPCM2 cells. The other possibility is that the highly unstable chromosomal segregation of HPCM2 may cause a selection pressure for balanced genomic materials for its daughter cells. Actually, numerical instability of chromosomes may be deleterious for cancer cells, and recently, “just‐right” chromosomal instability would be important for tumorigenesis (see review by Hosea et al. [44]). So, the numerical instability of chromosomes of HPCM2 might not allow the cell to have the frequent numeric abnormalities of cancer‐related genes.

Whether the established cell lines continuously retain the characteristics of their original organs is an open question. Yu et al. compared RNA‐seq data of 8282 primary tumors from TCGA with 666 cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle) across 22 overlapping tumor origins and found that most of the cell lines had similar characteristics to the TCGA data that showed high tumor contents in the samples [45]. Yu et al. analyzed the three ATCC cell lines (BICR6, FaDu, and HSC‐3) used in this study. We attempted similar approaches to determine whether the RNA‐seq data exhibited similarities at the subsite level, but they were unsuccessful (Figure S1). The five cell lines exhibited closely related distributions on the UMAP plot, suggesting that adaptation to cell culture conditions may cause convergence of the transcriptome to a common feature.

Genetic and transcriptomic analyses revealed differences in transcription efficiency among the amplified genes. Previous studies showed that not all the amplified genes in an amplicon show an increase in gene expression [46, 47]. Mahieu et al. showed that FGF19, FGF4, and FGF3 are expressed at low or undetectable levels in tumors with a weak correlation between expression and copy numbers in TCGA HNSCC cancers, whereas CCND1 showed high mRNA expression in those cancers [39]. The authors also showed that the HNSCC cell line FaDu is not dependent on the three FGF genes. These data are compatible with our results. We also showed that the more efficiently expressed genes (CCND1 and EGFR) could be actionable driver genes in HNSCCs (Figure 3A,B). These results suggest that transcriptome analyses of cancer samples are critical for determining the actionable amplified oncogenes for HNSCCs and the feasibility of patient‐derived cancer cell lines for screening chemotherapeutic agents for patients, although the procedures are somewhat labor‐intensive for clinical application.

The present study also suggests that KIF18A is a potential therapeutic target for HNSCCs with chromosome segregation errors. Several KIF18A inhibitors are currently being tested in clinical trials. All five HNSCC cell lines tested in this study showed chromosome segregation errors (Figure 4), four of which were vulnerable to both KIF18A siRNA and the KIF18A inhibitors BTB1 and sovilnesib (Figure 5). Interestingly, the growth of HPCM2 cells was not affected by either the siRNA or BTB1. Previous studies have indicated that mitotic checkpoint defects can confer resistance to anti‐KIF18A therapy [10, 11, 13] although there are no clinically testable markers for the defect of the mitotic spindle checkpoint. Hence, it is possible that HPCM2 depends on other molecules for chromosomal congression during the M phase because the expression of KIF18A protein was relatively low in HPCM2 cells compared with that in the other four cell lines (Figure S4A). Alternatively, the relatively low expression of the spindle checkpoint genes in the HPCM2 cells may be related to the resistance to anti‐KIF18A therapy (Table S4). Further studies are required to elucidate the mechanism of anti‐KIF18A resistance in HPCM2 cells.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cell lines was small, resulting in low statistical power. Second, there is a possibility that in‐house cell lines (HPCM1 and HPCM2) may have changed their biological characteristics since they were initially established by Imai et al. [15]. Although these cell lines have been used extensively in our laboratories to characterize a gene critical for the stemness of hypopharyngeal cancer cells, CD271 [15, 17, 18, 48], these cell lines consistently depended on the CD271 function during the previous studies. Therefore, the effect of repeated culturing on these cell lines may be minimal.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that HNSCC patient‐derived cells could be a valuable platform for precision medicine in oncology research.
