Diagnostic report generation for macular diseases by natural language processing algorithms
Authors: Xufeng Zhao, Chunshi Li, Jingyuan Yang, Xingwang Gu, Bing Li, Yuelin Wang, Bi-lei Zhang, Xirong Li, Jianchun Zhao, Jie Wang, Weihong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo investigate rule-based and deep learning (DL)-based methods for the automatically generating natural language diagnostic reports for macular diseases.MethodsThis diagnostic study collected the ophthalmic images of 2261 eyes from 1303 patients. Colour fundus photographs and optical coherence tomography images were obtained. Eyes without retinal diseases as well as eyes diagnosed with four macular diseases were included. For each eye, a diagnostic report was written with a format consisting of lesion descriptions, diagnoses and recommendations. Subsequently, a rule-based natural language processing (NLP) and a DL-based NLP system were developed to automatically generate a diagnostic report. To assess the effectiveness of these models, two junior ophthalmologists wrote diagnostic reports for the collected images independently. A questionnaire was designed and judged by two retina specialists to grade each report’s readability, correctness of diagnosis, lesion description and recommendations.ResultsThe rule-based NLP reports achieved higher grades over junior ophthalmologists in correctness of diagnosis (9.13±1.52 vs 9.03±1.42 points) and recommendations (8.55±2.74 vs 8.50±2.53 points). Furthermore, the DL-based NLP reports got slightly lower grades to those of junior ophthalmologists in lesion description (8.82±1.84 vs 9.12±1.20 points, p<0.05), correctness of diagnosis (8.72±2.36 vs 9.08±1.55 points, p<0.05) and recommendations (8.81±2.52 vs 9.15±1.65 points, p<0.05). For readability, the DL-based reports performed better than junior ophthalmologists, with scores of 9.98±0.17 vs 9.94±0.25 points (p=0.094).ConclusionsThe multimodal AI system, coupled with the NLP algorithm, has demonstrated competence in generating reports for four macular diseases compared with junior ophthalmologists.Keywords: Diagnostic tests/Investigation, Macula, Retina

Introduction

Natural language processing (NLP) stands as a pivotal subfield within computer science, centred on the analysis of natural language data and the development of computer systems capable of comprehending, recognising and generating human language.1 In its early stages, NLP operated through rule-based mechanisms, where predefined sets of rules guided its functionalities. It shows practical advantages in dealing with well-structured input data governed by predefined rules, such as medical documents.2 However, these rule-based systems encountered limitations due to their dependence on predefined rules, eventually giving way to more intricate strategies.3 4 Concurrently, the advent of deep learning (DL) heralded the next era of NLP, integrating the prowess of DL technologies with formidable computational capabilities and wide-ranging applications.5 Rule-based and DL-based NLP excels in processing vast datasets with minimal constraints on input data. Its appeal lies in fields such as ophthalmology due to its flexibility and adaptability. Both these paradigms have found utility in ophthalmology, especially in the realm of text processing.6

The development of ophthalmic examination equipment and artificial intelligence (AI) technology has significantly facilitated the capability and accuracy of ocular assessments within larger populations.7 It has made screening for retinal diseases cost-effective in clinical practice.8 9 Nevertheless, the difficulties in comprehending AI-generated outcomes and providing suitable treatment suggestions without expertise highlight the importance of developing algorithmic models that generate easily understandable reports.

Given these attributes, NLP technology serves as a conduit between AI outcomes and primary eye clinic personnel by translating AI-generated results into comprehensible language with high efficiency. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the feasibility of a multimodal approach for automatically generating natural language diagnostic reports for macular diseases. We investigated both the rule-based and DL-based methodologies to accomplish this goal.

Methods

Collection of colour fundus photographs (CFP) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) images

This prospective diagnostic study successively collected retina images of patients diagnosed with four major macular diseases including epiretinal membrane (ERM), dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD), neovascular AMD (nAMD) and diabetic retinopathy (DR) who visited the department of ophthalmology at Peking Union Medical College Hospital from July 2020 to December 2020 and underwent OCT examinations using the Topcon 3D OCT-1 Maestro (Topcon, Japan). Additionally, eyes without retinal diseases that underwent the same examinations during the same period were also included. Scans with poor image quality (image quality<70) were excluded from the dataset. In total, 2261 eyes from 1303 patients were included in this study. Each subject’s scan comprised 12 OCT B scans (6 mm cross-sectional radial scan pattern) and 1 CFP captured by Topcon 3D OCT-1 Maestro (Topcon, Japan).

In total, 10 ophthalmologists, each possessing 2-year experience in retinal diseases, wrote diagnostic reports for the eyes included in the study. These reports were structured with lesion descriptions, diagnoses and recommendations. Additionally, 10% reports were randomly selected to conduct a quality control by two experts specialising in retinal diseases.

Rule-based NLP report generation

In order to detect retinal lesions and diseases from CFP and OCT images, three pretrained neural networks, that is, Lesion-Net, uncertainty-based teacher plus (UbT+) and multimodal convolutional neural network (MM-CNN), were employed in this study.10,12 First, for lesion detection in CFP images, we adopt Lesion-Net, a fully convolutional image segmentation network specifically designed and trained for retinal lesion segmentation.10 By effectively learning from annotations with imprecise boundaries and reducing false alarms for small lesions, Lesion-Net outputs segmentation of varied lesions, such as microaneurysm, intraretinal haemorrhage, hard exudate, drusen, etc. Second, for lesion detection in OCT B-scan images, we employ UbT+, which trains a Faster R-CNN-based object detection network in a semisupervised manner, enabling learning from a few hundred of labelled OCT images and thousands of unlabelled OCT data.11 13 UbT+ can recognise up to nine lesions including varied types of pigment epithelial detachment, subretinal fluid, macular hole, etc. At last, MM-CNN is a multimodal convolutional neural network that performs multilabel retinal disease recognition.12 Specifically, in order to fully exploit the multimodal input (consisting of a CFP image and an array of 12 OCT B-scan images), the MM-CNN network has a unique multimodal multiple instance learning (MM-MIL) module. Viewing each subimage autocropped from the CFP image and each OCT B-scan as instances, MM-MIL aggregates the multimodal instance-level features in a weighted manner, with the weights calculated by an attention mechanism and thus reflecting the contribution of each instance to the final prediction. In order to ensure the consistency among the outputs of the three networks, a multimodal AI post-fusion is performed as follows. With our domain knowledge about how a specific lesion is clinically related to a specific disease, we built a lesion-to-disease mapping. Based on the mapping, lesions detected yet not related to the predicted disease(s) are automatically removed from the result. Such a rule-based post-fusion ensures zero conflict in the disease and lesion predictions for subsequent diagnostic report generation. After the multimodal post-fusing, a final output of lesions and diagnoses for each scan was obtained. A collection of rules was established to include all potential AI-generated outputs. Lesions identified in OCT/CFP images were correlated with corresponding descriptions, and subsequent diagnoses were linked to treatment recommendations based on predefined rules. For example, the presence of macular drusen would be described as ‘yellowish subretinal lesions’, and a recommendation of ‘observation’ would be provided for a diagnosis of dry AMD. The NLP algorithm then processed the neural network’s final output. After the afore-mentioned steps, a diagnostic report, structured with descriptions of OCT or CFP findings, diagnoses and recommendations, was generated (Figure 1).

Three pretrained deep neural networks were used to detect varied retinal lesions from the CFP/OCT images and predict multiple disease classes from both modalities, respectively. The post-fusion was performed to ensure the coherence between the detected lesions and the predicted diseases. Then a rule-based NLP algorithm aggregated the clinical manifestations of checked lesions and diseases to generate a diagnostic report with descriptions and corresponding treatment recommendations. DL-based NLP report generation

The same pretrained neural networks used in the rule-based NLP report generation for disease prediction and feature extraction from CFP/OCT images were employed. Simultaneously, an MM-MIL module of the disease recognition network calculated the importance of each OCT image. The most crucial OCT images were selected for generating the diagnostic reports with a CFP image. In the subsequent step, the report generation module integrated features of the selected OCT images, the CFP image and the predicted diseases. We repurpose the Show, Attend and Tell (SAT) network,14 a classical image captioning model, for generating natural language descriptions of the fundus images. The SAT network dynamically aligns image features with the sequence of words being generated, enhancing both coherence and interpretability. In this study, we trained two SAT networks, one for CFP and the other for OCT. To enhance alignment between lesion descriptions and diseases, predicted diseases are embedded into the image features and regenerated in the output text. Such a strategy improves consistency between diagnostic findings and natural language descriptions. The final diagnostic report was generated by combining the predicted diseases and the lesion descriptions from both modalities after a coherence check. This report adhered to the identical structure as the reports based on rule-based NLP (Figure 2).

The same pretrained neural networks were used to predict macular diseases and extract features of CFP/OCT images. Using instance attention weights, an MM-MIL module aggregates these features into a unified case-level representation, prioritising the most informative features from both modalities. These attention weights capture the relative significance of each image modality in contributing to the final disease classification. The most weighted OCT image would be selected to generate diagnostic reports. In the second step, features of the CFP, the selected OCT image and the predicted diseases were fed into a report generation module. An SAT network was used as the base network, outputting natural language descriptions of the image. Two SAT networks were used in the report generation module to depict lesions of the two models (CFP/OCT image), respectively. To ensure alignment between lesion descriptions and diseases, predicted diseases are embedded into the image features and regenerated in the output text. This process ensures consistency between diagnostic findings and natural language descriptions. The final diagnostic report would be obtained by combining the predicted diseases and the two-modal lesion descriptions after the sanity check. Evaluation of generated reports

The assessment of NLP reports involved a comparison with the performance of two junior ophthalmologists (considered as the control group) who completed a 1-year training in the diagnosis and treatment of retinal diseases. To quantitatively evaluate the capability of each report in terms of readability, correctness of diagnosis, lesion description and recommendations, two questionnaires (figure 3A for rule-based and figure 3B for DL-based NLP reports) were collaboratively designed and reviewed by two senior ophthalmologists (CL and WY). While the questionnaires were slightly tailored to reflect the distinct output formats of the two models, the core evaluation metrics—including lesion description accuracy, diagnostic correctness, treatment recommendations and readability—were consistently applied. All reports were anonymised to avoid potential bias. Sensitivity and specificity per class were also objectively assessed. Statistical analysis of these metrics was conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.26.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

A questionnaire was designed and cooperatively judged by two retina specialists to quantitatively grade each report ((A) for the rule-based NLP model and (B) for the DL-based NLP model). NLP, natural language processing; DL, deep learning.

Rule-based NLP model performance

The test set for the rule-based NLP model was built with 172 eyes from 127 patients. Compared with the diagnoses by senior ophthalmologists, rule-based NLP reports achieved higher grades in correctness of diagnosis (9.13±1.52 vs 9.03±1.42 points) and recommendations (8.55±2.74 vs 8.50±2.53 points) compared with the control group, but the differences were not statistically significant (p=0.43 and 0.85, respectively). Both groups exhibited satisfactory performance in terms of report readability (9.87±0.56 vs 9.88±0.46 points, p=0.87). The sensitivity and specificity of the rule-based NLP group demonstrated comparable performance to the control group, with average sensitivities of 0.74 and 0.75, respectively (table 2). Both groups achieved satisfactory specificity, with values of 0.94 in the NLP group and 0.97 in the control group. For the diagnosis of nAMD, the rule-based NLP group demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.91 and specificity of 0.99. However, the sensitivity was lower: 0.53 for dry AMD and 0.59 for DR in the NLP group.

DL-based NLP model performance

The DL-based NLP model was developed using a training set of 659 images and a validation set of 215 images. An additional test set comprising 210 images was established to evaluate the performance of the DL-based NLP model. These afore-mentioned images were randomly selected in accordance with the distribution of each macular disease. The reports generated by DL-based NLP demonstrated comparable performance when compared with that of junior ophthalmologists: After being evaluated by senior ophthalmologists, the DL-based NLP model got lower grades than the control group in lesion description (8.82±1.84 vs 9.12±1.20 points, p<0.05), correctness of diagnosis (8.72±2.36 vs 9.08±1.55 points, p<0.05) and recommendations (8.81±2.52 vs 9.15±1.65 points, p<0.05). In terms of readability, the DL-based reports showed better performance compared with the control group (9.98±0.17 vs 9.94±0.25 points), although statistical significance was not reached (p=0.094). DL-based NLP displayed a lower average sensitivity (0.72) in contrast to the control group (0.79), while its average specificity (0.96) was slightly lower than that of the control group (0.97) (table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using NLP technology to generate diagnostic reports based on multimodal retina images. The utilisation of NLP in the healthcare field has experienced notable advancements in recent years. The extraction of information from electronic documents for retrospective analysis has traditionally depended on the recognition and comprehension of natural language, making the advent of DL a significant development in leveraging natural language generation capabilities.5 Recent breakthroughs, including the development of Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4, have brought AI-generated language to a level that is often indistinguishable from human communication in certain contexts.15,17

Compared with its advancement in other fields, the use of natural language generation in the medical field continues to rely on the rule-based generation.18 19 A recent example is the application of converting images into medical reports within the field of nuclear cardiology.19 In that study, rule-based NLP and expert systems were employed to produce structured reports for myocardial perfusion imaging, achieving comparable performance to that of nine expert nuclear cardiologists. This situation can be attributed to challenges related to the reliability of training data and the lack of expert supervision, which have impeded the successful integration of DL-based natural language models in medical settings. Given these factors, the rule-based NLP system demonstrates its effectiveness, affordability and dependability in primary eye clinics. In our study, while slight differences in evaluation questionnaires were necessary to reflect the distinct output formats of the two models, the core evaluation metrics were consistently applied to ensure fairness. As no significant inferiority in the performance of the rule-based model was observed, our findings suggested the competence of rule-based NLP in macular disease screening and suggested its applicability in community medical agencies lacking ophthalmologists.

While the coverage of AI is expanding, novel challenges emerge in its wider implementation, especially in primary medical institutions.20,22 Beyond device availability, a critical factor is the scarcity of specialists capable of interpreting AI-generated output. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated AI’s diagnostic performance characterised by high sensitivity and specificity.23,25 However, the interpretation of these technical outputs could be challenging for individuals lacking sufficient expertise, particularly within specialised domains such as ophthalmology. Efforts have been made to train individuals within medical institutions, yielding favourable results in controlled settings.26 27 Nevertheless, this methodology proves to be labour intensive and inadequate for widespread implementation, particularly in regions characterised by varying economic resources. To address this challenge more efficiently, we adopted NLP to further process AI-generated output. Previous literature has evaluated NLP’s performance in analysing and summarising medical reports, but this is the first study that attempts and evaluates NLP-based diagnostic reports in ophthalmology.28 29 The results of this study offer empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of integrating diagnostic AI and NLP models in the generation of medical reports, indicating the potential for instructing AI models to generate more comprehensible outputs rather than relying on human interpretation.

By comparing the two models with junior ophthalmologists, our research indicates that DL-based NLP models may not outperform rule-based models in the generation of reports demanding a high level of specialisation and precision in the field of ophthalmology. Recent studies have shown that rule-based NLP in medical text processing exhibits inferior performance compared with NLP algorithms developed based on AI: in a study aimed at extracting fall-related data from clinical notes, the rule-based NLP model exhibited a comparatively lower F-score in contrast to the traditional machine learning (ML) cohort, with a database including 750 notes.30 However, in another study involving the analysis of radiology reports to detect fractures in specific skeletal sites, the ML-based group changed to a DL-based approach. Researchers discovered that the rule-based NLP outperformed the DL-based method. This result was observed in a dataset consisting of less than 1000 cases.31 Based on the afore-mentioned findings, our study reached a comparable conclusion within the field of ophthalmology: the suitability of NLP methodologies, whether DL-based or rule-based, remains uncertain when based on a limited database.

Despite the merits, this research has its limitations. First, to ensure report comprehension by individuals without ophthalmological expertise, the quantity of information in diagnostic reports was streamlined. Second, the evaluation of rule-based NLP was conducted on a small sample, resulting in discrepancies in diagnostic accuracy of the junior ophthalmologists between the rule-based part and DL-based part. Finally, the direct comparison between the two NLP approaches was absent in this study, which could be attributed to the utilisation of distinct test sets. In addition, it is crucial to acknowledge that DL-based NLP, despite facing current challenges, remains at the forefront of NLP technology. Therefore, future research should focus on optimising neural network architectures and establishing comprehensive databases to enhance the training of DL-based NLP models.

In conclusion, this study involved the development and evaluation of a rule-based NLP and a DL-based NLP model for generating diagnostic reports for various macular diseases using multimodal examinations (CFP/OCT). The findings indicate that incorporating NLP techniques into multimodal AI systems shows promise in detecting significant macular diseases in primary healthcare settings. This approach may offer a cost-effective and efficient method for screening macular diseases in a broader population, especially given the increasing global prevalence of age-related conditions.
