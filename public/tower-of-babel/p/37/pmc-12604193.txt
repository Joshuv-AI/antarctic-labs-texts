Ring-and-flap: a novel and accessible alternative to fix-and-flap in Gustilo IIIB-C open tibia fractures (an institutional experience)
Authors: Jonathan Yeo, Jeremy Mingfa Sun, Ing How Moo, Wei Yuan, Ho Chin Boo, Andy Kuei Siong Yeo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe fix-and-flap regime is resource intense and may not be readily available. As an alternative, the ring fixator serves as a versatile option for limb salvage, however it poses challenges to soft tissue reconstruction due to the limited working space around the rings and pins. We describe an institutional experience with a novel ring-and-flap regime utilising an open ring technique—which optimises the ring fixator construct for the flexibility of interval soft-tissue reconstruction.MethodsA retrospective review of patients treated with a novel ring-and-flap regime over 2017–2024 was conducted. Demographic data, comorbidities, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were collected.Results6 patients were included. Soft tissue reconstruction took place at a median of 12 days (minimum 2, maximum 154) after ring fixation. The median duration on the ring fixator was 144 days (minimum 74, maximum 270). Most patients (4/6) developed osteomyelitis but all achieved union and regained ambulatory function. 2/6 required flap revision due to vascular thrombosis. The ring fixator served as definitive fixation in 2 patients; whilst the remainder opted for internalisation.ConclusionThe novel ring-and-flap regime may provide a practical and safe pathway for limb salvage in resource limited settings, bridging the gap until specialised ortho-plastics care is available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09281-9.

Introduction

The fix-and-flap regime has been regarded as the gold standard for Gustilo IIIB-C open tibia fractures to mitigate infection risks and achieve faster union rates [1]. However, it is resource intense, requiring a dedicated ortho-plastics unit with adequate expertise and operating theatre facility available [2]. In centres where such resources are not readily available, the fix-and-flap regime may not be possible. In such cases, and especially where there is significant bone or soft tissue defect, external fixation with an ilizarov frame comes into consideration.

Despite the versatility of a ring fixator, the foreign body effect of a standard ring fixator poses challenges to soft tissue reconstruction owing to the limited working space around the pins and rings, often narrowing down the options of soft tissue coverage. To overcome this limitation, it has been suggested that the ilizarov frame is applied after soft tissue reconstruction in a combined procedure [3]. Alternatively, various techniques have been described for the plastic surgeon to adapt to the constraints of the working space, including the use of long microsurgery instruments, high power loupes, the use of perforators as recipients, and a reverse sural flap [4–6]. However, to date, there is a lack of literature describing modifications to the fixator itself to optimise surgical access.

To address these challenges of fix-and-flap and standard ring fixators, we describe our institutional experience with a novel ring-and-flap regime. The keystone of this technique lies in the open ring construct, which not only serves as a practical solution for definitive fixation whilst awaiting soft-tissue reconstruction, but also optimises the working space for interval soft-tissue reconstruction.

Materials and methods

A retrospective review was performed from 2017 to 2024 for patients who underwent treatment with a novel ring-and-flap regime utilising an open ring technique, by senior Orthopaedic and Plastic Surgeons at our institution. Demographic data, comorbidities, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were collected, spanning from the index injury to the last follow-up or discharge in the outpatient setting. Patients presenting with Gustilo IIIB-C open tibial fractures were treated with the following 3-stage workflow (Fig. 1).

3-stage workflow

Stage 1: preliminary stabilisation

In the first stage, preliminary stabilisation of the fracture site was achieved with a temporising external fixator, together with wound debridement, in accordance with the guidelines for the management of open fractures published by the British Association of Plastic, Reconstructive and Aesthetic surgeons (BAPRAS) and British Orthopaedic Association (BOA) [7]. A re-look wound debridement was typically done 24–72 h after the index debridement, followed by serial wound debridement as needed until the wound was satisfactory. When haemodynamically stable, a Computed Tomographic Angiogram of the limb was done to aid surgical planning for ring fixation and soft tissue reconstruction, to assess the vascularity of the lower limb.

Stage 2: open ring fixation

In the second stage, ring fixation using the open ring technique was done. The ring fixator served as definitive fixation until clinical and radiological union was achieved. Using the open ring technique as described below, the ring was designed to allow a zone of opening for access to soft tissue reconstruction. With the frame in-situ, patients were allowed weight-bearing as tolerated, facilitating early mobilisation and rehabilitation.

Zone of opening

Depending on the soft tissue reconstruction required, the vascularity of the lower limb was assessed, usually with the aid of a Computed Tomographic Angiography and intra-operative Ultrasound Doppler to determine the primary selected vessel for the free flap. This was determined by the plastic surgeon and is described in a clock face pattern with 12 o’clock being the anterior surface of the tibia. Depending on the defect, the ring was constructed with medial or lateral side opening (Fig. 2).

Positioning of elements for stability

The constructed frame comprised a 4 or 5 ring construct with full rings at the ends and the middle rings consisting of 2 half-rings assembled together. The full rings are necessary to give overall stability to the construct, especially in the open ring mode. The fixation elements at the end rings were as per standard fixation. Usage of full wires at the level is acceptable but care must be taken to avoid the targeted pedicle where the free flap would be anastomosed to. When the pedicle location was indeterminate or if wounds constrained their insertion, half pins were used. Due to the instability caused by opening the middle rings, drop pins from the end rings are recommended for stability. For the middle rings which needed to be opened, the only elements were half pins from the opposite side. Olive wires can be used for reduction from the flap/defect side but they would most likely be cut short to facilitate flap application.

Ring to ring stability

To facilitate ring opening, the unopened half of the construct was secured with at least 3 connecting rods between the various rings and half rings. This confers stability to the ring once opened.

Stage 3: soft tissue reconstruction

The third stage entailed soft tissue reconstruction with the ring fixator in the ‘open mode’ (Fig. 3). An ortho-plastic approach to soft tissue reconstruction where close collaboration between the orthopaedist and the reconstruction surgeon was utilised. The middle rings (half rings) were opened by the Orthopaedic Surgeon, allowing the Plastic Surgeon 180 deg access to the soft tissue defect and recipient vessel through the predetermined zone of opening. The rings at the proximal and distal extents of the operating window offer stability to the surgeon’s hands during microsurgery by serving as a hand support. The frame was supplemented with ‘foot struts’ to prevent rotation of the leg and maintain an ergonomic operating field. As local flaps tend to be within the zone of injury with unpredictable perfusion, our preference was an anterolateral thigh free flap with the posterior tibial artery as the most commonly used recipient vessel. After soft tissue reconstruction was completed, the half rings were reconstructed by the Orthopaedic Surgeon, converting back to the ‘closed mode’ (Video 1, Supplementary material).

Results

A total of 6 patients were included in this study, with their characteristics and outcomes summarised in Tables 1 and 2. All patients were followed up past clinical and radiological union. The mechanism of injury was a road traffic accident for all except one patient involved in a blast injury. The mean age of patients involved was 40.5 years-old, all of them being male. Most patients had sustained unilateral open tibia fractures of Gustilo grade 3B, except for one patient who sustained bilateral tibia Gustilo 3 C open fractures (Fig. 4). The approximate skin defect prior to soft tissue reconstruction ranged from 5 × 2 cm to 20 × 15 cm. Majority of patients (5 out of 6) underwent soft tissue reconstruction with an anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap. Other flaps used include a hemisoleus flap, superficial circumflex iliac artery flap, and rectus femoris flap. The median duration from injury to ring fixator application was 13 days (minimum 6, maximum 154). In 4 patients, the ring fixator was applied during the index admission, between 6 and 16 days after the injury. Whereas in 2 patients, they underwent ring fixation in a delayed setting (40 and 154 days after injury), when they presented with signs of osteomyelitis and peri-implant infection after initial intramedullary nailing during the index admission. The median duration from ring fixator application to soft tissue reconstruction was 12 days (minimum 2, maximum 154). The median total duration on the ring fixator was 144 (minimum 74, maximum 270 days), whilst the median duration to union from ring fixation was 13 months (minimum 7, maximum 19). Most patients developed osteomyelitis (4 out of 6), treated with antibiotics, but all achieved union and regained ambulatory function. Flap revision was required in 2 patients, due to arterial flap thrombosis in one case, and venous thrombosis in the other. Discussion

Advantages of the ring-and-flap approach

The primary advantage of the ring-and-flap regime is the ease of access for soft tissue reconstruction through the open ring construct, at an interval setting after the ring fixator is applied. This allows flexibility for surgical planning between the Orthopaedic and Plastic Surgeons, whilst reducing the overall demand on operating theatre staff and resources, potentially avoiding undue stress in the operating room. Patients have the added flexibility of being transferred to specialist centres to receive necessary reconstructive procedures, whilst maintaining early fracture stabilisation.

While many believe the rings to be a hinderance during free flap reconstruction, it can also offer stability to the surgeon’s hands during microsurgery. The rings at proximal and distal extents of the operating window supports the surgeon’s hand, reducing tremor. Supporting “foot struts” can also be fixed to the ring to prevent rotation of the leg and fixing the position of the operating field in an ergonomic and ideal manner. An important point to note is that if the operating window is created too small it can hinder the microsurgery.

The rings also complement flap survival post-operatively by protecting against mechanical compression and flap thrombosis. Free tissue transfer is most at risk of vascular anastomosis failure during the first 48 h [8]. Compression is often one of the culprits that tip the scale towards thrombosis. The ring fixator can keep the reconstructed soft tissue off the bed and prevent any mechanical compression of the flap, mitigating the risk of flap thrombosis. When flap complications occur, we observe that the open ring construct allows easy access for flap revision or rescue procedures, as experienced with case 6 (SM) where the ring was opened multiple times for flap revision and rescue procedures.

Comparison with the fix-and-flap regime

Whilst the fix-and-flap regime has traditionally been the gold standard, there are circumstances in which the ring-and-flap regime may be preferred due to institutional resource limitations, patient and injury factors.

The ring-and-flap allows for management of significant bone defects through distraction osteogenesis or bone transport, which is not possible with internal fixation in the fix-and-flap. This is advantageous for fractures with significant bone loss. In cases with active infection or extensive contamination, the ring-and-flap allows for serial debridement and interval coverage, which may be preferred over fix-and-flap. Furthermore, the ring fixator mitigates the risk of deep infection when compared with internal fixation.

Patients with severe medical comorbidities or haemodynamic instability from polytrauma may not be fit for prolonged operating time required for fix-and-flap surgery. By staging the fixation from coverage in the ring-and-flap regime, operating time can be shortened. This mitigates the risks of prolonged anaesthesia, offering a safer option for high risk patients.

Limitations and challenges

Deep infection remains as one of the major challenges in treating Gustilo IIIB-C open fractures. In our series, majority of patients were treated for osteomyelitis based on positive bone cultures. Nevertheless, all patients were adequately treated with a long course of antibiotics whilst continuing treatment on the ilizarov frame, without the need for surgical intervention or hardware revision, and there were no cases of chronic osteomyelitis. This may suggest that the ilizarov frame serves as a durable device to insure stability whilst mitigating the risk of infection in contaminated open fractures.

Whilst the open ring confers advantageous access for soft tissue work, we recognise that orthopaedic expertise is required to facilitate the opening and closing of the ring to enable access. This may be a limiting factor where expertise is not available, especially where patients are transferred to distant centres to receive further treatment.

Opening the ring may theoretically also destabilise the construct of the ilizarov frame, compromising the stability at the fracture site. However, we did not observe significant loss of reduction from opening the ring in our series. Satisfactory stability was maintained by the use of half-pins described above.

There a several limitations of this study. First, our findings are based on a small sample size of 7 patients, treated at a single centre, limiting the generalisability of our findings. Second, given the lack of readily available resource for a combined fix-and-flap regime at our institution, our findings may be biased towards the use of a ring fixator with interval soft tissue coverage. Further multicentre studies with larger sample size and direct comparison of patients treated with a ring-and-flap versus fix-and-flap regime are required to address these limitations.

Clinical implications and future perspectives

Our case series presents positive early experience utilising this method, with good rates of limb-salvage, flap survival, and return to ambulation in patients presenting with Gustilo IIIB-C open tibia fractures, peri-implant infection, or infective non-union. This approach allows early stabilisation and safe transfer for flap coverage when needed, which can be life and limb-saving in resource limited centres.

Conclusion

The novel ring-and-flap regime may provide a practical and safe pathway for limb salvage in resource limited settings, bridging the gap until specialised ortho-plastics care is available.
