Unlocking solar-derived CO2 conversion to hydrocarbons over plasmonic Cu-Fe tandem catalysts under industrially relevant conditions
Authors: Xinhuilan Wang, Alejandra Rendón-Patiño, Diego Mateo, Jorge Gascon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar-driven conversion of CO2 into hydrocarbons offers a sustainable route to carbon-neutral fuels, yet efficient C-C coupling under photo-thermal conditions remains challenging. Here, we report a potassium-promoted Cu2Fe1 tandem catalyst for selective photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation under industrially relevant conditions. Under illumination at 250 °C and 20 bar, the catalyst achieves 77.4% selectivity towards C2+ hydrocarbons, with a C2-5 hydrocarbon yield of 5.04 mmol g−1 h−1, representing highly competitive performance among reported photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation systems. Mechanistic studies reveal a tandem pathway in which Cu promotes the reverse water-gas shift reaction to generate CO, followed by Fischer-Tropsch-type hydrocarbon formation over in situ-formed χ-Fe5C2. In situ DRIFTS, in situ XRD and CO-TPR analyses further show that light-induced non-thermal effects, together with localized heating at the Cu-Fe interface, facilitate carbide formation and accelerate key intermediate evolution.

Introduction

Modern chemical manufacturing remains strongly dependent on fossil resources as both energy carriers and carbon feedstocks, a reliance that is increasingly untenable given finite reserves, geopolitical vulnerability, and the growing climate burden associated with CO2 emissions1–3. Consequently, the development of sustainable carbon utilization strategies that simultaneously mitigate emissions while ensuring a secure supply of fuels and chemicals has therefore become imperative4–6. In this context, the catalytic conversion of CO2 to hydrocarbons via tandem reaction pathways combining the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction with Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) offers a direct route to drop-in synthetic fuels compatible with existing infrastructure7–9. Achieving this transformation under energy-efficient and industrially relevant conditions, however, remains a formidable challenge.

To address these challenges, the development of non-conventional heating strategies beyond classical thermocatalysis has become increasingly important. Traditional catalytic processes rely on bulk external heating, which is typically supplied by the combustion of fossil fuels and is inherently energy-intensive and inefficient due to substantial heat dissipation throughout the reactor. In contrast, emerging approaches such as induction heating10,11, Joule heating12,13, and photo-thermal activation14–17 enable rapid and localized energy delivery directly to the catalyst. These methods could enhance heat transfer efficiency and energy utilization by delivering energy more directly to active sites on the catalysts.

Particularly, photo-thermal catalysis has emerged as an effective strategy for driving chemical transformations by synergistically coupling light absorption with thermally activated catalysis18–20. By converting photon energy into localized heat at catalytically active sites, photo-thermal systems generate nanoscale hot spots that accelerate reaction kinetics while maintaining moderate temperatures, thereby offering a promising route to overcome the intrinsic limitations of CO2 activation21,22. Unlike conventional thermal heating, this spatially confined heat generation enables more efficient energy utilization, enhances catalytic efficiency, and maximizes solar energy harvesting while reducing overall energy consumption23,24. Efficient photo-thermal catalysis requires broadband absorption across the ultraviolet (UV), visible, and near-infrared (NIR) regions of the solar spectrum. Accordingly, diverse materials, including carbon-based structures19,25, semiconductors26,27, and plasmonic metal nanoparticles28,29, have been explored. In particular, plasmonic metals such as Au30–32, Cu33, and Ag34 exhibit localized surface plasmon resonance, enabling efficient photo-thermal conversion via plasmon decay, localized heating, and the generation of energetic charge carriers that promote reactant adsorption and stabilize reaction intermediates35.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation reaction, particularly in C1 chemistry, such as CO2 methanation36, RWGS reaction37–39, and methanol synthesis40,41. However, efficient C-C coupling toward value-added C2+ hydrocarbons remains fundamentally constrained by high kinetic barriers, while excessive methane formation and the inherent selectivity limits of the Anderson-Schulz-Flory distribution further restrict hydrocarbon productivity42,43. To address these challenges, extensive efforts have been devoted to developing catalytic systems that enable efficient C-C bond formation under photo-thermal conditions44,45. For example, Zhang and co-workers developed CoFe alloy catalysts derived from layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanosheets, demonstrating a 60% CH4 selectivity and 35 % C2+ selectivity at 320 °C under light illumination in a batch reactor at 0.18 MPa46. Ozin and co-workers employed CoFe2O4 spinel catalysts in a flow reactor under ambient pressure, achieving a C2-C4 hydrocarbon production rate of 1.1 mmol g−1 h−1 with 29.8 % selectivity47. Similarly, Ning et al. developed a Co-based catalyst for sunlight-driven CO2 hydrogenation, displaying a C2-C4 hydrocarbon production rate of 1.3 mmol g−1 h−1 with a 62.5 % selectivity under light irradiation at 3 bar14. More recently, Ye and co-workers reported a light-induced phase transformation from CoFe2O4 to (Fe1-xCox)5C2, which enabled a C2-4 olefin productivity of 2.05 mmol g−1 h−1 in a batch reactor operated at 3 bar48. Nevertheless, despite these efforts, the development of photo-thermal catalysts that operate under industrially relevant conditions while combining structural robustness with efficient utilization of solar energy remains an unresolved challenge.

Over the last years, tandem catalysis has gained increasing attention as a strategy for integrating sequential reactions in a single operation, reducing process complexity while enhancing efficiency and sustainability49. In this study, we report for the first time a potassium-promoted Cu2Fe1 tandem catalyst for direct photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction, achieving efficient carbon chain growth under continuous-flow operation at industrially relevant conditions. In contrast to conventional heating, this process exploits photo-thermal energy input from solar illumination, enabling localized energy delivery at catalytically active sites driven by plasmonic excitation of Cu (Fig. 1). Under illumination at 250 °C, 20 bar, and a space velocity of 12000 mL g−1 h−1, the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst exhibits exceptional activity and selectivity, reaching 77.4 % selectivity toward C2+ hydrocarbons with a C2-5 hydrocarbon yield of 5.04 mmol g−1 h−1. This performance not only exceeds previously reported photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation benchmarks but also approaches operating windows relevant to industrial Fischer-Tropsch processes. Experimental evidence confirms that the catalyst operates via a tandem mechanism, where Cu catalyzes the RWGS reaction, while in situ-formed χ-Fe5C2 ensures catalytic activity and stability for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. Mechanistic studies reveal that light-induced non-thermal effects dominate the reaction pathway, synergistically enhancing thermally driven processes. Furthermore, in situ DRIFTS, in situ XRD, and CO-TPR analyses reveal that light irradiation accelerates the formation of reaction intermediates, thereby enhancing catalytic activity, while simultaneously lowering the energy barrier for χ-Fe5C2 formation from metallic Fe. This effect is attributed to hot carrier generation and efficient electron transfer at the Cu-Fe interface under illumination, enabling effective operation at reduced temperatures. Overall, this study demonstrates a synergistic integration of a bimetallic tandem catalyst, achieving unprecedented performance in photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon conversion and advancing the process toward industrial feasibility.Fig. 1Photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation to hydrocarbons over 2 K Cu2Fe1.Schematic illustration of the tandem CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction over the potassium-promoted Cu2Fe1 catalyst under photo-thermal conditions.

Results

Photo-thermal Catalytic Performance

Herein, we investigate the catalytic performance of bimetallic copper-iron materials as tandem catalysts for the photo-thermal conversion of CO2 to hydrocarbons (Supplementary Fig. 1). First, we optimized the Cu/Fe molar ratio to maximize CO2 conversion and hydrocarbon selectivity. To this end, a series of CuFe catalysts with varying compositions were synthesized via co-precipitation, followed by calcination at 450 °C for 3 h. Based on H2-temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), all catalysts were pre-reduced at 400 °C under a 6 vol% H2/Ar flow for 2 h and subsequently passivated under 5 vol% O2/N2 at room temperature for 1 h. XRD patterns revealed the presence of CuO and Cu2FeO4 phases in all calcined bimetallic CuFe catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 3a).N2 adsorption-desorption measurements reveal the bulk oxide nature and mesoporous characteristics, as evidenced by type-IV isotherms with hysteresis loops (Supplementary Fig. 4). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on the calcined Cu2Fe1 oxide catalyst as a representative of the bimetallic CuFe system. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, the deconvoluted Cu 2p spectra revealed two peaks at 932.3 eV and 933.9 eV for Cu 2p3/2, along with corresponding peaks at 952.1 eV and 953.7 eV for Cu 2p1/2, which are attributed to Cu+ and Cu2+ species, respectively50. Additionally, three satellite peaks at 940.1 eV, 941.4 eV, and 943.2 eV further confirm the presence of Cu2+51. For the Fe 2p core level, peaks at 709.8 eV and 723.4 eV correspond to Fe2+ species, while peaks at 711.1 eV and 725.1 eV are assigned to Fe3+. Satellite peaks at 718.8 eV, 727.2 eV, and 732.6 eV further support the presence of oxidized iron species52.

These XPS results provide clear evidence for the formation of a CuFe2O4 spinel structure, which is corroborated by the diffraction pattern of CuFe2O4 observed in XRD. Upon reduction, metallic Cu and Fe were identified as the predominant phases, with minor Fe3O4 detected in reduced 2 K Cu1Fe1 and 2 K Cu1Fe2 catalysts, likely due to surface passivation (Supplementary Fig. 3b). In contrast, Fe3O4 was the dominant phase in the reduced monometallic 2 K Fe catalyst, attributed to the higher reduction temperature.

Initially, the catalytic performance and product selectivity of the different CuFe catalysts were assessed under photo-thermal conditions using a 300 W Xe lamp (4 W cm−2 light intensity) with external heating at 250 °C, 10 bar pressure, and a space velocity of 12000 mL g−1 h−1. Monometallic 2 K Fe catalysts exhibited 24% CO2 conversion and 22.1% selectivity toward C2+ products, highlighting their potential in CO2 hydrogenation to hydrocarbons (Supplementary Fig. 6). H2-TPR (Supplementary Fig. 2) and PXRD data (Supplementary Fig. 3b) confirmed that Fe3O4 is active for the RWGS reaction, while carburized iron derived from metallic Fe promotes C-C coupling53. However, bimetallic catalysts demonstrated enhanced performance, with increased copper content boosting RWGS activity and improving hydrocarbon selectivity. Notably, the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst achieved the highest CO2 conversion (33.8 %) and C2+ hydrocarbon selectivity (30.6 %), establishing an optimal Cu/Fe molar ratio of 2 for CO2-to-hydrocarbon conversion. However, excessive Cu content led to diminished performance, likely due to insufficient iron species to facilitate C-C coupling (Supplementary Fig. 6). The near-complete selectivity towards CO observed for the 2 K Cu catalyst emphasizes the pivotal role of copper in the RWGS reaction, but also highlights its limited catalytic activity, possibly due to larger crystalline sizes and agglomeration. (Supplementary Table 2). In view of these results, we selected a Cu/Fe molar ratio of 2 and further optimizations were performed with the Cu2Fe1 sample.

The role of potassium (K) as a promoter was also investigated due to its known influence on the electronic structure of catalysts and product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis9,54. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, optimal catalytic activity was observed at a K loading of 2 wt%. CO2-temperature-programmed desorption (CO2-TPD) was conducted to evaluate the CO2 adsorption ability of 2 K Cu2Fe1 and pure Cu2Fe1 catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst exhibited a notably higher desorption peak intensity than pure Cu2Fe1, highlighting the role of K promotion in enhancing CO2 adsorption. The first desorption peak, observed at 98 °C for both catalysts, corresponds to physically adsorbed CO2 on the catalyst surface. Notably, a second desorption peak at 146 °C was only detected for 2 K Cu2Fe1, indicative of HCO3- decomposition, as confirmed by the corresponding MS signal (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The peak at around 203 °C is attributed to the decomposition of metastable carbonate species, while thermally stable carbonate species decompose at 717 °C and 603 °C for 2 K Cu2Fe1 and pure Cu2Fe1, respectively37,55,56. These findings suggest that K promotion not only enhances CO2 adsorption capacity but also strengthens surface interactions between CO2 and the catalyst. Moreover, CO-temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) analyses (Supplementary Fig. 9) further revealed that K promotion enhances both CO adsorption capacity and binding strength on the catalyst surface. Additionally, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, K promotion increased the chain growth probability, favoring the formation of longer hydrocarbons. This suggests that K not only enhances CO2 adsorption and CO-derived intermediate formation, but may also moderate H2 activation and surface hydrogen coverage. By suppressing excessive hydrogenation of CO intermediates, K helps shift the product distribution toward C2+ hydrocarbons. However, excessive K loading ( >3 wt%) led to a decline in activity, likely due to surface over-coverage inhibiting active sites. Overall, the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst exhibited superior performance in photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation, achieving the highest CO2 conversion and C2+ hydrocarbon selectivity, so further catalytic tests and mechanistic studies were conducted with this sample unless otherwise indicated.

In a subsequent step, we evaluated the photo-thermal performance of the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst under industrially relevant conditions, utilizing external heating at 250 °C, a space velocity of 12000 mL g−1 h−1, pressures ranging from 10 to 20 bar, and a H2/CO2 ratio of 4. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the CO2 conversion and product selectivity required approximately 14 h to reach the steady state, attributed to the gradual formation of active sites. Accordingly, all catalytic performance data were collected after 20 h on stream to ensure steady-state conditions had been established, unless otherwise indicated. As shown in Fig. 2a, CO2 conversion increased significantly from 33.8 % to 53.5 %, while CO selectivity decreased from 56.4 % to 23.5% as the pressure increased from 10 to 20 bar. Hydrocarbon selectivity in CO-free conditions (Fig. 2b) revealed an outstanding C2+ hydrocarbon selectivity of 77.4 % with a C2-5= olefin selectivity of 43.2 % at 20 bar, surpassing prior benchmarks for photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon conversion. Under these conditions, the C2-5 hydrocarbon yield reached 5.04 mmol g−1 h−1, with 4.1 mmol g–1 h−1 attributed to C2-5= olefins, representing one of the highest catalytic performances compared to previously reported results (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 8). Furthermore, reactions at a reduced space velocity of 7500 mL g−1 h−1 could further enhance CO2 conversion to 37.5 %, 48.0 %, and 54.5 % at 10, 15, and 20 bar, respectively, accompanied by a drop in CO selectivity to 47.0 %, 24.1 %, and 18.8 %, attributed to increased residence time (Fig. 2c, d). To better align with a potential industrial application, the long-term stability of our system was tested under cyclic conditions (12-h light on/off cycles) simulating natural sunlight intermittency. It is worth mentioning that these cycling tests were performed after the activation process of the catalyst (ca. 14–15 h). Over the next 50 h, the catalyst demonstrated stable CO2 conversion and selectivity toward CO and hydrocarbons without noticeable degradation (Fig. 2e). A detailed product distribution analysis was conducted to assess chain growth probability (α) and the olefin/paraffin (O/P) ratio. Hydrocarbon products from C3 to C5 adhered to the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, while the O/P ratio was evaluated for C2 to C5 products. The chain growth factor (α) decreased from 0.59 to 0.48 as pressure increased from 10 to 20 bar at a space velocity of 12000 mL g-1 h-1, suggesting a shift toward lighter hydrocarbon products at higher pressures (Supplementary Fig. 12). This trend is consistent with the reduced availability of surface-active sites for chain propagation, as the elevated pressure promotes more extensive surface coverage by reactants and intermediates57,58. At a lower space velocity of 7500 mL g−1 h−1, this effect is amplified due to longer residence times, allowing for higher CO2 conversion but favoring termination reactions over chain growth. Moreover, the O/P ratio could reflect the selectivity between olefins and paraffins (Supplementary Table 7). The decline in the O/P ratio from 5.2 to 3.7 with increasing pressure from 10 to 20 bar at 12000 mL g–1 h–1 indicates a relative suppression of olefin formation, likely driven by increased hydrogenation rates at higher pressures. This is further accentuated at the reduced space velocity, where prolonged contact time enhances hydrogenation, resulting in an even lower O/P ratio. To further investigate the influence of space velocity and the H2/CO2 ratio on hydrocarbon distribution, additional experiments were conducted at a lower space velocity of 4500 mL g−1 h−1 and 20 bar with varying H2/CO2 ratios (Supplementary Fig. 13, 14). At a H2/CO2 ratio of 4, CO selectivity decreased to 8.9 %, though prolonged contact time increased paraffin production and CH4 selectivity. Lower H2/CO2 ratios enhanced the O/P ratio but reduced catalytic activity, demonstrating a delicate balance between activity and selectivity. These findings highlight the intricate interplay between operating parameters, emphasizing the importance of precise optimization to balance CO2 conversion, hydrocarbon yield, and selectivity in photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation.

To gain deeper insight into the active phases of the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst in the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction, a series of characterizations were performed on the catalyst both before and after the reaction to investigate the phases of Cu and Fe nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 15, the pre-reduced 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst consisted of metallic Cu and metallic Fe species. High-magnification TEM images (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b) of the pre-reduced 2 K Cu2Fe1 sample revealed lattice spacings of 0.210 nm and 0.141 nm, corresponding to the (111) plane of metallic Cu and the (200) plane of metallic Fe, respectively, in good agreement with PXRD patterns. Additionally, a thin Fe3O4 layer was observed on the particle surface in the reduced sample (Supplementary Fig. 16c, d), likely formed during the passivation process. In addition, it also confirmed the homogeneous distribution of the K promoter after reduction. Furthermore, the surface composition of the pre-reduced 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst was analyzed by XPS. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, metallic Fe and metallic Cu were observed at binding energies of 706.2 eV and 931.8 eV, respectively59,60. Additionally, peaks at 709.8 eV, 711.4 eV, 933.2 eV, and 935.1 eV correspond to Fe2+, Fe3+, Cu+, and Cu2+, respectively, indicating the formation of an oxidized layer during the passivation process. Moreover, the presence of satellite peaks in the Cu 2p and Fe 2p spectra further confirms the existence of mixed Cu/Fe oxide species on the catalyst surface. However, those oxide phases were not detected in the PXRD patterns, presumably due to their low concentration. As shown in Fig. 3b, c, Mössbauer spectroscopy of the pre-reduced 2 K Cu2Fe1 sample exhibited a major component of metallic Fe (94 %), with a minor fraction (6%) present as Fe3+ (spm), likely due to surface oxidation during the passivation process or sample handling. This observation is consistent with the XPS results.

After the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction, a significant phase transformation was observed. The PXRD patterns of the spent 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst after the stability test exhibited well-defined diffraction peaks corresponding to the χ-Fe5C2 phase (Fig. 3a). Mössbauer spectroscopy further confirmed this transformation, showing 95.5 % χ-Fe5C2 in the spent sample (Fig. 3b, c). Neither metallic Fe nor other iron carbide phases were detected, indicating that the metallic Fe phase is completely carburized during the reaction. Moreover, a small fraction of Fe3+ (spm) ( <5 %) was detected in the spent catalysts, likely resulting from exposure to air during sample handling and/or a partial oxidation caused by water generated during the reaction. TEM images of 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst after the stability test further confirmed the formation of χ-Fe5C2, with a lattice spacing of 0.205 nm corresponding to the (510) plane (Fig. 3e). Additionally, elemental mapping (Fig. 3f) revealed a homogeneous distribution of K throughout the spent sample. The overlapping distribution of Fe and C further supports the presence of the χ-Fe5C2 phase. However, the detected carbon originated not only from χ-Fe5C2 but also from the presence of surface-adsorbed hydrocarbons. Notably, some carbon was found outside the sample and exhibited a similar distribution to oxygen, likely corresponding to adsorbed hydrocarbons and crystalline wax formed on the catalyst surface, consistent with the PXRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 15). XPS analysis could further corroborate these findings. Compared to the reduced 2 K Cu2Fe1 sample, the Fe 2p core-level spectrum of the spent sample exhibited a peak at 709.1 eV, attributed to Fe–C bonding (Supplementary Fig. 19). In the C 1 s spectrum, a corresponding Fe–C peak also appeared at 283.8 eV, along with four additional components at 284.8, 285.4, 286.6, and 288.3 eV, assigned to C = C (sp2), C–C (sp3), C–O–C/C–OH, and HO–C = O species, respectively, indicating the presence of surface hydrocarbons61,62. When it comes to the Cu 2p core level spectrum, the main peaks could be deconvoluted into two components attributable to metallic Cu and Cu+.

In view of these findings, the nature of carbonaceous species in the spent sample was further investigated by thermogravimetric-temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TGA-TPH) analysis. The weight loss and corresponding deconvoluted CH4 signals of the spent 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst after the stability test are presented in Fig. 3d. The CH4 signal in the low-temperature range (150–250 °C) corresponds to adsorbed hydrocarbons and amorphous carbon on the catalyst surface. A peak at around 335 °C is attributed to atomic carbon and surface carbide. Additionally, the peak at 395 °C is assigned to crystalline wax, while the signal at 437 °C corresponds to crystalline χ-Fe5C263,64. The major weight loss in the TGA profile occurred between 350–450 °C, indicating that carbide hydrogenation is the primary contributor. Furthermore, the water mass signal exhibited peaks at around 100 °C and 350 °C, which are associated with surface hydrocarbons and potential iron oxide species formed due to partial oxidation and sample handling, consistent with the Mössbauer and XPS results (Supplementary Fig. 20). Previous studies on the reactivity of surface carbon species suggest that the hydrogenolysis of χ-Fe5C2 occurs between 400 °C and 500 °C, whereas cementite (Fe3C) decomposes at around 600 °C65,66. Notably, both the TPH signal and TGA profiles (Fig. 3d) revealed no CH4 signal or weight loss at 600 °C, indicating the absence of Fe3C. Overall, those observations are in good agreement with PXRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy, confirming that the χ-Fe5C2 phase, which is exclusively in situ formed during the reaction, serves as an active phase for the Fischer-Tropsch process, emphasizing its crucial role in facilitating C-C coupling and hydrocarbon production during CO2 hydrogenation. Additionally, the spent 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst was also analyzed after the reaction at 20 bar, revealing a phase composition similar to that of the catalyst after the stability test at 10 bar (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Fig. 21–24). Moreover, a thin carbon layer was observed on the spent catalyst following the 20 bar reaction (Supplementary Fig. 21), however, it did not appear to affect the stability of the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Mechanistic Study

The photo-thermal effect results from a synergistic interplay between photo-chemical and thermal contributions of sunlight. To determine which of the pathways predominantly drives the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction in our system, direct and indirect illumination experiments were conducted, following the methodology outlined by Liu and Everitt67. These experiments aimed to isolate the photo-chemical contribution by employing a thin layer ( ≤ 1 mm thickness) of Ti2O3 (a material with exceptional light-to-heat conversion properties but inactive for CO2 hydrogenation reaction)68 on top of the catalyst bed. This configuration ensured that the catalyst under the Ti2O3 layer was not directly illuminated while maintaining a similar temperature profile to that of the direct photo-thermal reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 25). Comparing catalytic performance under direct and indirect illumination provided insight into the relative contributions of photo-chemical and pure thermal effects. The CO2 conversion and product selectivity under varying light intensities with 250 °C external heating at 10 bar are shown in Fig. 4a. Under direct illumination, CO2 conversion exhibited a linear dependence on light intensity between 2.8 and 4.0 W cm-2, suggesting that non-thermal effects dominate the reaction pathway, accompanied by a minor thermal contribution (Fig. 4a). Such linear behavior is characteristic of electron-driven processes, as noted in previous studies, and aligns with the commonly reported mechanism for plasmonic effects23,69,70. However, at higher light intensity (4.8 W cm-2), the CO2 conversion deviated from the linear trend, indicating that non-thermal contributions had reached a plateau. This suggests that beyond a critical light intensity, further increases do not enhance non-thermal effects, emphasizing the importance of optimizing light intensity to maximize light utilization efficiency and take full advantage of non-thermal effects71. In contrast, indirect illumination experiments, where the Ti2O3 layer blocked photo-chemical effects, revealed a significant reduction in CO2 conversion compared to direct illumination, further confirming the dominance of non-thermal contributions in the photo-thermal reaction. Additionally, the Arrhenius-type dependence of CO2 conversion on light intensity (2.8 - 4.0 W cm-2) under indirect illumination highlighted the prevailing role of thermal effects when photo-chemical pathways were suppressed (Fig. 4b). Moreover, the difference in CO2 conversion between direct and indirect illumination decreases from 85% to 44% as the light intensity increases from 2.8 to 4.8 W cm-2 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 4). This trend indicated that the contribution of hot carriers generated by photo-excitation in the bimetallic CuFe catalyst gradually diminishes at higher light intensities, probably due to the more elevated temperatures displayed by the catalyst upon increasing the irradiance that eventually favor charge recombination.

Non-thermal effects have proved to influence selectivity patterns, however, a comparative analysis of hydrocarbon selectivity under direct (2.8 W cm-2) and indirect (4.8 W cm-2) illumination at comparable CO2 conversion levels revealed no discernible differences in product distribution. Additionally, PXRD analysis of the spent catalysts from both direct and indirect illumination conditions at 4 W cm-2 with external heating at 250 °C confirmed identical phase compositions (Fig. 4e), indicating the complete formation of χ-Fe5C2 at 250 °C, irrespective of light exposure. These findings suggest that when the active phase remains constant, illumination does not influence hydrocarbon selectivity, implying an invariant reaction pathway under light and dark conditions, as we will corroborate below through in situ DRIFTS measurements.

To further investigate the role of light irradiation in the reaction mechanism, catalytic performance was assessed under direct and indirect illumination at a lower reaction temperature (200 °C). As shown in Fig. 4d, light irradiation (4 W cm-2) led to a CO2 conversion of 17% with hydrocarbon selectivity exceeding 20%, demonstrating that the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon conversion on 2 K Cu2Fe1 is viable at only 200 °C. In contrast, in the absence of light, CO2 conversion reached only 11.5%, with nearly exclusive CO selectivity (95.6%). Notably, PXRD analysis (Fig. 4e) revealed the formation of χ-Fe5C2 under light irradiation at 200 °C, whereas metallic iron remained predominant under dark conditions. This suggests that light could somehow improve the formation of χ-Fe5C2, which is a key performer in the Fischer-Tropsch process. In the absence of χ-Fe5C2 phase, the reaction proceeds primarily via the RWGS pathway, yielding CO as the dominant product. In view of these results, we propose that light-induced charge carriers could not only enhance catalytic activity but also accelerate χ-Fe5C2 formation, which is crucial for hydrocarbon production at lower reaction temperatures.

Once we assessed the relative contributions of thermal and non-thermal effects, our focus shifted to elucidate the mechanistic insights of the CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction on the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst. To this end, we first performed in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS) measurements under dark conditions at 10 bar and temperatures ranging from room temperature to 250 °C (Fig. 5a). At approximately 100 °C, bicarbonate (HCO3-, 1662 cm−1) and bidentate carbonate (b-CO32-, 1285 and 836 cm−1) species were detected, indicative of CO₂ adsorption on the catalyst surface72–74. These species diminished at around 200 °C, consisting of the emergence of formate (HCOO-, 1572 and 1363 cm−1), suggesting its role as a key intermediate in the RWGS reaction75,76. At elevated temperatures, adsorbed CO species (2076 and 2058 cm−1) became prominent, indicating the decomposition of formate species to CO77. Upon reaching 250 °C, hydrocarbon-related bands emerged, with signals at 3013 and 1301 cm−1 assigned to gaseous CH4 and *CH3 species, respectively, confirming methane formation78. Additionally, bands at 1683, 1648, and 1623 cm−1 were attributed to C = C stretching vibrations, while signals at 976 and 860 cm−1 corresponded to out-of-plane C–H bending vibrations from hydrocarbons and alkenes8,79–81. These observations collectively support the formation of hydrocarbons via the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) pathway. Residual thermally stable carbonate species, including monodentate carbonate (m-CO32-, 1554 cm−1) and polydentate carbonate (p-CO32-, 1080 cm−1), were also identified at 250 °C74,82. We also analyzed in situ DRIFTS spectra of monometallic 2 K Cu and 2 K Fe catalysts to distinguish the respective roles of Cu and Fe in the CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27. On the 2 K Cu catalyst, bicarbonate (HCO3-, 1665 cm−1), bidentate carbonate (b-CO32-, 1765, 1273 cm−1), monodentate carbonate (m-CO32-, 1554 cm−1), and polydentate carbonate (p-CO32-, 1465 cm−1) species were observed upon CO2 exposure. Formate intermediates (HCOO-, 2893, 1592, 1374 cm−1) were subsequently generated through hydrogenation, and adsorbed CO species (2129, 2091, 2076, 2056 cm−1) were detected at higher temperatures, confirming the activity of RWGS reaction. However, no significant CH4 signals were observed, consistent with catalytic data (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that Cu primarily facilitates the RWGS reaction. In contrast, on the 2 K Fe catalyst, bicarbonate, carbonate, and formate species were similarly detected, alongside adsorbed *CO and gaseous CO species, supporting the hypothesis that residual Fe3O4 acts as an active phase for the RWGS reaction. At elevated temperatures, weak bands corresponding to *CH₃ and C = C species (3012, 2976, 2934 cm−1) emerged, confirming CO hydrogenation to hydrocarbons18,79. These results underscore the tandem catalytic behavior of the 2 K Cu2Fe1 system, with Cu sites predominantly driving the RWGS reaction and Fe sites enabling the Fischer-Tropsch process.

To further investigate the photo-chemical effects of light irradiation, in situ DRIFTS measurements were conducted under light illumination (Fig. 5b). The low light intensity of the irradiation source used in these experiments ensured negligible surface temperature changes, allowing the isolation of pure photo-chemical effects (Supplementary Fig. 28). Under illumination, the same species and intermediates were observed as in dark conditions, indicating that light does not alter the reaction mechanism, in good agreement with the catalytic results described above. However, the intermediates associated with the RWGS reaction and Fischer–Tropsch process, including bicarbonate/carbonate species (HCO3- at 1654 cm−1, b-CO32- at 1280 and 836 cm−1, and p-CO32- at 1418 cm−1), adsorbed CO (2076 and 2058 cm−1), *C-H species (1301, 1005, and 976 cm−1), and olefinic species (*C = CH at 2980 and 2937 cm−1, =*C-H at 860 cm−1), were observed at lower temperatures with significantly enhanced peak intensities under light illumination. These findings demonstrate that light irradiation effectively accelerates the intermediate formation, thereby improving CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction activity.

The formation rates of key intermediates were further examined during the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction. In this case, the IR peak intensities of bicarbonate (HCO3-), adsorbed CO (*CO), and =*C-H species were compared under light and dark conditions in the temperature range of 25-250 °C (Fig. 5c). Under illumination, the HCO3- peak intensity increased significantly, reaching a maximum at around 60 °C. Similarly, *CO and =*C-H species exhibited enhanced intensities upon light exposure. With elevated temperature, HCO3- intensity decreased due to the hydrogenation process in the RWGS reaction, while *CO and =*C-H intensities increased, reflecting CO formation and subsequent hydrocarbon production via the Fischer-Tropsch pathway. In contrast, under dark conditions, key intermediates reached their first plateau at 120 °C. Notably, when the temperature is steady at 250 °C, the intensities of *CO and =*C-H species rose significantly in both light and dark conditions, indicating the activity of the Fischer-Tropsch process. However, the differences between light and dark conditions diminished at elevated temperatures, suggesting reduced photo-chemical contributions. These results demonstrate that light illumination could accelerate the formation of key intermediates, thereby enhancing the overall CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction rate and hydrocarbon yield.

Building on our earlier investigations into the role of light intensity, we hypothesize that light irradiation could facilitate the formation of χ-Fe5C2 at lower reaction temperatures. To explore this theory, in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted under both illuminated and dark conditions (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 29). As shown in Fig. 6a, b, the diffraction of metallic Fe at 20.1° progressively diminished with increasing temperature, attributed to the iron carburization process during the CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction. Notably, under light irradiation, the diffraction corresponding to metallic Fe nearly disappeared at around 250 °C, whereas a small Fe peak persisted up to 300 °C under dark conditions. These observations were also confirmed by the change in the normalized intensity of the Fe peak (Fig. 6c). Moreover, diffractions of χ-Fe5C2 at 19.81° and 19.89° emerged around 250 °C and intensified with further temperature increase in the case of the irradiated sample. In fact, following a 120-minute hold at 300 °C, the in situ and long-term XRD patterns confirmed the formation of χ-Fe5C2 under illuminated conditions (Supplementary Fig. 30). Overall, these observations provide direct evidence for the conversion of metallic Fe to χ-Fe5C2 during the catalyst activation process. To further explore the detailed mechanism for Fe carburization, we propose three potential mechanisms by which light accelerates the χ-Fe5C2 formation: 1) Under light illumination, enhanced RWGS activity by excited Cu species increases the availability and partial pressure of CO, which accelerates Fe carburization. 2) Light irradiation may also excite Fe species, resulting in localized heating that enhances the carburization process. 3) The plasmonic effect on Cu not only enhances local temperatures across the catalyst surface but also facilitates energy transfer to the Fe species. The plasmonic Cu species act as a light absorber, capturing light and transferring energy via localized heating and/or electron transfer to the neighboring Fe species. These interactions could further reduce the carburization temperature of Fe, promoting the formation of χ-Fe5C2 at lower reaction temperatures. To confirm these hypotheses and to elucidate the role of light irradiation in iron carburization, CO-temperature-programmed reduction (CO-TPR) was performed on the 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalysts under both dark and illuminated conditions. As shown in Fig. 6d, the initial CO consumption, corresponding to CO adsorption on Cu species, occurs at 130 °C under illumination and at 190 °C in the dark for 2 K Cu2Fe183. The subsequent reduction of the Fe phase, associated with χ-Fe5C2 formation, is observed at around 260 °C under illumination, nearly 50 °C lower than under dark conditions. This reduction in temperature suggests that light irradiation could somehow facilitate χ-Fe5C2 formation. CO2-temperature-programmed surface reaction (CO2-TPSR) experiments could further support this interpretation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 31, compared with the dark condition, illumination lowers the CO₂ consumption temperature and the formation temperature of CO, CH₄, and hydrocarbon-related fragments by approximately 50 °C. In addition, the CH3 signal (m/z = 15) reaches a maximum at around 410 °C and then decreases significantly, suggesting further transformation of CHₓ species toward chain growth rather than complete desorption as methane. These observations are consistent with the in situ DRIFTS results, confirming that light irradiation accelerates the RWGS step, and further agree with the CO-TPR and in situ XRD results, showing that illumination lowers the Fe carburization temperature, consequently promoting hydrocarbon formation at lower temperatures. Taken together, CO-TPR and CO2-TPSR demonstrate that light irradiation promotes χ-Fe5C2 formation, most likely through plasmon excitation of Cu sites84,85. Indeed, UV–vis spectroscopy measurements (Supplementary Fig. 32a, solid line) reveal a characteristic plasmon absorption band of metallic Cu at 505 nm for 2 K Cu2Fe186,87. For deeper insight into the possible charge transfer events between both Cu and Fe species under irradiation, transient photocurrent measurements (Supplementary Fig. 32b) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy were performed on 2 K Cu2Fe1, 2 K Cu, and 2 K Fe (Supplementary Fig. 32c, d and Supplementary Table 6). The 2 K Cu2Fe1 catalyst exhibits the highest photocurrent intensity, surpassing that of the monometallic samples, which indicates improved charge separation and transfer efficiency between Cu and Fe in the bimetallic catalyst88. Notably, the 2 K Fe catalyst displayed an unstable photocurrent, suggesting inefficient charge carrier dynamics on Fe species. As for TRPL spectroscopy, the calculated PL decay lifetime of 2 K Cu2Fe1 (4.35 ns) is shorter than that of 2 K Cu (5.11 ns) and 2 K Fe (5.68 ns), indicating that the interaction between Cu and Fe species facilitates a rapid electron transfer between both metals. Moreover, the shorter lifetime in the case of the bimetallic sample suggests that photogenerated hot carriers efficiently migrate between Cu and Fe, providing additional non-radiative relaxation pathways for charge recombination and decreasing the PL lifetime. In fact, additional photocurrent measurements using quasi-monochromatic light in the range of 400–600 nm perfectly matched the absorption spectra of the sample, exhibiting a maximum intensity at 500 nm that correlates well with the plasmon band of Cu (Supplementary Fig. 32a, colored dots). Interestingly, these experiments provide more evidence of the presence of pure photochemical effects in the 2 K Cu2Fe1 sample under irradiation.

To further clarify the relationship between the plasmonic response of Cu and the catalytic performance in the photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon reaction, wavelength-dependent catalytic tests were conducted over 2 K Cu2Fe1 using bandpass filters under 300 °C external heating and 10 bar. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33a, the catalytic performance exhibits a clear dependence on the irradiation wavelength. The higher CO2 conversion is achieved under 500 nm illumination, whereas irradiation through the 600 nm bandpass filter results in the lowest CO2 conversion among the illuminated conditions. Notably, the light intensity and catalyst surface temperature are comparable under different quasi-monochromatic illumination conditions (Supplementary Fig. 33b), indicating that the wavelength-dependent activity trend cannot be simply ascribed to differences in macroscopic heating. In addition, the dark control experiment gives lower CO2 conversion and higher CO selectivity compared with the illuminated tests, further confirming the promoting role of photon irradiation in catalytic activity and product distribution. These results indicated that the light-induced enhancement arises from the combined contributions of photo-thermal heating and Cu-related plasmonic excitation84,89. In particular, the superior catalytic performance observed under 500 nm illumination supports the involvement of Cu-associated LSPR effect in promoting CO2 hydrogenation toward hydrocarbon products.

Revisiting the three hypotheses proposed above, our results point to the third mechanism as the most plausible origin of the light-promoted Fe carburization. Under illumination, plasmonic Cu sites can efficiently generate hot carriers, thereby accelerating the RWGS step through enhanced formation and transformation of bicarbonate/carbonate intermediates. This process increases the availability of CO-derived species required for subsequent Fe carburization. In parallel, plasmon-induced charge carriers may promote interfacial electron transfer at the Cu-Fe interface, modulating the electronic structure of adjacent Fe sites and enhancing their ability to activate and incorporate carbon-containing intermediates33,84,90. Such synergistic interactions between plasmonic Cu and Fe sites lower the apparent activation barrier for χ-Fe5C2 formation, consistent with the earlier emergence of the carbide phase observed under illumination in the in situ XRD measurements. To further confirm this synergistic effect, CO-TPR was also conducted on the monometallic 2 K Fe catalyst under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 34). Unlike 2 K Cu2Fe1 sample, the reduction in 2 K Fe catalyst occurs at nearly identical temperatures under both conditions, with no evidence of an early-stage CO uptake, reinforcing that the initial CO adsorption in 2 K Cu2Fe1 is associated with Cu. Additional mechanistic insights were gained from in situ DRIFTS experiments on 2 K Fe under illumination (Supplementary Fig. 35). While carbonate intermediates (e.g., b-CO32- at 1191 and 1176 cm−1) were observed under illumination for 2 K Fe, the temperature-dependent evolution of *CO and gaseous CO remained nearly unchanged between light and dark conditions. These findings reinforced the hypothesis that the plasmonic effect on Cu could improve hot carrier generation and effective electron transfer at the Cu-Fe interface. Those photo-chemical effects could contribute to activate the Fe sites, thus reducing the χ-Fe5C2 formation temperature and accelerating the χ-Fe5C2 formation.

Discussion

In summary, we reported a K-promoted Cu2Fe1 tandem photo-thermal catalyst that efficiently converts CO2 into hydrocarbons under light illumination, achieving a high C2+ selectivity of 77.4 % and an impressive C2-5 production rate of 5.04 mmol g−1 h−1 at 250 °C and 20 bar, significantly surpassing previous photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation benchmarks. This exceptional catalytic performance under mild reaction conditions is attributed to hot carrier generation and localized heating on the catalyst surface, induced by the plasmonic effect of Cu and efficient charge transfer at the Cu-Fe interface. Mechanistic studies revealed that light illumination promotes χ-Fe5C2 formation and accelerates intermediate formation, thereby reducing the C-C coupling barrier and enhancing photo-thermal CO2-to-hydrocarbon catalytic activity. This work represents a new paradigm in photo-thermal tandem catalysis by integrating plasmonic photoexcitation with bimetallic synergy to drive an efficient route for solar CO2-to-hydrocarbon conversion, advancing its potential for industrial implementation.

Methods

Catalyst Preparation

The bimetallic Cu-Fe catalysts were synthesized using a co-precipitation method with metal nitrate salts. Specifically, 16 mmol of Cu(NO3)2 ∙ 3H2O (Thermo Scientific, 99%) and 8 mmol of Fe(NO3)3 ∙ 9H2O (Thermo Scientific, >99%) were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water. Ammonium hydroxide (Sigma Aldrich, 28.0–30.0% NH3 basis) was then added dropwise under continuous stirring at room temperature until the pH of the solution reached 7. The resulting mixture was aged at room temperature for 3 h under stirring. The precipitate was filtered, thoroughly washed with deionized water, and dried overnight at 80 °C. Subsequently, 1 g of the dried precursor was ground and impregnated with an appropriate amount of KNO3 (Thermo Scientific, 99%) dissolved in 0.7 g of deionized water. The potassium promoter was introduced at different loadings of 1, 2, 3, and 5 wt%. The resulting catalysts are denoted as xK Cu2Fe1, where x represents the potassium loading. Subsequently, the impregnated precursors were calcined at 450 °C for 3 h. Lastly, the calcined sample was reduced in a flow of 6 vol% H2/Ar at 400 °C for 2 h, and then passivated in a flow of 5 vol% O2/N2 at room temperature for 1 h. Before the catalytic testing, the reduced samples were kept in a vial with a seal inside a glove box to prevent further oxidation. The catalyst with a Cu/Fe molar ratio of 2 was designated as Cu2Fe1. Additionally, bimetallic Fe-Cu catalysts with varying Cu/Fe ratios were prepared and labeled as Cu3Fe1 (Cu/Fe = 3), Cu1Fe1 (Cu/Fe = 1), and Cu1Fe2 (Cu/Fe = 0.5).

Photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation reaction

The photo-thermal CO2 hydrogenation reaction was evaluated using a commercial flow reactor equipped with a quartz window (Harrick, HVC-MRA-5) and a 300 W Xe lamp (PE300BFA, Perfectlight) as the irradiation source. In a typical experiment, 50 mg of pre-reduced catalyst was loaded on the reactor. A thermocouple to monitor the temperature was located ca. 1 mm below the catalyst’s surface. The reaction gas mixture (H2/CO2 molar ratio of 4: 1) was introduced through the reactor with a total flow rate of 10 mL min−1 (GSHV = 12000 mL h−1 g−1). Nitrogen was used as an internal standard. The evolved gases were analyzed by a gas compact chromatograph (CompactGC, Thermo Fisher) directly connected to the reactor outlet.
