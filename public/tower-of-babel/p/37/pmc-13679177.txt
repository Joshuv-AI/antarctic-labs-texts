Associations of body mass index categories with all-cause mortality across metabolic status in 3518 Japanese adults: a 10- and 22-year prospective cohort follow-up study
Authors: Ryuichi Kawamoto, Asuka Kikuchi, Daisuke Ninomiya, Masanori Abe, Teru Kumagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether metabolic status modifies the association between body mass index (BMI) and all-cause mortality in a Japanese general population.MethodsWe analyzed data from 3518 community-dwelling adults (1554 men and 1964 women; mean age: 64 ± 13 years) enrolled in the Nomura cohort studies (baseline surveys in 2002 or 2014). Mortality status was confirmed using Japan’s Basic Resident Register. Participants were classified into phenotypes based on BMI categories and the number of MetS components. Cox proportional hazards models with age as the time scale were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality.ResultsOver a median follow-up period of 17.3 years, 34.0% of participants died. Mortality risks varied according to BMI category and metabolic status. Among participants with 0–1 and 2 MetS components, underweight individuals consistently exhibited the highest incidence rates, the lowest cumulative survival, and significantly increased HRs for all-cause mortality (1.71; 95% CI, 1.25–2.34 and 2.26; 95% CI, 1.47–3.48, respectively). In contrast, among individuals with 3–4 MetS components, mortality risk was highest in those with obesity (2.07; 95% CI, 1.18–3.64), followed by those with underweight. A significant interaction was observed between BMI and metabolic status (p = 0.030). Across BMI categories, participants with greater numbers of MetS components generally exhibited higher incidence rates of all-cause mortality.ConclusionsThe association between BMI and all-cause mortality differed according to metabolic status. These findings suggest that metabolic status may influence the relationship between BMI and mortality risk in Japanese adults.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is commonly described as a cluster of coexisting metabolic abnormalities that synergistically contribute to cardiovascular risk, including insulin resistance, high blood pressure, impaired glycemic control, hypertriglyceridemia, and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels [1, 2]. MetS has emerged as a major global public health concern. In Japan, epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of MetS among individuals aged 20 years or older is approximately 27.0% in men and 10.0% in women [3]. The burden of MetS is expected to rise further alongside increasing obesity prevalence. Accumulating research has increasingly highlighted MetS as a key pathogenic factor underlying the onset of atherosclerosis [4], type 2 diabetes mellitus [5], and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [6]. Consistent with these findings, meta-analyses of large prospective cohort studies have demonstrated that MetS is associated with an approximately 1.5-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality, corresponding to a 24%–58% excess risk depending on population characteristics and diagnostic criteria [7].

Overweight and obesity are likewise major global public health challenges owing to their high prevalence and strong associations with a wide range of chronic diseases. According to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria based on body mass index (BMI), overweight is defined as 25.0–29.9 kg/m², while obesity is defined as 30.0 kg/m² or greater. However, BMI thresholds developed primarily in Western populations may not fully reflect adiposity-related risks in Asian populations. Global data from 2021 suggest that over one billion individuals were living with obesity, comprising about 650 million adults, 340 million adolescents, and 39 million children [8]. Obesity is widely recognized as a leading preventable contributor to premature mortality [9] and imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems globally. Although metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) has been regarded by some investigators as a relatively benign phenotype [10], this interpretation remains controversial, and numerous studies have challenged such a view. Conversely, underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) has also been associated with increased mortality, in some cases comparable to or exceeding that observed in obesity [11]. However, these associations may partly result from reverse causation or confounding factors such as smoking [12]. Additionally, BMI does not differentiate body composition or metabolic status, and accumulating evidence suggests that mortality risk across the BMI spectrum may vary according to metabolic health [13].

Importantly, a subset of normal-weight and underweight individuals develops metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance through mechanisms that are not solely explained by excess adiposity. The literature commonly refers to these conditions as metabolically unhealthy normal-weight (MUHNW) and metabolically unhealthy underweight (MUHUW) phenotypes [14–18]. In Asian populations, normal-weight and underweight individuals may exhibit metabolic abnormalities comparable to those observed in obesity, highlighting the need for heightened clinical awareness [15]. Compared with metabolically healthy normal-weight (MHNW) individuals, MUHNW has been repeatedly linked to markedly elevated risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [19]. Underweight individuals with underlying metabolic abnormalities may therefore represent a particularly high-risk group, especially among older adults. This observation suggests that metabolic dysfunction may influence the association between low BMI and mortality. Although this relationship remains insufficiently investigated, particularly in Asian populations [15, 20], metabolic abnormalities are closely linked to adiposity, and the relationship between BMI, metabolic dysfunction, and mortality is likely to be biologically interconnected rather than fully independent. Thus, beyond the traditional focus on obesity, it is important to clarify whether metabolic status influences the association between low BMI and mortality in East Asian populations, where lean but metabolically unhealthy phenotypes are relatively common.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate whether metabolic status modifies the association between BMI categories and all-cause mortality in a community-based Japanese cohort.

Materials & Methods

This study utilized data from the Nomura studies [21], which consisted of two prospective cohort investigations conducted in 2002 and 2014. The study population consisted of residents from rural regions of Ehime Prefecture, Japan, most of whom participated in regular annual health checkups conducted at the Nomura Health and Welfare Center. The initial cohort included 3,164 individuals, while the subsequent cohort enrolled 1,832 participants, with ages ranging from 19 to 90 years. Study procedures conformed to ethical standards and relevant regulations. Participants provided self-reported information on lifestyle, medical history, health conditions, and medication through questionnaires. A flowchart of participant inclusion and exclusion is presented in Fig. 1. Participants in Wave 1 were followed from 2002 to 2024, whereas those in Wave 2 were followed from 2014 to 2024. Median follow-up duration was 17.3 years (interquartile range: 10.4–22.2 years). Information on participants’ vital status was obtained from Japan’s Basic Resident Register. Information on cause-specific mortality or incident CVD events was not available in the Nomura studies [21]. A total of 3518 participants from the first and second Nomura cohorts were included in this analysis. Approval for the study protocol was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Ehime University Hospital (No. 1903018), and all participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study.

Data on demographic variables and health-related risk factors were obtained from medical records. BMI was derived using the standard formula of weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Smoking burden was summarized as pack-years, calculated by multiplying daily cigarette consumption (packs/day) by the number of years of smoking. Based on this scheme, participants were classified into four smoking categories: never smokers, former smokers, light smokers (< 20 pack-years), and heavy smokers (≥ 20 pack-years). Alcohol intake was quantified using Japanese standard liquor units, with one unit equivalent to 22.9 g of ethanol. Accordingly, participants were grouped as non-drinkers, occasional drinkers (< 1 unit/day), light habitual drinkers (1–2 units/day), or heavy habitual drinkers (2–3 units/day). No participants exceeded an intake of 3 units per day. Blood pressure was assessed with an automated device (BP-103i; Colin, Aichi, Japan) following a minimum of five minutes of seated rest. The cuff size was selected to fit the participant’s arm and placed on the right upper arm, with the average of two successive measurements recorded. Fasting blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast to determine serum concentrations of HDL-C, triglycerides (TG), serum creatinine (Cr), uric acid (SUA), and fasting plasma glucose (FPG). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated separately for men and women using serum Cr–specific equations based on threshold values [22]. Sex-specific eGFR equations were applied using different serum creatinine thresholds for men (0.9 mg/dL) and women (0.7 mg/dL), with separate formulas used above and below each cutoff.

Participants were classified as having CKD if their eGFR was below 60 mL/min/1.73 m². CVD was defined as a history of ischemic heart disease, ischemic stroke, or peripheral arterial disease.

Definition of metabolically unhealthy profile, underweight, normal weight, pre-obesity, and obesity

Because this study focused on BMI-based stratification of metabolically unhealthy phenotypes, waist circumference was not included in the criteria, thereby minimizing the influence of body fat distribution or visceral fat. Consequently, metabolic unhealthiness has commonly been defined as the presence of at least two MetS components [14, 23–25]. Participants were classified as having metabolic syndrome if they exhibited at least three metabolic disturbances, defined as elevated blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medication), hypertriglyceridemia (TG ≥ 150 mg/dL), low HDL-C levels (< 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women), or elevated FPG, indicated by FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL or treatment for diabetes [26]. BMI categories were defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, classifying participants as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), pre-obese (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), or obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²) [27]. For stratified analyses, participants were additionally categorized according to the number of MetS components as follows: 0–1 components, 2 components, and 3–4 components. These categories were used to evaluate the combined associations of BMI categories and metabolic status with all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Variables following a normal distribution are presented as means ± standard deviations, whereas those with skewed distributions are shown as medians with interquartile ranges (e.g., TG and FPG). Prior to analysis, non-normally distributed variables underwent logarithmic transformation. Continuous variables were compared across groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality. Follow-up duration was calculated from baseline examination until death or censoring. Primary multivariable models were adjusted for sex, age, smoking status, and drinking status to minimize potential overadjustment for downstream cardiometabolic conditions. To improve the interpretability of the interaction model and reduce potential multicollinearity, BMI was mean-centered before inclusion of the BMI × MetS interaction term in the Cox proportional hazards models. For interaction analyses, MetS was dichotomized as 0–2 versus 3–4 MetS components to distinguish participants with more severe metabolic abnormalities. Sensitivity analyses stratified by cohort wave and age group, as well as analyses excluding deaths occurring within the first 3 years of follow-up, were additionally performed to assess the robustness of the findings. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log-minus-log survival plots. Interaction terms between centered BMI and dichotomized MetS status were included to evaluate potential effect modification. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants according to BMI categories

A total of 3518 participants were analyzed, consisting of 1554 male participants (mean age 63 ± 14 years, range 20–90 years) and 1964 female participants (mean age 65 ± 12 years, range 19–89 years). The median duration of follow-up was 6316 days (17.3 years), with an interquartile range of 3828–8121 days (10.5–22.2 years). Over the course of follow-up, 1197 deaths occurred (34.0% of the cohort), comprising 605 men (38.9% of male participants) and 592 women (30.1% of female participants). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics according to BMI categories are shown in Table 1. In total, 177 participants were categorized as underweight, 2393 as normal weight, 851 as pre-obese, and 97 as obese. Younger age, higher BMI, SBP, DBP, TG, FPG, and the prevalence of antihypertensive and antidiabetic medication use increased significantly with increasing BMI category. In contrast, HDL-C levels decreased significantly as BMI category increased. Baseline metabolic characteristics according to BMI categories are summarized in Table 2. The prevalence of elevated blood pressure, low HDL-cholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and elevated fasting plasma glucose increased significantly with increasing BMI category. Similarly, the prevalence of participants with 2 or more MetS components increased progressively across BMI categories.

Incidence rates of all-cause mortality according to BMI categories and metabolic status

Figure 2 shows the person-years, number of events, and incidence rates (per 1000 person-years) of all-cause mortality across different metabolic–BMI phenotypes. Among participants with 0–1 and 2 MetS components, the incidence rates of all-cause mortality were highest in those who were underweight compared with those with normal weight, pre-obesity, or obesity. In contrast, among participants with 3–4 MetS components, the incidence rates of all-cause mortality were highest in the obesity group, followed by the underweight group. In addition, participants with a greater number of MetS components generally exhibited higher incidence rates of all-cause mortality across BMI categories.

Adjusted survival curves for all-cause mortality according to BMI categories and metabolic status

Adjusted survival curves were generated to assess cumulative survival across BMI categories (Fig. 3). In the strata comprising 0–1 and 2 MetS components, lower BMI categories were associated with lower cumulative survival probabilities (p = 0.006 and p = 0.001, respectively). Conversely, among participants with 3–4 MetS components, the lowest survival probability was observed in the obesity group, followed by the underweight group (p = 0.065).

Associations of BMI and MetS with all-cause mortality

Table 3 summarizes the HRs and 95% CIs for all-cause mortality according to BMI and MetS based on Cox proportional hazards models. In the fully adjusted model, lower centered BMI was significantly associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality. Although MetS itself was not independently associated with mortality risk, a significant interaction between centered BMI and MetS was observed (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.02–1.13), indicating that the association between BMI and mortality differed according to metabolic status.

Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for all-cause mortality according to BMI categories and metabolic status

Discussion

The present cohort study examined the associations of BMI categories and metabolic status with all-cause mortality in a rural Japanese population. We found that mortality risk differed according to BMI category and metabolic status. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have examined these associations in a community-based Japanese cohort. Specifically, our findings suggest that metabolically unhealthy phenotypes among normal-weight and underweight individuals may be relatively common in Japanese populations [25]. Among participants with two or fewer MetS components, the risk of all-cause mortality was highest among underweight individuals. By contrast, among participants with three or four MetS components, the relationship between BMI categories and all-cause mortality exhibited a J-shaped pattern. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of careful clinical assessment not only of individuals with metabolically unhealthy obesity but also of underweight individuals, particularly among those with multiple MetS components.

Growing evidence from diverse cohorts has demonstrated that BMI categories substantially influence mortality risk across different metabolic health statuses. Our findings align with several large international prospective studies [28, 29]. For example, a population-based investigation in the United Kingdom reported that individuals with two or more metabolic abnormalities had markedly higher risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, irrespective of their obesity status [30]. In an additional cohort comprising 10,824 individuals (mean age 55.3 years), a total of 2274 deaths occurred during a mean follow-up period of 18.4 years, corresponding to 198,776 person-years of observation. In participants with multiple metabolic abnormalities, adjusted analyses identified a J-shaped association between BMI and all-cause mortality, characterized by higher risks at both low and high BMI levels. The corresponding HRs (95% CIs) were 2.0 (1.5–2.7) for underweight and 2.8 (2.1–3.6) for obesity [25]. Similarly, recent findings from a large U.S. cohort (50,013 adults) demonstrated that metabolic unhealthiness was associated with increased mortality risk, regardless of obesity status, with metabolically unhealthy non-obese individuals showing particularly elevated disease-specific mortality. Among metabolically unhealthy individuals, obesity was paradoxically associated with a modestly reduced risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.85–0.98), underscoring the complex interplay between adiposity and health outcomes [31]. Collectively, these results reinforce the importance of considering both BMI category and metabolic status to achieve more accurate risk stratification [31]. Consistent with prior research, our study also showed that among individuals with up to two metabolic risk factors, underweight status was associated with higher mortality risk than obesity. However, among those with three or more metabolic abnormalities, the highest mortality risk was observed in the obesity group in our cohort.

In contrast, underweight individuals exhibited a consistently higher risk for mortality outcomes regardless of metabolic status, except for non-fatal CHD [29]. In a cohort analysis of 12,467 individuals drawn from the Swedish Twin Registry, analyses incorporating combined BMI and metabolic health categories demonstrated that, relative to metabolically healthy normal-weight (MHNW) participants, those classified as metabolically unhealthy with obesity (MUHO) experienced a 53% increase in mortality risk during midlife (HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.19–1.96). Additionally, during late adulthood, mortality risk was significantly elevated across all BMI strata, encompassing normal weight (HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.01–1.25), overweight (HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.01–1.21), and obesity (HR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.15–1.49). By contrast, among older individuals, metabolically healthy overweight status was associated with a modest reduction in all-cause mortality (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.83–0.99) [28]. Similarly, because the majority of participants in our cohort were middle-aged or older adults, our findings are consistent with the possibility that the association between BMI and mortality differs according to metabolic status.

Several mechanisms may account for these findings. Previous studies have demonstrated that metabolically abnormal individuals with normal body weight may exhibit physiological disturbances, including diminished insulin responsiveness, increased oxidative stress, and elevated blood pressure comparable to levels observed in obese populations [32, 33]. In our study, even among metabolically healthy individuals, those with lower body weight exhibited a higher mortality risk. Beyond the mechanisms noted above, factors such as inadequate nutritional reserves and the presence of undiagnosed comorbidities (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and sarcopenia) may also contribute to the elevated mortality risk [34, 35]. In addition, because Asian populations are prone to metabolic abnormalities at lower BMI levels [36], BMI alone may not adequately capture high-risk individuals in Japan.

Study strengths and limitations

This study is strengthened by its extended follow-up period, relatively large sample size, and adjustment for several potential confounding variables. At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants were predominantly community-dwelling adults of middle to older age (mean age 64 ± 12 years) from rural Japanese communities, all of whom underwent regular health examinations. This may restrict the applicability of the findings to populations beyond the study setting. Second, mortality data were solely derived from the basic resident register, and individuals who moved outside the study area during the follow-up period were not included. Third, the influence of baseline or follow-up factors—such as the use of medications (e.g., antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or antidiabetic drugs), pre-existing medical conditions, and lifestyle changes—could not be fully accounted for. Finally, the relatively small number of participants in some subgroup analyses, particularly the obesity group, may have limited the statistical power to detect weaker associations.

Conclusions

In this prospective cohort of Japanese adults aged 19 years or older, the association between BMI and all-cause mortality differed according to metabolic status. Underweight individuals exhibited higher mortality risk among participants with fewer MetS components, whereas obesity was associated with the highest mortality risk among those with multiple metabolic abnormalities. These findings underscore the importance of considering both BMI category and metabolic status when evaluating mortality risk. Further studies are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to inform more individualized prevention and management strategies.
