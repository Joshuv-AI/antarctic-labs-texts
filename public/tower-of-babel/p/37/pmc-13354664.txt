Histological evaluation of energy-intensive irrigation systems for debridement of uninstrumented oval root canals with different access cavity designs
Authors: Olcay Özdemir, Mustafa Gündoğar, Taha Özyürek, Nergiz Bolat, Özgecan Gündoğar, Sena Kaşıkçı, Markus Haapasalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of energy-intensive irrigation activation systems for removing residual pulp tissue in completely uninstrumented oval root canals with different access cavity designs, using histological analysis. Seventy extracted human mandibular premolars with oval root canal morphology and organic tissue remnants were randomly assigned to five irrigation protocols: syringe-needle irrigation (control), Er: YAG laser (SWEEPS, Fotona), Er, Cr: YSGG laser (Waterlase iPlus, Biolase), multisonic high-speed irrigation (GentleWave G4 with ProControl, SonEndo, 5-min protocol), and an extended multisonic protocol (9-min). Each group was further divided according to access cavity design into ultra-conservative and traditional cavities (n = 7). Following irrigation with sodium hypochlorite and EDTA, serial histological sections from the apical third were prepared, and the percentage of residual pulp tissue was quantified using ImageJ software. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). The irrigation activation system significantly affected residual tissue percentages (p < 0.001; η²=0.736), whereas access cavity design showed no significant effect (p = 0.965). Syringe-needle irrigation demonstrated the highest residual tissue values (≈ 20%). Both laser activation systems significantly reduced residual tissue compared with needle irrigation but showed comparable performance (≈ 4–6%). Multisonic high-speed irrigation further improved canal cleanliness, with the 5-min protocol showing lower residual tissue values (≈ 2%), and the extended 9-min protocol achieving the lowest percentages (< 1%), approaching near-complete debridement. Within the limitations of this in vitro histological study, energy-intensive irrigation systems significantly improved the removal of residual pulp tissue in uninstrumented oval root canals. The extended multisonic irrigation protocol demonstrated the highest debridement efficiency under the tested conditions. These findings may inform future minimally invasive endodontic strategies; however, they should not be interpreted as supporting the clinical replacement of mechanical instrumentation.

Introduction

In recent decades, the shift of medicine toward minimally invasive procedures has been both remarkable and scientifically well supported. As dentistry naturally mirrors this evolution, vital pulp preservation, conservative access cavity designs, minimally invasive instrumentation, and micro-operative procedures have emerged as a central focus in endodontics [1–3]. The most conservative approach is considered to be the preservation of tooth vitality whenever possible [4, 5]. In this context, the classification of pulp pathology has been revised from the traditional dichotomy of reversible or irreversible to a graded system of severe, moderate, mild, and initial, with the term “irreversible” being questioned in endodontic diagnostic terminology [6]. Nevertheless, when pulp tissue is severely compromised to preclude vital approaches, the current trend emphasizes preparations that are adequate yet as limited as possible for both access cavity design and root canal instrumentation. Silva et al. proposed a new terminology for cavity designs as follows: traditional, conservative, ultra-conservative, truss, caries-driven, and restorative-driven access cavities [2]. The primary drawback of these geometric designs is that, although they preserve natural tissues, they demand advanced equipment, particularly superior irrigation systems, to address biomechanical challenges such as incomplete debridement and persistent infected residues. The impact of cavity design on the quality of root canal cleaning in uninstrumented root canals is poorly known.

Irrigation activation has for a long time been a focus of special interest in endodontics, as it is well recognised that instrumentation alone is mostly insufficient to ensure a successful root canal treatment prognosis [7, 8], and even passive ultrasonic activation that currently regarded as a standard irrigation activation method, shows weaker cleaning effects compared with newer energy-intensive methods, which refer to activation technologies that deliver additional physical energy to irrigants by various mechanisms, such as laser activation or multisonic high-speed irrigation [9–11].

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting minimally invasive approaches in recent years, a fundamental question that could represent a paradigm shift in endodontics remains unanswered: Is it possible to perform root canal treatment without instrumentation in cases of severely compromised pulpal pathology? In this context, advanced irrigation activation systems that deliver additional physical energy to irrigants beyond conventional syringe irrigation have attracted growing interest [12–14]. By enhancing fluid movement, acoustic streaming, cavitation-related effects, and multisonic hydrodynamic activity within the root canal system, these energy-intensive irrigation approaches may improve irrigant exchange and tissue disruption in anatomically complex or minimally prepared canals. Among these systems, Er: YAG laser activation, Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation, and GentleWave multisonic high-speed irrigation have been proposed as potential strategies to enhance debridement efficacy under minimally instrumented conditions [12–16].

To the best of our knowledge, evidence systematically comparing debridement efficiency between lasers and multisonic high-speed irrigation systems in a completely uninstrumented model of oval root canals with organic tissue remnants, evaluated by histological analysis, is limited. In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the debridement efficacy of Er: YAG laser activation, Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation, and GentleWave G4 with ProControl at different application times in completely uninstrumented oval root canals with two different access cavity designs using histological analysis. The experimental design aims to provide a novel perspective for root canal treatment protocols without mechanical instrumentation. Two null hypotheses were established, as follows: (1) There would be no significant difference in residual pulp tissue percentages among the advanced energy-intensive irrigation systems used in uninstrumented oval root canals. (2) Access cavity design would have no significant effect on residual pulp tissue percentages regardless of the irrigation activation system used.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines. Ethics approval was obtained from the local Non-Interventional Ethics Committee (protocol no: 2025/2384). The methodology is outlined below: sample size calculation, sample selection and eligibility; preparation of samples and group allocation; root canal debridement; histologic analysis, statistical analysis, and interpretation.

Sample size calculation, sample selection, and eligibility

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (V3, Kiel University, Kiel, Germany). Based on previous histological studies evaluating residual tissue percentages, an effect size (f) of 0.53 was estimated [7]. The calculation was based on a one-way ANOVA fixed effects model comparing the groups. The analysis indicated a minimum sample size of 14 per group (α = 0.05, 1-beta = 0.95), with a total of 70 tooth samples across the five main groups.

The inclusion criteria comprised oval mandibular premolars with a single root canal and an intact coronal structure, selected with the highest possible standardization, extracted from patients aged 18–35 years. Eligible teeth were those planned to be extracted for orthodontic reasons. No tooth was extracted specifically for this study, in accordance with ethical considerations; instead, samples were obtained from teeth extracted for clinical reasons that met the inclusion criteria, following written informed consent from the patients for their use. The exclusion criteria included teeth with multiple root canals, non-oval morphology, structural anomalies, immaturity, previous endodontic treatment, limited restorability due to tissue loss, or cracks or fracture lines.

After extraction, teeth were mechanically cleaned of soft tissue remnants and stored in 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C to prevent dehydration and microbial growth until use. Prior to experimentation, the samples were rinsed thoroughly under running water to remove storage residues. The minimum storage period of two weeks was intended to allow spontaneous necrosis of residual pulp tissue. The oval root canal configuration was determined as follows: measurement of the space corresponding to the root canal lumen at 5 mm from the apex. The root canals were classified as oval when the mesiodistal diameter was 2.5 times longer than the buccolingual diameter on periapical radiographs, as proposed in previous studies [17, 18].

Allocation of groups

Randomization was performed by labeling the selected samples sequentially from 1 to 70. Using web-based randomizer software (https://www.randomizer.org), five main groups for irrigation activation systems and two subgroups for each regarding access cavity designs were generated, each comprising seven unique numbers within this range, to ensure a coded experimental design.

Preparation of the samples

Two different access cavity designs were selected, and the samples were prepared to achieve either traditional (T-AC) or ultra-conservative access cavities (UC-AC) [2]. For the ultra-conservative access cavity, a standardized preparation was created corresponding to the width of a diamond fissure bur (no: SF13-C, 1.6 mm) at the center of the occlusal table. For the traditional access cavity, preparation was performed according to the projection of the canal walls onto the occlusal table (Fig. 1) [7, 8]. The preparations were performed by a single experienced endodontist to ensure methodological standardization. After access cavity preparations, apical patency was confirmed using a #10 K-file (M-Access, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) to determine the working length. For standardization, apical sizes greater than #10 were excluded from samples. The working length was defined as 1 mm short of the length at which the file tip became visible at the apical foramen. Following the determination of working length, sample lengths were standardized by occlusal reduction. Subsequently, the specimens were embedded in silicone impression material (C-type, Zhermack Zetaplus, Badia Polesine, Italy) to simulate root sockets and create a closed canal system.

Before irrigation protocols, passive insertion of the irrigation or activation tips to the intended depth was verified in each specimen. Because only teeth with wide, single, oval canals and specimens allowing passive placement to the intended depth without binding were included for standardization, tip placement 2–3 mm short of the working length was achievable in all specimens.

Root canal debridement

Syringe-needle irrigation (n = 14)

Using a 30-gauge side-vented needle (NaviTip, Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) inserted passively up to 2–3 mm short of the working length without binding, the root canals were irrigated via a needle tip without activation using 5 mL of 5% sodium hypochlorite (Endoart, Inci Dental, Istanbul, Türkiye), applied in cycles of 20 s of continuous rinsing followed by a 20-second resting phase inside the canal. This was followed by intermediate rinsing with 2 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, 2 mL of 17% EDTA (Inci Dental, Istanbul, Türkiye) was delivered using the same cycle protocol (20 s of continuous rinsing and 20 s of resting), and the irrigation sequence was finalized with 2 mL of sodium hypochlorite with the same protocol and the same time interval, including continuous rinsing and resting phases, and 2 mL of distilled water as a final rinse.

Laser-based irrigation (Er: YAG laser) (n = 14)

The SWEEPS laser (Skypulse Laser, Fotona, Ljubljana, Slovenia, 2940 nm wavelength, with air–water cooling-off application) was used to activate the solution within the access cavity. The irrigation protocol consisted of five cycles with sodium hypochlorite (5 mL, 5%), followed by two cycles with EDTA (2 mL, 17%) and two cycles with sodium hypochlorite. Each cycle included a 20-second solution refresh, a 20-second resting phase inside the canal, and 20 s of laser activation. Between sodium hypochlorite and EDTA applications, and upon completion of the irrigation protocol, 2 mL of distilled water was used as an intermediate and final rinse without activation. A 30-gauge side-vented needle was used to deliver the mentioned solutions to the root canal system.

Laser-based irrigation (Er, Cr: YSGG) (n = 14)

The Waterlase iPlus laser (Biolase Technology Inc., San Clemente, CA, 2780 nm wavelength, with air–water cooling-off application) was used to activate the irrigant in the root canal system with a fiber tip (RFT2-25). The console settings were adjusted to 50 Hz, 25 mJ, and 1.25 W. The fiber tip was positioned 2–3 mm short of the working length. The irrigation protocol consisted of five cycles with 5 mL of 5% sodium hypochlorite, followed by two cycles with EDTA and two cycles with sodium hypochlorite. Each cycle included a 20-second solution refresh, a 20-second resting phase inside the canal, and 20 s of laser activation with a 1–4 mm back-and-forth movement. Between sodium hypochlorite and EDTA applications, and at the end of the irrigation protocol, 2 mL of distilled water was used as an intermediate and final rinse without activation. A 30-gauge side-vented needle was used to deliver the mentioned solutions to the root canal system.

Multisonic high-speed irrigation (MHSI, Standard application- 5 min) (n = 14)

The GentleWave G4 system (Sonendo Inc., Laguna Hills, CA) was used with ProControl software in Research Mode Enabled, operated via a CleanFlow handpiece. An occlusal platform was fabricated by SoundSeal (Sonendo Inc., CA) for the airtight access cavity. Fluid dynamics were generated by the GentleWave console, which was set to operate at a flow rate of 45–50 mL/min. ProControl was used to adjust the NaOCl concentration to 5%, and the irrigation protocol consisted of 5 min of sodium hypochlorite, followed by 20 s of distilled water, 2 min of EDTA (8%), and 45 s of distilled water.

Multisonic high-speed irrigation (MHSI, Extended time application- 9 min) (n = 14)

The GentleWave G4 system was used with ProControl™ software in Research Mode Enabled, operated via a CleanFlow handpiece. An occlusal platform was fabricated by SoundSeal for the airtight access cavity. Fluid dynamics were generated by the GentleWave console, which was set to operate at a flow rate of 45–50 mL/min. ProControl was used to adjust the NaOCl concentration to 5%, and the irrigation protocol consisted of 9 min of sodium hypochlorite, followed by 20 s of distilled water, 2 min of EDTA (8%), and 45 s of distilled water.

Histological analysis

Each root canal was rinsed with 1 mL of distilled water and dried before histological procedures. The specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h, then demineralized in a solution of 22.5% (w/v) formic acid and 10% (w/v) sodium citrate for three weeks. After demineralization, the samples were rinsed under running tap water for 24 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin for histological processing. Serial sections of 3 μm thickness were obtained at 0.3 mm intervals, starting from the 5-mm apical level. The sections were mounted on glass slides and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E). Sample coding was performed prior to analysis to maintain examiner blinding. From each tooth, three sections were selected from the apical 1–3 mm region at standardized intervals. Sections were considered eligible when the canal lumen was clearly visible, the section was free from processing artifacts such as tearing, folding, or tissue detachment, and the canal outline could be reliably delineated for area measurement. Histological processing and sectioning were conducted by an independent investigator who was not involved in the experimental procedures.

Observations were primarily performed at ×10 magnification for standardized area measurement; however, additional higher magnifications (×20 and ×40) were used qualitatively to confirm tissue identification at the pulp–dentin interface (Olympus BX51, Tokyo, Japan). Histological images were coded prior to evaluation, and examiners were blinded to group assignment. Images were analyzed using ImageJ (web-based version, https://ij.imjoy.io). For each histological image, the total canal lumen area was first delineated manually using the freehand selection tool in ImageJ. Residual pulp tissue remnants within the canal space were then independently identified and outlined on H&E-stained sections by calibrated examiners. For each section, the mean value of the two examiners’ measurements was calculated. Overall, 21 histological Sect. (3 sections from each of 7 teeth) were evaluated per subgroup. The mean value of the three sections from each tooth was subsequently calculated, and the tooth was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis. Histological evaluations and recordings were performed independently by two calibrated examiners who were blinded to group allocation. Interobserver reproducibility between the two calibrated examiners was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), demonstrating strong agreement (ICC > 0.80).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 31, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis, and values within ± 1.96 were considered indicative of a normal distribution. In addition, the Shapiro–Wilk test demonstrated no significant deviation from normality (p > 0.05). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) for all groups. To evaluate the effects of irrigation activation systems and access cavity designs on the amount of residual pulp tissue, two-way analysis of variance (Two-Way ANOVA/factorial ANOVA) was applied. Within this model, the main effects of the irrigation activation system and cavity design, and the interaction between the two factors, were examined. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η²) values.

Independent-samples t-tests were used to assess differences between conservative and traditional cavity designs within each irrigation activation system. In addition, one-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate differences among irrigation activation systems within each cavity design group. When statistically significant differences were detected, Bonferroni multiple comparison tests were applied to identify pairwise group differences. For all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The two-way ANOVA analysis showed that the irrigation activation system had a statistically significant impact on the residual pulp tissue percentage (p¹ < 0.001; η² = 0.736). This suggests that a large portion of the variance in the amount of residual pulp tissue remaining in the root canal was due to the irrigation system used. In contrast, the access cavity design did not have a statistically significant effect on residual pulp tissue percentage (p¹ = 0.965; η² = 0.000). Furthermore, the interaction effect between the irrigation activation system and cavity design was not statistically significant (p¹ = 0.988; η² = 0.002). One-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni multiple comparisons tests, was conducted to evaluate differences among irrigation systems within each cavity design group. The results revealed statistically significant differences among irrigation activation systems in both conservative and traditional cavity groups (p³ < 0.001).

According to the multiple comparison analysis, the highest residual tissue percentages were observed in the SNI group, which showed significantly higher values than all other groups. The laser groups showed similar levels of residual tissue and did not differ significantly from one another. The GentleWave 5-min protocol yielded lower residual pulp tissue values than the laser groups. The lowest residual pulp tissue percentages were found in the GentleWave 9-min group, which demonstrated significantly better cleaning performance than all other irrigation protocols. Discussion

The present study compared the effectiveness of various advanced irrigation activation techniques on the quality of debridement of organic tissue remnants from non-instrumented premolar root canals using a minimally invasive approach, and performed histological analysis of the apical portion. There were two null hypotheses regarding the effects of irrigation activation techniques and access cavity designs. The first null hypothesis, which proposed that no significant differences would exist among the irrigation activation systems, was rejected. Besides, none of the protocols achieved complete tissue removal in all specimens, indicating that “complete debridement” remains challenging even with advanced activation. Additionally, within-group pairwise comparisons showed no differences in residual apical tissue among teeth with different cavity designs; therefore, the second null hypothesis could not be rejected.

It must be emphasized that clinical root canal treatment relies on the synergistic interaction between mechanical instrumentation and chemical irrigation [8, 19]. The present study intentionally excluded mechanical preparation to isolate the independent effect of irrigation activation systems. Although this approach enables a controlled comparison of activation technologies, it limits direct clinical extrapolation. The findings, therefore, reflect a proof-of-concept model rather than a substitute for conventional biomechanical preparation or irrigation protocols.

The analysis in the present study was limited to the apical 1–3 mm region because this area is among the most anatomically complex and clinically challenging portions of the root canal system [20]. Previous histological and microbiological studies have demonstrated that residual tissue remnants and microorganisms may persist within apical ramifications, isthmuses, and inaccessible recesses despite instrumentation and irrigation procedures [20, 21]. Furthermore, contemporary irrigation-centered concepts in endodontics emphasize that effective debridement depends not only on apical enlargement but also on optimizing irrigant exchange, activation, and hydrodynamic interaction within the functional canal volume [22]. In this context, the apical third remains a critical region for evaluating the debridement potential of advanced irrigation systems in anatomically complex canals, particularly under minimally prepared or uninstrumented conditions.

The results showed that conventional needle irrigation, which served as the control group, had the highest percentage of residual tissue in the apical canals. This result, while validating the experimental setup and confirming the presence of organic tissue, is also consistent with previous studies demonstrating that needle irrigation alone is insufficient to ensure complete debridement of the root canal system [23, 24].

The laser-activated irrigation systems, both Er: YAG (SWEEPS) and Er, Cr: YSGG (Waterlase iPlus), significantly reduced residual tissue compared with needle irrigation and did not differ significantly from each other. This finding is consistent with earlier research reporting that activation, regardless of technique, improves irrigant penetration and debris removal compared with conventional irrigation [25]. However, in the context of uninstrumented root canal treatment, the present study demonstrated relatively parallel rates of residual pulp tissue to those reported by Gündoğar et al., who used a similar methodology with traditional access cavities but minimal instrumentation (20/04) [11]. The similarity in residual tissue percentages may be related to the increased number of activation cycles used in the present study. It should be noted, however, that while the laser tip of the Er: YAG system is always recommended to be used in the pulp chamber, the recommendations and practice for the Er, Cr: YSGG laser show much variation. The recommendations regarding laser tip placement vary from the pulp chamber to the apical third of the canal [11, 26]. Concerns have been raised about high pressure and possible damage to the canal wall dentin when the tip is placed deep in the canal [15]. In the present study, we placed the tip close to the WL, which may explain the insignificant difference between the two lasers.

Among the various systems tested, multisonic high-speed irrigation (GentleWave G4 System with ProControl), regardless of access cavity design or application time, achieved the lowest residual tissue percentages. These results suggest that advanced circulation-driven activation enhances fluid dynamics, generating more effective shear forces along the root canal walls and anatomical complexities, consistent with previous studies [10, 27]. Furthermore, for the uninstrumented root canal treatment concept, the GentleWave G4 ProControl software (5 min), recently introduced as an updated version, yielded results comparable to the reported study of its kind for minimally invasive root canal treatment [11]. However, the extended-time protocol (9 min) resulted in the lowest residual tissue percentages among all groups and demonstrated nearly complete debridement independent of cavity design, even under completely uninstrumented conditions. These findings further emphasize the importance of sodium hypochlorite volume and continuous irrigant exchange for effective debridement [28]. The large volume of irrigant processed by the multisonic high-speed irrigation system over a relatively short period may represent an important advantage for both instrumented and minimally prepared canal concepts.

Nevertheless, the findings of the present study, particularly those related to the extended-time multisonic protocol, should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence under controlled experimental conditions and should not be extrapolated directly to clinical non-instrumentation protocols without further validation. Therefore, the superior performance of the multisonic protocols cannot be attributed solely to the activation mechanism itself, but may also be associated with greater irrigant volume, continuous replenishment, longer application time, and system-specific fluid dynamics.

Standardization is essential for in vitro studies evaluating irrigation efficacy [19]. A major limitation of the present study is the heterogeneity in irrigation protocols across groups, including differences in irrigant volume, EDTA concentration (17% vs. 8%), activation dynamics, and total application time. These variations reflect manufacturer and evidence-based clinical protocols, as well as the intrinsic operational characteristics of each system [29]. However, they prevent the strict isolation of activation technology as the sole independent variable and may have influenced the magnitude of intergroup differences.

The multisonic high-speed irrigation system recirculates approximately 45–50 mL of irrigant per minute, a volume that is clinically difficult to match using conventional methods [13, 14, 16]. Since equalizing solution volumes is not practically feasible, the number of activation cycles and total solution volumes was increased for laser-based groups, particularly within a non-instrumentation concept, unlike in previous studies [11, 30–32], to minimize the impact of this factor. 17% EDTA was used across all experimental groups to align with previous studies and common clinical practice [29], whereas the multisonic high-speed irrigation system protocol uses 8% EDTA. This discrepancy can be justified by the fact that the primary role of EDTA is a chelation agent and used for smear layer removal [30, 33], and because the present study was based on a non-instrumentation concept, no smear layer formation was expected; thus, the difference in concentration is unlikely to have affected the outcomes. Therefore, the present study should be interpreted as a comparison of clinically recommended irrigation protocols rather than a strict evaluation of activation technology alone.

As another limitation, only one type of anatomical configuration (oval-shaped single-rooted premolars) was evaluated. While this selection may limit generalizability, it can also be considered representative of clinical conditions, as premolars show high anatomical variability in the apical third despite having relatively similar mid-root and coronal morphologies [18]. Furthermore, since the study focused on non-instrumentation, oval premolars were intentionally selected because they typically exhibit the greatest intracanal tissue volume, providing a more rigorous test of these irrigation protocols to achieve the expected results in this experimental setup. Another limitation of this study is that bacterial inoculation and culturing methods, which pose some complex anatomical challenges, were not used. Besides, it should be noted that the results are only applicable to wide root canals, such as mandibular premolars with oval anatomy and open canals where the laser probe can be placed near the working length, which may be another limitation. Also, it should be acknowledged that storage conditions may influence dentin properties and the composition of residual organic tissue, which represents a limitation of the ex vivo design.

Despite the remarkable improvements observed, residual tissue was still detected in all groups, indicating that no irrigation protocol can fully clean the canal system. These results are consistent with previous reports emphasizing that complete debridement remains challenging due to the anatomical complexity of root canal systems [19, 20, 34]. However, recent technology provides promising outcomes and supports a shift toward more conservative endodontic approaches. Future studies should explore optimized activation parameters, combinations of activation techniques (instrumented or non-instrumented), various complex anatomical structures, the efficiency in necrotic or vital tissues, and their impact on long-term clinical outcomes, such as periapical healing and treatment success.

In conclusion, within the limitations of the study, energy-intensive irrigation systems markedly enhance the debridement of uninstrumented oval canals by increasing solution volume and the number of activation cycles. The multisonic high-speed irrigation system extended-time protocol provided the most effective cleaning within the conditions tested, and may inform future minimally invasive strategies, although mechanical instrumentation remains essential in current clinical protocols. Caution is warranted regarding the direct clinical application of a non-instrumentation concept, which is not yet recommended and must await further research and clinical follow-up. Ongoing research on advanced irrigation techniques may contribute to the development of more conservative endodontic treatment strategies without compromising cleaning efficacy.
