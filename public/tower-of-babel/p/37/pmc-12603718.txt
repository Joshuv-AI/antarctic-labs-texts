Meta-analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of computed tomography angiography compared with invasive coronary angiography in preoperative cardiac surgery planning: a focus on valve surgery patients
Authors: Dina Alwaheidi, Ahsan Ehtesham, Samim Azizi, Laith Tbishat, Mohd Lateef Wani, Abdulwahid Almulla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective To investigate the diagnostic performance of coronary CT angiography (CCTA) for assessing significant coronary artery disease (CAD) in patients referred for surgical aortic valve replacement or transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI)\transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), with invasive coronary angiography (ICA) as the reference standard.MethodsWe performed a meta-analysis of 28 studies to compare CCTA with ICA for preoperative coronary evaluation. Studies were stratified into two subgroups: the first consisting of those which included only patients undergoing valve surgery (n=19) and the second including TAVI or mixed (TAVI and surgical) populations (n=9). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and accuracy were recorded or determined, and a summary diagnostic performance was obtained by a random effects model. Pooled forest plots and summary receiver operating characteristic curves were also analysed.ResultsThe overall sensitivity of CCTA to diagnose significant CAD varied between 18 studies, ranging from 85% to 94%; the pooled sensitivity over all 28 studies was 91% (95% CI 88% to 93%) and the specificity was 88% (95% CI 84% to 91%). The pooled PPV was 78% (95% CI 72% to 83%), while the NPV was 95% (95% CI 93% to 97%). The diagnostic performance of the study was 89.8%.ConclusionsCCTA is a trustworthy, non-invasive diagnostic option to rule out significant CAD in patients undergoing valve surgery. Its high specificity in surgical candidates favours its use as a ‘gatekeeper’ to ICA with a potential reduction in unnecessary invasive surgery.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) often coincides with valvular heart disease, particularly in elderly patients who are undergoing aortic valve or mitral valve replacement and in patients being considered for transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Adequate recognition of significant CAD is important in this group, for concurrent coronary disease can affect procedural planning, prognosis and the risk of perioperative events.

This subgroup of patients is frequently older and frail, with a high burden of coronary calcification and arrhythmias, which complicates both non-invasive imaging and invasive coronary angiography (ICA). At the same time, this population is at greater procedural risk from ICA, underscoring the need for accurate, less invasive diagnostic strategies.

Coronary angiography is the gold standard to assess the presence of CAD before valve procedures, with mostly ICA. Nevertheless, ICA is an invasive technique that carries the possibility of bleeding, vascular complications, contrast nephropathy and increased cost (procedure and hospital stay). In such a setting, non-invasive imaging techniques, coronary CT angiography (CCTA) being one of them, are considered as potential alternatives for CAD assessment in patients undergoing valve interventions. It is estimated that 20–70% of candidates for preoperative evaluation can safely be excluded from ICA on the basis of CCTA results, thus lowering the risks associated with the procedure and saving resources.13

CCTA provides high-resolution, three-dimensional visualisation of coronary anatomy, and its high accuracy has been shown in many series. Yet, the diagnostic performance of this non-invasive, less radiation-exposing imaging technique can be influenced by factors such as heavy calcification, arrhythmias and tachycardia, which are not infrequent in patients with advanced valvular disease.

Many single studies and former meta-analyses confirmed the accuracy of CCTA in TAVI patients who are mostly older with more calcifications. However, little is known regarding its accuracy in those referred to surgical valve intervention, who are typically younger with potentially less calcification and who may differ substantially with regard to anatomy, clinical characteristics and the extent of CAD.

CCTA has a low risk of procedural complications, significantly lower than ICA, which comes with vascular, embolic and contrast-induced nephropathy risks.4 No major adverse cardiovascular events have been reported in patients treated with CCTA alone at follow-up of up to 1 year. The radiation dose of contemporary CCTA is dramatically below that of early procedures, now equivalent to or lower than other cardiac imaging modalities.5 6 Adverse reactions related to contrast are unusual but must be accounted for, with renal failure particularly in mind.7 Furthermore, evidence from studies indicates that with the new generation CT scanners (≥64-slice, dual-source, photon-counting), higher quality and diagnostic accuracy are achieved compared with lower end CT scanners.8

As interest continues to increase in ascertaining methods of reducing invasive testing and further streamlining preoperative pathways, it is important that we accurately establish the diagnostic performance of CCTA in both surgical and transcatheter valve populations. The aim of this meta-analysis was to assess the overall diagnostic performance of CCTA against ICA for assessing significant CAD in patients referred for surgical valve interventions and TAVI patients, respectively. With subgroup analyses, we intend to ascertain the role of the CCTA for various procedural populations and possibly as a non-invasive gatekeeper for ICA.

Unlike prior meta-analyses in general CAD populations, this study specifically focuses on surgical valve and also reviews TAVI patients, offering subgroup insights and the most updated synthesis to date.

Furthermore, the application of Computed Tomography-Fractional Flow Reserve (CT-FFR) and ultra-high-resolution CT is being actively investigated to improve diagnostic performance in future.9 These existing gaps define implications for patient safety, procedural planning and healthcare expenditures, highlighting proper prospective assessment.10 Brendel et al assessed the diagnostic efficiency of artificial intelligence (AI) modules for coronary artery disease (CAD) diagnosis for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) candidates. The results imply that by use of AI, there may be a promising future to boost CT accuracy, especially for frail comorbid patients, and may substantially reduce the safety concerns associated with invasive techniques.11

This systematic review aims to pool the existing evidence of the diagnostic capabilities, feasibility and clinical implications of CCTA relative to ICA in preoperative surgical valve patients. The objective of this review is to delineate CCTA’s ability to safely rule out significant CAD, decrease invasive procedures and influence clinical practice with regard to current knowledge deficiencies and future research and practice.

Methods

We carried out a meta-analysis and systematic review based on Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). A full literature review was conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus and Cochrane Library databases, from inception to July 2025. The search strategy encompassed the words ‘CT angiography’, ‘coronary computed tomography angiography’, ‘CCTA’, ‘valve surgery’, ‘transcatheter aortic valve implantation’, ‘TAVI’, ‘TAVR/Transcatheter aortic valve replacement’, ‘coronary artery disease’ and ‘diagnostic accuracy’. No language restrictions were applied. In addition, the reference lists of included studies and related reviews were manually searched to identify additional eligible studies.

Titles and abstracts were screened by two review authors (initials blinded) working independently. The full texts of articles were subsequently evaluated for inclusion using predetermined criteria. Divergences were resolved by consensus or consulting a third reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were independently extracted by two authors onto a predefined Excel sheet. The following data were extracted: study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), patient population (TAVI vs surgical valve), scanner technology, CAD prevalence (present vs not present) and 2×2 diagnostic data (TP, FP, TN, FN). When more than one threshold was applied, data of ≥50% stenosis were preferred. Risk of bias was analysed by means of the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool, which investigates four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard and flow/timing. Categories of judgements on risk of bias were categorised as low, high or unclear.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were prespecified for surgical valve replacement versus TAVI candidates, acknowledging important clinical differences. Surgical valve patients tend to be younger with lower calcification burden, while TAVI candidates are older, frequently frail and more prone to coronary calcification and arrhythmias, factors known to affect CCTA image quality and diagnostic accuracy. This stratification was undertaken to clarify potential variation in CCTA performance between these two clinically distinct groups.

Statistical analysis

Pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and diagnostic OR (DOR) were calculated using a bivariate random effects model. Summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were generated. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, and sources of heterogeneity were explored based on scanner generation, CAD definition thresholds and population characteristics. Publication bias was evaluated with funnel plots of log DOR against SE. Analyses were performed using STATA (version 19).

We explicitly acknowledge that variability across CT acquisition protocols, reconstruction algorithms and CAD definitions (≥50% vs ≥70% stenosis) introduces heterogeneity, which was addressed through sensitivity analyses.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Database searching yielded 3000 records in total. Following duplicate removal and screening, 28 articles satisfied inclusion criteria and were eligible for quantitative synthesis (figure 1: PRISMA diagram). 19 of these articles focused on patients who underwent a surgical valve procedure (surgical valve group), whereas nine included patients who underwent either TAVI or a mixed cohort of patients undergoing TAVI and surgical valve (TAVI/mixed group). Overall, the pooled cohort included 8525 patients, with a median prevalence of significant CAD across studies of 29% (range: 12–61%) (table 1).

Overall diagnostic performance

Pooled sensitivity and specificity for CCTA for detection of significant CAD across all 28 studies were 91% (95% CI 88% to 93%) and 88% (95% CI 84% to 91%), respectively. The pooled PPV and NPV were 78% (95% CI 72% to 83%) and 95% (95% CI 93% to 97%), respectively.

The pooled DOR was 69.1 (95% CI 32.9 to 145.0), consistent with strong discriminatory performance. The SROC curve demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94, consistent with excellent overall diagnostic efficacy (figure 2).

Subgroup analysis

Valve surgery group (n=19 studies, 5132 patients)

For surgical valve interventions, the pooled sensitivity was 92% (95% CI 89% to 94%) and the specificity was 87% (95% CI 82% to 91%). The combined PPV and NPV were 76% (95% CI 69% to 83%) and 96% (95% CI 93% to 98%), respectively. The diagnostic sensitivity in this subgroup was 89.4%, and the AUC was 0.94 on SROC analysis (figure 4A).

TAVI/mixed population group (n=9 studies, 3393 individuals)

For studies considering patients undergoing TAVI, or mixed TAVI and surgical procedures, the combined sensitivity was 89% and specificity was 89% (95% CI 84% to 92% and 95% CI 85% to 93%, respectively). The PPV and NPV were 82% (95% CI 76% to 87%) and 93% (95% CI 90% to 96%), respectively. The total diagnostic value was 89.7%, and the AUC was 0.94 (figure 4B). The pooled DOR in the valve surgery subgroup was 92.3 (95% CI 33.3 to 255.5) and in the TAVI/mixed subgroup was 41.9 (95% CI 8.94 to 196.4), indicating a trend towards lower discriminatory power in TAVI cohorts, consistent with heavier coronary calcification and arrhythmia.

Caveats

Outliers were detected in a few studies which we believe may be a result of the study population or sample size. The small range of results indicates good and consistent sensitivity performance of CCTA for the detection of significant CAD in preoperative valve patients (figure 5).

Heterogeneity and quality assessment

There was moderate between-study heterogeneity for sensitivity (I²=51%) and specificity (I²=61%). Sensitivity analyses stratified by scanner generation (≥64-slice vs advanced dual-source), stenosis threshold (≥50% vs ≥70%) and baseline CAD prevalence demonstrated that scanner technology and CAD definition thresholds contributed most to heterogeneity. Subgroup differences (surgical vs TAVI) also accounted for part of the observed variability.

With regard to QUADAS-2 evaluation, most of the 28 studies were at low risk of bias across domains, but six were classified as unclear in the flow and timing domain due to lack of reporting on the interval between CCTA and ICA (figure 6).

Discussion

This meta-analysis, based on 28 studies with 19 260 patients, shows that CCTA is an accurate non-invasive technique for preoperative coronary evaluation in both surgical cohorts and TAVI patients. It represents the largest meta-analysis on presurgical patient assessment to date. The combined sensitivity and specificity across all studies were high at 96% and 90%, respectively, demonstrating the excellent accuracy of CCTA for detecting significant CAD.

In subgroup analyses, CCTA’s performance remained strong in both valve surgery and TAVI/mixed populations. In particular, in 19 studies (n=10 430) including only surgical valve populations, the CCTA sensitivity was 96% (95% CI 94% to 98%) and specificity was 91% (95% CI 88% to 93%), with an AUC of 0.97. These results were consistent with previous studies, including Ren et al,12 with a reported sensitivity of 89% and specificity of 90% for significant CAD. In TAVI and mixed population studies (n=8830), sensitivity was also high at 94% (95% CI 90% to 97%), though specificity was slightly lower at 87% (95% CI 83% to 91%). This modest reduction in specificity in TAVI cohorts is consistent with prior reports and likely reflects the impact of heavy coronary calcification and arrhythmias that create blooming artefacts and reduce interpretability, as shown in other studies such as Opolski et al13 and Lopes et al.14

The consistently high NPV (96% across subgroups) confirms the reliability of CCTA for excluding CAD in these patients. Several of the included studies (eg, Larsen et al15 and Caldonazo et al16) also highlight the importance of CCTA in preoperative planning, suggesting benefits for surgical timing and resource allocation. Moreover, works such as Joshi et al17 and Bettencourt et al18 demonstrate practical application of CCTA in routine preoperative protocols.

Nonetheless, moderate heterogeneity was observed (I²=51% for sensitivity, I²=61% for specificity). This variability was largely attributable to differences in scanner generation (64-slice vs dual-source vs photon-counting), thresholds for significant stenosis (≥50% vs ≥70%) and baseline CAD prevalence. Patient-level differences, particularly in TAVI cohorts with calcification and arrhythmias, further contributed to reduced specificity.

Studies such as Xuewen et al19 focused on the diagnostic limitations of Computed Tomography Coronary Angiography (CCTA) in patients with rheumatic mitral disease and arrhythmias. The safety profile of CTCA is much better than that of ICA, with significantly fewer procedural complications and much less risk of vascular injury, embolisation and contrast nephropathy. Modern CT protocols have reduced the radiation exposure to a level similar to that of other commonly used cardiac imaging modalities. Clinical endpoints have not been statistically different whether ICA is omitted after negative CTCA, suggesting the safety of CTCA within preoperative pathways.

However, outcome-level evidence remains sparse. None of the included studies directly linked CCTA-based decisions to perioperative MACE (major adverse cardiac events), myocardial infarction or mortality outcomes, limiting the clinical validation of adopting CCTA as a universal gatekeeper strategy.

Limitations

Despite these positive results, several limitations exist. Heterogeneity in study methodology, imaging protocols and patient selection limits uniform applicability. There are limited long-term outcome data and no randomised controlled trial evidence demonstrating the impact of CCTA-guided management on morbidity, mortality or cost-effectiveness in valve patients. Cost-effectiveness analysis was not available in included studies, despite being essential for supporting the broader implementation of a CCTA-first strategy in preoperative pathways.

The recently published CT2TAVI registry reinforces the feasibility of CCTA, particularly when combined with CT-FFR, showing that ICA could be deferred in nearly 60% of TAVI patients without increased adverse events at 1 year.20 Similarly, Becker et al found CCTA sensitivity of 94.0% but only moderate specificity (72.4%) for ruling out obstructive CAD in TAVI patients, with specificity improved when CT-FFR was incorporated.21 These findings highlight that while CCTA is highly effective at ruling out CAD, it is less reliable at confirming significant stenosis in heavily calcified arteries, and adjunctive technologies (CT-FFR, AI-based algorithms, photon-counting CT) may help mitigate this limitation in the future. A recent meta-analysis of Diller et al with CCTA, which exhibited a high pooled sensitivity (95%) and NPV (95%) for CCTA in detection of CAD, demonstrated its significant exclusion performance. However, specificity (73%) and PPV (64%) were relatively low, reflecting CCTA’s role as a gatekeeper and limitations in the presence of heavily calcified arteries and protocol standardisation.22

Other studies (eg, Fahrni et al23) show improved performance with ultra-high-resolution CT, underscoring the value of technological advances. Implementation of standardised reporting (eg, Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System CAD-RADS) could further enhance reproducibility and clinical adoption. Economic lean with potential cost reduction in terms of fewer invasive procedures and earlier hospital motion also supports practical application of CTCA in the precardiac surgery evaluation pathway.24

Crucially, the results are consistent with contemporary European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS) guidelines recommending CCTA in selected individuals with low pretest probability and optimal image quality. Because of its invasiveness and associated risks, our data lend support to the increasingly held opinion that CCTA is a safe gatekeeper to ICA in the perioperative evaluation, particularly if performed with ≥64-slice or new generation scanners.

Publication bias is also possible, given that most included studies were single-centre, observational designs with small sample sizes and a tendency to publish positive findings.

Conclusion

This extensive meta-analysis, including 28 studies with over 8000 patients, is the largest systematic review to date to evaluate CCTA for preoperative coronary assessment. Our results reaffirm that CCTA has high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 96%; specificity 90%) in surgical compared with TAVI cohorts. In view of its high NPV and comparable sensitivity to ICA, CCTA is a safe, effective, non-invasive tool for excluding CAD before valve surgery .

Nevertheless, moderate heterogeneity, reduced specificity in heavily calcified TAVI populations, lack of outcome-level data and absence of cost-effectiveness studies should temper enthusiasm for universal implementation. Larger multicentre studies with standardised protocols, outcome validation and economic analyses are required to define the role of CCTA as a definitive gatekeeper to ICA in surgical valve pathways. The broader application of CCTA in TAVI populations, supported by systematic imaging protocols and multicentre registries, has provided important insights that can be translated to surgical valve patients to improve preoperative assessment and streamline care pathways.
