Exploring the interplay between systemic immune–inflammatory response, nutritional patterns, and metabolic health in MAFLD
Authors: Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy, Ismail Kandil, Gehad Mohsen ELmazny, Ahmed M Salah, Mohammad Abohassan, Mansuor A Alanazi, Safa labib, Elsayed A Eid, Faria Jafarzadeh, Reem Elmahdy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) represents a highly prevalent liver disorder characterized by hepatic fat accumulation in the context of metabolic impairment. Persistent low-grade inflammation is recognized as a central mechanism underlying its development and progression. The Systemic Immune–Inflammation Index (SII), derived from circulating neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, has emerged as a composite indicator of inflammatory and immune status. Nevertheless, limited evidence exists regarding its association with dietary quality and cardiometabolic features in individuals with MAFLD. This aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between SII levels, metabolic parameters and adherence to a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern in adults diagnosed with MAFLD. In this cross-sectional study, a total of 254 adults with confirmed MAFLD were enrolled in this cross-sectional analysis. Participants were stratified into tertiles according to SII values. Anthropometric measurements, biochemical parameters, and dietary data were obtained for all subjects. Habitual food consumption was evaluated using a validated food frequency questionnaire, and diet quality was quantified through the Mediterranean Dietary Quality Index (MED-DQI). Between-group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance and Chi-square tests. Partial correlation analysis was used to assess the association between SII, MED-DQI and metabolic variables adjusted for the confounders. Also, multivariable general linear models were applied to control for potential confounding variables. Participants within the highest SII tertile demonstrated significantly higher serum triglyceride concentrations (T3: 197.42 ± 108.83 mg/dL vs. T1: 147.55 ± 87.77 mg/dL; P < 0.05) and markedly lower HDL-C levels compared with those in lower tertiles (T1: 51.94 ± 13.34 mg/dL vs. T3: 45.28 ± 11.08 mg/dL; P < 0.001). Although overall MED-DQI scores were not significantly different between SII tertiles, poorer scores in the fruits and vegetables component were observed among individuals with elevated SII. A positive correlation was found between SII and neutrophil counts (r = 0.438), platelet counts (r = 0.309) and a negative correlation with lymphocyte counts (r = -0.466) and HDL cholesterol (r = -0.238; for all of them P < 0.001). Triglyceride levels was also positively correlated with SII (r = 0.214, P = 0.001). A significant positive correlation was observed between MED-DQI and lymphocyte counts (r = 0.215) and HDL (r = 0.158, P < 0.001 and P < 0.05 respectively). Higher systemic immune–inflammatory burden is associated with higher TG and lower HDL and lower dietary fruits and vegetables score in patients with MAFLD. These findings highlight a potential link between inflammatory activation, dietary inadequacy, and metabolic disturbances. Due to cross-sectional design of the current study, casualty cannot be directly inferred and prospective investigations are warranted to clarify the causal direction of these associations.Keywords: Systemic Immune–Inflammation Index, Metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease, Mediterranean dietary pattern, Dyslipidemia, Antioxidant intakeSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Endocrinology, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Metabolically-dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is a newly proposed term that reflects the metabolic underpinnings of fatty liver disease more accurately than the traditional term non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)1,2. MAFLD is diagnosed based on the presence of hepatic steatosis in conjunction with metabolic risk factors such as overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or evidence of metabolic dysregulation with minimal or no significant alcohol consumption3. This redefinition shifts into inclusion of metabolic dysfunction, offering a more clinically meaningful and inclusive framework for identifying individuals at risk4,5. The global prevalence of MAFLD is estimated at approximately 25–30%, with rising trends paralleling the increase in obesity and metabolic syndrome6,7. MAFLD is associated with a wide range of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease and has become a leading cause of chronic liver-related morbidity and mortality worldwide1,2. Recent studies indicate that MAFLD is independently associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF), potentially mediated by chronic inflammation and immune responses8. The latest research indicates that the dysregulation of the AMPK-SREBP1-FASN (ASF) signaling axis plays a core role in mediating abnormal fatty acid synthesis and the accumulation of lipotoxic metabolites in MAFLD, serving as a key molecular hub linking hepatic metabolic disorders with immune inflammatory responses9. Dysregulated ASF axis and the subsequent lipotoxic products not only directly impair hepatocellular organelle function but also, by disrupting intercellular communication and reprogramming immune cell and hepatocyte metabolism, drive a self-perpetuating “metabolic-immune” vicious cycle10.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a central pathophysiological mechanism in MAFLD, contributing to hepatic fat accumulation, insulin resistance, and fibrogenesis11,12. The interplay between immune cells and metabolic stress results in the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which accelerate hepatic injury and systemic metabolic dysfunction13,14. While traditional inflammatory biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin (IL)-6 provide limited insight into the full spectrum of immune responses, the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII)—calculated from neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts—offers a comprehensive reflection of immune-inflammatory balance15,16. SII has been validated in oncology and cardiovascular disease settings, as a predictor of poor outcomes and has recently gained attention in metabolic liver diseases17. Elevated SII levels have been linked to increased severity of hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and cardiometabolic risk in individuals with MAFLD, suggesting its potential utility as a noninvasive marker of systemic inflammation and disease progression17–19.

Diet is a modifiable determinant of MAFLD, influencing both hepatic lipid metabolism and systemic inflammatory responses20. Diets high in saturated fat, refined carbohydrates, and processed foods are associated with increased oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release—all of which promote hepatic steatosis and fibrosis promoting fat accumulation in the liver by activating pathways like Sterol Regulatory Element-Binding Protein 1c (SREBP-1c), leading to steatosis21,22. In contrast, diets rich in anti-inflammatory nutrients—such as polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), fiber, polyphenols, and antioxidant vitamins—may reduce liver fat accumulation and systemic inflammation23,24. The Mediterranean diet, in particular, has demonstrated beneficial effects on liver enzymes, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory markers by reduce liver inflammation and fat buildup by improving cellular energy balance and reducing oxidative stress in individuals with MAFLD25,26. The interplay between diet and hepatic steatosis, the hallmark of MAFLD, is critically important, with dietary patterns playing a pivotal role in both disease etiology and management. Emerging evidence suggests that adherence to diets like the Mediterranean diet, rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil, may offer significant protective benefits against liver dysfunctions20. These components are associated with reduced inflammation, improved insulin sensitivity, and favorable lipid profiles – all key factors in mitigating hepatic steatosis20,25–27. Specifically, the high content of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and polyphenols in olive oil, the fiber and antioxidants in fruits and vegetables collectively combat oxidative stress and modulate metabolic pathways implicated in MAFLD pathogenesis20,25–27. While the precise mechanisms are still under investigation, these dietary principles represent a promising therapeutic avenue. Concurrently, it is crucial to acknowledge that alcohol consumption can act as a significant confounding factor, potentially masking or distorting the specific associations between diet, systemic inflammation, and hepatic outcomes. Therefore, carefully accounting for and often excluding subjects with significant alcohol intake is essential to isolate the distinct impact of beneficial dietary patterns like the Mediterranean diet and immune-inflammatory markers on metabolic health, thereby ensuring the precision and validity of the observed correlations28. The Mediterranean Dietary Quality Index (MED-DQI) is a validated tool used to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet, a dietary pattern known for its cardiometabolic and anti-inflammatory benefits29. Lower MED-DQI scores, indicating higher adherence, have been associated with reduced risk of metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance in liver disorders27. Given that MAFLD is strongly linked to poor diet quality and systemic inflammation, evaluating MED-DQI in this population may provide insights into modifiable dietary strategies for managing disease progression.

The SII has emerged as a robust marker for predicting outcomes in various chronic conditions. Existing literature underscores its clinical utility, including predicting mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)30, its associations with psychological distress such as depression31, and its correlation with thyroid nodule development in T2DM patients32. Furthermore, SII has been linked to adverse metabolic and structural changes, such as elevated body fat—particularly android adiposity33—and increased risks of sarcopenia34 and osteoporosis35. Beyond systemic conditions, SII has also demonstrated prognostic value in oncology, specifically in predicting outcomes following curative resection for hepatocellular carcinoma15. Despite these established clinical associations, the role of SII in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD) remains largely unexplored. To date, only one study (2025) has identified SII as a potential risk factor for MAFLD19. Crucially, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding the interplay between SII and the metabolic-nutritional profile of these patients. While the inflammatory nature of MAFLD and the impact of dietary patterns on metabolic health are well-documented individually, no study has yet concurrently investigated how the systemic immune-inflammatory milieu correlates with specific metabolic parameters and dietary behaviors in the MAFLD population. Addressing this tripartite relationship is essential to deciphering the complex etiology of MAFLD and identifying precise, modifiable targets for therapeutic intervention. Given the emerging recognition of SII as a composite, noninvasive marker of inflammatory and immune status, it may serve as a valuable tool in assessing metabolic risk in this population. Therefore, the current study aims to evaluate (a) the association between the SII, metabolic parameters including blood sugar, lipids and liver enzymes and MED-DQI in patients with MAFLD and to assess (b) the correlation between SII and MED-DQI with metabolic parameters in these patients.

Methods

Subjects

This cross-sectional study included adult participants diagnosed with MAFLD. Individuals were recruited from outpatient clinics affiliated to King Khalid University by convenience sampling method. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were adults aged 18 to 50 years with a confirmed diagnosis of MAFLD based on clinical and laboratory assessments. Individuals were required to have a stable health status, with no history of acute illness in the past three months. Only participants with stable levels of physical activity (no major changes in exercise routine during the previous three months) were included. MAFLD was defined based on the international consensus criteria as the presence of hepatic steatosis, confirmed by ultrasound imaging by an experienced radiologist, and the findings were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by a gastroenterologist to enhance diagnostic reliability, in combination with at least one of the following three conditions: Overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 25 kg/m²), T2DM, or evidence of metabolic dysregulation (i.e., at least two metabolic risk abnormalities such as increased waist circumference, hypertension, elevated triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), prediabetes, or high-sensitivity CRP > 2 mg/L)1. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: Significant alcohol consumption (> 30 g/day for men and > 20 g/day for women)36–38, known viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV) or autoimmune liver disease, or current use of hepatotoxic medications, acute infection within the preceding 4 weeks or any febrile illness at the time of assessment, chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disorders (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease), hematologic disorders that affect blood cell counts (e.g., hemolytic anemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, chronic leukemias), advanced renal disease or decompensated hepatic failure, recent major surgery or hospitalization within the previous 3 months, current pregnancy or lactation, use of systemic corticosteroids, cytotoxic chemotherapy, long-term immunosuppressive therapy, or other medications known to markedly alter inflammatory markers or hematologic parameters. All individuals with any history of alcohol consumption were excluded from this study. Additionally, we recorded use of commonly prescribed medications (e.g., statins, antihypertensive medications, and vitamin supplements). The required sample size was calculated using the single-mean formula: n= , where n is the initial sample size, Z α/2 is the Z-score corresponding to the desired confidence level (1.96 for 95%), σ is the estimated standard deviation of the variable, and d is the margin of error. Based on a standard deviation of 71 mg/dl for triglycerides reported in a similar study39 and a margin of error of ± 9.16 mg/dl, the calculated initial sample size was 231. To account for an anticipated 10% dropout, the target recruitment was adjusted to 254 participants.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mansoura University (Ethics code: R.26.02.3585.R2). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before participation, written informed consent was obtained from all individuals, and all collected data were stored in anonymized form and handled in accordance with institutional data protection regulations.

Measurements of variables

Anthropometric measurements were collected using standard procedures; body weight and height were measured using a Seca digital scale (Seca GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height in meters squared (m²). Waist circumference (WC) was measured using a non-stretchable tape with the participants standing, arms relaxed at the sides, and wearing light clothing. WC was recorded at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest at the end of a normal expiration. Hip circumference (HC) was measured at the level of the greatest protrusion of the buttocks, ensuring the tape was held horizontally and without compressing the skin. The waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated by dividing WC (cm) by HC (cm). After a 12-hour overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected and analyzed at a certified clinical laboratory. The following biochemical parameters were assessed: Lipid profile including, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (calculated using the Friedewald formula if TG < 400 mg/dL)40. All measurements were performed using enzymatic colorimetric kits from Roche Diagnostics (Mannheim, Germany) on a Roche Cobas c311 analyzer. Liver enzymes including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were also analyzed using Roche kits and the Cobas c311 system. Fasting blood sugar (FBS) was measured by the glucose oxidase method using Roche kits on the Roche Cobas c311 analyzer and complete blood count (CBC), including neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts were obtained using a Sysmex XN-1000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). These values were used to calculate the SII, using the following formula15: SII=. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)41. Participants were asked to report their usual frequency of consumption of each food item over the past year, along with portion sizes. The reported frequencies were then converted to grams per day using household measures. Energy and nutrient intakes were calculated using Nutritionist IV software (First Databank Inc., Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, USA). Dietary intake was assessed by a trained clinical nutritionist. Participants with implausible total energy intake values (e.g., < 800 or > 4200 kcal/day for men, < 600 or > 3500 kcal/day for women) were excluded from dietary analyses42. The MED-DQI was used to assess participants’ adherence to the Mediterranean dietary pattern as described by Gerber M et al.43–45. This index is based on the intake of seven key dietary components, each scored from 0 (optimal) to 2 (least favorable), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 14. A lower MED-DQI score reflects better adherence to the Mediterranean diet and higher dietary quality. The components of the MED-DQI and its scoring method are shown in Fig. 1. Physical activity (PA) was assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)46.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The final study sample included 254 individuals, divided evenly into tertiles of SII (approximately 85 participants per tertile). This stratification ensured sufficient power (> 80%) to detect moderate differences in metabolic and dietary indicators between groups at an alpha level of 0.05. The tertile cutoffs were as follows: first tertile (T1), < 344.52; second tertile (T2), 344.52 ≤ SII < 550.56 and third tertile (T3), ≥ 550.56. This tertile-based classification is widely used in cross-sectional nutritional research to uncover potential dose–response trends and to address non-linearity in biomarker distributions47,48.

The data were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to compare means across different groups. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, the model assumptions were formally assessed. Normality of residuals was examined using Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. The assumption of independence of errors was considered satisfied based on the study design. Post-hoc analyses were performed using the Bonferroni correction to identify specific group differences where the overall ANOVA was significant.

For categorical data, group differences were evaluated using the Chi-square test. To investigate the confounder-adjusted comparison between SII tertiles and biochemical and dietary outcomes, we applied general linear models (GLMs) in two models. The first model was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, physical activity, smoking status and stage of steatosis while the second model additionally included hypertension and medication use (e.g., statins, antihypertensive medications, and vitamin supplements. All analyses were performed using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Also, linear regression analysis was performed to assess the linear trend of variables across SII tertiles and β coefficients, corresponding 95% confidence intervals and P-for trends after adjustment for all of variables were reported. Partial correlation analysis was also used to further evaluate the associations between SII and MED-DQI with study variables. A p-value below 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

As presented in Table 1, no statistically significant differences were observed in age, sex distribution, stage of steatosis, WC, WHR, BMI, smoking status, medication use, physical activity, and hypertension prevalence across SII tertiles (all P > 0.05). The mean age ranged from 39.96 to 40.25 years, and BMI ranged from 31.42 to 32.04 kg/m² across tertiles. The proportion of male participants was also similar across tertiles (T1: 47.6%, T2: 45.9%, T3: 42.4%). All of the patients were in the grade 1 or 2 of the Steatosis severity grading and there was no significant difference in steatosis grading distribution across SII tertiles (P = 0.941). Table 2 summarizes the comparison of biochemical markers among the tertiles of SII. Participants in the highest SII tertile had significantly higher TG levels (T3: 197.42 ± 108.83 mg/dL) compared to those in the lowest tertile in crude and confounder-adjusted models (T1: 147.55 ± 87.77 mg/dL; P < 0.05). Similarly, HDL-C levels significantly decreased across tertiles in all three models (T1: 51.94 ± 13.34 mg/dL vs. T3: 45.28 ± 11.08 mg/dL; P < 0.001).

Neutrophil counts progressively increased across SII tertiles (T1: 4.12, T2: 6.90, T3: 8.37 × 10⁹/L; P < 0.001), showing a positive correlation (β = 0.492). Conversely, lymphocyte counts significantly decreased with higher SII tertiles (T1: 4.75, T2: 4.10, T3: 1.43 × 10⁹/L; P < 0.001), indicating a negative correlation (β=−0.636). Platelet counts demonstrated an increase from T1 (165.42 × 10⁹/L) to T2 (235.80 × 10⁹/L), followed by a plateau in T3 (236.36 × 10⁹/L), with a positive overall association with SII (β = 0.320; P < 0.001). P for trend was statistically significant for these three variables.

No significant differences were observed in FBS, TC, LDL-C, ALT, or AST levels across SII tertiles. Table 3 shows the comparison of dietary intake of energy, macronutrients, several antioxidant vitamins and MED-DQI components across SII tertiles. Total energy, macronutrient intake (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats), vitamin D, E and C intake did not significantly differ across tertiles. Also, no statistically significant difference was observed for dietary PUFA, MUFA and fiber intake across tertiles of SII. Regarding MED-DQI components, significant differences were observed in fruits and vegetables intake (P = 0.045), with higher SII tertiles associated with slightly worse fruits and vegetables quality scores. This difference remained significance even after adjustment for confounders. No significant differences were detected for other MED-DQI components, including saturated fatty acids, cholesterol, fish, olive oil, cereal scores. The overall MED-DQI score did not significantly differ across tertiles (P > 0.05). Partial correlation analysis revealed significant associations between the SII and several hematologic and biochemical parameters. A notable positive correlation was found between SII and neutrophil counts (r = 0.438, P < 0.001) and platelet counts (r = 0.309, P < 0.001). Conversely, SII showed a significant negative correlation with lymphocyte counts (r = -0.466, P < 0.001) and HDL cholesterol (r = -0.238, P < 0.001). Triglyceride levels also exhibited a positive correlation with SII (r = 0.214, P = 0.001). FBS, TC, LDL, ALT, and AST did not show significant correlations with SII. When examining the MED-DQI, a significant positive correlation was observed with lymphocyte counts (r = 0.215, P < 0.001) and HDL cholesterol (r = 0.158, P = 0.013). No significant correlations were found between MED-DQI and FBS, TC, LDL, TG, ALT, AST, neutrophils, or platelets (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between the SII, metabolic parameters, and the Mediterranean dietary quality among individuals with MAFLD (Fig. 2). Our results suggest that elevated SII is associated with an unfavorable lipid profile indicating a potential role of systemic inflammation in MAFLD pathophysiology. Our findings demonstrated a significant increase in TG levels and a decrease in HDL-C across SII tertiles. These alterations in lipid parameters are consistent with the pro-inflammatory state marked by elevated SII, which may impair lipid metabolism through multiple pathways. Higher SII reflects elevated neutrophil counts and reduced platelet levels, indicating a heightened systemic inflammatory state. Chronic inflammation promotes hepatic lipogenesis through cytokine-driven activation of SREBP-1c, which increases triglyceride synthesis49. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α also impair lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity, thereby reducing peripheral TG clearance50. In addition, inflammation stimulates hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue, increasing free fatty acid flux to the liver and further enhancing hepatic TG accumulation51. These mechanisms collectively explain the higher TG levels observed among participants in the upper SII tertile. The inverse association between SII and HDL-C may be explained by inflammation-induced alterations in HDL metabolism. Systemic inflammation increases serum amyloid A (SAA) production, which displaces apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I) from HDL particles, resulting in accelerated HDL clearance50,52. Oxidative stress from activated neutrophils further modifies HDL structure and impairs its reverse cholesterol transport capacity53. Moreover, TNF-α and IL-1β suppress hepatic ApoA-I synthesis, reducing HDL particle formation54.

Similar associations have been documented in previous research. In the study by Hotamisligil et al. inflammatory signaling interfered with lipid metabolism and insulin action, particularly through the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways55. Feingold and Grunfeld et al., also emphasized the role of inflammation in upregulating hepatic triglyceride production and altering lipoprotein profiles in the context of infection or chronic disease56.

Although there is no study that evaluates the association between SII and metabolic parameters in MAFLD patients, however, some of the studies found comparable results in other disease; in a population-based study among individuals aged 18–59 years old by Zhao J et al.34, those at the highest quartile of SII had significantly higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia (67.51%) versus those in the lowest quartile (58.58%; P < 0.001). Similarly, in the study by Liu X et al.33, data from 11,192 adults of NHANES III study showed that those at the highest quartile of SII had higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia (63.82%) compared with those at the lowest quartile (60.36%; P < 0.0001) and higher prevalence of fatty liver disorder (35.63% in the highest versus 33.47% in the lowest quartile ; P < 0.0001). In the study by Meng C et al.30, no significant difference was observed between TG and HDL across different SII groups; however, they used a single point threshold for SII (e.g. 983.5714) to divide participants into two similar groups; this categorization was different from the Quantile-based stratification as performed in our study and previous studies. SII incorporates the balance of innate (neutrophils and platelets) and adaptive (lymphocytes) immune components. Neutrophils release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), contributing to hepatic injury and inflammation in MAFLD11.

The observation that a specific subcomponent of the MED-DQI, namely the fruits and vegetables score, was significantly lower in the higher SII tertiles, despite no significant difference in the overall MED-DQI, suggests a nuanced interplay between diet quality and systemic inflammation. While the overall dietary pattern might not be drastically different, the lower intake of fruits and vegetables in individuals with higher systemic inflammation (indicated by higher SII) points towards potential deficiencies in micronutrients and bioactive compounds typically abundant in these food groups. These micronutrients, such as vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants (e.g., flavonoids, carotenoids), play crucial roles in modulating inflammatory pathways, supporting immune function, and combating oxidative stress57–61. However, it is critical to emphasize that this finding represents an association and does not imply causality. The observed relationship indicates a correlation between higher SII and poorer intake of fruits and vegetables, but it does not prove that reduced fruit and vegetable consumption directly causes higher SII, nor vice versa. Other lifestyle factors, underlying health conditions, or even reverse causation could contribute to this association. Therefore, while these findings highlight the importance of fruit and vegetable consumption in managing inflammation, further interventional studies are needed to establish a causal link. The absence of significant changes in other MED-DQI components may be attributed to overall low adherence to a Mediterranean-style diet in this population. Nevertheless, several studies such as Papadaki et al. and Bonaccio et al. have shown an inverse association between Mediterranean diet adherence and inflammatory markers, including SII62,63.

Strengths and limitations

Our study benefits from examining SII in relation to metabolic and dietary parameters. Additionally, analyses were adjusted for important confounding variables, which helps reduce potential bias. However, this study is subject to several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, its cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the associations observed between dietary factors and SII; reverse causation is a distinct possibility, where the inflammatory state associated with higher SII may influence dietary patterns, appetite, or nutrient absorption. Secondly, the absence of fibrosis staging in our MAFLD cohort represents another limitation as it might affects interpretation of our findings. Also, the sensitivity limitations of ultrasound imaging in detecting early steatosis should be noted. Thirdly, dietary intake was assessed using a FFQ, which is susceptible to measurement error and recall bias, potentially attenuating the true magnitude of the associations. Finally, the observed associations between most dietary variables and SII were of modest magnitude, suggesting that while statistically significant, these factors may explain only a small proportion of the variance in SII within this population, and other unmeasured factors likely play a more substantial role.

A significant strength of our study lies in the multi-dimensional framework employed to characterize the pathophysiological spectrum of MAFLD. By integrating abdominal ultrasound imaging-the gold standard for non-invasive detection of hepatic steatosis—with established biochemical markers of hepatocellular injury (AST, ALT) and metabolic profiles, we captured both the structural and metabolic facets of the disease. Furthermore, the incorporation of the SII represents a major methodological strength, as it provides a robust, composite measure of systemic inflammation by reflecting the dynamic interplay between innate (neutrophils), adaptive (lymphocytes), and hemostatic (platelets) immune pathways. This holistic approach, reinforced by stringent exclusion criteria—including the exclusion of alcohol-related liver disease and viral hepatitis—ensures that our findings are highly specific to the inflammatory-metabolic milieu of MAFLD, offering a more nuanced understanding of liver injury progression beyond what is provided by traditional single-marker assessments.To our knowledge, this is the first clinic-based investigation that simultaneously assesses the association between SII and detailed Mediterranean dietary patterns specifically within an MAFLD population. Unlike prior publications that often focus on either inflammatory markers or diet separately, our research integrates these elements and provides a more comprehensive view of their interplay in a real-world clinical setting. While the contribution is incremental, it is clinically relevant because it highlights potential dietary and inflammatory targets for managing MAFLD, emphasizing the importance of diet-inflammation interactions. In summary, our work extends previous findings by combining detailed dietary scoring with inflammatory markers in a dedicated clinical cohort, offering new insights that can inform future dietary and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant association between higher systemic immune-inflammation index and an unfavorable lipid profile in individuals with MAFLD. These findings underscore the importance of inflammation in the pathophysiology of MAFLD. Also, due to lower fruits and vegetables score of MED-DQI in the highest versus lowest SII groups, our study suggest that diet quality—particularly fruits and vegetable intake—may be associated with inflammatory status in MAFLD patients. Future prospective studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and assess the potential of SII as a clinical tool in dietary and lifestyle interventions for MAFLD.
