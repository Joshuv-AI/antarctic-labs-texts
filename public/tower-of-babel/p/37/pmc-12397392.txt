Evaluating high-protein wheat as a protein source for broiler chicken
Authors: Anna Szuba-Trznadel, Anna Rząsa, Adrianna Lazar, Izabela Janusz, Joanna Kamińska, Tomasz Hikawczuk, Anna Jama-Rodzeńska, Zygmunt Król, Paweł Przybylski, Bogusław Fuchs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Today, broiler feed formulators focus not only on minimizing costs but also on reducing environmental impact and maintaining broiler health. Lowering crude protein (CP) levels is a key strategy for addressing these concerns. The least cost formulation of diets based on the ideal protein concept – where amino acids are provided in appropriate proportions - is the most effective way to supply an economically viable and balanced amino acids feed for broilers. Such formulations also help reduce nitrogen excretion during the rearing period. Reducing CP levels in feed mixtures is possible through the use of crystalline amino acids (AAs). Given the high and fluctuating prices of soybean meal (SBM), the European Union (EU) is actively seeking alternative protein sources to reduce dependence on imported protein. The study aimed to explore the potential of wheat-based broiler diets as an alternative to SBM. It also evaluated the impact of increasing high-protein wheat proportions on the nutritional value and quality of feed mixtures. High-protein wheat, when appropriately supplemented with crystalline amino acids (AAs) by current nutritional requirements, appears to be a cost-effective solution for farmers. The substitution of SBM with wheat increases the feed grain and starch content, potentially reducing CP levels. This, in turn, could lower nitrogen and ammonia emissions, benefiting the environment. While this approach could help cut feed costs, further research is required to assess the long-term effects of such diets, including the influence of wheat processing technologies and the optimal dosage of feed enzymes to counteract the anti-nutritional effects of non-starch polysaccharides in wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17803-7.

Introduction

Soybean meal (SBM) is the main imported protein feed component in the European Union (EU) because of the limited area of cultivation1,2. The increase in global demand for SBM has led to land use changes around the world and has been directly linked to deforestation in South America, which has increased the carbon footprint of the feed3–8. Discussion about imported soybeans has raised questions as to what extent they can be linked to deforestation and loss of native vegetation in the Amazon and other areas of South America9–11 and to what extent they can be replaced by regional feed production12,13. Brazil, Argentina and the USA were responsible for 82% of soybean global production in 2018/1914. Cultivation of soybean in Asia and Europe (especially Ukraine, Russia, Italy and France) is significantly lower compared to North and South America but shows an increasing trend. The intensification of livestock farming causes the protein deficit in European countries, lower competitiveness of soybean compared with other crops, European policy promotion of cereal production and increasingly unfavorable climate conditions and pest infestations on protein crops12. This deficit concerns livestock production. At the European level, this “protein gap” has been caused by environmental conditions resulting from the import of large quantities of protein raw materials for feed production from different countries and their variable quality. Moreover, this is associated with higher feed transport costs. To become independent of SBM imports, we are obliged to look for alternatives that allow for their partial substitution. In addition to avoiding importing non-GMO (non-genetically modified organisms) feed raw material, which is a more expensive solution, research should be carried out on the possibility of replacing SBM in the nutrition of monogastric animals, especially pigs and poultry15,16also taking into account local materials for feed production17. While formulating feed mixtures for broilers, the main emphasis is placed on the crude protein (CP) level because this strongly affects performance results and feed cost18–22.

There is a tendency to look for innovative nutritional solutions given the existing interest and efforts to increase the competitiveness of broiler chicken feed. Currently, the most widely available protein components of plant-derived protein components used in poultry nutrition are SBM (mostly genetically modified), legumes and some cereals. Owing to the physiology of the digestive tract of birds, as well as their high demand for high protein content with its well-balanced and highly digestible amino acids (AAs), complete replacement of SBM in feed is very difficult23–27. However, the rich market availability of crystalline AAs supplements allows for this treatment28–32.

From a nutritional point of view, one of the valuable sources of protein is the use of wheat varieties with high protein levels as an alternative in Polish conditions to expensive imported SBM (price quotations from March 13, 2024). Among these, the Activus wheat (AW) – quality group A – seems very attractive as a feed component. Apart from its nutritional value, its adaptation to different soil moistures (drought or excessive moisture) is also highly attractive. Due to its well-developed root system, AW is particularly resistant to water shortages, and yield is not as low as with traditional cereals33. The Activus variety is characterized by high protein content (high proportion of true protein TP in CP) with a high sum of essential AAs, and high fat (EE) gross energy (GE) value, which affects final quality34.

Apart from the nutritional value of the components, the structure of the feed mixtures is also very important35–39. Particle size (PS) is a pivotal feed trait because it affects voluntary feed intake and gastrointestinal tract (GIT) development in birds40–42. Excessive amounts of coarse fractions can contribute to feed stratification and promote an increase in dust in barns, which can result in a higher morbidity rate from respiratory tract43,44. However, reducing PS is a common practice to improve the digestibility, feed efficiency and growth performance of broilers45–47. There are few reports focusing on optimal PS of feed mixtures. Recently, it has been recommended for broilers, as we do for laying hens, to use whole or crushed grain, which has a positive effect on the development of the GIT, higher health status and better production performance42,48,49.

This study aimed to assess the possibility of replacing SBM with AW and the preparation of feed mixtures for three-stage feeding of broilers. The main objective was also an analysis of the biochemical compositions and nutritional value of the feed mixtures using near-infrared spectrometry (NIRS) and chemical analysis to optimize percentages for the level of AW in broiler feed. In addition, the granulometry of the prepared mixtures was assessed for complete assessment of their digestibility in an in vivo study, the results of which will be presented in a separate manuscript.

Materials and methods

Feed mixtures

Experimental feed mixtures (Starter, Grower and Finisher) were prepared according to our recipes by the accepted assumptions of the experiment: for four groups differing in the proportions of SBM and AW. The first feed mixture (Control Group) contained approximately 60% AW and 30% SBM, and the remaining 10% contained other components. In subsequent experimental groups (Groups 2–4), SBM was reduced and replaced by wheat by 50% steps each time (Table 1). In the final feed mixtures prepared according to Broiler Nutrition Specifications (https://aviagen.com/eu/brands/ross/products/ross-308), the contents of AW and SBM differed from the assumed and presented in Table 1 in terms of their nutritional value, which had been assessed in an earlier field study carried out by Szuba-Trznadel et al. (2024)34,50. According to Poultry Nutrition Standards (PNS)51the maximum recommended share of wheat in mixtures for broilers is 40%, but after supplementation with an enzyme preparation, wheat can be used without restrictions52. The composition and nutritional values of experimental feed mixtures are presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4; moreover, Table 1S (supplementary materials) presents changes in CP and selected essential AAs levels.

The grain of the winter AW variety used for the preparation of feed mixtures came from a field experiment conducted in 2019, and the characteristics of this component were described by Szuba-Trznadel et al. (2024)34. The protein content (CP) in AW was 16–17%. The share of TP (true protein) in CP amounted to 95%.The essential AAs constituted approximately 33% of the total AAs. Compared to standard wheat, the AW had a higher content of essential AAs (tryptophan 12%, lysine 15%, threonine, valine, isoleucine, leucine, histidine, cystine approximately 20% and tyrosine even 30%). However, the dominant AAs were proline (Pro) and glutamic acid (Glu). The sum of Pro and Glu was 63 g kg−1, constituting 45% of the AAs. Per 100 g of CP, the total content of Pro and Glu was 38 g (the share of Pro in CP was 6% and Glu in CP 18%)34. The GE of this cultivar was 18.6 MJ kg−1 34. Based on these parameters, it could be concluded that AW is characterized by significant potential for its use in compound feed mixtures for monogastric animals. Other components and feed additives were obtained from the feed company Neorol Sp. z o.o. located in Chrzanów (Poland). Pre-mixtures of the complete experimental feed mixtures were prepared at this company53. Then they were mixed with other components in the laboratory of the Department of Animal Nutrition and Feed Science, Wrocław University of Environmental and Life Sciences. All experimental feed mixtures contained commercially available feed-grade amino acids (as per EU feed additive regulations: food.ec.europa.eu). Unfortunately, L-isoleucine was not available during the formulation period. When the experiment was carried out, the problem affecting all feed manufacturers on the domestic feed market.

Nutritional value of feed mixtures

NIRS

The basic chemical composition of the feed mixtures was evaluated using near-infrared spectrometry (FOSS NIRS–DS–2500–F, measuring absorbance at wavelengths from 800 to 2500 nm). Dry matter, crude protein, crude ash, crude fat, crude fiber and starch were assessed.

Chemical analysis (AOAC) of grain

The chemical composition of feed mixtures was evaluated according to the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) methodology54. Dry matter (Zalmed SML 32/250 dryer – AOAC, 930.15), crude ash (in a muffle furnace – AOAC, 942.05), nitrogen (Kjeltec 2300 Foss Tecator (Hillerod, Denmark) – AOAC, 984.13), crude protein conversion factor of 6.25 N, crude fat (ether extraction in Buchi Extraction System B-811 – AOAC, 920.39) and crude fiber (Foss Fibertec 1020, Sweden – AOAC, 978.10) were assessed.

Amino acids (AAs) were determined by ion exchange chromatography using an AAA 400 amino acid analyzer (INGOS, Prague, Czech Republic) according to AOAC standard protocol (AOAC: 994.12). Tryptophan was determined using a 2000 RS spectrophotometer (Aqualytic, Dortmund, Germany) at 590.0 nm (AOAC: 988.15).

The gross energy (GE) content was determined using a KL-11 “Mikado” calorimetric bomb-calorimeter (Precyzja-Bit Sp. z o.o., Bydgoszcz, Poland), according to the ISO 1928:2009 standard.

Other methods of chemical analysis in plant material

Starch content was determined using the polarimetric method described by Evers and modified by Grossfeld55,56. Total sugar content (TSC) and reducing sugar content (RSC) were determined by colorimetry using 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS)57.

Granulometric composition

The physical structures of the feed mixtures were assessed using dry sieving with an analytical sieve shaker: LPzE-2e (Multiserw-Morek, Marcyporęba, Poland). According to Polish Standard PN-89/R-64,798, a representative sample from each mixture, weighing 100 g, was sieved for 10 min using nine sieves with diameters from 2500 to 160 μm. The size of the sieves was chosen based on requirements presented in the Ross Broiler Management Handbook – Provision of Feed and Water (2018)58. After the sieving, residuals on each sieve were weighted, and particle size distribution (PSD) and geometric mean diameter (GMD) were determined according to the guidelines of the American Society of Agricultural Engineers (ANSI/ASAE S319.4 FEB2008 (R2022) – Method of determining and expressing fineness of feed materials by sieving). Additionally, to check the homogeneity of the prepared feed mixtures, GSD (log normal SD) was used; this is considered to be a homogeneity indicator of the diet.

Cost of produced mixtures

To compare the economic value of feed mixtures used in the study, there were comparable prices for 1 ton of every prepared mixture. The price was calculated by the feed producer, taking into account raw component value (price quotes from March 13, 2024). Usually, feed accounts for 60–65% of production costs. It is essential to check whether lower feed costs do not compensate for a reduction in animal productivity. Moreover, an increase in wheat share in broiler diets is related to higher usage of feed additives (enzymes, crystalline AAs), which increases the final cost of mixtures.

Statistics

Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using Statistica version 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc.) and R Statistical Software (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021)59. To assess the consistency of PSD for each feed mixture and experimental diet (Starter, Grower, Finisher), the Wilcoxon paired t-test was implemented with a significance level of α = 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to analyze and compare the PSDs of the three feed mixtures. PCA, based on the covariance matrix, estimates the eigenvalues with corresponding eigenvectors. This analysis makes it possible to distinguish the principal components (here two) and to identify the most important features differentiating the four types of feed mixture based on granulometric composition.

Results

Feed mixtures

Introducing higher levels of AW to the mixture recipes increased the energy value of the mixtures to exceed the nutritional requirements of broilers. To address this problem, wheat flour was introduced. Consequently, a uniform energy level of soybean oil was added, especially in the 1 st and 2nd mixtures, to obtain isoenergetic feed (Tables 2, 3 and 4).

However, the CP content of the experimental mixtures was below that recommended by the PNS51Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4S. AAs requirements were satisfied. Crystalline AAs were added to the mixtures according to current recommendations based on the results of earlier studies presented by Szuba-Trznadel et al. (2024)34,50.

Nutritional value of feed mixtures

The nutritional values of the feed mixtures (i.e., theoretically calculated and presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4) and actual values (determined by chemical analyses and presented in Table 5) are similar, which means that the raw components for the production of feed mixtures were of high quality.

Comparing the results from chemical analyses using the NIR method (typically employed for rapid evaluation of the product in field conditions at the feed factory) with those from laboratory AOEC, it is evident that the results exhibit the same trend. Generally, according to NIR, we obtained lower values for all parameters, except crude fat level.

Adding high-protein wheat, dietary CP concentrations were reduced in the experimental groups by an average of 27 g·kg−1 (197 vs. 236 g·kg−1) in the starter feed mixture, 23 g·kg−1 (199 vs. 225 g·kg−1) in the grower feed mixture and 19 g·kg−1 (177 vs. 207 g·kg−1) in the finisher feed mixture, as shown in Table 5. Therefore, in each feeding stage, the average CP reduction in the second experimental group was 14.82 g·kg−1 compared to the control group, while in groups 3 and 4 it was 21.65 and 33.27 g·kg−1, respectively. This approach reduced the average SBM inclusions by ca. 106, 158 and 210 g·kg−1 in groups 2, 3 and 4, respectively, accompanied by increases in crystalline AAs ca. 22, 27 and 31 g·kg−1, respectively, and average wheat inclusions increased by ca. 108, 161 and 214 g·kg−1 in groups 2, 3 and 4, respectively, compared to the control groups. An increase in wheat-based diet (while reducing CP level in the feed mixture) contributes to increased starch: protein ratios in birds. In the presented study, dietary analysis was performed for groups 1 (control), 2, 3 and 4 at each feeding stage, and it was found that the starch: protein ratios expanded on average from 1.73 to 2.01, 2.17 and 2.36, respectively.

Diets with reduced CP levels in diets (2–4) were formulated to get similar amounts of Lys, Met, Thr, Trp, Arg and Val by introducing crystalline AAs into diet formulas. The AAs analyses of the diets were in close agreement with calculated levels (Table 5). AAs levels that were not supplemented were lower than those of the control diet. With the increasing share of AW in the diets, the levels of non-essential AAs (Phe, Tyr, Ala, Gly, Ser, Glu) decreased, while the level of Pro increased. The share of essential AAs in the total AAs was on average 45%. However, in all experimental groups (diets from 2nd to 4th in each feeding period), the percentage was 1% point higher compared to the control group (1st diet).

Granulometric composition

PSD is illustrated in Figs. 1, 2 and 3 and Table 2S, while Table 7 presents the values of GMD and GSD for the respective diets used in the study.

The granulometric composition of all mixtures was similar across the experimental groups and mostly conformed to the recommendations for Ross-308 broilers (Broiler Nutrition Specifications: https://aviagen.com/eu/brands/ross/products/ross-308). The experimental feeds (Starter and Finisher) exhibited a similar distribution of particles across the groups. The fractions in the range 1.6–1.18 mm were dominant; however, in group 4, the proportion of the smallest fraction (≤ 0.16 mm) was two times higher than that in the other groups. There was no similar tendency in the Finisher mixtures for the smallest particles, and the share of particles in the range 1.6–1.18 mm was dominant in all groups (Fig. 3). Because there was no possibility of changing the granulation size, the GMD of all the feed mixtures used were similar. An increase in the AW content did not affect the GMD of the diets (Table 7).

In groups 1 and 2 of the Grower feed mixture, the fraction up to 0.4 is significant, exceeding the share of fractions 1.6–1.18.

To analyze the granulometric composition of the four mixtures tested in the three feed types—Starter, Grower and Finisher—a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The results of the PCA analysis indicated a distinct differentiation of the feeding mixtures owing to the granulometric composition. The biplots (Figs. 4, 5 and 6) show with arrows the correlation coefficients of the proportion of individual granulometric fractions with the determined principal components (Dim1, Dim2). The colored markers of smaller size indicate the coordinates of the individual measurement points in the newly determined two-dimensional space: Dim1, Dim2; the larger markers indicate the location of the average values from the measurements of a given group. For each feed, the first two principal components described a very high proportion of variability (Starter – 85.5%, Grower – 93.1%, Finisher – 89.6%.), so they characterize the specificity of the feed to a significant extent (Table 8).

Starter (fig. 4)

The first component (Dim1) is strongly positively correlated with the shares of the smallest particles in the feed (less than or equal to 160 μm, eigenvector components 0.436 and 0.380, respectively, Table 8) and negatively with the proportion of large and medium particles (components from − 0.434 to −0.193). Therefore, the component distinguishes the extreme PS of the feed, as indicated by the coefficients of the variables in the equation for this component. The second component (Dim2) mainly distinguishes medium PS (negative correlations with the shares of particles between 630 and 850 μm in diameter) from the largest particles (over 2.5 mm). Each mixture (marked with different shapes in Fig. 4) formed a separate cluster in the PCA diagram, which means that the mixtures under study were significantly different in granulometric composition, and the two components indicated were sufficient to confirm this differentiation. In detail, the first principal component fully differentiates between mixtures 3 and 4, indicating negative values (about − 2) for mixture 3 and positive values (about 3) for mixture 4. Both principal components differentiate mixtures 1 and 2 much less well by assigning slightly lower values to the former.

Fig. 4Principal component analysis for particle size distribution of Starter mixtures (PCA).

Principal component analysis for particle size distribution of Starter mixtures (PCA).

Grower (fig. 5)

The first component (Dim1) was strongly negatively correlated with medium-sized particles (from 315 to 850 μm) with a correlation coefficient of −0.368 to 0.0322. It strongly positively correlated with large particles (diameter greater than 1.18 mm) with a correlation coefficient from 0.304 to 0.331. The first component explains almost 75% of the variation in granulometric composition, which means that the variation in granulometric composition between the mixtures tested is mainly due to the different proportion of medium-sized particles compared with the other particle sizes. The second component mainly determines the proportions of the smallest particles (≤ 160 μm), with which it is strongly positively correlated. Thus, for Grower mixtures, the first component (Dim1) indicates the proportions of small- or medium-sized particles, and the second component (Dim2) is the smallest particle. The two components accounted for 93.1% of the granulometric variability of the mixtures. The most varied component values were recorded for mixture 4 (purple squares in Fig. 5); however, they are all in the same quadrant of the graph, and a clear separation is maintained. In particular, the first principal component clearly differentiates mixtures 1 and 2, and it is indistinguishable between 3 and 4. The second component differentiates mixture 4 by awarding it the most significant values (ca. 2).

Principal component analysis for particle size distribution of Grower mixtures (PCA).

Finisher (fig. 6)

The first component is strongly positively correlated with the proportions of the largest particles (diameter greater than 1.6 mm), with a correlation coefficient of 0.350 to 0.366. It is negatively correlated with the proportions of smaller particles (component values from − 0.263 to −0.376). The second component carries information mainly about medium-sized particles: a strong positive correlation with the proportion of particles up to 1.18 mm in diameter (correlation 0.631) and a strong negative correlation with the proportion of particles up to 315 and 400 μm in diameter (coefficient = −0.511 and 0.437, respectively). The four mixtures considered differ the most for the Finisher. At the same time, the composition is very similar for each group (clear clusters located in the outer corners of the respective quadrants of the coordinate system). To be precise, the first principal component differentiates between two pairs of mixtures, 1,2 and 3,4. The second component differentiates between two other pairs of mixtures, 1,4 and 2,3. Thus, information about the values of the first two principal components fully identifies the granulometric specificity of the mixture.

Discussion

Feed mixtures

The high content of AW in the experimental mixtures allows it to be used as a substitute for SBM; however, it is difficult to identify confirmation for this in the literature. A wheat level higher than 40% in mixtures for broiler chickens has been investigated with positive results by other authors60,61. Biesek et al. (2022)62 tested mixtures for Ross-308 with 10, 25 and 50% wheat and obtained satisfactory results. However, when they used 50% of wheat, slaughter performance, calculated using the European Production Efficiency Factor and the European Broiler Index, was lower for the group with the highest content of wheat.

Nutritional value of feed mixtures

The nutritional values of the feed mixtures (i.e., theoretical (calculated) and real (determined by chemical analyses) were similar, indicating that the raw materials for the production of experimental mixtures were appropriate51. Wheat has always been considered a valuable source of nutrients, particularly starch. It is usually stated that wheat is an important source of ME and can supply up to 70% of the ME requirements for broilers (but only 35% of the protein requirements for broilers)63. As wheat increased in the experimental feed mixtures, we noted an increase in energy and protein supply. Energy content was stabilized by oil additives. Walczyński (2006)64 also emphasized the difficulty in balancing the energy value of mixtures containing a high content of whole wheat grain.

Based on the available literature, it can be concluded that the results of evaluations of reduced-CP wheat-based diets vary (from promising65,66 to unfavorable67,68). In our study, dietary CP concentrations were reduced by an average of 32 g·kg-1 which resulted from a reduction of SBM of ca. 57%, accompanied by increases in crystalline AAs from 7.13 to 24.34 g·kg-1 and average wheat inclusions increased from 553 to 686 g·kg−1. This deficiency has been offset by the addition of crystalline AAs to maintain the future production performance of broilers69,70. The partial substitution of SBM with crystalline AAs and/or alternative protein feed materials is an enormous potential advantage in reduced-CP diets. Such actions allow independence from the import of expensive SBM69–71.

In most cases, reducing the CP level in broiler diets is achieved by decreasing the dietary inclusion of SBM while increasing the levels of cereal grains and crystalline AAs. Studies conducted by Woyengo et al. (2023)72 have shown that CP levels in broiler feed mixtures from the Starter to Finisher period can be reduced to 162 g·kg-1 without significantly affecting broiler body weight, provided that both essential and non-essential AAs are adequately supplemented. Specifically, CP levels in Starter and Finisher diets can be safely reduced to 190 g·kg−1 and 170 g·kg−1, respectively, without compromising broiler growth performance. This can be achieved by supplementing low-CP diets with crystalline Lys, Met, Thr, and Val to meet the dietary requirements for these first four limiting essential AAs, a method also applied in our study.

Furthermore, CP levels in broiler Starter and Finisher feed mixtures can potentially be reduced even further to 180 g·kg-1 and 160 g·kg−1, respectively, without negatively impacting growth performance if the diets are supplemented not only with essential AAs but also with Gly. Additionally, the CP level may be reduced by an extra 10 g·kg-1 and 5 g·kg-1 in Starter and Finisher diets, respectively, when enzyme supplementation is included. However, further research is necessary to confirm these findings72.

Musigwa et al. (2024)73 suggest that low-CP diets containing a balance of 60% essential AAs and 40% non-essential AAs enhance nutrient utilization and support growth performance comparable to that of broilers fed conventional diets. However, some studies have reported that broilers fed low CP diets tend to exhibit lower overall performance, which may be attributed to the absence of certain crystalline AAs74. The decline in growth performance observed with substantial reductions in CP levels has been partially linked to nitrogen deficiency or the lack of specific dispensable AAs, which may become conditionally essential in low-protein diets75.

Granulometric composition

Based on the available literature, it can be concluded that there is a limited amount of research on the optimum PS of different feed mixtures for efficient poultry production, and the presented results are often contradictory35,36,76–78. This follows our own results, where it was not possible to demonstrate the influence of various shares of wheat in the mixture on its homogeneity, expressed by its distribution on the sieves. Several authors indicate that raw materials with a very fine, fine and medium size fineness correspond to a PS in the range of 0.4 to 2 mm, and these are favorable for poultry79,80. In our study, we were unable to confirm such a relationship due to the inability to fractionate with in a grinder. Wolf et al. (2012)81 described the specific size of particles as coarse: above 1.4 mm, medium: 0.8–1.4, fine: 0.4–0.8, and very fine: below 0.4 mm. In our study, the fraction in the range of 1.6–1.18 mm dominated, which confirmed its good quality. Our results are in line with the study conducted by Matuszek et al. (2012)39 where the highest share of the fraction was recorded with a PS of 1.25 mm.

The optimal PSD should correspond to physiological needs, which enables the optimal use of nutrients and increases the productivity of animals77,78,82. Our raw material was the same; that is why we did not find statistically significant differences between experimental mixtures, and we had to perform additional PCA analyses to calculate and compare the GMD of the experimental mixtures. Rejecting the extreme values allowed us to demonstrate the dependence of the quality of the mixture on the percentage content of AW. Some suggestions regarding recommended GMD were found in a study conducted by Brum et al. (1998)83. A very fine GMD of corn (less than 400 μm in mash and crumbled feeds) may impair feed intake owing to the presence of dust, which may cause respiratory disorders, contaminate drinking water with feed and increase the water intake and moisture content of the litter. Amerah et al. (2007)77,78 concluded that feed PS, expressed as GMD of broiler diets based on maize or sorghum, should be between 600 and 900 μm. This is consistent with our research, although we achieved a broader range of 700–1 500 μm.

Conclusions

The study highlights the potential for formulating high-quality feed mixtures with a high wheat content, optimal nutritional value, and proper granulometric composition, potentially reducing feed costs. However, further research is needed, particularly in optimizing wheat processing technologies, determining the optimal dosage of feed enzymes, and selecting the appropriate fatty acid and fiber types.

The increasing challenges associated with SBM—including rising costs, ethical concerns, environmental impact, and land-use competition—have driven the search for alternative protein sources in broiler feed. The inclusion of high-protein wheat presents a promising solution. Our findings suggest that replacing SBM with wheat can lower feed costs, particularly in Starter and Grower mixtures, which are commonly used for extended periods in broiler production. This substitution has the potential to benefit farmers by reducing production costs without compromising the nutritional value of the feed.

However, further research is essential to fully explore the potential of local high-protein plants in poultry diets, ensuring that such replacements do not negatively affect growth performance or meat quality. Additionally, future studies should investigate the specific effects of wheat-based mixtures on animal health, feeding behaviour, and optimal feeding strategies. In vivo studies will be crucial for identifying the ideal level of wheat inclusion in broiler diets, forming the foundation for future advancements in poultry nutrition and sustainable feed practices.
