Feasibility study of an embodied and embedded mindfulness- and compassion-based intervention for non-suicidal self-injury disorder
Authors: Emma Schmelefske, Megan Per, Leena Anand, Bassam Khoury, Nancy Heath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is associated with suicide risk, as well as a number of psychological disorders. This, coupled with its high prevalence rate, make it imperative that effective treatments for those who engage in NSSI are investigated and made available to the public. Despite this, few interventions specifically targeting NSSI have been researched. This study aimed to address this gap in the existing research by investigating the feasibility and acceptability of an embodied and embedded mindfulness and compassion treatment (EEMCT) for individuals who engage in self-injury.MethodsSix participants attended eight weekly two-hour group therapy sessions. Feasibility and acceptability were assessed using qualitative and quantitative methods. Outcomes measured included urges to self-injure, as well as mental health symptoms commonly associated with NSSI (i.e., depression, anxiety, difficulty with emotion regulation, perceived stress). Outcomes were measured at pre-intervention, post-intervention, and six months follow-up.ResultsQuantitative results supported the acceptability of the intervention. Mixed results were found for the intervention’s feasibility. Participants gave feedback about the intervention in semi-structured interviews. They noted benefits of the intervention (e.g., learning self-kindness and awareness of thoughts and feelings), as well as several ways in which the intervention could be improved (e.g., more take-home practice material, shorter meditations). For several participants, clinically meaningful change was seen in anxiety from pre-intervention to post-intervention and from pre-intervention to follow-up. Some participants also showed meaningful decreases in depressive symptoms and emotion regulation from pre-intervention to follow-up.ConclusionsOverall, the results of this study suggest that EEMCT may be helpful and acceptable to individuals who engage in NSSI; however, more research is needed to confirm these findings. Suggestions for improving the feasibility of the intervention are discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-04962-3.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Mindfulness, Self-compassion, Feasibility study

Background

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), defined as the deliberate destruction of one’s own body tissue in the absence of suicidal intent [1], is a serious mental health concern with lifetime prevalence rates ranging from 17.9% among adolescents to 13.4% among young adults [2]. NSSI has been shown to be a risk factor for a number of mental health concerns [3–5] and is a particularly salient risk factor for both death by suicide and suicide attempts [6, 7]. Given the strength of its association with suicide, it is imperative that treatments that address NSSI be evaluated and made available to the public.

Although some research in recent years has targeted interventions for self-injurious thoughts and behaviours (SITB), a recent meta-analysis found that interventions researched to date have small effects on SITB [8]. Much of the existing NSSI treatment research has used samples of participants diagnosed with borderline personality disorder (BPD). Although engagement in NSSI is a symptom of BPD, many people with BPD do not engage in NSSI [4]. Furthermore, much of the population that engages in self-injury does not meet diagnostic criteria for BPD [9]. Indeed, NSSI is seen as a distinct phenomenon from BPD [9–11], to the point that non-suicidal self-injury disorder (NSSID) was approved as a condition for further study in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [12]. Many researchers have noted this problem and highlight the need for treatments that specifically target NSSI [13, 14]. A systematic review by Fox et al. found that among treatment studies where NSSI was measured as an outcome (regardless of the target of the intervention), no significant effect was found on NSSI occurrence, frequency, or intensity [8]. A systematic review by Calvo et al. identified studies of therapeutic interventions that were specifically developed for adolescents engaging in NSSI [15]. The study found preliminary support for six interventions in treating individuals who self-injure. Four of these interventions (i.e., the Cutting Down Program, Emotional Regulation Individual Therapy for Adolescents, Intensive Contextual Treatment, and Developmental Group Psychotherapy) incorporated elements of Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT). DBT, a third-wave cognitive behavioral therapy, was originally designed for individuals diagnosed with BPD. DBT emphasizes the cultivation of emotion regulation and interpersonal skills by practicing mindfulness, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness [16]. Studies suggest that the mindfulness component of DBT stands out as particularly effective in reducing instances of self-harm behaviour [17, 18]. Although the results of Calvo et al.’s review suggest that DBT-based interventions are promising approaches for adolescents who self-injure, the extent to which these findings generalize to adults remains unclear.

Embodied and embedded mindfulness and self-compassion

Dispositional mindfulness is defined by most Western researchers as the practice of deliberately attending to experiences in the present moment, with an attitude of non-judgement, openness, and acceptance [19]. Embodied mindfulness emphasizes the connection between mind and body by highlighting the role of the body as simultaneously the object and subject of present moment awareness [20]. While the majority of Western conceptualizations of mindfulness highlight the intrapersonal dimensions of mindfulness, embedded mindfulness extends the definition of mindfulness to include interpersonal or social dimensions. Embedded mindfulness requires that one directs their attention and non-judgement to others and to their external environment in addition to attending to the self [21]. Although little research has examined the relationship between NSSI and embodied and embedded mindfulness, NSSI has been found to be negatively associated with dispositional mindfulness, as those who self-injure tend to demonstrate less dispositional mindfulness than those who do not engage in NSSI [22–24]. In addition, dispositional mindfulness seems to buffer the effects of risk factors associated with NSSI, such as life stressors [23] and depressive symptoms [24].

Dispositional self-compassion is often viewed as a complementary clinical strategy to mindfulness. The three primary components of dispositional self-compassion are self-kindness (i.e., demonstrating kindness and understanding toward oneself, even in the face of adversity), common humanity (i.e., viewing one’s experience as part of a broader, human experience, rather than in isolation), and mindfulness (i.e., taking a non-judgmental stance toward thoughts and feelings rather than avoiding or becoming attached to them) [25]. Embodied and embedded compassion elaborate on traditional Western understandings of dispositional self-compassion by highlighting embodied cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and interpersonal skills/abilities involved in cultivating compassion towards self and others [26]. Little research has examined the relationship between embodied and embedded compassion and NSSI; however, dispositional self-compassion appears to mitigate the effects of several psychological risk factors on NSSI, including depressive symptoms, shame, and self-criticism [27, 28]. Furthermore, analyses of autobiographical online accounts of NSSI suggest that self-compassion may alleviate distress and promote recovery for those who engage in self-injury [29].

The experiential avoidance model of self-harm posits that NSSI is maintained through reinforcement in the form of avoidance of or escape from undesired emotions [30]. In this model, self-injury functions as a means of attaining temporary relief from feelings like anger, sadness, fear, etc., that can be difficult to tolerate. Indeed, many who engage in NSSI report that self-injury provides relief from unwanted internal experiences [5, 31, 32]. Mindfulness requires attending to and accepting present-moment experiences, including difficult or unwanted feelings, rather than trying to avoid or escape them. Although different constructs, NSSI and suicide overlap considerably and NSSI is a strong risk factor for suicide [33]. A meta-analysis by Schmelefske et al. found that mindfulness-based interventions effectively reduce suicidal ideation [34]. It is possible that cultivating mindfulness may reduce the need to avoid difficult thoughts and feelings through engagement in NSSI.

For many who self-injure, NSSI appears to function as a means of escaping aversive states of awareness (e.g., dissociation, numbness) [5, 35]. Embodied mindfulness’ emphasis on the bidirectional connection between mind and body in addition to awareness of thoughts and feelings might address feelings of numbness or dissociation among those who self-injure. A recent review highlighted that many who engage in NSSI do so to communicate distress to others, and that self-injury can also function as a means of having interpersonal needs met, especially when those needs are not being met in the social or relational environment [36]. Based on their findings, the authors note that relationally-oriented psychotherapeutic approaches may be beneficial to those who engage in NSSI. Fostering embedded (i.e., interpersonal and environmentally based) mindfulness and compassion might address the interpersonal functions of self-injury by improving awareness, non-judgement, acceptance, and compassion towards others’ thoughts, feelings, and behaviours, thereby improving social relationships.

Individuals who engage in NSSI tend to report higher levels of self-directed negative emotions (e.g., anger, shame) and lower self-esteem than those who do not [37]. Many who engage in NSSI report doing so in an effort to punish themselves, possibly in an effort to reduce shame or self-criticism [38, 39]. This suggests a causal relationship could exist between negative, self-directed emotion and self-injury. Self-punishment stands in contrast to self-compassion, where one adopts a kind and understanding attitude toward oneself even when faced with difficult or painful thoughts and feelings [25], rather than being self-critical. It is worth wondering whether cultivating self-compassion may reduce the need for self-punishment among those who engage in NSSI.

Many individuals who engage in NSSI report that they experience minimal or no pain while self-injuring [40]. Moreover, those who engage in self-injury experience heightened pain tolerance compared to those who do not engage in such behaviours [41]. Increased pain tolerance has been linked to a greater risk of death by suicide [42]. Introducing practices that promote awareness of the physical body, such as embodied mindfulness or compassion, could protect against NSSI and death by suicide by increasing awareness of pain and compassion for the physical body.

As most individuals who engage in NSSI report more than one reason for doing so [5, 31], it is important that any intervention aimed at helping those who self-injure addresses many of the functions that NSSI serves. An embodied and embedded framework for mindfulness and self-compassion appears to address several of these functions.

Current study

Despite both theory and research pointing to embodied and embedded mindfulness and compassion as potential tools for addressing NSSI, little research has examined the acceptability or feasibility of such interventions with this population. Although Rees et al. published a protocol for a randomized controlled trial of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for individuals who self-injure, to our knowledge, the results of this study have not been published [43]. Research suggests that brief mindfulness inductions can buffer the effects of stress-induction tasks on those who engage in self-injury [44, 45]. Although this points to mindfulness-based interventions as being potentially helpful for those who engage in NSSI, virtually no existing peer-reviewed research has studied larger-scale mindfulness-based interventions with this population. Although DBT teaches mindfulness skills, it does not qualify as a purely mindfulness-based intervention due to the absence of meditation practice in all sessions [46–48]. Although one study has found that Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT) reduces self-harm behaviour among adolescents [49], self-compassion has otherwise been neglected as an intervention strategy for NSSI. CFT aims to address self-directed hostility and shame by helping clients cultivate kindness, warmth, and non-judgement toward the self; [50] however, unlike embodied and embedded self-compassion, it does not emphasize compassion for others or one’s physical body.

Although NSSI (i.e., self-injury without suicidal intent) is an increasingly common operational definition in treatment studies, many studies use alternative constructs such as self-harm, which encompasses both self-injury and suicide attempts [51]. While substantial overlap exists between those who self-injure and those who engage in self-harm, findings from self-harm treatment studies may not necessarily be generalizable to NSSI populations.

The present study aimed to address these gaps in the literature by piloting an embodied and embedded mindfulness- and compassion-based treatment (EEMCT) for individuals who engage in self-injury. The intervention was piloted with a group of individuals who met criteria for NSSID with the goal of (1) assessing the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention; (2) measuring whether participants’ urges to self-injure, anxiety, depression, emotion regulation, and perceived stress changed meaningfully over the course of the intervention; (3) understanding participants’ experiences in the intervention and identifying ways in which the intervention can be improved.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from an existing pool of university students who had taken part in the screening phase of a study on stress and coping in undergraduate students, as well as from the community through posters and advertisements on social media (e.g., Facebook). To be eligible to participate, participants needed to: be at least 18 years of age, meet the proposed criteria for NSSID in the DSM-5, never have been diagnosed with a psychotic disorder, and not be experiencing active suicidal ideation, which for the purposes of this study was defined as self-reported suicidal ideation within the past month. Participants were six adults (Mage = 21.00). Five of the participants were undergraduate university students while one was a collège d’enseignement general et professionnel (CEGEP) student. In Quebec, CEGEPs are schools that offer the first year of post-secondary education. All participants resided in the greater Montreal area. All participants were white. Five participants were cis-gendered women while one identified as both a woman and non-binary. Two participants identified as heterosexual, two as bisexual, one as questioning, and one as gay/lesbian/queer. All participants reported engaging in more than one form of self-injury but indicated that cutting was their primary self-injury method.

Measures

Interested participants were invited to participate in pre-screening interviews to assess their eligibility for the study. Pre-screening interviews were conducted by two doctoral counselling psychology students. Eligible participants were asked to complete a series of questionnaires prior to the intervention, after the final intervention session, and six months after the intervention had ended.

Urges to self-injure were measured using the Alexian Brothers Urge to Self-Injure Scale (ABUSI) [52] which assesses the frequency and intensity of urges to self-injure. The ABUSI contains five self-report questions, each of which is rated on a scale from 1 to 7, with total scores ranging from 5 to 35. Higher scores indicate stronger urges to self-injure. The ABUSI has demonstrated good test-retest reliability (r =.84) and internal consistency (α = 0.92).37 In the present study, the ABUSI demonstrated strong internal consistency at pre-intervention (α = 0.86), post-intervention (α = 0.95), and follow-up (α = 0.98).

Dispositional mindfulness was assessed using the Five-Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) [53]. The FFMQ is a 39-item self-report measure that assesses five facets of dispositional mindfulness. The FFMQ has demonstrated good construct validity in its total score as well as across all facets (α = 0.67 − 0.92) [54–57]. In the present study, the FFMQ demonstrated strong internal consistency at pre-intervention (α = 0.88), post intervention (α = 0.89), and follow-up (α = 0.81).

Dispositional self-compassion was measured using the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) [58]. The subscales of the SCS are linked to Neff’s [25] conceptualization of self-compassion and assess mindfulness, self-kindness, and common humanity. The SCS has good internal consistency across each of its subscales (α = 0.75 − 0.81).58 In the present study, the SCS demonstrated strong internal consistency pre-intervention (α = 0.89), post-intervention (α = 0.91), and follow-up (α = 0.95).

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) [59] was used to assess emotion regulation. A meta-analysis by Wolff et al. found that high levels of emotion dysregulation are associated with high levels of risk for NSSI [60]. Emotion dysregulation also fully mediates the relationship between mindfulness and NSSI engagement [61]. The DERS is a 17-item self-report questionnaire, containing six subscales measuring different aspects of emotion regulation: difficulty accepting emotions (non-acceptance), difficulty engaging in goal-directed behaviour (goals), impulsiveness (impulse), difficulty using emotion regulation strategies (strategies), and challenges with emotional clarity (clarity). Some research has raised questions about the factor structure of the DERS, noting limited reliability and validity of the awareness subscale [62, 63]. In contrast, some studies have found support for the reliability and validity of the DERS total score [64, 65]. Because of this, we opted to calculate the DERS total score following the scale authors’ original guidelines. The DERS total score has been shown to have high construct validity, concurrent validity, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency (α = 0.82 − 0.83) [66]. In the present study, the DERS demonstrated strong internal consistency at pre-intervention (α = 0.94), post-intervention (α = 0.94), and follow-up (α = 0.90).

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [67] was used to assess depressive symptoms. Depression is common among individuals who engage in NSSI [3], and mindfulness partially mediates the effect of depressive symptoms on NSSI [24]. The PHQ-9 is a 9-item self-report questionnaire. Higher scores indicate the presence of more depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 has been shown to have high internal consistency (α = 0.86 − 0.89) [68] and high convergent validity with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (r =.68) [69]. In the present study, the PHQ-9 demonstrated adequate internal consistency at pre-intervention (α = 0.73), and strong internal consistency at post-intervention (α = 0.92) and follow-up (α = 0.96).

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [70] questionnaire was used to assess anxiety symptoms, which are prevalent among individuals who engage in self-injury [71]. Mindfulness also appears to moderate the relationship between anxiety symptoms and NSSI recurrence, as well as the relationship between anxiety symptoms and NSSI versatility [71]. The GAD-7 has been shown to have good internal consistency (α = 0.89) [68]. In the present study, the GAD-7 demonstrated strong internal consistency at pre-intervention (α = 0.86), post-intervention (α = 0.91), and follow-up (α = 0.89).

Participants’ perceptions of their level of stress were assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [72]. A review by Lee [73] found that the in eleven of twelve studies of the PSS’ psychometric properties, the scale demonstrated adequate internal consistency (i.e., α > 0.70). The author also noted strong test-retest reliability (i.e., r >.70) in two of the three studies where test-retest reliability statistics were obtained. In the present study, internal consistency was good pre-intervention (α = 0.86), adequate post-intervention (α = 0.77), and strong at follow-up (α = 0.87). Perceived stress was chosen as an outcome based on research that has identified it as a risk factor for NSSI [74] that often co-occurs with low levels of self-compassion [75].

Post-intervention interviews

After completing the intervention, participants were invited to take part in a semi-structured interview. They were asked to provide feedback on their experience with the intervention, which could be used to improve the intervention for future groups. Questions aimed to elicit feedback on participants’ overall experiences in the group, opinions about the group content and structure, and what they learned from the group. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviews were conducted by the third author, who was not present during the intervention, in hopes that this would facilitate participants giving honest feedback about their experiences in the group.

Intervention

The theoretical foundation of EEMCT is rooted in a framework that harmonizes Eastern and Western perspectives on mindfulness and compassion. This framework comprehensively integrates the intra-personal (i.e., embodied) and inter-personal (i.e., embedded) dimensions of mindfulness and compassion. Central to this conceptualization is the recognition of the bidirectional relationships between body, mind, and environment as fundamental pathways to fostering psychological well-being, physical health, and self-regulation [76]. This differs from other mindfulness- and self-compassion-based approaches which tend to emphasize the mind over the body or the environment. EEMCT took place once weekly over the course of eight weeks. Each session was approximately two hours long. This was done through group discussion of personal experiences with mindfulness, compassion, and self-injury, as well as through formal guided mindfulness and compassion exercises (e.g., meditation). Each week covered a different topic related to mindfulness and/or self-compassion (see Table 1 for an overview of topics covered). The intervention was developed by reviewing and compiling existing theory and evidence, professional experience from clinical work with individuals who engage in self-injury, as well as elements from other existing mindfulness-based interventions. Participants were asked to practice mindfulness or self-compassion for at least 30 min per day outside of the scheduled intervention sessions. Participants were provided with practice materials (audio recordings of guided meditations links to online meditation videos) each week that were relevant to the topics covered in session but were welcome to practice using materials from previous weeks or engage in unguided practice. Sessions were led by a PhD-level clinical psychologist with over 10 years of experience with mindfulness-based interventions and clients who engage in NSSI, with support from two graduate student therapists experienced in mindfulness-based intervention. The intervention was group-based and was conducted in-person in a private psychotherapy clinic.

Data analysis

Acceptability

Participants’ overall satisfaction with the intervention was assessed following their participation in the eight-week intervention. They were asked to rate how helpful they found the intervention on a scale from zero to ten. An average rating ≥ seven indicated acceptability, based on previous group-therapy intervention research indicating that client ratings of seven out of ten suggest acceptability [77]. Acceptability was also assessed qualitatively during interviews following the intervention, where participants were asked to provide feedback on the intervention. Interview transcripts were analyzed using inductive content analysis [78]. Transcription and analyses were conducted by the first author, a cis-gendered white woman and PhD candidate in counselling psychology with a research background in mindfulness, self-compassion, and self-injury. In the first stage of analyses, the first author read and re-read the interview transcripts to familiarize herself with the data. In the second stage, data were organized into broad categories that pertained to the research questions at hand. In the third stage, these broad categories were broken down into subcategories. In the fourth stage, subcategories were revised and refined into codes. In the fifth stage, codes were synthesized and interpreted.

Feasibility

Feasibility was assessed by examining adherence, recruitment, and retention rates of participants. Treatment adherence was assessed by examining participants’ attendance rates. An average attendance rate of 50% was considered to indicate feasibility, based on standards used to evaluate mindfulness-based interventions in previous research [79]. Participants were also asked to self-report how many minutes of mindfulness practice they had engaged in over the previous week at the beginning of each session. Feasibility was indicated if, on average, participants completed the requested 30 min of weekly practice. Recruitment rates were assessed by comparing the number of participants expressing interest in the study to the number of participants who completed pre-screening interviews, as well as the number of individuals screened who met criteria for the study and/or participated in the intervention. Guidelines for pilot trials recommend a minimum of n = 30 participants as an indication of feasibility [80]. Retention was assessed by examining the participant attrition rate. An attrition rate equal to or lower than 29%, the average attrition rate for mindfulness-based intervention studies [47], indicated feasibility.

Clinically meaningful change

The sample size of this study was too small to obtain adequate power to draw definite conclusions from statistical significance. Single-case analyses were considered, but ultimately not used because data collection time points did not allow for the establishment of a “baseline” for the outcome variables of interest for each participant. Clinically meaningful change (CMC) was assessed using both distribution-based and anchor-based methods.

Distribution-based methods quantify CMC using characteristics of the obtained sample (e.g., mean, standard deviation). In the present study, mean pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up scores were calculated for each outcome variable. A standardized individual difference (SID) score was then calculated for each outcome for each participant. SID is a statistic that is commonly used to assess individual intervention responsiveness. SIDs were calculated by dividing the difference between pre- and post-intervention (or pre-intervention and follow-up) scores for a given outcome by the standard deviation of the differences between these scores for each participant. Based on recommendations from Estrada et al., individual change was considered clinically meaningful when SID scores were ≥ 1.645 or ≤ -1.645 (one-tailed) based on whether increasing or decreasing scores indicated improvement for the corresponding measure [81]. Ferrer and Pardo found that SID demonstrates acceptable reliability, internal consistency, and rates of false positives when used to evaluate clinical change and that SID performs better than other distribution-based methods (e.g., individual effect size, reliable change index; Pardo & Ferrer, 2014) [82].

Anchor-based methods quantify CMC by comparing participant scores to an external criterion (e.g., existing clinical cutoff scores) and assess the clinical importance of change. In the present study, means at pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up were compared to established clinical cutoffs for the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSS (see Figs. 1, 2 and 3 for clinical cutoff scores used).

Quantitative results

Feasibility and acceptability

One treatment group was run with a total of six participants. Twenty-four individuals responded to advertisements seeking more information about the study. Ten of these participants were not interested in participating or did not respond to follow-up emails from the research team. Fourteen individuals completed pre-screening interviews. A total of seven participants were eligible for the study, one of whom dropped out prior to the first session due to a scheduling conflict. This was much lower than the standard of 30 participants, which did not support the feasibility of the recruitment for the intervention. All remaining participants completed the intervention. They were asked to rate how helpful they found the intervention on a scale from zero to ten. Participants’ mean rating was M = 7.94 (SD = 0.52, range = 7.25–8.5), indicating acceptability of the intervention. Participants attended an average of M = 6.33 (SD = 1.63, range = 4–8) out of eight sessions and completed an average of M = 36.19 (SD = 19.09, range = 17–70) minutes of mindfulness or self-compassion practice per week outside of sessions, indicating feasibility of the intervention. Anchor-based CMC

Average GAD-7 scores fell from severe to the moderate range from pre-intervention (M = 14.83) to post-intervention (M = 9.33), and from post-intervention to follow-up (M = 8.50; see Fig. 1). Average scores on the PHQ-9 fell from the moderate range pre-intervention (M = 11.50) and post-intervention (M = 10.50) to the mild range at follow-up (M = 7.25; see Fig. 2). Mean scores on the PSS increased from the moderate range pre-intervention (M = 25.83) and post-intervention (M = 24.75) to the high range at follow-up (M = 27.00; see Fig. 3).

Qualitative results

Participants’ feedback from post-intervention interviews was collated into three primary categories: benefits, barriers to practicing, and intervention characteristics. Within these categories, several important codes emerged, which are described below. See Fig. 6 for a complete list of codes and categories.

Inductive Content Analysis Results

Benefits

Acceptance

Many participants highlighted that learning to accept thoughts and feelings was one of the most helpful aspects of learning about mindfulness during the intervention. As one participant described: “[…] even if I can’t do anything about it, […] I know it’s good to just say, ‘I know I upset, I’m sad, I’m angry,’ whatever. And at least know that it’s there, then go from there, you know?”

Self-kindness

Several participants also noted that practicing being kinder to themselves was an important takeaway from the sessions. This being said, several participants also noted that although they understood the value of being kind to oneself, this was an ongoing challenge at the time that they were interviewed. One participant explained her experience following a discussion related to self-compassion:

[…] something that I’ve kind of, like, internalized is, like, “I don’t deserve happiness,” or, like, “I don’t deserve loving,” […] but who says what you deserve, and, like, who decides that? And that was, like, really important for me to hear, because […] there’s no one saying like, “You’re not deserving of that.”

Some participants highlighted that although they found self-kindness beneficial, it was still an area for growth. As one participant explained: “If you’re asking me if I can practice and like believe in [self-kindness]? Right now, no. But like it is something that’s going to have to take practice, and it’s going to be over time.”

Non-judgement

In addition to accepting thoughts and feelings, many participants also discussed viewing thoughts and feelings with a less critical attitude than they did prior to the intervention. For example, one participant described telling herself, “This is a normal thing to be stressed about,” and “This doesn’t make you a bad person,” when she noticed herself feeling stressed following a meeting with a supervisor.

Distance from emotions

Four participants noted that creating distance between themselves and their emotions made their emotions easier to tolerate. One participant explained how her relationship with her emotions changed over the course of the intervention: “I’m not just feeling the anger and just thinking about the anger. Like, I no longer am the anger.”

Awareness

Several participants noted being more aware of bodily sensations, thoughts, feelings, or their physical environments as a positive takeaway from the group. Some described attending to bodily sensations as a strategy they now use when they experience difficult emotions or urges to self-injure:

[…] whenever I have, I have urges or something, I always think of the group and like what we learned. And like I try to remember more, sometimes it comes up randomly, other times I’m just left with, “What is your physical body telling you;” but I also just generally, like, outside of my urges I’ve tried to, like, listen to what my body is telling me a lot more […].

Intervention

Facilitator characteristics

Participants highlighted several characteristics of the group facilitators that they felt benefitted the intervention. Two of the facilitators were in their twenties. The mean age of the group was 21.00. Some participants found that having facilitators who were close in age to them made them feel more understood and made the facilitators more relatable. Participants also mentioned that facilitators were approachable and friendly, which made them feel more comfortable in the group. Some participants also highlighted that they appreciated facilitators’ responsiveness to group needs. As one participant explained,

[…] when we did like have a struggle or something, [the facilitator] took a really long time to like actually go in depth and like, talk about that, rather than like talking about it a bit and then like going back to the topic. So, I found that really helpful.

Structure

Feedback was mixed regarding the format of the sessions. Some participants appreciated that the intervention was flexible and had a blend of didactic training, group meditation practice, and group discussion. Others found the transition between these components challenging: “It, like, kind of felt abrupt talking and then it would be like, ‘Okay, let’s do a practice now,’ and we’d, like, do like 10 minutes of meditation then like go back to talking.” All participants mentioned that they appreciated that the intervention was group-based. Specifically, participants highlighted learning from others’ experiences and feeling comfortable to share, even if they were nervous about opening up to the group at the beginning of the intervention. As one participant described:

[Group therapy] forces you to see things from other people’s perspectives and I liked that a lot. And, so, in that sense I think I was a little surprised ‘cause I was not expecting it to be so, like, close. You know like we all felt very like, close with each other but at the same time we were very, like, everyone brought int their own experiences and had different things to say and it always made me, like, re-think where I was coming from, which I, like, enjoyed.

Logistics

Most participants felt that the sessions were an appropriate length; however, some mentioned the 2-hour sessions felt too long. Both the timing and location of the intervention also presented an obstacle to some participants. Most group members felt that the number of sessions was appropriate. Some participants, however, felt that having more than eight sessions would have given them more time to practice skills with support from the group before practicing on their own. All participants appreciated that snacks were provided during the sessions. Participants were given “stamp cards” and received stickers after each session they attended. Several participants mentioned that they enjoyed these cards.

Barriers to practice

Length of meditations

Nearly all participants mentioned that the length of some of the meditations provided either in-session or as homework presented a barrier to practicing mindfulness. Some participants noted feeling sleepy or physically uncomfortable while sitting during longer meditations. Participants preferred meditations that lasted fifteen minutes or less.

Difficulty remembering content

Some participants expressed that it was difficult to remember content from the intervention. Several suggested that offering more take-home material (e.g., worksheets, handouts) would be helpful for them to remember what was practiced or discussed during sessions and would have made it easier for them to practice what they had learned once the intervention ended.

Difficulty establishing routine

Several participants expressed that although they found the skills that they learned during the intervention helpful, it had been difficult for them to establish a habit or routine that allowed them to practice these skills once the intervention ended. A major reason for this was time; several participants explained that, especially during busy times of year (e.g., exams), it was difficult for them to find time to practice. As one participant explained: “I think [how often I meditate] also changes in, like, in parallel to, like, how much schoolwork I have. Like, if I didn’t have a lot of work to do, I could be like ‘oh, I can focus on like taking care of myself.’”.

Relevance of content

Although all participants said that they found the groups helpful, some noted that aspects of the intervention did not feel relevant to them. Some found it difficult to understand how they could apply concepts discussed during sessions in their everyday lives. As one participant explained:

[…] I know I’m not the only person who was like struggling with the [guided] meditation part, um, and that was like good, because or else I probably would have been mad at myself, but, yeah. I think somehow bridging like the reflection, like kind of maybe more reflection on meditation and like putting, kind of applying that to like everyday stuff [would improve the intervention].

Practicing during crises

Many participants found it more challenging to apply what they had learned during the intervention during times when they were in crisis or feeling very overwhelmed. Some predicted that practicing mindfulness or self-compassion regularly might make the skills they had learned easier to use during particularly difficult times. As one participant explained,

when you do get into like feeling really overwhelmed by negative emotions it’s something that, like to push yourself to actually be nice to yourself– that’s like the hardest part of it, but you don’t get to that part unless you practice it on a daily basis, which is something I have to work on.

Discussion

This is the first study to examine the feasibility of mindfulness and compassion for treating NSSID through an embodied and embedded (i.e., interpersonal) framework. The purpose of this mixed-methods study was to investigate the feasibility and acceptability of a mindfulness- and self-compassion-based intervention for individuals who engage in self-injury. Results provided some support for this aim and suggested that further investigation into the effectiveness of mindfulness-and self-compassion-based interventions for NSSI is warranted; however, results suggest several areas in which study design, recruitment, and the intervention itself could be improved upon in future research.

Results provided some support for the acceptability of the intervention. Participants rated the intervention as helpful and highlighted several benefits of the intervention during interviews; however, they also noted several ways in which the intervention could be improved. Some support was also found for the intervention’s feasibility. One participant dropped out before beginning the intervention, citing a scheduling conflict. No participants dropped out of the study once the intervention began, and all participants completed at least half of the sessions; however, only two participants completed all eight sessions. Although dropout rates were low, recruitment presented a serious challenge. Nearly half of those who requested information about the study did not schedule screening interviews. Of those who completed screening interviews, only 50% met inclusion criteria for the study. This has potentially negative implications for the intervention’s feasibility. Partnering with a clinic that specializes in providing service to those who self-injure could facilitate participant recruitment in future research. Participants could only participate in the study if they met the proposed criteria for NSSID. This meant that many who were interested in taking part in the project but had engaged in self-injury on fewer than five days in the past year were excluded from the study. Given that recent research has raised questions regarding the utility of NSSID’s diagnostic criteria [83, 85], using less restrictive inclusion criteria could also facilitate participant recruitment. Individuals could only participant if they had not experienced suicidal ideation within the past month. Though none of the participants who completed screening interviews reported past-month ideation, this inclusion criteria may have deterred potential participants from contacting the study team. Future research using less conservative inclusion criteria related to suicidal ideation could also facilitate recruitment in future research.

In feedback interviews, participants noted increased awareness of thoughts, feelings, and physical sensations, self-kindness, and acceptance and non-judgement of thoughts and feelings and attributed these changes to the intervention. Individuals who engage in self-injury demonstrate lower levels of the awareness and non-judgement facets of trait mindfulness than those who do not [22]. The present study did not assess mechanisms of change; however, it is encouraging that participants described these components of mindfulness as helpful take-aways.

The results of the present study suggest that mindfulness and self-compassion-based interventions may have benefits for individuals who engage in NSSI, although further research is needed to confirm these findings. Clinically meaningful reductions in anxiety were noted from pre-intervention to post-intervention for half of participants, and from post-intervention to six months follow-up for all participants. While these findings should be interpreted with caution given the study’s small sample size, this is well aligned with previous research that has shown that both mindfulness- and self-compassion-based interventions show large effects on anxiety-related outcomes [47, 84]. It is important to note, however, that because the present study did not have a control group it is possible changes in anxiety may have been caused by confounding variables (e.g., time of year). Clinically meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms were also noted from pre-intervention to follow-up for all participants. These findings are similar to those of previous research that has shown that mindfulness- and self-compassion-based interventions have moderate effects on depressive symptomology [47, 84]. These results are important given that engagement in NSSI can co-occur with anxiety and depressive disorders [86]. A clinically meaningful reduction was also noted in difficulties with emotion regulation from pre-intervention to follow-up for half of participants. This corresponds with previous research suggesting both dispositional mindfulness and self-compassion are associated with fewer difficulties with emotion regulation [87, 88], as well as research showing improvements in emotion regulation following mindfulness-based interventions [89–91].

Only one participant showed clinically meaningful improvement in urges to self-injure, from pre- to post-intervention, and this improvement was not found at follow-up. In interviews, several participants noted that they found it challenging to use skills they had learned in the intervention when they were in crisis or felt overwhelmed. Given that NSSI is often used as a coping strategy to regulate difficult emotions, it may be that the urge to use this strategy persisted in times where participants found it particularly challenging to regulate their emotions.

Participants’ average scores for perceived stress increased from the moderate to high range from pre- to post-intervention. This stands in contrast to previous research showing reductions in perceived stress following participation in mindfulness-based interventions [83, 92]. Much of the previous research examining the effects of mindfulness-based interventions on perceived stress has been conducted using non-clinical samples. It may be that perceived stress is less responsive to mindfulness-based interventions among those who engage in NSSI; however more research, specifically research with larger samples and control groups, would be needed to confirm this finding.

Limitations and directions for future research

The results of the present study should be considered in the context of several important limitations. The biggest limitation is the study’s sample size. Only six participants were included final sample, which precluded the use of significance testing to draw conclusions about the effectiveness of the intervention. Future research using significance testing is necessary to know whether changes observed in the present study are statistically significant. Two participants were lost at follow-up. It is possible that this led to attrition bias in the analyses that compared participants’ pre-intervention and follow-up scores, as participants who were less responsive to the intervention may have been less likely to respond at follow-up.

In addition to being small, the sample used in this study was relatively homogenous in terms of age and gender. The majority of participants (5) were cis women, all were white, and the age range was nineteen to twenty-two. This limits the generalizability of the study’s findings.

The present study did not have a control group, which limits the conclusions that can be drawn from the results. While clinically meaningful changes were noted in some outcomes, we cannot say definitively that these changes were caused by the intervention. The COVID-19 pandemic began following participants’ post-intervention interviews in January 2020, but before follow-up data was collected in April 2020. It is possible that the changes noted in outcomes from pre-intervention to follow-up were caused by the pandemic, rather than by the intervention itself. Given that all participants were students, it is also possible that seasonal factors related to the time of academic year may have impacted the variations in outcome measures observed. Indeed, attendance seemed to be low during week four of the intervention, which coincided with midterm exams. Artefacts such as regression to the mean could also account for the changes observed. Future research using a control group is needed to confirm the findings of the present study.

Although urges to self-injure were assessed at each time point, other NSSI-related variables, such as frequency of NSSI behaviour, NSSI severity, or NSSI versatility, were not measured. This presents a limitation, as it is important to know how the intervention might address NSSI on a behavioural level. Future research could examine how EEMCT might affect NSSI behaviour or severity.

Participants noted several benefits to practicing mindfulness and self-compassion, as well as several ways in which the intervention used in the present study could be improved. Though some logistical factors (e.g., location, scheduling), may be unique to the present study, future research investigating such interventions could include more at-home practice material or handouts that summarize session content, focus on group discussion in addition to meditative practice, and emphasize shorter (under 15-minute) meditation practices for those who may be new to meditation. During the intervention, participants completed more than 30 min (M = 36.19) of homework on average per week. However, given that several participants noted it was difficult for them to establish a routine for practicing what they had learned in the intervention once the intervention had ended, it might be helpful for future interventions to emphasize relapse prevention and planning for how participants can practice mindfulness and self-compassion at home during the final sessions.

Conclusions

The results of the present study suggest that embodied and embedded mindfulness and compassion interventions are acceptable and may have benefits for those who engage in self-injury. Given the small sample size used, further research is needed to establish whether this intervention leads to statistically significant change and understand the mechanisms through which mindfulness- and self-compassion-based therapy might benefit those who engage in NSSI.
