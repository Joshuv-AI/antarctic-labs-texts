Potential of low‐field MRI for increasing participation of Filipino Americans and underrepresented populations in Alzheimer's and dementia research
Authors: Mikkael Lamoca, Sandra Rothenberg, Roman Czornobil, Eugenie Mamuyac, Karl Korfmacher, Iris Asllani, Jessica de Leon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLack of racial and ethnic minority representation in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) research studies leads to inaccurate trial results and health outcomes disparities. This study examines barriers to ADRD research participation in under‐studied Filipino American participants and explores the potential of low‐field magnetic resonance imaging (LF‐MRI) to mitigate these barriers.METHODSWe utilized GIS mapping to analyze demographic differences across geographic distances from Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) in the United States. The number of visits in racial and ethnic minority populations was examined using regression analysis of participant data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC). Surveys and interviews were conducted with researchers and participants from the University of California, San Francisco Memory and Aging Center to identify barriers and explore LF‐MRI's potential to increase Filipino American participation. We conclude with recommendations for LF‐MRI implementation in ADRD research.RESULTSLocation‐based analysis demonstrates that LF‐MRI may help diversify ADRD research participation by offering portable, cheap imaging modalities in satellite locations. Moreover, many racial and ethnic minority populations, particularly Asians, are underrepresented and less likely to have multiple ADRC visits compared to non‐Hispanic and White individuals. Interviews and surveys identified participation barriers, including navigation, scheduling, fear of MRI, health‐related stigma, and discomfort with conventional MRI procedures. It also suggested that LF‐MRI may offer increased portability, reduced costs, improved physical comfort, and less anxiety. Additionally, researchers identified barriers to implementation, including lack of efficacy and reliability data for advanced imaging requirements, and logistical, privacy, and staff bandwidth concerns.DISCUSSIONThese findings contribute to improved understanding of clinical research participation barriers among racial and ethnic minority populations. LF‐MRI implementation was explored as a potential way to mitigate these barriers. Future research should address scientific and infrastructural limitations and explore ways to leverage the benefits of LF‐MRI in advancing diversity in ADRD research.Highlights Racial and ethnic minorities in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) research are underrepresented, including Asians.Expanding ADRD research access for Asian and minority groups needs broader outreach.Many minority populations are located beyond the typical travel distance from Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs).low‐field magnetic resonance imaging (LF‐MRI) may address comfort, cost, accessibility, and geographical barriers.LF‐MRI implementation may lead to an increase in research participation. Keywords: inequality, ADRD research, low‐field MRI, ADRCs, minority populations, underrepresented populations, and Asian American populations

INTRODUCTION

Healthcare inequality

Inequalities in healthcare access in the United States lead to lower life expectancy and higher mortality rates for many racial and ethnic minority populations 1 . Several studies have shown that factors correlated with healthcare inequality include race, income level, age, biological sex, gender identification, and geographic location. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 More specifically, previous research has shown that race plays an underlying factor in participation in medical research, especially in the Black, Hispanic, and Asian American populations. 9 , 10 , 11 Despite policies and practices aimed at mitigating these inequalities, substantial healthcare disparities remain prevalent. 12 , 13 These inequities are significant because different racial and ethnic populations may have varying genetic predispositions, socioeconomic factors, and health circumstances that can impact diagnosis and therapeutic outcomes, including responses to and outcomes from certain drugs and diagnostic methods. 13 While improvements in representation have been made, these problems persist. 14 , 15

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias inequality

Similar to health care inequality, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD)‐related clinical and neuroimaging studies have little to no representation of American Indian/Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian/Other populations and underrepresentation of Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, and Black populations. 16 , 17 Research suggests these discrepancies are even more pronounced when combined with socioeconomic status. 18 This trend is important given that there are predeposition and sociocultural differences among subpopulations in the United States, which can make the results of ADRD studies less relevant for these subpopulations. For example, biomarker baselines are predominantly based on White males, and past research has found that socioeconomically disadvantaged racial and ethnic minorities are more likely to experience misdiagnosis in ADRD due to differences in cognitive reserve, genetic predisposition, and risk factors. 19 , 20 , 21

ADRD research participation barriers

Finding ways to increase the participation of racial and ethnic minority communities in ADRD research is crucial to ensure that studies accurately reflect the broader population and demographics of the United States; however, understanding current barriers is essential for developing effective strategies. Distance has been a barrier in ADRD clinical practice and research, despite the higher prevalence of ADRD in rural populations due to the aging population; however, fewer ADRD resources are available in these areas. 22 , 23 On the clinical side, prior research has shown that historically underrepresented racial and ethnic group populations’ average distance to neurology physicians is farther compared to White populations, especially in rural areas. 24 In addition to distance, other participation barriers include limited awareness of the existence of ADRD research, knowledge of the differences between ADRD symptoms and normal aging, deep‐rooted historical mistrust caused by unequal treatment, differences in cultural norms, restrictive inclusion/exclusion criteria, caregiver and partner burden due to the time and effort required for research participation, socioeconomic constraints, and comorbidities increasing physical travel limitations, particularly in minority populations. 9 , 10 , 11 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Older Asian American participants may especially hesitate to participate in studies due to the stigma associated with dementia and cognitive decline, which can lead to social isolation or discrimination. In addition, past studies have highlighted that the fear of incidental findings from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans can lead to anxiety in potential research participants. 33 Lastly, high level of attrition is also observed in multi‐year ADRD research and is exacerbated by distance in longitudinal aging studies. 21 , 34 Mitigating distance with satellite centers and ride services has increased participation in ADRD research in the Latino community population. 35 Similar mitigating measures have not been studied in the Asian American community.

Asian American population in ADRD research

The Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) population (originating from East Asia, Southeast Asia, India, and the Pacific Islands) is the fastest‐growing ethnic group in the United States. 36 , 37 However, research on Asian American specific barriers to ADRD research recruitment and participation remains limited, including within Filipino Americans, the largest Southeast Asian American population (Includes Bruneian, Burmese Americans, Cambodian, Filipino, Hmong, Indonesian, Iu Mien, Karen, Laotian, Malaysian, Singaporean, Thai, Timorese and Vietnamese Americans). 11 , 38 Previous research has shown that Asian Americans are less likely to receive ADRD diagnostic tools compared to the White population. 39 Moreover, among Asian American populations, Filipino Americans have the highest dementia incidence rates and have low representation in ADRD research studies. 38 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Yet, a recent scoping review found only 8 of 177 studies included the Filipino American population as an AANHPI subgroup in published ADRD literature. 41 Furthermore, several current extensive AANHPI studies have underrepresented or omitted Filipino Americans in their study designs. 38 , 40 , 43 Therefore, understanding participation barriers within the Filipino American community is crucial for devising effective mitigation strategies to increase participation.

Filipino American ADRD participation barriers

In addition to the aforementioned barriers likely shared across underrepresented racial and ethnic minority populations, there are also factors unique to the Filipino American that may impact their research participation. Colonial mentality, which is the perception of ethnic and cultural inferiority, is prevalent among Filipinos and Filipino Americans (30%–50%) and has been shown to affect healthcare‐seeking behaviors and mental health negatively, and may influence their attitudes towards clinical and mental health care, as well as participation in research. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 Filipino Americans also have varying rates of acculturation and enculturation to the ways of life in the United States, which may also affect attitudes towards clinical and mental health care and research participation. 48 Hiya, a Tagalog term for a sense of propriety or shame, remains prevalent in the community regarding dementia diagnoses 41 , 49 , 50 , 51 Families may hesitate to seek care due to a sense of lakas ng loob, which is the need for resilience and the ability to bear hardships, or a sense of bahala na, a culturally grounded belief that health factors are in God's hands and beyond their control. 50 The community may also face challenges in accessing research due to language barriers, cultural mistrust, a lack of understanding of the research's purpose, or hesitations about participating without direct benefits of a treatment or cure. 52 , 53 , 54 Together, these factors may lower rates of diagnosis and healthcare utilization for ADRD, as well as participation in research.

Low‐field MRI potential

Various strategies have been used to increase the participation of racial and ethnic minority populations in ADRD research. These efforts primarily focus on improving research awareness and building community trust. 55 Fewer strategies focus on reducing participant burden, partly because conventional MRIs used in ADRD research are not portable and have high upfront and maintenance costs. These conventional MRIs are an important tool in the diagnosis of ADRD. It is used to rule out other potential causes of cognitive changes, such as brain tumors, subdural hemorrhages, or normal pressure. 56 Additionally, conventional MRI plays a crucial role in the diagnostic criteria for ADRD. For example, for several types of ADRD, it elevates the likelihood of a diagnosis from possible to probable or imaging. 57 , 58 , 59 Furthermore, conventional MRI can help to differentiate among types of ADRD, including Alzheimer's dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and vascular dementia. 60 However, significant barriers exist to establishing portable systems or satellite centers to increase representation in ADRD research. Portable and low‐cost low‐field (LF) MRI may present one avenue for strategies that reduce participant burden. 61 The advantages and disadvantages of LF‐MRI are summarized in Table 1. 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 LF‐MRI utilizes a lower magnetic field strength (<0.2 T) compared to traditional 1.5 and 3 T MRI. 66 While image quality may be lower, even with advances in AI, they are cheaper than conventional MRIs and portable, lowering the barriers to use in mobile or satellite research centers.

Research questions

Given the importance of understanding barriers and developing recruitment strategies for racial and ethnic minority populations, including the Filipino American population, we seek to answer the following questions: (1) Are self‐defined/identified Asian American individuals living in the US, as identified by the US Census, underrepresented in ADRD research and in ADRD research locales? (2) What barriers affect Filipino Americans' participation in ADRD Research? (3) Can LF‐MRI be used and accepted by researchers and participants in ADRD research, and promote Filipino American participation through mitigating participation barriers?

METHODS

US Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers research locations

For each of the Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs), we utilized a geographic information system (GIS) to extract Census Tract locations within 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 250 miles using existing road infrastructure (ArcGIS 10.2.2) adapted from previously published work. 67 The percentage of self‐identified racial and ethnic groups, aged 55+, those with difficulty living independently, and the population living in poverty within each road network distance was obtained for each Census Tract and tabulated with the Census Tract extracted from GIS. One ADRD location was omitted due to the incomplete availability of the Census data set. The total number of people in each demographic group within each road network distance was normalized to the total number of people in the corresponding demographic group and used to measure access and availability. Distances of 12.5 and 25 miles were used to analyze the current demographic reach of ADRC. Further distances were examined to assess the potential of a portable LF system (50 and 100 miles) or satellite centers to reach racial and ethnic minority populations (100 and 250 miles).

US Individual National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center research participation methodology

Data was obtained from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Query System. The NACC is a data repository for the National Institute of Aging's (NIA's) ADRC Program, which comprises 35 research centers across the United States. The 54,025 participant database as of March 2025 data freeze was analyzed via a logistic multi‐regression analysis, with the dependent variable being multi‐visit (0 = 1 visit, 1 = 2 or more visits) as a measure of continued participation and reported as the odds ratio (OR) and confidence interval (CI) (GraphPad Prism 10.3.1). Only participants enrolled before 2021 were included in the analysis and regression to allow for ample time for a follow‐up visit by March 2025. 68 Moreover, we explored differences across self‐identified racial and ethnic backgrounds while controlling for other factors, including sex, education, age, APOE e4 carrier status, cognitive impairment status, cognitive impairment family history, and current activity status of the participants. We controlled for education, as previous research has shown that years of education are linked to participation in ADRD research. 69 Age and cognitive impairment findings were also included because ADRD is an age‐related disease typically involving cognitive ability that may lead to more travel barriers, thus affecting participation. 70 Additionally, apolipoprotein E (APOE) e4 carrier status and family history of dementia have also been previously shown to be associated with higher retention in Alzheimer's disease clinical trials; therefore, we also included whether they have one or two APOE e4 alleles and a family history of cognitive impairment. 71 Lastly, the absence of multicollinearity and covariance was verified using GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 and additional multiple linear regressions (number of visits dependent variable) with the same independent and control variables are included in the Supplementary Material.

Semi‐Structured survey and interviews with University of California, San Francisco Memory and Aging Center

Participant surveys

To look at users' perceptions of barriers to participation in ADRD research and perceptions of potential LF‐MRI use in this research, we surveyed a representative sample of an established Filipino American research cohort from the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Memory and Aging Center (MAC), one of the 35 National Institutes of Health (NIH)‐funded ADRCs at major medical institutions (N = 13). The representative participants are healthy controls and represent our larger cohort in terms of age, sex, education, distance from our research center, and whether they completed MRI brain imaging for the study (Table SA1). The survey was administered via phone and included questions about personal demographics, MRI‐related barriers, and the implementation of LF‐MRI. Responses were recorded on a Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. After completion, participants received a $10 gift card.

Researcher semi‐structured interviews

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted and recorded by Zoom with trained staff researchers and principal investigators. The first part of the interviews explored topics about research participation barriers and solutions. Interviewees were then asked about their knowledge of LF‐MRIs, given a brief background on the technology, and explored its advantages and disadvantages for increasing participation by racial and ethnic minority populations, with a focus on Filipino American populations. After theoretical saturation, researchers were provided with basic information about LF‐MRI compared to traditional MRI, including magnet strength, cost, benefit, and limitations (Table 1), in addition to photos of the devices and examples of FLAIR and T2 scans of LF‐MRIs used in subacute ischemic stroke and brain hemorrhage. 72 , 73 The researchers were then asked about the potential use of LF‐MRI in ADRD research. Interviews were recorded and transcribed, and an emergent coding structure was developed with Dedoose 9.2 to identify common themes.

RESULTS

Locations of ADRCs relative to socioeconomically‐defined groups

Based on patient participation research, participants traveled a median distance of 25 miles to participate. 74 We found that 16.20% of White, 11.86% of Black, 2.95% of Hispanic, 5.58% of Asian, 0% of American Indian/Native Alaskan, and 0% of Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander groups live within 25 miles of an ADRC (Table 2). Increasing this to 50 miles outside of ADRC led to an increase in the percentage of White, Black, Hispanic, Asian individuals, over 55 years of age, independent living difficulty, and poverty population groups' access to ADRDs. This increase in access continued with road network distances of 100 and 250 miles. Notably, American Indian/Native Alaskan (0%) and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander (0%) remain inadequately served even within 250 miles of ADRCs.

Demographic characteristics and returning study visits of NACC research participants

Of the 46037 total research participants in the NACC database enrolled on or before the year 2021, participants had an average of 15.16 ± 3.44 years of education, had an average age of 71.39 ± 10.50, and primarily self‐identified as non‐Hispanic (91.94%) and White (80.17%) (Tables SA2 and SA3). Moreover, historically underrepresented populations tended to have a lower likelihood of signing up for more than 1 visit or an inactive status (Table 3). More specifically, Asian and Other populations were significantly less likely to have multiple visits (odds ratio [OR]: 0.756; confidence interval [CI]: 0.6323 to 0.9083; P = 0.0025 and OR: 0.6152; CI: 0.4753 to 0.7990; P = 0.0002, respectively). UCSF MAC interview and survey results

Filipino American research participation results

The Filipino American survey respondents (N = 13) were 77% female, with a mean of 12.38 years of education, and the majority were over 65 years old (69%). To reach the UCSF MAC research center, 69% of the cohort commute over 45 min using various means of transportation, including driving (driving themselves or relying on friends and family), rideshare apps, and public transportation. 85% of the cohort had received an MRI, and 62% received an MRI as part of UCSF MAC research studies.

Likert scale survey results are summarized in Table 4. In our sample of 13 participants, participants ranked understanding why MRIs are used in ADRD research as 1.77 ± 1.01 and 2.31 ± 1.25 in understanding of how MRIs work (Table 4A). The greatest concern with MRIs was the high decibel volume of traditional MRIs ranked at 3.92 ± 1.26, and the low level of comfort at 3.92 ± 1.26. Other less frequently identified themes included concerns about magnet strength, feelings of claustrophobia, MRI safety concerns, traveling to the MRI, and staying still during MRIs.

When images of both an LF‐MRI (Hyperfine Swoop System) and a conventional MRI were shown, all participants stated that they would prefer to use the LF‐MRI, stating that it was visually less intimidating. Participants were also asked about their likelihood of participating if MRIs had qualities found in LF‐MRIs (Table 4B). Participants ranked that they were more likely to participate if MRIs had reduced noise levels at 4.00 ± 1.35, were portable and could be brought close to or to their homes at 3.62 ± 1.26, had a lower magnet strength at 3.61 ± 1.19, and had more room at 3.54 ± 1.20. Lastly, some participants also mentioned that they would be more likely to participate if they could bring other people to sit with them during the scan.

Research participation and the use of conventional MRI and LF‐MRI researcher interview results

Common themes from the researchers' interviews included navigation, scheduling, fear and stigma, socioeconomic factors, trust, MRI comfort, and the advantages and disadvantages of LF‐MRIs (Table 5). All the researchers agreed that traveling to San Francisco was a potential barrier to research participation, despite efforts to mitigate transportation and parking costs. All but one researcher also mentioned limitations related to scheduling availability, such as conflicts with childcare, elderly care, work, or other obligations.

Fear and stigma were mentioned in three of the interviews. Researchers noted that participants fear receiving unfavorable MRI diagnostic results or the discovery of incidental findings, particularly if the community has preexisting stigmas about dementia and brain health. All six researchers identified fear and stigma regarding the safety of the MRI itself as a barrier to participation. Four of the researchers mentioned that participants fear that MRIs may cause harm or do not understand why it is necessary at all. Half of the interviewees mentioned that building trusting relationships before recruiting participants, especially within racial and ethnic minority populations, can help alleviate these concerns.

Similar to research participants, researchers also mentioned several barriers to using conventional MRI, such as claustrophobia (four of six), inability to remain still (four of six), discomfort with loud sounds (two of six), and general discomfort with MRIs (two fo six), which in some cases has led to early termination of the scan. Lastly, two of six of researchers also mentioned that MRI research scanners are in high demand, which can limit the amount of data that can be collected in a single session.

Researchers thought that LF‐MRI had the potential to be a portable imaging technology that could operate both within a medical setting and in the community (Table 4). A primary identified advantage was that researchers could bring imaging to more geographically convenient locations within underserved communities, reducing cost and time barriers for participants. Bringing the MRI to participants would also help build trusting relationships with the communities and reduce fear by conducting testing in places that are more familiar to the participants. Moreover, since patients have more flexibility in terms of time and location when using LF‐MRIs, researchers have also stated that it may make it easier to complete imaging and cognitive testing during a single visit, thereby reducing participant burden.

Like the research participants, researchers stated that concerns associated with noise, claustrophobia, patient movement, and discomfort during an MRI scan would also be mitigated. The presence of a companion during the scanning session could also help participants with anxiety and fear. Since LF‐MRI costs significantly less to purchase and maintain than conventional MRIs, its use can improve access and potentially allow for the purchase of several LF‐MRIs with the budget that would otherwise be reserved for a single traditional MRI.

Despite the advantages of LF‐MRI, interviewees identified three main limitations: image quality, infrastructure requirements, and increased privacy issues. One researcher stated that LF‐MRIs would not be applicable to their current study due to the reduced image quality. Still, they could be implemented in future projects, particularly with the aid of AI. Respondents also emphasized the need to establish an infrastructure for LF‐MRI technology, including clear protocols, defined goals, staff training, and sufficient bandwidth. Another concern was that researchers would need to take on the burden of traveling previously held by participants. Lastly, two researchers emphasized the need to address privacy and access issues prior to implementation. However, they also discussed how these concerns could be mitigated through clear procedures, protocols, and adequate funding.

DISCUSSION

This study contributes to the growing interest in understanding the barriers faced by ADRD research participants from racial and ethnic minority populations. Previous research has shown that race plays an underlying factor in participation in research, especially in the Black, Hispanic, and Asian American populations. 9 , 10 , 11 More specifically, our analysis supports that Asian and other racial and ethnic minority populations visit less and are geographically farther away from ADRCs in comparison to the White non‐Hispanic populations. For example, approximately 0.5% of the US population identifies as Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (1.6 million individuals, including those identifying as either only or more than one race), with the highest populations in Hawaii (25%), California (21%), and Washington (7%). 37 , 75 Since there are no current ADRCs in Hawaii, this likely adds further geographical barriers to their participation in research. These results confirm that new strategies are needed to increase participation. Research focused on Filipino Americans, the largest Southeast Asian population in the United States, is limited despite their high rates of dementia and large population in the United States, even within an AANHPI context. 11 , 37 , 38 , 42 To address this gap in research, our study also provides insight into barriers to participation for these Filipino American populations, which could be expanded to other racial and ethnic minority populations. It also provides solutions beyond current engagement and outreach efforts to increase the participation of racial and ethnic minority populations in ADRD.

In this study, researchers with years of experience in recruiting and working with Filipino Americans discussed how these cultural factors can impact research participation and their strategies to develop trust. Filipino American participants identified other barriers to participation, including navigation to the research site, scheduling, fear, cost, trust, MRI auditory volume, claustrophobia, and more. Additionally researchers pointed out that LF‐MRI may assist with minority recruitment on two fronts: expanding the reach of ADRCs and more effectively recruiting local communities. Focusing on expanding the reach of ADRCs, LF‐MRI have key advantages in comparison to conventional MRIs (Table 1). More specifically, conventional MRIs are an essential tool in ADRD research (e.g., assess patterns of brain atrophy and/or rule out potential causes of cognitive changes, including brain tumors, subdural hemorrhages, or normal pressure, among other uses), but they can sometimes be challenging to obtain due to distance, accessibility, and high upfront and maintenance costs. 76 , 77 Researchers thought that some of these barriers could be addressed by using LF‐MRI. 56 LF‐MRI would allow potential participants to travel to a closer or more convenient site, where there could also be personnel available to assist with other elements of a typical research visit, including blood draws, neurological examinations, and/or cognitive testing, and/or other community outreach activities. All of these study components could be performed in standard facilities (such as existing clinic spaces, elderly care facilities, or office buildings). Notably, these elements, including LF‐MRI, can be performed in a mobile setting, and the imaging information from an LF‐MRI can be uploaded from either the satellite center or any other portable location. Overall, the use of LF‐MRI could open up the possibility that a research participant would not have to travel to a specialized research facility that has been configured to house a standard MRI machine. Of note, mobile LF‐MRIs have also been created and used in low‐resource hospitals, highlighting their potential to reach a broader population. 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 Researchers also highlighted the ability to take LF‐MRIs to local communities to potentially increase recruitment by increasing awareness and building trust. Researchers also raised several issues and concerns before LF‐MRI implementation, including evidence of efficacy and efficiency, and having an infrastructure ready for a portable system before implementation. One proposed model is to start building LF‐MRI evidence in specific applications with lower image resolution requirements (stroke/trauma) and move on to other applications until the efficacy and validity have been established. Future research can be centered on comparisons with high and low‐field imaging or community‐facing grants to purchase LF‐MRIs for initial screenings.

There are limitations to consider for future studies. First, the multilogistic regression model covers a variance of less than 7%. Second, our survey only includes study participants; thus, it is unclear if comfort with medical settings, large cities, medical technology, and traveling to research locations reflects the larger Filipino American community, missing key elements from those who chose not to enroll or drop out of the study. Yet, our participants still raised these concerns, thereby highlighting the need to explore further. It is also essential to note that while the US Census Bureau classifies Filipino Americans as Asians, Filipino Americans may self‐identify in other defined categories, opting for the “Other” or “Pacific Islander” categories; thus, there is a need for data disaggregation to enable individuals to categorize themselves accurately. Additionally, the current sociopolitical environment may pose further challenges for individuals in terms of their comfort level with research participation and in accurately identifying their immigrant status. Using an ordinal scale, although commonly used for measuring attitudes and opinions, also presents limitations, such as differences in scale interpretation and the higher likelihood of skewed distributions; for example, survey respondents may be more likely to choose the highest option or level of agreement than other options. Lastly, the use of a cohort based at an NIH‐funded research center also limits the potential generalizability of the findings, as participants may have encountered different barriers to participation if they had considered enrollment through community or clinic‐based research centers.

Future work can examine additional factors contributing to participation, as highlighted in our case study and the literature, such as general health conditions, mental health, willingness to participate, transportation, conflicting obligations, discrimination, stigma and beliefs, trust, perceived ADRD risk, literacy, and others. 25 , 82 In addition, institutions with non‐NIH‐funded ADRCs and different types of research projects (with and without imaging components) should be included in the geographical distance analysis, as they can influence the demographic reach of overall ADRD research and analysis of the advantages of portable LF‐MRI or satellite centers. Moreover, a multi‐site interview and survey data collection with a larger sample size would yield more generalizable results. This survey and interview can serve as a template for future studies exploring historically underrepresented population barriers in ADRD participation and the use of LF‐MRI in racial and ethnic minority populations or other research institutes. It is also important for future studies to explore why potential participants chose not to enroll in research studies or decline to return for additional study visits; this may yield additional information that is not reflected in data from those with multiple visits. Overall, if the use of LF‐MRI in ADRD research is sufficient for neuroimaging, this technology could increase the participation of racial and ethnic minority populations with adequate training and support for the required infrastructure.
