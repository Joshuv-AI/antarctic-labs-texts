An acute presentation of Wunderlich’s syndrome with angiomyolipoma: a case report
Authors: Rachel Wu, Cuong Tran, Benjamin Archambeau, Fanglong Dong, Michael M Neeki
Published: 2025

Introduction

Angiomyolipomas (AMLs) are a rare type of soft tissue benign tumors that contain fat, blood vessels, and smooth muscles, affecting < 1% of the population [1]. While the proportions of components vary among tumors, imaging studies often reveal a large amount of adipose tissue seen on imaging studies [1]. Among the benign kidney tumors, AMLs are the most common [2], and they are predominantly seen in women [1]. Approximately 80% of these tumors occur sporadically, but there is a known association between AMLs and tuberous sclerosis [3]. Tuberous sclerosis is a rare genetic condition characterized by seizures, mental retardation, and facial angiomas. AMLs associated with tuberous sclerosis tend to be larger, bilateral, and multiple as opposed to isolated AMLs [4].

AMLs are most commonly asymptomatic and are most often diagnosed incidentally on computed tomography (CT) [5]. Among patients who have symptomatic AMLs, the classic triad of symptoms is flank pain, palpable mass, and hematuria [6]. The main complication of AML is retroperitoneal hemorrhage or Wunderlich’s syndrome caused by rupture of the tumor [2]. This complication is historically seen in approximately 15% of patients with AML [7]. Here, we present a patient with retroperitoneal hemorrhage caused by an AML with a unique presentation unlike the traditional triad of symptoms. Case presentation

A 48-year-old Hispanic female patient presented to the Emergency Department (ED) for hematemesis for the past 2 days associated with generalized weakness, abdominal pain, nausea, and subjective fevers. The patient’s initial vital signs were significant for a heart rate of 127 beats per minute, blood pressure of 92/62 mmHg, temporal temperature of 36.6 °C, respiration rate of 20 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. Past medical history was significant for childhood seizures, and a cerebral vascular accident more than 15 years ago with mild residual left side weakness. Her physical examination on arrival at the ED was notable for significant skin pallor and diffuse abdominal tenderness that was most pronounced in the epigastric area and left lower quadrant. No costovertebral angle tenderness, rebound tenderness, or guarding were noted. The remainder of her physical examination was unremarkable. Given the reported history of hematemesis with tachycardia, a complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, lipase, abdominal X-ray, and urinalysis were ordered. Laboratory studies were significant for a venous lactate of 3.53 mmol/L, hemoglobin of 9.2 g/dL, hematocrit of 26%, leukocytosis at white cell count of 23.7 × 109/L, creatinine level at 4.23 mg/dL, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 12.3 mL/min. An abdominal portable kidney, ureter, and bladder x-ray showed a nonobstructive bowel gas pattern (Fig. 1).

Abdominal X-ray demonstrating nonobstructive gas pattern

Her electrocardiogram revealed sinus tachycardia with no acute ST segment changes. The patient was unable to urinate during this time to provide a sample for analysis. Owing to concern for acute kidney injury with creatinine of 4.23 mg/dL and an eGFR of 12.3 mL/min, the decision was made to perform a CT of the abdomen and pelvis without contrast. The CT of the abdomen indicated probable large bilateral angiomyolipomas in the kidneys with hemorrhage, left worse than right (Fig. 2). Normal renal tissue could not be visualized on the CT owing to angiomyolipomas. The lack of intravenous (IV) contrast limited the evaluation of active hemorrhage in the retroperitoneal area.

Computed tomography scan demonstrating large bilateral angiomyolipomas of the kidneys with hemorrhage

Subsequently, the patient was started on IV fluids resulting in improved tachycardia. Following the results of CT abdomen and pelvis demonstrating signs of hemorrhage, interventional radiologist was consulted regarding possible selective arterial embolization. Interventional radiologist did not recommend any acute intervention at that time. The recommendation was to continue to trend hemoglobin and hematocrit every 4 h, repeat creatinine after adequate fluid resuscitation, and admit the patient for observation. If the patient demonstrated clinical evidence of persistent bleeding, reconsultation for possible embolization was recommended. General surgery and nephrology were also consulted, with no acute surgical intervention and no emergent indication for hemodialysis recommended at that time.

The patient was admitted to the internal medicine for close monitoring owing to concern for active hemorrhage of angiomyolipomas with concern of impending hemorrhagic shock. Repeat laboratory studies performed 3 hours later showed that the patient’s hemoglobin and hematocrit had trended down to levels of 6.1 g/dL and 18%, respectively. The patient was transfused with two units of packed red blood cells and subsequently underwent bilateral arterial embolization of left lower pole renal artery, left upper pole renal artery, left pole segmental renal artery, and right upper pole renal artery by interventional radiology team.

Serial serum studies after the embolization demonstrated that the patient’s hemoglobin and hematocrit had stabilized. Her kidney function showed minimal improvement during the course of her hospital stay. The patient did not have any further hematemesis and was discharged with close nephrology follow-up for likely necessary hemodialysis in the future along with consideration of kidney transplant. She was advised to seek further genetic counseling for potential undiagnosed tuberous sclerosis owing to her history of childhood seizures and bilateral AMLs.

Discussion

Renal angiomyolipomas are often found coincidentally on imaging and require no immediate intervention [8]. Increased risk of bleeding has been associated with tumors that are larger in size (> 4.0 cm in diameter), with multicentric angiomyolipomas, and those associated with tuberous sclerosis [9]. This may be attributed to abnormal low level of elastic tissue support in the structure and the component of blood vessels, leading to intralesional pseudoaneurysm [10]. Angiomyolipomas that hemorrhage can lead to Wunderlich syndrome, acute onset of nontraumatic renal hemorrhage into subcapsular and perirenal spaces. AMLs are the most common benign neoplasm to cause this syndrome [11]. Patients with Wunderlich syndrome traditionally present with a classic triad [6]. However, in this case, the patient did not present with the classic symptoms. This patient’s pain was limited to bilateral lower quadrants, not involving the flanks, and no palpable masses were identified on physical examination. Of note, the patient was unable to provide a urine sample in the ED for evaluation of hematuria. Her poor urine output, however, was likely a direct complication of her AML, secondary to acute renal failure. Research reports that these classic symptoms are found in less than half of individuals treated for AMLs [6]. This case highlights the diverse presentations of Wunderlich syndrome in a medical setting. Common symptoms of Wunderlich syndrome, such as anemia and renal insufficiency, were observed in this patient [12]. These findings are often nonspecific, though, and can be seen in various other pathologies.

Renal angiomyolipomas are commonly associated with tuberous sclerosis. Tuberous sclerosis patients are often diagnosed first with skin manifestations or secondary to seizures. These symptoms usually manifest in childhood [13]. Our patient did not have a past medical diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis, but owing to her history of childhood seizures along with large bilateral AMLs, this diagnosis cannot be ruled out. Studies have shown that renal AMLs are more likely to hemorrhage if a patient has an associated diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis [9].

Diagnosis of renal angiomyolipomas is classically found on CT scan. This method can identify the location and demonstrate complications such as bleeding [5]. CT is the most sensitive diagnostic method for evaluation. This is especially true when it results in recognition of active hemorrhaging, as seen in this case, which led to early intervention and treatment [5].

Most patients can often be managed conservatively if they present asymptomatically with renal angiomyolipomas that are less than 4.0 cm in diameter. First-line treatment is selective arterial embolization. Nephrectomy can be indicated if there is persistent hemorrhage or failed embolization [6]. Selective arterial embolization has not only shown to have good short-term stabilization of patients, but also has long-term efficacy in preventing additional hemorrhagic complications [14, 15].

Conclusion

This report presents a case of Wunderlich’s syndrome caused by hemorrhage of renal angiomyolipomas, presenting as a medical emergency. Although renal angiomyolipomas are rare benign tumors, complications such as bleeding can occur. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent life-threatening complications. Therefore, clinicians should consider renal angiomyolipomas in patients with acute renal failure, anemia, and abdominal pain to enable early diagnosis and treatment.
