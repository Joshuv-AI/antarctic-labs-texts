A novel method for assessing leg length discrepancy after total hip arthroplasty: Measurement of the trochanter major–ischial tuberosity distance
Authors: Uğur Yüzügüldü, Bilge Kağan Yilmaz, Süleyman Çakirtürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leg length discrepancy is a common and clinically important complication following total hip arthroplasty. It may lead to patient dissatisfaction, altered gait, and functional impairment. Although radiographic assessment is widely used, existing measurement methods can be affected by pelvic rotation and anatomical variations. This study aimed to evaluate the reliability and validity of a novel radiographic method based on the distance between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity for assessing leg length discrepancy after hip arthroplasty. This retrospective cohort study included 56 patients who underwent hip arthroplasty between 2020 and 2023 and had appropriate postoperative standing pelvic radiographs. Leg length was measured clinically using the anterior superior iliac spine–medial malleolus distance, and radiographically using 3 different methods: teardrop to lesser trochanter, greater trochanter to ischial tuberosity, and anterior superior iliac spine to ankle. All radiographic measurements were performed twice, 2 weeks apart, by 2 orthopedic surgeons. Intraobserver and interobserver reliability were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between the measurement methods. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of each method in identifying leg length discrepancy of ≥ 10 mm. The mean age of the patients was 66.8 ± 9.1 years. The greater trochanter–ischial tuberosity method showed excellent intraobserver and interobserver reliability (ICC > 0.84). A strong positive correlation was found between this method and the anterior superior iliac spine–ankle measurement (P < .001). The novel method also demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy in detecting leg length discrepancies ≥ 10 mm (AUC: 0.96). The greater trochanter–ischial tuberosity distance is a reliable, reproducible, and practical radiographic measurement method for assessing leg length discrepancy after hip arthroplasty. Its reduced sensitivity to pelvic rotation and anatomical variation makes it a promising alternative to traditional methods, especially in clinical settings where full-limb imaging is not available.

1. Introduction

Hip osteoarthritis is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal disorder. Total hip arthroplasty (THA), performed in advanced-stage hip osteoarthritis, is an effective treatment method that improves function and reduces pain.[1,2] Therefore, THA is considered one of the most successful orthopedic surgeries, and its use is increasing.[3–5] However, leg length discrepancy (LLD), which can be observed preoperatively and postoperatively in THA patients, is a significant problem and is considered a challenging complication.[6] Postoperative LLD can occur in 6–32% of patients after THA and can lead to outcomes such as abnormal gait, sciatica and low back pain, dislocation, patient dissatisfaction, and legal issues.[7–10]

Numerous clinical and radiological methods for measuring LLD have been described in the literature; each has its advantages and weaknesses.[11–15] Clinical methods based on measuring the distance between 2 anatomical structures with a tape measure are widely used, but have lower reliability compared to radiographic methods.[16] Although radiographic techniques are generally considered more accurate than clinical methods for assessing LLD, they involve exposure to ionizing radiation.[17] Depending on the available imaging equipment, leg length radiographs can be obtained using different techniques; however, these methods may fail to detect angular or fixed flexion deformities of the femur or tibia.[18] Computed tomography (CT) allows more precise measurement of leg length discrepancy; however, its use is limited by greater radiation exposure and higher cost.[19] Furthermore, the need for additional radiological evaluation reduces the practicality of CT. Plain pelvic radiographs are widely used to assess hip-related LLD because they are simple, reproducible, involve relatively low radiation exposure, and allow bilateral comparison.[12,15]

In pelvic radiographic measurements, the teardrops and ischial tubercles (IT) are used as pelvic references, while the femoral head center and lesser trochanter are used on the femoral side.[20] The inferior margin of the acetabular teardrops (TD) and the lesser trochanters (LT) are most commonly used for LLD calculation.[10,21] It has also been suggested that the use of the femoral head center is reproducible and may be affected by subjective interpretation,[15,22,23] and intraobserver reliability has been reported to be moderate.[12,14] Reference point definition can be affected by changes in anatomy associated with conditions such as hip dysplasia, pelvic positioning, and femoral rotation. Therefore, surgeons may need to change the reference points for measuring LLD in such patients.[23] In cases where reference point selection is difficult, determining objective reference points for calculating LLD would be of great clinical benefit. In a recent deep learning study, Jang et al reported that LLD measurements from the IT to the greater trochanter (GT) had the highest ICC values and showed the least variation in LLD between patients.[24]

The present study introduces a novel measurement approach that uses the superior portion of the greater trochanter as the femoral landmark and the inferior margin of the ischial tuberosity as the pelvic reference point. The study hypothesizes that radiographic GT-IT measurement is an alternative method for determining LLD. The aim of this study was to assess the interobserver reliability and reproducibility of the radiographic GT–IT measurement and to determine its accuracy by comparing it with conventional methods for evaluating LLD.

2. Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Ethics Committee (No. 2023/2, date: 03.02.2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Records of patients who underwent THA at our institution between 2020 and 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Participants were clinically evaluated during routine outpatient clinic visits, and informed consent was obtained. Patients who had a weight-bearing (WB) anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiograph postoperatively were included in the study. Inclusion criteria included patients who had undergone THA and had appropriate postoperative radiographs. Exclusion criteria included patients with severe lower extremity deformity, history of hip, knee, or ankle surgery, severe knee arthritis-related deformity, hip and/or knee flexion contracture, acetabular dysplasia or previous related surgery, greater trochanter fractures, and patients with unsuitable radiographs. Demographic data were collected from patients who attended routine outpatient clinic checkups following the study plan, and LLD measurements were performed using the clinical measuring tape (by the SC). Appropriate AP radiographs were taken in the WB position and included radiographs showing the acetabular cartilage, femoral head, acetabular teardrop structures, ischial tuberosities, and lesser and greater trochanters; no transverse rotation; and centering of the coccyx over the pubic symphysis. To ensure measurement consistency, all radiographs were obtained with both lower extremities positioned in neutral rotation and with symmetrical pelvic alignment. A total of 56 patients meeting these criteria were included in the study.

For pelvic LLD, the lowest point of the TD, the most medial point of the LT, the point of the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), the apex of the GT, and the lowest point of the IT were marked on the WB AP pelvic radiograph as described by Meermans et al[15] The most distal point of the tibial plafond (ankle) was also marked on the orthoroentgenogram. Radiographically, the perpendicular distance between the line connecting the TDs and the line connecting the LTs was measured for the TD-LT measurement, the perpendicular distance between the line connecting the GTs and the line connecting the ITs for the ASIS-Ankle measurement, and the perpendicular distance between the line connecting the GTs and the line connecting the ITs for the GT-IT measurement (Fig. 1). Clinically, the distance between the ASIS and the medial malleolus was measured using the tape measure method. LLD was defined as the difference between the measurements obtained from the 2 sides. All measurements were performed independently by 2 orthopedic surgeons (UY and BKY) using PACS software (Picture Archiving and Communication System; Nucleus MBS, MONAD Yazilim ve Danişmanlik A.Ş., Ankara, Turkey). Each observer repeated the measurements twice with a 2-week interval between sessions. Measurements indicating a longer operated limb were recorded as positive values, whereas those indicating a shorter limb were recorded as negative values.

2.1. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (v3.11). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The intraobserver and interobserver reliability of the measurements performed by 2 observers (UY and BKY) were assessed using the ICC. ICC values and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated separately for each measurement method and interpreted accordingly. The interpretation of ICC values was as follows: >0.80 indicated almost perfect agreement; 0.70 to 0.80, strong agreement; 0.50 to 0.69, moderate agreement; 0.30 to 0.49, fair agreement; and 0.00 to 0.29, poor agreement. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess the linear association between measurement methods. Differences in measurement values according to sex and side (right/left) were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Finally, to assess the diagnostic performance of each method in predicting a LLD ≥ 10 mm, a ROC analysis was conducted, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were calculated. The ASIS–Ankle distance was considered the reference standard, and a discrepancy of ≥ 10 mm was defined as clinically significant. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

3. Results

A total of 56 patients were included in this study. The mean age of the participants was 66.8 ± 9.1 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 29.5 ± 3.6 kg/m2. Of the patients, 58.9% were female (n = 33) and 41.1% were male (n = 23). The distribution of the measured sides was 55.4% right (n = 31) and 44.6% left (n = 25) (Table 1).

4. Discussion

In this study, a new radiographic method for the assessment of LLD in patients undergoing THA, the GT–IT distance measurement, was described and compared with other conventional methods. The results show that the GT–IT measurement has both high interobserver reliability and a strong correlation with the ASIS–ankle distance. Our findings indicate that the GT–IT measurement can serve as a useful approach for evaluating LLD on pelvic radiographs.

LLD is a relatively frequent postoperative complication and may result in patient dissatisfaction as well as medicolegal concerns.[25] LLD is a critical issue in THA because large LLDs can lead to adverse patient outcomes.[8–10,26,27] Accordingly, assessment of leg length discrepancy should be performed carefully throughout the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative stages of arthroplasty. The best technique for measuring and handling LLD remains unclear. This also poses legal issues in orthopedic practice.[28] Surgeons can assess LLD radiologically on pelvic AP radiography; however, this only includes anatomical references at the level of the hip joint.[29,30] In these measurements, the acetabular teardrops, the inferior border of the ischial tuberosity, and the medial process of the lesser trochanter are commonly used.[15,31–33] Despite the availability of multiple assessment techniques, uncertainty persists regarding their accuracy and reproducibility in measuring leg length discrepancy. Konyves et al reported that pre- and postoperative LLD could be accurately assessed using the acetabular teardrops and the medial process of the lesser trochanter after THA.[10] In contrast, Hardwick-Morris et al reported that the traditional teardrop LT measurement of LLD did not correlate well with other long-leg measurements.[11] Heaver et al reported that measuring from the inferior aspect of the ischial tuberosities to the most prominent medial point of the lesser trochanter on an AP pelvic radiograph was the most reproducible method.[18] Meermans et al suggested that the ischial tuberosity should not be used, that the teardrop is the correct landmark, and that the center of the femoral head is a more reliable femoral landmark than the lesser trochanter.[15] Tsang et al suggested that this reference point would not be used clinically, considering that it would not create a LLD between the center of the femoral head and the lesser trochanter in cases of osteoarthritis. They reported that the obturator foramen line and the lesser trochanter provide more reliable reference points and can be used to estimate the actual leg length discrepancy.[34] The most important advantage of the GT-IT distance used in our proposed measurement method is the ease of determining these reference points. This may be explained by the fact that hip rotation makes the lesser trochanter difficult to identify, whereas the tip of the greater trochanter is less affected by rotational changes. Similarly, the ischial tuberosities are visible on all pelvic AP radiographs and are not affected by rotation or angular deformities.

Conflicting results have been reported in the literature regarding the reference point to be used in pelvic LLD measurements. Jang et al in their measurements using deep learning in a large patient group, reported significant differences in LLD depending on the selection of the pelvic/femoral reference point.[24] While Pettit et al noted that there is no commonly accepted reference point standard for radiographs, Jang et al suggested using the superior portion of the greater trochanter as the femoral reference point, rather than the lesser trochanter, when determining leg length discrepancy in total hip arthroplasty.[14,24] They stated that LLD measurements from the obturator foramen and ischial tuberosity to the greater trochanter had the highest ICC values as a pelvic reference point and showed the least variation in LLD.[24] They also highlighted that this preference was mainly due to the difficulty in identifying the appropriate reference point on pelvic radiographs affected by rotation.[24] The GT-IT distance measurement emphasized in our study eliminates the inadequacies in determining the anatomic reference point. Our proposed measurement method may provide more reliable detection of LLD compared to other pelvic radiographic methods.

Evaluation of intraobserver and interobserver agreement in LLD assessment methods contributes to the reliability of that method. Pettit et al reported that both intraobserver and interobserver agreement for assessing leg length discrepancy on AP pelvic radiographs varied widely, ranging from poor to excellent.[14] In contrast, Kjellberg et al demonstrated excellent interobserver reliability and reproducibility of LLD measurements obtained from plain radiographs.[35] Hardwick-Morris et al noted that traditional measurement of LLD on AP pelvic radiographs does not correlate well with long-leg measurements and may not provide an accurate assessment of LLD.[11] In the current study, intraobserver and interobserver agreement were excellent for the GT-IT and ASIS-ankle methods and moderate to excellent for the TD-LT method.

Accurate assessment of true leg length discrepancy requires evaluation of the entire limb. Recent studies have questioned the reliability of relying solely on AP pelvic radiographs for detecting LLD and therefore do not recommend this approach.[11,12,35,36] Hardwick-Morris et al noted that the traditional measurement of LLD on AP pelvic radiography does not correlate well with long-leg measurements and may not provide an accurate assessment of LLD. They reported that the ASIS-ankle measurement better detects potential LLD than other EOS and radiography measurements.[11] Imaging techniques that allow visualization of the entire lower limb, such as teleoroentgenograms and orthoroentgenograms, can provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the different sources contributing to leg length discrepancy.[36] Pelvic radiography can be considered a simplified alternative to orthoroentgenograms for detecting LLD. Because it is relatively inexpensive, associated with lower radiation exposure, easy to perform, and sufficiently effective, pelvic radiography is used far more frequently in clinical practice than orthoroentgenograms.[37] Furthermore, since limb length alterations before and after surgery mainly arise from changes at the hip joint, comparing preoperative and postoperative pelvic radiographs may offer a reliable estimation of LLD.[12] In this context, it is anticipated that AP pelvic radiography will retain its importance in the assessment of LLD after THA. Indeed, in our study, a strong positive correlation was found between the newly defined GT–IT measurement method and the gold standard ASIS–ankle (orthoroentgenogram) measurement. These findings suggest that GT–IT measurement, although performed solely on AP pelvic radiographs, may be a practical and reliable alternative method.

The current study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and relatively small number of patients may limit generalizability. Although the results demonstrated strong reliability and diagnostic performance, larger patient populations may better reflect the variability encountered in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, only postoperative radiographs were evaluated, and patient-reported outcome measures or the patient-perceived level of LLD were not included in the study. In addition, femoral offset measurements, one of the important factors that may influence patients’ perception of LLD, were not included in the analysis. Strengths of the study include the combined use of both clinical (using a tape measure) and radiological (standing full-length orthoroentgenogram) measurement methods, the exclusion of patients with concomitant osteoarthritis or deformities of the lower extremities, thus ensuring homogeneity of the sample. Furthermore, the application of the GT–IT measurement method, described for the first time in the literature, and the assessment of both intra- and inter-observer reliability for this method are among the study’s unique and methodological strengths.

5. Conclusions

The GT-IT measurement method applied to plain pelvic radiographs demonstrates a high level of correlation with conventional methods described in the literature and provides excellent intra- and inter-observer agreement. Although this method does not consider length differences originating from the distal parts of the lower extremity, it enables reliable evaluation of leg length discrepancy, particularly when comparing the pre- and post-THA periods. Its practical applicability, low cost, and reproducibility make it a reliable alternative suitable for clinical use. Nevertheless, additional comparative studies including larger patient populations and various measurement techniques are required to further validate the method and enhance its generalizability. In particular, prospective and multicenter studies including diverse patient populations may help confirm the diagnostic performance and clinical applicability of the GT–IT measurement method in routine clinical practice.
