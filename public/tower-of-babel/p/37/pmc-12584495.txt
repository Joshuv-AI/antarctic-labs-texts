Impact of varying diurnal temperature ranges on regulation of multiple physiological traits of a drought-tolerant moss (Grimmia pilifera)
Authors: Ling Dai, Dong Qiu, Xiao-Dong Ma, Yue Zhang, Ye Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mosses that usually exist as gametophytes lack true roots, stems, and leaves, and their physiological activity is highly sensitive to environmental changes. The increase of diurnal temperature range (DTR) is one of the important characteristics of global climate change, but it is still unclear how mosses respond to the change of DTR. The typical drought-tolerant and saxicolous moss Grimmia pilifera was used as the research subject, two DTR treatments were set up: 20/10 ℃ (day/night; as natural habitat temperature) and 30/10 ℃ (day/night; as increased DTR). After homogenized cultivation, three dehydration-rehydration cycles and physiological measurements were conducted on days 7, 14, and 21 to investigate the comprehensive response of mosses to change in DTR over time. The results showed that under different diurnal temperature range (DTR) treatments, the chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of Grimmia pilifera exhibited marked responses to the dehydration–rehydration process, characterized by inhibited PSII activity, enhanced energy dissipation, and restricted electron transport during dehydration, with partial recovery of these parameters upon rehydration. Compared to the 20/10°C treatment, the 30/10°C treatment resulted in more pronounced inhibition during dehydration, but also demonstrated a stronger recovery capacity after rehydration. Water content (WC) declined over time, and its recovery after rehydration was significantly lower under 30/10°C than 20/10°C. Overall, the MDA content at both 14 and 21 days was significantly lower than the initial level, with a more substantial decrease observed under the 30/10°C treatment on day 21. The 30/10°C treatment exhibited lower activity of antioxidant enzymes (primarily CAT), and showed higher levels of osmotic adjustment substances (PRO, SP). The plant trait network analysis revealed that the core traits on days 7, 14, and 21 under the 20/10 ℃ treatment were WC, PI abs, and ETo/RC, respectively; while under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, the core traits were PI abs, SP, and PRO content, respectively. The stability of trait network structure was higher under the 20/10 ℃ treatment than under 30/10 ℃. Analysis of pooled data showed that the core traits under both treatments were photosynthetic parameters (Fv/Fo under 20/10 ℃ and PI abs under 30/10 ℃), indicating that G. pilifera exhibited differential changes in trait network structure and core traits over time under varying DTRs, but overall relied on coordinated regulation of the photosynthetic system to cope with environmental stress. These findings provide new physiological and ecological insights into how mosses respond to global climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07511-9.

Introduction

As pioneers in terrestrial ecosystems, mosses play an important ecological function in forests, mountains, tundra and deserts. Grimmia pilifera is a typical lithophytic and drought-tolerant moss species, and it has specialized ecological adaptability [1–3]. Its gametophyte is robust and rigid, sparsely clustered in patches, with the upper parts appearing green or dark green. In response to desiccation, the leaves cling to the stem, forming a compact imbricate arrangement. Upon rehydration, the leaves resume an outwardly spreading posture. Its leaf narrows gradually from a broad ovate base to a lanceolate apex, the leaf apices are distinctly keeled and finely serrated, with entire margins that are either flat or slightly recurved. The species is dioicous, with separate male and female gametophytes [4, 5]. G. pilifera is often referred to as a “pioneer of nature” due to its remarkable ability to survive on exposed rock surfaces under intense solar radiation. Its physiological adaptability is particularly pronounced in habitats characterized by substantial fluctuations in water availability. Many moss species, exemplified by G. pilifera, can form diverse patches on rock surfaces. They are capable of secreting acidic compounds into rock crevices, gradually weathering the rock surface, facilitating soil formation, and accumulating nutrients. This process helps optimize the microecological environment, thereby laying a solid foundation for the establishment and growth of other vascular plants [6, 7]. As such, G. pilifera is always faced with unpredictable environmental conditions, and its survival mechanism must be special.

In recent years, the intensification of global climate change has been accompanied by an increasing frequency of extreme weather events [8, 9]. One of the most prominent anthropogenic signals of climate change is the rise in global mean surface temperature. Global extreme heat events not only become more frequent, but their intensity has also increased, with an estimated annual growth rate of 0.82 days/year in frequency and 0.023 ℃/year in intensity [10]. In response to high-temperature and arid environmental stress, mosses can maintain osmotic homeostasis by accumulating proline and soluble sugars. Meanwhile, they synthesize large amounts of antioxidant enzymes to effectively scavenge excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby protecting themselves from dehydration-induced damage. Different moss species exhibit varying responses to temperature fluctuations. Report has indicated that summer drought treatments result in a decrease in the cover of Rhytidiadelphus squarrosus and Calliergonella cuspidata, whereas the cover of Fissidens dubius increased. In contrast, winter warming led to an increase in the cover of Campyliadelphus chrysophyllus, while the cover of Rhytidiadelphus squarrosus and overall species richness decline [11]. Under high-temperature stress, both Plagiomnium acutum and Thuidium cymbifolium exhibit increased peroxidase (POD) activity [12] and cell membrane permeability [13] with rising temperatures. However, when the temperature exceeds a critical threshold (45–50 ℃) and the exposure duration surpasses a certain limit (4–6 h), POD activity begins to decline.

Although numerous studies have investigated the effects of temperature on mosses, most have primarily focused on the responses of mosses to extreme high or low temperature stress, while often overlooking a key environmental factor in natural ecosystems—diurnal temperature range (DTR). As a ubiquitous climatic phenomenon, DTR has been shown to significantly influence plant growth, development, and physiological metabolism [14–17]. Under conditions of adequate water availability (primarily referring to controlled environments involving agricultural and forestry crops) higher daytime temperatures generally enhance photosynthetic activity and promote the accumulation of photosynthates, whereas lower nighttime temperatures reduce respiratory losses, thereby facilitating dry matter retention and accumulation [18, 19]. Since the 1990 s, the global DTR has exhibited a widening trend, primarily driven by rising surface air temperatures and reflected in pronounced daytime warming. This daytime warming may also be partly attributable to enhanced solar radiation associated with reduced cloud cover in recent decades [20]. Such expansion in DTR may have wide-ranging ecological and physiological implications, potentially affecting crop yields, plant growth, animal welfare, and human health. Under natural conditions, an increased DTR can enhance carbon assimilation in mesophytic and hygrophytic species in humid regions. However, for xerophytic species in arid regions, elevated daytime temperatures exacerbate evapotranspiration, leading to rapid water loss and negatively impacting growth [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the effects of DTR variation on mosses remain largely unexplored. Although this topic has received limited attention, many researchers have emphasized the affinity of mosses for low temperatures while largely overlooking the potential impact of temperature fluctuations. Notably, several studies have suggested that the inhibition of nighttime respiration plays a key role in improving the carbon balance of bryophytes [23–26]. Moreover, mosses grown under oscillating temperature regimes exhibited higher relative growth rates (RGR) than those cultivated under constant conditions [27]. Overall, despite these preliminary insights, the physiological responses of mosses to DTR variation remain insufficiently studied.

To address this research gap, the present study employed a controlled indoor experiment to simulate two DTRs—normal (20/10 ℃) and elevated (30/10 ℃)—and investigated the physiological responses of G. pilifera during different stages of dehydration and rehydration. Key physiological traits examined included, but were not limited to, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (e.g., ABS/RC, DIo/RC, ETo/RC, φDo, Fv/Fm, Fv/Fo, PI abs), antioxidant enzyme activities (POD, SOD, CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA) content, proline (PRO) accumulation, andwater content (WC). This study aimed to address the following central questions: (1) How do the physiological traits of G. pilifera change during dehydration and rehydration under contrasting DTRs? (2) Do the correlations among multiple physiological traits of G. pilifera differ under different DTR treatments, and what do these differences reveal about its physiological adaptation strategies? Based on existing knowledge of plant responses to increasing DTR under natural conditions, we hypothesized that an expanded DTR (30/10 ℃) would exert negative effects on the physiological activity of the xerophytic moss G. pilifera, making it less capable of maintaining normal physiological function compared with the 20/10 ℃ condition. The outcomes of this study are expected to deepen our understanding of the physiological and biochemical responses of mosses to DTR fluctuations and offer novel insights and empirical evidence for assessing the ecological adaptability of mosses under global climate change.

Methods

Study area

The moss species used in this study was Grimmia pilifera, which belongs to the family Grimmiaceae and the genus Grimmia. The G. pilifera specimens used in the experiment were collected from the Bogda Mountain region in the eastern section of the Tianshan Mountains (Fig. 1). The plant material was formally identified by Dilihumar·Abudukeremu, and a voucher specimen has been deposited in the Herbarium of the Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences (voucher number: XJBI00177626). This area is located on the windward slope, influenced by westerly air masses (with moisture ultimately sourced from the Atlantic Ocean) and by Arctic air masses, resulting in a relatively humid climate [28]. The mean annual temperature is approximately 2.5 ℃, with January being the coldest month (average − 12.4 ℃) and July the warmest (average 15.9 ℃). Annual precipitation ranges from 400 to 700 mm. The altitude range of the area is 800–5400 m, and there are seven complete vertical natural zones: temperate desert zone, montane steppe zone, montane coniferous forest zone, subalpine meadow zone, alpine meadow zone, alpine cushion vegetation zone and ice and snow zone [28]. Due to its ecological diversity and representative value, the area has been designated as a UNESCO “Man and the Biosphere Reserve” and holds significant scientific importance as a representative site of the arid regions in Central Asia. G. pilifera is predominantly found on exposed rock surfaces at an elevation of approximately 1700 m within the Tianshan spruce (Picea schrenkiana) forest zone. It is one of the dominant moss species colonizing bare rock substrates in this region (Fig. 1).

Collection and processing of moss samples

To ensure consistency in the source and genetic background of experimental samples, patches of G. pilifera were collected from rock surfaces that were relatively flat, exhibited large, continuous populations, and displayed uniform coloration—verified by fully hydrating the mosses through spraying (Fig. 1). After rehydration, G. pilifera exhibits increased flexibility and reduced susceptibility to damage; therefore, samples were carefully detached from the rock surface using a blade after complete hydration. We collected 30 patches, each with a diameter of 10–15 cm, and combined them into a composite sample. Fresh samples were air-dried on A4 paper under natural light for three days, placed in paper envelopes, and stored for five days before experimental treatments.

To eliminate potential differences caused by the original habitat, artificial simulated patches were constructed [29] and placed in an artificial climate chamber for homogenized cultivation [30]. The cultivation conditions were set as follows: light intensity of 2400 lx, temperature of 20 °C/10°C (day/night), relative humidity of 80%, and a 12 h/12 h light–dark photoperiod. The mosses were continuously cultivated under these conditions for 7 days. The DTR reflects the typical springtime conditions at the sampling site during the active growth period of G. pilifera. During the cultivation period, an appropriate amount of distilled water was added daily, and the filter paper beneath the samples was replaced every 2–3 days to prevent microbial growth.

Experimental design

After 7 days of homogenized cultivation, two diurnal temperature regimes were established: 20 ℃/10 ℃ and 30 ℃/10 ℃ (day/night), with a 12 h/12 h light/dark photoperiod and relative humidity maintained at 80%. Notably, the 20 ℃/10 ℃ treatment corresponds to the typical DTR during the spring–summer growing season of mosses at the sampling site and was used as the control treatment, while the 30 ℃/10 ℃ treatment represents an expanded DTR simulating conditions under global warming, particularly elevated daytime temperatures. The same sample underwent repeated dehydration–rehydration cycles, with each cycle consisting of a 48-hour dehydration phase followed by a 24-hour rehydration phase. Sampling was conducted after each cycle, specifically on days 7, 14, and 21 following the onset of the treatments. For samples collected on day 7, the rehydration period lasted 6 days. After the first sampling, due to the 48-hour dehydration and 24-hour rehydration cycles, samples collected on days 14 and 21 were preceded by an additional 4-day rehydration period to ensure normal recovery (Tab. S1). The physiological state of samples collected before the first dehydration–rehydration treatment was defined as CK (control). For dehydration, the filter paper within the simulated moss patches was first removed, and any residual moisture was gently blotted. The samples were then transferred to another growth chamber with a relative humidity maintained at 50%, while all other environmental conditions remained unchanged, to initiate the dehydration phase.

Based on the commonly adopted sampling time points for dehydration–rehydration experiments in drought-tolerant mosses such as Syntrichia caninervis and Bryum argenteum [30, 31], four dehydration sampling time points were set for G. pilifera: 0 h, 2 h, 24 h, and 48 h. Immediately after dehydration sampling, rehydration treatments were initiated. Given the rapid water uptake of G. pilifera, three rehydration sampling time points were established: 2 min, 2 h, and 24 h. During rehydration, the relative humidity in the growth chamber was reset to 80%, filter paper was reinserted into the artificial moss patches, and 12 mL of water was added to maintain substrate moisture for 24 h. At each sampling point, water content (WC) and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured simultaneously. The remaining tissue samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at ultra-low temperatures for subsequent physiological analyses.

Determination of physiological and biochemical parameters

Measurement of water content (WC): Fresh-weighted G. pilifera samples were placed in an oven, initially dried at 105 °C for 15 min, and then further dried at 80 °C until a constant weight was reached. The dry mass was measured using an electronic analytical balance with a precision of 0.0001 g. Under environmental stress conditions such as drought, high temperature, and salinity-alkalinity, mosses tend to accumulate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to lipid peroxidation of cell membranes and oxidative damage. To cope with oxidative stress generated between cells under environmental stress, plants typically activate a range of antioxidant enzyme systems and accumulate osmotic adjustment substances to maintain intracellular redox balance [32]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD) are key antioxidant enzymes whose primary function is to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) within plant tissues. Oxidative stress is commonly assessed using malondialdehyde (MDA), which serves as an indicator of the extent of oxidative damage in plants. Osmotic adjustment substances such as proline (PRO) and soluble protein content (SP) play crucial roles in maintaining osmotic balance and stabilizing protein structures, thereby protecting plant cells under stress conditions. Superoxide dismutase (SOD, U/g) activity was determined using the WST-8 method. Catalase (CAT, U/g) activity was measured by a visible colorimetric method. Peroxidase (POD, ▲OD470/min/g), malondialdehyde (MDA, nmol/g), and proline (PRO, µg/g) contents were determined using visible spectrophotometry, while soluble protein content (SP, mg/g) was measured using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method. All enzyme activity assay kits used in this study were purchased from Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China, https://www.geruisi-bio.com/). All physiological indices were calculated based on the fresh weight (FW) of the samples.

Measurement of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters: Chlorophyll fluorescence kinetics were measured using a portable fluorometer (Handy-PEA, Hansatech Instruments, Norfolk, UK). The excitation light intensity was 3000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and fluorescence was recorded over a 2-second interval at a room temperature of approximately 25 ℃. Each treatment was replicated 10 times. Due to the requirement for at least 15 min of dark adaptation prior to measurement, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were not recorded at 0 min of dehydration or 2 min of rehydration. Additionally, plants dehydrated for 48 h exhibited relative water content below the detection threshold, and thus fluorescence measurements were not conducted [33]. Therefore, fluorescence measurements were conducted at four time points: 2 h and 24 h during dehydration, and 2 h and 24 h during rehydration (Tab. S1). The following parameters were measured in this study: ABS/RC: light energy absorbed per PSII reaction center. DIo/RC: energy dissipated as heat per PSII reaction center. ETo/RC: energy used for electron transport per PSII reaction center. φDo: quantum yield of energy dissipation. Fv/Fm: maximum photochemical efficiency. Fv/Fo: potential photochemical efficiency. PI abs: performance index on absorption basis. φ(Eo): quantum yield of electron transport beyond QA. Ψ(Eo): probability that a trapped exciton moves an electron into the electron transport chain beyond QA. REo/RC: energy transferred to the terminal electron acceptors per reaction center. TRo/RC: energy captured by PSII per reaction center.

Statistics

Data preprocessing was performed using Microsoft Excel 2016, and statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2). The R packages tidyr, dplyr, and ggplot2 were used to process the data and generate scatter plots illustrating the temporal changes in chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and water content of G. pilifera under different DTR treatments. The mean values of each trait from the untreated samples under both temperature regimes were used as controls (CK), and are represented in the figures as black dashed lines at the zero baseline. The effect size of the treatment on each variable was quantified by calculating the logarithm of the response ratio (RR) (Eq. 2). The variance (v) of each RR was calculated by Eq. (3). Effect values were expressed as 95% confidence intervals (CIs); an effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not overlap with the zero baseline (P < 0.05). Finally, independent-sample t-tests were conducted to assess the significance of trait differences between temperature treatments. Significant differences (P < 0.05) between the two treatments are indicated by asterisks (*) in the figures. To further elucidate the covariance relationships among physiological traits, Spearman correlation analysis was first conducted using the “psych” package in R to examine the associations among traits under both temperature treatments among different treatment periods (7, 14, and 21 days), as well as for the pooled data across the entire experimental period. This preliminary analysis was used to identify statistically significant trait associations. Subsequently, plant trait network (PTN) analysis was employed to explore the complex interrelationships and relative importance among multiple physiological traits [34]. Topological parameters of the PTN—degree, betweenness, clustering coefficient, edge density, and modularity—were used to assess differences in network structure [35]. Prior to constructing the PTNs, Spearman correlation coefficients (r) were calculated, and only significantly correlated trait pairs (P < 0.05) were retained to minimize the inclusion of spurious correlations [36, 37]. The node parameters of the PTN include: degree, which represents the number of edges connected to a given node; betweenness, defined as the number of shortest paths that pass through a specific node; clustering coefficient, which measures the probability that the neighbors of a given trait (node) are also connected to each other; and Closeness, calculated as the reciprocal of the average shortest path length between a given trait and all other traits in the network. The global parameters of the PTN include the following: edge density, which refers to the ratio of the actual number of edges in the network to the maximum possible number of edges; Diameter, defined as the longest shortest path between any two connected nodes in the network; Average path length, representing the mean value of the shortest paths between all pairs of trait nodes; Average clustering coefficient, calculated as the mean clustering coefficient of all nodes in the network; and modularity, which reflects the extent to which the network can be divided into distinct “subnetworks” or modules. A higher modularity value indicates tighter connections within modules and looser connections between modules. Finally, the PTN and its associated parameters were visualized using the “igraph” package in R 4.3.2. The definitions and ecological significance of all PTN parameters are provided in Tab. S2 (Supplementary Materials).

Results

Changes in chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of G. pilifera under different treatments

Under a dehydration-rehydration background, the chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of G. pilifera exhibited pronounced responses to DTR (Fig. 2). Fv/Fm and Fv/Fo showed significant declines at the critical dehydration time points (7D24h, 14D24h, and 21D24h), as indicated by t-tests (P < 0.05). indicating that PSII activity was inhibited. Moreover, the values decreased progressively with increasing treatment duration (from day 7 to day 21). After 24 h of rehydration, PI abs under the 20/10 ℃ treatment recovered to 76.93%–84.59% of the pre-treatment level, while under the 30/10℃ treatment it recovered to 75.42%–91.43%. For other parameters, the values measured after 24 h of rehydration differed by less than 20% from their pre-treatment levels under both temperature regimes, with greater recovery observed under the 30/10℃ treatment (Tab. S3). The energy dissipation parameter ABS/RC increased significantly during dehydration, with a more pronounced rise under the 30/10℃ treatment. DIo/RC also increased concurrently, indicating that a larger portion of the absorbed energy was dissipated rather than used for electron transport, reflecting enhanced non-photochemical quenching. In contrast, parameters associated with electron transport efficiency (e.g., ETo/RC, REo/RC) decreased during dehydration but increased after rehydration, suggesting that electron flow from PSII to PSI was inhibited under stress but partially restored upon rehydration. During dehydration, the φDo value of plants under the 20/10℃ treatment was significantly lower than that under the 30/10℃ treatment, but it became significantly higher during rehydration (P < 0.05; Fig. 2a). This further suggests the involvement of potential energy dissipation mechanisms. In summary, compared with the 20/10℃ treatment, plants under the 30/10℃ treatment exhibited stronger photosynthetic inhibition during dehydration but showed greater recovery capacity after rehydration.

Effects of different treatments on thewater content (WC) of G. pilifera

Water content (WC) is a key indicator reflecting plant hydration status. Throughout the entire dehydration–rehydration process, the WC of G. pilifera remained consistently lower than the pre-treatment level (CK) (Fig. 3). With increasing treatment duration and number of dehydration–rehydration cycles, the reduction in RWC became more pronounced at D24h and D48h, while the difference between the two temperature regimes increased from 7 d to 14 d but was not significant at 21D24h or 21D48h (P ≥ 0.05; Fig. 3,S3). On day 7, all dehydration and rehydration time points showed significant differences in WC between the 20/10 ℃ and 30/10 ℃ treatments (P < 0.05). On day 14, WC differed significantly at all time points except R2min. On day 21, significant differences were observed at D0h, D24h, D48h, and R2h (P < 0.05). Clearly, although WC showed partial recovery after rehydration on days 7, 14, and 21 under both temperature regimes, it never returned to the baseline level observed in the untreated control (Fig. S1). Under the 30/10°C treatment, the highest water content was observed on day 7 after 24 h of rehydration, recovering to 92.83% of the control level. In comparison, under the 20/10°C treatment, water content peaked on day 7 after 24 h of rehydration at 94.18% of the control level. Moreover, the recovery of WC was consistently lower under the 30/10 ℃ treatment.

Effects of different treatments on malondialdehyde (MDA) content and antioxidant enzyme activities in G. pilifera

MDA is the final product of lipid peroxidation and serves as an indicator of oxidative damage to plant cell membranes. In this study, MDA content decreased under both temperature treatments in most treatments, especially after 14 d and 21 d (Fig. 4a). On day 7, the MDA content differed significantly (P < 0.05) between the 20/10°C and 30/10°C treatments during both the dehydration and rehydration phases, but with inconsistent directions. On day 14, no significant difference was found between treatments at D2h and R24h, while on day 21, significant differences emerged at R2min and R2h. By day 21, the negative effect of the 30/10 ℃ treatment on MDA accumulation was markedly greater than that of the 20/10 ℃ treatment. CAT activity was significantly reduced under both treatments (Fig. 4b). As the duration of treatment increased (from 7 to 14 to 21 days), CAT activity gradually declined at corresponding dehydration–rehydration time points. The decline was more pronounced under the 30/10 ℃ treatment. Significant differences in CAT activity between the two treatments were observed at 7D2h, 7D24h, 7R24h, 14D0h, 14D2h, 14D48h, 14R2min, 14R24h, and 21D0h (P < 0.05).

Under the 20/10 ℃ treatment, significantly higher peroxidase (POD) activity compared with the initial state was observed only at 7D24h, 7D48h, 7R2min, and 7R24h (Fig. 4c). In contrast, POD activity at 14R2min, 14R2h, and 14R24h was significantly lower than the baseline level. At all other time points, the error bars overlapped with the zero baseline, indicating no significant difference from the initial state (P > 0.05). Under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, POD activity was significantly lower than the initial level at 21D48h, 21R2min, 21R2h, and 21R24h, while no significant differences were observed at 14R2h, 14D0h, 7R24h, and 7D2h. At all other time points, POD activity was significantly elevated compared with the baseline. Between the DTR treatments, significant differences in POD activity were detected at most time points (P < 0.05), except for 7R2h, 7R24h, 14R2min, and 21D24h. Overall, POD activity tended to be higher under the 30/10 ℃ treatment than under the 20/10 ℃ treatment. The increase in POD activity likely contributes to enhanced scavenging of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress-induced damage.

Under the 20/10 ℃ treatment, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was significantly higher than the initial level only at 7D2h (Fig. 4d). At 7R2min, 21D0h, 21D24h, 21D48h, and 21R2min, there were no significant differences in SOD activity compared with the baseline. At all other time points, SOD activity was significantly lower than the initial state. Under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, SOD activity was significantly higher than the initial state only at 7D2h and 21D48h, while significantly lower activity was observed at 7D24h, 7D48h, 14D48h, 14R2min, 14R2h, and 21D24h. No significant differences were found at the remaining time points. When comparing the treatments, no significant differences in SOD activity were observed at D48h and R24h on day 7, and at R2min on day 14. On day 21, significant differences between treatments were found only at D48h and R24h (P < 0.05).

Differences in the physiological trait network structure of G. pilifera

Plant trait networks (PTNs) were constructed for each of the three cultivation stages under both temperature treatments (Fig. S3). Under the 20/10 ℃ treatment, the number of edges on days 7, 14, and 21 was 63, 68, and 69, respectively; under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, the corresponding values were 70, 79, and 54 (Fig. S4d). Both treatments exhibited a similar trend in network topology: average path length and diameter first decreased and then increased over time, whereas edge density showed the opposite pattern (Fig. S4a, b,e). For the 20/10 ℃ treatment, modularity decreased linearly with cultivation time, whereas under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, modularity initially declined and then increased. Overall, comparison of the overall parameters of PTNs between the 20/10℃ and 30/10℃ treatments across different time points (Fig. S4) showed that the 20/10℃ networks exhibited higher total edge number, edge density, and clustering coefficient than those under the 30/10℃ treatment. In contrast, Average path length and Diameter were consistently higher under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, suggesting a lower degree of network stability (Fig. S4b, e).

Comparison of the node parameters of trait networks between the 20/10℃ and 30/10℃ treatments across different time points (Figs. S5, S6) showed that under the 20/10℃ treatment, WC was the trait with the highest degree on day 7 (Fig. S5d). On day 14, WC, φDo, Fv/Fm, φ(Eo), PI abs all exhibited the highest degree values, with PI abs showing a notably higher betweenness centrality (Fig. S5e, S6e). On day 21, ETo/RC was identified as the trait with the highest degree (Fig. S5f). In PTN analysis, traits with both high degree and high betweenness are considered central traits. Therefore, the central traits under the 20/10 ℃ treatment were WC on day 7, PI abs on day 14, and ETo/RC on day 21—indicating a transition in regulatory focus from water retention to photosynthetic adjustment over time (Fig. S3). Similarly, under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, φEo and PI abs were the traits with the highest degrees on day 7, with PI abs having a higher betweenness centrality(Fig. S5a, S6a). On day 14, SP content had the highest degree, while on day 21, PRO content showed both the highest degree and betweenness, making it the central trait. Thus, the central traits under the 30/10 ℃ treatment were PI abs (day 7), SP content (day 14), and PRO content (day 21), suggesting a temporal shift from photosynthetic regulation to osmotic physiological adjustment (Fig. S3). These results indicate that the core regulatory traits of G. pilifera diverged over time under different treatments, likely reflecting intrinsic physiological responses to the different DTR treatments.

Pooled analysis of data across all dehydration–rehydration time points under both treatments revealed that PI abs and Fv/Fo had the highest degrees in the overall trait network of the 20/10 ℃ treatment. However, Fv/Fo exhibited greater betweenness centrality, indicating that it served as the central trait within the overall network structure under this treatment (Fig. 7). In contrast, PI abs had the highest degree in the overall network under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, making it the central trait in that condition. Under the 20/10°C treatment, physiological traits were divided into two modules: one consisting of photosynthetic traits, and the other comprising seven traits, including water content, osmotic regulators, and enzyme activities (Fig. 7b). In comparison, the trait network under the 30/10 ℃ treatment was divided into three modules (Fig. 7a). Although water content, enzyme activities, and osmoregulatory substances remained grouped into a single module, photosynthetic traits were split into two distinct modules, suggesting looser interconnections among them. These results indicated that although photosynthetic traits were the central traits under both temperature regimes, the overall trait network topology and node-level parameters differed markedly. This suggests the presence of divergent patterns of functional trait covariance in G. pilifera in response to varying DTRs.

Discussion

Photosynthetic responses of G. pilifera under different DTR treatments

Photosynthesis is fundamental to plant survival, and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters serve as intrinsic indicators reflecting the physiological state of photosynthetic apparatus. These parameters are widely regarded as key physiological indicators for studying plant responses to temperature fluctuations [38], and are especially important in research on moss responses to environmental stress [31]. Photosystem II (PSII) is among the most thermally sensitive components of the photosynthetic machinery [38]. Under heat stress, both the water-splitting reaction on the donor side of PSII and the downstream carbon assimilation processes are suppressed [38]. Elevated temperatures can impair PSII function by reducing electron transport rates and the formation of assimilatory power, thereby inhibiting overall photosynthetic performance [39]. In this study, during the dehydration phase, G. pilifera under the 20/10℃ treatment exhibited higher maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm), higher potential photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fo), and lower quantum yield for energy dissipation (φDo) compared with the 30/10℃ treatment, which was consistent with the higher WC maintained under 20/10℃. The lower WC in the 30/10℃ treatment, likely due to reduced humidity around the mosses at higher temperature, may partly explain their greater desiccation and enhanced energy dissipation during dehydration. However, during rehydration, the opposite pattern was observed: Fv/Fm and Fv/Fo were lower, and φDo was higher under the 20/10 ℃ treatment, despite the fact that WC was higher in 20/10℃ than in 30/10℃ under well-watered conditions. This paradoxical result, that plants under the 20/10℃ regime maintained higher WC but exhibited lower photochemical efficiency (lower Fv/Fm and Fv/Fo and higher φDo) compared to those under 30/10℃, suggests that water status alone does not determine photosynthetic recovery during rehydration. One possible explanation is that under the relatively mild thermal regime (20/10℃), the maintenance of higher tissue hydration might be associated with metabolic or structural constraints in the photosynthetic apparatus, leading to sustained photoinhibition and enhanced energy dissipation. By contrast, plants under the more stressful 30/10℃ regime may have developed more effective photoprotective adjustments (e.g., non-photochemical quenching or antioxidant defenses) that allowed faster recovery of photochemical efficiency upon rehydration. This finding highlights that differential temperature regimes can decouple plant water status from photosynthetic performance, revealing a critical trade-off between water retention and the efficiency of energy utilization under fluctuating water availability. As dehydration progressed, both Fv/Fm and Fv/Fo declined under both temperature regimes, suggesting that oxygen-evolving complex (OEC) activity was inhibited under stress, thereby reducing the efficiency of electron transport on the acceptor side of PSII. According to Demmig [40], such a decline in Fv/Fm indicates a loss of activity in PSII reaction centers. Specific energy flux parameters provide a more precise assessment of the photosynthetic apparatus in terms of light absorption, energy conversion, and dissipation [41]. Previous studies have shown that repeated drought stress can lead to reductions in RC/CSo, ABS/CSo, and ETo/CSo in desert plants, ultimately lowering the energy available for electron transport [42]. In the current study, after 24 h of dehydration on days 7 and 21, DIo/RC, ABS/RC, and TRo/RC were significantly higher under the 30/10 ℃ treatment than under 20/10 ℃. With longer dehydration time, φDo, DIo/RC, and ABS/RC increased at 24 h compared with 2 h in both treatments, indicating increased energy dissipation and reduced energy allocated to electron transport. In both temperature treatments, Fv/Fm and the performance index (PI abs) declined synchronously during dehydration and recovered upon rehydration, which is consistent with the findings reported by Li [43].

Photoprotective mechanisms employed by plants under stress conditions primarily include reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging [44], thermal dissipation of excess excitation energy [45], and regulation of photosynthetic electron transport [46]. In both temperature treatments, the quantum yield for energy dissipation (φDo) in G. pilifera was consistently higher during dehydration than during rehydration, and φDo increased progressively with longer dehydration durations. During dehydration, more of the absorbed light energy in the PSII reaction centers of plants under the 30/10 ℃ treatment was dissipated as heat, resulting in a reduced portion available for electron transport, ultimately reflected in lower photochemical efficiency compared with the 20/10 ℃ treatment. However, during rehydration, plants under the 30/10 ℃ treatment unexpectedly exhibited higher photochemical efficiency than those under 20/10 ℃. This observation may instead reflect a shift in the temperature optimum of the photosynthetic apparatus. In fact, higher photochemical efficiency under 30/10℃ than 20/10℃ during rehydration may be explained by an optimum for photosynthetic electron transport closer to 30℃ than 20℃. Similar patterns have been reported even in Antarctic mosses, where electron transport rates often peak around 25℃ and can remain higher at 30℃ than at 20℃ [24]. Nonetheless, several studies have suggested that photoinhibition does not necessarily imply permanent damage to the photosynthetic system; rather, it may reflect the activation of thermal energy dissipation as a protective response [5]. For instance, S. caninervis, a desiccation-tolerant moss from arid regions, is capable of recovering fluorescence activity and reaching a physiological balance after prolonged desiccation [47, 48]. Similarly, B. argenteum shows a significant decline in photosynthetic rate with increasing dehydration rate, but full recovery is observed after rehydration [30, 31], which is consistent with the results of the present study. During dehydration, as water content decreases in mosses, the rate of respiration declines more slowly than the rate of photosynthesis, leading to a shift from positive to negative net photosynthesis, which progressively approaches zero with increasing desiccation severity [49]. Previous research has shown that in G. pilifera, the recovery of ETo/ABS, ETo/TRo, and RC/CSo after dehydration–rehydration is comparable to that of Fv/Fm, indicating that the maximum light-harvesting efficiency, effective photochemical capacity, electron transport chain activity on the acceptor side of PSII, and the integrity of the PSII reaction centers all recover rapidly and effectively [33]. In the present study, after rehydration on days 7, 14, and 21, the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) in both temperature treatments recovered to over 90% of the baseline level, suggesting that while increased DTR did affect the photosynthetic performance of G. pilifera, the damage caused by dehydration–rehydration cycles over a short-term period was largely reversible. However, as global climate change intensifies, the long-term physiological responses and adaptability of mosses under persistently altered temperature regimes remain to be further investigated. Moreover, simultaneous measurements of PSI and PSII are essential for accurately inferring the FLV-mediated electron transport pathway. To further investigate the specific electron transport routes in mosses, it is necessary to integrate synchronous assessments of PSI and PSII, enabling a comprehensive elucidation of their photoelectron transport characteristics and the underlying response mechanisms to environmental changes, such as diurnal temperature range (DTR).

Effects of different DTR treatments on MDA content, antioxidant enzyme activities, and osmoregulatory substances in G. pilifera

The cell membrane serves as a critical interface between plant cells and the external environment, and its structural integrity is essential for maintaining normal physiological function. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is the final product of lipid peroxidation under stress conditions and is widely used as an indicator of membrane damage. Elevated MDA levels reflect increased oxidative stress and decreased antioxidant enzyme activity, which in turn aggravate membrane damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [50, 51]. Thus, MDA content is commonly used to assess the degree of stress experienced by plants. A negative correlation has been reported between a plant’s stress tolerance and its MDA molar concentration—lower MDA levels generally indicate less cellular injury [52]. However, different moss species exhibit varying physiological responses to dehydration, heat, and drought due to their ecological adaptations. For instance, MDA content in the terricolous moss Didymodon vinealis increases significantly with drought severity, whereas in B. argenteum it tends to decrease [53]. Similarly, MDA content in Brachythecium pulchellum and Plagiomnium cuspidatum has been shown to decline under water stress [54]. In Homomallium simlaense, MDA levels decrease progressively under prolonged drought without rehydration but rise significantly when water stress reaches a critical level [55]. In the present study, MDA content in G. pilifera decreased significantly with increasing dehydration intensity and frequency. After rehydration on day 7, MDA levels approached those of the control group. However, on days 14 and 21, MDA levels failed to recover to the same extent, even though water content had largely returned to normal, indicating that oxidative stress was not solely dependent on water status. Notably, MDA levels in the treatments remained consistently lower than in CK, suggesting that CK plants may have retained a higher stress load, possibly reflecting a physiological state closer to natural field conditions, whereas repeated cycles in the treatment groups may have triggered acclimation responses that mitigated oxidative damage. These results indicate that G. pilifera developed stronger membrane-level antioxidant protection under repeated dehydration–rehydration cycles at 30/10℃ compared with 20/10℃.

The antioxidant defense system plays a critical role in plant stress responses. Under stress conditions, the activities of key antioxidant enzymes—including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD)—are often upregulated to eliminate excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigate oxidative damage. Previous studies have shown that in Hypnum plumaeforme and Pogonatum inflexum, SOD activity decreased under low-temperature stress, while MDA levels remained relatively stable, suggesting the involvement of non-enzymatic antioxidant mechanisms or enhanced ROS tolerance [56]. In contrast, S. caninervis exhibited significantly increased MDA content and SOD, POD, and CAT activities with decreasing snow cover and increasing freeze–thaw cycle frequency [57], highlighting the importance of antioxidant enzymes in coping with intensified abiotic stress. In the present study, G. pilifera exhibited enhanced POD activity under the 30/10℃ treatment, likely to counteract excess ROS generated under the higher DTR. This is coherent with the higher quantum efficiency observed at 30/10℃, since greater light utilization requires a more active metabolism to consume the produced NADPH and ATP, which also increases the risk of oversaturation of the electron transport chain and ROS production [58]. However, SOD activity did not show a substantial increase, and its response was relatively stable under the 30/10 ℃ treatment. Similarly, POD and SOD activities under 20/10 ℃ remained largely unchanged. CAT activity, in contrast, declined under both temperature treatments, although the degree of reduction varied, suggesting differential regulation of antioxidant enzyme systems in response to diurnal temperature fluctuation. Although antioxidant enzyme activities underwent notable changes as part of the physiological response, their structural position within the overall trait coordination network was relatively peripheral. This may suggest that these enzymes exert a more localized regulatory role, rather than functioning as central hubs driving coordinated trait responses under stress.

Plants can adapt to environmental stress by regulating the accumulation of osmoregulatory substances such as free proline (PRO) and soluble proteins (SP). PRO, as a key water-soluble non-enzymatic antioxidant, not only participates in osmotic adjustment but also plays an important role in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plant cells [59]. In vascular plants, PRO levels can increase several-fold under abiotic stress [60]. Kaul reported that heavy metal stress rapidly induces proline accumulation [61]. However, in G. pilifera, PRO content was found to first increase and then decrease with rising Pb concentrations. In Taxiphyllum aomoriense, drought stress did not significantly alter proline content, but led to a marked accumulation of soluble sugars [62]. In S. caninervis, free PRO increased significantly under conditions of reduced snow cover and fewer freeze–thaw cycles [57]. In H. simlaense, PRO levels remained relatively stable at low WC but gradually increased with increasing tissue hydration. In the present study, the PRO content of G. pilifera increased significantly with dehydration duration on day 7, but returned to baseline levels after rehydration. By contrast, after dehydration–rehydration cycles on days 14 and 21, PRO levels remained significantly higher than the initial control values, with lower recovery observed on day 21 compared with day 14. Furthermore, the fluctuations in PRO content were more stable under the 20/10 ℃ treatment than under the 30/10 ℃ treatment.

Soluble proteins (SP) are also important regulatory compounds in plants, and their content is often used as an indicator of metabolic activity—higher levels typically reflect more vigorous metabolism. Studies have shown that as stress severity increases, the plant’s ability to synthesize proteins declines, and regulatory mechanisms become impaired, leading to a reduction in metabolic activity. However, in Racomitrium japonicum, SP content was reported to increase as tissue water content decreased [63]. In contrast, H. simlaense exhibited lower SP content under conditions of low water availability [55]. In the present study, SP content in G. pilifera was significantly higher than the control on day 7 under both temperature treatments. However, with prolonged exposure and repeated dehydration–rehydration cycles, SP content decreased significantly on days 14 and 21, falling below control levels. Significant differences were observed between the two temperature treatments during this period, although by 21DR24h, no significant difference was detected between them. The decline in SP content under stress is generally attributed to two main factors: the denaturation and degradation of existing proteins, and the inhibition of new protein synthesis [64]. However, the specific mechanisms responsible for the SP decrease observed in this study remain unclear. Further investigations using proteomic or transcriptomic approaches may help elucidate the underlying causes. On day 7, the increase in SP content under both temperature regimes likely contributed to enhanced osmotic adjustment and cellular metabolic maintenance. However, by day 14, the pattern of SP decline became irregular, possibly reflecting a physiological re-adjustment phase. By day 21, the trend appeared to stabilize, which may indicate a weakening of regulatory mechanisms and a general reduction in metabolic activity. Repeated dehydration–rehydration cycles under both temperature treatments may have impaired cellular structure in G. pilifera, limiting its ability to synthesize or accumulate osmoregulatory substances such as SP, thereby resulting in reduced SP levels.

Plants exhibit a coordinated interplay of multiple defense mechanisms when subjected to abiotic stress [13]. Among these mechanisms, soluble proteins (SP) and proline (PRO), as osmotic adjustment substances, can enhance cellular resistance to dehydration and rehydration by regulating membrane systems and protein structures, as well as stabilizing organelle function. Meanwhile, abiotic stress often induces the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage to cells. To mitigate this, plants typically regulate the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT to scavenge ROS. Our study revealed a significant positive correlation between SP/PRO levels and antioxidant enzyme activities, indicating a coordinated response between osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defense mechanisms under stress conditions [55]. Such responses commonly occur under various environmental stress conditions, including drought, temperature extremes, and salinity-alkalinity stress, reflecting the plant’s strategy of “multi-pathway coordination” to mitigate the multifaceted damage to cellular structures and metabolic systems caused by environmental stressors. Previous studies have also suggested that osmotic adjustment substances and the ROS-scavenging system may be regulated by common stress signaling pathways, such as abscisic acid (ABA) or calcium (Ca2+) signals, leading to coordinated upregulation at the transcriptional or translational level [57]. Therefore, the positive correlation between SP/PRO levels and antioxidant enzyme activities not only reveals the adaptive strategies employed by plants under stress conditions, but also suggests that the temperature treatments in this study may have triggered a compound stress response mechanism. This finding is of great significance for advancing our understanding of the stress-resistance physiology in mosses. A limitation of this study is that all biochemical traits (e.g., MDA, SP, and PRO) were expressed on a fresh-weight (FW) basis, which may be affected by changes in water content. Although FW is a widely adopted normalization index and, particularly for mosses, captures their integrated response to concurrent water regulation and metabolic adjustments—as also reflected in the observed negative correlations between these metabolites and water content—this approach does not fully disentangle the effects of water loss from those of metabolic induction. Therefore, we emphasize that future studies should, whenever feasible, incorporate both FW- and dry-weight (DW)-based normalization to provide more robust and generalizable conclusions.

Effects of DTR on the structure of plant trait networks

Plants adapt to environmental changes by modifying both individual functional traits and the interactions among them. Plant trait network (PTN) analysis provides an effective approach to explore inter-trait relationships, enhancing our understanding of how coordinated trait interactions regulate plant growth and physiological processes under environmental stress [34, 36, 65]. Furthermore, PTNs offer novel perspectives for predicting plant adaptive strategies under future climate change scenarios [66]. In this study, under harsher environmental conditions—specifically, after 21 days of dehydration–rehydration under the 30/10 ℃ treatment—the trait network of G. pilifera exhibited higher modularity and lower edge density. This pattern is consistent with previous findings on woody plants at the global scale, where species in more stressful environments tend to exhibit more modular but less connected trait networks [67]. Diameter and Average path length reflect the degree of connectivity among trait clusters. In our results, both temperature treatments showed a dynamic trend in edge density, path length, network diameter, and clustering coefficient: a decline followed by an increase over the 21-day cultivation period. Notably, on day 14, inter-trait connectivity weakened significantly under both treatments, possibly reflecting a short-term compensatory response (e.g., increased edge density and more compact network structure) to transient stress. However, by day 21, cumulative stress effects became more pronounced—especially under the 30/10 ℃ regime—leading to a marked decline in network function. These results suggest that the expanded DTR negatively impacted the stability and coordination of the physiological trait network in G. pilifera. Trait networks can be divided into functional modules, and modularity quantifies the extent of separation between them [68]. High modularity indicates strong internal connectivity within modules and weak interactions between them. Networks with high modularity tend to organize traits into distinct functional groups or “dimensions,” which execute specialized functions [69]. For example, PTN analysis of 18 physiological and morphological traits in three submerged aquatic plants revealed that highly connected traits were primarily related to biomass allocation (e.g., total biomass, stem mass ratio, leaf mass ratio, and ramet number), while phenotypic traits (e.g., plant height, root length) and energy storage traits (e.g., stem starch) exhibited weaker associations [70]. In the present study, the modularity of both temperature treatments followed a similar trend on days 7 and 14. However, by day 21, divergent patterns emerged: modularity continued to decline under the 20/10 ℃ treatment but peaked under the 30/10 ℃ treatment. This suggests that, under the higher temperature regime, physiological traits of G. pilifera became more distinctly grouped into functional modules. Traits within the same module—such as those related to osmotic regulation and MDA accumulation—may interact via changes in antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby influencing broader inter-trait relationships and maintaining basic physiological function. Nevertheless, in the final overall network structure, traits under the 20/10 ℃ treatment were grouped into two major modules, while traits under the 30/10 ℃ treatment formed three distinct modules. The increase in modularity under the 30/10 ℃ regime indicates a stronger negative impact, potentially reflecting disrupted trait coordination and network fragmentation. Similar modularization has been observed in a short-lived desert annual in response to nitrogen and phosphorus addition, where trait network reorganization significantly altered multiple functional traits and trait associations [71]. In summary, the 30/10 ℃ treatment may have constrained the integrative interactions among traits in G. pilifera, thereby reducing the overall stability and coordination of its physiological trait network. These results clearly demonstrate that PTN-derived parameters differed between the two temperature regimes, with the 20/10℃ networks consistently exhibiting greater structural stability than those under 30/10℃ (Figs. S4–S5, Fig. 7). This observation is consistent with ecosystem-scale evidence showing that PTN structure shifts along environmental gradients such as increasing aridity [72]. By extending this framework to an experimental setting, our analysis highlights that, even within a single species, PTN parameters provide valuable insights into how plants adjust trait coordination to cope with contrasting environmental conditions.

Another key function of plant trait networks is the identification of core traits—traits that are most highly connected with others (i.e., have the highest degree) within the network. For instance, a trait network constructed from 23 traits across 2,530 individuals of 126 plant species at 381 sites in northwestern Europe revealed that biomass allocation traits and stem specific length were the core traits in perennial herbaceous plants [36]. In our study, the overall core trait under the 20/10 ℃ treatment was Fv/Fo, while under the 30/10 ℃ treatment it was PI abs, both of which are photosynthetic traits. This indicates that photosynthetic recovery plays a pivotal role in the reactivation of G. pilifera following dehydration stress. Photosynthesis underpins plant growth, and its products (such as glucose and starch) serve as the material basis for plant development, reproduction, secondary metabolism, and stress responses. PSII, as the core component of the photosynthetic system, is highly sensitive to environmental fluctuations. Disruption of the PSII electron transport chain can impair photosynthetic performance, which in turn affects other physiological traits such as soluble protein levels, osmoregulatory substances, and antioxidant enzyme activities. Interestingly, the core traits varied across time points. Under the 20/10 ℃ treatment, the core trait wasWC on day 7, but shifted to photosynthetic traits PI abs and ETo/RC on days 14 and 21, respectively. In contrast, under the 30/10 ℃ treatment, PI abs was the core trait on day 7, while SP and PRO contents (both osmoregulatory substances) became core traits on days 14 and 21. Similar patterns have been reported in other species. For example, Qiu found that the core traits within annual branch networks of Malus sieversii varied across years [73]. After nitrogen and phosphorus addition, stem traits of short branches became highly connected and functioned as core traits [74]. Moreover, M. sieversii seedlings were shown to adopt different combinations of functional traits in response to nutrient addition [75]. These shifts in core traits further suggest that G. pilifera adjusts multiple physiological traits in a coordinated manner to cope with changing DTRs. Among these, the adjustment of the photosynthetic system emerges as a particularly important adaptive strategy.

Conclusions

This study used a controlled indoor experiment to systematically compare the physiological responses of G. pilifera to different DTRs during dehydration–rehydration cycles. The results revealed that, compared to the 20/10°C treatment, plants under the 30/10°C treatment exhibited more pronounced inhibition during the dehydration phase but demonstrated a stronger recovery capacity thereafter. Under the 30/10 ℃ regime, more light energy absorbed by the PSII reaction center was dissipated as heat, while the opposite trend occurred during rehydration. Compared with the 20/10 ℃ treatment, the 30/10 ℃ treatment resulted in lower WC and weaker recovery, stronger negative effects in MDA accumulation, lower antioxidant enzyme activity (particularly CAT), and higher accumulation of osmoregulatory substances (PRO and SP). PTN analysis showed that the network under the 20/10 ℃ treatment exhibited greater structural stability than that under 30/10 ℃. The coordination among functional traits of G. pilifera varied significantly between treatments, indicating the need for integrated regulation involving multiple traits in response to DTR variation. Among these, modulation of the photosynthetic system emerged as the key regulatory mechanism. The findings indicate that an expanded DTR from 20 to 30℃ does not necessarily exert a considerable negative impact on the physiological activity of mosses—non-vascular and morphologically diminutive plants—at least when night temperatures remain at 10℃. Instead, mosses under the 30/10℃ regime exhibited relatively higher photochemical efficiency and consistently lower MDA levels, suggesting that moderate daytime warming may even facilitate physiological adjustment under fluctuating water availability. Nevertheless, G. pilifera may compensate for such environmental stress through increased modularity in its trait network, thereby enhancing the functional performance of specific trait groups. This study provides novel insight into how mosses physiologically adjust to cope with climate change and highlights the importance of trait-based network analysis in understanding plant adaptation strategies. Photosynthesis is one of the most fundamental life processes in plants, and the protection of the photosynthetic apparatus becomes particularly critical under stress conditions. The accumulation of osmotic adjustment substances helps maintain cell turgor, stabilize proteins and membranes, and simultaneously promotes the activity of antioxidant enzymes, facilitating the scavenging of excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) and suppressing the accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA) from lipid peroxidation. These coordinated responses collectively support the maintenance of normal physiological functions.
