Effects of Cedirogant on the Pharmacokinetics of Sensitive Cytochrome P450 Probe Substrates
Authors: Ronilda D'Cunha, Yuli Qian, Shuai Hao, Jason Eccleston, Gweneth F Levy, Shashikanth Gannu, David G Rizzo, Wei Liu, Mohamed‐Eslam F Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to characterize the effects of cedirogant, an inverse agonist of retinoic acid–related orphan receptor gamma thymus (RORγt), on activities of cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes in healthy adults. Twenty study participants received a single oral dose of the modified Cooperstown 5+1 cocktail CYP probe drugs alone without cedirogant (Period 1, midazolam on Day 1 and other CYP substrates on Day 2), and again following once‐daily dosing of 375 mg cedirogant (Period 2, cedirogant on Days 1‐18, midazolam on Day 13, and other CYP substrates on Day 14). Blood samples and 12‐hour urine samples were collected to measure the exposures of the CYP probe drugs and selected metabolites. The point estimates (90% confidence intervals) of the area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUCinf) ratio with and without cedirogant co‐administration were 0.370 (0.325‐0.420) for midazolam (CYP3A), 1.161 (1.031‐1.307) for caffeine (CYP1A2), and 0.788 (0.761‐0.816) for S‐Warfarin (CYP2C9). The estimated ratios of plasma metabolite/parent AUC ratio for omeprazole (CYP2C19) and parent/metabolite molar urine recovery ratio for dextromethorphan (CYP2D6) with versus without cedirogant co‐administration were 2.115 (1.813‐2.467) and 0.951 (0.802‐1.128), respectively. Based on these findings, once‐daily administration of 375‐mg cedirogant showed moderate induction of CYP3A and CYP2C19, a weak effect on CYP2C9, and no clinically relevant effects on CYP1A2 or CYP2D6.Keywords: cedirogant, cytochrome P450, drug–drug interactions, pharmacokinetics

T helper 17 (Th17) cells are a subset of T helper cells that produce several pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐17. 1 The retinoic acid–related orphan receptor gamma, thymus (RORγt) is a key transcription factor that is responsible for IL‐17 synthesis in vivo and is the master regulator of Th17 cell lineage. 2 Thus, RORγt is a key regulator of the adaptive immune system and a potential therapeutic target for several autoimmune chronic inflammatory diseases, including psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn's disease, and multiple sclerosis. 2

Cedirogant (ABBV‐157) is an orally bioavailable inverse agonist of RORγt that has been investigated for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe active chronic plaque psoriasis. 3 The chemical structure of cedirogant is provided in Figure S1. Cedirogant pharmacokinetics have been characterized in healthy participants and patients with psoriasis, 4 is minimally impacted by food consumption, 4 and is generally consistent between Western and Asian populations. 5 Following administration of a single dose of cedirogant under fasting conditions, the maximum cedirogant plasma concentration (Cmax) was achieved within a median time of 2‐4 h, and the estimated harmonic mean elimination half‐life was approximately 16‐23 h. 4 With repeated administration of cedirogant, steady‐state exposures were less than dose proportional for dosing of 75‐225 mg once daily. However, steady‐state exposures were approximately dose proportional for dosing of 225‐375 mg once daily. 4

It has been postulated that the observed dose disproportionality of cedirogant pharmacokinetics during steady‐state may be attributed to auto‐induction of cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A, the major CYP isoform responsible for cedirogant metabolism. 4 In vitro studies demonstrated that cedirogant induced CYP3A and inhibited CYP3A as well as several other CYP enzymes (unpublished data). The impact of cedirogant on the expression and activities of multiple CYP enzymes in humans has not been previously evaluated, but is important for informing the potential for drug–drug interactions and guiding the use of concomitant medications in future clinical trials.

In this study, we aim to characterize the effects of repeated cedirogant dosing on the pharmacokinetics of selected probe substrates for CYP3A (midazolam), CYP1A2 (caffeine), CYP2C9 (warfarin), CYP2C19 (omeprazole), and CYP2D6 (dextromethorphan) using the modified Cooperstown 5 + 1 cocktail, 6 , 7 , 8 a commonly used CYP cocktail approach, in healthy adults.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation guidelines, applicable regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki. The Advarra Institutional Review Board (Columbia, MD) approved the study protocol, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials before patient enrollment. All patients provided written informed consent before screening.

Study Design and Participants

This was a Phase 1, single‐center, multiple‐dose, open‐label, two‐period single‐arm study designed to examine the effects of repeated cedirogant dosing on co‐administered sensitive probes for different CYP enzymes (modified Cooperstown 5 + 1 cocktail) in healthy participants (N = 20). The study design is fully illustrated in Figure 1. Briefly, the study comprised two periods. Study participants received a single dose of 5 mg midazolam syrup on Day 1 of Period 1 under fasting conditions, followed by a single dose of the remaining components of the CYP cocktail (200 mg of caffeine tablet, 10 mg of warfarin tablet + 10 mg of vitamin K capsule, 40 mg of omeprazole capsule, and 30 mg of dextromethorphan liquid) on Day 2 of Period 1. In Period 2, study participants received once‐daily doses of 375 mg of cedirogant for 18 days with coadministration of the same single doses of midazolam and the rest of the CYP cocktail on Day 13 and Day 14, respectively. The CYP probe substrate dose in Period 1 and the first cedirogant dose in Period 2 were separated by a washout of at least 5 days. All CYP cocktail doses were administered after a minimum of 10‐h fast and approximately 4 h before lunch. Because food has only a minimal effect on cedirogant exposure, 4 cedirogant doses on other days were administered under fed conditions to reduce the burden of study participation.

Study design. Participants were confined to the study site for approximately 28 days and received CYP cocktails (represented by the upward arrows) alone or with cedirogant (represented by the downward arrows) over two periods, respectively. CYP, cytochrome P450.

All participants were between the ages of 18 and 55 years of age, had a body mass index between 18.0 and 29.9 kg/m2, and were in good health based on medical history, physical examination, vital signs assessment, laboratory profile, and a 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Female participants had to be of non‐childbearing potential (postmenopausal or surgically sterilized), and male participants of childbearing potential had to take birth control measures from the first day of the study through ≥30 days after the last study drug dose. Participants who used tobacco or nicotine‐containing products within 180 days prior to the first dose of study drug were not allowed to enroll. In addition, participants must not have had prior exposure to cedirogant and must not have used any known inhibitors or inducers of CYP or uridine diphosphate glucuronosyl transferases enzymes within 1 month prior to study drug administration.

Participants were confined to the study site for approximately 28 days in the study. Confinement started one day prior to Day 1 in each period and ended after the collection of the 120‐h blood sample (on Day 7 in Period 1 and Day 19 in Period 2). Serial blood samples for CYP probes and their possible metabolites were collected for up to 120 h after dosing in both Periods 1 and 2.

The largest within‐participant variance was 0.2947 for the logarithm of the pharmacokinetic variables analyzed for midazolam, caffeine, warfarin, omeprazole, and dextromethorphan in prior studies (unpublished data). Using this value, complete data from 18 participants provided ≥81.3% power to detect a 70% increase (41% decrease) in probe drug exposures. A total of 20 participants were enrolled to account for possible study dropouts.

Pharmacokinetic Sampling and Bioanalytical Methods

CYP Probe Substrates

Serial blood samples were collected for pharmacokinetic assessments of CYP probe substrates into sodium heparin‐containing tubes. The analytes of interest included midazolam, caffeine, S‐warfarin, omeprazole, 5‐hydroxyomeprazole (metabolite of omeprazole by CYP2C19), and dextromethorphan. For all these analytes, samples were collected prior to (0 h) and 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h after dosing in both periods (midazolam: Period 1 Day 1, Period 2 Day 13; all other CYP substrates: Period 1 Day 2, Period 2 Day 14). Additional blood samples were collected 36 h after dosing for caffeine; 48, 72, 96, and 120 h after dosing for S‐warfarin; and 48 and 72 h after dosing for dextromethorphan. Additionally, urine samples for dextromethorphan and dextrorphan (metabolite of dextromethorphan by CYP2D6) assays were collected in containers without preservatives before dosing (0 h) and over the following intervals: 0 to 6 h and 6 to 12 h on Day 2 of Period 1 and Day 14 of Period 2.

Plasma concentrations of midazolam, caffeine, S‐warfarin, R‐warfarin, omeprazole, 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole, and dextromethorphan were determined using validated liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometric detection (LC‐MS/MS) assays developed by PPD Laboratories (Middleton, WI). Briefly, analytes and their stable‐labeled internal standards (SLIS) were extracted from human plasma containing sodium heparin and subsequently analyzed using LC‐MS/MS. Chromatographic separation was achieved by reverse‐phase chromatography, followed by detection using a mass spectrometer. The lower limits of quantitation (LLOQ) for midazolam, caffeine, S‐warfarin, R‐warfarin, omeprazole, 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole, and dextromethorphan were established at 0.100, 27.3, 5.00, 5.00, 1.00, 1.00, and 0.0500 ng/mL, respectively. Samples quantified below the lowest standard were reported as zero.

Urine concentrations of dextromethorphan and dextrorphan were determined using an LC‐MS/MS method developed by PPD Laboratories. Briefly, analytes and their stable‐labeled internal standards were extracted from human urine and subsequently analyzed using LC‐MS/MS. Chromatographic separation was achieved by reverse‐phase chromatography, followed by detection using a mass spectrometer. The LLOQs for dextromethorphan and dextrorphan were established at 1.0 and 20 ng/mL, respectively. Samples quantified below the lowest standard were reported as zero.

Cedirogant

Serial blood samples for cedirogant assay were collected into dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2EDTA)‐containing collection tubes prior to dosing (0 h) and at 1.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h after dosing on Period 2 Day 12. Plasma concentrations of cedirogant were determined using a validated tandem mass spectrometric detection method. 4 For this study, the LLOQ for cedirogant was established at 50.2 ng/mL. Samples quantified below LLOQ were reported as zero.

Safety Assessments

Safety was evaluated throughout the entire study duration via adverse event (AE) monitoring, physical examination, vital sign measurement, ECG evaluation, and clinical laboratory testing (hematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis). Participants received a follow‐up safety telephone call 30 days after receiving their last dose of study drug.

Pharmacokinetic Analyses

The pharmacokinetic parameters for midazolam, caffeine, S‐warfarin, omeprazole, 5‐hydroxyomeprazole, and dextromethorphan were estimated using non‐compartmental methods with Phoenix software (version 8.2.0, Certara, Princeton, NJ) and included: maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax), time to Cmax (Tmax), area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) from time 0 to time of last measured concentration (AUCt) and infinity (AUCinf), terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2), and apparent oral plasma clearance (CL/F). The AUCt‐ and AUCinf‐based 5‐hydroxyomeprazole‐to‐omeprazole metabolic ratios and the 12‐h dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan molar urinary metabolic ratio were also calculated. The value of the apparent terminal phase elimination rate constant (β) was defined as the slope of the least squares linear regression line of the logarithms of the plasma concentration versus time profile of the terminal log‐linear phase. The data range included in the β calculation was identified using the maximum adjusted R2 value with examined data points between the last three non‐zero concentrations and up to but not including Tmax. Selected data points were visually inspected and, when appropriate, data points removed/added and the regression rerun. The t1/2 was calculated as ln(2)/β. For AUCinf calculations, extrapolated AUC (AUCext) was calculated as the last measurable concentration (Ct) divided by β, and AUCinf was calculated as the sum of AUCt and AUCext. Pharmacokinetic parameters calculated for cedirogant included Cmax, Tmax, minimum plasma concentration at the end of the dosing interval (Ctrough), and AUC over a dosing interval (AUCtau) at steady state.

The effects of repeated doses of cedirogant on caffeine, R‐warfarin, S‐warfarin, omeprazole, 5‐hydroxyomeprazole, and dextromethorphan were assessed using a linear mixed effects repeated measures analysis performed for the natural logarithms of Cmax and AUC utilizing data from Period 1, Day 2 and Period 2, Day 14 (SAS PROC MIXED, SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). The model included day as a fixed effect, and within‐subject correlation was accounted for by applying a compound symmetry covariance structure. The relative bioavailability of CYP probe substrates when co‐administered with cedirogant to that of those administered alone was assessed based on the point estimates and 90% confidence intervals for the difference of the least square means obtained from the repeated measures analyses of the natural logarithms of Cmax and AUC. Similar analyses were also performed for the urinary 5‐hydroxyomeprazole‐to‐omeprazole AUC ratio and dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan molar urinary ratio. The effect of cedirogant on midazolam was assessed using a similar analysis performed utilizing data from Period 1, Day 1 and Period2, Day 13.

Available data from all participants were included in the safety analyses. AEs were coded using standard MedDRA coding (v22.1) and analyzed by the following time periods: midazolam alone (Period 1, Day 1), CYP cocktail dosing (Period 1, Day 2 through Period 2, Day 1), repeated cedirogant dosing (Period 2, Days 1–12), and repeated cedirogant dosing with CYP cocktail dosing (Period 2, Day 13 through study end).

Safety

Overall, seven participants (35.0%) reported at least one treatment‐emergent AE, the majority of which were mild in severity. Observed treatment‐emergent AEs are summarized by study time segment in Table S5. Two participants discontinued from the study due to an occurrence of allergic dermatitis (severity: mild [n = 1], moderate [n = 1]), which was assessed as possibly related to cedirogant in both patients. No deaths or serious AEs were reported during the study. There was no pattern to the AEs reported, and no new safety issues for cedirogant were identified from this study.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that cedirogant had moderate induction potential on CYP3A and CYP2C19 with the examined dosing regimen (375 mg once daily for 14 days), as suggested by the approximate 55%‐65% decrease in midazolam and omeprazole AUC. On the other hand, cedirogant had no meaningful effect on the activities of CYP1A2 and CYP2D6 and a weak effect on the activity of CYP2C9 in vivo. The 375 mg dose was chosen because it is the highest potential clinical dose based on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic findings of single‐ascending and multiple‐ascending dose studies. 4 , 9 Further, measuring DDI potential on Day 14 of daily cedirogant administration was of sufficient duration for exposures to reach steady‐state and achieve maximum CYP induction. In a previously reported multiple‐dose study, cedirogant steady‐state concentrations were reached within 12 days with 375‐mg daily dosing, with observation of declining trough drug concentration levels between the 7th and 13th days. 4 Although the DDI assessment (i.e., co‐administration of CYP probe substrates in Period 2) in the current study was not conducted after steady state had been maintained for at least 7 days, the study design allowed for assessment of cedirogant's worst‐case induction effect due to the occurrence of auto‐induction.

Cedirogant has been previously shown to have a nonlinear pharmacokinetic profile following repeated once‐daily drug administration. Cedirogant exposures became less than dose‐proportional at steady state, with dose‐normalized steady state AUCtau lower than dose‐normalized AUCinf following a single dose administration. 4 , 5 Since cedirogant induces CYP3A4 in vitro 10 and is a clinical substrate of CYP3A, 4 its nonlinear pharmacokinetics were speculated to be attributed to CYP3A auto‐induction. The approximately 60% decrease in midazolam exposure in the presence of cedirogant demonstrated here supported this hypothesis.

The observed reductions in midazolam and omeprazole exposures in the presence of cedirogant indicate moderate CYP3A and CYP2C19 induction, respectively, by cedirogant. Given that sensitive and selective probe substrates were utilized in this study, the magnitude of decreases in plasma exposures for other less sensitive CYP3A or CYP2C19 drug substrates is expected to be smaller than that observed for cedirogant on midazolam or omeprazole, respectively. Additionally, drug interactions involving enzyme induction are generally considered to pose minimal safety concerns, as exposures to the victim drugs are decreased. However, this exposure decrease could result in reduced efficacy for victim drugs, particularly for medications with a narrow therapeutic index. Accordingly, concurrent use of CYP3A or CYP2C19 substrate medications with cedirogant was allowed in a Phase 2 study, with investigators cautioned about a potential efficacy reduction for applicable medications. 3

Assessment of CYP2D6 activity based on dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan urinary molar ratio is commonly performed in drug interaction studies and clinical practice. 6 However, continuous urine sample collection, as was done for 12 h in the current study, is demanding and resource‐intensive. A prior study showed consistent results between measures based on the dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan urinary molar ratio and dextromethorphan plasma AUC, 8 indicating that the more easily measured dextromethorphan plasma AUC might serve as a surrogate to intensive urinary measurements. In the current study, a slightly reduced dextromethorphan plasma AUC (by approximately 20%) was observed in the presence of cedirogant, but the urinary metabolic ratio remained largely unchanged (by <5%). This may be explained by the fact that dextromethorphan is also metabolized by CYP3A via N‐demethylation, 11 , 12 which was induced by cedirogant. The increased N‐demethylation likely contributed to the observed reduction in dextromethorphan plasma AUC. This finding suggests that caution is warranted when interpreting the dextromethorphan plasma AUC as a marker, particularly if the study drug modulates CYP3A activity.

Similarly, the magnitude of change in plasma 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole‐to‐omeprazole ratio (increased by approximately 100%), a commonly utilized CYP2C19 marker, was less compared to that in plasma omeprazole AUC (reduced by approximately 65% [i.e., activity increased by approximately 180%]). This may be explained by the fact that omeprazole is also subject to metabolism by CYP3A to omeprazole sulphone. 13 , 14 , 15 Notably, 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole is further metabolized to 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole sulfone by CYP3A4. 16 Induction of CYP3A by cedirogant may lead to an augmented clearance of 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole, offering another explanation for the observed discrepancy. However, the plasma 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole‐to‐omeprazole ratio was not statistically significantly impacted by co‐administered carbamazepine 15 or grapefruit juice, 17 known clinical inducer and inhibitor of CYP3A, respectively. Therefore, for the current study, the plasma 5‐hydroxy‐omeprazole‐to‐omeprazole ratio is more representative of CYP2C19 activity.

In the current study, midazolam was administered one day before the other probe drugs to avoid potential CYP3A‐mediated interactions that could have confounded study results. Many of the probe substrates in the Cooperstown 5+1 cocktail, such as dextromethorphan, 11 , 12 omeprazole, 13 and R‐warfarin, 18 are indeed also subject to metabolism by CYP3A but to a much lower extent. Consequently, they may compete for binding to CYP3A, particularly when midazolam, the probe substrate of CYP3A, is present. This may have impacted the metabolism of midazolam and the other probe drugs, further confounding cedirogant's effects. Additionally, midazolam was dosed orally in the current study, which was modified from the original Cooperstown 5+1 cocktail approach, where an intravenous formulation of midazolam was utilized. 6 Therefore, midazolam in this study was dosed on a separate day from the other probe drugs to also avoid any potential confounding effect from the oral administration of midazolam.

This study had several limitations. First, it lacked a Day 2 assessment in Period 2, which would have allowed separation of the inhibition component from the net induction/inhibition effect. However, in vitro data were not suggestive of CYP inhibition by cedirogant at the doses that have been evaluated in clinical studies. 10 Further, the main study objective was to evaluate the net effects of repeated cedirogant dosing on CYP enzyme activities in real‐world scenarios where CYP substrate medications are concomitantly administered during chronic cedirogant use. Second, participant genotyping was not conducted, which would have allowed exploration of how genetic polymorphisms may have influenced drug interaction magnitude. However, the relatively small sample size of the current study might not have supported such genotype/phenotype subgroup analyses. Of note, 45% of participants were African‐American, a population that may have overall lower CYP2D6 activity than the general population. 19 This may explain the observed high interindividual variability (i.e., %CV >100%) in several pharmacokinetic parameters of dextromethorphan relative to the other probe drugs. However, the dextromethorphan exposure and dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan molar urinary ratio were not meaningfully changed and did not exhibit a trend relative to the individual baselines (Figure 2) in the presence of cedirogant. This suggests minimal impact of CYP2D6 genotypes on the results of the current study. Third, plasma dextrorphan concentrations were not measured, which may otherwise allow evaluation of dextromethorphan‐to‐dextrorphan plasma molar AUC ratio as a surrogate for the intensive urinary measurements.

In conclusion, once‐daily administration of 375 mg of cedirogant in healthy adults resulted in moderate induction of CYP3A and CYP2C19 enzymes, had a limited impact on CYP2C9 activity, and showed no clinically relevant effects on CYP1A2 or CYP2D6. These DDI findings guided the use of concomitant medications that are metabolized by these CYP enzymes in subsequent clinical trials of cedirogant.
