Primary Streptococcal Peritonitis in a Postpartum Patient: A Rare but Life‐Threatening Condition
Authors: Matthias Pimiskern, Helmut Trimmel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present the case of a 43‐year‐old postpartum woman who developed primary streptococcal peritonitis with a fulminant septic course requiring intensive care. Initial symptoms were nonspecific and improved temporarily, but the patient returned the next day with recurrent pain and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Imaging confirmed peritonitis, and despite empirical antibiotics, the patient deteriorated into septic shock. Laparotomy revealed purulent peritonitis without a clear source, and Streptococcus pyogenes was detected via PCR and intraoperative cultures. The patient responded to escalated antimicrobial therapy and intensive supportive measures. However, a serious complication occurred following chest tube placement, leading to significant bleeding and prolonged ICU stay. This case underlines the importance of early recognition of rare pathogens, the necessity for prompt and adequate antimicrobial therapy, and the need for careful risk–benefit analysis of invasive procedures in the ICU setting.

Introduction

Primary bacterial peritonitis is rare in healthy individuals, and even more so when caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus, GAS). This report presents an unusual case of fulminant primary peritonitis in a postpartum woman, complicated by secondary iatrogenic bleeding, and discusses the clinical and therapeutic challenges encountered.

Case History/Examination

The following report details the case of a 43‐year‐old female patient. The patient presented at the emergency department with acute abdominal pain. Following symptomatic analgesics, in conjunction with abdomen ultrasound and laboratory findings within limits, the patient was discharged home after symptoms improved significantly.

However, the following day, she returned with the same symptoms. The laboratory results now show clearly elevated inflammatory parameters, and a CT scan of the abdomen reveals signs of peritonitis and thickened loops of small bowel. As the patient had given birth without complications one month before, a gynecological consultation is arranged; however, a respective focus was ruled out. Thus, she is admitted to the surgical department; antibiotic treatment (Cefuroxime and Metronidazole) is established. The next day, the patient has to be admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) as she deteriorates rapidly to septic shock.

Laboratory findings on ICU admission revealed highly septic findings (Table 1). As deterioration occurred under the established antibiotics, this was empirically switched to Cefepime and Fosfomycin. The indication for laparotomy is made, clinically as well as in respect to CT findings. Abundant cloudy ascites and four‐quadrant peritonitis are found—however, no discernible cause is detected. Subsequent to this, S. pyogenes can be detected in the Light Cycler Septifast test from the central venous blood sample on ICU admission as well as in the intraoperative swab (Table 2). In order to clarify an occult focus, consultations were made with the oncology, cardiology, and ENT departments, but without result.

A review of the patient's medical history revealed that her father had died the previous year from a fever of unknown origin. The attending physicians suspected hyperinflammatory syndrome; hepatosplenomegaly led to suspicion of hemophagocytic lymph histiocytosis.

Under antibiotic and intensive medical treatment, the patient's condition stabilizes over the following days. The inflammatory parameters showed a downward trend followed the modification of antibiotic treatment and the surgical measures (Table 1). During the initial 2 weeks of treatment in the ICU, the patient demonstrated a positive recovery trajectory from her primary illness. Catecholamine support is discontinued, gas exchange is satisfactory with nasal O2 (3 L/min).

Due to recurrent pleural effusions, in conjunction with dyspnoea, a series of pleural punctures are undertaken. Finally, the decision is taken to proceed with the installation of a Bülau's drain. Unfortunately, the chest tubing leads to a serious bleeding complication, which requires several blood transfusions and a 2‐week prolongation of the patient's stay in intensive care. Subsequently, however, the patient can be transferred to the general ward in good health.

Differential Diagnosis

Streptococcus pyogenes , also known as Group A Streptococcus (GAS), is an almost exclusively human pathogen that is associated in the majority of cases with infections in the ear, nose and throat (ENT) area. Scarlet fever is among the most prevalent forms of the disease. However, GAS has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of soft tissue infections, including impetigo contagiosa, erysipelas, and necrotising fasciitis. A considerable number of individuals are found to be colonized without displaying any symptoms. Another feared complication is S. pyogenes sepsis as part of toxic shock syndrome. Penicillin G is the antibiotic of choice. However, it is mandatory to report any cases of the disease in Austria [1]. The Austrian Federal Ministry of Social Affairs, Health, Care and Consumer Protection numbers 364 reported infections with group A streptococcus but no deaths in 2023 [2]. S. pyogenes is regarded as a relatively uncommon cause of primary peritonitis. Patients suffering from cirrhosis of the liver and ascites appear to be particularly vulnerable, as they demonstrate an elevated risk of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), a condition typically triggered by Gram‐negative bacteria, though streptococci (particularly Streptococcus pneumoniae or viridans) have also been implicated [3]. The underlying cause of this condition is a compromised immune system, with bacterial translocation from the intestine being a contributing factor. Patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis are also susceptible to streptococcal peritonitis. The following species of streptococci have been documented in this context: Streptococcus mitis , S. viridans , S. pneumoniae and β‐haemolytic streptococci. The catheter or contaminated dialysis fluid act as entry points [4, 5].

Discussion

Cause and Transmission

The presence of female gender, immunosuppression and a previous varicella infection are all factors that are likely to be favorable for GAS Infections [6].

Streptococcus pyogenes is an exclusively human pathogen which can cause invasive diseases through hematogenous dissemination, translocation from the gastrointestinal tract, or ascending infection from the female genital tract. In postpartum women, mucosal disruption, transient immune modulation, and alterations of the vaginal microbiome may facilitate bacterial invasion and subsequent peritoneal infection. A systematic review analyzed reported cases of primary S. pyogenes peritonitis. A genital tract origin was suspected in roughly 15% of all cases; in many patients, no definite route of transmission could be identified [7]. In women with gynecological infections, or particularly in the postpartum state, it should be borne in mind that, although rare, GAS can ascend from the genital tract and cause peritonitis, especially in the postpartum period or in cases of infection of the internal reproductive organs.

Clinical Manifestation

Clinical presentation of primary GAS peritonitis is often non‐specific and includes diffuse abdominal pain, fever, nausea, and vomiting. Early symptoms may temporarily improve, which can delay diagnosis. Despite this, rapid progression to severe sepsis or septic shock is frequently reported due to the high virulence and toxin‐mediated inflammatory response of GAS. In pooled analyses of published cases, more than 70% of patients developed septic shock [7].

Regrettably, no bacteriological examination was performed on the day of admission, despite repeated indications of a potential gynecological source of infection and referral to the department of gynecology.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis of primary GAS peritonitis is challenging and is largely a diagnosis of exclusion. Imaging typically demonstrates signs of peritonitis without an identifiable intra‐abdominal source. Definitive diagnosis requires microbiological confirmation via blood cultures, peritoneal fluid cultures, or molecular diagnostic methods such as PCR. Early microbiological sampling prior to antibiotic administration significantly increases diagnostic yield [6].

A case report and metanalysis details a case of life‐threatening peritonitis in a 56‐year‐old woman with no pre‐existing medical conditions, attributed to bilateral salpingitis caused by GAS. A total of 65 case reports were analyzed in this paper, of which 80% involved women. Many of these women required surgical intervention, and the mortality rate was found to be approximately 4.6% [7].

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses include secondary bacterial peritonitis caused by gastrointestinal perforation, appendicitis, pancreatitis, mesenteric ischemia, and gynecological infections such as salpingitis or endometritis. In postpartum patients, exclusion of obstetric and gynecological sources is mandatory before establishing the diagnosis of primary peritonitis [7].

Despite the rheumatic and immunological causes of the disease being effectively excluded by the internal medicine and oncology teams in our case, there was a suspicion of potentially reduced immunocompetence in the family history.

Treatment

Management of invasive GAS infections requires immediate initiation of broad‐spectrum empirical antibiotic therapy, followed by targeted therapy once the pathogen is identified. Penicillin remains the antibiotic of choice; however, in severe invasive infections, combination therapy with clindamycin is recommended due to its inhibitory effect on toxin production. Surgical exploration is frequently necessary to exclude secondary causes and achieve source control [8].

Prognosis

Although overall mortality of primary GAS peritonitis is relatively low (approximately 4%–5%), morbidity is substantial. Prognosis depends on early recognition, prompt antimicrobial therapy, and adequate source control. Delayed diagnosis or complications related to invasive procedures may significantly prolong ICU stay and recovery [7].

A recent varicella infection could not be ascertained in our patient. The microbiological results did not demonstrate an elevated bacterial load, a finding that was also discussed in the context of our hospital's antibiotic stewardship program. Patients with compromised immune systems, such as those undergoing chemotherapy, systemic corticosteroid therapy or organ transplantation, are naturally included in the overall high‐risk category. As the immune system is weakened, the peritoneal cavity becomes a more accessible environment for the entry of streptococci. A recent analysis of the literature on PubMed revealed further 45 publications. A recent study by Tayler et al. found 26 published cases of this type in children since 1980. Subsequently, the same authors analyzed 256 cases from 2010 to 2022 that were treated at Starship Children's Hospital (Auckland). S. pyogenes was detected in a mere 11 cases in this study [9]. In 2019, Johnson et al. conducted a review of 32 case reports. The authors report an increase in infections over the last decade. However, it should be noted that the swabs and blood cultures were only taken subsequent to the administration of the initial antibiotic treatment. This is a crucial consideration when evaluating the bacterial load. The fundamental question that arises from this case is whether immunosupportive therapy, such as IgM‐enriched immunoglobulins, would have provided relevant support or shortened the course of the disease. A recent meta‐analysis by Pan et al. provides support for the administration of the treatment in the context of sepsis [10]. The prolonged stay on the ICU can be attributed primarily to the occurrence of a serious complication. Consequently, the patient required intensive medical care within a short period of time due to two life‐threatening events.

Clinical Reasoning and ICU Complication

In the present case, prolonged intensive care treatment was not solely attributable to the primary infectious process but was significantly influenced by a severe iatrogenic complication following chest tubing. Although recurrent pleural effusions justified intervention, the bleeding complication highlights the increased procedural risk in critically ill septic patients. This observation supports the conclusion that continuous reassessment of the risk–benefit ratio of invasive procedures is essential, particularly when patients are already clinically improving.

Conclusion

Primary peritonitis caused by S. pyogenes is an exceptionally rare but potentially fatal condition. Early diagnosis, broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy, and surgical intervention are essential. Special attention must be paid to invasive interventions in critically ill patients, especially when complications can substantially prolong recovery. This case highlights the need for clinical awareness of atypical presentations of postpartum infections and the risk of iatrogenic complications in ICU management.
