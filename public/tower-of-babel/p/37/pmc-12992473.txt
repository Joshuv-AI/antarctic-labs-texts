KANK4 Regulates CXCL16 Glycosylation Through TMEM260 to Modulate Microglial Activation in Sepsis-associated Encephalopathy
Authors: Mao Peng, Sinian Tan, Ao Li, Qiong Guo, Lin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is a significant clinical condition leading to cognitive impairment, yet its molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. This study demonstrates that KANK4 expression is significantly downregulated in the hippocampus of SAE mice and in LPS-induced microglial cells, and this downregulation closely correlates with microglial overactivation and increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α). Importantly, hippocampal overexpression of KANK4 not only markedly improved cognitive performance in SAE mice in the novel object recognition and Morris water maze (MWM) tests but also effectively alleviated hippocampal neuronal damage. Mechanistically, we confirmed that KANK4 directly binds to the 3’UTR of TMEM260 mRNA to enhance its stability, thereby inhibiting the O-mannosylation modification (with S117 as the key site) and protein expression of CXCL16. A series of functional experiments showed that TMEM260 overexpression significantly reduced CXCL16 levels and attenuated neuroinflammation, whereas CXCL16 overexpression completely reversed the neuroprotective effects of TMEM260. These findings reveal for the first time the crucial role of the KANK4-TMEM260-CXCL16 axis in regulating microglial activation, providing a new therapeutic target for SAE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02481-y.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, resulting in high morbidity and mortality 1, 2. SAE, a common complication of sepsis, involves diffuse brain dysfunction and neurological symptoms 3. The main pathophysiological mechanisms of SAE include blood-brain barrier disruption, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation 4, 5. Neuroinflammatory processes involve the breakdown of the blood-brain barrier, the conduction of inflammatory mediators, and the activation of microglial cells. Microglial cells exacerbate this process by secreting more inflammatory cytokines 6, 7. Therefore, this study aims to explore the mechanisms of microglial cell activation in SAE, with a focus on its impact on neuroinflammation.

KN motif and KANK family member 4 (KANK4) belongs to the KANK protein family (KANK1-4). Its N-terminal contains a KN motif and a coiled-coil domain, while the C-terminal contains a unique skeletal repeat domain 8, 9. KANK4 is predominantly expressed in the cytoplasm and microtubule cytoskeleton, where it regulates cytoskeletal dynamics 10. In multiple sclerosis, KANK4 expression is significantly downregulated in chronic active lesions compared to non-active lesions, suggesting a link between KANK4 downregulation and microglial activation 11. A study by Xu et al. using an SAE mouse model also found downregulation of KANK4 in SAE mice, supporting this link 12. Thus, we hypothesize that the expression of KANK4 is related to microglial activation in SAE.

Based on predictions from the RNAct and RPISeq websites, KANK4 may act as an RNA-binding protein and interact with the mRNA of Transmembrane protein 260 (TMEM260). TMEM260 is a transmembrane protein and an O-mannosyltransferase enzyme, which specifically glycosylates the IPT domain 13. Additionally, Xu et al.’s transcriptomic analysis indicated that TMEM260 expression was reduced in SAE mice, but its relationship with KANK4 remains unclear. The regulatory mechanism of KANK4 on TMEM260 requires further study.

Through a search on the Biogrid website, we found that TMEM260 interacts with C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 16 (CXCL16). CXCL16 is a member of the ELR⁻ CXC subfamily with low homology to other CXC chemokines and is involved in various inflammatory and immune responses 14–16. Studies show CXCL16 expression in BV2 microglial cells is significantly increased under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, and silencing CXCL16 reduces neuroinflammatory factor expression and M1-type microglial markers 17. Previous research has pointed out that the mucin-like stalk region of transmembrane CXCL16 (mCXCL16), rich in serine and threonine, is a highly O-glycosylated region, which is crucial for the functional presentation of the chemokine 18. Glycosylation plays a key role in protein function and interactions 19, but systematic studies on CXCL16 glycosylation are lacking.

This study aims to investigate whether KANK4 modulates CXCL16 glycosylation through TMEM260, thereby influencing microglial activation and the inflammatory response. Using an SAE mouse model and an LPS-induced BV2 cell inflammation model, we will analyze the role of KANK4 in microglial activation, inflammatory factor expression, and its interaction with TMEM260 and CXCL16. We aim to provide new insights into treating infection-related brain dysfunction.

Materials and Methods

Animal Models

A total of 36 male C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks (Beijing SBF Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), were used in this experiment. The mice were adapted in a SPF-level animal laboratory (temperature: 21–26 °C, humidity: 30%−70%) for one week with free access to food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. In the first stage, the mice were divided into two groups, with 6 mice per group: the Sham group (sham surgery group) and the SAE group (sepsis-associated encephalopathy model group). In the second stage, the experimental mice were divided into four groups, with 6 mice per group: the Sham group (sham surgery group), the SAE group (sepsis-associated encephalopathy model group), the SAE + AAV-NC group (model+empty virus control group), and the SAE + AAV-KANK4 group (model+KANK4 overexpression group). Prior to surgery, all mice were fasted and water-deprived for 12 h. They were then anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of avertin (0.2 mL/10 g body weight).

For the SAE + AAV-NC group and SAE + AAV-KANK4 group, viral injections were performed 8 weeks before the modeling procedure. The virus used in this experiment was an AAV-PhP.eB serotype customized by Cyagen, in which KANK4 was overexpressed under the control of the Iba-1 promoter. The viral titer was 1 × 10¹² GC/mL. During stereotaxic injection, anesthetized mice received AAV-KANK4 or an equivalent volume of control virus (2 µL per site) at three coordinates: AP − 1.94 mm, ML ± 2.3 mm, DV − 1.8 mm; AP − 2.3 mm, ML ± 2.6 mm, DV − 2.0 mm; and AP − 2.54 mm, ML ± 2.9 mm, DV − 2.25 mm, followed by suturing and postoperative recovery.

SAE was induced via CLP. The mice were anesthetized, fixed on a 37 °C temperature-controlled surgical table, and their vital signs were monitored. The abdomen was disinfected, and a 2 cm incision was made along the midline of the lower abdomen. The cecum was exposed and gently pulled out. The distal 1/2 of the cecum was ligated with a 4 − 0 surgical suture, and a 7-gauge needle was used to puncture the ligation site and extrude a small amount of intestinal contents. The cecum was then replaced, and the abdominal wall was sutured in layers. The Sham group underwent the same abdominal procedure with cecum exposure but without ligation or puncture.

After surgery, all mice were placed in a temperature-controlled recovery chamber post-surgery and were fed normally thereafter. On day 14, the novel object recognition test was conducted. From days 15 to 19, water maze training was performed (data on escape latency were recorded). On day 20, the water maze test was conducted (data without platform were recorded). Following behavioral testing, mice were euthanized and brain tissues were collected. Primary microglial were isolated as required for subsequent experiments. The remaining tissue samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for later analysis. All procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Third Xiangya Affiliated Hospital of Central South University Ethics Committee and adhered to ethical standards for animal experimentation (APU-2025-0294).

Cell Culture and Treatment

Mouse microglial BV2 cells were purchased from Qingqi (Shanghai) Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. BV2 cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. To induce a neuroinflammatory model, cells were treated with 1 µg/mL [20] LPS for 12 h (as reported in [20], and confirmed by our own validation that this treatment condition did not significantly affect cell viability, with the final treatment time determined based on our experimental results). O-GlcNAcase (OGA) was applied to remove the O-GlcNAc modification from CXCL16 protein to verify the presence of this glycosylation.

Isolation and Purity Detection of Microglial Cells from Hippocampal Tissue

Mice from the Sham, SAE, SAE + AAV-NC, and SAE + AAV-KANK4 groups were euthanized by decapitation after disinfecting their bodies with 75% ethanol. The hippocampal tissue was rapidly isolated on ice. The tissue was rinsed with Hank’s solution containing 2% penicillin-streptomycin (Sevier), and then mechanically minced. Tissues were digested with 70 U/ml type I collagenase (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 15 min to prepare a single-cell suspension. The suspension was filtered through a 70 μm mesh, and then centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, discarding the supernatant. After culturing for 48 h, the medium was collected and centrifuged at 1100 g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended. The pellet was resuspended and plated onto poly-L-lysine-coated plates in DMEM/F-12 medium (with HEPES) (Meilunbio) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Microglial cell purity was assessed using Trypan Blue staining (Solarbio), APC-CD11b antibody (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, 2513551), and flow cytometry (Agilent, RMNNC-3000). Primary microglial cells were first isolated from brain tissue and cultured to 80%−90% confluence. The cells were then digested with 0.25% trypsin and washed with PBS to remove residual enzyme. Trypan Blue staining was used to count viable cells, ensuring a viability rate of ≥ 90%. Cells were adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL and incubated with APC-CD11b antibody for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the CD11b-positive cell proportion was determined by flow cytometry to assess microglial cell purity.

Following the product instructions, transient transfection was performed using the Lipo8000™ transfection reagent (Beyotime, C0533). The siRNA sequences are listed in Table 1.

Novel Object Recognition (NOR) Test

Mice were first allowed to acclimate in the empty experimental apparatus for 10 min. During the familiarization phase, two identical objects (A and B) were placed in the apparatus (fixed and odorless, 10 cm away from the side walls). The mice were placed with their backs to the objects, and their exploration behavior was recorded for 5 min (defined as nose or mouth contact with the object or being within 2–3 cm of it). Exploration frequency, duration, and movement trajectories were analyzed. After 1 h, the testing phase began, where one of the objects was replaced with a novel object (AC or BC). Mice were again placed with their backs to the objects, and their exploration behavior was recorded for 5 min. The recognition memory ability was assessed by comparing the exploration time difference between the new and old objects (Recognition index = (Time spent exploring the new object - Time spent exploring the old object)/Total exploration time). The entire experiment was recorded and analyzed automatically using a video tracking system (e.g., EthoVision).

Morris Water Maze Test (MWM Test)

The experiment consisted of 5 days of acquisition training and 1 day of probe training. During the acquisition phase, each mouse underwent 4 training trials per day (with a 20-min interval between trials). Each time, the mouse was placed headfirst into the water from one of four randomly selected starting positions (East, West, South, North). The escape latency to find the hidden platform was recorded. If the mouse did not find the platform within 120 s, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to stay there for 10 s. After each trial, the mouse was dried off and, if necessary, warmed under a 150 W incandescent lamp for 5 min. The average escape latency for each day was calculated. During the probe training phase, the platform was removed, and the mouse was placed into the water from the quadrant opposite to the original platform location. The time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform crossings were recorded within 120 s. All trials were video-tracked for movement trajectory analysis.

Histological Staining

Hippocampal tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. The tissue was sectioned into 4–5 μm thin slices for the following histological staining to assess pathological changes in the hippocampal region:

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Hippocampal sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin (Sevier) for 10 min, and then rinsed. They were subsequently stained with eosin (Sevier) for 5 min, washed, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with mounting medium. Pathological changes and the severity of neuronal damage in the hippocampus were observed under a microscope (DM500, Leica).

Nissl Staining

After dewaxing and rehydrating hippocampal sections, they were stained with Nissl stain solution (Sevier) for 5 min, followed by slight differentiation with 0.1% ice acetic acid. After water washing, dehydration and clearing were performed. Finally, sections were mounted with mounting medium, and Nissl body distribution and pathological changes in neurons were observed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The hippocampal tissue of mice was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) for 24 h, followed by conventional paraffin embedding and sectioning into 4 μm thick slices. After antigen retrieval, the sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime) at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime) for 1 h. For detection of microglial activation, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Iba-1, Boster, PB0517, 1:200; CD11b, Proteintech, 66519-1-lg, 1:200). To examine the co-localization and expression of KANK4 with astrocytes (GFAP-labeled), neurons (NeuN-labeled), and microglia (Iba1-labeled), sections were incubated with KANK4 antibody (Pinke Biological, DF15100, 1:200), GFAP antibody (Proteintech, 16825-1-AP, 1:200), NeuN antibody (Proteintech, 26975-1-AP, 1:200), and Iba1 antibody (Boster, PB0517, 1:200). To verify successful infection and overexpression of AAV-KANK4 in microglia within the hippocampus of SAE mice, sections were incubated with antibodies against KANK4 (Pinke Biological, DF15100, 1:200) and Iba-1 (Boster, PB0517, 1:200). The sections were then incubated with Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, Sevier, GB21303, 1:100; goat anti-rat IgG, Sevier, GB21302, 1:100) for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sevier) for 15 min. After mounting, the sections were dried at 37 °C and stored. The sections were then observed under a fluorescence microscope.

For BV2 cells, the cells were seeded onto coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After blocking with 5% BSA, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Iba-1, 1:200; CD11b, Proteintech, 21851-1-AP, 1:200; KANK4, Affinity, DF15100, 1:200). The cells were then incubated for 1 h in the dark with FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime, A0568, 1:300). Afterward, the cells were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X800), and fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ.

ELISA

Mouse hippocampal tissue and BV2 microglial cells were collected, homogenized, and sonicated. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5–10 min at 2–8 °C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent detection. The procedures were carried out according to the instructions of the ELISA kits (IL-1β/IL-6/TNF-α, Elabscience, catalog numbers E-EL-M0037/E-EL-M0044/E-EL-M3063). The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines (pg/mL) were calculated using the standard curve method. Three replicate wells were used for each group, and the experiment was repeated three times.

qRT-PCR

The expression levels of KANK4, TMEM260, and CXCL16 were measured in microglial cells isolated from hippocampal tissue and BV2 microglial cells. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent. Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit (Takara) for amplification. Specific primers were used to detect the expression of target genes, with β-actin as the reference gene for normalization. The primer sequences used are listed in Table 2. The relative expression levels were analyzed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. To ensure data reliability, each sample was analyzed in at least three technical replicates.

Western Blot

Hippocampal tissue-derived microglial cells and BV2 microglial cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF (Bio Sharp) to extract proteins. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method (Bio Sharp). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h. For detection of CXCL16 glycosylation, the membranes were incubated with 10 µg/mL ConA-HRP probe, ensuring that buffer without mannose was used. The membranes were incubated on a shaker at 4 °C overnight or at room temperature for 2 h to ensure complete binding of ConA to the mannose glycosylation sites. The membranes were then incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP (Abcam, ab6721, 1:5000) were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. After washing with TBST, chemiluminescence detection was performed using Immobilon Western HRP Substrate (Millipore). Finally, the intensity of each band was analyzed using a gel imaging system (SINSAGE), and protein expression levels were assessed. β-actin (Proteintech, 81115-1-RR, 1:5000) was used as an internal reference. The following primary antibodies were used for immunoblotting: KANK4 (Abcam, ab121410, 1:1000), TMEM260 (Bio-techne, NBP2-85949, 1:500), CXCL16 (Proteintech, 60123-1-Ig, 1:1000), and Anti-His (Abcam, ab9108, 1:1000).

Western blot Detection of CXCL16 Glycosylation Level

PCR primers for S117A, S137A, and S139A mutations were synthesized as follows: F-S117A: TTGCCTCAAGCCAGgcTACCCAGACCCCTGAGGCC; R-S117A: TAgcCTGGCTTGAGGCAAATGTTTTTGGTGGTG. F-S137A: ACATAGgcTCAGAGCACTCAGCACTCCACTCTT; R-S137A: AGTGCTCTGAgcCTATGTGCAGGGGTGCTCGTG. F-S139A: ATAGTCAGAGgcCACTCAGCACTCCACTCTTCCA; R-S139A: TGAGTGgcCTCTGACTATGTGCAGGGGTGCTCG. Point mutation plasmids were constructed through PCR, restriction enzyme digestion, and ligation, and then transfected into microglial cells. Both wild-type CXCL16 and glycosylation site mutants (S117A, S137A, S139A) were transfected into the microglial cells. After culturing, Western blot analysis was performed to assess the protein expression and glycosylation levels of CXCL16, following standard Western blot procedures.

CCK-8

Cell proliferation activity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan FC). BV2 microglial cells were digested and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cells were stimulated with different concentrations of LPS (0.5, 1, 2 µg/mL) to select the optimal concentration. Ultimately, 1 µg/mL LPS was chosen, and cells were treated for 0, 6, 12, 24, or 48 h to determine the optimal treatment time.

To further evaluate the effect of LPS on cell proliferation, cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h, and cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit. 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by 1 h of incubation in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and cell activity was calculated based on the measured OD values.

Database

Bioinformatics analysis predicted that KANK4 might interact with TMEM260 mRNA as an RNA-binding protein (RNAct database prediction, https://rnact.tartaglialab.com/). Protein–protein interaction between CXCL16 and TMEM260 was predicted via the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC detection was performed using the PV-6000 universal kit (Zhongshan Golden Bridge). After dewaxing and rehydrating the slices, antigen retrieval solution and endogenous peroxidase inhibitor were applied to the paraffin sections. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the anti-CXCL16 antibody (Thermo Fisher, PA5-115068, 1:200). Subsequently, the slices were incubated with 100 µL of enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer at 37 °C for 20 min. Finally, the sections were stained with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. The slices were observed and interpreted under an optical microscope.

RNA Pull-down Assay

The RNA pull-down assay was performed using the RNA pull-down kit (Sangon, B605110). Biotin-labeled TMEM260 mRNA fragments were synthesized by in vitro transcription and incubated with BV2 cell lysate at 4 °C for 2 h. Nucleic-Acid Compatible Streptavidin Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher, 88817) were then added and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. After washing five times with PBS, the bound proteins were eluted using RIPA lysis buffer. KANK4 protein in the eluted products was detected by Western blot (antibody: Abcam, ab121410, 1:1000). Biotin-labeled irrelevant RNA fragments were used as a negative control. The experiment was repeated three times, and the binding efficiency was quantified using ImageJ.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) Assay

The RIP assay was conducted using the GenSeq® RIP Kit (Yunxu Biotechnology, GS-ET-006). The procedure followed the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 × 10⁷ BV2 cells were lysed, and the lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg of anti-KANK4 antibody (Abcam, ab121410, 1:1000) or an isotype IgG control (negative control). The next day, PGM Beads magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. The samples were then washed five times with RIP washing buffer (containing 0.1% Tween-20). Finally, co-precipitated RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and TMEM260 mRNA was detected by qRT-PCR. Data were analyzed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method to calculate the fold enrichment of TMEM260 mRNA in the KANK4 group relative to the IgG control group. The experiment was independently repeated three times.

Cycloheximide (CHX) Experiment

To assess the stability of CXCL16 protein, CHX (MCE, HY-12320) was used to block new protein synthesis, and a protein stability tracking experiment was conducted. Briefly, when BV2 microglial cells reached 80% confluence, they were treated with 100 µg/mL CHX 21 for different time points (0, 4, 8, 12 h). After treatment, cells were washed with pre-chilled PBS, lysed (RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors), and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. CXCL16 protein levels were measured by Western blot (antibody: Proteintech, 60123-1-Ig, 1:1000), and the band intensity was quantified using ImageJ. The experiment was independently repeated three times.

Puromycin Incorporation Assay

In this study, the puromycin incorporation assay was used to measure the translation efficiency of TMEM260 protein. BV2 cells were cultured to 60%−70% confluence and treated with 200 ng/mL puromycin (Solarbio, P8230) for 30 min, after which the reaction was stopped on ice. After washing with PBS, total protein was extracted. Western blot was used to detect TMEM260 (antibody: Bio-techne, NBP2-85949, 1:500) and puromycin-labeled nascent proteins (anti-puromycin antibody, Sigma-Aldrich, MABE343), with β-actin (1:5000) as the loading control. The secondary antibody was HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H&L) (Abcam, ab6721, 1:5000). All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Actinomycin D (Act D) Experiment

BV2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) were treated with 3 µg/mL Act D (Selleck, S8964) for each group. Cells were collected at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h, and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. TMEM260 mRNA levels were measured by qRT-PCR, with β-actin as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The untreated group served as the control, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Experiment

To investigate the interaction between TMEM260 and CXCL16 in microglial cells, cells were first seeded in six-well plates and cultured to logarithmic growth. Total protein was extracted using RIPA (Bio sharp) lysis buffer, followed by enrichment of the target protein using a Co-IP experiment. Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime) and anti-CXCL16 (Proteintech, 60123-1-Ig, 1:1000) or anti-His (Abcam, ab9108, 1:1000) antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C to immunoprecipitate CXCL16 and His-CXCL16 protein complexes, respectively. As a control, IgG antibody was used for IP to verify specific binding. The precipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and detected by Western blot for the interaction between TMEM260 and CXCL16. Band intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in three independent trials to ensure the reliability of the results.

Data Analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two‑tailed unpaired Student’s t‑test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post‑hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

KANK4 Downregulation and Microglial Activation Are Associated with Cognitive Impairment and Neuroinflammation in SAE Mice

Following the surgical induction of the SAE mouse model via CLP, behavioral experiments (Fig. 1A-D, Supplementary Fig. 1A-B) revealed that the recognition index of SAE mice in the NOR test was significantly lower than that of the Sham group (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1 A). In the MWM test, SAE mice exhibited a significantly longer escape latency, fewer platform crossings, and reduced time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 1B-D, Supplementary Fig. 1B), suggesting cognitive impairment in SAE mice. Histological analysis of hippocampal tissue through H&E and Nissl staining (Fig. 1E-F, Supplementary Fig. 1D) showed disordered cellular arrangement, nuclear pyknosis, neuronal loss, and increased Nissl bodies, indicating morphological damage in the hippocampus of SAE mice. IF staining demonstrated no significant co-localization of KANK4 with GFAP (astrocytes) or NeuN (neurons) in Sham or SAE groups. Notably, compared with the Sham group, the co-localization of KANK4 with microglia (Iba-1-labeled) was markedly increased in the SAE group, suggesting that KANK4 signals are more closely associated with microglia (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Furthermore, the SAE group displayed a greater number of Iba-1 and CD11b-positive microglial cells with an activated morphology (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Fig. 1E). Furthermore, following the application of a high-purity microglia sorting protocol from normal hippocampal tissue (purity: 96.56%, Supplementary Fig. 1 F), we analyzed microglia isolated from the hippocampal tissues of Sham and SAE mice. The results showed that, compared to the Sham group, SAE-derived microglia exhibited not only significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α; Fig. 1H) but also a marked downregulation in both KANK4 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 1I-J). These findings directly indicate the activated state of microglia in SAE and suggest the involvement of KANK4 in this pathological process.

Neuroprotective Effects of KANK4 on Cognitive Function and Hippocampal Pathology in SAE Mice

To elucidate the role of KANK4 in cognitive function and hippocampal pathology in SAE mice, a series of experiments were conducted. First, the viral infection efficiency of the lentivirus was successfully validated (Supplementary Fig. 4A-B). IF staining confirmed the successful infection of the adeno-associated virus overexpressing KANK4 in the hippocampal tissue of SAE mice, with co-localization observed with the microglial marker Iba-1 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A), thereby providing a foundation for subsequent cellular localization and functional studies. Behavioral experiments showed that the SAE group performed significantly worse in the NOR test (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 3B) and the MWM test (Fig. 3B-D, Supplementary Fig. 3 C), with significantly longer escape latencies (Fig. 3B), fewer platform crossings (Fig. 3C), and less time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 3D) compared to the Sham group, indicating impaired cognitive and spatial memory functions. The SAE + AAV-KANK4 group showed improvements in these parameters, demonstrating better cognitive function recovery. H&E (Fig. 3E) and Nissl staining (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 3D) results showed that hippocampal tissue in the SAE and SAE + AAV-NC groups exhibited nuclear pyknosis, neuronal loss, and an increase in Nissl bodies, indicating neuronal damage. In contrast, the SAE + AAV-KANK4 group showed reduced nuclear pyknosis, fewer Nissl bodies, and better-preserved neurons, suggesting that KANK4 has neuroprotective effects. IF staining (Fig. 3G-H, Supplementary Fig. 3E) showed significant activation of microglial cells in the SAE group, with elevated expression of Iba-1 and CD11b, indicating neuroinflammation. The SAE + AAV-KANK4 group, however, displayed reduced microglial activation, suggesting that KANK4 can suppress inflammatory responses. ELISA analysis (Fig. 3I) revealed significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in the SAE group, while these levels significantly decreased in the SAE + AAV-KANK4 group, indicating that KANK4 has anti-inflammatory effects. Molecular level analysis (Fig. 3J-L) showed that KANK4 mRNA and protein expression were lower in the SAE group, whereas KANK4 expression was significantly upregulated in the SAE + AAV-KANK4 group, supporting its potential role in inflammation suppression and repair. These results suggest that KANK4 may play a critical role in improving cognitive impairments, neuronal damage, and inflammatory responses induced by SAE.

Interaction between KANK4 Protein and TMEM260 mRNA

Experiments were conducted to predict and validate the interaction between KANK4 and TMEM260 mRNA. According to predictions from the RNAct website (Fig. 4A), KANK4 was likely to interact with TMEM260 mRNA. RPISeq analysis further supported this, showing high RNA-protein binding probability scores (RF = 0.85, SVM = 0.99), which indicated a likely direct interaction between KANK4 protein and TMEM260 mRNA (Fig. 4B). The microglial cells were isolated from the hippocampus tissue of SAE mouse model, qRT-PCR and Western blot results (Fig. 4C-D) showed that TMEM260 expression was significantly downregulated in the SAE group. However, TMEM260 expression was reversed after KANK4 overexpression, suggesting that the expression of TMEM260 in the SAE model might have been regulated by KANK4. Meanwhile, significantly downregulated TMEM260 expression results were observed in the LPS-induced BV2 cell model (Fig. 4E-F). RNA pull-down assays (Fig. 4G) and RIP experiments (Fig. 4H) further confirmed the direct binding between KANK4 and TMEM260 mRNA, indicating that KANK4 regulated the expression of TMEM260 by binding to its mRNA. LPS-induced BV2 cell lines were treated with OE-NC/OE-KANK4 and si-NC/si-KANK4 (knockdown efficiency shown in Supplementary Fig. 4C-D). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses (Fig. 4I-K) revealed that knockdown of KANK4 significantly reduced TMEM260 expression, while KANK4 overexpression significantly increased TMEM260 expression. To further investigate the mechanism by which KANK4 regulated TMEM260 expression, puromycin incorporation assays (Fig. 4L) showed that, although KANK4 overexpression or knockdown affected the expression of TMEM260, it did not alter the translation efficiency of TMEM260 protein. Additionally, Act D experiments (Fig. 4M) demonstrated that TMEM260 mRNA expression declined over time, indicating that Act D effectively inhibited the synthesis of new mRNA. In contrast, the degradation rate of TMEM260 mRNA was significantly accelerated in the si-KANK4 group, while the KANK4 overexpression group exhibited a slower mRNA degradation trend. These results further suggested that KANK4 influenced the gene and protein expression of TMEM260 by regulating its mRNA stability.

KANK4 Regulates TMEM260 Expression To Alleviate LPS-induced Microglial Activation

Through a series of experiments, we investigated the role of KANK4 in microglial activation and its relationship with TMEM260. CCK-8 assays (Fig. 5A) showed that LPS treatment did not significantly affect microglial cell viability. However, KANK4 overexpression (C4) significantly reduced cell viability, while KANK4 overexpression combined with TMEM260 knockdown (C6, knockdown efficiency shown in Supplementary Fig. 4E-F) resulted in a significant increase in cell viability, suggesting that TMEM260 knockdown could partially restore the reduction in cell viability induced by KANK4 overexpression. qRT-PCR (Fig. 5B) and Western blot (Fig. 5C-D) analyses revealed that KANK4 and TMEM260 gene and protein expression were significantly downregulated in LPS-treated microglial cells. KANK4 overexpression (C4) significantly increased the expression of both KANK4 and TMEM260. In contrast, in the KANK4 overexpression combined with TMEM260 knockdown group (C6), KANK4 expression showed no significant change, but TMEM260 expression was significantly reduced, further confirming that KANK4 regulates TMEM260 expression. ELISA assays (Fig. 5E) showed that the concentrations of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in LPS-treated microglial cells. In the KANK4 overexpression group (C4), the levels of inflammatory cytokines were significantly reduced. However, in the KANK4 overexpression combined with TMEM260 knockdown group (C6), inflammatory cytokine levels were significantly increased, suggesting that TMEM260 is involved in the regulation of microglial inflammation by KANK4. IF staining (Fig. 5F) revealed that the number of Iba-1 (green) and CD11b (red) positive microglial cells was significantly increased in the LPS group, indicating microglial activation. In the KANK4 overexpression group (C4), microglial activation was significantly reduced, while in the KANK4 overexpression combined with TMEM260 knockdown group (C6), microglial activation was elevated. These results suggest that KANK4 alleviates LPS-induced microglial activation and inflammation by regulating TMEM260 expression.

Furthermore, in experiments with TMEM260 overexpression and/or CXCL16 overexpression (Fig. 6L, Supplementary Fig. 5H-I), TMEM260 overexpression led to a significant reduction in CXCL16 expression, but when CXCL16 was co-overexpressed, CXCL16 expression was significantly restored, with no significant impact on TMEM260 expression. IF (Fig. 6N) and inflammatory cytokine analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5 J) showed that in LPS-treated microglial cells, the number of Iba-1 and CD11b positive cells was significantly increased, and the expression of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α was significantly elevated. In contrast, microglial activation was significantly reduced in the TMEM260 overexpression group, and inflammatory cytokine expression was decreased. In the TMEM260 overexpression combined with CXCL16 overexpression group, microglial activation was significantly enhanced, and inflammatory cytokine expression was elevated. In summary, KANK4 regulates CXCL16 expression and glycosylation via TMEM260, thereby influencing microglial activation and the inflammatory response.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the role of KANK4 in the inflammatory process of the CNS based on the SAE mouse model and the LPS-induced microglial inflammation model. The results showed that SAE mice exhibited typical cognitive dysfunction and hippocampal tissue damage, accompanied by enhanced microglial activation and significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). Similar inflammatory response characteristics were also observed in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells. These results are consistent with the pathological features of SAE reported in previous studies, further validating the reliability of our model 22, 23. On this basis, we found that KANK4 was persistently downregulated under inflammatory conditions, suggesting that it may play an important role in regulating microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses, laying the foundation for further exploration of its molecular mechanisms.

The KANK protein family is believed to be involved in the pathological processes of neurodevelopmental disorders and neurological diseases 24, 25. Moreover, KANK4 has been identified as one of the differentially expressed genes associated with COVID-19 infection, indicating that it may play an important role in host immunity, inflammatory responses, and cell signaling regulation 26. In the CNS, microglia are the main executors of innate immunity, and their activation state is often used to assess the degree of neuroinflammation 27, 28. Consistent with previous reports, we detected significant downregulation of KANK4 in the SAE mouse model 12; a similar reduction in KANK4 levels was observed in the LPS-induced BV2 inflammation model. Based on this, we further validated and supplemented the role of KANK4 from a cell-specific perspective using IF double labeling experiments. By overexpressing KANK4 in the hippocampus of SAE mice via adeno-associated virus, we observed that KANK4 predominantly localized in microglia, showing high co-localization with Iba-1, a marker of microglia. In contrast, no obvious co-localization of KANK4 was observed with GFAP (a marker of astrocytes) or NeuN (a marker of neurons). These results support our core hypothesis from both spatial distribution and cell-specific perspectives: the observed KANK4-related effects mainly arise from microglia, rather than changes in neurons or astrocytes, thereby strengthening the interpretation of KANK4 as a “microglia-specific regulatory factor.” The activation state of microglia is usually assessed through two classic markers, Iba-1 and CD11b 29: Iba-1 is a calcium-binding protein widely used for immunohistochemical labeling of microglia in the CNS 30. Additionally, CD11b is a surface adhesion molecule of microglia, whose expression is significantly upregulated upon activation 31. In both the SAE mouse model and the LPS inflammation model established in this study, we detected significant increases in Iba-1 and CD11b levels, as well as a marked increase in microglial cell numbers, in line with the commonly used definition standards of microglial activation 32. As the immune sentinels of the CNS, microglia are rapidly activated by pathological stimuli and participate in the regulation and amplification of neuroinflammation through the secretion of various pro-inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-23, and IL-18) 33. In this study, the expression levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly elevated, further validating the excessive activation state of microglia in both the SAE mouse model and the LPS inflammation model.

Although previous studies have generally suggested that CLP-induced neuroinflammation follows a temporal pattern characterized by a pronounced acute-phase activation followed by a gradual resolution, with microglia being rapidly activated within hours to days after surgery and tending to subside within 1–2 weeks 34, 35, this process does not represent a simple, linear decay. Instead, accumulating evidence indicates that the central immune response following CLP exhibits substantial temporal heterogeneity and region-specificity, resulting in complex spatiotemporal dynamics under different experimental conditions. In recent years, several studies have reported persistent neuroinflammatory signals at relatively late time points. For instance, Giridharan et al. demonstrated that elevated levels of glial-associated markers and inflammatory mediators were still detectable in the brain at 10 days after CLP 36, suggesting that central immune activation may persist during the sepsis survival phase. Similarly, studies focusing on HMGB1 assessed behavioral and histological alterations at 14–18 days post-CLP and collected brain tissue for biochemical analyses at day 19, revealing sustained microglial activation in the hippocampus concomitant with cognitive impairment 37. Collectively, these findings are not inconsistent with our observation of increased Iba-1 and CD11b expression and sustained upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus at 20 days after CLP; rather, they support the existence of a delayed or persistent window of neuroimmune activation in SAE/CLP models, rather than a complete resolution following the acute inflammatory phase.

In this study, we selected TMEM260 as the primary target for investigating KANK4, based on several considerations. First, KANK4 is considered a non-classical RNA-binding protein, and its function within the cell remains not fully understood. Although KANK4 is known to interact with several proteins, such as NOL8 [38] and KIAA1429 [39–41], which are RNA-binding proteins, it is unclear whether KANK4 itself possesses similar RNA-binding functionality. Therefore, we conducted an initial prediction using the RNAct database, which suggested that KANK4 may interact with TMEM260 mRNA. To further validate this hypothesis, we incorporated RPISeq prediction results, which indicated a high probability of KANK4 binding to TMEM260 mRNA, further supporting KANK4’s potential as an RNA-binding protein. Based on these predictions, we decided to focus on TMEM260 for further investigation. We first directly validated the interaction between KANK4 and TMEM260 mRNA using RNA pull-down and RIP assays. We then directly verified this interaction through RNA pull-down and RIP experiments. Functional results showed that this binding significantly delayed the degradation of TMEM260 mRNA: after transcription termination induced by Act D, the half-life of TMEM260 mRNA was significantly prolonged in the KANK4 overexpression group, while it was accelerated in the KANK4 knockdown group. Meanwhile, puromycin incorporation experiments confirmed that the translation rate of TMEM260 remained unchanged, regardless of whether KANK4 was upregulated or knocked down, ruling out changes in translation efficiency. Functional studies demonstrated that KANK4 plays an important role in regulating microglial activation by modulating the expression of TMEM260. Overexpression of KANK4 not only upregulated the mRNA and protein levels of TMEM260 but also significantly inhibited LPS-induced microglial activation, as indicated by reduced expression of Iba-1 and CD11b and decreased release of inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α). Conversely, when TMEM260 was interfered with, this inhibitory effect was partially reversed. In summary, KANK4 increases the stability of TMEM260 mRNA by directly binding to it, thereby upregulating TMEM260 protein levels and participating in the regulation of inflammatory signaling. This “stability-dependent” rather than “translation-dependent” regulatory mechanism has not been reported before and is of significant novelty.

Using the BioGRID database to search for proteins interacting with TMEM260, we found that, aside from CXCL16, other molecules have not been widely reported in studies related to microglial cell activation. Based on the correlation between CXCL16 and microglial cell activation, as well as the immunological functions of CXCL16 [17, 42], we selected CXCL16 as the target for further investigation. Recent studies have identified TMEM260 as a substrate-specific O-mannosyltransferase capable of O-Man modification on the extracellular domain of transmembrane proteins 13. CXCL16, itself a transmembrane chemokine, contains a typical CXC chemokine fold and a mucin-like region rich in Ser/Thr, which provides potential receptor sites for O-glycosylation 43, 44. Combining the interaction information from the database and structural features, we hypothesize that TMEM260 may regulate the protein stability and functional state of CXCL16 by directly binding to and O-mannosylating its mucin-like region. To verify the interaction between TMEM260 and CXCL16 and its functional role, we performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments and found that TMEM260 directly interacts with CXCL16 in cells, indicating a physical interaction between the two. This finding supports the hypothesis that TMEM260 regulates CXCL16 stability through glycosylation, thereby modulating microglial immune responses. Furthermore, based on predictions from the NetOGlyc-4.0 database, we constructed mutant plasmids for the glycosylation sites of CXCL16 and performed functional validation. The results revealed that the S117 site of CXCL16 is a critical glycosylation site, and glycosylation at this site is crucial for CXCL16’s regulation of microglial cytokine release. In the SAE mouse model, we observed a significant increase in the O-mannose glycosylation of CXCL16, which was consistent with the activation of microglia and increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, further emphasizing the important role of glycosylation in immune regulation. TMEM260 regulates CXCL16 stability and function by mediating its O-mannose glycosylation, thereby influencing microglial immune responses. Additionally, these findings provide new insights into the role of glycosylation in neuroimmune responses, suggesting that glycosylation plays a key role in more complex immune regulatory networks. From a clinical perspective, the regulatory effect of TMEM260 on CXCL16 glycosylation provides a potential target for designing new immune intervention strategies, particularly in neuroinflammatory diseases such as SAE, and may offer new therapeutic strategies for targeting microglia.

In summary, KANK4, TMEM260, and CXCL16 form a complete regulatory chain that spans from RNA stability to post-translational modifications and functional protein output. While much research has focused on the roles of receptor-ligand interactions and transcriptional networks in microglial polarization, our data suggest the existence of a neglected “third layer of regulation”—a post-transcriptional and post-translational coupling module involving RNA-binding protein (KANK4)-mRNA stability-protein glycosylation. KANK4 delays the degradation of TMEM260 mRNA, further influencing the O-mannosylation and protein fate of CXCL16. This linkage connects two traditionally separate pathways—RNA metabolism and glycan modification.

Although this study provides important insights into the role of KANK4 in microglial cells, there are still some limitations. First, whether KANK4 is specifically and highly expressed in microglial cells needs to be further validated using larger-scale single-cell transcriptomic datasets. Although our immunofluorescence results show that KANK4 expression is low in neurons and astrocytes, we cannot completely rule out its potential function in other brain cell types. Therefore, future studies should further explore the expression of KANK4 in different brain cell populations and assess its potential impact on the neural microenvironment. Second, although we have studied the O-mannosylation of CXCL16 and constructed glycosylation site mutants, this study has not yet directly confirmed the specific catalytic function of TMEM260 toward CXCL16 through in vitro enzymatic activity assays (such as using purified TMEM260 protein and CXCL16 substrate). Therefore, further experimental validation is still required to determine the precise enzymatic mechanisms of these glycosylation modifications and their functional significance in neuroinflammation. Additionally, while immunostaining results show that AAV-KANK4 primarily localizes to microglial cells, we enhanced cell specificity using the Iba-1 promoter, but we cannot fully exclude the possibility of low-level viral infection in other glial cells or neurons. To further improve targeting precision, future studies could consider using more specific microglial cell promoters (e.g., TMEM119 or P2RY12), or employing dual recombinase systems (such as Cre-LoxP) to achieve more precise gene regulation in microglial cells. Finally, the upstream mechanisms underlying the downregulation of KANK4 expression remain unclear, and may involve transcriptional regulation, epigenetic modifications, or protein degradation pathways. These potential regulatory mechanisms need to be further explored in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a novel KANK4-TMEM260-CXCL16 axis involved in microglial activation and neuroinflammation, uncovering a potential neuroprotective role for KANK4 in CNS inflammatory diseases. This provides a foundation for targeted therapies aimed at neuroinflammation.

Clinical Significance and Future Research Directions

This study reveals the important role of the KANK4-TMEM260-CXCL16 axis in microglial activation and neuroinflammation, providing a new potential target for the treatment of neuroinflammatory diseases such as sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE). We found that KANK4 regulates TMEM260, which in turn affects the glycosylation modification of CXCL16, a process that plays a key role in microglial immune responses. Therefore, targeting the KANK4-TMEM260-CXCL16 axis may offer a new strategy for modulating neuroinflammation and inhibiting excessive microglial activation.

Future studies should further explore the clinical application of this axis, particularly the development of therapies targeting KANK4 or CXCL16 glycosylation. Additionally, research should extend to other neuroinflammatory diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, to evaluate the role of this regulatory axis in different conditions. Through validation in preclinical models and clinical samples, this discovery may be translated into effective therapeutic strategies, advancing the treatment of neuroinflammatory diseases.
