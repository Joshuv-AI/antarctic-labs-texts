Mycobiome Simplification in Wheat Is Associated With the Pathogen Parastagonospora nodorum
Authors: Xavious J. Allen, Christina Cowger, Gina Brown‐Guedira, Christine V. Hawkes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant mycobiomes are essential to plant health, yet their assembly under biotic stressors such as pathogen infection remains poorly understood. Plant pathogens can influence microbial community composition through direct antagonism and suppression of host immune responses, potentially altering mycobiome composition in ways that could affect plant performance. We investigated how the wheat ( Triticum aestivum L.) foliar mycobiome was associated with inoculation with the fungal pathogen Parastagonospora nodorum (Berk.) Quaedvlieg, Verkley & Crous, which can cause substantial loss of yield and grain density throughout its range. To address this, we studied inoculation effects in four wheat cultivars planted in a randomized block design at two North Carolina field sites. We used ITS amplicon metagenomics to characterize wheat mycobiome richness, composition, and structure. We found that P. nodorum inoculation reduced fungal richness by up to 38.5%. We also found simplified foliar fungal networks for plants inoculated with P. nodorum, with up to 13.1% fewer taxa present and up to 41.2% fewer associations among those taxa. As part of these changes, increasing P. nodorum absolute abundance was correlated with increasing proportional representation of pathogens in wheat leaves due to loss of non‐pathogenic taxa. Fewer fungal taxa and reduced network connectivity were particularly evident in reportedly susceptible cultivars and at one of the two sites where conditions favoured pathogen success. Based on these results, we suggest that pathogen infection plays a significant role in mycobiome assembly and has implications for disease management and mycobiome‐based interventions in agricultural systems.

Introduction

The mycobiomes of plants play a pivotal role in plant performance and are considered a promising tool for agriculture, but future efforts to actively manage them will require an understanding of how those mycobiomes assemble. Abiotic stressors such as drought can select for beneficial microbial communities, resulting in enrichment of several bacterial strains that are known to promote plant growth (Bechtold et al. 2021). However, we know substantially less about how biotic stressors—such as pathogens—alter the plant mycobiome. Given the effects that pathogens have on host plants, pathogenic taxa may be a key component of overall plant mycobiome community assembly.

Multiple virulence strategies employed by pathogens are likely to affect the success of other microbial taxa. Fungal pathogens can directly interact with other fungi (Agler et al. 2016), shift plant metabolite profiles (Kudjordjie et al. 2022), and both trigger and suppress host immune responses (Veselova et al. 2021). Suppression of host pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI), in particular, is a potentially important factor in the assembly of the plant mycobiome because PTI serves to restrict microbial colonization of plant tissues (de Lamo et al. 2021). Microorganisms that successfully colonize the host must suppress or evade the host immune response, as observed for Cladosporium fulvum, a fungal pathogen of tomato (van den Burg et al. 2006). Pathogen‐mediated suppression of immune responses could present an opportunity for other microbial taxa to colonize and grow within the plant that otherwise might be unable to do so. This includes enabling other pathogenic microbes, as pathogen coinfection has been observed in multiple pathosystems (Gao et al. 2021).

Alternatively, pathogens can negatively affect other plant‐associated microorganisms, resulting in a loss of microbial diversity that may have cascading effects on plant health. For example, the presence of Zymoseptoria tritici (Desm.) on wheat leaves was associated with reductions in bacterial richness of a resistant cultivar and promoted coinfection of Pseudomonas syringae in a susceptible cultivar (Seybold et al. 2020). Similarly, Fusarium wilt disease caused significant reductions in both fungal and bacterial richness on pepper plants (Gao et al. 2021). Increases in microbial diversity and abundance are thought to support functional redundancy and complementarity in ecological roles (Bittleston 2024), including pathogen control (Yang et al. 2017). This loss of microbial diversity and abundance may further disrupt the plant's natural defence mechanisms, potentially increasing susceptibility to infection and impacting overall plant health.

We hypothesized that the mycobiomes of plants infected by a fungal pathogen would be different from those of uninfected plants, with reduced fungal richness, shifts in composition that favoured other pathogenic taxa, and a breakdown in structure resulting in fewer, weaker connections in the fungal community network. To address these hypotheses, we asked how field inoculation with Parastagonospora nodorum (Berk.) Quaedvlieg, Verkley & Crous (Quaedvlieg et al. 2013)—the causal agent of Septoria nodorum blotch (SNB)—affected the richness, composition, and structure of the foliar fungal mycobiome in four wheat cultivars. We repeated the inoculations at two common garden locations that varied in soil and climate. Resulting fungal communities were characterised using ITS amplicon sequencing. To assess effects on fungal richness and composition, we analysed alpha and beta diversity and the abundance of pathogenic taxa as a function of the three treatments: P. nodorum inoculation, common garden location, and cultivar. To examine changes in fungal community structure, we compared network topologies across these treatments. Finally, we asked whether the abundance of P. nodorum predicted the relative abundance of other fungal pathogens.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

We grew four winter wheat cultivars with and without pathogen inoculation at two common garden sites in North Carolina in 2020–2021. The sites were located at the USDA‐ARS MidPines Research Station in Wake (35.7325846, −78.7096646) and the NCSU‐NCDACS Tidewater Research Station in Washington (35.849739, −76.659121) counties, which differ in climate and soil type (Table S1). We will refer to the sites by their county name hereafter. Wake is at the edge of the Piedmont region in central North Carolina and has clay‐rich soil, while Washington is in the eastern Tidewater region of the state and has dark, mineral organic soil.

The wheat cultivars were chosen for their varied growth characteristics and resistance to P. nodorum, including three soft red winter wheat varieties, Hilliard, Shirley, and USG3640, and one hard red winter wheat variety, Catawba (Table S2). The cultivars had varying levels of resistance to P. nodorum in leaves and glumes, with USG3640 being considered the most susceptible followed by Catawba. Both Hilliard and Shirley are considered moderately resistant to SNB (Table S2). The pathogen, P. nodorum, is a narrow‐range necrotrophic fungal pathogen of wheat that infects leaves, stems, and glumes, and is prevalent in Australia, Canada, Central and Eastern Europe, the eastern United States, and South America (Downie et al. 2021). Parastagonospora nodorum causes SNB in wheat through the secretion of necrotrophic effector proteins that target host susceptibility genes and trigger plant cell death. However, wheat grown in the eastern United States has durable quantitative resistance to SNB, making outbreaks infrequent (Cowger et al. 2020). SNB is rarely the dominant disease in a population and often occurs with other diseases caused by necrotrophic fungal pathogens such as septoria tritici blotch (Zymoseptoria tritici) and tan spot (Pyrenophora tritici‐repentis) (Downie et al. 2021).

We grew each wheat cultivar in 1.3 × 1.3 m plots at the two field sites in a randomised complete block design with five replicate blocks. At the Wake site, replicate blocks were 6.1 m wide and 7.3 m long. At the Washington site, replicate blocks were 1.3 m wide and 36.6 m long. The blocks included plots with every combination of cultivar and inoculation treatments (4 cultivars × 2 inoculation treatments × 5 blocks × 2 sites = 80 total plots). Four of the eight plots in each block were inoculated with P. nodorum. Plots designated for pathogen treatment were inoculated by applying wheat straw infected with naturally occurring P. nodorum (Cowger and Murphy 2007) just prior to stem elongation (~Zadoks 30). Uninoculated plots received no straw. Large‐droplet irrigation was applied in Wake, although not in Washington, from boot to early grain‐fill stages to promote SNB development and spread to upper plant parts. To create a barrier between inoculated and uninoculated wheat, the wheat plots were arranged in a checkerboard design interspersed with plots of barley because it is not a host for the wheat‐adapted P. nodorum biotype (Adhikari et al. 2023). Experimental plots at the Washington site were centred in the blocks with additional barley plots on both ends. One replicate block at the Wake site and two plots at the Washington site were dropped due to incorrect sowing and failure to germinate, respectively.

To characterise the foliar mycobiome of wheat, each plot was sampled during anthesis (Zadoks 61–69) by clipping flag leaves with no signs of disease from four randomly selected plants. The leaves were immediately flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept on dry ice before transport to the lab, where they were stored at −80°C until processing for DNA extraction.

DNA Extraction

Leaf samples were lyophilized for 48 h (Freezone Freeze Dryer, LABCONCO, Kansas City, MO, USA) and ground to powder by beating with two 5‐mm chrome‐steel beads for 30 s twice, freezing in liquid nitrogen between each beating (GenoGrinder, Cole‐Parmer, Metuchen, NJ, USA). We extracted DNA from 10 mg of lyophilized, ground leaf tissue with Mag Bind Plant DNA DS kits (Omega Bio‐Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) run on a BioSprint 96 workstation (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). As whole leaves were processed, the entire foliar mycobiome was captured.

Amplicon Sequencing Library Preparation

Library preparation followed the Illumina ITS Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation workflow using half reactions (25 μL). A set of three synthetic sequences (Palmer et al. 2018) was used as spike‐in standards to estimate absolute fungal abundance as total ITS copies per mg of extracted DNA. We added 0.5 fg of each internal standard (400 copies) to each sample prior to the first round of PCR. To characterise fungal communities, ITS rDNA was amplified with primers ITS1‐F_KYO2 (5′‐TAGAGGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAA‐3′) and ITS86R (5′‐TTCAAAGATTCGATGATTCAC‐3′) (Scibetta et al. 2018), both modified with Illumina adapters. PCR reactions included 1 × KAPA HiFi HS Ready Mix (Roche, Pleasanton, CA, USA), 0.2 μM of each primer, 8 ng μL−1 of DNA template, and water up to 25 μL. Amplicon fragments < 50 bp in size were removed using Agencourt AMpure XP beads in a ratio of 1:1.25 (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Libraries were normalized to 20 ng μL−1 and pooled. Extractions with < 3 ng μL−1 DNA were run with two extra technical replicates to ensure good coverage. Both negative (sterile water) and positive (P. nodorum biomass) controls were also included. Pooled libraries were diluted to 3.5 ng μL−1 and sequenced on Illumina MiSeq (v2) 2 × 250 PE by the Genomic Sciences Laboratory at North Carolina State University (Raleigh, NC, USA).

Bioinformatics Analysis

All bioinformatics and statistics were carried out with R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024) in RStudio (Posit Team 2024) and code is available in Dryad (Allen and Hawkes 2026). The average number of reads per sample was 13,441. We removed primers with cutadapt v4.5 (Martin 2011) and performed quality control and read processing with dada2 v1.32.0 (Callahan et al. 2016), with a truncation length of 230 bp and otherwise default settings. Post‐clustering curation on the resulting amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was done with lulu v0.1 using default settings (Frøslev et al. 2017). Quality control and curation resulted in 6093 reads per sample, which was sufficient to capture foliar fungal diversity in all samples (Figure S1). To estimate the absolute abundance of ASV amplicons, we divided, per sample, the observed read count of each ASV by the total read count of the spike‐in sequences and then multiplied the result by the total number of spike‐in fragments (i.e., the absolute abundance of spike‐in sequences) per microgram of DNA template added to each library.

Taxonomic assignments of ASVs were made using phylogenetic tree assignments obtained from the Tree‐Based Alignment Selector (T‐BAS) toolkit (Carbone et al. 2017). ASV guild assignments (animal parasite, foliar endophyte, lichen parasite, mycoparasite, plant pathogen, saprotroph, sooty mould) were made at the genus level and were based on the FungalTraits database v1.2 (Põlme et al. 2020). The saprotroph assignment included all saprotroph guilds (dung, litter, pollen, soil, wood, and unspecified).

Statistical Analysis

To address the hypothesis that P. nodorum reduces fungal richness, we calculated alpha diversity as observed richness using phyloseq v1.48.0 (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). We then used a mixed‐effects ANOVA with Type III SS (lmerTest v3.1–3; Kuznetsova et al. 2017) to examine how fungal richness varied as a function of inoculation treatment, cultivar, site, block, and their interactions. Block was considered a random effect, whereas inoculation and cultivar were fixed effects. Denominator degrees of freedom were calculated using the Kenward–Roger approximation method, which matches the mean and variance of a scaled Wald statistic to an approximated F distribution (Kenward and Roger 2009). Any significant interactions were investigated with one‐way ANOVA. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment was used to control the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Benjamini and Liu 1999).

We similarly addressed shifts in composition using the same model, but with PERMANOVA on the Bray‐Curtis community dissimilarity matrix using residual randomization in permutation procedure (RRPP v2.0.3; Collyer and Adams 2018). Compositional results were visualized with non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) calculated from the Bray‐Curtis community dissimilarity matrix (phyloseq v1.48.0; McMurdie and Holmes 2013). To compare changes in estimated absolute abundance at the genus level, we performed a differential abundance analysis on ASVs agglomerated to genera using DESeq2 v1.44.0 (Love et al. 2014). Because estimated absolute abundances were used, the differential abundance analysis was completed without count normalisation.

To address the hypothesis that pathogen inoculation would result in a breakdown of microbial community structure, we generated association networks using the Semi‐Parametric Rank‐based approach for INference in Graphical model (SPRING) (Yoon et al. 2019) using NetCoMi v1.1 (Peschel et al. 2021). SPRING was chosen due to its ability to analyse quantitative microbiome data. To reduce noise and minimize spurious connections, we limited the network analysis to taxa found in at least 5% of all plots (4/80). We used a StARS stability threshold value of 0.17 to maximize network stability while retaining P. nodorum in the resulting network (Liu et al. 2010). Each cultivar‐specific network included data from 8 to 9 samples and each site‐specific network included 30 samples (Wake) or 38 samples (Washington). Cytoscape (Shannon et al. 2003) was used to visualize the SPRING networks and calculate network statistics. Network connectivity was used to assess deterioration in community structure, which was indicated by reductions in the number of nodes, number of edges, and average node degree.

To examine whether P. nodorum abundance predicted the proportional abundance of other pathogenic taxa, we used beta regression analysis (betareg v3.2–1) (Cribari‐Neto and Zeileis 2010). The proportional abundance of taxa identified as pathogenic (excluding P. nodorum) was calculated per sample by averaging the estimated absolute abundances of plant pathogenic ASVs and dividing by the sum of estimated absolute abundances of all ASVs. The proportion data were log transformed to achieve normality. To investigate which factors contributed to changes in proportional abundance of plant pathogens, we performed a differential abundance analysis on all ASVs using DESeq2 without count normalisation. Abundances of all ASVs were compared between inoculated and uninoculated plots for each cultivar within each site. ASVs were categorized as ‘gained’ (absent in uninoculated plots and present in inoculated plots), ‘increased’ (more abundant in inoculated plots), ‘decreased’ (less abundant in inoculated plots), and ‘lost’ (present in uninoculated plots and absent in inoculated plots).

Results

Alpha Diversity

Fungal species richness ranged from 5 to 80 ASVs per sample, with an average of 34 per sample. There was a significant interaction of treatment with cultivar (p = 0.028, Table S3) reflecting 38.5% lower fungal richness in USG3640 plants inoculated with P. nodorum vs. uninoculated plants (Figure 1A). In addition, P. nodorum inoculation decreased foliar fungal richness in Catwaba compared to Shirley (p = 0.021), although the uninoculated cultivars did not differ (Figure 1A). There was also a cultivar‐by‐site interaction (p = 0.017, Table S3) driven by lower species richness in Catawba and Hilliard compared to Shirley and USG3640 at Washington, whereas at Wake cultivars did not differ in fungal species richness (Figure 1B).

Fungal Community Composition

The P. nodorum inoculation treatment did not change overall mycobiome composition (Table S4, Figure 2). Instead, the composition of foliar fungi depended on site (R 2 = 0.031, p = 0.002), with an overall shift in dominance from Cladosporium at Wake (Figure 3A) to Puccinia at Washington (Figure 3B). There was also an effect of cultivar (R 2 = 0.026, p = 0.019) and an interaction of site by cultivar (R 2 = 0.034, p = 0.002; Table S4; Figure 2) on fungal community composition that at least partly reflected cultivar resistance to site‐specific pathogens. At Wake, the mycobiomes of Catawba plants were significantly different from those of other cultivars (all p ≤ 0.021), with 100× lower absolute abundance of Udeniomyces compared to all other cultivars (all p ≤ 0.049) and 8× greater absolute abundance of Blumeria (p = 0.049) compared to USG3640 (Figure 3A). We observed a similar pattern at Washington, where the mycobiome compositions of Catawba and Hilliard plants were significantly different from those of Shirley and USG3640. This is illustrated by differences in the abundance of Puccinia, which was 30× less abundant in rust‐resistant USG3640 compared to other cultivars (all p < 0.001). Between sites, mycobiome composition was significantly different for all cultivars (all p < 0.002).

Fungal Community Structure

Inoculation with P. nodorum resulted in simpler microbial association networks with fewer, weaker connections (Figure 4). At the Wake site (Figure 4A,B), inoculation resulted in 6.7% fewer nodes (ASVs), 8.1% fewer edges, and a 6.6% decline in average node degree in plots inoculated with P. nodorum compared to uninoculated controls. Network simplification was more severe at the Washington site (Figure 4C,D), where the number of nodes was reduced by 13.1%, the number of edges was reduced by 41.2%, and average node degree declined by 33.5% in the inoculated plots.

At the cultivar level, network simplification increased with host pathogen susceptibility. Catawba and USG3640, the two considered to be most susceptible to SNB, had the most severe network simplification, with a reduction in the number of taxa (nodes) in the network (30.0% and 58.1%, respectively) and reduced connectedness in terms of both edges (46.7% and 82.0%, respectively) and average node degree (24.1% and 49.0%, respectively; Figure 5, Table S5). Hilliard, which is reported to be moderately resistant to SNB, followed the same trend but was less severe: 9.6% fewer nodes, 34.2% fewer edges, and a 24.5% reduction in average node degree (Figure 5, Table S5). The cultivar Shirley was the exception to the trend, as this reportedly moderately SNB‐resistant cultivar was associated with both increased nodes (33.3%) and edges (29.2%) with SNB inoculation, while having no effect on average node degree (Figure 5, Table S5).

Parastagonospora Abundance Effects on Fungal Diversity

The abundance of P. nodorum was 93% and 113% higher after inoculation at Wake and Washington, respectively (Figure 6A), but was present in the majority (80%) of samples regardless of whether or not the sampled plots were inoculated with P. nodorum and did not differ significantly between them (p = 0.088). We asked whether the abundance of this pathogen was related to the abundance of other pathogens. Because of the different overall pathogen abundances at Wake and Washington, we analysed this relationship separately for the two sites. We found that P. nodorum abundance was positively correlated with the proportion of taxa identified as pathogenic regardless of site, but the relationship was stronger at Washington (β = 0.110, Pseudo R 2 = 0.214, p = 0.008) compared to Wake (β = 0.083, Pseudo R 2 = 0.142, p = 0.027).

Parastagonospora nodorum abundance was calculated as the number of ITS1 rRNA gene copies per microgram of total DNA input. (B) Linear relationship between proportion of plant‐pathogenic taxa and P. nodorum abundance (log transformed) in the wheat flag leaf.

To better understand the change in proportional abundance of pathogens, we identified taxa that were differentially abundant between inoculation and control treatments. Only 12% of fungal taxa at each site significantly increased or decreased in abundance in plants subjected to P. nodorum inoculation compared to uninoculated plants and just 3.5%–5% of all fungi at each site were pathogens that significantly differed in abundance (Figure 7). In all but one case, a significant change in abundance meant a change in presence as well, so that taxa were either gained or lost from the inoculated plots. Of the taxa that responded to P. nodorum inoculation, 65% were considered non‐pathogenic and 35% were pathogenic, but responses varied substantially between sites and cultivars. Among non‐pathogenic, differentially abundant fungi, 18 responded only in one cultivar in one site (9 losses, 9 gains), 7 were consistently lost and 4 consistently gained in at least 2 cultivars or sites, and 18 had mixed responses. Fungal taxa identified as pathogenic were similar, with 9 responding to inoculation only in one condition (5 losses, 4 gains), 3 consistently lost from at least 2 cultivars or sites, and mixed responses in the remaining 13 (Table S6).

Nevertheless, there were some patterns across sites and cultivars (Table S6, Figure 7). At Wake, an equal number of fungal taxa (15) were gained or lost in the inoculated plots (Figure 7A), whereas at Washington, 18 fungal taxa were lost and only nine taxa were gained overall (Figure 7B). Although most of the responsive taxa were non‐pathogenic, 27% of taxa gained and 53% of those lost were identified as pathogenic at Wake, as were 44% gained and 17% lost at Washington. For example, several fungi that decreased with P. nodorum inoculation at Wake were known wheat pathogens: Puccinia triticina (ASV 10), Ustilago tritici (ASV 19), and Neoascochyta graminicola (ASV 57) (Table 1, Table S6). The most abundant ASV at Washington, Puccinia triticina (ASV 1; Table 1), increased by 183% with inoculation at this site.

Discussion

In line with our hypotheses, P. nodorum inoculation was associated with changes in leaf community structure in a cultivar‐ and site‐dependent manner, and generally resulted in simpler, less diverse, and less connected fungal networks with increased proportional abundance of pathogenic taxa. This was true even though at the time of sampling there was no evidence of disease on the sampled leaves. Such cascading effects of an individual pathogen on other fungi suggest that there are broader ecological consequences of pathogen infection beyond direct host damage. Plant pathogens may shape the host mycobiome to facilitate their own infections, and other pathogens may also respond to this manipulation—our results are consistent with the growing recognition that pathogens may have indirect impacts via the mycobiome (Abdullah et al. 2017; Martínez‐Arias et al. 2023; Rojas et al. 2020).

The effects of P. nodorum were broadly visible in the simplification of foliar fungal community structure, with fewer nodes and fewer connections in network analysis indicating loss of fungal species and less frequent co‐occurrence of the remaining taxa. This simplification of structure occurred at both sites, though the effect was more pronounced at Washington where P. nodorum was more abundant. The decline in fungal richness was largely driven by the two cultivars considered to be more susceptible to SNB, USG3640 and Catawba (Griffey et al. 2020, 2010). A similar effect has been observed in tobacco, where target spot, caused by Rhizoctonia solani, reduced microbial alpha diversity of more severely infected leaves compared to less diseased leaves (Sun et al. 2023).

In contrast, Shirley showed the least breakdown in mycobiome networks when inoculated with P. nodorum, possibly due to higher resistance to leaf blotch (Kaur et al. 2024). Shirley's response to P. nodorum inoculation may be due to earlier activation of defence responses compared to the other cultivars, as seen in tomato where defence responses against a Ralstonia solanacearum strain were similar between resistant and susceptible cultivars, but occurred earlier in the resistant cultivar (Milling et al. 2011). Timing differences in defence responses potentially delay establishment of pathogenic taxa, allowing other taxa to establish first and compete. Alternatively, Shirley, as a cultivar resistant to multiple diseases caused by plant pathogens common to the southeastern United States (Griffey et al. 2010), may facilitate the success of non‐pathogenic leaf fungi that compete with P. nodorum or enhance host defence responses, as observed previously in the rhizosphere of disease resistant banana (Liu et al. 2023). The host plant is known to act as a filter in mycobiome assembly (Xiong et al. 2021) and host genotype is expected to influence the mycobiome (Bashir et al. 2022). Although cultivar filtering is often minor (Bashir et al. 2022; Whitaker et al. 2018; Xiong et al. 2021), differences created by breeding for pathogen resistance may magnify these effects. Our results suggest a role for plant host resistance in how pathogens are associated with overall mycobiome structure, and further corroborate a recent study showing that disease‐resistant wheat cultivars have more diverse foliar fungi and more stable co‐occurrence networks compared to susceptible cultivars exposed to yellow mosaic virus (Wu et al. 2025).

Based on our findings, inoculation with P. nodorum is positively correlated with the proportional abundance of other leaf pathogens. However, only ~1% of all observed fungal taxa were pathogens that increased in abundance with P. nodorum, and these responses were rarely consistent either between sites or across cultivars. The diversity and heterogeneity of pathogenic taxa responding positively to inoculation suggest that these are largely opportunistic occurrences that may not be easily predictable. A good illustration of this is Puccinia triticina, a biotrophic pathogen that causes wheat leaf rust and was significantly differentially abundant depending on P. nodorum inoculation at both sites. At Washington, which is known for regular rust epidemics, P. triticina was the most abundant ASV on average and increased by 183% in plots inoculated with P. nodorum relative to control plots, including a significant increase on the rust‐resistant cultivar Shirley. In contrast, after P. nodorum inoculation at Wake, where P. triticina was present in only 31% of samples, the latter pathogen was lost from the moderately rust‐resistant cultivar Catawba and responses on Shirley were strain‐specific—ASV 1 increased while ASV 10 decreased. We observed a similar dependence on cultivar and site for two strains of Neoascochyta sp. (ASV56, ASV57) that can cause leaf scorch on wheat (Golzar et al. 2019). Such opportunistic infections may occur when one fungus benefits from resources or conditions generated by the effects of another. This type of opportunistic exploitation was previously reported to occur between Pyrenophora tritici‐repentis (tan spot of wheat) and P. triticina (Al‐Naimi et al. 2005). It is also possible that this increase in pathogens was due to the application of wheat straw, rather than to P. nodorum, as other fungi can sporulate on wheat straw and colonize the plant via the same pathways (Bowen and Harper 1990).

Given the limited and inconsistent increases in pathogenic taxa with inoculation, the correlation between P. nodorum abundance and the proportional abundance of other leaf pathogens is most likely due to losses of non‐pathogenic taxa. In support of this, 4%–6% of all fungi not classified as pathogens were sensitive to inoculation in one or more cultivars, of which 30% were only lost at Wake and 53% were only lost at Washington in plots treated with P. nodorum. The losses of non‐pathogenic taxa occurred primarily in the reported SNB‐susceptible cultivars, Catawba and USG3640 (Table S6). Fungi whose absolute abundances decreased might be unable to compete for space with P. nodorum or other taxa that increased in P. nodorum's presence. Alternatively, some non‐pathogenic fungi may be sensitive to any changes in the wheat host plant resulting from P. nodorum inoculation that were not measured here. A similar effect was observed in Arabidopsis thaliana, where colonization by the pathogen Albugo laibachii had strong negative effects on the diversity of phyllosphere bacteria (Agler et al. 2016). Loss of fungal taxa could potentially lessen any protective effects of the phyllosphere mycobiome if the displaced fungi were beneficial to the host (Rojas et al. 2020; Seybold et al. 2020). Nevertheless, the majority of fungi (88%) were unaffected by inoculation with P. nodorum, suggesting that most foliar fungi are resistant to at least some pathogens, including the associated host responses, and can coexist on or in the leaf.

Many of the taxa identified as pathogens that increased with P. nodorum inoculation are not pathogenic to wheat and may not have any direct effects on the wheat host plant. For example, seven of the ASVs that shifted in abundance with inoculation were known pathogens of trees, likely arriving via wind dispersal from surrounding vegetation. This is consistent with previous work showing a positive correlation between nearby natural vegetation and foliar fungal community diversity of wheat and other crops (Whitaker et al. 2023). These tree pathogens and other fungi classified as saprotrophic may be transients or commensals in wheat, although some might act as beneficial endophytes given the complexity of many fungal life cycles (Collinge et al. 2022). The presence of such fungi alongside host‐adapted pathogenic fungi could have implications for foliar fungal dynamics both currently via ecological interactions and in the future depending on evolutionary processes (e.g., host range expansion) (Inoue et al. 2017).

Pathogen invasion dynamics can be highly dependent on site (Harms et al. 2020), which our results reflect. Plants at Washington had much greater relative P. nodorum abundances (2%–26%; Figure 3F,G) and proportions of plant pathogenic taxa (Figure 6B), which may be caused by differences in climate and soil type. Soil at the Washington site is predominantly hydric Portsmouth fine sandy loam, which experiences regular waterlogging. Soil at Wake, however, is a well‐drained Cecil gravelly sandy loam. Washington also exhibits 10.8% higher mean annual precipitation than Wake (Table S1), an important factor for the spread of many leaf pathogens as rain splash dispersal is a major mechanism of spread throughout the phyllosphere. Taken together, environmental conditions at the Washington site favoured growth and spread of P. nodorum more than did Wake, potentially explaining the difference in abundance. Cladosporium, a saprophyte and plant endophyte (Fisher and Petrini 1992), made up a much larger proportion of total fungal load in plants grown at Wake than at Washington. The most likely explanation was the regular irrigation at the Wake site, applied to ensure sufficient SNB levels for resistance scoring, and not applied at Washington. In addition, the lower pathogen load at Wake could have allowed for Cladosporium and other beneficial or commensal fungi to establish and persist. That said, several strains of other non‐pathogenic genera, such as Cystofilobasidium, Sporobolomyces, and Dioszegia, were present in similar abundance at both sites (Figure 4), likely reflecting taxa that can successfully colonize wheat under a broader range of environmental and biotic conditions.

Despite the clear association of P. nodorum inoculation with wheat foliar fungal mycobiome structure and the subset of the fungal community identified as pathogens, there was no effect of the inoculation treatment on overall fungal community composition. This was likely because the full community included many rare taxa, resulting in a high degree of heterogeneity among blocks and in block interactions with cultivar and treatment, which together explained ~29% of the variation in fungal community composition. Block and block interaction effects on foliar fungal community composition were substantially larger than the effects of site, cultivar, or treatment (R 2 < 3% for each factor). Stochastic processes have been shown to play an important role in plant‐associated mycobiome community assembly in various plant systems, especially over time (Meyer et al. 2022). Nevertheless, stochastic effects are less frequently studied than environmental or host filters, but are perhaps more important in highly disturbed annual agricultural ecosystems where the foliar microbiome depends heavily on landscape context and available sources (Whitaker et al. 2023).

Although we observed large shifts in the foliar fungal mycobiomes of wheat under a range of P. nodorum loads, all interactions in this study are putative based on co‐occurrence and abundances of taxa. We have no insight into the mechanisms underpinning the observed shifts, and additional studies are needed to understand whether these taxa are directly interacting (e.g., Hassall et al. 2021; Shalev et al. 2022). Additionally, low sample size is a point of concern, particularly for the cultivar‐specific networks that were generated with a maximum of just eight samples. Reduced network edge count and node degree can result from small sample size, although SPRING mitigates this somewhat through rank‐based correlation that is robust to distributional problems and regularization with subsampling of provided data to only retain edges that are in the majority of subnetworks (Yoon et al. 2019). SPRING uses a user‐supplied retention threshold to control the sparsity of the resulting network, which we set at 0.17 to maximize network stability. This retention threshold is used by SPRING to determine the optimal value for a penalty parameter to minimize spurious connections and maximize replicability given that the data have many more potential features than samples, a common problem with network analyses (Liu et al. 2010).

There are several other caveats to this work. Despite a trend for increased abundance with P. nodorum inoculation, we did not find a significant difference between uninoculated and inoculated plots at either site. This may be due to spatial heterogeneity in the efficacy of inoculation combined with the natural background population of P. nodorum. Alternatively, our approach to estimating absolute abundance might be inadequate if the synthetic spike‐in standards are prone to PCR bias in ways that differ from P. nodorum. However, we mitigated this by using a mixture of standards (Tourlousse et al. 2017). In addition, cultivar resistance to SNB was based on previous reports for Catawba, Shirley, and Hilliard and personal observations for USG3640 (Table S2). Scores for SNB were not taken in this study, and, as such, associations between disease resistance and P. nodorum effects are putative. Finally, as noted above, our analyses revealed that spatial effects (i.e., block and its interactions) collectively explained twice as much variance in mycobiome composition as treatment, cultivar, and site, which may indicate local variation in fungal source pools or micro‐environmental conditions that affect assembly. Future studies could attempt to account for distributions of source populations in soil and air. Larger plot size and increased replication may also be useful where space is available.

We demonstrated that P. nodorum inoculation is associated with the structure of the wheat foliar mycobiome, destabilizing fungal association networks and shifting the balance of plant pathogenic and non‐pathogenic fungi. While the magnitude of these effects differed across sites and cultivars, our findings support the growing idea that some pathogens may shape microbial communities particularly in cultivars with lower resistance. The potential for pathogens to alter the foliar mycobiome—directly or indirectly—raises important questions for disease management and plant breeding, emphasizing a need for further research on the functional consequences of these mycobiome shifts. As microbial diversity is associated with plant health, mycobiome stability could be incorporated into breeding strategies as a potential indicator for disease resistance. Similarly, synthetic microbial consortia tailored for enhanced stability could be beneficial as field amendments to preemptively protect the native community from perturbation by plant pathogens.
