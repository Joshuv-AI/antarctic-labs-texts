Listing for Pediatric Donation After Circulatory Death Heart Transplantation Is Associated With Improved Waitlist Outcomes
Authors: Michael A. Catalano, Max Shin, Omar Toubat, Radhika Rastogi, Halil Beqaj, Amit Iyengar, Benjamin F. Smood, Nikia Toomey, Jonathan B. Edelson, Joseph W. Rossano, Matthew J. O'Connor, Humera Ahmed, J. William Gaynor, Katsuhide Maeda, Constantine D. Mavroudis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in procurement techniques have enabled use of hearts obtained via donation after circulatory death (DCD), expanding the donor pool; however, the impact of DCD heart transplant on outcomes in children is not well described. We aim to characterize waitlist survival and outcomes in pediatric heart transplant candidates listed for donation after brain death (DBD) versus DCD hearts.MethodsThe United Network for Organ Sharing database was queried for patients aged <18 years at listing, waitlisted for heart transplant between January 2021 and June 2024. Patients were stratified by listing for DBD exclusively, versus DBD and DCD hearts. Waitlist and posttransplant outcomes were compared using Fine–Gray and Kaplan–Meier analyses.ResultsOf 2449 listed patients, 2353 (96.1%) were listed for DBD hearts exclusively, and 96 (3.9%) for DCD hearts as well. DCD recipient candidates had increased rates of cardiopulmonary support and were more likely to be status 1A. Of DCD recipient candidates, 15 (15.6%) received DCD hearts and 50 (52.1%) received DBD hearts. When controlling for status, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, blood type, region, and year, DCD listing was not associated with likelihood of transplant or waitlist death. Among status 1B/2 patients, DCD listing was associated with increased likelihood of transplant (adjusted subhazard ratio, 2.05 [95% CI, 1.16–3.62]; P=0.01). No patients in the status 1B/2 DCD subgroup died while waitlisted. There were no differences in 1‐year posttransplant survival.ConclusionsAmong candidates for pediatric heart transplant listed as status 1B/2, patients listed for DCD hearts in addition to DBD hearts have shorter waitlist duration, improved transplant rates, lower waitlist mortality rates, and comparable survival to patients listed for DBD hearts alone.

Pediatric patients awaiting heart transplantation represent a complex, heterogeneous, and high‐risk population, with a reported waitlist mortality rate of 13% to 21% over the past 3 decades, greatly exceeding that of adult patients. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Efforts have been made to expand the number of hearts available for this population through the use of marginal donors and, more recently, through the reintroduction of donation after circulatory death (DCD) organ donations.

While the first successful human heart transplant performed was procured from a DCD donor, 5 and a series of 3 pediatric DCD heart transplants was successfully performed in Colorado from 2004 to 2007, 6 expansion of the practice in pediatric patients has since been limited by technical and procurement challenges, as well as ethical and legal concerns. 7 , 8 The standard of care in pediatric patients for the past 2 decades has thus been use of donation after brain death (DBD) hearts. However, advances in procurement and perfusion techniques have facilitated the expansion of DCD organ use in adult heart transplantation. There is increasing evidence that the availability of DCD hearts has reduced waitlist times and improved waitlist outcomes among adult patients 9 , 10 , 11 with excellent posttransplant outcomes. 12 , 13 , 14 In this setting, there has been renewed interest among pediatric centers in DCD heart transplant as an option to expand the donor pool and improve waitlist outcomes. Early reports have demonstrated acceptable posttransplant outcomes in DCD heart recipients, 9 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 but the impact of DCD heart availability on waitlist duration and outcomes in children has yet to be described.

In the present study, we sought to evaluate contemporary waitlist and posttransplant outcomes among pediatric patients listed for DCD hearts in the United States, stratified by United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) listing status, as compared with patients listed exclusively for DBD hearts.

Methods

This study was deemed exempt from approval by the Institutional Review Board of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia. The data reported here were supplied by UNOS as the contractor for the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network and are publicly available upon formal request to UNOS. The interpretation and reporting of these data are the responsibility of the authors and in no way should be seen as an official policy of or interpretation by the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network or the US government. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Patient Selection

A retrospective analysis of the UNOS database was performed. The database was queried for all pediatric patients (aged <18 years) waitlisted for orthotopic heart transplant between January 2021 and June 2024. Candidates for multiorgan transplant were excluded. Patients with a prior history of heart transplant were not excluded. The lower cutoff date was chosen to account for the period during which pediatric DCD transplantations regained prevalence in the United States. 9 Patients were stratified by their listing for DCD hearts using the “non–heart‐beating donors” variable; specifically, they were identified as being listed exclusively for a DBD heart (ie, DBD listing) versus being listed for both DBD and DCD hearts (ie, DCD listing).

Notably, patient listing status may change during the waitlist period. Therefore, we also identified changes in donor criteria. Three total groups were ultimately created on the basis of listing status: patients listed for DBD hearts exclusively, patients listed for DCD hearts in addition to DBD hearts at initial listing, and patients listed for DBD hearts at initial listing who later transitioned to accept DCD hearts (ie, “DCD crossover”). To isolate the association of listing status with waitlist outcomes, our primary analyses included only patients listed as DCD at the time of initial registration. A separate subanalysis was conducted to identify differences between patients within DCD listing and patients listed as DCD crossover.

Variables and Outcomes

Baseline characteristics and comorbidities were collected for each patient. This includes age, sex, height, weight, ethnicity, diabetes, estimated glomerular filtration rate, total bilirubin, primary cardiomyopathy, degree of support at time of listing (ie, ventilator, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, inotropic support, durable left ventricular assist device), invasive hemodynamics, and blood group. Status at listing, region of listing, and listing year were also collected. Donor characteristics, including demographics, body mass index, ejection fraction, cause of death, and distance from recipient, were collected. Finally, the procurement technique for DCD hearts was identified as direct procurement and perfusion (DPP) versus normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) on the basis of time between death pronouncement and donor cross clamp. A threshold of >20 minutes was identified as NRP, as has been used in previously published work. 23

Primary end points included waitlist outcome, classified as 1 of the following: (1) death or deterioration requiring delisting; (2) transplantation; (3) delisting due to recovery; (4) removal for other reason; or (5) still waiting, and duration to that outcome. Given that the primary analysis focused on DCD recipient candidacy at initial listing, waitlist duration was quantified as the time from listing to the specified outcome. One‐year posttransplant survival was assessed as a secondary end point.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics at listing were queried and summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or frequency (percentage), as appropriate. Normality of demographic variables was assessed with skewness and kurtosis tests. Student's t test and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were used to compare normally distributed and nonnormally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate for expected cell counts.

Primary analysis of categorized patient waitlist outcomes was modeled with cumulative incidence functions and competing risk regressions via Fine–Gray proportional subhazards models, with censoring of waitlist data at 3 years from initial listing. Models were adjusted according to differences in listing status, listing region, ABO blood group, ventilator support, and temporary mechanical circulatory support. These models were also applied to subgroups of patients within status 1A and 1B/2. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis, where observations with missing values for any variable included in the analysis were excluded.

Kaplan–Meier analyses with a log‐rank test were used to compare posttransplant survival between listing status as well as recipient type with censoring at 1 year. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/IC 14.2 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 2486 patients were listed for heart transplant in the specified time frame, including 2353 of 2486 (94.7%) DBD recipient candidates and 133 of 2486 (5.4%) DCD recipient candidates. Of the identified DCD recipient candidates, 96 of 133 (72.2%) were offered access to DCD hearts at time of listing, whereas 37 of 133 (27.8%) patients were identified as DCD crossover. Exclusion of patients listed as DCD crossover left a final sample of 2449 patients (DBD, 2353/2449 [96.1%]; DCD: 96/2449 [3.9%]). Waitlist Outcomes

Waitlist outcomes and corresponding median waitlist durations, for the entire cohort and the subgroups of status 1A and status 1B/2, are presented in Table 2. Of candidates listed for DBD hearts alone, 1504 of 2353 (63.9%) underwent transplant and 227 of 2353 (9.7%) died on the waitlist. Of candidates listed for DCD hearts, 65 of 96 (67.7%) underwent transplant, and 14 of 96 (14.6%) died on the waitlist. There was no difference in likelihood of any waitlist outcome in the overall cohort, nor in the 2 subgroups. However, overall waitlist durations were significantly shorter among DCD recipient candidates (63 [IQR, 15–143] days versus 100 [IQR, 34–204] days; P<0.01), driven by shorter waitlist duration among transplanted patients (42 [IQR, 11–88] days versus 84 [IQR, 25–166] days; P<0.01). There was no difference in waitlist durations among the subgroup of patients within status 1A; however, among patients within status 1B/2, DCD listing was associated with a reduced waitlist duration in the overall cohort (50 [IQR, 18.5–255] days versus 165 [IQR, 65–383] days; P<0.01) and among heart recipients (34 [IQR, 15–82] days versus 112 [IQR, 44–248] days; P<0.01). To further stratify these findings, outcomes of status 1B and status 2 groups were assessed individually; findings of this analysis suggest that the differential outcomes of patients within status 1B are the primary drivers of this observed difference (Tables S1–S7).

Posttransplant Outcomes

A total of 1569 patients received transplants. Among the DCD recipient candidates who underwent transplantation, 15 of 65 (23.1%) received hearts from DCD donors, whereas 50 of 65 (76.9%) received hearts from DBD donors. Of the 15 DCD heart transplants performed, 10 of 15 (66.7%) went to patients within status 1A and 5 of 15 (33.3%) went to patients within status 1B/2.

There were no statistically significant differences in donor age, gender, or ethnicity between patients receiving DBD versus DCD hearts (Table S2). DCD donors were procured from significantly greater distances (473 [IQR, 267.0–590.0] miles versus 290 [IQR, 155.0–410.0] miles; P<0.01) as compared with DBD donors, and DCD donors had higher ejection fraction (70% [IQR, 65%–70%] versus 64% [IQR, 60%–68%]; P=0.004). Among DCD donors, 5 were harvested using a DPP technique, and 9 were harvested using NRP; the procurement strategy for 1 patient was unable to be ascertained with available information within the database. Recipients of NRP‐harvested hearts were significantly younger than those procured via DPP (0 [IQR, 0–1] years versus 14 [IQR, 12–14] years; P<0.01), which is likely related to the inability to use ex vivo normothermic machine perfusion in the setting of DPP in hearts from donors weighing ≤40 kg; instead, the most commonly used technique in smaller children is NRP followed by static cold storage. 24

Among those listed for DCD versus DBD hearts, regardless of type of donor used, there was no statistically significant difference in 1‐year posttransplant survival (92.8% [95% CI, 78.9–97.6] versus 92.4% [95% CI, 90.7–93.8]; P=0.71; Figure S2). Similarly, there was no statistical difference in 1‐year posttransplant survival between recipients of DCD versus DBD hearts (85.7% [95% CI, 33.4–97.9] versus 92.4% [95% CI, 90.8–93.8]; P=0.60; Figure S3).

Institutional Concentration

There was notable institutional concentration of DCD listing and transplantation. In this sample, 65 institutions listed patients for any heart transplant; all 65 institutions performed at least 1 transplant. Of those, 10 of 65 (15.4%) listed patients for DCD transplant, and 5 of 65 (7.7%) performed at least 1 DCD transplant. One institution performed 10 of 15 (66.7%) total DCD heart transplants. Institutions that listed any patients for DCD heart transplant were not found to be more likely to accept marginal donors, including those with ejection fraction <50%, prolonged ischemic time (>5 hours), or significant donor–recipient weight ratio discrepancies (<0.6 or >3.0; Table S3).

DCD Crossover Population

Baseline characteristics of patients initially listed for DCD hearts versus patients listed for DCD crossover are presented in Table S4. Patients listed for DCD crossover were lower status at listing, with 72 of 96 (75.0%) of patients listed initially for DCD hearts listed as status 1A, compared with 10 of 37 (27.0%) of patients listed for DCD crossover (P<0.001). Patients listed for DCD crossover had higher estimated glomerular filtration rate at listing; there were no other statistical differences in comorbidities or degrees of pretransplant support.

Patients listed for DCD crossover had longer overall waitlist durations (243 [IQR, 130–459] days versus 63 [IQR, 14.5–142.5] days; P<0.001) and longer waitlist durations among those that were ultimately transplanted (144 [IQR, 115–332] days versus 42 [IQR, 11–88] days; P<0.001). The median duration spent in non‐DCD waitlist time for patients listed for DCD crossover was 98 days; there was no statistically significant difference between the period waitlisted as DCD candidates (58 [IQR, 28–195] days versus 63 [IQR, 15–143] days; P=0.17).

Discussion

There has been renewed interest among pediatric transplant centers in DCD heart transplantation to expand the donor pool and improve waitlist outcomes for pediatric candidates for heart transplantation. Early reports have demonstrated acceptable posttransplant outcomes in pediatric DCD heart recipients, 9 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 and DCD listing has improved waitlist outcomes among adult patients. 10 , 11 , 25 With an unacceptably high waitlist mortality rate in pediatric patients awaiting heart transplantation 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and estimates that DCD heart donation may increase the donor pool by up to 15% to 30%, 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 the potential benefits of DCD transplantation are significant. This study is the first to assess the impact of DCD listing on waitlist duration and outcomes among pediatric heart transplant candidates, as well as the first to stratify this analysis by UNOS listing status. In doing so, we have demonstrated that listing of pediatric patients for DCD hearts is associated with increased likelihood of transplantation and reduced waitlist durations, particularly among patients listed as status 1B/2. This contributes to the growing body of evidence that DCD heart transplantation may improve outcomes for both pediatric and adult patients on the waitlist.

Our finding that DCD listing increases the likelihood of transplantation and reduces waitlist duration in pediatric patients is consistent with recent literature in adult heart transplant populations. Using UNOS data, Hess and colleagues10 demonstrated that DCD listing, as compared with DBD listing, was associated with increased likelihood of transplantation, lower likelihood of waitlist death, and reduced waitlist duration in adult patients, particularly among status 3 and 4 recipients. They identified no difference in posttransplant survival between patients receiving DCD versus DBD hearts. The methodology used in our assessment of pediatric patients was similar; however, we distinguished between up‐front DCD listing and those who transitioned to DCD listing while on the waitlist. This was done to reduce the confounding effects on waitlist duration of the prolonged period before DCD crossover. Additionally, we demonstrated that patients listed for DCD hearts at initial listing represent a characteristically different population than the DCD crossover group, with patients listed for DCD crossover likely to be listed at a lower status. Ahmed and colleagues 25 also assessed waitlist outcomes of adult heart transplant recipients, stratified by receipt of DCD versus DBD hearts. They identified that status 3 and 4 DCD heart recipients had reduced waitlist times compared with similar DBD heart recipients. Interestingly, each of these studies identified lower rates of status 1 listing and mechanical support among adult patients who were listed for or received DCD hearts, compared with patients listed for DBD. 10 , 25 In contrast, we identified higher rates of status 1A listing, mechanical support, and comorbidities among pediatric patients listed for DCD hearts. This may be explained by the novel introduction and uptake of DCD heart transplantation in pediatric patients, leading to a focus on expanding the donor pool for the highest‐risk candidates. Additionally, the differences in adult versus pediatric organ allocation and listing criteria, with increased stratification in adult patients, may contribute to this observed difference. Despite these differences, we identified the consistent finding that patients in lower listing statuses benefit more in waitlist duration reduction with DCD listing. Given the prolonged waitlist times of these patients at baseline, expanded donor availability provides a greater marginal benefit.

This study also highlights that, although listing and transplantation of DCD hearts in pediatric patients is increasing over time, use remains limited. In the study period of January 2021 to June 2024, 3.9% of patients were initially listed with willingness to accept DCD hearts, and 1.0% of transplanted patients received DCD hearts. These cases were heavily concentrated at a small number of institutions; all DCD heart transplants were performed across 5 institutions, and 1 institution performed 66.7% of total DCD heart transplants. This is similar to early reports of DCD heart transplantation in adult populations: In 2019, only 3 adult centers performed any DCD heart transplants, 11 and 4 centers had performed 70% of all DCD heart transplants through 2021. 23 However, the adult practice has grown dramatically since, with recent studies demonstrating that at least 52 of 138 (37.7%) of heart transplant centers now offer DCD heart transplantation, 10 2516 of 14803 (17.0%) of heart transplant candidates were listed for DCD hearts from October 2018 to December 2022, 10 and 425 of 10403 (4.1%) of heart recipients from January 2019 to June 2022 received DCD hearts. 25

Nonetheless, this is the largest national series to date analyzing pediatric patients listed for and undergoing DCD heart transplantation. The first series of DCD heart transplants in children was reported by Boucek and colleagues 6 in 2008. This series described the successful transplantation of DCD hearts to 3 high‐risk infants from 2004 to 2007, with 100% 6‐month survival. Follow‐up data of these patients, as well as one additional patient undergoing DCD transplantation at a later date, revealed a 10‐year survival of 50%. 8 An assessment of UNOS data from 2004 to 2022 identified that after the early period of DCD heart transplants from 2004 to 2010, there were no DCD heart transplants performed until 2021, 9 with reluctance driven by challenges with perfusion and procurement techniques, as well as ethical concerns and differences in the interpretation of laws surrounding the definition of donor death. 7 , 21 Notably, we excluded the early DCD heart transplants performed between 2004 and 2010 given the limitation to single‐center protocols, as well as the evolution in procurement techniques and allocation policies since that time. Given the inclusion of more recent study years during which DCD has expanded, our study provides a substantially larger sample size.

The largest international series of pediatric DCD heart transplants was published by Kleinmahon and colleagues 22 in 2017. The International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation Registry was queried, identifying 3877 pediatric heart transplants performed, of which 21 (0.5%) were DCD. Survival was significantly lower at 1 and 3 years among patients listed for DCD. In our more contemporary data set, DCD versus DBD listing status and DCD versus DBD heart transplantation were not associated with 1‐year survival.

It is important to note that despite the continued expansion of DCD heart transplantation in pediatric patients, ethical concerns persist. Specifically, there remains inconsistency across institutions and organ procurement organizations regarding the interpretation of the legal definition of donor death as an irreversible and permanent cessation of circulation and, thus, the ethics of NRP. 30 Accordingly, there exists variation in the allowance of NRP as a procurement technique across institutions, and prior studies have demonstrated significant regional variation in uptake of DCD heart transplantation, as well as rates of NRP use. 31 , 32 This may have a particularly significant impact on pediatric DCD heart allocation, while the concerns surrounding NRP can be avoided in adult patients through the use of DPP and ex vivo normothermic machine perfusion as indicated, smaller hearts are not able to be perfused with existing ex vivo perfusion devices. 24 Therefore, smaller children may be unable to receive DCD hearts coming from hospitals in which NRP is not permitted. Given this, as well as the demonstrated regional variation of practice in the adult literature, UNOS region serves as an important covariate in our models. However, it is to be acknowledged that region is a covariate with inherent limitations given the current landscape of pediatric DCD heart transplantation and the uneven regional distribution of centers performing DCD transplants. For that reason, additional multivariable competing risk analyses have been conducted, with the exclusion of region as a covariate, which have demonstrated similar key findings (Tables S5–S7).

There are several limitations to this study. First, given the recent reintroduction of DCD heart transplantation in pediatric patients, the number of patients listed for and undergoing DCD transplantation remains small, leading to analyses with limited power. Next, as with all retrospective analyses using the UNOS database, the data lack granularity that may be ascertained by institutional chart review. There may also be unmeasured confounding that persists despite controlling for key available variables in a multivariable model. Additionally, there were patients with no posttransplant follow‐up data, leading to their exclusion from survival analysis. Despite these data‐specific limitations, the use of contemporary national data has facilitated a description of the largest current sample of pediatric patients listed for and undergoing DCD heart transplantation. Furthermore, the DCD cohort used in our primary analyses includes only patients listed as DCD recipient candidates at the time of registration. While we view this as a strength of our analysis, our findings comparing DCD with DBD listing cannot be generalized to patients whose donor options later expanded to include DCD while on the waitlist. Further research is needed to better understand outcomes between those listed as DCD up front and those who transitioned to DCD recipient candidacy. There may also exist bias in our analysis due to the limited number of institutions listing for and performing DCD heart transplantation. In particular, with 1 institution performing two thirds of all DCD transplants in the sample, there is the risk that observed differences are driven by institution‐specific characteristics; however, we identified no difference in propensity toward more aggressive organ acceptance by institution. Additionally, institutions listing for and performing high volumes of DCD heart transplantation tend to list for and perform high volumes of DBD heart transplantation; thus, institution‐specific effects on waitlist outcomes will affect both treatment and control groups, limiting the impact of this bias. Furthermore, early studies and trials of DCD heart transplantation in adult populations faced similar limitations. In the DCD Heart (Clinical Trial to Evaluate the Safety and Effectiveness of the Portable Organ Care System Heart for Resuscitation, Preservation, and Assessment of Hearts From Donors After Circulatory Death) trial, for example, only 6 of 13 centers enrolled >10 total patients, with even fewer enrolling >10 patients to the DCD heart transplant group. 13 In a retrospective review of early DCD heart transplant outcomes in adults, it was found that 4 centers performed ≈70% of all DCD heart transplants. 23 Despite similar limitations in the early adult literature, the use of DCD hearts has continued to expand, and ongoing studies have continued to demonstrate benefit to waitlisted patients. While the small sample size does not allow a stratification of institutional volume, the use of region as a covariate in our analyses. in addition to adjusting for state‐based variation in DCD and NRP practice, may mitigate center effect to a certain degree.

Conclusions

Among pediatric candidates for heart transplantation listed as status 1B/2, patients initially listed to accept DCD hearts in addition to DBD hearts have shorter waitlist duration, improved rates of transplantation, lower waitlist mortality rate, and comparable survival to patients listed exclusively for DBD hearts. These findings support the continued expansion of DCD heart use in the pediatric population as a strategy to improve waitlist outcomes.
