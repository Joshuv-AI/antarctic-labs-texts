Comprehensive engineering of novel glycosyltransferase for efficient, donor-promiscuous, and regioselective glycosylation of flavonoids
Authors: Yang Lu, Lianghua Xie, Jiahong Xie, Gexin Liu, Jiaqi Fu, Luheng Zou, Qiu Lin, Yong Wang, Wei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Flavonoid O-glycosylation, catalyzed by uridine diphosphate (UDP)–glycosyltransferases, is crucial for their therapeutic efficacy. However, most UDP-glycosyltransferases encounter three major limitations: low activity, poor regioselectivity, and restricted substrate availability, hindering their pharmaceutical applications. To address these challenges, we conducted protein engineering on a previously unidentified glycosyltransferase, UGT75AJ2, which had 3′,7-O-glycosylation capabilities. Our approach involved three strategies: (i) development of a tailored focused rational iterative site-specific mutagenesis strategy, augmented by virtual screening and iterative mutagenesis, to design mutant Mut4-1 (S367A/V274A/F82V/I132T) with a 128-fold enhancement in relative catalytic activity; (ii) enhancement of the enzyme’s compatibility with a broader spectrum of sugar donors achieved through structural-based engineering, yielding mutant S14G/F366H/S367G and demonstrating effective utilization of diverse donors; (iii) construction of a targeted mutant library to enhance regioselectivity by active site analysis, leading to mutants with high selectivity for targeted glycosylation sites. This comprehensive study tackles predominant challenges in UDP-glycosyltransferases protein engineering, providing innovative approaches and insights that enhance the development of flavonoid glycosides.

INTRODUCTION

Flavonoids, a diverse group of plant secondary metabolites, mainly include flavonols, flavones, flavanones, flavanols, and anthocyanins, among other subgroups (1). These compounds play a crucial role in human health, offering substantial dietary and medicinal advantages (2–4). Numerous studies highlight their antibacterial (5, 6), anti-inflammatory (7), antioxidation (8, 9), immunomodulatory (10), metabolic stabilizing (11), and anticancer properties (12–14), among other health benefits (15, 16). Glycosylation, a crucial postmodification in plant secondary metabolism, not only modifies the catalytic efficiency, solubility, and stability of aglycones (17, 18), but also enhances the pharmacological effectiveness and bioavailability of these compounds (19, 20). Glycosides such as cyanidin-3-O-glucoside (C3G), quercetin-3-O-glycoside, and luteolin-7-O-glycoside, known for their roles in combating oxidative stress, inflammation, and cancer, underline the importance of these natural medicines (21–23). To date, a total of 36 flavonoid-based drug candidates are in various stages of clinical trials. Among them, 19 kinds of flavonoids, including daidzein, hesperidin, and baicalein, have been approved for the market, and flavonoid glycosides account for 36.8% (24). While traditional chemical glycosylation usually requires cumbersome protection and deprotection steps and suffers low specificity and yield, biocatalytic glycosylation provides a viable alternative in an environmentally friendly way. Uridine diphosphate (UDP)–dependent glycosyltransferases (UGTs) (EC 2.4.x.y), as members of family 1 glycosyltransferases, which use UDP-sugars as donor and transfer sugars to nucleophilic acceptor (25), are crucial in the biosynthesis of natural glycosides such as flavonoid glycosides. Given their essential role in these biosynthetic pathways, plant UGTs have attracted wide attention for their potential to enhance the glycosylation of plant-derived natural products. So far, most of the O-glycosyltransferases identified from Arabidopsis, Camellia sinensis, Glycyrrhiza uralensis, and other plants are primarily responsible for the glycosylation of hydroxyl groups at the C-3, C-5, and C-7 positions. (19, 26–29).

However, most wild-type (WT) O-glycosyltransferases are plagued by the drawback of low enzyme activity, a narrow range of substrate, and glycosyl donor compatibility, which seriously hindered their application in pharmaceutical development (30–32). To address these challenges, structure-guided protein evolution strategies, such as combinatorial active-site saturation test (CAST), iterative saturation mutagenesis (ISM), and focused rational iterative site-specific mutagenesis (FRISM), have been extensively studied. These methods aim to alter key residues within the active site of UGTs, tailoring their catalytic functions to meet specific requirements (33–37). For instance, the enzyme WsUGT73A16 from Withania somnifera underwent directed evolution to alter its substrate binding pocket conformation, resulting in mutants A337C and Q339A, which achieved 2.61- and 6.78-fold increases in catalytic efficiency, respectively, compared to the WT (38). In addition, through CAST/ISM, the enzyme MiCGT from Mangifera indica yielded the VFAH quadruple mutant (W93V/V124F/F191A/R282H) with enhanced 3-O-glycosylation selectivity and a 120-fold increase in catalytic activity toward quercetin (39). Using ISM, the mutant M7 (T79Y/L48M/R28H/L109I/S15A/M76L/H47R) of UGT74AC1 from Siraitia grosvenorii increased catalytic efficiency and extended substrate promiscuity (40). Likewise, three double mutants of UGT74AC2 from the same species demonstrated improved conversion rates and regioselectivity to 3-OH, 7-OH, and 3,7-OH after directed evolution (41). ScUGT51 from Saccharomyces cerevisiae was engineered through site-directed mutagenesis and ISM, with the mutant M5-1+E96K/K92A showing a 1800-fold increase in catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) compared to the WT (42). Even with the current advancements, the dearth and inadequacy of exhaustive protein engineering research on O-glycosyltransferases continue to erect substantial technical hurdles in the biosynthesis of related glycoside compounds, which have immense application potential in the fields of agriculture, food, and pharmaceuticals. The comprehensive protein engineering strategies reported above generally require complex high-throughput mutant screening steps, presenting tremendous challenges such as lengthy experimental timelines, substantial labor, and a low probability of finding beneficial mutations. Thus, developing a rapid, reliable, and widely applicable strategy for the directed evolution of O-glycosyltransferases is critical.

In this study, using gene mining, we found a previously unidentified plant glycosyltransferase UGT75AJ2, which was capable of catalyzing the glycosylation of flavonoids at the 3′-OH and 7-OH positions (the detailed methods for gene mining were provided in the Supplementary Materials). This distinctive enzymatic activity of UGT75AJ2 makes it a promising candidate for the biosynthesis of natural blue pigments and high-value flavonoid glycosides (43–44). However, the WT UGT75AJ2 displayed weak catalytic ability in terms of enzyme activity, sugar donor promiscuity, and regioselectivity. Here, we devised a tailored FRISM evolutionary strategy, taking C3G (1) as a benchmark substrate. This method was informed by an analysis of average mutation energies, the substrate binding pocket conformation, sequence conservation, and the active site properties of UGT75AJ2. Last, substantial increases in catalytic efficiency, broader substrate and donor promiscuity, and strict regioselectivity were achieved in the engineered UGT75AJ2 mutants. Our study establishes a methodological foundation for refining enzyme engineering procedures and reducing the reliance on extensive mutant library screening. This approach provides a flexible and adaptable framework for tailoring enzyme, thereby enabling the precise synthesis of flavonoid glycosides with promising applications across various fields, including biopharmaceuticals, functional foods, and plant breeding.

RESULTS

Functional characterization of UGT75AJ2 and bioactivity verification of its glycosylation products

Vaccinium corymbosum, renowned for its rich variety of anthocyanins, implies the presence of diverse anthocyanin glycosyltransferases. Through transcriptome sequencing and functional analyses of both ripe and unripe fruits of V. corymbosum (fig. S1), we identified a gene exhibiting significantly differential expression among six anthocyanin glycosyltransferases. This gene was designated as VcUGT1 (gene symbol: UGT75AJ2) according to the UGT Nomenclature Committee (45). As shown in fig. S2, UGT75AJ2 showed higher enzyme activity when using UDP–glucose (Glc) as sugar donor and C3G as well as quercetin as substrates, compared with other five anthocyanin glycosyltransferases that were identified via transcriptomics analyses. Given that UGT75AJ2 not only showed the highest expression level but also displayed significantly differential expression (as evidenced in fig. S1K), and considering its highest enzyme activity among the six anthocyanin glycosyltransferases identified through transcriptomics analyses, UGT75AJ2 was selected as the target protein for our investigation. Constructing a phylogenetic tree to place UGT75AJ2 among 68 known plant UGTs (table S2) revealed that UGT75AJ2 belongs to the flavonoid 5-O-UGT cluster (fig. S3A). The cDNA sequence of UGT75AJ2 comprises an open reading frame of 1361 base pairs, encoding a protein of 453 amino acids with an estimated molecular weight of 50 kDa. Using specific primers (table S5), the gene was successfully cloned from V. corymbosum by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), and the resultant fragment was subcloned into a pET-28a vector for expression in a heterologous system (fig. S4, A and B). The produced UGT75AJ2 enzyme was then purified via Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography (fig. S4C), resulting in a soluble form with a molecular weight of ~54 kDa, as expected. Following the identification of UGT75AJ2, glycosyltransferase activity tests were performed using C3G (1) and quercetin (18) as substrates, with UDP-Glc as the sugar donor (Fig. 1, A and B). These assays detected the formation of two distinct glycosylation products from each substrate, albeit with a low catalytic efficiency.

In particular, two glucosylated derivatives (labeled 1a and 1b) were produced from C3G (Fig. 1C), exhibiting a mass increase of 162 Da, as indicated by their [M+H]+ ions at a mass/charge ratio (m/z) of 611 (Fig. 1D). Contrary to expectations based on UGT75AJ2’s classification in the flavonoid 5-O-UGT group, neither 1a nor 1b resulted from 5-O-glycosylation. Further structural elucidation through correlation spectroscopy (COSY), heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC), and heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) analyses identified these compounds as 3′-O and 7-O-glycosylation products (figs. S89 to S91), highlighting their rarity and potential as precursors for developing anthocyanin derivatives with medicinal properties. Similarly, glycosylation products identified from quercetin, 18a and 18b, were determined to be its 3′-O-glucoside and 7-O-glucoside forms, respectively, demonstrating UGT75AJ2’s ability to catalyze glycosylation at these specific sites (Fig. 1, E and F).

The hydroxyl group in the chemical structure of flavonoids has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hypoglycemic activities. However, it has been observed that the hydroxyl groups at positions C-3′ and C-7 can attenuate their biological activities to some extent (46). Therefore, glycosylation of the hydroxyl groups at positions C-3′ and C-7 not only enhances water solubility and chemical stability of flavonoid aglycones but also further augments their bioactivity. To substantiate this conclusion, we selected several representative flavonoid glycoside products catalyzed by the UGT75AJ2 and conducted in vitro and in vivo enzyme inhibition experiments against α-amylase, a crucial target for regulating postprandial blood Glc levels, to evaluate the hypoglycemic activity of these flavonoid glycosides. The results of in vitro and in vivo α-amylase inhibition assays showed that quercetin-3′-O-glucoside, quercetin-7-O-glucoside, and apigenin-7-O-glucoside exhibited promising hypoglycemic effects compared with their aglycone counterparts (table S6 and fig. S5A). These findings revealed that glycosylation of the hydroxyl groups at positions C-3′ and C-7 could enhance the hyperglycemic effects of flavonoids, thereby further highlighting the great potential of UGT75AJ2 in the biosynthesis of flavonoid glycosides with medicinal value.

Despite the fact that a few glycosyltransferases have been reported to catalyze 3′-O and 7-O-glycosylation reactions, they generally exhibit relatively low catalytic efficiency and poor regioselectivity, and these glycosyltransferases could only accept a limited range of substrates and sugar donors (33, 34, 47). This notablely obstructs their capacity to broaden the diversity of glycoside compounds and to selectively synthesize particular glycosides that exhibit substantial pharmaceutical potential. Consequently, our subsequent research efforts aim to improve the catalytic efficiency of UGT75AJ2.

Identifying mutational hotspots for UGT75AJ2 by in silico approach

To enhance the catalytic performance of UGT75AJ2, we embarked on a tailored FRISM evolutionary strategy (fig. S6), which started with an in silico approach, using AlphaFold2, RoseTTAFold All-Atom, and homology modeling for predicting the enzyme’s atomic structure, subsequently validated through all-atom explicit solvent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (figs. S7 to S9). Molecular docking was then applied to discern ~70 critical mutation hotspots (fig. S10), facilitating the creation of a targeted mutation library by assessing the binding energy alterations of these identified sites. As shown in Fig. 2A and figs. S11A and S12A, virtual saturation mutation energies for 4140 (72 × 20 + 75 × 20 + 60 × 20) sites were calculated. Subsequently, the average saturated mutation binding energy ( ΔΔGmut¯) > 0 kcal/mol represents destabilizing sites) for the substitutions at each residue was evaluated. One-sample t test methods were used to compare the average mutation binding energies ( ΔΔGmut¯ ) of WT and mutants. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure is a tool that control for multiple hypotheses testing. Here, mutation sites were identified as statistically significant if the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. This process identified 38 residues as potentially destabilizing the enzyme-substrate interaction, marked by an average mutation binding energy greater than 0 kcal/mol (Fig. 2B and figs. S11B and S12B). Incorporation of evolutionary conservation insights led to the exclusion of four key conserved catalytic residues (Q15, H17, W347, and Q369) (Fig. 2C and fig. S13). This refined our focus to 34 residues that significantly affect the protein-ligand complex, earmarking them for further investigation through alanine scanning (Fig. 2D and fig. S14), thus laying the groundwork for targeted improvements in UGT75AJ2’s efficiency. As shown in fig. S14, among the identified 34 hotspot residues, 21 hotspot residues contain hydrophobic side-chain groups (P13, G16, L18, F61, Y65, Y75, M79, F82, L112, W115, F178, P184, F185, F270, G271, V274, V275, L276, P364, F366, and M370), accounting for approximately two-thirds of the total hotspot residues and forming the core hydrophobic binding pocket. The remaining 13 hydrophilic amino acids (C12, T39, H42, T76, E81, S177, S272, R300, H344, C345, S349, Q365, and S367) are predominantly distributed at the peripheral junctions of the pocket. The 34 hotspot residues are uniformly distributed around the substrate receptor (C3G, shown in yellow), collectively forming an inwardly concave “U-shaped” pocket. This structural arrangement provides a stable and favorable environment for substrate entry/exit, as well as the formation of relevant catalytic intermediates.

Enhancing UGT75AJ2 catalytic activity

To enhance the catalytic activity of UGT75AJ2, we targeted these 34 hotspot residues identified for mutation to alanine using site-directed mutagenesis, assessing the impact on catalytic performance toward substrate C3G (1) in vitro (fig. S15). Notably, several mutants showed a substantial boost in enzyme activity compared to the WT (Fig. 3B). Specifically, the mutants Mut1-1 (S367A), Mut1-2 (V274A), and Mut1-3 (F82A) exhibited at least a sixfold increase in activity, with the activity of the WT catalysis of C3G (1) to generate cyanidin-3,3′-di-O-glucoside (1a) set as a baseline. Conversely, the mutants H344A, C345A, Q365A, and M370A exhibited a complete loss of catalytic activity, likely due to their location in the catalytically critical PSPG motif.

UGTs are recognized for catalyzing SN2-like single displacement reaction, forming glycosidic bonds through a mechanism typically involving a conserved “His-Asp dyad” near the active site aids, which deprotonates the hydroxyl group of substrates (fig. S16C) (48). Contrary to this norm, UGT75AJ2 uniquely replaces the aspartate with serine, a variation identified through sequence comparisons (fig. S16, A and B). Experimental mutations, specifically H17A and S110A, revealed that while H17A leads to a complete loss of catalytic activity, the S110A variant retained function. Moreover, S110D mutation failed to improve the catalytic activity of UGT75AJ2 (fig. S16, E and F). Therefore, these findings implied that His17 plays a critical role in substrate interaction, forming a “His-acceptor dyad” that facilitates catalysis without the necessity for aspartic acid, marking it different from the traditional triad mechanism (fig. S16D).

Leveraging the mutant Mut1-1 (S367A), which showed the most significant enhancement in catalytic activity in the initial round of alanine scanning, we targeted the V274A mutant, which showed sevenfold catalytic activity improvement compared to the WT, for further ISM (fig. S17). This approach led to the construction of 19 mutants, built upon the Mut1-1 template. Among these, the double mutant Mut2-1 (S367A/V274A) showcased an impressive 2.5- and 45-fold increase in activity over Mut1-1 and WT, respectively (Fig. 3C). Recent research underscores the substantial role of the size of the substrate binding pocket in determining substrate compatibility (49, 50). In particular, the bulky side chain of Phe82 near the active site might function as a gate regulating substrate entry (fig. S18B). This insight prompted us to substitute Phe82 with six different hydrophobic amino acids varying in side chain volume, including Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Pro, and Trp (fig. S18A). The modifications led to the triple mutant Mut3-1 (S367A/V274A/F82V) and Mut3-2 (S367A/V274A/F82A), which exhibited a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in activity compared to Mut2-1 (Fig. 3D). Mut3-1 achieved a 21.8- and 2.5-fold boost in kcat/Km in comparison to WT and Mut2-1, respectively, underscoring a substantial leap in enzymatic efficiency (table S24).

In the evolutionary process, mutations that enhance protein functionality tend to be naturally selected and conserved (51). Using this principle, incorporating consensus sequences from multiple sequence alignment emerges as an effective strategy to improve enzyme catalytic activity and thermal stability. In this work, an activity-based sequence conservative analysis method was applied (42), comparing the N-terminal domain of UGT75AJ2 against thirteen other plant glycosyltransferases with a known 5-O-glycosylation activity (table S3). This comparison revealed eleven highly conserved residues, with conservation levels quantified on a scale from 0.1 to 0.9, where higher values denote stronger conservation (fig. S18C). When residue Ile132 was replaced by threonine, the quadruple mutant Mut4-1 (S367A/V274A/F82V/I132T) displayed a 1.6- and 128-fold increased activity than Mut3-1 and WT, respectively (Fig. 3, E and F). Moreover, we also chose Vc3GT from V. corymbosum and Ac3GT from Aralia cordata, both 3-O-glycosyltransferases with higher catalytic activities, as the focal points of our investigation. Using an identical, meticulously designed evolution strategy, we endeavored to amplify their catalytic capabilities even further (figs. S19 to S26). The results indicate that the quadruple mutant of Vc3GT (H82A/V139C/K322E/F198W) exhibits catalytic activities toward malvidin and quercetin that are 10.64 and 7.22 times higher than those of the WT, respectively. In addition, the quadruple mutant of Ac3GT (W138A/F198W/F371W/I147V) shows catalytic activities for cyanidin and quercetin that are 10.48- and 8.18-fold higher compared with the WT, respectively. Therefore, this approach not only enhances the catalytic activities of these two enzymes but also compellingly validates the broad applicability and the remarkable effectiveness of the protein evolution strategy described above. Then, in vivo fermentation was carried out for both Mut4-1 and WT UGT75AJ2 in Escherichia coli using quercetin as substrate and UDP-Glc as sugar donor. As shown in fig. S27, following the in vivo fermentation, Mut4-1 exhibited higher conversion rates and titers compared to the WT. Specifically, Mut4-1 achieved a conversion rate of 22.45% and a titer of 52.13 mg/liter, while the WT displayed a conversion rate of 9.77% and a titer of 22.71 mg/liter. This indicates that the conversion rate and titer of Mut4-1 were 1.30-fold higher than those of the WT. Furthermore, when examining the specific products quercetin-3′-O-glucoside and quercetin-7-O-glucoside, their conversion rates and titers in Mut4-1 were increased by 13-fold and 1.19-fold, respectively, compared to the WT.

To further elucidate the mechanisms of improved catalytic activity of UGT75AJ2, we performed molecular docking and MD simulations. The results revealed an increase in hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions within the Mut4-1 complex compared to the WT complex (figs. S28; S29, A to D; and S30 to S31), mainly due to the contributions of the mutated residues S367A, V274A, and I132T. These interactions helped stabilize the enzyme’s catalytic conformation. MD simulations further highlighted the stability of Mut4-1 in the presence of UDP-Glc and C3G, surpassing that of the WT complex (fig. S29, E and F). In addition, the active site of Mut4-1 exhibited increased flexibility, particularly in four N-terminal loops (loop2, loop5, loop7, and loop9), implying an improved capacity for substrate binding (fig. S29G). This structural flexibility was complemented by a closer proximity between C3G 3′-O atom and UDP-Glc anomeric C atom in Mut4-1 compared to WT (fig. S29H). Volume measurements confirmed that the active site pocket of Mut4-1 was larger than that of WT, thus favoring the ligand interactions (fig. S29, I and J). These collective structural adjustments significantly amplify UGT75AJ2’s catalytic activity in the Mut4-1 variant.

To determine the most favorable conditions for UGT75AJ2 catalysis, we tested a variety of substrates, including C3G (1), malvidin-3-O-glucoside (M3G) (4), malvidin-3-O-galactoside (M3Ga) (5), petunidin-3-O-glucoside (Pet3G) (7), peonidin-3-O-galactoside (Pn3Ga) (9), delphinidin-3-O-glucoside (D3G) (12), and UDP-Glc. The experimental setup evaluated the enzyme performance across various factors, including addition of divalent metal ions (fig. S32A), incubation duration (fig. S32B), environmental pH (fig. S32C), and reaction temperature (fig. S32D). The findings revealed that UGT75AJ2 reached its peak activity within a 50 mM tris-HCl buffer at a neutral pH of 7.0 in the presence of Mg2+ over a 12-hour period at 30°C.

Broadening the substrate spectrum of UGT75AJ2 and its Mut4-1 mutant

To assess the substrate versatility of UGT75AJ2 and its enhanced variant Mut4-1 in glycosylation reactions, we evaluated 36 flavonoids with pharmaceutical potential (Fig. 4B, fig. S33, and table S1) using UDP-Glc as the glycosyl donor. These flavonoids were sorted into several groups: anthocyanins (1–13), flavones (14–17), flavonols (18–21), isoflavones (22–24), flavonones (25–28), flavanonols (29–30), and anthocyanidins (31–36) (Fig. 4B). Initial screenings via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) indicated a predominant lack of activity in WT for glycosylating these compounds, with numerous substrates showing minimal or null responses (Fig. 4A). Glycosylation products at the 3′-O and 7-O positions were pinpointed using tandem mass spectrometry analysis and verified with known standards (figs. S34 to S63). In contrast to WT, Mut4-1 demonstrated superior catalytic efficiency, effectively glycosylating 29 flavonoid substrates (Fig. 4A), excluding puerarin (23) and the anthocyanidins (31–36). Notably, puerarin (23) has a glycosyl group at the C-8 position, creating steric hindrance that particularly affects the 7-OH site, leading to an absence of activity for both WT and Mut4-1. Given the inherent instability of anthocyanin aglycones, their glycosylation at the C-3 position serves as a stabilizing and necessary precursor for additional modifications (52, 53). This specific glycosylation incapability by UGT75AJ2 and its variants clarifies their nonreactivity with anthocyanin aglycone (figs. S64 and S65). Moreover, with substrates such as luteolin (14), quercetin (18), and others, Mut4-1 was capable of producing a diverse array of glycoside products, including mono- and diglycosides. This enhancement underscores the mutants’ role in expanding the diversity and availability of flavonoid glycosides, thereby enriching the pool for natural drug compound screening.

Expanding sugar donor compatibility of UGT75AJ2

To further explore the sugar donor specificity of UGT75AJ2, we assessed its activity with UDP-galactose (UDP-Gal), UDP–glucuronic acid (UDP-GlcA), UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), and UDP-xylose (UDP-Xyl), using C3G (1) and apigenin (15) as acceptors. The WT enzyme was limited to using only UDP-Glc, showing no activity toward the other tested sugar donors. While the Mut4-1 mutant displayed some degree of acceptance toward UDP-Gal, UDP-GlcNAc, and UDP-Xyl, the catalytic activities remained markedly low (Fig. 5, D and E). This led to the adoption of a structure-based protein engineering approach aimed at broadening the sugar donor range of UGT75AJ2. Structural analysis revealed that the glycosyl donor typically occupies a relatively conserved, elongated, and narrow channel within UGT75AJ2 (Fig. 5A). Twenty-seven residues in proximity to the glycosyl moiety were categorized into five distinct zones (Fig. 5B). Comparative sequence analysis of these zones against seven plant flavonoid UGTs (table S4) known for broad sugar donor promiscuity identified six residues with variation from the consensus: S14, M273, C327, S328, F366, and S367 (Fig. 5C). Mutation of these residues to their most frequent variants across the studied UGTs led to enhanced sugar donor flexibility. Specifically, using C3G (1) as a substrate, the mutants S14G, F366H, and S367G demonstrated the ability to catalyze glycosylation with UDP-Gal and UDP-GlcNAc, with S367G also accommodating UDP-Xyl (Fig. 5D). These capabilities were similarly observed when apigenin served as the substrate (Fig. 5E). Further mutations on the most efficient mutant, Mut4-1, did not yield significant improvements in sugar donor scope, highlighting the complex nature of enzyme-substrate compatibility.

Molecular docking of UDP-GlcNAc and UGT75AJ2 revealed that F366 was positioned near the 2-N-acetyl group of UDP-GlcNAc, impeding the formation of effective interactions between UDP-GlcNAc and surrounding residues (Fig. 5, F and G). This observation led to the identification of F366 as a potential pivotal residue influencing the enzyme’s compatibility with various sugar donors. Subsequent saturation mutagenesis of F366 and evaluation of the catalytic efficiency across 19 resulting mutants revealed that F366H notably outperformed others in catalytic activity (Fig. 5H). This improvement might be attributed to histidine having a smaller side chain compared to phenylalanine, facilitating the enzyme’s adaptation to diverse glycosyl donors. Based on the results presented in Fig. 5H and figs. S66 and S67, the catalytic activities of the F366M and F366H mutants, when using C3G as substrate and UDP-GlcNAc as sugar donor, were higher than those of F366 (WT). In addition, the catalytic activities of the F366H mutants, when using C3G as substrate and UDP-Gal as sugar donor, were also higher than those of F366 (WT). However, none of the F366 mutants exhibited catalytic activities when using C3G as substrate and UDP-GlcA or UDP-Xyl as sugar donors. As illustrated in fig. S84, among the 11 F366 mutants (F366I, F366M, F366C, F366L, F366R, F366Q, F366E, F366Y, F366G, F366H, and F366A), the catalytic activities of all mutants, when using apigenin as substrate and UDP-GlcNAc as sugar donor, were higher than those of F366 (WT). In contrast, only the catalytic activities of the F366H mutant, when using apigenin as substrate and UDP-Gal or UDP-Xyl as sugar donors, were higher than those of F366 (WT). Notably, none of the F366 mutants exhibited catalytic activities when using C3G as substrate and UDP-GlcA as sugar donor. In summary, the F366 residue is a critical determinant influencing the enzyme’s compatibility with various sugar donors. However, the enhanced catalytic activities of the F366 mutants were predominantly observed when using UDP-GlcNAc as sugar donor. To further enhance sugar donor acceptance, a triple mutant combining S14G, F366H, and S367G was engineered. This variant demonstrated the ability to use UDP-Gal, UDP-GlcNAc, and UDP-Xyl, albeit not UDP-GlcA, showcasing an increased acceptance of sugar donors compared to the individual mutants S14G, F366H, and S367G, regardless of the substrate used (Fig. 5, I and J, and figs. S68 to S74). Particularly with apigenin (15) as the substrate, this mutant showed high catalytic activity toward both UDP-GlcNAc and UDP-Xyl, with the conversion rate of UDP-GlcNAc reaching nearly 100% (Fig. 5J).

Further MD simulations of the WT complex, incorporating sugar donors and acceptors, demonstrated that the crucial catalytic residue H17 engaged in unwanted hydrogen bonding with the glycosyl donor (figs. S75 to S80, A and G), which hindered the formation of the His-acceptor dyad intermediate, thus preventing WT from engaging with various glycosyl donors. In contrast, within the GHG mutant (S14G/F366H/S367G), not only was the substrate able to achieve a correct catalytic alignment with H17, reinforcing the protein’s structural integrity (figs. S75 to S80), but the proximity between the nucleophilic oxygen and the anomeric carbon also significantly decreased in the GHG complex relative to the WT (figs. S75 to S80, E, F, K, and L). These alterations enhanced the glycosyl donor versatility observed in the GHG mutant.

Enhancing the regioselectivity of UGT75AJ2

To further assess the regioselectivity of WT UGT75AJ2 and its Mut4-1 mutant, enzyme activity assays were conducted using C3G (1) and quercetin (18) as substrates, with UDP-Glc serving as the glycosyl donor (fig. S81A). The results showed that both WT and Mut4-1 exhibited poor selectivity for the 3′-O and 7-O-glycosylation sites on these substrates (fig. S81, B to D).

A thorough investigation into the active site pinpointed 18 residues suitable for an exhaustive series of saturated virtual mutations (Fig. 6, A and C), with the binding energies of these mutations subsequently evaluated (Fig. 6, D and E). Mutations with a positive binding energy shift (>0.5 kcal/mol), indicating potential instability, were earmarked for further experimental exploration (54, 55). A specialized mutant library was subsequently established, targeting eight key residues implicated in destabilizing the enzyme conformation (C12, Y75, F82, L112, I132, F185, M273, and S367). For these mutations, six amino acids representing a broad spectrum of chemical characteristics were chosen for substitution (Fig. 6F), including alanine/glycine (nonpolar, aliphatic), tyrosine (polar, uncharged), phenylalanine (nonpolar, aromatic), lysine (basic), and glutamic acid (acidic). These mutants were then assessed through enzyme activity assays, using C3G (1) and quercetin (18) as substrates and UDP-Glc as the glycosyl donor (fig. S82A).

The HPLC results of mutant library screening revealed that the I132E and S367F mutants exhibited highly regioselective activity, facilitating 3′-O and 5-O-glycosylations on substrate C3G (1) (Fig. 6, H and J, and figs. S82B and S83A). S367F resulted in an unexpected 5-O-glycosylation product absent in WT (fig. S84), indicating the pivotal influence of residue 367 in determining regioselective glycosylation of anthocyanins and potentially serving as a switch for activating 5-OH glycosylation. Molecular docking indicated that H17 could form a hydrogen bond with 5-OH of C3G, while F367 could be engaged in a hydrophobic interaction with the C ring of C3G (figs. S85 to S87, G and H). Moreover, the MD simulations of WT and these mutants in complex with C3G revealed that S367F was particularly proficient at promoting 5-O-glycosylation catalytic conformations (fig. S85 to S87I).

In addition, mutants I132E and S367G showed strict selectivity for the 3′-O and 7-O-glycosylation sites, respectively, when using quercetin (18) as the substrate (Fig. 6, I and K, and figs. S82B and S83B). This led to the identification of residues 132 and 367 as key determinants of regioselectivity, prompting further investigation via saturation mutagenesis. The results showed that S367D displayed high 7-O selectivity toward substrate C3G (1), albeit with a modest conversion rate. In addition, the I132V, S367M, and S367W mutants maintained similar 7-O selectivity as S367G with quercetin (18) (fig. S82D). Molecular docking and MD simulations of the highly regioselective mutants I132E and S367W in complex with quercetin (18) showed that 3′-O and 7-O-glycosylations were the dominant conformations, respectively (fig. S88, A and D). Furthermore, quercetin (18) engaged in increased hydrophobicity interactions with the residues of these mutants compared to WT (fig. S88, B and E). Active conformation statistics revealed that I132E and S367W tended to generate 3′-O-glycosylation (fig. S88C) and 7-O-glycosylation (fig. S88F) conformations, respectively.

Subsequently, to refine mutants with superior regioselectivity and efficiency for substrate C3G (1), we combined previously identified high-performing mutants into a triple mutant Y75E/F185Y/I132T (Fig. 6G). This combination resulted in a striking 99% selectivity and 93% conversion toward the 3′-O site (Fig. 6, H and J, and fig. S83A). Molecular docking indicated that 3′-O-glycosylation was the predominant conformation (fig. S87A) with His17 forming a hydrogen bond with 3′-O, and Glu75, Phe178, and Val188 engaging in both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with substrate C3G (1) (fig. S87B). MD simulations further suggested the formation of a suitably structured active site conducive to 3′-O-glycosylation of C3G (fig. S87C).

For enhancing 7-O-glycosylation, the L112F/M273E/S367D triple mutant combined variants with high regioselectivity, leading to 85% selectivity and 80% conversion for the glycosylated product cyanidin-3,7-di-O-glucoside (1b) (Fig. 6J). Molecular docking showed 7-O-glycosylation as the main conformation (fig. S87, D and E), with essential hydrogen and hydrophobic interactions contributed by His17, Glu273, and Asp367, while Phe112 indirectly affected the substrate spatial orientation. The MD simulation indicated a preference for 7-O-glycosylation product formation (fig. S87F).

DISCUSSION

Emerging research underscores the medicinal potential of flavonoid glycosides and their derivatives due to their robust biological activities and chemical stability (19). For instance, cyanidin 3,5-O-diglucoside outperforms its aglycone counterparts in inhibiting dipeptidyl peptidase-4 and α-glucosidase, marking it as a potential antidiabetic agent (56, 57). Likewise, glycosylated derivatives of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), such as EGCG 3′-O-glucoside, exhibit augmented antioxidant properties. This enhancement is achieved through the reduction of reactive oxygen species production and the mitigation of stress responses. As a result, these glycosylated derivatives offer therapeutic benefits that surpass those of both EGCG itself and EGCG 7-O-glucoside (30). Enzymatic glycosylation, particularly using the enzyme UGT75AJ2 from V. corymbosum, is presented here as a superior alternative to traditional synthesis for producing valuable flavonoid glycosides. UGT75AJ2, unique for its dual 3′-OH and 7-OH glycosylation activity, expands the chemical diversity of flavonoid glycosides, vital for advancing drug discovery. However, the applications of naturally occurring 3′-O or 7-O-glycosyltransferases are often limited by their low catalytic activity and specificity.

To address these challenges, we first designed a targeted structure-guided directed evolution strategy to optimize the UGT75AJ2 enzyme for efficient glycosylation applications (32, 35). Our engineered quadruple mutant, Mut4-1, exhibited a remarkable 128-fold improvement in activity toward C3G (1) compared to the WT. The mutant Mut4-1 showed a Km value of 63.46 ± 2.71 μM for the substrate, representing a 4.43-fold reduction compared to the WT enzyme, which indicated enhanced enzyme-substrate affinity due to the lower Km. Furthermore, Mut4-1 demonstrated a kcat/Km value of 0.81 ± 0.011 mM−1·s−1, which was 73.64-fold higher than that of the WT enzyme, signifying a remarkable improvement in catalytic efficiency. In comparison, Wang et al. (58) used structure-guided rational design to improve the catalytic activity of the glycosyltransferase UGT71AP2, resulting in the optimal mutant (L138T/V179D/M180T) that exhibited a 1.99-fold increase in the catalytic activity of 2′-O-glycosylation. This mutant also demonstrated a 2.96-fold decrease in Km value (from 97.40 ± 12.09 μM to 32.87 ± 5.21 μM) and a 12.1-fold increase in kcat/Km value (from 0.019 μM−1·s−1 to 0.23 μM−1·s−1) compared to the WT (58). Huang et al. (59) used structure-based site-directed mutagenesis and activity-based sequence conservation analysis of the glycosyltransferase UGT74AN2, yielding the optimal mutant (I284R/W390H/V391G). This mutant displayed an 11.4-fold increase in catalytic activity compared to the WT, which had a Km of 10.38 μM and a kcat/Km value of 1.58 mM−1·s−1 (59). In addition, Guo et al. (60) conducted rational design of the glycosyltransferase UGT76G1, resulting in the optimal mutant (UGT76G1-T284S/M88L/L200A). This mutant exhibited a 2.38-fold increase in catalytic activity, a 1.93-fold decrease in Km value (from 627.32 ± 10.5 μM to 324.25 ± 7.33 μM), along with a 2.99-fold increase in kcat/Km value (from 2.47 ± 0.19 mM−1·s−1 to 7.38 ± 0.43 mM−1·s−1), compared to the WT (60). Therefore, compared with previous reports, our customized FRISM strategy was efficient in generating the optimal quadruple mutant Mut4-1, which exhibited significantly enhanced catalytic activity. MD simulations highlighted that Mut4-1 formed more stabilizing hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, benefits from an enlarged and adaptable substrate binding pocket. Mut4-1 also exhibited an extensive substrate range and elevated activity across multiple medicinal flavonoid classes, surpassing the WT in versatility and performance and promising a broader applicability in the natural drug compound library. Furthermore, our tailored strategy of protein engineering is also applicable to UGTs with higher catalytic activities from other plants. This convenient, reliable, and universal method of protein engineering is of notable reference and inspiration for improving the cumbersome, costly, and lengthy high-throughput screening methods currently used in traditional enzyme engineering.

Moreover, the high degree of sugar donor promiscuity is as important as the broader substrate spectrum of glycosyltransferases in natural drug discovery (61). Recent studies have shown that glycosyl moiety not only plays an important role in the structural diversity of flavonoids, but also has a major influence on their bioactivities (59, 62). For example, the rare and costly apigenin-7-O-glucoside exhibits excellent antitumor activity (63), while apigenin-7-O-galactoside has antioxidant effects (64). In addition, apigenin-7-O-glucuronide and apigenin-7-O-xyloside respectively exhibit therapeutic functions for cardiovascular diseases and certain antibacterial activities (65–67). Therefore, increasing the glycosyl diversity of flavonoid glycosides through protein engineering techniques may be an effective way to contribute to natural drug development (68, 69).

Despite these advances, our observations revealed that the WT UGT75AJ2 is restricted to using only UDP-Glc. Although the Mut4-1 mutant can act on other sugar donors like UDP-Gal, UDP-GlcNAc, and UDP-Xyl, its transfer rates remain suboptimal. This study represented the comprehensive structure-guided sequence analysis of the sugar donor binding region in UGT75AJ2. By comparing residues across five zones (A to E) with those of other highly promiscuous glycosyltransferases, we were able to engineer the UGT75AJ2 enzyme to construct the triple mutant S14G/F366H/S367G. This mutant showed significantly improved catalytic activities over Mut4-1, particularly with apigenin (15) as a substrate, where it nearly achieved 100% utilization rates for UDP-GlcNAc and UDP-Xyl.

In addition to enhancing donor promiscuity, engineering the regioselectivity of glycosyltransferase to meet the requirements for specific site glycosylation is also a major challenge in the natural drug synthesis field (20, 25, 70). For flavonoid biosynthesis, only a few pivotal residues around the hydrophobic substrate binding pocket had been proven to be effective in regioselective control (35). Until now, traditional directed evolution has also been applied to refine regioselectivity. However, these labor-intensive methods have limited adoption due to their reliance on extensive screening. Therefore, there is a clear need for a systematic and universally applicable approach to engineer flavonoid glycosylation regioselectivity.

In response, we further devised a strategy to engineer UGT75AJ2’s regioselectivity using a FRISM-based combinatorial mutagenesis approach, eliminating the need for large mutation libraries. Key active site residues identified through virtual screening were mutated to alter the pocket’s conformation and properties. In particular, residues 132 and 367 were pinpointed as crucial for regioselectivity, leading to further saturation mutagenesis. Combinatorial mutagenesis on the highest regioselectivity mutants yielded two triple mutants, Y75E/F185Y/I132T and L112F/M273E/S367D, with 99 and 85% selectivity for the 3′-O and 7-O sites of C3G (1), respectively. These concerted efforts in achieving precise and efficient glycosylation of anthocyanins at the 3′-O and 7-O positions establish a robust foundation for the biosynthesis of rare, naturally occurring blue pigments, thereby promoting the application of natural blue pigments in plant breeding, food, and pharmaceutical industries. Notably, mutant S367F was able to glycosylate the 5-O site of C3G (1), an activity not seen in WT or previous mutants. This groundbreaking capability offers fresh perspectives on the biosynthesis of 5-O-glycosylated anthocyanins, such as cyanidin-3,5-O-diglucoside and malvidin-3,5-O-diglucoside, with various medicinal potential (71, 72). Testing our protein engineering strategy with quercetin (18), mutants I132E and S367W demonstrated strict selectivity for the 3′-OH and 7-OH sites, respectively. Molecular docking and MD simulations provided insight into the selective glycosylation preferences of these mutants.

Overall, this study marks a notable leap forward in the field of O-glycosyltransferase protein engineering. Using a directed evolution strategy that integrates virtual screening and sequence and structure-guided enzyme modifications, we present a powerful, swift, and versatile methodology for augmenting enzyme functionality. This innovative approach not only elevates catalytic efficiency but also expands the spectrum of glycosyl donors and enhances regioselectivity among glycosyltransferases. Such advancements hold the promise of substantially boosting the targeted and efficient production of natural flavonoid glycosides with therapeutic potential.

Rational design of mutations in UGT75AJ2

All the selected active residues were separately mutated to alanine using primers (table S7) containing GCG codon at specified sites. ISM was conducted using degenerate primer NNK (table S11) on plasmid template from the last round of mutation. The other specific primers for site-directed or combined mutagenesis are provided in tables S8 to S10, S12, and S13. All different mutants were created by the recombinant vector pET32a-UGT75AJ2. First, the whole mutant plasmids were obtained by PCR using KOD One PCR Master Mix-Blue-polymerase and primers. Next, the PCR product was incubated with DMT enzyme (TransGen, Beijing) at 30°C for 1 hour. Electrophoresis of the incubated product was identified, and the product was purified by gel extraction kit. The mutant PCR product was sent to Tsingke Biotechnology for sequencing. Last, the pure PCR product was transformed into DMT competent cells, followed by heterologous expression as described above. The catalytic activity of mutants was measured in 200 μl of the reaction buffer, including 50 μg of purified mutants, 0.5 mM acceptor, 1.0 mM UDP-Glc, and 50 mM tris-HCl. A unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to consume 1 μmol of substrate per minute.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, USA). Data were presented as means ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments. The normality and the homogeneity of variance were checked by Shapiro-Wilk test before applying Student’s t test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Student’s t test was used to compare data between two groups, while one-way ANOVA analysis was used for comparison between multiple groups. The boxes and error bars in the figures represent mean values and standard deviation, respectively. Statistical significance was detected if P < 0.05.
