Homebound older adults in self-care using a mobile health-assisted, person-centred care approach: study protocol for a hybrid effectiveness-implementation design
Authors: Arkers Kwan Ching Wong, Frances Kam Yuet Wong, Shamay Sheung Mei Ng, Martin Chi Sang Wong, Axel Wolf, Andy Shu Kei Cheng, Ka Wu, Namkee G Choi, Paul Hong Lee, Rick Yiu Cho Kwan, Ruiyi Liu, Yung Pong Leung, Shun Yan Choi

AbstractBackgroundHomebound older adults often struggle with managing their health due to reduced mobility, limited social support, and fragmented access to healthcare services. Person-centred care (PCC), which emphasizes individualized support and active patient engagement, has demonstrated benefits in promoting self-management for chronic conditions. When delivered through mobile health (mHealth) technologies, PCC can be extended into the home environment, offering timely, tailored support. Background

The global aging population presents both opportunities and challenges for healthcare systems, particularly in supporting older adults with chronic conditions and limited mobility [1]. Among them, homebound older adults represent a growing yet underserved subgroup who, despite facing barriers such as restricted access to healthcare, social isolation, and elevated risks of physical and psychological decline [2], retain a strong desire to maintain independence and quality of life. As healthcare systems increasingly transition toward community-based care, there is a recognized need to move beyond conventional provider-led models—which often reinforce passive care-seeking—toward strategies that promote self-efficacy and empower older adults to actively manage their health [3]. Fostering autonomy and engagement not only enhances individual well-being but also contributes to the sustainability of healthcare systems [4].

Various home healthcare programs developed by governments and community organizations have sought to address the challenges encountered by homebound older adults. These programs aim to enhance functional independence, improve mental health, and facilitate healthcare access through home-based care and outreach services [5]. For instance, China’s Home Health Care (HHC) program [6] and the United States’ Home-Based Primary Care (HBPC) program [7] have shown effectiveness in improving clinical outcomes, reducing hospitalizations, and enhancing patient satisfaction. However, these programs are largely provider-driven, often limiting opportunities for older adults to build self-management capabilities. Furthermore, many of these initiatives struggle with workforce shortages and financial constraints, limiting their scalability and long-term viability, particularly in resource-constrained settings [8]. A recent study reported that while 80% of homebound older adults received some form of long-term care, nearly half (46.6%) received only minimal support, highlighting systemic barriers and an urgent need for scalable, cost-effective solutions [9].

Person-centred care (PCC) has emerged as a highly recommended approach in geriatric care, emphasizing individualized care planning, shared decision-making processes, and the active involvement of patients in managing and co-creation of their healthcare plan [10]. By aligning care delivery with older adults’ preferences and capabilities, PCC has been shown to improve self-efficacy, adherence to self-care routines, and overall health outcomes [11]. Although PCC has demonstrated effectiveness in institutional and long-term care settings, its implementation to support home-based self-care among older adults remains limited [12]. Scaling such interventions to reach homebound populations is further hindered by resource constraints, high provider workloads, and logistical challenges in delivering care directly to the home.

Mobile health (mHealth) technologies offer a promising pathway to overcome these limitations and extend the reach of PCC interventions. Evidence from community-based digital health programs supports the feasibility of using information and communication technologies to address both health and social needs in older populations [13]. mHealth platforms can deliver low-cost, accessible, and user-friendly tools for remote engagement, health education, and care coordination [14]. Popular applications such as WhatsApp and Zoom can facilitate real-time monitoring, personalized support, and psychosocial interaction. Given the growing adoption of smartphones among older adults in Hong Kong and their enhanced digital literacy following the COVID-19 pandemic [15], PCC-based mHealth interventions are particularly well-positioned to bridge healthcare gaps and promote sustained self-care practices among homebound older adults.

Emerging evidence underscores the interconnection between physical and mental health in homebound older adults, reinforcing the importance of integrated, person-centred approaches to self-care [16]. Although PCC has demonstrated effectiveness in institutional and long-term care settings, its application to support self-management in homebound populations—especially through scalable, technology-enabled interventions—remains limited. To address this gap, this study develops and evaluates a PCC-based mHealth intervention designed to enhance self-management capabilities among homebound older adults. The intervention draws on Bandura’s self-efficacy theory [17], which highlights the role of individuals’ confidence in their ability to carry out health-related behaviors as a key driver of sustained behavior change. Complementing this, the intervention is also guided by the Gothenburg Centre for Person-Centred Care (GPCC) model [18], which emphasizes shared decision-making, individualized care planning, and active patient involvement. Together, these frameworks inform a personalized, digitally supported intervention that combines structured health education, social encouragement, and collaborative goal setting. By targeting the psychosocial, behavioral, and environmental barriers to self-care, this study aims to generate robust evidence on both the clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes of PCC-based mHealth interventions. The findings will inform the design of scalable, community-based care models, enhance support for aging in place, and contribute to more equitable and sustainable healthcare delivery for underserved older adult populations.

Objective

This study aims to evaluate both the clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes of a person-centred care (PCC)-based mobile health (mHealth) intervention designed to support self-care among homebound older adults. In this study, self-care is conceptualized as older adults’ ability and confidence to manage health-related needs and maintain independence in daily living, with particular emphasis on instrumental activities that support aging in place. The primary objective is to assess the effects of the intervention on self-efficacy and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). The secondary objectives are to: (a) examine the intervention’s impact on quality of life, functional mobility, perceived empowerment, psychological distress, loneliness, sedentary behaviour, and healthcare utilization; and (b) evaluate the feasibility and scalability of the intervention using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [19].

Methods

This protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [20].

Study design and setting

This study adopts a hybrid Type I effectiveness-implementation design, which primarily evaluates the intervention’s effectiveness while concurrently exploring contextual and implementation factors [21, 22]. For the effectiveness component, an assessor-blinded, two-arm cluster-randomized controlled trial (cRCT) will be conducted with assessments at three time points: baseline (T1), end of intervention at 3 months after baseline (T2), and follow-up at 6 months after baseline (T3). The primary outcomes are self-efficacy and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). Secondary outcomes include quality of life, psychological distress, loneliness, functional mobility, sedentary behaviour, healthcare utilization, and perceived empowerment.

To evaluate implementation, a mixed-method process evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework will be conducted to explore adoption, implementation fidelity, barriers, facilitators, and sustainability of the intervention.

The study will be implemented in 26 community centres across Hong Kong, operated by the Salvation Army, Yan Oi Tong, Pok Oi Hospital, and the Hong Kong Lutheran Social Service. These centres span Hong Kong Island, Kowloon, and the New Territories, enhancing the geographic and demographic diversity of participants. Centre staff will identify and refer eligible participants to the research team.

Participants

Eligible participants must meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 60 or above; (2) unable to leave home more than twice per week due to physical or functional limitations (excluding essential outings such as medical appointments); (3) own and demonstrate basic proficiency in using a smartphone; (4) cognitively competent, defined as scoring ≥ 22 on the Hong Kong version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (HK-MoCA) [23]; (5) classified as moderately frail or less, with a Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) score ≤ 6 [24]; and (6) Cantonese-speaking. Exclusion criteria include: (1) inability to communicate effectively due to sensory or cognitive impairments; (2) being completely bedbound; or (3) residing in areas without stable internet access.

Participants will receive a user-friendly manual with graphic and textual instructions for using WhatsApp, and a home-based trial run will be conducted following recruitment. Technical support will be available through a dedicated community centre hotline throughout the intervention period.

Service provider

Two nurse case managers (NCMs), matched in qualifications and experience, will deliver the intervention. NCMs are ideal facilitators of PCC due to their holistic, client-centred approach and ability to integrate clinical knowledge into individualized care [25, 26]. Each NCM will be assigned to either the intervention or control arm to ensure role consistency. Intervention fidelity will be maintained through regular supervision and monitoring. The NCMs will work in collaboration with an interdisciplinary team (IDT) comprising a physician, traditional Chinese medicine practitioner (TCMP), occupational therapist (OT), physiotherapist (PT), and social workers (SWs).

Randomization

Centre supervisors will provide lists of potentially eligible participants. Research assistants (RAs), blinded to group allocation, will conduct initial telephone screening followed by a home visit for eligibility confirmation, informed consent, training, and baseline data collection. To maintain confidentiality, each participant will be assigned a unique case identification number. The principal investigator will use a computer-generated random sequence to randomize the 26 community centres (1:1 ratio) into intervention or control arms. While blinding providers is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, outcome assessors will remain blinded throughout the study.

Intervention group and control group

Both the intervention and control groups receive the same number of contacts delivered via WhatsApp video calls. This design controls for contact frequency and delivery modality, allowing the evaluation to focus on the added value of PCC components rather than the use of mHealth technology alone.

Intervention group

Participants in the intervention group will receive six bi-weekly WhatsApp video call sessions over three months, delivered using a PCC approach that emphasizes shared decision-making, co-created care planning, and structured self-efficacy enhancement strategies. Each session will be scheduled collaboratively between the participant and their assigned NCM, who will remain the same throughout the intervention period.

Session 1

The NCM will assess participants’ preferences using the Preferences for Everyday Living Inventory (PELI) [27] and perform a comprehensive health and social needs assessment using the Omaha System [28]. Based on these assessments, the NCM will initiate care activities including counselling, guidance, case management, and follow-up surveillance.

Sessions 2–4

Participants will receive personalized educational materials (text, audio, and video) through WhatsApp. During subsequent video sessions, the Nurse Case Manager (NCM) will explain the content, address participant questions, and collaboratively develop a care plan with the participant. The care plan will specify self-care goals, required actions, and anticipated facilitators and barriers.

To operationalize these resources effectively, the NCM will play a central role in guiding participants through the care plan by providing structured support and monitoring progress. Participants will be encouraged to engage actively in self-care activities, while the NCM will assist in identifying appropriate personal and community resources. Where necessary, local NGOs or community health organizations may be involved to provide additional support, such as peer counseling, resource navigation, or financial aid for necessary interventions.

Bandura’s self-efficacy techniques—such as goal-setting, mastery experiences, modeling, and verbal encouragement—will be systematically embedded into the process. The NCM will facilitate these strategies by helping participants set achievable goals, providing examples of successful self-care practices, and offering continuous reinforcement to enhance motivation and adherence to the care plan [17].

Sessions 5–6

Participants and a designated family member will receive a WhatsApp message summarizing their preferences, needs, care plan, and goals. The NCM will help evaluate participants’ progress and encourage reflection on affective and physiological states related to their self-care practices, reinforcing mastery and self-efficacy.

Referrals and Interdisciplinary Support:

With participant consent, NCMs may initiate referrals to the IDT according to established referral protocols. A monthly case conference will be held online, involving the participant, NCM, and relevant team members to discuss concerns and optimize care strategies.

Control group

Control group participants will also receive six bi-weekly WhatsApp video calls over three months, delivered care will follow a conventional provider-led model without participant involvement in goal-setting, shared decision-making, or structured self-efficacy enhancement strategies. The same assessments (PELI and Omaha System) will be conducted in the first session. However, the care plan will be developed solely by the NCM, without participant involvement in goal-setting or self-efficacy techniques. In subsequent sessions, the NCM will monitor progress and make adjustments based on standard protocols. While referrals to the IDT may occur, participants will not interact with team members directly. The care approach remains conventional, with minimal personalization and no structured strategies for empowering self-management.

Service provider training and supervision

A 10-day virtual training workshop will be conducted by the PI, GPCC director, and co-investigators. The training will cover: PELI, Omaha System, PCC principles (for intervention NCMs), referral pathways, documentation procedures, video call protocols, and WhatsApp troubleshooting. A simulated case exercise and a standardized competency assessment will be used to ensure readiness. Prior to launch, a reinforcement workshop will be held. Monthly training sessions will continue throughout the study to review PCC principles and address implementation challenges.

Intervention fidelity will be monitored through video recordings, supervision by GPCC coordinators, and compliance checklists.

Implementation advisory group

An implementation advisory group (n = 14), consisting of GPCC leaders, community centre managers, and interdisciplinary team members (TCMP, OT, PT, SW), will convene for six structured meetings. The group will support recruitment strategies, tailor site-specific implementation procedures, refine evaluation methods, and define IDT roles. The group will also engage in bi-monthly consultations during implementation and participate in a post-programme sustainability planning meeting. Minutes will be documented by RAs to support real-time evaluation and inform continuous improvement efforts.

Outcome measures

Demographic variables

Demographic variables will be assessed at baseline, including age, sex, marital status, number of chronic conditions, primary reason for homebound status, household composition, use of home care services, housing type, home accessibility, use of assistive devices, and time spent by informal caregivers with participants.

Co-primary outcomes

Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy, a core construct in PCC, reflects an individual's confidence in managing health and social needs. It will be measured using the Chinese version of the 10-item General SelfEfficacy Scale (CGSE), based on Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy [29, 30]. Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The CGSE has demonstrated excellent reliability in older Chinese adults (Cronbach’s α = .89).

Instrumental activities of daily living (IADL)

The selection of IADL as a co-primary functional outcome because they are more sensitive than basic activities of daily living (ADL) to early functional changes among community dwelling and homebound older adults. IADL will be assessed using the Chinese version of the 9-item Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (C-LIADL) [31, 32]. Each item is rated from 0 (dependent) to 3 (independent), with higher total scores indicating greater functional independence. The C-LIADL has demonstrated strong inter-rater reliability (0.99) and test–retest reliability (0.90).

Secondary outcomes

Quality of life

The Chinese version of the 12-item Short Form Health Survey (C-SF-12v2) [33, 34] will be used to measure health-related quality of life. The instrument generates physical and mental health component scores based on Likert-scale responses, with norm-based scoring used for interpretation. The C-SF-12v2 is validated in the Hong Kong Chinese population.

Psychological distress

Psychological distress will be assessed using the Chinese version of the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (C-DASS-21) [35, 36], which includes three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Higher scores indicate greater distress. The C-DASS-21 has demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.95).

Loneliness

Loneliness will be measured using the Chinese version of the UCLA 3-item Loneliness Scale [37, 38]. Total scores range from 3 to 9, with higher scores reflecting greater perceived loneliness. While scores of 3 and 4 may correspond to mild and moderate depressive symptoms, the tool is not diagnostic.

Functional mobilitynal mobility

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test [39] will assess functional mobility by timing participants as they rise from a chair, walk three meters, turn, return, and sit down. A duration under 20 s is considered a clinical benchmark of functional independence in daily activities. A change exceeding 2.08 s is deemed clinically meaningful [40].

Sedentary behaviour

Sedentary behaviour will be measured using the Chinese version of the Sedentary Behaviour Questionnaire for the Elderly [41], a validated 10-item instrument assessing time spent in sedentary activities. It has demonstrated strong psychometric properties.

Health service utilization

Health service utilization will include self-reported data on unplanned outpatient visits, general practitioner consultations, emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and other healthcare engagements over the three months preceding each time point (T1, T2, and T3). These self-reports will be cross-validated using medical records and attendance certificates to enhance reliability.

Self-care empowerment

Empowerment in self-care will be assessed using the Chinese version of the 44-item Client Empowerment Scale (CCES) [42, 43]. Scores range from 44 to 220, with higher scores indicating greater perceived empowerment. The CCES has demonstrated high internal consistency and test–retest reliability among older adult populations.

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcomes will be evaluated using the RE-AIM framework [19].

Reach

Reach will be assessed by documenting the number and proportion of eligible individuals who enroll in the study, as recorded in recruitment logs.

Adoption

Adoption will examine the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention from both participant and provider perspectives. Participant satisfaction will be measured using the Chinese version of the 15-item Four-Dimension Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (C-4D-CSQ) [44], with total scores ranging from 15 to 75 (higher scores indicate greater satisfaction). Satisfaction surveys will be administered at T2 and T3. In addition, focus group interviews with participants and providers will be conducted at T2 to explore their experiences with the PCC-based intervention, perceived barriers and facilitators, and suggestions for refinement.

Implementation fidelity

Fidelity will be assessed by evaluating adherence to the intervention protocol. Reasons for missed video call sessions will be documented for each participant. A monthly performance checklist will be completed by the principal investigator (PI) for both arms. In addition, approximately 5% of all participant records and video call recordings (n ≈ 6) will be randomly selected for review by the PI to assess fidelity.

Maintenance

Maintenance will be assessed at both the individual and program levels.

At the individual level, sustained effects on co-primary and secondary outcomes will be evaluated at 3-month follow-up (T3). Dropout rates, reasons for attrition, and any participant-initiated group switches will be documented in the CONSORT diagram (Table 1).

At the program level, a cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted to determine the feasibility of future scaling. Costs will be calculated from both provider and societal perspectives using an ingredient-based costing approach [45]. The provider perspective will include program implementation costs, healthcare and social care utilization, and staff time. The societal perspective will include all costs excluding public-sector co-payments and out-of-pocket participant expenses.

Total costs and outcomes will be compared between groups using the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), expressed as cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained. QALYs will be derived from utility scores based on C-SF-12v2 data [46].

Sample size

Sample size calculations were conducted to ensure 80% power to detect a clinically meaningful effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.536) in the primary outcome (CGSE scores), with a two-tailed significance level of 5% [47]. Accounting for the cluster-randomized design, an intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 3.64% was applied [48]. Assuming an average of five participants per cluster across 26 community centres, the design-adjusted required sample size is 65 participants per arm, yielding a total of 130 participants.

To evaluate the acceptability of the PCC-based intervention and explore contextual implementation factors, focus group interviews will be conducted with key stakeholders. Participants will include: one NCM), one physician, one TCMP, one OT, one PT, 26 social workers (one per community centre), and approximately 20% of participants from the intervention group (n = 13).

Recruitment timelines

Recruitment will be conducted over a 24-month period. Community centres will provide lists of potential participants weekly. Initial contact and screening by RAs via phone will be completed as soon as possible of receiving participant lists depending on the length of the lists. Home visits for eligibility confirmation, consent, and baseline data collection will be based on participants’ available time over the phone screening. The recruitment status and adherence to timelines will be monitored by the PI.

Data collection

Quantitative data will be collected at three time points: baseline (T1), end of intervention at 3 months after baseline (T2), and follow-up at 6 months after baseline (T3) to evaluate both immediate effects and programme sustainability. At each time point, trained research assistants (RAs), who are blinded to group allocation, will conduct in-home surveys to collect participant data. To ensure data accuracy and quality assurance, a randomly selected 5% of the data will be independently reviewed.

Qualitative data will be collected through semi-structured focus group interviews following completion of the intervention (T2). Interviews will be facilitated by two experienced qualitative researchers using a semi-structured topic guide informed by the Social Ecological Model (SEM) [49]. The guide will explore individual, interpersonal, organizational, and system-level factors influencing the adoption and perceived value of the PCC approach. Each focus group will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically to identify key implementation insights.

Data management

Strict measures will be implemented to safeguard participant confidentiality and data security throughout and after the study. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identification code, minimizing the use of personal data wherever possible. Only authorized research team members will have access to personal and research data. Responsible parties from The Hong Kong Polytechnic University may access data for monitoring and auditing purposes.

All data management procedures will adhere to university policies and ethical guidelines. Identifiable paper records will be stored in locked cabinets within the School of Nursing. Quantitative data will be entered into encrypted, password-protected databases immediately after collection. Qualitative data, including audio recordings and transcripts, will be stored on encrypted servers, and all transcripts will be anonymized during transcription. Daily data backups will be performed. Identifiable data will be securely destroyed three years after study completion, while anonymized data will be archived indefinitely for future secondary analyses.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data analysis will be conducted using R statistical software [50]. Descriptive statistics (e.g., means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, and frequencies) will be used to summarize baseline characteristics and outcome variables at T1, T2, and T3.

To evaluate intervention effects over time, linear mixed-effects models will be employed for continuous outcomes (e.g., CGSE, C-LIADL, C-SF-12v2, C-DASS-21), and generalized linear mixed-effects models will be used for categorical or count-based outcomes (e.g., healthcare utilization). Models will incorporate an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated measures and clustering within community centres. The primary analysis will focus on the group-by-time interaction to determine differential effects between intervention and control arms over time.

Relevant covariates (e.g., age, baseline health status, chronic conditions, socioeconomic status) will be adjusted in the models. Multiple imputation techniques will address missing data, and sensitivity analyses will include complete-case analysis and the exclusion of influential clusters. Exploratory subgroup analyses will be conducted to assess intervention effect variability across predefined demographic and clinical groups. Statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05.

Qualitative data will be analyzed using a directed content analysis approach. The analysis will follow a three-phase process: (1) open coding to identify key concepts, (2) synthesis to recognize patterns and relationships, and (3) theorization to interpret participant perspectives regarding the acceptability and implementation of the intervention. All focus group interviews will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim in Chinese, and managed using NVivo 14 software to ensure transparency and traceability. Codes will be organized into key themes related to acceptability, facilitators, barriers, and contextual adaptation.

To ensure methodological rigor, several strategies will be employed: transcripts will be cross-verified against audio recordings to ensure accuracy; two experienced researchers will independently code the first two transcripts and resolve discrepancies through discussion to ensure coding consistency. A research team member will oversee the coding and thematic development process to maintain analytical integrity through investigator triangulation. Additional quality assurance methods will include maintaining a detailed audit trail, participant validation (member checking), data triangulation, and the use of reflective field notes.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy, a core construct in PCC, reflects an individual's confidence in managing health and social needs. It will be measured using the Chinese version of the 10-item General Self-Efficacy Scale (CGSE), based on Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy [29, 30]. Total scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The CGSE has demonstrated excellent reliability in older Chinese adults (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Discussion

This study evaluates the clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes of a PCC-based, mHealth-assisted self-care support program for homebound older adults in Hong Kong. By embedding PCC principles—such as collaborative goal-setting and individualized care planning—into a digitally delivered intervention, the study aims to address a key gap in existing models of home-based care: the lack of tailored, goal-oriented support for self-management among older adults with restricted mobility. Delivered via bi-weekly WhatsApp video calls, the intervention is designed to enhance participants' self-efficacy, functional independence, and overall well-being, compared to a conventional provider-led care model.

While existing home health care programs report high satisfaction with provider relationships and early intervention capabilities, they often suffer from limited contact time and inadequate follow-up [6]. This study directly addresses these gaps by offering a structured, low-cost, and scalable mHealth intervention that maintains continuity of care and promotes patient engagement. By combining structured telecare delivery with PCC principles, this model offers a viable alternative or complement to traditional provider-led approaches and is responsive to the needs of a diverse and often underserved population.

Importantly, this intervention aligns with Moo and Schwartz’s [51] call for telehealth models that reflect the 4Ms of age-friendly care: mobility, mentation, medication, and what matters most. Building upon insights from a pilot trial conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [47], the intervention incorporates self-efficacy-enhancing techniques, goal alignment with patient priorities, and multidisciplinary support. Future iterations may benefit from deeper integration of virtual multidisciplinary care and a more holistic digital ecosystem to further optimize outcomes in geriatric populations.

Implementation considerations

This study adopts a pragmatic and innovation-driven approach to implementation, designed to anticipate real-world challenges in delivering person-centred digital care to homebound older adults. Rather than relying solely on standard implementation metrics, the intervention is embedded within the operational workflows of 26 community centres—leveraging existing community assets, social infrastructure, and staff relationships to support program uptake and sustainability. This contextually grounded approach enhances both scalability and ecological validity.

To overcome well-documented digital access barriers among older adults [52], the intervention goes beyond mere technology provision. It includes a tailored onboarding process featuring visual guides, WhatsApp-based practice sessions, and a real-time technical support hotline—designed not only to improve usability, but also to foster digital confidence and long-term engagement with technology. Importantly, this support system was co-developed with frontline workers and older adults during pilot testing, ensuring that it responds to actual, not assumed, needs.

The fidelity of the PCC delivery will be actively monitored using a combination of video call observations, participant reflections, and structured checklists completed by researchers. However, fidelity is not treated as static adherence—it will be assessed dynamically through a relational lens that prioritizes trust, adaptability, and participant-defined relevance, all essential features in the delivery of PCC to socially isolated individuals.

Crucially, this study also introduces participatory feedback mechanisms to support continuous refinement. Post-intervention interviews will not only assess acceptability but also capture insights into how participants believe the programme should evolve. In line with emerging models of community-engaged research [53], future iterations of this intervention may establish an ongoing advisory group composed of older adults, caregivers, and service providers who co-interpret findings and co-design next-phase improvements.

From a system-level perspective, the study’s cost-effectiveness analysis is designed to inform value-based resource allocation—a key consideration for aging societies. Yet beyond conventional costing, this study also explores implementation return-on-investment: the potential for mHealth-facilitated PCC to reduce avoidable health service use, preserve functional independence, and delay institutionalization, all of which have significant implications for health system sustainability. By embedding implementation inquiry directly into a community health infrastructure, this study serves as a prototype for how PCC-based mHealth models can be translated from research into scalable practice.

Conclusion

This study is one of the first cluster randomized controlled trials in Hong Kong to evaluate a PCC-based mHealth intervention designed specifically for homebound older adults. By combining evidence-based behavioural theory with PCC principles and accessible digital technology, the intervention aims to improve self-care capabilities, promote functional independence, and enhance quality of life among a vulnerable and underserved population.

Findings from this trial will contribute valuable evidence to support the integration of PCC-based digital health interventions into routine geriatric care. The study also offers practical guidance for community healthcare providers seeking to implement scalable, low-cost mHealth solutions, and provides a model for delivering personalized care that respects individual priorities while addressing structural limitations. Through its dual focus on clinical outcomes and implementation feasibility, the study has the potential to inform health policy, reduce disparities in access to care, and strengthen community-based healthcare delivery for homebound older adults.
