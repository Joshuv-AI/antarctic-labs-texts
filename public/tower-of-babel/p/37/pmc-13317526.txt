Right internal carotid artery C7 segment aneurysm with carotid occlusion following traumatic brain injury: illustrative case
Authors: Qiang Chen, Yuyang Pan, Wen Tan, Kuangyang Yu, Yong Jiang, Jianhua Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntracranial aneurysms caused by trauma are rare, and occlusion of the carotid vessels caused by trauma is also uncommon. The authors report on the case of a patient who had an internal carotid artery (ICA) aneurysm accompanied by occlusion because of a car accident.OBSERVATIONSA 37-year-old man presented after a motor vehicle accident with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, multiple skull fractures, and right ICA occlusion. Initial CT angiography and digital subtraction angiography showed no aneurysm, and emergency external ventricular drainage was performed. One month later, repeat angiography demonstrated a right C7 ICA aneurysm. CT perfusion showed mild hypoperfusion in the right hemisphere. The patient underwent craniotomy with aneurysm trapping and superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass. He later required ventriculoperitoneal shunt insertion for hydrocephalus. Follow-up imaging showed improved perfusion, but marked neurological deficits remained at discharge.LESSONSIn selected patients with traumatic aneurysm and carotid occlusion, microsurgical trapping combined with extracranial-intracranial bypass can be a practical treatment option when endovascular therapy is not suitable. Blunt or penetrating injury to the intracranial vasculature can result in traumatic intracranial aneurysms. Intracranial aneurysms due to trauma are rare, accounting for less than 1% of all intracranial aneurysms, but they are associated with a high mortality rate.1 Internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion caused by trauma is also rare,2,3 and the occurrence of both caused by trauma is even rarer. Here, we report a case of right ICA C7 segment aneurysm accompanied by right ICA occlusion following traumatic brain injury (TBI).

Illustrative Case

At Admission

A 37-year-old man was admitted to the emergency department due to a car accident that left him unconscious. His left pupil was 2.5 mm with a slow light response. The right pupil could not be examined because of the swollen right lid. Positive signs included neck stiffness, bilateral Babinski signs, and intermittent twitching of the extremities. The patient underwent tracheal intubation and had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E1VTM2 = 3T. Head CT (Fig. 1A– C) showed severe subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and multiple skull fractures, and CT angiography (CTA) (Fig. 1D) showed occlusion of the right ICA. Subsequently, the patient underwent digital subtraction angiography (DSA) (Fig. 1E and F), which showed the same results as CTA. Despite severe SAH at admission, no aneurysm was identified on the initial CTA or DSA study. The patient’s family refused endovascular recanalization of the occluded ICA, and emergency external ventricular drainage (EVD) was performed.

A–C: Head CT images showing severe hemorrhage in the suprasellar cistern and ventricle and multiple fractures of the right frontal bone, temporal bone, sphenoid bone, zygomatic bone, maxillary sinus, and orbital bone. The skull fragments were displaced to the area of the right ICA C5–6 segments. D: CT angiogram showing no contrast filling in the right ICA C1–6 segments; only the C7 segment was visualized. E and F: The anterior communicating artery (ACoA) was open, the right cerebral hemisphere was supplied by the left ICA via the ACoA, and the vessels in the right hemisphere were slender and poorly visualized.

One Month After Admission

CTA was performed again 1 month after admission, showing an aneurysm in the C7 segment of the right ICA (Fig. 2A). CT perfusion (CTP) showed mild hypoperfusion in the right frontal, parietal, temporal, and insular regions (Fig. 2B). The patient underwent craniotomy for C7 aneurysm trapping and superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass surgery. Postoperative CTA suggested improved cerebrovascular visualization in the right hemisphere (Fig. 2C). Although he underwent EVD, hydrocephalus was not relieved after severe SAH (Fig. 3A). He underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement 1 month after aneurysm trapping (Fig. 3B). Soon after the operation, he was transferred to the department of rehabilitation for follow-up treatment.

A: CT angiogram showing an aneurysm in the right ICA C7 segment. B: CTP image showing mild hypoperfusion in the right frontal, parietal, temporal and insular regions; a mild decrease of cerebral blood volume (CBV) and cerebral blood flow (CBF); and mild prolongation of mean transit time (MTT) and time to peak (TTP). C: After STA-MCA bypass, the number of visualized vessels in the right hemisphere increased compared with the previous CT angiogram.

A:CT image showing hydrocephalus with interstitial cerebral edema. B: CT image demonstrating postoperative changes consistent with ventriculoperitoneal shunt insertion at the 6-month follow-up.

Condition at Discharge and Follow-Up

At discharge, the patient had right eyelid ptosis and deviation of the mouth to the right. The left pupil was 3 mm and reactive to light, whereas the right pupil was 5 mm and nonreactive. Muscle strength was grade 0 in the left limbs and grade 3 in the right limbs. His modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was 5, his GCS score was 12 (E4V3M5), and his Glasgow Outcome Scale score was 3. At the 3-month follow-up visit, his cognitive function had slightly improved, and he was able to perform simple tasks such as opening and closing his eyes and protruding his tongue in response to commands.

Discussion

Traumatic intracranial aneurysms can be divided into three types according to different formation mechanisms, including pseudoaneurysms, mixed aneurysms, and dissecting aneurysms.4–6 Pseudoaneurysms are caused by the rupture of the artery wall. A hematoma is formed around the rupture, a false lumen is formed inside the hematoma, and the aneurysm wall consists mainly of fibrous tissue

The rupture is communicated with the false lumen, forming an aneurysm without vascular wall structure. Mixed aneurysms refer to the formation of a hematoma around the rupture of a true aneurysm, and the hematoma gradually develops into a pseudoaneurysm. Dissecting aneurysms are formed when the injury leads to separation of the intima and the media of the vessel to form a false lumen, and the blood flows into the false lumen to form the distension.5

There are no clear diagnostic criteria for traumatic aneurysms at present, and most of the published reports rely on the authors’ inferences based on the patient’s trauma history and imaging findings (CTA/DSA). A small number of traumatic aneurysms could be definitively diagnosed, because the authors resected or clipped the patient’s aneurysm, submitted the wall tissue for pathological examination, and confirmed that it was a pseudoaneurysm.7 Traumatic aneurysms caused by traffic accidents are often accompanied by skull fractures.8–10 After sustaining a TBI, the friction of the dura mater, bony prominences, or skull fragments against the blood vessel may damage the vessel wall, which can lead to the formation of traumatic aneurysms.11,12 In our case, the patient sustained multiple skull fractures due to trauma (frontal bone, temporal bone, sphenoid bone, zygomatic bone, maxillary sinus, and orbital wall), and the skull fragments were displaced to the area of the right ICA C5–6 segments; therefore, we believe that the formation of a pseudoaneurysm in a patient may be caused by an injury due to the friction between the ICA wall and skull fragments. As for the cause of his ICA occlusion, it could be an acute dissection of the carotid vessel caused by the blunt compression of the helmet worn during the accident. Given the trauma history and vascular findings, a dissecting pseudoaneurysm was also considered in the differential diagnosis. Traumatic intracranial aneurysms account for 0.23%–0.64% of all intracranial aneurysms, which may be less than the true incidence, because most are asymptomatic in the early stage and are often missed or diagnosed when severe symptoms appear. Therefore, CTA and DSA should be performed for patients with severe TBI, skull fracture (especially skull base fracture), sudden deterioration of neurological symptoms, or severe SAH.13 Even if the first examination is negative, a short follow-up examination is necessary because not all traumatic intracranial aneurysms can be diagnosed on the day of admission or at the first CTA/DSA study.14,15

There are various treatments for traumatic intracranial aneurysms, and endovascular therapy is currently the preferred option.16 It has also been shown in some literature that coiling is not suitable for pseudoaneurysms caused by trauma, because coiling will lead to a higher risk of intraoperative rupture and postoperative recurrence.17,18 In this case, the patient’s family refused endovascular treatment, so we did not pursue endovascular therapy for the aneurysm and the ICA occlusion. CTP showed hypoperfusion in the right hemisphere, and we therefore decided to perform right ICA aneurysm clipping and STA-MCA bypass surgery. The management of a traumatic aneurysm should be tailored to the patient’s specific circumstances. In the absence of contraindications, endovascular therapy may be preferred because it is less invasive. As for combined ICA occlusion, there have been cases of successful conservative treatment.15,19 DSA and CTP may help determine whether cerebral perfusion is adequately compensated and whether surgical intervention is necessary.

Common causes of secondary hydrocephalus include SAH, TBI, stroke, neural tumors, and CNS infections, which can lead to cognitive decline and even dementia.20 The patient in this case had severe SAH at admission and developed shunt-dependent hydrocephalus, and had to undergo ventriculoperitoneal shunt insertion at a later stage. The patient was discharged with a poor prognosis (mRS score 5), which may be related to the severe hydrocephalus and cerebral edema that occurred soon after admission. A study showed that rupture of a posterior circulation aneurysm, a low GCS score on admission, a high Fisher score, an excessive hematoma in the ventricles, and a low glucose content in the CSF may increase the risk of chronic hydrocephalus, while lumbar puncture or EVD can reduce the incidence of hydrocephalus.21

Observations

This case illustrates two clinically important points. First, traumatic aneurysms may not be visible on initial vascular imaging and can appear later during follow-up. Second, in selected patients with traumatic aneurysm accompanied by carotid occlusion and impaired hemispheric perfusion, microsurgical trapping with extracranial-intracranial bypass may be a reasonable treatment strategy when endovascular treatment is not feasible.

Lessons

We report a case of right ICA C7 segment aneurysm accompanied by occlusion after severe TBI. When traumatic aneurysm and carotid occlusion occur simultaneously, endovascular therapy may be preferred in appropriate patients. When it is not feasible to perform endovascular treatment, microsurgical trapping of the aneurysm combined with extracranial-intracranial bypass may be considered in selected patients. For TBI patients with skull fractures or severe SAH, traumatic intracranial aneurysms should be highly suspected. Even if the first CTA/DSA study is negative, a follow-up examination should be performed days or weeks later to diagnose and treat the aneurysm as early as possible. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment may help reduce complications and improve outcomes.
