Class A1 scavenger receptor antibody improves murine colitis by influencing macrophage and gut microbiota
Authors: Jingling Su, Lupeng Liu, Yandan Ren, Yutong Gan, Yumei Lin, Chenxi Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate whether class A1 scavenger receptor (SR-A1) regulated macrophage polarization and gut microbial alteration during intestinal inflammation of colitis. A murine colitis model was established by feeding with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), and treatment groups were injected intravenously with SR-A1 antibody. Results showed a preventive effect on colitis symptoms and fewer inflammatory cell infiltrates in treatment groups. Down-regulation of inflammatory cytokines and up-regulation of anti-inflammatory cytokine related to macrophages were seen in murine PBMC and LPMC after injected with SR-A1 antibody. The percentage of M2 macrophages was also elevated in treatment groups. In addition, SR-A1 antibody treatment resulted in the decreased apoptosis and increased proliferation of colonic epithelial cells. Other findings indicated that SR-A1 antibody injection could mediate its anti-inflammatory effect via inhibiting TLR4-MyD88-NF-kB signaling pathway and alterating the gut microbiota composition. Our research identified SR-A1 as a potential therapeutic target in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).Keywords: DSS-induced colitis, Class A1 scavenger receptor, Macrophage polarization, Gut microbial alterationSubject terms: Cell biology, Immunology, Molecular biology, Gastroenterology

Introduction

As a first-line housekeeper of complex innate immune system, monocyte-derived mononuclear phagocyte, particularly macrophage, plays a variety of roles to the maintenance of gut homeostasis in the steady state1,2. On one hand, macrophages rapidly eliminate apoptotic cells and life-threatening pathogens by direct phagocytosis and production of inflammatory cytokines and soluble factors. On the other, they also recognize food-derived antigens or commensal microbiota in a tolerating manner in the absence of co-stimulatory signals3,4. Intestinal microenvironmental alterations determine the polarization of macrophages into different phenotypes, including classically activated (M1) macrophages and alternatively activated (M2) macrophages, and simultaneously act as pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory function. Failure to achieve this polarized balance between tolerance and responsiveness in intestinal lamina propria can lead to development of chronic immune disorder such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)5. Therefore, targeting of macrophage-related regulatory switches may offer a new approach to the immune treatment of IBD.

Like most tissue macrophages, intestinal macrophages exert their removal and cytokine secretion effects by stimulation of pattern recognition receptors (PRR) activation. Class A1 scavenger receptor (SR-A1), also known as SCARA1, CD204 or macrophage scavenger receptor 1, is a prototypic member of PRRs family primarily expressed in macrophages6. SR-A1 was initially identified by its ability to mediate the formation of foam cells, functioning as regulating the process of atherosclerosis in cardiovascular diseases7. Subsequent lines of evidence reveal that SR-A1 is implicated in other disease pathogenesis by modulating macrophage activity and polarization. For example, in a murine orthotopic glioma model, SR-A1 deletion promoted M2-like tumor-associated macrophage polarization by activating STAT3 and STAT68. SR-A1 activation induces the expressions of TLR-3-mediated IFN-β and TLR-9-mediated TNF-α in human cytomegalovirus-exposed monocytes9. Recent studies have shown that malondialdehyde-acetaldehyde (MAA)-induced lung inflammation is attenuated in the absence of SR-A110.

Our previous study showed that pre-treatment with SR-A1 antibody could inhibit the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and the expression of M1 phenotype in mouse macrophage RAW264.7, which attenuates the pro-apoptotic effect on colonic epithelial cells induced by macrophages and protects the integrity of intestinal barrier11. In the present study, a DSS-induced colitis model was established in mouse and the therapeutic effects of SR-A1 antibody on the intestinal inflammation were further evaluated, in order to assess the influence of SR-A1 on immune response and cell polarization of macrophages. In addition, we also investigated whether SR-A1 was involved in the maintaining of gut microbial homeostasis in the pathogenesis of IBD.

Results

SR-A1 antibody treatment attenuated DSS-induced colitis

The therapeutic efficacy of SR-A1 antibody was assessed by establishing a 3% DSS-induced colitis model in mice. Compared with the control group, the body weight of mice in experimental group continued to decrease following ingestion of DSS after Day 2. Administration of SR-A1 antibody in treatment group significantly reduced body weight loss (Fig. 1A), accompanied by dramatic symptomatic improvement, including ruffled hair, loss of appetite, and bloody diarrhea. Colon length, which indicates the degree of colon damage, is an indirect marker of intestinal inflammation. As expected, DSS-induced colon shortening was improved by SR-A1 treatment (Fig. 1B). Consistent with the clinical parameters mentioned above, histopathological analyses of colons revealed DSS-induced histological damage, including inflammatory infiltration and crypt structure injury, was significantly attenuated in mice receiving SR-A1 antibody compared with model group (Fig. 1C,D).

Data were representatives from at least three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± SEM. (A) Loss of body weight in each group, *P < 0.05 vs DSS group. (B) The colon length in each group, *P < 0.05. (C) Histology images of colon in each group, H&E staining, 100 × . (D) The comparison of histological score in each group, based on the extent of edema, ulceration, crypt loss and inflammatory cells infiltration. *P < 0.05.

SR-A1 antibody treatment affected the expression of macrophage-related cytokines

SR-A1 has been defined by its ability to mediate the accumulation of immune response in macrophages, as manifested by cytokines secretion12,13. Therefore, we detected the expression of several macrophage-related inflammatory or anti-inflammatory mediators in PBMC and LPMC by RT-PCR. Compared with control group, the mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-1β and iNOS was increased significantly, but that of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was decreased markedly in both LPMC and PBMC after DSS ingestion. Treatment with SR-A1 antibody dramatically up-regulated IL-10 expression in both PBMC and LPMC, whereas down-regulated the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β and iNOS (Fig. 2).

Macrophage-related cytokines expression of LPMC (A) and PBMC (B) in DSS-induced colitis mice. The expression of several macrophage-related inflammatory or anti-inflammatory mediators in PBMC and LPMC was detected by qPCR. Results were obtained from three independent experiments. Data were presented as mean ± SEM, **P < 0.05 vs control, ## P < 0.05 vs DSS.

Anti-inflammatory effect of SR-A1 antibody during DSS colitis was dependent of the induction of M2 macrophage polarization

Following the M1–M2 paradigm, macrophages are classified as pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages14. To investigate whether SR-A1 antibody contribute to the polarization of M1–M2 paradigm in DSS-induced colitis model, flow cytometry was done to evaluate the phenotype of macrophages in LPMC. The results indicated that the percentage of F4/80+CD206+ macrophages decreased significantly during DSS colitis, whereas this percentage increased rapidly after SR-A1 antibody treatment. Interestingly, we found that SR-A1 antibody treatment had no influence on the elevated percentage of F4/80+CD11c+ macrophages induced by DSS colitis, which suggested that SR-A1 antibody exerted its anti-inflammatory effect via induction of M2 macrophage polarization rather than inhibition of M1 polarization (Fig. 3).

The change of macrophage phenotype in LPMC before and after the treatment of SR-A1 antibody in DSS-induced colitis mice. Flow cytometry was done to evaluate the macrophages phenotype in LPMC. The F4/80+CD11c+macrophage represents M1 phenotype (A), and the F4/80+CD206+macrophage represents M2 phenotype (B). Data were presented as mean ± SEM, *P < 0.05.

Effect of SR-A1 antibody inhibition on TLR4-NF-kB pathway of colonic mucosa

As a subclass of the membrane-bound PRRs, SR-A1 was shown to collaborate synergistically with other PRRs, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which mediate signaling through their cytoplasmic TIR domains linked to adaptor proteins, such as MyD88, finally inducing the activation of inflammatory transcription factor NF-kB. Evidence was provided that SR-A1 might be a physiological regulator of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated immune consequences15,16. Accordingly, the interaction between SR-A1 and TLR-4 signaling was detected by western blotting. As Fig. 4 shown, the protein expressions of TLR4, MyD88 and NF-kB p65 were substantially increased during DSS colitis, and significantly decreased after SR-A1 antibody treatment. These findings suggested that SR-A1 antibody exerted its anti-inflammatory effect via modulation of TLR4-MyD88-NF-kB signaling pathway.

The expression of TLR4-MyD88-NF-kB signaling pathway protein in colonic mucosa before and after the treatment of SR-A1 antibody. The expression of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-kB was detected by western blotting. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. # P < 0.05 vs control, *P < 0.05 vs DSS. (The blot images were cropped and the original blots are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1).

SR-A1 antibody treatment resulted in the decreased apoptosis and increased proliferation of colonic epithelial cells

TUNEL staining was performed to examine the apoptotic cells in colonic epithelium to identify the role of SR-A1 antibody in the treatment of DSS-induced colitis. As shown in Fig. 5, we observed a significantly elevated level of cell apoptosis in colonic epithelium as compared with control group, which could be reversed by injection of SR-A1 antibody. Additionally, the proliferation of colonic epithelial cells was assessed through determining the expressions of Ki67 using immunohistochemical staining. Results indicated that the expressions of Ki67 in colonic epithelium was decreased in model group when an intestinal inflammation induction. Administration of SR-A1 antibody, associated with reduced inflammatory response in colonic mucosa, markedly elevated the expression of Ki67-positive cells in treatment group. These finding suggested that SR-A1 antibody treatment could result in the decreased apoptosis and increased proliferation of colonic epithelial cells in DSS-induced colitis model.

Enhanced alteration of gut microbiota by SR-A1 antibody treatment

There is a growing evidence that dysbiosis is associated with several human diseases such as IBD, and some of macrophage-related mediators may be implicated in this process17. Figure 4 indicated that SR-A1 might participated in TLR4-MyD88-NF-kB inflammatory signaling pathway. Considering TLR4 as a recognition receptor of lipopolysaccharide in Gram-negative bacteria, we investigated whether SR-A1 was involved in the process of dysbiosis during DSS colitis via determining alteration of gut microbiota by 16S-rDNA sequencing. We observed that DSS treatment caused a disturbance in gut microbiota, as manifested by the decreased diversity of microbial population compared with control group. Surprisingly, SR-A1 antibody injection resulted in further lower diversity of microbial population, discording with the degree of mucosal inflammatory remission (Fig. 6A). Further investigation was performed to evaluate the specific composition of intestinal microbiota from each group. The data revealed that the intestinal predominant strain in control group was dominated by un_f_Muribaculaceae, accompanied by a variety of associated microbiota such as lachnospiraceae and lactobacillus. The relative abundance of un_f_Muribaculaceae was significantly lower and the relative abundance of acinetobacter was much higher in DSS-treated mice. Intriguingly, significantly higher proportions of a novel probiotics, Akkermansia, was observed in SR-A1 antibody treated group (Fig. 6B). These findings suggested that mucosal healing after SR-A1 antibody treatment might be accompanied with alteration of the novel probiotics in gut lumen.

The alteration of gut microbiota after SR-A1 antibody treatment in DSS-induced colitis mice. 16S-rDNA sequencing and data analysis were used to measure the alteration of gut microbiota. (A) Effect of SR-A1 antibody treatment on α- and β-diversity of fecal microbiota. (B) Effect of SR-A1 antibody treatment on the specific composition of gut microbiota.

Discussion

SR-A1, the first scavenger receptor to be identified, is a type II membrane glycoprotein that forms homotrimers. Multiple observational studies support the potential role of SR-A1 in macrophage-mediated immune response18,19. The most extensively studied area is atherosclerosis pathogenesis, in which phagocytic receptor SR-A1 is demonstrated to be involved in uptake of oxidized low-density lipoproteins in macrophage20. SR-A1 also emerges as a recognition receptor of macrophage via discriminating and binding different immune ligands. Additionally, SR-A1 helps to guide cell apoptosis and proliferation by initiating multiple cellular signaling pathways21,22. Under the influence of local microenvironment, SR-A1 can induce macrophages polarization into different subtypes23.

As a pivotal member of innate immune system, macrophages discriminate between innocuous antigens and potential pathogens to maintain intestinal homeostasis. They control pathogen invasion by activating an appropriate immune response24,25. Besides traditional macrophages, other macrophages, such as scavenger receptor positive macrophages, also are enriched in peripheral blood as well as in colonic mucosa of IBD patients. CD163 is one of scavenger receptors, and Franzè et al.26 reported that the fractions of CD163-positive monocytes were increased in IBD patients as compared to controls, and cross-linking of CD163 could enhance tumor necrosis factor-α synthesis. In our study, the result showed that scavenger receptor SR-A1 antibody treatment significantly improved DSS-induced colitis symptom, in addition to its established efficacy in reducing mucosal inflammatory infiltration and crypt structure injury. The mRNA expression of macrophage-related cytokines was detected by qPCR, and results revealed that pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β and iNOS, was decreased but anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was increased markedly in both PBMC and LPMC after SR-A1 antibody treatment. Further analysis of flow cytometry showed that SR-A1 antibody treatment had no influence on elevated percentage of F4/80+CD11c+ macrophages induced by DSS colitis, but could increase percentage of F4/80+CD206+ macrophages. These findings suggest that SR-A1 antibody treatment reduces circular and mucosal immune response mainly via inducing anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage activity rather than suppressing pro-inflammatory M1-related immunity, finally avoiding amplification of inflammatory cascade and exerting an anti-inflammatory effect.

In the process of macrophage activity induced by SR-A1 receptor, other PRRs, such as Toll-like receptors, can act as a coactivator, bind to intracellular molecules and activate specific transcription factors. Upon HCMV exposure to THP-1 monocytes, the disruption of SR-A1 signal transduction not only blocks the activation of downstream TLR-9 pathway but also alters the downstream TLR-3 pathway, and upregulation of non-canonical TLR-3 induces the expression of NF-κB-dependent p35 subunit of IL-12 gene transcription9. Other study demonstrated that SR-A1 has a synergistic coordination with TLR4-mediated immune consequences, further inducing the activation of transcription factor NF-κB27. Consistent with other previous studies, we also observed that blockage of SR-A1 with its antibody could inhibit the expression of TLR4, MyD88 and NF-kB p65 in intestinal mucosa during DSS colitis. Therefore, we hypothesize that SR-A1 may promote the activation of inflammatory macrophages via TLR4-MyD88-NF-kB signaling pathway.

Several studies indicated that the presence of macrophages is essential to intestinal epithelial cell regeneration. Depending on the repair microenvironment, they contribute to wound repair and tissue remodeling by clearing apoptotic neutrophils and helping to reduce autoimmune and chronic inflammatory responses5,28. Tissue-resident macrophages represent a heterogeneous population of non-migratory cells that respond to injury or infection by sensing related molecule. In a punch-biopsy-induced colonic injury model, the infiltration of TREM2+ macrophages was required for epithelial cell proliferation and colonic mucosa healing29. Another investigation suggested that regenerative response to intestinal epithelium injury could not be observed in the absence of monocyte recruitment in Csf1op/op mice30. Our results revealed that treatment of SR-A1 antibody could significantly drop the level of cell apoptosis and elevate the level of cell proliferation in colonic epithelium while converting macrophages to M2 subtype, which indicated SR-A1 antibody treatment is beneficial to intestinal epithelial cell regeneration. However, whether specific macrophage type exerts its function in this process remains to be further studied.

A large number of studies have linked gut dysbiosis to IBD pathogenesis. Accompanied by immune cells activation and gut microbiota, including its derived metabolites, can exert anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory effects31,32. For example, adherent-invasive E. coli (AIEC) has the ability to invade intestinal epithelial cells in IBD pathogenesis, which can be swallowed by macrophages and replicates inside them due to the defect in autophagy33. Ren et al.34 reported that acacetin alleviated clinical symptoms of DSS-induced colitis by inhibiting the macrophage inflammatory response in vitro, while improving the imbalance of gut microbiota. We demonstrated that SR-A1 could modulate TLR4 -related inflammatory signaling pathway, and TLR4 was recognized as a receptor of lipopolysaccharide in Gram-negative bacteria. According to the research mentioned above, SR-A1 may participate in the dysbiosis in the process of intestinal inflammation. So we detected the composition of gut microbiota before and after the treatment of SR-A1 antibody. It was observed the blockage of SR-A1 could further reduce the diversity of microbial population although mucosal inflammatory degree had been improved to some extent. However, the proportions of a novel probiotics, Akkermansia, significantly increased after the treatment of SR-A1 antibody. There has been growing evidence in the association of Akkermansia with health and disease in humans35,36. Akkermansia has the capacity to stimulate mucin synthesis and immune molecules production. Mice fed a high-fat diet with daily gavage of Akkermansia, showed enhanced production of the antimicrobial peptide Reg3γ in the colon37. Akkermansia is significantly reduced in patients with IBD and mice with colitis. Hence, in our study, it is tempting to speculate that anti-inflammatory effect of SR-A1 antibody on DSS-induced colitis partially accounts for altering the specific strain rather than the overall diversity of gut microbiota.

There were also some deficiencies in our study. We just discussed the mechanism of SR-A1 antibody treatment by regulating macrophage polarization, and did not investigate whether SR-A1 antibody could play its therapeutic role through the response of other immune cells. In addition, the phenomenon of gut microbiota alteration after SR-A1 antibody treatment was observed in our study, but the interaction mechanism between immune cells and gut microbiota still need to be further considered.

Materials and methods

Experimental animal

42 male C57BL/6 mice (aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 18 ± 2 g) were purchased from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Research Center (Shanghai, China). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (20–25 °C), humidity (40 ~ 70%) and 12–12 h light–dark cycle and given ad libitum access to food and water.

Establishment and treatment of the mouse model of colitis

After adaptive feeding for 1 week, these experimental mice were divided into control group, model group and treatment group using random number table method. Control group (n = 12) drank water alone, and model group (n = 12) drank water containing 3% DSS (MW: 36–50 kDa; MP Biochemicals, Shanghai, China) for 7 days. Besides drinking DSS solution, mice in treatment group (n = 12) injected with SR-A1 antibody (50 ~ 100ug/kg, once/day, R&D Systems, cat. no. 351620) through tail vein on days 1, 3, 5 and 7. Mice in each group were sacrificed on Day 8. The peripheral blood and colon tissues were harvested for further detection.

Disease activity evaluation

The alteration of hair, mental status, and activity in these mice were observed daily. The weight loss was recorded to determine disease activity. Colon tissues specimens from each mouse were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for three days, and sliced into 5-μm sections for histological analysis. After hematoxylin–eosin staining, lesion score was judged by the histological criterion based on extent of edema, ulceration, crypt loss and inflammatory cells infiltration as previously described38.

Real-time PCR

Mouse LP mononuclear cells (LPMC) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from each group were isolated as previously described39. Total RNA was extracted from LPMC and PBMC using the RNeasy Kit, and complementary DNA was synthesized with SYBR PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-PCR was performed with the CFX Connect Real-Time System sequence detector (BIO-RAD, California, USA). PCR was performed in triplicate wells using following conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 42 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. β-actin was used as the endogenous reference gene. The primer sequences for PCR are described follow: β-actin: forward 5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′, reverse 5′-CCAAGAAGGAAGGCTGGAAA-3′.TNF: forward 5′-GACGTGGAACTGGCAGAAGAG-3′, reverse 5′-TTGGTGGTTTGTGAGTGTGAG-3′; IL-1: forward 5′-GCAACTGTTCCTGAACTCAACT-3′, reverse 5′-ATCTTTTGGGGTCCGTCAACT-3′; IL-10: forward 5′-GCTCTTACTGACTGGCATGAG-3′, reverse 5′-CGCAGCTCTAGGAGCATGTG-3′; iNOS: forward 5′-GTTCTCAGCCCAACAATACAAGA-3′, reverse 5′-GTGGACGGGTCGATGTCAC-3; COX-2: forward 5′-TTCAACACACTCTATCACTGGC-3′, reverse 5′-AGAAGCGTTTGCGGTACTCAT-3.

Flow cytometry

Isolated LPMC were washed with cold PBS and resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and stained with PE-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 (eBiosciences, CA, USA), APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c (eBiosciences, CA, USA), and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD206 (eBiosciences, CA, USA) antibodies at 2 µg/mL for 30 min at 4 °C. Then cells were fixed in 0.5 mL of 3% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to flow cytometry on FACS Calibur instrument (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). Data were analyzed with CellQuest software.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from colonic mucosa of each group using RIPA buffer with a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After quantification of protein concentration using BCA assay by microplate spectrophotometry (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA), proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). Membranes were treated with primary antibodies: anti-TLR4 (abcam, SF, USA), NF-kB p65 (abcam, SF, USA), MyD88 (abcam, SF, USA) and GAPDH (Bioworld, MN, USA) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with secondary antibody, immunoblot detection was performed by Odyssey Imaging System (Li-COR Biosciences, Nebraska, USA).

Histological examination

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using a rabbit monoclonal antibody against mouse Ki67 (1:100, Abcam, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C followed by biotin-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) at 1:400 for 30 min with 3,3′diaminobenzidine (DAB) as a chromogen. Sections were treated with isotype-matched mouse IgG1 or PBS as negative controls. 5–10 high-power fields of intestinal mucosa were randomly selected, and Ki67-expressing and total stromal cells were subsequently counted for obtaining positive cells percentage.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cell death in colonic mucosa was quantified with the use of TUNEL kit from ROCHE Corporation. After conventional deparaffinization, sections were permeabilized with proteinase K for 20 min at room temperature, then terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) in reaction buffer were applied to serial sections for 1 h at 37 °C before the slides were placed in wash buffer for 10 min. 50 μl converter-POD was applied for 30 min following washes. Visualization of apoptotic cells was done with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine followed by a haematoxylin counterstain.

DNA extraction and 16S-rDNA sequencing and data analysis

Total DNA was extracted from fecal content as described previously40. The variable regions V3–V4 of 16S rDNA gene were amplified and sequenced. The PCRs were performed in triplicate in a total volume of 20 µL containing 4 µL of 5xFastPfu Buffer, 2.5 mM dNTPs, 5 mM forward primer, 5 mM reverse primer, 0.4 µL of FastPfu polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ultra-pure water. The reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and finally 72 °C for 10 min. Amplicons produced form different fecal samples were sent to a commercial company (Treatgut Bio-technology Co.LTD, Xiamen, China) for sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw sequences were deposited in NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession number PRJNA296796. Microbial diversity was analyzed on the basis of sequencing results.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for all statistical tests. Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Differences between means were determined using student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance for comparison. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
