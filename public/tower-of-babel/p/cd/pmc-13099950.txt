Motor imagery perspective shapes corticospinal excitability with effector-specific effects
Authors: Gleb Perevoznyuk, Artyom Batov, Aleksandra Pleskovskaya, Anthony Mensah, Daria Ponomareva, Oleg Shevtsov, Aynur Ragimova, Victoria Moiseeva, Matteo Feurra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotor imagery (MI) allows individuals to mentally simulate movements without execution, engaging neural pathways that overlap with those used during real actions. However, how imagery perspective influences corticospinal excitability across different effectors remains unclear. Using neuronavigated transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), we compared kinesthetic (KMI), first-person visual (VMI-1PP), and third-person visual (VMI-3PP) imagery of elbow flexion-extension in nineteen healthy adults. Single-pulse TMS was synchronized with a metronome marking the imagined peak contraction, and motor-evoked potentials were recorded from upper-arm and intrinsic hand muscles. All imagery types enhanced corticospinal excitability relative to a pacing-only baseline. For upper-arm muscles, VMI-3PP and KMI produced stronger facilitation than VMI-1PP, with no difference between KMI and VMI-3PP. In contrast, intrinsic hand muscles showed similar facilitation across all imagery types. These findings reveal that imagery perspective and effector interact to shape corticospinal output: proximal muscles are more sensitive to perspective, whereas distal muscles respond uniformly. The observation that third-person visual imagery can match kinesthetic imagery in enhancing corticospinal excitability highlights its potential as a practical and effective strategy for training and neurorehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42691-w.Keywords: Motor imagery, Perspective, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Kinesthetic imagery, Visual imagery, Primary motor cortexSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Motor imagery (MI) is the mental simulation of action without overt movement, during which individuals rehearse an action by either feeling it internally or seeing it in the mind’s eye1–3. Beyond elite sport and clinical rehabilitation, MI is woven into everyday life. People routinely preview how they will lift a weight at the gym, visualize how to pass through a narrow doorway with a box, rehearse posture before a squat, or plan how to reach and rotate the arm while placing an object on a high shelf. The broad utility of MI explains its central role across performance science, neurorehabilitation, and brain-computer interfaces4–9 Neuroimaging and neurophysiology converge on a distributed action network recruited during MI those overlaps, but is not identical, with action execution and action observation, including premotor, parietal, and primary sensorimotor regions10–12.

A key distinction is between kinesthetic MI (KMI) and visual MI (VMI). KMI prioritizes internal sensations such as muscle tension, joint position, and skin stretch. VMI prioritizes how the action looks and can be performed from a first-person perspective (VMI-1PP) or a third-person perspective (VMI-3PP), where one sees oneself from an external viewpoint13,14. These variants are not interchangeable. Targeted studies and meta-analytic work indicate partly distinct neural mechanisms and context-dependent outcomes for KMI versus VMI and for VMI-1PP versus VMI-3PP perspectives10–12. Despite this, most transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) studies have emphasized KMI and VMI-1PP. Direct within-subject comparisons that also include VMI-3PP remain uncommon, especially in designs that standardize timing and deliver pulses at behaviorally meaningful phases of the imagined action14–16.

Effector specificity is a second conceptual issue. Many MI studies target distal or intrinsic hand muscles because they yield robust motor-evoked potential (MEP) and have a well-defined cortical representation. Yet many practical goals concern proximal or upper-arm effectors that support lifting, carrying, and whole limb trajectory control. It is therefore important to test whether modality and perspective effects generalize from intrinsic hand muscles to upper-arm muscles or whether upper-arm effectors show a different sensitivity profile. Prior work shows reliable facilitation for KMI, with more variable effects for VMI that appear to depend on task constraints and viewpoint14,15. Initial perspective manipulations suggest that VMI-3PP can also facilitate corticospinal output, but systematic within-subject comparisons that span KMI, VMI-1PP, and VMI-3PP in a single experiment remain scarce14,15.

We asked two linked questions. We asked a primary question about the magnitude of corticospinal facilitation. How large is the corticospinal facilitation elicited by VMI-3PP, and can it rival KMI and VMI-1PP in modulating corticospinal excitability. Second, do imagery perspective effects depend on the target effector, with intrinsic hand muscles showing broadly similar facilitation across imagery types while upper-arm muscles show stronger dependence on perspective. These questions are theoretically motivated. Work on action observation shows clear viewpoint selectivity in mirror circuits: macaque ventral premotor neurons encode actions differently when viewed from egocentric versus allocentric angles17, and human premotor-parietal networks also vary with first versus third person views18,19. Because third-person observation typically highlights extrinsic limb configuration and trajectory, it can yield strong motor resonance, especially for multi-joint, proximal control. Within motor imagery, TMS studies further demonstrate that third-person variants can modulate corticospinal excitability14. On this basis, testing VMI-3PP alongside KMI and VMI-1PP provides a principled way to ask when perspective matters for proximal versus distal effectors. By testing VMI-3PP alongside the canonical KMI and VMI-1PP strategies under controlled timing and explicit instruction, this study clarifies when and where visual perspective matters for corticospinal output and expands the repertoire of imagery strategies available for daily function, performance, and rehabilitation.

Therefore, the present study responds to these gaps with a randomized within-subject design that directly compares KMI, VMI-1PP, and VMI-3PP while participants mentally simulate upper-arm flexion-extension at rest. We implement explicit, perspective specific instructions that align with current reporting guidance, and we use metronome pacing to standardize timing and align pulses to the imagined peak contraction. To test generalization and provide a stringent control, we record MEPs not only from upper-arm muscles, our primary targets, but also from intrinsic hand muscles that play a stabilizing role in the practiced movement. In addition to classical resting baselines, we include a baseline with metronome condition that reproduces the auditory pacing without any imagery requirement, which isolates any influence of pacing per se on corticospinal excitability.

Beyond transient changes in corticospinal excitability, motor imagery contributes to motor (re)learning and use-dependent plasticity, and emerging work suggests that task instructions and imagery modality can shape these adaptations. Situating our comparison of KMI, VMI-1PP, and VMI-3PP within this framework helps link perspective-specific facilitation to potential downstream learning effects20,21.

In studies comparing motor imagery modalities and perspectives, methodological clarity is essential for cumulative progress. When instructions are vague, participants can drift between modalities or perspectives within a block16, which blurs both behavioral outcomes and MEP. Recent reporting guidance for action simulation studies recommends that authors explicitly state MI modality and perspective, document procedures that promote compliance, and report timing and control conditions so that results are interpretable and reproducible22. In parallel, precise timing matters. Individuals vary in the spontaneous tempo of imagined movements, which can misalign pulses with the intended imagery phase and inflate variability. Metronome pacing provides a simple standardization tool. By coupling the imagined flexion-extension cycle to an auditory rhythm and placing the pulse at the imagined peak contraction, one can deliver TMS at reproducible time points across participants and conditions15,23. By explicitly identifying when visual perspective matters-and when it does not-this study refines theoretical models of motor imagery and expands the practical repertoire of imagery strategies for training and rehabilitation.

Materials and methods

G power analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.724 for a repeated-measures ANOVA design with within-subject factors (5 muscles × 4 conditions). Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and desired power of 0.80, the required sample size was estimated at N = 10 participants. This value was selected as a conservative estimate based on prior MI-TMS studies that have reported medium-to-large effects on corticospinal excitability and have used assumed effect sizes of f ~ 0.4 in GPower analyses25. Accordingly, we recruited 20 participants to ensure adequate power. However, one participant was excluded from the final analysis because a large proportion of their condition-level averaged MEP amplitudes (21 of 30 muscle-condition combinations) fell outside the group mean ± 2 SD range, indicating excessive deviation from the group distribution and insufficient data reliability. The final sample therefore comprised N = 19 participants.

To verify the adequacy of this sample size, we additionally performed post hoc power analyses based on the observed effect sizes (partial η²). These analyses confirmed that the final sample size (N = 19) still provided sufficient power (1 - β > 0.80) to detect effects of interest. Notably, the number of participants in our study is comparable to that of previous MI TMS investigations26,27, further supporting the robustness of our design.

Participants

We recruited 20 healthy, right-handed volunteers, aged 18–35 (M = 24.15, SD = 3.73) that have no previous experience of MI-tasks (Supplementary Table 1). Absence of prior experience with motor imagery tasks was assessed by self-report during recruitment and confirmed verbally before the experimental session; participants indicated that they had not previously taken part in studies involving explicit motor imagery instructions or training. Of those, 9 were male and 11 were female. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders (including epilepsy). Normal or corrected-to-normal vision was assessed by self-report during recruitment, with participants indicating the absence of uncorrected visual impairments; this information was reconfirmed prior to testing. To ensure consistency of physiological conditions, participants abstained from alcohol for 24 h prior to the experiment, did not consume coffee for 4 h, and did not smoke for 1 h before testing. Additionally, all participants reported having slept for at least 7 h the night before the experiment. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the National Research University Higher School of Economics, Moscow. Prior to testing, each participant provided written informed consent and received financial compensation for participation. Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, which confirmed right-hand preference in all participants (mean = 72.71, SD = 18.85, minimum = 42.86, maximum = 100) (Supplementary Table 2).

Questionnaires

Participants completed three standardized motor imagery questionnaires (MIQ-328,29, KVIQ30, and MIQ-RS31) prior to the experiment, which assess vividness and ease of generating kinesthetic and visual imagery. Questionnaire scores were used to evaluate participants’ ability to perform the required tasks and were further analyzed in relation to neurophysiological measures.

Multiple motor imagery questionnaires were administered to provide a comprehensive characterization of participants’ imagery abilities. The MIQ-3, KVIQ, and MIQ-RS assess partially overlapping but complementary aspects of motor imagery, including visual and kinesthetic modalities and different perspective-related components. Using multiple well-established instruments allowed us to verify that participants were capable of engaging in the required imagery tasks and to situate the sample relative to prior motor imagery literature employing different assessment tools. Questionnaire scores were used for descriptive and screening purposes.

Descriptive results from the MIQ-3 indicated that the final study sample could readily generate different imagery modalities, with mean scores of 5.89 ± 1.23 for internal visual, 5.97 ± 1.29 for external visual, and 5.78 ± 1.14 for kinesthetic imagery28. On the KVIQ, mean scores were 44.68 ± 8.36 for visual imagery and 44 ± 7.46 for kinesthetic imagery. On the MIQ-RS, participants reported mean vividness scores of 6.29 ± 0.99 for visual imagery and 5.70 ± 1.10 for kinesthetic imagery (Supplementary Tables 3, 4, 5).

To contextualize these scores, we compared them with published values. In large validation samples, MIQ-3 subscale means in healthy adults are around 4.8-5.0 on the 1–7 scale, with a global mean of approximately 4.932, whereas scores in the present sample ranged from 5.78 to 5.97, For the KVIQ, typical healthy adult means are approximately 37/50 for both visual and kinesthetic subscales33, while our sample’s means (approximately 45/50). Similarly, MIQ-RS vividness ratings in healthy and athletic samples usually fall around 5–6 per item31, which is consistent with the high visual (6.29) and kinesthetic (5.70) imagery vividness observed here. Collectively, these comparisons indicate that participants possessed average to high motor imagery ability, supporting the adequacy of imagery performance for the present experimental protocol.

sEMG and TMS

Surface electromyography (sEMG) data were collected from five right-arm muscles: Biceps brachii (BB), Triceps brachii (TB), First dorsal interosseous (FDI), Abductor pollicis brevis (APB), and Abductor digiti minimi (ADM) using sEMG clips with Skintact RT-34 electrodes connected to a BrainVision ExG AUX system. Before electrode placement, the skin was prepped with an electrically conductive scrub and cleansed with an alcohol wipe to reduce impedance (Fig. 1).

Surface EMG electrode montage. Schematic illustration of the electrode placement used for recording muscle activity from the right upper limb. Surface EMG electrodes were positioned over five target muscles: biceps brachii (BB), triceps brachii (TB), first dorsal interosseous (FDI), abductor pollicis brevis (APB), and abductor digiti minimi (ADM). Reference electrodes (BB_R, TB_R, FDI_R, APB_R, ADM_R) were placed over electrically neutral areas adjacent to each muscle, and a common ground electrode was positioned near the wrist. The figure depicts approximate anatomical locations used for consistent signal acquisition across participants.

Muscle inactivity during imagery and baseline conditions was monitored continuously using online sEMG visualization to prevent overt contractions or co-contraction. Participants were instructed to remain fully relaxed throughout all imagery blocks, and EMG signals were visually inspected by the experimenter in real time. Trials were accepted only when background EMG showed no visible muscle activation and remained below approximately 20 µV peak-to-peak. To objectively rule out subtle residual muscle activity or micro-movements, all trials were additionally screened offline. Epochs were rejected if pre-stimulus EMG activity exceeded 100 µV peak-to-peak within a 50 ms window prior to the TMS pulse, ensuring that only trials recorded under electromyographically silent conditions were included in the analysis.

Neuronavigated TMS was administered over the left primary motor cortex (M1) using a MagPro X100 stimulator (MagVenture, Farum, Denmark) with a C-B60 butterfly induction coil. A frameless neuronavigation system (Localite TMS Navigator, Localite GmbH, Sankt Augustin, Germany) facilitated MRI-guided navigation based on individual T1 weighted MR scans, ensuring accurate coil positioning. After manually locating the hotspot with the C-B60 coil, the device was switched to an analogous coil with the same magnetic field characteristics at the same hotspot, a Cool-B65 CO butterfly coil on an Axilum TMS Cobot system (Brainbox Ltd, United Kingdom). This coil has equivalent magnetic field properties and was positioned at the same hotspot location. During the sessions, biphasic single-pulse TMS (spTMS) was applied over the left M1 using a figure-of-eight coil. The coil was positioned tangentially to the scalp, with the handle oriented 45° backward and laterally from the midline, approximately perpendicular to the central sulcus, to optimize the posterior-anterior current flow in the brain34,35. This orientation was chosen based on previous findings demonstrating that the lowest motor threshold is achieved when the induced electric current flows perpendicular to the central sulcus, thereby facilitating transsynaptic activation of corticospinal neurons36.

Hotspot localization was initially performed using a C-B60 butterfly coil. The stimulation coil was then switched to a Cool-B65 CO butterfly coil for all subsequent procedures, including resting motor threshold determination and the main experimental recordings. The C-B60 and Cool-B65 CO coils share the same butterfly geometry and manufacturer-specified magnetic field characteristics (e.g., penetration depth and field gradient), with the Cool-B65 CO incorporating active cooling to support prolonged stimulation protocols. After switching coils, the hotspot location was re-confirmed under neuronavigation to ensure consistency of coil positioning. Importantly, all stimulation intensities used in the experiment were defined and applied exclusively with the Cool-B65 CO coil, ensuring that any potential differences between coils did not affect the experimental measurements.

Coil positioning was controlled using a robotic neuronavigation system with continuous real-time monitoring of coil position and orientation relative to the target. Prior to each experimental session, the robotic system and coil were calibrated, and individual head registration based on individual structural scan was performed for each participant. Coil-to-target deviations were monitored online throughout the experiment, and stimulation was permitted only when predefined spatial and angular tolerance thresholds were met. Specifically, movement control thresholds were set to 1 mm for translational displacement and 1° for angular deviation (green zone), with warning thresholds at 3 mm and 3° (yellow zone). For robotic position control, allowable deviations were defined separately for vertical and lateral displacement (≤ 4 mm and ≤ 2.5 mm, respectively) and for coil orientation (≤ 1.5°), with larger deviations (≥ 8 mm vertical, ≥ 5 mm lateral, ≥ 3° angular) triggering error states. Head movements were also continuously monitored, with maximum tolerated head displacement set to 30 mm and angular deviation to 15°. Because coil position and orientation were continuously tracked and corrected online by the robotic system, no discrete manual drift-correction steps were required during the session.

In addition, a force sensor integrated into the robotic coil holder was used to monitor coil-scalp contact. Force sensor sensitivity was adjusted individually for each participant (typically within a range of 2–3 units) based on head and body morphology and participant comfort, with the aim of maintaining stable contact without excessive pressure. This adjustment served to ensure consistent coil positioning and participant comfort and did not affect stimulation parameters.

All TMS procedures were conducted in accordance with established international safety guidelines for transcranial magnetic stimulation37. Participants were screened for contraindications prior to participation and were monitored throughout the experiment. After stimulation blocks, participants completed a standardized safety questionnaire assessing stimulation related sensations38. No adverse events were reported.

The hotspot was defined as the scalp position that consistently produced the largest MEPs in the BB muscle, as a primary target, at the lowest stimulator intensity. Resting motor threshold (RMT) for BB was determined as the minimal intensity that elicited an MEPs exceeding 50 µV peak-to-peak amplitude in 5 of 10 consecutive trials39,40. Stimulator intensity was then increased in 10% increments relative to RMT, until BB responses exceeded 300 µV in at least 7 of 10 trials and TB, FDI, APB, and ADM responses exceeded 50 µV.

Experimental task

Experimental sessions were scheduled primarily in the late afternoon or early evening (typically after 17:00), reflecting participant availability due to academic and work commitments. Although time of day was not strictly controlled or counterbalanced across participants, this scheduling approach limited large circadian variability by clustering sessions within a relatively narrow time window. We therefore consider it unlikely that circadian fluctuations substantially confounded the observed effects.

All execution-based training phases in the experiment, including the initial general motor training and the brief refresher training preceding each motor imagery block, were designed as short familiarization procedures rather than extended physical practice. Their purpose was to ensure that participants understood the target movement, the imagery instructions, and the temporal alignment with the metronome, not to induce motor learning or physiological adaptation.

At the beginning of each session, participants completed an initial baseline recording (B1) consisting of 15 single-pulse TMS stimulations delivered at rest with eyes closed (Fig. 2).

Experimental procedure. Overview of the experimental timeline and task structure. Each session began with an initial resting baseline (B1) followed by a general motor training phase in which participants performed paced elbow flexion-extension movements synchronized to metronome beeps. The main experiment comprised four randomized conditions: kinesthetic motor imagery (KMI), visual motor imagery from the first-person perspective (VMI-1PP), visual motor imagery from the third-person perspective (VMI-3PP), and a baseline with metronome only (BM). In the imagery conditions, single-pulse TMS was automatically triggered in synchrony with the metronome beep marking the imagined peak contraction. After completion of all blocks, a final resting baseline (B2) was recorded. Self-ratings of imagery efficiency and vividness were obtained after each imagery block.

Following B1, participants underwent a general motor task training phase to ensure that they understood the target movement and its timing. They repeatedly performed elbow flexion-extension of the upper-arm muscles while holding a 1.5 kg dumbbell, synchronizing the peak contraction with the metronome beep (1000 Hz, 100 ms duration, one beep every 7 s). The number of repetitions was not fixed; training continued until participants reliably aligned the contraction peak with the metronome beat. This training was performed with eyes open. The executed flexion-extension movements were used exclusively for familiarization and timing calibration; no EMG recordings or TMS pulses were acquired during movement execution.

During motor imagery blocks, TMS pulses were delivered at a predefined time point corresponding to the imagined peak of the flexion-extension cycle, that is, the moment when participants imagined the arm to be maximally flexed. Importantly, stimulation was applied during imagery performed at rest, without any overt movement or joint kinematics. Thus, pulses were not delivered during actual upward (concentric) or downward (eccentric) movement phases, but were temporally aligned to a specific imagined movement phase used as a reference across conditions.

Following the general training, participants proceeded to the main experimental task blocks, which were presented in randomized order using PsychoPy (version 2024.1.5). Each block consisted of 30 single-pulse TMS stimulations automatically triggered in synchrony with the metronome beep, ensuring precise temporal alignment between stimulation and the imagined peak contraction. The task set included three types of MI-kinesthetic imagery (KMI), visual imagery from the first-person perspective (VMI-1PP), visual imagery from the third-person perspective (VMI-3PP), and a baseline with metronome (BM) condition.

Before the start of each imagery block, participants underwent a brief, condition-specific refresher training. This involved a short open-eyes rehearsal of the five elbow flexion-extension movements with the dumbbell synchronized to the metronome beeps (one cycle every 7 s; total duration ≈ 35 s), accompanied by tailored instructions that directed their attention to the relevant imagery modality: for example, focusing on the visual appearance of the arm and the biceps during the movement for the visual conditions, or on the bodily sensations of contraction, joint movement, and skin tension for the kinesthetic condition. The exact wording and structure of these condition-specific instructions are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary tasks instructions).

For the VMI-3PP condition, additional individualized training was provided to ensure that participants clearly understood the external perspective as self-related. Each participant was filmed while performing five repetitions of the flexion-extension movement with the dumbbell, synchronized to the metronome beeps (one cycle every 7 s; total duration ≈ 35 s). The video was recorded from a distance of approximately one meter, corresponding to a natural third-person viewpoint, and included the participant’s own auditory pacing signals. During subsequent training, participants were shown this recording and explicitly instructed to recognize the depicted arm as their own and to imagine the action as if they were watching themselves from that same reference point.

The brief video shown in the VMI-3PP condition was intended solely as a reference to establish the required third-person perspective and was not used as repeated training or practice. The video was presented only one time during the training (approximately 35 s) and depicted the participant’s own arm performing the movement at the target tempo, after which imagery was performed with eyes closed. This reference was included because third-person imagery requires explicit calibration of an external viewpoint to the self, whereas kinesthetic imagery and first-person visual imagery rely on internally available somatosensory or egocentric visual representations. Across all conditions, instructional depth and engagement were matched through modality-specific instructions and brief physical rehearsal to ensure clear understanding of the imagery task, while avoiding additional sensory stimulation during imagery execution.

Once the rehearsal was complete, participants were asked to close their eyes and continue with the corresponding imagery task during the block itself. In all imagery conditions, participants remained at rest with eyes closed and mentally simulated the movement in synchrony with the metronome, aligning the imagined peak contraction with the auditory cue. In the BM condition, the metronome pacing was presented in the same manner, but participants were instructed to remain fully relaxed without engaging in any form of imagery, providing a pacing-matched control.

Across all training phases, participants performed a standardized minimum of five flexion-extension repetitions, with additional repetitions provided only when necessary to achieve reliable synchronization with the metronome or to clarify imagery instructions. Across participants, the number of repetitions rarely exceeded a few additional cycles beyond the minimum of five. The task involved a light load and long inter-beat intervals, and participants were encouraged to rest as needed. Participants were verbally queried about discomfort or muscle fatigue during training, and no participant reported fatigue. Given the brief duration, low physical demands, and rest opportunities, fatigue was considered unlikely to influence corticospinal excitability or subsequent imagery performance.

At the end of the experimental session, a final baseline (B2) was collected under the same conditions as B1, consisting of 15 single-pulse TMS stimulations at rest with eyes closed. Importantly, self-ratings were obtained only after the MI conditions. Following each imagery block, participants completed three seven-point Likert scales assessing the proportion of trials they were able to imagine (imagery efficiency (1 = none, 7 = all trials)), the vividness of the entire imagery sequence (ranging from no image or sensation to as vivid as actual vision or execution, 1 = no image for VMI / no sensation for KMI, 7 = as vivid as actual vision for VMI / as intense as real execution for KMI), and the vividness of the imagined peak contraction aligned with the metronome beep (using the same anchors as the overall vividness scale). These ratings allowed us to monitor participants’ engagement with MI conditions.

Mean self-ratings indicated that participants were highly engaged in the imagery tasks across all conditions. For the visual first-person condition (VMI-1PP), imagery efficiency averaged 4.89 ± 1.24, vividness of the entire imagery sequence 5.05 ± 1.35, and vividness of the imagined peak contraction 5.05 ± 1.39. In the visual third-person condition (VMI-3PP), the corresponding values were 4.84 ± 1.30, 5.00 ± 1.29, and 4.95 ± 1.17, respectively. For the kinesthetic condition (KMI), participants reported efficiency 4.89 ± 1.37, full-sequence vividness 4.84 ± 1.57, and peak vividness 5.00 ± 1.79.

These results confirm that participants maintained high compliance and vivid engagement across all motor imagery conditions.

Data preprocessing

We preprocessed the sEMG data using Python with the MNE41, NumPy42 and SciPy43 libraries. A second-order Butterworth high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 15 Hz was applied to the continuous raw signal to attenuate low-frequency noise44. As with all other filtering steps, the filter was implemented in zero-phase mode using filtfilt45 to avoid phase distortion. To remain as close to the raw signal as possible, no notch filter was applied46.

Following filtering, MEPs were evaluated for quality using a semi-automatic procedure supported by experimenter oversight. For inspection, each participant’s dataset was epoched from − 1.5 to 1 s relative to the TMS trigger, allowing the operator to visualize the entire signal before and after stimulation. Epochs were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: peak-to-peak amplitude below 50 µV, pre-stimulus peak-to-peak activity above 100 µV within a 50 ms window prior to the TMS pulse47. In total, approximately 5.7% of all recorded epochs across muscles were excluded from analysis (Supplementary Table 6).

For valid epochs, MEP amplitudes were extracted within a 10–60 ms post-stimulus window48. The peak-to-peak amplitude was computed as the difference between the maximum and minimum values of the sEMG signal identified within this window using peak detection algorithms.

All preprocessing, rejection, and MEPs quantification were performed using a custom-written Python script, which allowed both automated extraction of multiple MEPs features and manual verification of trial quality49.

MEPs were first organized into participant-level tables for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table 7). Within each muscle and condition, outliers were identified using a threshold of ± 2 standard deviations from the condition mean. Trials exceeding this criterion were replaced with the corresponding condition mean, after which a new mean was recalculated (Supplementary Table 8). This procedure reduced the influence of extreme values while preserving equal trial counts across conditions. On average, 0.89 trials per participant were replaced.

To account for inter-individual variability, MEP amplitudes were normalized to baseline recordings (Supplementary Table 9). Baseline MEPs collected at the beginning (B1) and end (B2) of the experiment were first compared separately for intrinsic hand muscles and upper-arm muscles (biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB), treated as an antagonistic pair). Repeated-measures ANOVAs indicated no significant differences between B1 and B2 for either muscle group (intrinsic hand muscles (ADM, APB and FDI): F(1,18) = 3.89, p = .064, ηp² = 0.178; upper-arm muscles (BB, TB): F(1,18) = 3.00, p = .100, ηp² = 0.143). On this basis, B1 and B2 values were averaged to yield a single collapsed baseline for each muscle.

Condition-specific MEP amplitudes were then divided by the corresponding collapsed baseline value, resulting in normalized scores. Finally, mean normalized amplitudes were calculated for each muscle and condition, and these values were entered into subsequent statistical analyses. Of note, the same overall pattern of results was reproduced when normalization was performed using B1 alone or B2 alone, confirming that the choice of baseline did not affect the main conclusions. The corresponding analyses are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary analysis b1 and b2 separate normalization).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM SPSS Software, n.d., version 27.0.1.0)[50]. The dependent variable was the mean normalized MEP amplitude. A repeated-measures ANOVA (Type III sums of squares) was conducted with two within-subject factors: Condition (baseline with metronome (BM), kinesthetic imagery (KMI), visual imagery first-person perspective (VMI-1PP), and visual imagery third-person perspective (VMI-3PP), and Muscle (five levels: biceps brachii (BB), triceps brachii (TB), first dorsal interosseous (FDI), abductor pollicis brevis (APB), and abductor digiti minimi (ADM)), as well as their interaction. Post hoc comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction.

In addition to this full-model analysis, we also examined upper-arm muscles and intrinsic hand muscles separately. This distinction reflects the different task demands placed on the two groups: the intrinsic hand muscles (FDI, APB, ADM) were primarily engaged in static stabilization of the dumbbell, whereas the upper-arm muscles (BB and TB) were directly involved in the dynamic flexion-extension movement. Separate repeated-measures ANOVAs were therefore performed for upper-arm and intrinsic hand muscles groups, in parallel with the combined analysis including all muscles. This approach allowed us to assess condition-specific effects both across the full muscle set and within functionally distinct subgroups.

Results

Upper-arm muscles (BB, TB)

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on collapsed baselines-normalized MEP amplitudes recorded from the antagonistic pair of upper-arm muscles (BB, TB), with Condition (BM, KMI, VMI-1PP, VMI-3PP) and Muscle as within-subjects factors.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Condition [F(3, 54) = 14.23, p < .001, partial η² = 0.44], indicating reliable modulation of corticospinal excitability across imagery conditions. The main effect of Muscle [F(1,18) = 0.83, p = .38, partial η² = 0.04] and the Condition × Muscle interaction [F(3,54) = 0.35, p = .79, partial η² = 0.02] were not significant.

Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons showed that corticospinal excitability was significantly higher during KMI (p = .003), VMI-1PP (p = .015), and VMI-3PP (p < .001) compared with the BM condition. In addition, VMI-3PP elicited greater responses than VMI-1PP (p = .021), and KMI responses were also greater than VMI-1PP (p = .013). No significant difference was found between KMI and VMI-3PP (p > .05).

Together, these findings demonstrate that motor imagery of upper-arm movements enhances corticospinal excitability relative to a non-imagery baseline. Importantly, third-person visual imagery (VMI-3PP) produced significantly stronger responses than first-person visual imagery (VMI-1PP), reaching a level comparable to kinesthetic imagery (KMI) (Fig. 3).

Intrinsic hand muscles (ADM, APB, FDI)

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on collapsed baseline-normalized MEP amplitudes recorded from the intrinsic hand muscles (ADM, APB, FDI), with Condition (BM, KMI, VMI-1PP, VMI-3PP), and Muscle as within-subjects factors.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Condition [F(3,54) = 12.906, p < .001, partial η² = 0.418], indicating that corticospinal excitability differed across imagery conditions. In contrast, the main effect of Muscle [F(2,36) = 0.875, p = .426, partial η² = 0.046] and the Condition × Muscle interaction [F(6,108) = 0.595, p = .734, partial η² = 0.032] were not significant.

Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons showed that corticospinal excitability was significantly higher during KMI (p < .001), VMI-1PP (p = .006), and VMI-3PP (p < .001) compared with the BM condition. No reliable differences were found among the three imagery conditions themselves (all p > .05), including between KMI and VMI-3PP (Fig. 4).

Corticospinal excitability during motor imagery in intrinsic hand muscles. Mean baseline-normalized MEP amplitudes recorded from the abductor digiti minimi (ADM), abductor pollicis brevis (APB), and first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscles across four experimental conditions: baseline with metronome (BM), kinesthetic imagery (KMI), visual imagery from the first-person perspective (VMI-1PP), and visual imagery from the third-person perspective (VMI-3PP). Error bars represent the standard error of the mean (SEM). All motor imagery conditions (KMI, VMI-1PP, and VMI-3PP) produced significantly greater corticospinal excitability compared with the BM condition. No significant differences were observed among the three imagery types, indicating comparable facilitation across kinesthetic and visual perspectives in intrinsic hand muscles.

In intrinsic hand muscles, all imagery conditions increased excitability over baseline and did not differ from one another. By contrast, in upper-arm muscles VMI-3PP and KMI exceeded VMI-1PP, with no difference between VMI-3PP and KMI. Thus, hand muscles showed uniform facilitation, whereas upper-arm muscles showed perspective-dependent modulation.

Discussion

The present study investigated how motor imagery modality and perspective modulate corticospinal excitability across upper-arm muscles and intrinsic hand muscles. Using a neuronavigated TMS design with controlled timing and explicit instructions, we compared kinesthetic (KMI), first-person visual (VMI-1PP), and third-person visual (VMI-3PP) imagery against a pacing-matched baseline. Across both upper-arm muscles and intrinsic hand muscles, all forms of imagery significantly increased MEP amplitudes relative to baseline with metronome (BM), reinforcing prior evidence that MI reliably facilitates corticospinal output14,15. Crucially, however, the pattern of facilitation diverged between upper-arm muscles and intrinsic hand muscles.

Our primary target was the antagonistic pair of upper-arm muscles (BB, TB). Here, all three imagery conditions significantly increased corticospinal excitability compared with baseline, including VMI-1PP, which despite being weaker than KMI and VMI-3PP, still exceeded BM. Importantly, both KMI and VMI-3PP produced stronger facilitation than VMI-1PP, while no difference emerged between KMI and VMI-3PP. This result is notable because kinesthetic imagery has traditionally been regarded as the most effective strategy for eliciting corticospinal facilitation15, whereas external visual imagery has often been neglected or considered less powerful. Our findings challenge this view, showing that third-person imagery can rival kinesthetic imagery in upper-arm muscles. The effect may be reinforced by the specific instructions emphasizing visualization of the biceps, which could have enhanced imagery precision, and by the dynamic nature of upper-arm muscles whose length changes during flexion-extension may be more salient when imagined from an external perspective.

In contrast, hand muscles (FDI, APB, ADM) excitability increased uniformly across all imagery types, with no reliable differences between kinesthetic (KMI) and visual strategies (VMI-1PP, VMI-3PP). This uniform effect suggests that, at least in the context of our protocol, visual imagery, even from a first-person perspective, can be as effective as kinesthetic imagery for intrinsic hand muscles. One explanation is task-related: participants were explicitly instructed to focus their imagery on the upper-arm effectors, so intrinsic hand muscles - although active in stabilizing the dumbbell, were not the primary imagery target. Another possibility is the strong cortical representation of hand muscles, which supports a wide range of imagery perspectives and may render them less sensitive to the modality-specific distinctions observed elsewhere.

With the intrinsic hand muscles pattern clarified, we now focus again on the primary result in upper-arm muscles. We show that VMI-3PP can be as effective as KMI in upper-arm muscles, while VMI-1PP, although weaker-still produces significant facilitation relative to BM. This effector-specific dissociation refines current understanding of MI perspective effects and highlights that visual strategies, when precisely instructed, can match kinesthetic ones. From an applied standpoint, the finding that VMI-3PP robustly facilitates corticospinal excitability in upper-arm muscles has direct implications for training and rehabilitation. External perspectives are already natural in many learning contexts, athletes observe themselves in mirrors and patients watch their movements in therapy and our results suggest that such perspectives are not only intuitive but also neurophysiologically powerful18,51–54.

Moreover, we observed that for the antagonistic pair of upper-arm muscles: biceps brachii (BB) and triceps brachii (TB), corticospinal excitability was significantly higher during third-person visual motor imagery (VMI-3PP) than during first-person imagery (VMI-1PP). One plausible explanation involves stronger engagement of the mirror neuron system (MNS) during VMI-3PP. Most action-observation paradigms present movements from an external viewpoint, typically depicting another person’s limb rather than the participant’s own17,55–59 In our paradigm, participants used a video recording of their own arm as a reference during the VMI-3PP condition. This self-referential visual cue could plausibly contribute to stronger sensorimotor engagement under third-person imagery, although this mechanism was not directly assessed here. These Importantly, these interpretations are offered as plausible mechanisms rather than definitive explanations, as the present study did not include direct measures of mirror neuron activity, embodiment, or self-referential processing.

Perspective itself appears to modulate this activity, based on prior neurophysiological work: recordings in both human and non-human primates have shown that MNS respond differentially depending on whether the action is seen from an egocentric or allocentric perspective60. Furthermore, self-referential and embodiment-related processes may contribute to the observed facilitation. A sense of agency or self-consciousness toward the imagined movement can strengthen sensorimotor coupling19, and several studies have demonstrated that observing or imagining one’s own hand, or even inducing a feeling of ownership over the viewed effector, enhances neural engagement compared with others61,62. In our experiment, participants were explicitly instructed to imagine themselves performing the movement from the same external perspective as shown in their reference video. This explicit alignment between visual reference and self-agency may have amplified embodiment-related processes, thereby increasing corticospinal excitability in the VMI-3PP condition relative to VMI-1PP.

Beyond the role of perspective and embodiment, the differential pattern of facilitation between upper-arm muscles and intrinsic hand muscles in our study also warrants consideration. While both muscle groups showed significant increases in corticospinal excitability during motor imagery, the pattern of modulation differed: in the upper-arm antagonistic muscles pair (BB and TB), VMI-3PP produced facilitation comparable to kinesthetic imagery (KMI) and both of them are greater than first-person visual imagery (VMI-1PP), whereas in the intrinsic hand muscles (FDI, APB, ADM) all imagery conditions yielded similar enhancement. Several factors may account for this dissociation.

First, participants were explicitly instructed to focus their imagery on the biceps brachii, which served as the primary target of the imagined movement. Such directed attentional focus may have preferentially engaged corticospinal representations of the upper arm, amplifying imagery-related facilitation in upper-arm muscles while reducing condition-specific differentiation in intrinsic hand muscles14,15,63.

Second, task biomechanics likely differ between upper-arm and intrinsic hand muscles. The imagined elbow flexion-extension maps onto isotonic (and partly eccentric) contractions for BB/TB, whereas intrinsic hand muscles chiefly stabilize the grip isometrically. Isotonic contractions are known to elicit larger muscle-length changes, richer proprioceptive/afferent input, and higher perceived effort than isometric tasks, which could promote stronger sensorimotor simulation, particularly under visually enriched perspectives such as VMI-3PP64; these factors could enhance sensorimotor simulation and thereby corticospinal modulation, potentially more so when specified by an external (third-person) visual perspective. In addition, modest agonist-antagonist co-activation around the imagined peak flexion may reduce BB-TB differences65. We present these as candidate mechanisms; we did not record EMG during executed movement to test them directly, and future work combining imagery with execution-phase EMG would be needed to adjudicate among these accounts. Contraction- and phase-dependent patterns during executed vertical arm movements have been reported elsewhere66. Testing this account would require a design that acquires EMG execution alongside imagery.

Third, anatomical and representational factors may also contribute to this pattern. Upper-arm muscles although physically larger, exhibit distinct cortical organization and excitability profiles compared with the highly differentiated and finely tuned representations of intrinsic hand muscles67,68. Consequently, imagery-related modulation may be more sensitive to perspective and attentional strategies in upper-arm muscles, whereas intrinsic hand muscles representations-optimized for precise motor control-may respond more uniformly across imagery types.

In summary, the present dissociation is most consistent with a primary contribution of task set and attentional focus on proximal effectors, together with viewpoint-dependent processing that is especially relevant for multi-joint control. Biomechanical factors and differences in cortical representation likely modulate this pattern but remain secondary until tested directly. Experiments that independently vary attentional focus, joint dynamics, and perspective will be needed to determine their relative weight.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, all recorded muscles were functionally involved in the task, which may have introduced partial overlap in activation patterns and limited the specificity of effector comparisons. Accordingly, future studies could include control muscles unrelated to the imagined movement to better isolate effector-specific facilitation. Second, the load of the dumbbell was not normalized to each participant’s maximal voluntary contraction, and variations in perceived effort may have modulated kinesthetic imagery vividness and corticospinal output. Third, despite explicit training and perspective-specific instructions, the vividness and accuracy of imagery were assessed only via self-report. Relatedly, only the third-person imagery condition included a brief self-video used to calibrate the external viewpoint. This additional reference could contribute to the stronger facilitation seen in VMI-3PP, suggesting that future work should equalize exposure and instructional materials across conditions. Compliance with the requested perspective was verified by self-report only, which limits certainty about trial-by-trial adherence to first- or third-person imagery; therefore, objective indices of perspective should be incorporated in future protocols. Beyond these methodological considerations, the sample comprised healthy young adults, limiting generalizability to clinical populations or older adults. Replication in patient groups will be essential to evaluate the translational value of these findings for rehabilitation protocols. Additionally, we did not acquire EMG during executed flexion-extension, so we cannot directly relate imagery-evoked modulation to execution-phase neuromuscular activity. Moreover, stimulation during imagery was restricted to a single predefined phase (imagined peak flexion, time-locked to the metronome cue), so we could not compare corticospinal excitability between distinct imagined movement phases (e.g., upward vs. downward imagery). Future studies could explicitly time-lock TMS to multiple imagined phases to test whether phase-specific modulation parallels the contraction-dependent patterns reported during executed movements. Finally, although experimental sessions were generally conducted within a consistent late-day time window, time-of-day effects were not formally controlled and cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies could therefore explicitly manipulate or counterbalance session timing to further assess circadian influences on motor imagery-related corticospinal excitability.

Our results identify conditions under which visual third-person imagery matches kinesthetic imagery in proximal muscles. Given reports that imagery can support practice-dependent plasticity and performance improvements, modality and perspective may not only modulate immediate corticospinal output but also influence longer-term learning trajectories. This provides a rationale for testing whether perspective-specific prescriptions (e.g., VMI-3PP for multi-joint upper-limb actions) optimize training dosage and retention in sport and rehabilitation20,21.

Finally, our results provide evidence that third person visual imagery can be as effective as kinesthetic imagery for proximal control, which has direct implications for neurorehabilitation and applied neuroscience. Third person imagery can be implemented with mirrors or brief self-videos, paced by a metronome to standardize timing, and combined with established methods such as action observation or mirror feedback to support shoulder and elbow function. Perspective specific prescriptions allow clinicians to tailor imagery to patient preference and stage of recovery, while objective indices can be used to track engagement. These features make third person imagery a scalable option for clinic and home programs and a promising component of personalized, mechanism informed rehabilitation protocols.
