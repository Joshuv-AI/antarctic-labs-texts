Reversible hydrogen atom exchange between phenoxyl radical and phenol at cobalt(ii): role of coordination-induced bond activation
Authors: Tuhin Sahana, Mohamed Ajeer Karayil, Ratnamay Kolay, Jeffery A. Bertke, Subrata Kundu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how transition metals influence net hydrogen-atom exchange is pivotal. This study reports isolation and comprehensive characterization of phenol complexes (N2SArOH)MIIBr2 (1-M; where, M = Co/Ni), supported by an unsymmetrical tripodal ligand N2SArOH. Application of Bordwell relationship affords the O–H bond dissociation free energy (BDFEOH) values as 71.25 for 1-Co and 80.57 kcal mol−1 for 1-Ni, thereby underscoring the critical role of the metal site in governing the thermochemistry of the coordinated phenol. H-atom exchange reaction between phenol in 1-Co and isolated 2,4,6-tri-tert-butylphenoxyl (tBu3ArO˙) results in a rare cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl radical complex 4-Co with S = 1 ground state. Reactivity of the phenoxyl functionality in 4-Co with H˙(/H++e−) donor reagents smoothly regenerates the phenol complex 1-Co, thereby illustrating reversible H-atom exchange. Detailed studies on these H-atom exchange reactions provide the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) and activation parameters (ΔH‡, ΔS‡, Ea), thereby enabling an experimental distinction between dichotomous H-atom transfer (HAT) and proton-coupled electron-transfer (PCET) pathways. Together, this complete set of isolated phenol, phenolate, and phenoxyl radical complexes delineates the relay of protons and H-atoms within the coordination sphere of the metal site, thereby showcasing the strong potential of the phenol-tethered coordination motifs in PCET/HAT based catalysis.

Introduction

Understanding the dichotomy between proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), as well as factors that govern such routes, is longstanding and fundamentally important.1–5 For instance, net hydrogen atom (H˙ <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="23.636364pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 23.636364 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.015909,-0.015909)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M80 600 l0 -40 600 0 600 0 0 40 0 40 -600 0 -600 0 0 -40z M80 440 l0 -40 600 0 600 0 0 40 0 40 -600 0 -600 0 0 -40z M80 280 l0 -40 600 0 600 0 0 40 0 40 -600 0 -600 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> H+/e−) transfers involving the O–H bond of tyrosine (Tyr) are essential for the functions of ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), photosystem II (PSII), cytochrome c oxidase (CcO), and galactose oxidase (GAO).6–9 The net H-atom transfer between the stacked tyrosine/tyrosyl (Tyr/Tyr˙) moieties in RNR has been proposed to follow HAT pathway,10 while the analogous process of transferring H+/e− from tyrosine has been proposed to follow PCET for PSII and CcO.11–16 The reactivity of the tyrosine residues within the coordination spheres of these metalloenzymes are dictated by their interaction with the metal site. For instance, in galactose oxidase (GAO), two tyrosine residues in the primary coordination sphere exhibits distinct structural and functional roles (Fig. 1).17,18 The relatively weakly coordinated Tyr495, positioned at ∼2.58 Å from the copper site, functions as a proton-responsive moiety, whereas the Tyr˙272 residue, at ∼1.91 Å, mediates H-atom abstraction from the α-C–H bond of the coordinated substrate.17,19 Interestingly, a recently characterized family of monooxygenases, namely lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs), features a T-shaped copper site typically accompanied by a tyrosine residue situated ∼2.7 Å from the copper centre.19–24 However, the precise role of the tyrosine residue in LPMO activity remains unclear. Computational studies propose that this proximal tyrosine residue participates in H-atom transfer based hole-hopping mechanism, and thus prevent oxidative damage to the active site during catalysis.22,23 Another recent study suggests that polyphenolic ligands (such as pyrogallol and catechol) can act as sustainable reductants for the copper(ii) active site of LPMO, where ligand coordination facilitates proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) by enhancing the acidity of the phenolic moiety.24 PCET from an iron(ii)–ethanol complex has also been proposed for the iron catalyzed reductive cross-coupling of alkenes.25 Hence, insights into the O–H bond formation and cleavage are not only crucial for understanding the functioning of diverse range of metalloenzyme active sites but also for the rational design of catalysts engineered to operate based on PCET/HAT pathways.

It is noteworthy that the PCET/HAT routes for the reactions of exogenous phenolic substrates with various oxidizing species such as metal–oxo,26–30 –peroxo,31,32 –superoxo,33–35 –hydroxo,36,37 –halide,38,39 and –nitrite40–44 complexes have been studied in details for gaining insights into the reactivity of these oxidizing species (Fig. 1). In contrast, understanding the reversible H-atom exchange between phenol and phenoxyl radical in the coordination sphere of a metal(II) site is distinctly important for developing strategies for phenol/phenoxyl radical mediated H-atom relay during catalytic cycles.45 Though prior computational studies outline that self H-atom exchange between phenol/phenoxyl follows PCET rather than a HAT pathway (Fig. 1),1 experimental illustration of the reversible net H-atom exchange between phenol and phenoxyl radical within the coordination sphere of a metal site is not known to the best of our knowledge. We hypothesize that a suitably designed phenol-appended metal(II) complex will promote subtle activation of the phenolic O–H bond, rendering it suitable for reversible H+ and H˙ relay.

Previous approaches in probing the coordination induced weakening of the O–H bonds in water have typically employed either early-to-mid transition metal sites (e.g. TiIII, MoI, MnII, FeII) or lanthanum(ii) sites (e.g. SmII).46–56 The Lewis acidity of these metal sites polarizes the O–H bond, while the electronic properties of these metal ions, being susceptible to one electron oxidation, facilitate the overall weakening of the O–H bond. Coordination of water at SmI2 leads to a dramatic decrease in the O–H bond dissociation free energy (BDFEOH) to ∼34 kcal mol−1,55 whereas the BDFEOH for the aqua ligand in [FeII(H2O)6]2+ is estimated to be 79.5 kcal mol−1 in comparison to that of free water 122.7 kcal mol−1.57 In contrast to the prevalence of coordination induced water activation, analogous phenolic O–H bond activation and underlying thermochemistry remain inadequately understood,58 due to the challenges associated with greater acidity, lower BDFEOH of phenol PhOH (pKa ∼10.0, BDFEOH 89.5 kcal mol−1 in aqueous medium),57 and weaker coordination ability in comparison to water. Despite these challenges, isolation of the metal complexes bearing an appended phenolic moiety in the coordination sphere is necessary for understanding the interconversion between phenol and phenoxyl radical for elucidating the role of the metal centre in the reversible H-atom exchange. Moreover, the appended phenolic moiety has been shown to offer preorganized interactions for turning on H-atom abstraction reactivity of copper-oxygen species.59

Utilizing a N-capped unsymmetrical tripodal ligand N2SArOH, we herein demonstrate isolation of cobalt(ii)–phenol, –phenolate, and –phenoxyl complexes (Fig. 2). The hemilabile phenolic O–H moiety tethered within the coordination spheres of cobalt/nickel(ii) centres offer subtle metal–phenol interaction, which has been quantified through experimental determination of pKa and bond dissociation free energy (BDFEOH) of the phenolic O–H bonds. These thermodynamic parameters enable strategic access to two distinct redox tautomers, namely [CoII](˙OAr) and [CoIII](OAr). Furthermore, detailed reactivity studies establish that the ArOH/ArO−/ArO˙ moieties at a transition metal site are capable of supporting proton as well as H-atom relay processes. Studies of analogous nickel(ii)–phenol and –phenolate complexes further highlight the role of metal site in phenolic O–H bond activation.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the ligand and complexes

The tetradentate ligand N2SArOH, with a 3°-amine-N, a pyridine-N, a thioether-S and a phenol(ate)-O donor atoms, was synthesized in 57% yield via a nucleophilic substitution reaction between 2-(bromomethyl)-4,6-di-tert-butylphenol and 2-(methylthio)-N-(pyridin-2-ylmethyl)aniline (Scheme S1, Fig. S1–S2). FTIR spectrum of the ligand exhibits ν(OH) at ∼3184 cm−1, which shifts to 2364 cm−1 for the mono-deuterated ligand N2SArOD (Fig. S3). X-ray crystal structure of the ligand shows H-bonding interaction between phenolic O–H and the apical 3°-amine (Fig. S4). Reactions of N2SArOH with CoBr2 or (dimethoxyethane)NiBr2 in acetonitrile provide the corresponding phenol-bound metal(II) complexes (N2SArOH)MIIBr2 (1-M, where, M = Co/Ni) in 85 and 88% yields, respectively (Fig. 2). FTIR spectra of these phenol complexes show distinct ν(OH) vibrational features at 3478 cm−1 (for 1-Co) and 3412 cm−1 (for 1-Ni). Dissolutions of complexes 1-M in ethanol-OD result in H/D-exchange, affording the corresponding OH-deuterated complexes 1D-Co/1D-Ni, which display downshifted ν(OD) vibrational bands to 2584 and 2547 cm−1, respectively (Fig. 3A and S5). The experimentally observed ν(OH)/ν(OD) ratios (1.344 and 1.340) are in close agreement with the calculated value of 1.374 predicted by Hooke's Law for O–H/D bonds. Thus, these FTIR analyses unambiguously suggest the presence of phenolic-OH in 1-M. X-ray crystal structures of 1-Co and 1-Ni reveal octahedral metal sites, each coordinated by an apical amine-N, a pyridine-N, a thioether-S, a phenolic-O, and two bromide ligands (Fig. 4A, S6, S8). The Co–O distance [2.434(2) Å] is longer compared to that of Ni–O [2.217(4) Å]. Notably, the higher ν(OH) vibrational frequency of (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) relative to 1-Ni is consistent with the longer M–phenol distance, suggesting that the metal–phenol interaction influences the property of phenolic-OH. Crystals of 1-M grown in the presence of trace amount of water provide minor crops of [(N2SArOH)MIIBr(H2O)]Br (1’-Co/1’-Ni). Interestingly, the molecular structures of the aqua-bound phenol complexes 1’-Co/1’-Ni show similar M–phenol distances 2.1315(10) and 2.1355(16) Å, respectively (Fig. S7, S9 and Tables S1–S2). Thus, these structural data illustrate that the phenolic arms in the [(N2SArOH)MII]2+ cores are hemilabile. Solution magnetic moment studies in CD3CN at room temperature suggest S = 3/2 (for 1-Co) and S = 1 (for 1-Ni) ground states. It is noteworthy that the phenol appended copper complexes are relatively common,60–62 as the preference for a lower coordination number renders the phenol moiety nearly non-interacting with the metal, while 1-M represent rare examples of octahedral phenol complexes of mid-to-late transition metals.58,63

Coordination of the ligand by the metal(II) bromide salts in the presence of triethylamine (Et3N) afford the corresponding phenolate complexes [(N2SArO)MIIBr complexes 2-M (M = Co/Ni)] in 85% and 68% yields, respectively. The crystal structure of 2-Co depicts a distorted trigonal bipyramidal cobalt(ii) site (τ5 = 0.66)64 with Co–N/S distances comparable to those in 1-Co (Fig. 4B, S12), thereby suggesting the same oxidation state of the metal site. The markedly shorter Co–O distance [1.8905(10) Å] in 2-Co reflects the coordination of an anionic phenol in 1-Co/1’-Co. The crystal structure of 2-Ni features two octahedral nickel sites bridged by two bromide ligands (Fig. S14). Similar to the cobalt(ii)–phenol(ate) complexes, the Ni–O distance 1.9984(14) Å in 2-Ni is shorter than that in the corresponding phenol complex. X-ray analyses on the crystals of 2-M, grown from wet acetonitrile, depict octahedral metal sites where the pyridine-N, thioether-S, phenolic-O, and an aqua ligand constitute the equatorial plane, whereas the axial sites are occupied by the amine-N and a bromide (Fig. S13, S15). Notably, the metal–N/S distances across all of these above-mentioned structures of 1-M/2-M are comparable, while the metal–phenol(ate) distance depends on the protonation state (Tables S1–S2). Comparisons of the UV-vis absorption spectra of these phenol(ate) complexes 1-M and 2-M in acetonitrile show the distinct presence of the phenolate-to-metal charge-transfer features λ/nm (ε M−1 cm−1) at 395 (1328) and 405 (644) for 2-Co and 2-Ni, respectively (Fig. 3B and S16–S18).

pKa and BDFEOH determination of the phenol complexes

Having established structural and spectroscopic features of the phenol(ate) complexes of cobalt(ii) and nickel(ii), we turned to determine the pKa of the phenolic-OH moiety in 1-M spectrophotometrically.65 UV-vis monitoring of the reactions of 1-M with a series of bases (pyrrole, aniline, pyridine, imidazole, morpholine and triethylamine) in acetonitrile allow to bracket the pKa ranges (Scheme S2, Table S3). Spectrophotometric titrations of 1-Co with the incremental concentrations of pyridine (pKa 12.5 in MeCN)66 reveal a gradual growth of the phenolate-to-cobalt(ii) absorption feature, enabling analysis of the phenol–phenolate equilibrium at cobalt(ii) and providing a pKa of 12.6 in acetonitrile (Fig. S20–S21). An analogous titration of 1-Ni with imidazole (pKa 15.1 in MeCN)66 affords the pKa 15.7 in acetonitrile (Fig. S22–S23). Notably, the nearly three-orders of magnitude difference in the pKa values of the phenolic-OH in 1-M underscores the relatively stronger Lewis-acidic interaction of phenol with cobalt(ii) compared to nickel(ii) in solution, though the metal–phenol bond distances in [(N2SArOH)MIIBr(H2O)]Br (1’-Co/1’-Ni) are comparable in solid state. Furthermore, the pKa value of 23.6 determined for the free ligand N2SArOH by 1H NMR spectroscopic titration using 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]undec-7-ene (DBU, pKa 24.3 in MeCN)66 demonstrates that coordination to the metal sites enhances the Brønsted acidity of phenolic-OH by 8–11 orders of magnitude, depending on the nature of metal-site (Fig. S24).67 Considering the thermodynamic scheme for the metal-coordinated phenol (Fig. 2), activation of the phenolic-OH bonds in 1-M can be evaluated from the O–H bond dissociation free energy (BDFEOH). The cyclic voltammograms (CV) of the isolated phenolate complexes 2-Co and 2-Ni in acetonitrile at room temperature display the quasi-reversible features at 0.06 V and 0.28 V (Fig. S25), respectively, corresponding to the first redox events of the respective phenolate complexes. Employing the Bordwell equation,57 which relates BDFEOH, pKa, and redox-potential (E1/2), the BDFEOH of the phenolic-OH in 1-Co and 1-Ni are estimated to be 71.25 and 80.57 kcal mol−1, respectively (Table 1). The estimated BDFEOH for 1-Co is comparable to that of the previously reported manganese(i)–phenol complex.58 Notably, the extent of coordination induced activation of phenols are significantly moderate in comparison to that of water activated by various manganese and iron sites.50–53

Reactions of phenol complexes with phenoxyl radical

A reaction of (N2SArOH)CoBr2 (1-Co) and 2,4,6-tri-tert-butylphenoxyl radical68tBu3ArO˙ (2.0 equiv) in acetonitrile over a longer reaction time (20 h) provides dark blue crystals of (N2S)CoBr2 (3-Co) in 36% isolated yield. Single crystal X-ray diffraction analysis on 3-Co reveals that the cobalt site is best described as a distorted trigonal bipyramidal geometry (τ5 = 0.72)64 supported by an oxidatively modified ligand N2S (Fig. 6 and S26). The phenolic arm of the initial ligand N2SArOH is covalently inserted onto the benzylic site (C8–O1 1.420(2) Å) linking the apical amine and the pyridine moieties. This oxidative modification of the ligand during the reaction of (N2SArOH)CoBr2 (1-Co) and tBu3ArO˙ is proposed to proceed through a phenolic H-atom transfer from 1-Co to tBu3ArO˙ and formation of a phenoxyl radical complex (N2SArO˙)CoBr2 (4-Co) (Scheme S3). Subsequently, in the presence of an additional equivalent of tBu3ArO˙, a second H-atom transfer from the benzylic C8 position followed by a radical recombination between the C8-centered and N2SArO˙ ligand-centered radicals, is proposed for resulting in the modified ligand N2S in 3-Co. Though the isolated yield of 3-Co is moderate, this proposed mechanism for the alkoxylation of the benzylic site of the ligand perhaps suggest the presence of localized radical character of the hemilabile phenoxyl radical ligand arm in the proposed complex (N2SArO˙)CoIIBr2 (4-Co), rather than its alternative redox formulation as the phenolate bound cobalt(iii) species (N2SArO)CoIIIBr2 (5-Co). Graciously, an equimolar reaction between isolated 2,4,6-tri-tert-butylphenoxyl radical (tBu3ArO˙) and (N2SArOH)CoBr2 (1-Co) in acetonitrile at room temperature smoothly affords blackish purple colored complex (N2SArO˙)CoIIBr2 (4-Co) in 82% isolated yield. The UV-vis spectrum of 4-Co in acetonitrile exhibits λ/nm (ε M−1 cm−1) at 380 (3360), 550 (1879) and 780 (1217) (Fig. S29). It is noteworthy that these intense low-energy absorption bands in the near-IR region of the spectrum are comparable to that of the recently reported phenoxyl radical complex [FeII](˙OPh), and also resembles to the previously reported cobalt(ii) bound radical ligands.69,70 UV-vis monitoring of the reactions of tBu3ArO˙ with (N2SArOH/D)CoBr21-Co/1D-Co at 30 °C shows that the growth of characteristic absorption band at 550 nm is slower for the reaction of 1D-Co (Fig. S31). The ratio of the initial rates of these reactions is estimated to be ∼3.4, thereby indicating direct involvement of the O–H/D bond of (N2SArOH/D)CoBr21-Co/1D-Co during its transformation to 4-Co. Comparison of the FTIR spectra of 1-Co and 4-Co also shows the absence of the characteristic ν(OH) feature for 4-Co (Fig. S32). Thus, these findings suggest that the reaction of phenoxyl radical tBu3ArO˙ with 1-Co proceeds via net H-atom transfer from the cobalt-bound phenol to the free phenoxyl radical (Fig. 5). In contrast, (N2SArOH)NiBr2 (1-Ni) does not react with tBu3ArO˙, consistent with the relatively higher BDFEOH of 1-Ni (80.57 kcal mol−1) than that of tBu3ArOH (77.1 kcal mol−1).57 Thus, these observations validate the estimated BDFEOH of 1-M. To gain insights into the electronic structure, we investigated the magnetic properties of the blackish purple species 4-Co. The variable temperature magnetic susceptibility studies on the powder samples of 1-Co and 4-Co provide the effective magnetic moments as 5.30 and 3.07 µB, respectively (Fig. 7 and S33).71 Furthermore, a solution magnetic moment of 2.57 µB for 4-Co (in acetonitrile) at room temperature is also consistent with solid state magnetic studies. Thus, these results suggests that 4-Co is paramagnetic with S = 1 spin state, originating from the antiferromagnetic coupling between [CoII] (S = 3/2) and N2SArO˙ (S = 1/2), thereby ruling out the alternative electronic formulation (N2SArO)CoIIIBr2, which would correspond to a low-spin d6, diamagnetic (S = 0) species. Notably, the transformation of cobalt(ii)–phenol to cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl radical is particularly intriguing, since the redox potential for ArO˙/ArOH typically exceeds that of the cobalt(III/II) couple.69 The handful number of prior examples of [CoII](˙OAr) species have been accessed chemically or electrochemically by narrowing the redox gap between ArO˙/ArOH and cobalt(III/II) couples, employing the phenolic ligands either conjugated with additional π-manifold or bearing multiple phenolic donors.72–75 The present study, in contrast, utilizes selective H-atom abstraction from the coordinated phenol at cobalt(ii) for accessing the cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl radical complex 4-Co.

UV-vis monitoring of the reaction between (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) and H2O2 in acetonitrile reveals the initial formation of intense absorption features at 550 and 780 nm, characteristic of complex 4-Co. These features subsequently decay to a brown species with relatively less intense and blue shifted bands centered around 500 and 700 nm (Fig. S34 and S35), which are comparable to that of the previously reported cobalt(iii)–phenolate complexes.76 Though attempts to crystallize this brown species generated from the H2O2 reaction was not successful, a reaction of (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) with NaNO2 in acetonitrile affords brown colored complex 5’-Co, which exhibits comparable absorption features λ/nm (ε M−1 cm−1) at 505 (1330) and 710 (573) (Fig. S36). These absorption features of 5’-Co are distinctly different in comparison to that of 4-Co (Fig. S37). Reaction of isolated 4-Co with NaNO2 also provides 5’-Co. X-ray crystal structure of 5’-Co portrays an octahedral cobalt(iii) site coordinated to the deprotonated phenolate ligand N2SArO, one bromide and a κ1-N-bound nitrite (Fig. 4C and S39). A comparison of the bond distances between the octahedral phenolate complexes (N2SArO)CoIIBr(H2O) (2’-Co) and (N2SArO)CoIIIBr(NO2) (5’-Co) shows that the metal–ligand distances in 5’-Co are shorter than the analogous ones in 2’-Co (Table S1), congruent with the higher oxidation state of cobalt in 5’-Co. 1H and 13C NMR studies on 5’-Co show sharp resonances (Fig. S40), thereby supporting diamagnetic (S = 0) nature of the cobalt site. These results suggest that the strategic H-atom abstraction-based oxidation of (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) provides the [CoII]–phenoxyl complex, while the oxidation reactions in the presence of reagents like H2O2 or NaNO2 afford the [CoIII]–phenolate complex. Comparison of the Raman spectra of 4-Co and 2-Co shows a set of new features at 1267, 1564, 1580, 1602, and 1626 cm−1 under 532 nm laser excitation, and at 1256, 1404, 1595 cm−1 under 638 nm laser excitation (Fig. S41). These vibrational bands are consistent with that of the previously reported phenoxyl species.77

Interestingly, the redox-tautomeric cores {(N2SArO˙)[CoII]}2+ (in 4-Co) and {(N2SArO)[CoIII]}2+ (in 5-Co) exhibits distinct reactivity profiles towards redox reactions. For instance, an equimolar reaction of 4-Co with ferrocene rapidly to provides the [CoII]–phenolate complex 2-Co and ferrocenium, as evaluated by UV-vis studies (Fig. S42). This finding also suggests that 4-Co is a one-electron oxidized form of the phenolate complex 2-Co. In contrast, UV-vis monitoring of the reaction of 5’-Co with ferrocene does not show any changes in the absorption features (Fig. S42).

H-atom exchange reactivity

We were curious to assess the H-atom abstraction ability of the isolated cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl complex 4-Co. UV-vis spectroscopic monitoring of the reaction of 4-Co with excess 4-methoxy-2,6-di-tert-butylphenol (4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH) in acetonitrile shows exponential decay profiles of the characteristic absorption bands and following an overall second-order rate law k2[4-Co][4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH] (Fig. 8 and S43). The rate of the reactions of 4-Co with 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH and mono-deuterated phenol 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OD at 30 °C provides a OH/OD-kinetic isotope effect (KIE) value 3.65, which is comparable to the value obtained for the transformations of 1-Co/1D-Co to 4-Co. Though the estimated BDFEOH of (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) is 71.3 kcal mol−1, to our surprise, the substituted phenols with relatively higher BDFEs, such as 4-methoxy-phenol (4-MeO-ArOH), 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol (2,4-DTBP), 4-methyl-phenol (4-Me-ArOH), 2,6-di-tert-butylphenol (2,6-DTBP), also react with (N2SArO˙)CoIIBr2 (4-Co) at room temperature to provide 1-Co albeit only in the presence of high concentration of the phenolic substrates (Fig. S44–S46). Interestingly, a relatively lower KIE value 1.93 is observed for the reaction of 4-Co with 4-MeO-ArOH and mono-deuterated phenol 4-MeO-ArOD at 30 °C (Fig. S45). A plot of (RT/F)ln(k2) versus the oxidation potential (E°ox) of the phenols exhibits a linear fit with a slope of −0.56 ± 0.07 for 4-MeO-ArOH, 2,4-DTBP, 4-Me-ArOH, and 2,6-DTBP, whereas 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH deviates from the linear trend (Fig. 8).31,32,78 The slope of the (RT/F)ln(k2) versus E°ox plot is expected to be −0.50, based on the Marcus theory of electron transfer for PCET reactions.79–82 Consistent with the previous reports,32,81,82 the relatively higher KIE value and deviation from the (RT/F)ln(k2) versus E°ox correlation suggest that the reaction of 4-Co and 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH follow hydrogen-atom-transfer (HAT) pathway. In contrast, the moderate KIE value along with the (RT/F)ln(k2) versus E°ox correlation slope around −0.50 for the reactions of 4-Co with relatively stronger O–H bond containing phenols rule out HAT and suggestive of PCET. However, a detailed analysis of the asynchronicity in the PCET process and the role of factors such as H-bonding interactions in the reactions of 4-Co with substituted phenols is beyond the scope of the present work.

Eyring and Arrhenius analyses for the HAT pathway with 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH over the temperature range of 20–60 °C reveal an activation barrier (Ea = +17.65 kcal mol−1) and activation parameters ΔH‡ = +17.03 kcal mol−1, ΔS‡ = −8.42 cal K−1 mol−1, and ΔG‡ (at 298.15 K) = +19.54 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 8, S47, Table S6). Notably, the Arrhenius analysis of the PCET pathway with 4-MeO-ArOH provide a relatively lower activation barrier (Ea = +9.39 kcal mol−1). Moreover, the activation parameters for the reaction of 4-MeO-ArOH is more entropically controlled and driven by a relatively more ordered transition state with ΔH‡ = +8.77 kcal mol−1, ΔS‡ = −36.32 cal K−1 mol−1, and ΔG‡ (at 298.15 K) = +19.60 kcal mol−1 (Fig. S47, Table 2). The more ordered transition state for the reaction of 4-MeO-ArOH (BDFEOH >78 kcal mol−1) with (N2SArO˙)CoIIBr2 (4-Co) is likely associated with the initial interaction of 4-MeO-ArOH at the cobalt site (Fig. 9), which is proposed to facilitate the corresponding O–H bond weakening. In contrast, the BDFEOH of the phenolic O–H bond in 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH (∼73.8 kcal mol−1) is comparable to that estimated for (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) (∼71.3 kcal mol−1). Consequently, the initial interaction of the exogenous phenolic substrate with the cobalt site is not required during the reaction of 4-Co and 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH, as reflected by the relatively lower negative value of ΔS‡. Furthermore, the proposed interaction of 4-MeO-ArOH with cobalt site during the course of the reaction enhances the acidity of the phenolic O–H, thereby favoring PCET route with a lower activation barrier. Thus, these results provide insights into the net H-atom change between phenol and phenoxyl radical within the coordination sphere of cobalt(ii). Notably, the pseudo-first order rate constants (kobs) for the reactions of 4-Co with excess equivalents of 4-R-2,6-DTBP-OH (where, R = Me/tBu) do not increase linearly with the increasing concentrations of the phenol (Fig. S48). This observation is attributed to an equilibrium between (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) and (N2SArO˙)CoIIBr2 (4-Co), facilitated by the presence of in situ generated relatively stable phenoxyl radicals 4-R-2,6-DTBP-O˙ (R = Me/tBu) (Scheme S4). In contrast, the phenoxyl radicals such as 4-H-2,6-DTBP-O˙, which are prone to radical dimerization to form 4,4′-bisphenol, do not favor the reverse reaction between 1-Co and 4-H-2,6-DTBP-O˙, thereby driving unidirectional transformation of 4-Co to 1-Co.

The phenoxyl radical complex 4-Co also reacts with H˙(/H++e−) donor reagents such as 4-tert-butylbenzylthiol (tBuBnSH) to yield the phenol complex 1-Co with a second order rate constant k2 = 1.58 M−1 cm−1 at 30 °C (Fig. 9 and S49–S50). These activation parameters are comparable to that of the PCET mechanism as observed for the reactions of 4-Co and substituted phenols. Moreover, N-benzyl-1,4-dihydronicotinamide (BNAH) reacts with 4-Co spontaneously (Fig. S52). Thus, these data illustrate net H-atom abstraction ability of the cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl species from various O/S/C–H bonds. In contrast, the cobalt(iii)–phenolate species (5’-Co) reacts with relatively weak bonds (such as 4-MeO-2,6-DTBP-OH) in the presence of significantly large excess of substrate (Fig. S53).

Conclusion

Taken together, this report demonstrates the isolation of octahedral phenol(ate) complexes of cobalt(ii) and nickel(ii) supported by an unsymmetrical tripodal ligand N2SArOH. In addition to the X-ray structural characterization of the phenol complexes (N2SArOH)MIIBr2 (1-M), FTIR spectroscopic analyses provide unambiguous evidence of the metal–phenol interactions in the solid state. UV-vis spectrophotometric titrations reveal that the metal–phenol interactions enhance the Brønsted acidity of the coordinated phenol by 8–11 orders of magnitude. Given the presence of isostructural coordination motif in 1-M, the variation of phenolic pKa further suggest that the cobalt site in [(N2SArOH)CoII]2+ exhibits more Lewis acidic interaction than its nickel(ii) counterpart. Additionally, the trend in the bond dissociation free energies for the phenolic O–H (BDFEOH) bonds in 1-M show that the cobalt(ii) site exerts greater influence than nickel(ii) in phenol activation.

Guided by the experimentally determined BDFEOH values, a strategic H-atom abstraction from (N2SArOH)CoIIBr2 (1-Co) using tBu3ArO˙ enabled the isolation of a rare cobalt(ii)–bound phenoxyl radical complex 4-Co. The UV-vis absorption features and spin state of the cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl radical core are distinct in comparison to the isoelectronic and redox-tautomeric cobalt(iii)–phenolate core in 5-Co/5’-Co. To the best of our knowledge, 1-Co, 2-Co, and 4-Co constitute the first complete set of phenol, phenolate, and phenoxyl radical complexes of cobalt(ii) supported by a common coordination motif. This unique series of complexes enabled detailed thermochemical analysis of the coordination-induced phenolic O–H bond activation towards homolysis and heterolysis. Comparison of the analogous nickel(ii)-phenol(ate) complexes highlights the critical role of the metal site in bond activation. These findings suggest that subtle interactions involving tyrosine residues within the active sites of various metalloenzymes, such as LPMO and GAO, fine-tune the pKa and BDFEO–H of the phenolic moiety. Such modulation is likely to play critical roles in governing proton- and H-atom-transfer.

Furthermore, H-atom exchange reactivity of the isolated cobalt(ii)–phenoxyl radical complex 4-Co with H˙(/H++e−) donor bonds, including O–H in substituted phenols, S–H in thiol, C–H in N-benzyl-1,4-dihydronicotinamide, outline a net H-atom transfer to yield the corresponding cobalt(ii)–phenol complex 1-Co. Utilization of a range of substituted phenols in the reactivity assay, kinetic isotope effect (KIE), Arrhenius, and Eyring analyses have been conducted for the direct comparison of the activation parameters for two closely related H-atom exchange routes, namely H-atom transfer (HAT) and proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) during the reactions of [CoII](˙OAr) core with phenolic O–H bonds of varied BDFEs. Overall, reversible proton–transfer equilibrium between phenol/phenolate in 1-Co/2-Co and the reversible net H-atom exchange between phenol/phenoxyl radical in 1-Co/4-Co illustrate that a phenol moiety (or a tyrosine residue in metalloenzymes) within the primary coordination sphere is well suited to function as both proton and H-atom relay. The reversibility between 1-Co and 4-Co is particularly intriguing, as it suggests that the metal-phenoxyl moieties are capable of relaying net H-atom between exogenous H-atom donor and the active site (Fig. 1C). Though the role of Tyr164 residue in LPMO activity has been discussed ambiguously in literature, the gained insights herein strongly suggest that Tyr164 residue potentially serve as proton- and/or H-atom-donor during key steps of the LPMO catalytic cycle. These findings also offer valuable guidance for the rational design of new catalytically active complexes with tethered phenol arm, tailored for small molecule activation as well as oxidative cleavage of strong bonds.
