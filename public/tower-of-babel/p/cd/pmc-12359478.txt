Parental knowledge, attitudes and practices on the use of antibiotics among their children in Jordan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdallah Y Naser, Hassan Al-Shehri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren are one of the most influential targeted age groups for diminishing the high and inappropriate use of antibiotics. Understanding how antibiotics are used in children requires apprehending the role of their parents in managing and using antibiotics for their children. This study aimed to investigate parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the use of antibiotics among their children in Jordan.MethodsThis is an online cross-sectional survey study that was conducted in Jordan between 20 April and 17 May 2025. Parents who had children between the ages of 1 and 12 years formed the study population.ResultsA total of 816 parents participated in this study. Participants aged 30–39 years (OR=1.58, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.47, p=0.045) and 40–49 years (OR=1.75, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.82, p=0.02) had significantly higher odds of better knowledge compared with those aged 20–29 years. Males showed lower odds of knowledge (OR=0.53, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.75, p<0.001) but higher odds of a positive attitude (OR=1.60, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.28, p=0.008) compared with females. Participants with postgraduate education had significantly better knowledge (OR=1.79, 95% CI 1.01 to 3.18, p=0.04) than those with high school education or less. Working in the medical field was strongly associated with higher knowledge (OR=2.45, 95% CI 1.49 to 4.03, p<0.001).ConclusionThis study highlighted a moderate level of knowledge, attitudes and practices among parents regarding the use of antibiotics for their children, with some variation. Increasing awareness about antibiotic indications and the importance of consulting a doctor and following their instructions is imperative. Older age, higher education level, female gender and medical fieldwork were associated with better antibiotic knowledge. These findings may aid in developing strategies, approaches and programmes to improve parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding antibiotics.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been recognised as one of the substantial global health threats,1 as it increases treatment difficulties and mortality rates and complicates the management of prevalent infectious diseases.2 Global estimates indicate that approximately 5 000 000 deaths occur annually due to AMR.3 Dissemination and development of AMR are primarily driven by improper use of antimicrobial agents, particularly antibiotics. These improper uses include poor treatment adherence, unprescribed antibiotics, misuse, overuse and self-medication.46

Indeed, infectious diseases of various causes are higher among children than among other age groups, leading to heightened use of antibiotics among them, consequently increasing the impact of AMR.7 This heightened use of antibiotics among children may also raise obesity rates and influence the healthy development of their gut microbiome.8 Therefore, children are one of the most influential targeted age groups for diminishing the high and inappropriate use of antibiotics.9 Accordingly, practical surveillance data for newborns and children are needed to provide strategies for developing international medicines to reduce the burden of AMR, especially in low- and middle-income countries.10 These data may, in turn, help ensure that these and other groups meet their needs. Research has indicated the crucial role of parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices in the use of antibiotics to treat their children. Ultimately, understanding how antibiotics are used in children requires apprehending the role that children’s primary decision-makers—their parents—play in managing and using antibiotics in their children.11

Multiple investigations have been performed in different countries worldwide, such as Jordan,12 Palestine,13 Iran,14 Saudi Arabia,15 the UAE16 and Australia,17 to assess parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding antibiotic use in their children. However, the findings of these studies have varied, with some indicating adequate knowledge, attitudes and practices, while others have demonstrated inadequate levels. Earlier studies in low- and middle-income countries like Jordan have revealed a lack of public awareness regarding antibiotic use.18 The level of awareness varies depending on the socioeconomic status of individuals.19 Despite this, there is a lack of up-to-date evidence on parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the use of antibiotics among their children in Jordan. Misuse of antibiotics is connected to self-medication practices, misconceptions and lack of awareness.20 The availability of up-to-date evidence would aid in developing targeted strategies, subsequently raise awareness and mitigate the economic and health burdens. Therefore, this study aims to investigate parental knowledge, attitudes and practices for the use of antibiotics among their children in Jordan. The findings of this study are expected to inform the decision-makers on policy regulations and educational campaigns that target parents on the rational use of antibiotics for their children.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is an online cross-sectional survey study that was conducted in Jordan between 20 April and 17 May 2025. This is an online survey study that used social media platforms targeting parents who have children. Qualtrics software was used in this study to collect the responses from the study participants.

Study population and sampling procedure

Parents who had children between the ages of 1 and 12 years formed the study population. A convenience sampling technique was used to invite eligible participants to participate in this study. Social media platforms (Facebook and WhatsApp) were used to invite the study participants. The study aims and inclusion criteria were highlighted in the invitation letter. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were only requested to participate in the study.

Study instrument

This study used a previously validated Arabic questionnaire tool that examined parental knowledge, attitudes and practices on the use of antibiotics in children, developed by Alkhalidi et al.16 The questionnaire consisted of three sections. The first section collected sociodemographic data from participants and included eight items such as age, gender, insurance status, number of family members, education, occupation, income and who is the primary caregiver for the child. The second section assessed knowledge through nine items, each with two possible answers: ‘yes’ or ‘no’. Correct answers were scored as 1 and incorrect answers as 0. The total knowledge score was computed by summing the corrected responses, with a higher score indicating better knowledge. In the knowledge score, the following items were coded reversibly: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. The third section evaluated participants’ attitudes using six items rated on a 3-point Likert scale (agree=3, neutral=2, disagree=1). The total score was calculated by summing the responses, where a higher score indicated a more positive attitude.

Sample size

At a CI of 95%, SD of 0.5, and a margin of error of 5%, the minimum required sample size was 383 parents.21

Questionnaire validity

The validity of the original questionnaire tool was examined through piloting it on 15 individuals. Based on their feedback, relevant adjustments were made to improve the clarity and reliability in the items of the questionnaire.

For this study, similarly, we conducted a pilot study on 40 parents who met the study inclusion criteria. The pilot study confirmed the clarity of the questionnaire tool. Besides, we conducted Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity to assess sample adequacy for factor analysis for knowledge and attitude items. For knowledge items, the KMO score was 0.710, indicating that the sample is adequate. The significant Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (χ²=670.080, df=36, p<0.001) suggests a strong relationship among the study variables. For attitude items, the KMO score was 0.719, indicating that the sample is adequate. The significant Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (χ²=734.708, df=15, p<0.001) suggests a strong relationship among the study variables.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the characteristics of the study participants, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables such as sociodemographic variables. The continuous data were expressed by mean and SD, such as the knowledge and attitude score. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables, and the data met the parametric assumption. For group comparisons, independent sample t-tests and one-way analysis of variance were employed; the Tukey post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons between the groups. The scores were categorised into two groups based on the median. For instance, the knowledge score median was 7 and 10 for the attitude. All data analysis was conducted using SPSS software, V.29. Regression analysis was used to identify predictors of participants’ knowledge and attitude. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate parental knowledge, attitudes and practices for the use of antibiotics among their children in Jordan. Participants aged 30–39 years and 40–49 years had significantly higher odds of better knowledge compared with those aged 20–29 years. Males showed lower odds of knowledge but higher odds of a positive attitude compared with females. Participants with postgraduate education had significantly better knowledge than those with high school education or less. Working in the medical field was strongly associated with higher knowledge.

There are significant differences in parents’ knowledge, behaviour and practices regarding antibiotic use among children across countries due to many factors, such as economic factors, family type, parental age and education level.22 Previous studies have indicated that middle- and low-income countries are less well-educated and knowledgeable about antibiotic use. This study found several key findings regarding parents’ knowledge, behaviour and practices regarding antibiotic use among children in Jordan, which may assist in planning and developing awareness strategies and programmes on the appropriate use of antibiotics.

One interesting finding from the current investigation is that 75.7% of parents correctly recognised that a prescription is required to dispense antibiotics. This finding indicates moderate knowledge of antibiotics among our participants, with approximately 24% incorrectly believing that antibiotics do not require a prescription. Prior studies have documented comparable findings. For instance, in India, about 72% of parents indicated that antibiotics cannot be purchased without a prescription.23 In Oman, 57% of parents implied that they did not give antibiotics to their children without a prescription.24 In Lebanon,25 Greece26 and the Republic of Srpska,27 only about 23%, 13% and 2% of parents, respectively, reported giving their children antibiotics without a prescription or a doctor’s recommendation. Moreover, parental knowledge of antibiotics among 45% of parents was also moderate in Bangladesh.22 It seems possible that the incorrect beliefs about antibiotics are due to low economic and educational status, lack of time, or because some parents believed the child had previously been prescribed this medication for the same symptoms.27 Thus, educational interventions can improve parents’ knowledge of antibiotic use and the harms of misuse, and research has shown improvements in this knowledge after educational interventions.28

In our study, 84.8% of parents disagreed with giving their children antibiotics when they have a fever, and 45.1% disagreed with requesting their physician to prescribe an antibiotic if their child has a cold. Still, misconceptions were widespread, with 23.3% of parents believing that all infections, including viral infections, could be cured with antibiotics and 45.3% assuming that antibiotics help children recover faster from coughs, runny nose and sore throat. These findings emphasised an intermediate understanding of the indications for antibiotics, with a misunderstanding regarding viral infections, including colds, coughs, runny noses and sore throat symptoms. In line with our results, several prior studies found varying levels of understanding and misunderstanding regarding antibiotic indications among parents. An earlier study among parents or caregivers in Nebraska found that 55% and 75% of participants strongly disagreed that antibiotics are necessary for a sore throat and can treat all infections, respectively.29 The same later study indicated that 87% and 95% disagreed that antibiotics treat viral infections and common colds, respectively.29 In previous surveys in Jordan, more than 72% of parents30 and nearly 50% of Jordanian and Syrian refugee parents31 believed that antibiotics should be given to children with fever. Similarly, approximately 68%–76% of parents in Malaysia thought that antibiotics help treat common colds, coughs and fever.32 48%, 79% and 83%, respectively, of parents in Pakistan,33 China34 and India23 indicated that antibiotics are used to treat children with viral infections. Approximately 80% of parents in India23 and 63% in Bangladesh22 reported that antibiotics can be used for colds and fever. Besides, about a quarter of parents in Palestine disagreed with giving antibiotics to children with influenza-like illnesses to help them recover.35 In the Republic of Srpska, all parents knew that influenza and colds are not treated with antibiotics, while 82% indicated that antibiotics are essential for treating fever.27 Several other studies have shown a lack of awareness and knowledge regarding the use of antibiotics.36 Indeed, the misuse of antibiotics to treat viral infections is associated with numerous adverse consequences, ranging from increased disturbance of normal flora to death resulting from worsening of the cases.37 Hence, there is a need for strategies, interventions and educational programmes that improve awareness and knowledge regarding the proper use of antibiotics, the importance of consulting a doctor and adherence to their instructions.

In this study, 54.3% of parents reported not reusing previously prescribed antibiotics when similar symptoms appeared, 66.8% of the parents stated they would not change their paediatrician if antibiotics were not prescribed sufficiently, and 56.9% of parents reported that they usually do not give antibiotics to their child without consulting a paediatrician. These results underscore the significance of consulting a doctor and adhering to their instructions. Consequently, in our investigation, parents’ positive attitudes regarding antibiotics are approximately average, implying a need to enhance these attitudes. Likewise, earlier research found comparable results, emphasising the importance of effective communication between parents and their paediatrician to ensure they receive advice that clarifies whether their child needs antibiotics after examining the child and explaining the nature of their illness.

In Oman, approximately 67% of parents declared that they would not change doctors if they did not prescribe antibiotics, 61% reported that the antibiotics prescription decision was for the physician, and 76% did not use leftover antibiotics.24 Similarly, in the Republic of Srpska, doctors were the primary source of information regarding antibiotic prescriptions for children for 98% of parents.27 In Greece, 86% of parents reported that they would not change their paediatrician if they did not prescribe antibiotics.26 In Bangladesh, 80% of parents were satisfied with prescriptions that did not contain antibiotics.22 These positive behaviours may be linked to strict antibiotic prescribing regulations in some countries.

In contrast, 58% of parents in Bangladesh did not consult a doctor when giving their children antibiotics.22 In Jordan, 39% of parents reported that they would change their child’s doctor if the doctor did not prescribe sufficient antibiotics.30 Many other studies also revealed that the public and parents stated leftover antibiotics can be stored and used for similar symptoms without a prescription.38 These negative attitudes may be attributed to parents’ lack of time, poor financial circumstances or poor assessment of symptoms.35 Thus, developed awareness programmes are imperative to increase parents’ and the community’s understanding of the consequences of antibiotic misuse. Besides, more stringent laws could help reduce the misuse of antibiotics, especially when dispensed without a prescription.

The current investigation found that parents aged 30–39 years (OR=1.58, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.47, p=0.045) and 40–49 years (OR=1.75, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.82, p=0.02) had significantly higher odds of better knowledge on the use of antibiotics among their children compared with those aged 20–29 years. Moreover, males showed lower odds of knowledge (OR=0.53, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.75, p<0.001) but higher odds of a positive attitude (OR=1.60, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.28, p=0.008) compared with females. Parents with postgraduate education had significantly better knowledge (OR=1.79, 95% CI 1.01 to 3.18, p=0.04) than those with high school education or less. Working in the medical field was strongly associated with higher knowledge (OR=2.45, 95% CI 1.49 to 4.03, p<0.001). Our findings are consistent with several prior studies that indicated a correlation between parental knowledge, attitudes and practices towards antibiotics and demographic factors such as age, gender and education. Older parents were associated with higher knowledge of antibiotics in Cyprus.39 Furthermore, higher parental education was associated with better antibiotic-related knowledge.23 A previous study in the Republic of Srpska found that parents’ information sources varied according to their education level, with parents with higher education using more reliable sources of information, which may explain the higher knowledge of educated parents.

Consistent with our study, positive attitudes regarding antibiotics were better among male parents in Bangladesh.22 Another study in Bangladesh found that females were more knowledgeable about antibiotic use.40 In Oman, knowledge scores were higher among fathers working in the medical field.24 Similarly, knowledge scores were higher among parents with previous medical training in India.23

Implications

Educational programmes aimed at increasing parental knowledge regarding antibiotics should focus on those younger, at lower education levels, fathers or those without medical experience. Besides, educational programmes to improve positive parental attitudes should focus on females.

Limitations

The online cross-sectional survey study design limited the ability to examine causality across the study variables. Besides, it restricted the generalisability of the study findings to those who have access to social media platforms. Moreover, self-administered survey studies are prone to reporting bias and social desirability bias, as the participants might not report their actual attitudes. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted carefully. Future longitudinal studies should examine parental knowledge, attitude and practices towards different aspects of children’s health, including self-efficacy in managing paediatric medications and treatments and vaccination, which are important aspects for the health of their children.

Conclusion

This study highlighted that parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the use of antibiotics among their children are approximately average in Jordan, with some variation. There is a need to increase awareness about the reasons for antibiotic use and the importance of consulting a doctor and following their instructions. Older age, higher education level, female gender and medical fieldwork were associated with better antibiotic knowledge. Furthermore, positive antibiotic behaviour was higher among males. These demographic factors should be considered when developing strategies, approaches and programmes to improve parents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding antibiotic use.
