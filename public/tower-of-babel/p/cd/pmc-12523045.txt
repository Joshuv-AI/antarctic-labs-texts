Post-translational modifications in ovarian cancer: implications for immunotherapy: a mini-review
Authors: Lei Sun, Ya-Fei Guo, Can-Can Duan, Cheng Chen, Lan Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer (OC) is the most lethal gynecologic malignancies worldwide, is characterized by poor prognosis and limited treatment options. While immunotherapy has shown promise in treating gynecologic cancers, particularly OC, its efficacy against OC remains suboptimal due to the distinct immune evasion mechanisms within the OC tumor microenvironment (TME). Post-translational modifications (PTMs), which involve enzyme-catalyzed attachment of functional groups to proteins, inducing structural and functional alterations, are emerging as critical regulators in tumor immunotherapy. In this review, we comprehensively summarize various PTMs that modulate the immune response in OC, primarily including ubiquitination, phosphorylation, glycosylation, and acetylation. We specifically focus on how these PTMs modulate key immune checkpoint molecules and immune evasion. Elucidating the roles of PTMs in the OC immune response could provide novel strategies for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.Keywords: Post-translational modifications, Immunotherapy, Ovarian cancer, Glycosylation

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the most lethal gynecological malignancy, with approximately 70% of patients diagnosed at advanced stages characterized by peritoneal metastasis and chemoresistance [1, 2]. Despite advances in debulking surgery and platinum-based therapies, the prognosis for OC remains dismal [3]. It is imperative to develop novel therapeutic strategies targeting drug resistance and recurrence in OC to effectively settle current treatment hurdles.

Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and adoptive T cell-receptor therapy has emerged as a promising treatment approach for gynecological cancers such as endometrial and cervical cancer, offering durable responses and improved survival outcomes [4–6]. However, the success of these immunotherapies has not been replicated in the clinical management of OC, primarily attributed to its unique immune evasion mechanisms, which differ from those of other gynecological malignancies [7–9]. The main mechanisms of immune evasion by tumor cells include surface immune checkpoint upregulation, soluble cytokine secretion, and chemokine axis dysregulation [10]. Previous studies have focused on the effect of immune checkpoints and cytokines, but emerging evidence demonstrates that post-translational modifications (PTMs) critically regulate the function of key immune checkpoints like PD-1, PD-L1, LAG-3, and CTLA-4 in immune evasion [11, 12]. PTMs occur following protein synthesis, involving chemical group additions to one or more amino acid residues that mediate alterations critical for regulating protein function, localization, stability, and interactions [13]. Common modifications include phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, glycosylation, and SUMOylation [14]. These modifications offer potential avenues for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy by altering the function of immune checkpoint proteins, disrupting immune cell signaling, remodeling TME, and modulating immune responses [12, 15, 16]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of PTMs will provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of OC immunotherapy and may uncover novel therapeutic opportunities to improve treatment outcomes.

In this review, we delineate the pivotal roles of PTMs in OC immunotherapy, aiming to provide novel perspectives for PTM-targeted therapies to enhance the efficacy of current immunotherapeutic approaches in OC.

Key post-translational modifications in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Studies have demonstrated that PTMs play a crucial role in regulating immune cell function and modulating immune evasion mechanisms. In the following section, we will discuss several PTMs highly relevant to OC immunotherapy.

Ubiquitination in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Ubiquitination, as a prevalent post-translational modification, plays pivotal roles in diverse cellular processes, including cell cycle progression, signal transduction, and transcriptional regulation. It also functions importantly in immune system operations such as lymphocyte differentiation, T-cell tolerance induction, antigen presentation, and immune evasion [17]. Aberrant ubiquitination of immune checkpoint proteins and intracellular signaling molecules drives immunotherapy resistance in OC by impairing antitumor immune responses [18].

Murine double minute 2 (MDM2), an E3 ubiquitin ligase overexpressed in ovarian clear cell carcinoma, has been found to suppress T cell-mediated tumor killing induced by immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Combination therapy with MDM2 inhibitors and anti-PD-L1 enhances T cell-mediated tumor cell killing and overcomes drug resistance, highlighting the potential of E3 ubiquitin ligases as therapeutic targets involved in PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy [19]. Moreover, chlorambucil, a PD-L1 depleting drug, has been shown to induce ubiquitination via the E3 ligase β-transducin repeat-containing protein (β-TRCP) and Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3β (GSK3β)/β-TRCP-mediated degradation of the PD-L1 proteasome, reversing PD-L1 therapy resistance in OC cells and promoting the antitumor activity of ICB [20]. The p53 protein has been identified as a key regulator of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in OC, by inducing p53-specific memory T-cell responses through cytokine production as well as facilitating the recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages [21, 22]. Mechanistically, the expression and function of p53 are tightly regulated by ubiquitination processes, which exhibits elective specificity for mutant p53 (mtp53). For instance, the E3 enzyme DTX3 mediates ubiquitination of mtp53 and maintains the stability of mtp53 by preventing MDM2-mediated ubiquitinated degradation of mtp53, thus exerting the pro-oncogenic effect and immune evasion of mtp53 [23]. In addition, UBR5 regulates tumor spheroid formation of OC through the p53/β-catenin pathway and then enhances immunosuppression by recruiting tumor-associated macrophages. Thus, targeting UBR5 significantly suppresses tumor growth and overcomes resistance to conventional chemotherapy and immunotherapy in OC, then enhances the efficacy of standard OC therapies [24]. Additionally, the deubiquitinase USP35 removes ubiquitin chains from the stimulator of interferon genes, thereby dampening interferon signaling and reducing cisplatin-induced antitumor immune responses [25] (Fig. 1).

Phosphorylation in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Phosphorylation, the most extensively characterized PTM, acts as a molecular “switch” that regulates cellular growth, signal transduction, immune cell activation, and cytokine secretion [26]. Increasing evidence indicates that phosphorylation of both signaling molecules and immune checkpoint proteins plays a critical role in immune evasion and antitumor immunity in OC [27].

The signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is a point of convergence for numerous oncogenic signaling pathways. Phosphorylated STAT3 plays a prominent role in immune responses by suppressing the expression of crucial immune activation regulators and promoting the production of immunosuppressive factors [28, 29]. In an OC mouse model, the loss of tissue transglutaminase (TG2) reduced the tumor burden in mice and decreased myeloid cell infiltration in the peritoneal TME. Mechanistically, TG2 deficiency elevated phosphorylated STAT1 and decreased phosphorylated STAT3, thereby supporting the differentiation and function of T cells [30]. Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis), a current mainstay therapy for OC, enhance the phosphorylation of STAT3 in both tumor and immune cells, leading to immunosuppression acquired in OC [31, 32]. Conversely, targeting poly(ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase, an enzyme that has the opposite function to PARP, reduces STAT3 phosphorylation. This suppression potentiates CD8 + T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and upregulates immunostimulatory molecules, such as Interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and granzyme B, ultimately fostering an immune-active tumor microenvironment [33]. This mechanism of inducing antitumor effects positions the phosphorylation of STAT3 as a promising therapeutic target for combination immunotherapy in OC. Furthermore, macrophage-derived CCL23 induces upregulation of immune checkpoint proteins on CD8+ T cells, including CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3 via phosphorylation of GSK3β, thereby driving T cell exhaustion and promoting an immunosuppressive TME in OC [34] (Fig. 2).

Glycosylation in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Glycosylation is a prevalent and intricate PTM wherein glycans are attached to target proteins, exerting profound implications on diverse cellular processes, including cell growth, differentiation, transformation, adhesion, and immune surveillance against tumors [35].

In OC, alterations in glycosylation represent a complex phenomenon influenced by multiple factors, including dysregulation of glycosyltransferase activity, alterations in the TME, and inflammation. Aberrant glycosylation patterns can promote immune evasion by shielding tumor cells from immune recognition, thereby contributing to disease progression [36, 37]. Glycosylation is mainly classified into two types, N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation. Homologous recombination (HR) proficient OC exploits elevated branched N-glycans as a mechanism to evade immune surveillance and reinforce PD-1/PD-L1 binding on T cells, thereby diminishing the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy (Fig. 3A). Inhibition of branched N-glycans sensitizes HR-proficient OC to ICB by overcoming immune evasion [38]. Beyond N-glycosylation, aberrant O-glycosylation of tumor surface proteins like MUC16 drives immune evasion. MUC16, a heavily glycosylated protein expressed on the surface of ovarian tumor cells, inhibits the formation of immune synapses between NK cells and ovarian tumor targets to evade immune recognition. The immune protection conferred by MUC16 may result in the selective survival of OC cells, thereby facilitating their peritoneal dissemination and ability to overcome anti-tumor innate immune responses [39] (Fig. 3A-B). Given the pivotal role of glycosylation in immune evasion, a prognostic risk model with glycosylation-related messenger RNAs (GRmRNAs) has been established in OC by integrating machine learning methods and statistical approaches. This GRmRNA-based risk model could accurately predict the prognosis, the immune microenvironment, and the immunotherapeutic efficacy of OC patients, where high-risk scores showed poor prognosis and low immune infiltration levels [40]. Notably, elevated GALNT10 expression, identified as an independent predictor of prognosis in high-grade ovarian serous cancer, correlates with increased regulatory CD4+ T cells infltration and decreased granzyme B expression in CD8+ T cells [41]. Moreover, glycan structures can influence immune responses by altering interactions with immune cell receptors and glycan-binding proteins, such as C-type lectins, Siglecs, and Galectins, which function as scavengers of cytokines or chemokines. Specifically, the Galectin-9/ PD-1/TIM-3 axis has been shown to mediate T cell exhaustion, and the correlation between elevated Galectin-9 and TIM-3 expression has also implicated this interaction in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of OC [42, 43]. Siglec-9, a negative glycoimmune checkpoint expressed on myeloid cells, NK cells, and specific T-cell subsets, also exerts immunosuppressive effects by binding to sialoglycan ligands on OC cells [44]. In addition, the CD24/Siglec-10 axis has been demonstrated to modulate anti-tumor immunity in OC. Antibody-mediated disruption of this interaction promotes macrophage phagocytosis of CD24 + tumor cells, reduces tumor growth, and enhances survival rates in vivo [45] (Fig. 3B).

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, as a rapidly evolving therapeutic approach within adoptive cell transfer, continues to face challenges in OC due to the scarcity of tumor-specific antigens, on-target/off-tumor effects, tumor antigen escape, and the immunosuppression [46]. Currently, some reports on CAR T cells with specifically altered glycosylation structures have been investigated. For instance, N-glycoslylated ganglioside monosialic 3 (NGcGM3) targeted CAR-T cells prevent OC progression, resulting in low toxicity in healthy tissues [47]. Furthermore, in a syngeneic OC model utilizing genetically modified ID8 cells, CAR T cells targeting the Tn-glycopeptide antigen demonstrated potent antitumor activity. Knockout of the glycosyltransferase chaperone COSMC (core 1 β3-galactosyltransferase specific molecular chaperone) in ID8 cells induced aberrant O-glycosylation and enhanced Tn expression, altering CAR binding affinity [48]. TAG72 (Tumor-associated Glycoprotein 72), an STn O-glycan carbohydrate on cell surface glycoproteins, is highly expressed in OC cells. In an intraperitoneal mouse model, TAG72-CAR-T cells exhibited potent antigen-specific cytotoxicity against both OC cell lines and patient-derived ascites tumor cells. Concurrently, a Phase I trial of TAG72-CAR-T cells is underway, assessing safety and determining the optimal dosage in platinum-resistant OC patients [49, 50] (Fig. 3C).

Acetylation in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Acetylation is a reversible PTM that significantly affects chromatin structure and regulates various cellular functions, including cell cycle progression, such as cell cycle progression, differentiation, and immune cell activation [51, 52]. This process can be regulated through specific enzymes, including histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) [53]. Dysregulation of HATs- and HDACs- mediated acetylation is strongly associated with the pathogenesis, resistance to chemotherapy, and alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment of OC [54].

IFNγ, primarily produced by activated T cells, has been found to induce PD-L1 expression in OC cells, leading to immune evasion and tumor growth. Mechanistically, IFNγ induces PD-L1 promoter acetylation. Suppression of JAK1/STAT1 signaling significantly reduces IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in OC cells, suggesting that targeting the JAK1/STAT1/IFNγ pathway may provide a strategy to modulate PD-L1 levels in OC [55, 56]. Moreover, recent research reveals that mannose enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy in OC by increasing the production of propionate and butyrate. These metabolites enhanced histone acetylation, which promoted the expansion of progenitor-exhausted CD8 + T cells. More importantly, the triple combination of mannose, anti-PD-1 therapy, and PARPi therapy achieved complete tumor regression in a subset of mice, providing a strong rationale for the design of this combinatorial treatment approach [57]. Based on the main role of acetylation in OC, a novel prognostic signature model for acetylation-related gene regulation was established using samples from TCGA and GTEx databases. Further analysis of the tumor microenvironment with this model showed significant correlations with tumor immune dysfunction, immunotherapy response, and drug sensitivity [58]. Numerous studies have revealed that HDAC inhibitors function as pivotal epigenetic regulators in cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and immune responses, demonstrating significant potential for anti-cancer immunotherapy [59]. Multiple studies have found that HDAC inhibitors play a vital role as epigenetic regulators in the regulation of cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and immune responses, demonstrating significant potential for anticancer immunotherapy. For instance, the combination of Niraparib and Entinostat induces a functional homologous recombination deficient (HRD) phenotype through activation of the STING pathway and increased histone acetylation. This combination therapy enhances immune cell cytotoxicity and promotes pro-immunogenic cell infiltration into malignant ascites, thus suppressing OC growth [60] (Fig. 4).

Other PTMs in ovarian cancer immunotherapy

Beyond the major modifications described above, emerging evidence highlights the growing investigation of atypical PTMs in shaping OC immunotherapy. Knockdown of dihydrolipoyl succinyltransferase (DLST), a subunit of α-KGDC in the tricarboxylic acid cycle in OC cells reduces oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and diminishes the expression and immunosuppressive function of markers in myeloid cells [61]. Concurrently, lactylation, a metabolite-driven PTM is emerging as a critical regulator of tumor-immune interplay. Recent studies have demonstrated that lactylation-related genes are closely related to tumor molecular subtypes and immunity in patients with OC [62]. Overall, LDHB mediates metabolic reprogramming and histone lactylation to activate PD-L1 and facilitate the immune evasion of OC cells [63]. Moreover, palmitoylation, the reversible attachment of fatty acids to cysteine residues, influences a broad array of cellular processes, including signal transduction and immune response [64]. Prognostic analyses across independent cohorts and public datasets have consistently identified low palmitoyl protein thioesterase-2 (PPT2), the key protein of palmitoylation, expression as a potential biomarker in OC. Intriguingly, PPT2 levels demonstrate negative correlations with immune cell infiltration and predictive sensitivity to therapeutic agents, implying that OC patients with PPT2 deficiency may exhibit enhanced vulnerability to immunotherapy and chemotherapy regimens [65]. Additionally, based on univariate-LASSO-multivariate Cox analyses, a prognostic palmitoylation-related genes risk model was constructed that effectively stratified OC patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct survival outcomes. The low-risk groups have demonstrated elevated immune scores and increased checkpoint expression (PD-1 and CTLA-4), suggesting enhanced immunotherapeutic responsiveness compared to their high-risk groups [66].

Conclusions and future perspectives

To date, it is well established that PTMs profoundly modulate protein function and regulate disease progression. Accumulating evidence demonstrates the vital role of PTMs in modulating OC immune responses. As discussed above, these modifications mainly impact the expression and function of key immune checkpoints and the infiltration of immune cells. Moreover, the prediction models constructed based on different types of PTMs are significantly associated with the response to immunotherapy in OC patients. Therefore, targeting PTMs represents a novel and promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy for OC. Furthermore, combining PTM modulators with immunotherapy may elicit synergistic effects. For instance, the combination of PTM-targeting drugs with CAR-T cell therapy has the promise for enhancing T cell persistence and therapeutic efficacy [67]. The combination of glycosylation inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade could further enhance T-cell activation and immune responses [68]. Similarly, combining HDAC inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade increases the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and reduces myeloid-derived suppressor cells in the TME [69]. However, PTM inhibitors have been currently studied in vitro experiments and animal models, with no clinical trials reported in OC immunotherapy to date. In summary, PTM-directed approaches not only synergize with immune checkpoint blockade to enhance therapeutic efficacy but also offer novel combination strategies to improve treatment outcomes in OC patients.

Despite the therapeutic promise of targeting PTMs, the translational medicine for OC faces multiple obstacles, including the genetic heterogeneity and dynamic complexity of TME. Thus, it is imperative to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which PTMs modulate the immunological responses in OC. The following points remain further investigation:

1) Elucidating diverse PTMs crosstalk in the OC TME.

Current studies predominantly focus on individual PTMs, but the functional interplay between PTMs in regulating immune checkpoints or immune cell function remains poorly defined. Priority should be given to dissecting such crosstalk in OC specific TME.

2) Identifying PTM-based predictive biomarkers for precision therapy.

A major barrier to translating PTM-targeted therapies is the lack of reliable biomarkers to identify patients most likely to benefit. Current prediction models are preliminary, and their clinical utility was unvalidated. Research priority should focus on developing PTM-specific signatures that correlate with immunotherapy response.

2) Optimizing combinatorial strategies with PTM modulators and immunotherapies.

While preclinical studies suggest synergies between PTM inhibitors and immunotherapies in vitro experiments and animal models, it is doubtful about its effectiveness in patients. Priority research should use patient-derived models to test combinatorial regimens, focusing on dose-finding and timing to maximize synergistic antitumor effects while minimizing toxicity to achieve successful clinical translation.

With the advancement of sophisticated technologies such as 3D organoid models, single-cell sequencing, nanotechnology, and mass spectrometry, future clinical strategy holds promise for more complex and personalized immunotherapies based on PTM and TME dynamics. Therefore, targeting PTMs holds promise for enhancing the benefits of immunotherapy for OC patients, thus promoting their clinical treatment outcomes.
