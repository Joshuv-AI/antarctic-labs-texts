Development of KASP marker to enhance breeding efficiency for phytophthora blight resistance in sesame through QTL analysis
Authors: Jeongeun Lee, Eunyoung Oh, Sungup Kim, Jung In Kim, Sang Woo Kim, Yejin Lee, Kwang-Soo Cho, Jin-Cheol Kim, Eunsoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhytophthora blight, caused by Phytophthora nicotianae, is a destructive disease that severely affects sesame (Sesamum indicum L.). Developing resistant cultivars remains the most effective management strategy. This study aimed to identify genomic regions associated with Phytophthora blight resistance and to develop molecular markers for use in sesame breeding.ResultsWhole-genome resequencing (WGS) was performed for the resistant cultivar Geonbaek, the susceptible cultivar Milsung, and 99 recombinant inbred lines (RILs). Using a high-density genetic map, we identified a major resistance locus on chromosome 10, designated qPB10. The IM-ADD analysis defined a 0.51-Mb marker interval with an LOD score of 25.67, and a complementary binary-trait analysis independently detected the same locus at 75.10 cM. Genomic analysis of the target region revealed several immune-related genes, including clusters encoding nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) proteins and other defense-associated factors. To utilize these findings in breeding, kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) markers were developed based on sequence polymorphisms within the qPB10 interval. Validation using the RIL population and a diverse panel of sesame cultivars identified a robust marker that reliably discriminates resistant genotypes.ConclusionThese findings establish qPB10 as the primary locus governing Phytophthora blight resistance in sesame. The KASP markers developed in this study enable efficient marker-assisted selection without the need for extensive disease phenotyping. These results provide a practical genomic resource for accelerated breeding of resistant cultivars and establish a foundation for future functional characterization of resistance mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09589-1.Keywords: Sesame, Phytophthora blight, QTL, KASP marker

Background

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is one of the oldest oilseed crops and is thought to have originated in Africa and India [1]. It is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions, with more than 95% of global production concentrated in Africa and Asia [2]. As the global demand for sesame continues to grow, achieving stable and sustainable production remains challenging due to diverse abiotic and biotic stresses [3]. Among these, plant diseases represent the primary constraint on yield stability.

Numerous studies involving diverse pathogens have examined diseases affecting sesame. Bacterial blight has largely been addressed through field-based germplasm screening to identify resistant sources [4], whereas powdery mildew resistance has been analyzed genetically and is reported to involve recessive inheritance with complementary epistasis [5]. In contrast, research on charcoal rot, Fusarium wilt, and Phytophthora blight has increasingly employed molecular marker-based approaches, particularly quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping, to identify resistance-associated genomic loci [6–8]. Despite these advances across various pathogens, translating genomic discoveries into practical breeding programs remains a significant challenge.

This gap is particularly critical for Phytophthora blight, one of the most serious threats to sesame production. The disease is caused by the soil- and waterborne oomycete Phytophthora nicotianae, which is highly destructive due to its rapid infection cycle and high virulence. The infection typically begins with stem discoloration and wilting near the soil surface, followed by rapid progression to severe stem necrosis. Under favorable conditions, substantial yield losses, and in extreme cases, complete crop failure may occur [9]. Disease severity is further exacerbated by the biological characteristics of P. nicotianae: it spreads efficiently under warm, humid conditions and persists in soil through the formation of long-lived chlamydospores [10, 11], which can remain viable for several years and serve as a continuous inoculum source. The combination of rapid infection, efficient dispersal, and prolonged soil survival complicates disease management and contributes to recurrent outbreaks in sesame-growing regions.

Current management strategies for Phytophthora blight in sesame rely primarily on fungicides and cultural practices such as crop rotation; however, these approaches have several limitations. Although fungicides can reduce disease severity, repeated use is environmentally unsustainable and costly [12]. Similarly, the effectiveness of crop rotation at lowering soil inoculum levels is constrained by the long-term survival of P. nicotianae in soil [13]. Additionally, projected increases in temperature and rainfall associated with climate change are expected to promote pathogen survival and infection [14]. These limitations highlight the need for genetically resistant cultivars as a durable and environmentally friendly approach for managing Phytophthora blight in sesame.

Breeding for disease resistance in sesame is further complicated by challenges in phenotypic evaluation. Resistance to Phytophthora blight is typically assessed under controlled conditions using artificial inoculation, but these results often do not fully reflect field performance due to environmental variability. Identifying resistance-associated loci and their linked markers enables marker-assisted selection (MAS), allowing reliable selection of resistant genotypes with minimal environmental influence [15]. MAS has been successfully implemented in several major crops, including potatoes and tomatoes, to improve resistance to diseases, including potato virus Y, Fusarium wilt, and late blight [16, 17]. However, its application in sesame disease resistance remains limited.

In this study, we aimed to identify genomic regions associated with Phytophthora blight resistance and to develop molecular markers for sesame breeding. Whole-genome resequencing (WGS) was conducted for the resistant cultivar Geonbaek and the susceptible cultivar Milsung, along with 99 recombinant inbred lines (RILs). Based on these data, QTLs and putative candidate genes associated with Phytophthora blight resistance were identified, and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the target region were subsequently converted into kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) markers. These markers were further evaluated using both the RIL population and sesame germplasm to assess their utility for selecting Phytophthora blight-resistant cultivars.

Methods

Plant materials and disease assessment

The F₇ RIL population used in this study was derived from a cross between the Phytophthora blight-resistant cultivar Geonbaek and the susceptible cultivar Milsung. Both parental cultivars were developed by the National Institute of Crop and Food Science (NICS), Rural Development Administration (RDA), Miryang, Republic of Korea, and the RIL population was previously developed and described by Asekova et al. [6]. The complete population, comprising 188 F₇ RILs and the two parental cultivars, was phenotypically evaluated for Phytophthora blight resistance using the disease-screening procedure described below. For whole-genome resequencing, 99 RILs were randomly selected without consideration of disease phenotype from lines with sufficient seed stocks and high-quality genomic DNA. These selected RILs, together with the parental cultivars, were used for whole-genome resequencing, genetic map construction, QTL mapping, and KASP marker development. Marker validation was conducted using an independent panel of 40 elite sesame cultivars, including 20 resistant and 20 susceptible accessions based on disease-screening results. These cultivars are maintained and routinely used as breeding parents in sesame improvement programs at NICS, RDA, Republic of Korea.

To determine their phenotypes, the cultivars were evaluated for Phytophthora blight resistance using the same inoculation and disease assessment procedures detailed in the following section, and were classified as resistant or susceptible. Inoculum preparation and disease screening were performed with slight modifications to a previously described protocol [18]. P. nicotianae isolate KACC48121 was cultured on agar medium, and agar plugs were subsequently transferred into V8 broth and incubated at 28℃ in the dark for five days. The resulting mycelia were rinsed with sterile water and further incubated under fluorescent light at 23℃ for seven days to induce zoospore production. The suspension was then filtered through cheesecloth. Inoculation was performed by applying 2 mL of the adjusted inoculum (1 × 104-1 × 105 zoospores mL− 1) to each seedling grown in a 128-cell tray using the soil drenching method. Following inoculation, the soil moisture was maintained at saturation. To ensure phenotypic reliability, the entire disease screening process was conducted with three independent biological replicates, evaluating 20 plants per replicate for each line. Disease severity (DS) was evaluated seven days after inoculation using the 0–9 scale described in the protocol, where 0 indicated no visible symptoms and 9 indicated complete wilting and plant death (Fig. 1). Based on the mean DS values, lines were classified according to the established criteria [18], resistant (R, 0–3), moderately resistant (MR, 3.1-5), moderately susceptible (MS, 5.1-7), or susceptible (S, 7.1-9). Statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.5.2) and statistical significance was assessed where applicable.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and genetic map construction

Genomic DNA was extracted from the first true leaves using a NucleoSpin Plant II Mini Kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co. KG, Germany), and DNA quality was assessed with a NanoDrop 3000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Whole-genome resequencing of the parental lines and 99 RILs was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [19] to remove low-quality bases (Phred quality score < 20) and residual adapter sequences. The filtered reads were aligned to the Zhongzhi No. 13 v2.0 reference genome using BWA v0.7.17 [20, 21]. Variant calling was conducted with the HaplotypeCaller module of Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.2.0.0 to identify SNPs and insertions/deletions (InDels) [22]. For downstream genetic mapping, high-confidence variants were selected using GATK hard-filtering criteria. Multi-allelic variants were excluded prior to filtering. SNPs were filtered using the following thresholds: QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < − 12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0. InDels were filtered using QD < 2.0, FS > 200.0, and ReadPosRankSum < − 20.0. Parental polymorphisms were required to be homozygous and polymorphic between the two parents, with allele depths ranging from 10 to 200. RIL genotypes were retained if they had allele depths of 5–200 and a genotype call rate ≥ 90%. Variants located within 100 bp of another variant were also removed.

Following variant calling, filtered SNP genotypes from the parental lines and RILs were refined using the Maximum Parsimony Recombination (MPR) method [23]. Recombination breakpoints were identified in each RIL using a sliding-window approach of nine consecutive SNPs [24]. A breakpoint was defined when the majority parental genotype within the window shifted from a 5:4 to a 4:5 allele ratio, or vice versa. Adjacent co-segregating SNPs were merged into single bins, and a graphical bin map was generated from the MPR output using MPR.genotyping v0.8 in R v4.2.2. Chromosomal segments derived from Geonbaek and Milsung were visualized in blue and red, respectively. Bin markers were subsequently used for genetic map construction in QTL IciMapping v4.2. SNPs were assigned to linkage groups (LGs) according to their physical positions, and RIL genotypes were coded based on parental alleles. Genetic distances were estimated using the Kosambi mapping function, and marker ordering within each LG was optimized using the k-optimality algorithm followed by rippling.

QTL analysis and gene annotation

QTL localization was initially conducted using the Interval Mapping Additive (IM-ADD) method in QTL IciMapping v4.2 with a scanning step of 1 cM. An exploratory LOD threshold of 2.5 was applied, and the resulting flanking markers were used to define the candidate interval for gene annotation and KASP marker development. For complementary binary-trait analysis, resistant and moderately resistant lines were coded as 0, whereas moderately susceptible and susceptible lines were coded as 1. Genome-wide interval mapping was then performed in R/qtl v1.74 under R v4.5.2 [25, 26]. Multipoint genotype probabilities were estimated at 1-cM intervals using the Kosambi mapping function with a genotyping error probability of 0.001. Genome scans were conducted using the expectation–maximization algorithm (scanone, model = “binary”, method = “em”). The 5% genome-wide significance threshold was determined from 1,000 permutation tests, and a 1.5-LOD support interval was calculated for the significant peak.

Genes located within the QTL interval were functionally annotated through comparative sequence analysis. Homologous genes were identified using BLASTP v2.10.1 + against Arabidopsis thaliana protein sequences with default parameters. Functional annotations were assigned based on the corresponding A. thaliana homologs, and additional gene information was obtained from the TAIR database (version 10).

Functional SNP identification and KASP marker development

To identify functional variants, SNPs were annotated using SnpEff v5.0e based on the Zhongzhi No. 13 v2.0 genome annotation [27]. Candidate SNPs located within annotated genes were further validated by Sanger sequencing. SNPs identified within the chromosome 10 QTL interval were selected for conversion into KASP markers. For each SNP, two allele-specific forward primers and one common reverse primer were designed using 50-bp flanking sequences on either side of the target locus and synthesized by LGC (Beverly, MA, USA) (Supplementary Table S1).

KASP reactions were prepared with slight modifications to the manufacturer’s instructions and contained 5 µL of 2× KASP master mix, 0.14 µL of primer mix, 0.5 µL of genomic DNA (10 ng µL⁻¹), and 4.36 µL of nuclease-free water, giving a total reaction volume of 10 µL. PCR amplification followed the standard KASP protocol, consisting of an initial activation step at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by 10 touchdown cycles of 94 °C for 20 s and 61–55 °C for 1 min (decreasing by 0.6 °C per cycle), and 26 additional cycles of 94 °C for 20 s and 55 °C for 1 min.

The predictive performance of the developed KASP markers was evaluated across the entire RIL population, comprising both lines subjected to whole-genome resequencing and those not included in the resequencing analysis. Marker accuracy was calculated as follows: [number of lines with matching genotype and phenotype / total number of evaluated lines] × 100.

Results

Phenotype segregation of Phytophthora blight resistance

Phenotypic segregation of Phytophthora blight resistance in the Geonbaek × Milsung population was previously reported [6], showing a 1:3 segregation ratio (resistant to susceptible) in the F₂ population (χ² = 1.88, P = 0.17), consistent with a predominantly qualitative resistance phenotype governed by a single major recessive factor. The F₇ RIL population subsequently exhibited a 1:1 segregation of resistant and susceptible lines, reflecting fixation of the resistance trait. In the present study, the complete population of 188 F₇ RILs, together with the two parental cultivars, was evaluated for Phytophthora blight resistance under artificial inoculation conditions. Among the eligible lines with sufficient seed stocks and high-quality genomic DNA, 99 RILs were randomly selected for whole-genome resequencing irrespective of disease-response phenotype. Geonbaek showed no visible symptoms, whereas the susceptible parent Milsung exhibited dark discoloration at the basal stem that progressed to stem rot and ultimately plant death. This phenotypic contrast was consistently observed under field conditions (Fig. 2). The 99 randomly selected RILs were re-evaluated for resistance, and their segregation (R = 50, S = 49) did not significantly deviate from the expected 1:1 ratio (χ2 = 0.01, P = 0.92).

Whole-genome resequencing, genetic map construction, and QTL analysis

Whole-genome resequencing (WGS) was performed for the parental lines Geonbaek and Milsung and 99 randomly selected F₇ RILs. After quality trimming, approximately 220.4 million reads were retained for Geonbaek and 93.2 million reads for Milsung. In total, approximately 4 billion trimmed reads from the parental lines and RIL population were aligned to the reference genome (Supplementary Table S2). Variant calling identified 26,694 SNPs and 4,693 InDels across the population. Following genotype refinement using the MPR method, a final set of 6,044 high-quality SNPs was retained for downstream analyses. Co-segregating SNPs were merged into bins, and recombination breakpoint analysis identified 3,927 breakpoints across the RIL population. A total of 4,309 bin markers were used to construct the final genetic map (Fig. 3), comprising 13 linkage groups (LGs) corresponding to the sesame chromosomes (Table 1). Chromosome 5 contained the fewest bins, whereas chromosome 6 contained the most, reflecting differences in recombination frequency and/or marker density.

QTL analysis identified a single major resistance locus on chromosome 10 associated with Phytophthora blight resistance. The QTL was located between markers Chr10_14425032 and Chr10_14941157, with an LOD score of 25.67 and a phenotypic variance explained (PVE) of 69.35% (Table 2). The PVE value of 69.35% estimated by IM-ADD refers to variation in the binary-coded disease-response phenotype rather than variation in the original 0–9 disease-severity score., a complementary binary-trait genome scan was conducted using R/qtl. A single genome-wide significant peak was detected on chromosome 10 at 75.10 cM (LOD = 17.73), exceeding the 5% genome-wide permutation threshold of 3.14 (Supplementary Figure S1). The 1.5-LOD support interval spanned 74.48–75.10 cM, corresponding to Chr10_14425032–Chr10_15225416 and encompassing the IM-ADD-defined qPB10 interval. Together, these analyses consistently identified the same major resistance locus on chromosome 10.

Notably, this QTL overlapped a chromosome 10 region previously identified by GBS using the same resistant parent, Geonbaek, and the KACC48121 isolate [6]. The earlier study defined a 2.01-Mb interval at 12.9–14.9 Mb, whereas the present WGS analysis refined the overlapping region to 14.4–14.9 Mb. Although LOD and PVE values were not directly comparable because of methodological differences, the repeated detection of this region supports its reproducibility, while the increased marker density provided by WGS enabled more precise QTL localization.

Identification of candidate genes

A QTL associated with Phytophthora blight resistance was identified on chromosome 10, spanning an approximately 0.51 Mb interval (Chr10:14,425,032 − 14,941,157 bp). Within this interval, 68 genes were predicted based on a comparative analysis using protein sequence information from A. thaliana (Supplementary Table S3). Among these, 11 genes were annotated as disease resistance-related (Fig. 4), including one gene homologous to SIN_1019011 (corresponding to AT5G67080.1), which encodes a mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK), and ten genes encoding nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) proteins. Within the QTL interval, 319 SNPs and 51 InDels were identified. Among these, non-synonymous SNPs causing amino acid substitutions were detected in 19 genes (Table 3). To validate these variants, Sanger sequencing was performed on the parental lines, Geonbaek and Milsung, which confirmed that 16 of these variants exhibited clear polymorphisms between the two genotypes (Geonbaek and Milsung).

Development of KASP markers

Based on the 16 validated SNP variants between Geonbaek and Milsung, KASP markers were developed, of which 10 successfully distinguished between the resistant and susceptible parental lines. These markers were subsequently evaluated across the entire RIL population, and their classification accuracies are summarized in Table 4. Among these, KASP_qPB10_8 showed the highest accuracy for identifying resistant lines (93.48%), whereas KASP_qPB10_4 exhibited the highest accuracy for identifying susceptible lines (89.26%). Based on their performances in the RIL population, KASP_qPB10_4 and KASP_qPB10_8 were further evaluated in a panel of 20 resistant and 20 susceptible sesame cultivars grown in Korea, which serve as important parental cultivars in breeding. Under optimized KASP reaction conditions, KASP_qPB10_4 correctly distinguished between the resistant and susceptible cultivars in accordance with their phenotypes. In contrast, KASP_qPB10_8 independently classified six resistant cultivars as susceptible genotypes (Fig. 5, Table 5). Overall, KASP_qPB10_4 demonstrated more consistent performance across both the RIL population and the independent cultivar panel, indicating that it is a more reliable marker for the MAS of Phytophthora blight resistance in sesame.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a major resistance locus associated with Phytophthora blight in sesame using whole-genome resequencing of a recombinant inbred line population derived from Geonbaek and Milsung. The IM-ADD analysis localized qPB10 to an approximately 0.51-Mb interval on chromosome 10, with an LOD score of 25.67 and a PVE of 69.35%. Because the phenotype used for linkage analysis was binary coded, the PVE and additive-effect estimate of 0.43 describe variation on the observed 0/1 scale and should not be interpreted as effects on the original 0–9 disease-severity scale. A complementary binary-trait R/qtl analysis independently detected a single genome-wide significant signal on chromosome 10, and its broader 1.5-LOD support interval encompassed the original IM-ADD-defined interval. The concordant localization of qPB10 supports the robustness of the chromosome 10 signal across different mapping approaches. Although disease severity was recorded on an ordinal 0–9 scale, the segregation patterns observed in the F₂ and RIL populations were consistent with inheritance by a single major recessive factor [6]. Resistance in the Geonbaek × Milsung population is therefore best interpreted as a predominantly qualitative major-locus trait with variable symptom expression, rather than as classical polygenic quantitative resistance. The term qPB10 is retained as linkage-mapping nomenclature, and the original flanking-marker interval was retained as the operational candidate region for subsequent gene annotation and KASP marker development.

Advances in DNA sequencing, linkage mapping, marker-assisted selection, and genomic selection have increased breeding efficiency in major crops with extensive genomic resources [28–30]. In minor crops such as sesame, however, limited genomic resources and the scarcity of validated disease-resistance markers continue to restrict the practical application of genome-wide breeding approaches [31, 32]. A previous study of Phytophthora blight resistance used GBS in a Geonbaek × Osan RIL population and also identified a resistance-associated region on chromosome 10 [6]. However, coding-region SNPs suitable for practical marker development were not identified, and candidate-gene inference relied primarily on differential gene expression. The more limited genome coverage and SNP density of GBS may restrict the detection of functional polymorphisms within coding regions [33]. In the present study, WGS enabled more comprehensive detection of genome-wide variation [34], localization of an overlapping chromosome 10 interval, and identification of non-synonymous SNPs in candidate genes. Because the previous and current studies differed in mapping population, phenotype coding, marker dataset, and statistical model, their LOD and PVE values should not be compared directly. Nevertheless, detection of overlapping chromosome 10 regions in the two populations supports the reproducibility of this resistance locus, while the identification and validation of coding-region polymorphisms provide a practical basis for KASP marker development.

A total of 68 annotated genes were identified within the qPB10 interval, of which 11 were classified as disease resistance-related. Several of these genes contained non-synonymous polymorphisms between the resistant and susceptible parental lines, suggesting potential functional variation associated with Phytophthora blight resistance. Comparative sequence analysis against the Arabidopsis thaliana database indicated that these genes included a putative mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK) and ten nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) proteins. The interval also contained genes encoding proteins with pentatricopeptide repeat, tetratricopeptide repeat, ankyrin repeat, and cytochrome P450 domains, which have been implicated in immune signaling, pathogen recognition, and defense regulation [35, 36]. Resistance phenotypes may be determined not only by individual resistance genes but also by the collective effects of clustered resistance genes and structural variation within resistance loci, which can generate functional diversity and influence pathogen recognition [37]. NLR genes are frequently organized in clusters within plant genomes, and such clustering may facilitate diversification of pathogen recognition through localized duplication, recombination, and diversifying selection [38]. In several plant–Phytophthora interactions, NLR-mediated resistance involves direct or indirect recognition of pathogen avirulence effectors, including RxLR-type effectors, followed by activation of effector-triggered immunity [39]. Structural variation within clustered NLR families may also contribute to allele-specific recognition and adaptation to changing oomycete effectors [40]. However, the present linkage data cannot determine whether resistance is conferred by a single NLR gene, another gene within the interval, or structural variation not represented in the reference genome. Functional validation, analysis of NLR cluster organization and structural variation, and characterization of candidate gene–effector interactions will therefore be required to identify the causal gene and clarify the molecular mechanism of resistance.

Non-synonymous variants within the candidate genes were validated by Sanger sequencing to confirm polymorphisms between Geonbaek and Milsung, and selected variants were converted into KASP markers for marker-assisted genotyping. Among the evaluated markers, KASP_qPB10_4 showed consistently high classification accuracy across both the RIL population and the independent panel of 40 elite Korean sesame cultivars. Its relatively stable performance may partly reflect its close linkage to SIN_1019015, which encodes a candidate NLR protein. Differences in classification accuracy among the developed markers may result from recombination between individual markers and the causal variant, differences in genetic background, structural variation, or uncertainty in phenotypic classification, rather than necessarily indicating a broadly polygenic resistance mechanism. The consistent performance of KASP_qPB10_4 in elite cultivars currently used as breeding parents supports its potential utility for marker-assisted selection within Korean sesame breeding programs.

Several limitations of the present study should nevertheless be acknowledged. Although all 188 RILs were phenotypically evaluated, the WGS-based mapping analysis used a relatively modest subset of 99 RILs, which may have limited the statistical power to detect minor-effect loci and the precision of QTL-effect estimates. Phenotyping was conducted using a single P. nicotianae isolate and a 7-day post-inoculation endpoint; therefore, dynamic disease progression, isolate-specific responses, and performance under diverse field environments were not evaluated. In addition, although KASP_qPB10_4 was validated in 40 elite Korean cultivars, its transferability to broader international germplasm and additional breeding populations remains to be established. The candidate genes were also identified primarily from reference genome annotations, without direct analyses of NLR phylogeny, genome collinearity, structural variation, or effector recognition. The resistance locus identified here may not fully capture the genetic architecture of resistance across diverse germplasm or pathogen populations, as resistance genes frequently occur in rapidly evolving clusters whose effects can vary with genetic background and selection history, warranting broader validation and screening for additional resistance loci [41]. Further studies using larger mapping populations, broader genetic backgrounds, multiple pathogen isolates, replicated field trials, and functional characterization of the candidate genes will be required. Within these limitations, KASP_qPB10_4 represents a promising marker for improving selection efficiency in the current Korean sesame breeding framework.

Conclusion

This study identified qPB10 as a major resistance locus associated with a predominantly qualitative, recessively inherited response to P. nicotianae in the Geonbaek × Milsung population. The resistance locus on chromosome 10 was enriched in immune-related genes, and KASP markers developed from this region enabled reliable discrimination between resistant and susceptible lines. These findings highlight the utility of sequence-based marker development for MAS, reducing reliance on conventional labor-intensive disease phenotyping under field or controlled inoculation conditions. Although further validation and functional characterization are required, the markers and genomic resources generated in this study provide a useful foundation for improving Phytophthora blight resistance in sesame breeding.
