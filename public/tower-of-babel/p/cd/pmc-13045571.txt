25-Year Evolution of Apical Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy in a Professional Athlete
Authors: Efstathios Papatheodorou, Vasiliki Vlagkouli, Aris Anastasakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. Risk stratification and disease evolution in athletes who continue competitive sport, particularly in apical phenotypes, remain incompletely defined.Case SummaryA 14-year-old academy football player was diagnosed with subclinical HCM during preparticipation screening and elected to continue competing despite contemporaneous guideline restrictions. Over 25 years of follow-up, he progressed to apical HCM with late gadolinium enhancement and mild ventricular ectopy but maintained excellent functional capacity and remained asymptomatic. After transferring to a lower-tier club, both palpitations and ventricular ectopy decreased. He completed a long professional career and developed an apical aneurysm shortly before retiring.DiscussionThis case highlights the complexity of exercise counseling in HCM and is consistent with contemporary guideline-supported, individualized decision-making, as it illustrates that selected low-risk athletes may tolerate long-term competition. The extended follow-up highlights the long-term natural history of apical HCM in an athlete and underscores persistent uncertainties regarding phenotype-specific risk modifiers, including myocardial fibrosis and apical aneurysm formation.Take-Home MessagePersonalized, shared decision-making is essential in athletes with HCM, and refined risk-stratification models are urgently needed.Key words: cardiomyopathy, echocardiography, ventricular tachycardia

Evolution of the Disease and of the Guidelines

Twenty-five-year clinical course of a professional athlete with HCM showing progression from subclinical disease to apical HCM with fibrosis and late apical aneurysm formation across evolving guideline eras. AHA/ACC = American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology; ESC = European Society of Cardiology; HCM = hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a significant cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in young athletes.1, 2, 3 For decades, all international guidelines did not allow competitive exercise in patients with HCM.4 However, a considerable minority of patients choose to exercise despite these recommendations.5 Most recent international recommendations have adopted a less stringent approach in the sports participation counseling of athletes with HCM.4,6 We present a 25-year follow-up of a competitive soccer player with HCM who completed a sports career at a high level.

History of Presentation

Initial presentation (2001)

A 14-year-old male Caucasian academy football player presented for a preparticipation screening in 2001. He was asymptomatic, and his physical examination was unremarkable. His resting electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with minor inferolateral T-wave inversion. There was normal left ventricular (LV) function and LV wall thickness on his 2-dimensional echocardiogram (Figure 1). There was no family history of SCD or cardiomyopathy. However, on familial screening, his father showed typical features of apical HCM, and consequently we diagnosed the young athlete with subclinical HCM.

He did not have any of the 5 conventional high-risk factors for SCD that were described at the time7: 1) the presence of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT); 2) family history of SCD; 3) abnormal blood pressure response to exercise; 4) LV hypertrophy >30 mm; or 5) syncope. He underwent a cardiopulmonary exercise test where he achieved a peak Vo2 of 52 mL/min/kg (125% of the predicted). No arrhythmias were recorded during exercise or 24-hour ambulatory monitoring. In accordance with contemporary guidelines, he was advised against competitive exercise, but he chose to continue playing and managed to sign a professional contract 3 years later.

Development of an HCM phenotype (2005)

He was kept under close follow-up with annual clinical appointments with ECG, echocardiogram, and ambulatory ECG monitoring. His ECG progressed to show marked repolarization abnormalities a year after initial evaluation. He eventually developed apical LV hypertrophy 3 years later with a maximal LV wall thickness of 15 mm (Figure 2). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) performed in 2015 and 2010 did not show any evidence of myocardial fibrosis.

Follow-Up Investigations (2005)

Electrocardiogram showing extreme left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and giant negative T waves with ST-segment depression in the lateral and inferior leads. Echocardiogram showing apical LV hypertrophy with a maximal wall thickness of 14 to 15 mm.

Follow-up (2005-2017)

He competed at a high and international level and remained completely asymptomatic for 16 years. In 2017, he reported infrequent episodes of palpitations. His resting ECG still showed repolarization abnormalities; however, QRS voltage and T-wave depth decreased (Figure 3). His 2-dimensional echocardiogram showed unchanged LV wall thickness dimensions with preserved LV function and no LV outflow tract obstruction. His 48-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring revealed an increase in the ventricular ectopy burden (0.6% per 24 hours) without episodes of NSVT. He achieved a peak Vo2 of 58 mL/kg/min (141% of the predicted) during cardiopulmonary exercise test. There were a few ventricular ectopic beats during exercise and 2 ventricular couplets during recovery. His CMR showed a new finding of circumferential subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) at the mid-segment to the apical segments, which became almost transmural at the apex with sparing of the apical septum (Figure 4, Videos 1 and 2). His 5-year European Society of Cardiology HCM SCD risk score was calculated as 1.61%. Because of the new findings of mild ventricular ectopy and LGE, there was discussion about terminating his career. He signed a new contract with a lower-tier club, resulting in a reduction in overall training and match exposure, although he continued to compete as a professional athlete. Clinically, he experienced fewer palpitations, and this was paralleled by evidence of reduced ventricular ectopy (<0.1% per 24 hours) and the absence of NSVT in the ambulatory ECG monitor. A repeat CMR in 2021 revealed true apex and lateral apical wall akinesia with transmural LGE in the apical lateral walls. Myocardial fibrosis was quantified as 25% of the total LV mass (Figure 5). Genetic testing using a comprehensive cardiomyopathy gene panel was performed in 2021 and did not identify any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants.

Latest Clinical Evaluation (2025)

In his latest follow-up in 2025, he remains asymptomatic. He just retired from his football career. His ECG has further developed to show low voltage in the lateral leads. In the echocardiogram, there is now evidence of a small dyskinetic area at the apex seen in the apical and subcoastal views consistent with apical aneurysm (Video 3). There is a paucity of ventricular arrhythmias in his ambulatory ECG monitor.

Discussion

Although it is undisputable that HCM is a major cause of SCD in young athletes,1,2,8 it is unclear how and whether exercise affects the natural history of the disease. A small-sized study of athletes with predominantly low risk of SCD who were followed for a mean period of 9 years did not show a difference between patients who continued to exercise and those who stopped.9 On the other hand, a large study in British academy football players revealed that 2 of the 8 SCD observed during a mean follow-up of 11.2 years occurred in patients who were diagnosed with HCM and elected to continue exercising.2 More recently, the large multicenter LIVE-HCM study provided prospective data on exercise participation in HCM, including 1,660 individuals, of whom 259 engaged in competitive vigorous-intensity exercise.5 Vigorous or competitive exercise was not associated with a higher rate of death, resuscitated cardiac arrest, arrhythmic syncope, or appropriate implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy compared with moderate or no exercise. The cohort included a substantial proportion of high-risk patients with traditional risk markers such as NSVT, family history of SCD, and implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation. Phenotype-specific risk modifiers such as extensive LGE were less common in the cohort, and less frequent features such as apical aneurysm were not detailed separately.

Risk stratification in athletes with HCM is challenging. The HCM-SCD risk score has not been validated and should not be used for risk stratification in elite athletes. However, the latest European Society of Cardiology recommendations state that adult athletes with mild clinical expressions of HCM and low HCM-SCD risk score may be allowed to compete.6 In the present case, the initial emergence of apical hypertrophy is consistent with the expected natural history of familial HCM during adolescence. The athlete subsequently developed mild ventricular ectopy and progressive myocardial fibrosis after 16 years of follow-up, at approximately 30 years of age. The significance of these findings remains uncertain, and there is no evidence to suggest that they are related to continued athletic participation, making a causal association with exercise unlikely. In keeping with this, Aengevaeren et al10 did not find any association between lifelong physical activity volume and LGE prevalence or the degree of LV hypertrophy in 102 patients with HCM and gene carriers. There are reports that myocardial fibrosis can be associated with higher risk of SCD, and cardiac fibrosis is a modulating factor in the current HCM risk stratification models but was not considered a marker of high risk in the European Society of Cardiology 2020 sports cardiology guidelines especially if not extensive (≥15% of LV myocardium). He subsequently developed an apical aneurysm, which is now considered a modifying risk factor in patients with HCM. In published cohorts, apical aneurysm formation is most often described in middle-aged or older patients; in our patient, this feature became evident at a relatively young age. Importantly, genetic testing did not identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants that have been linked to increased arrhythmic risk in apical HCM, supporting a relatively lower-risk profile in this case.

A universal approach of disqualification of athletes with low risk (calculated by standard risk scores), adopted for more than 3 decades, has certainly led to the exclusion of athletes who could have potentially trained safely and have had a successful career in sports such as the athlete presented here. In current clinical practice, a substantial proportion of individuals with HCM, including competitive athletes, continue to engage in vigorous exercise as demonstrated in the LIVE-HCM cohort.5 Recent sports participation recommendations4,6 are a step toward personalized risk estimation (Figure 5); include the concept of fully informed decision of the athlete, the physician, and the sports club; and have moved away from a rigid and unconditional approach. However, HCM remains a major cause of SCD in athletes, and the need for better risk stratification models in this group is still unmet.

Take-Home Message

•Personalized, shared decision-making is essential in athletes with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and refined risk-stratification models are urgently needed.
