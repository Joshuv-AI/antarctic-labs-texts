P2Y12 Inhibitor Response By Patient Background Factors: An ExploratoryPost Hoc Subgroup Analysis of the ACUTE-PRAS Study
Authors: Shigeru Fujimoto, Yasuyuki Iguchi, Hiroshi Yamagami, Masatoshi Koga, Ryo Itabashi, Yusuke Yakushiji, Kazuma Kowata, Naoto Kimura, Yuka Terasawa, Takahiro Shimizu, Yuichi Miyazaki, Koichi Oki, Osamu Masuo, Hideki Matsuoka, Shuji Arakawa, Toshihiro Ueda, Ryota Tanaka, Wataru Hashimoto, Satoru Abe, Go Kato, Taketoshi Furugori, Kazumi Kimura, on behalf of the ACUTE-PRAS Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: To explore whether the antiplatelet effects of prasugrel and clopidogrel vary according to patient background factors in the ACUTE-PRAS study. Methods: This was apost hoc, hypothesis-generating, exploratory analysis of the multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled ACUTE-PRAS study, in which 176 patients with acute atherothrombotic stroke or high-risk TIA received prasugrel or clopidogrel within 48 h of symptom onset. High platelet reactivity (HPR; platelet reaction units [PRU] ＞208) and absolute PRU were assessed on Day 5 in subgroups stratified by ABCD-GENE score, age, body mass index (BMI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, time from stroke onset to treatment, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and prior ischemic stroke. Results: Patients with prasugrel had numerically lower rates of HPR than those with clopidogrel in the high-risk stratum of ABCD-GENE score ≥ 10 (OR 2.73,p = 0.076), and favorable trends in prasugrel were also observed for CKD (8.06,p = 0.012), age ＞75 years (5.02,p = 0.025), BMI ＜25 kg/m² (4.61,p = 0.012), dyslipidemia (4.73,p = 0.009), DM (3.86,p = 0.038), treatment initiation ≤ 24 h (3.31,p = 0.010), and NIHSS ≤ 3 (2.77,p = 0.036) or ≥ 4 (9.00,p = 0.025). Prasugrel also reduced PRU numerically more than clopidogrel across most subgroups, except in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, treatment initiation ＞24 hours, or prior ischemic stroke, where only numerical differences were observed. Conclusions: Prasugrel provided favorable early platelet inhibition, particularly in subgroups characterized by advanced age, CKD, low BMI, metabolic comorbidities, or very early treatment start.

Introduction

Stroke recurrence is influenced by stroke subtype and comorbidities 1 - 4) . Large artery atherosclerosis (LAA), especially intracranial stenosis in Asian populations, confers a high risk of recurrent ischemic events 2 , 5 , 6) . Additionally, various clinical and vascular factors have been associated with increased atherosclerotic progression and early stroke recurrence 2 , 7 - 9) .

Given the multifactorial nature of recurrence risk, individualized risk assessment is essential for selecting antithrombotic therapy. The selection of an antiplatelet agent, dosing, and timing should be tailored to patient-related factors (e.g., LAA, comorbidities, prior events) to achieve rapid and consistent platelet inhibition while balancing bleeding risk.

Early dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) using clopidogrel plus aspirin is beneficial in patients with minor ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA, substantially lowering the risk of subsequent stroke 10 , 11) . Prasugrel, an alternative to clopidogrel, exhibits less dependence on CYP2C19 for its activation 12) , making it a potentially more reliable treatment option for patients with acute ischemic events. In Japan, prasugrel is indicated to prevent recurrent strokes in patients with LAA or small vessel occlusion. However, most previous prasugrel studies have focused on stable atherosclerotic disease 13 - 15) , and efficacy and safety data in acute stroke within 7 days of onset remain limited.

The primary analysis of the ACUTE-PRAS study demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in platelet reaction units (PRU) from baseline to Day 5 with prasugrel vs clopidogrel, regardless of CYP2C19 genetic polymorphisms 16) . Furthermore, a subanalysis of ACUTE-PRAS showed that the antiplatelet effects of prasugrel were comparable to clopidogrel across disease types (LAA and high-risk TIA), with evidence suggesting greater efficacy in patients with LAA 17) . Based on these findings, we conducted the present exploratory subanalysis to determine whether the antiplatelet activity of prasugrel is consistent or differs in clinically relevant patient subgroups.

Aim

The aim of this subanalysis was to clarify whether the PRU-lowering effect of prasugrel, as indicated by the primary analysis, remained consistent across various clinical backgrounds, such as differences in age, body mass index (BMI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus (DM), and time to treatment initiation. Specifically, we compared the incidence of high platelet reactivity (HPR) and PRU measured on Day 5 post-antiplatelet therapy initiation (prasugrel or clopidogrel) among subgroups with different clinical characteristics.

Methods

Study Design

This subanalysis is based on ACUTE-PRAS, a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled study 16) . In this subanalysis, data were analyzed in subgroups based on the ABCD-GENE score, which is a clinical tool used to predict HPR by incorporating the following factors: age, BMI, CKD, DM, and presence of CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles, which carries the highest weight in the composite score 18) . A score of ≥ 10 has been reported to have optimal sensitivity and specificity in correlating with HPR and major adverse cardiac events such as ischemic stroke 18) . In addition to the ABCD-GENE score (＜10 and ≥ 10), subgroup analyses by the following individual factors were also conducted: age (≤ 75 and ＞75 years); BMI (＜25 and ≥ 25 kg/m2); CKD based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; ＜60 and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2); DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia diagnosed by an attending physician; time from stroke onset to treatment (≤ 24 and ＞24 hours); National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score (≤ 3 and ≥ 4); and history of ischemic stroke. Although the analyses reported herein were conducted post hoc, these subgroups were selected based on published evidence that these variables may influence response to clopidogrel and prasugrel.

ACUTE-PRAS was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was approved by the ethics committee of the Hattori Clinic Certified Review Board (CRB3180027, April 22, 2022). The study was registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials under the identifier jRCTs031220079 (May 20, 2022). All patients provided written informed consent.

Patients

Patients were eligible if they were aged ≥ 20 years at the time of informed consent; had either ischemic stroke due to LAA per the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification 19) and an NIHSS score ≤ 10 20) , or high-risk TIA (defined as an ABCD2 risk score ≥ 4 or paralysis) at enrollment; had ≥ 1 risk factor (comorbid CKD, DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or a history of stroke prior to the onset of the last ischemic attack); and were able to initiate study treatment within 48 hours of symptom onset 21) . A detailed list of the eligibility criteria has been published previously 16) .

Interventions

Details of the study interventions and randomization have been previously described 16) . An initial oral dose of prasugrel or clopidogrel was administered within 48 hours of stroke symptom onset, followed by continuous treatment for 90 days. In accordance with the Japan Stroke Society treatment guidelines for stroke, patients received DAPT with aspirin during the first 21 days 22) . Patients in the prasugrel group were administered a dose of 3.75 mg or 2.5 mg once daily without the initial loading dose, in accordance with the Japanese package insert 23) . The initial dose (300 mg or 75 mg) and subsequent maintenance dose (75 mg or 50 mg once daily) for patients in the clopidogrel group were left to the discretion of the treating physician. The following concomitant treatments were prohibited: antiplatelet agents other than the study drugs and aspirin, anticoagulant agents (excluding argatroban), thrombolytic agents, omeprazole, rifampicin, and any investigational new drugs.

Outcomes

In the primary study, the primary efficacy endpoint was the PRU on Day 5 after starting the study drug treatment 16) . In the present subanalysis, HPR on Day 5 after starting treatment, as well as PRU at baseline and Day 5 after starting treatment, were analyzed in the patient subgroups by background characteristics. HPR was defined as PRU ＞208 on Day 5 after starting treatment. PRU values were measured using the VerifyNow® P2Y12 assay (Accumetrics Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) in 6- to 8-mL whole-blood samples, and standardized collection procedures were used across all participating facilities 24) .

Statistical Methods

The target sample size for the primary study was 200 patients (100 each in the prasugrel and clopidogrel groups) or the enrollment of at least 14 CYP2C19 poor metabolizers (PMs) in each group 16) . Efficacy endpoints were evaluated in the full analysis set, defined as all patients who received at least one dose of the study drug and for whom at least one post-baseline data point was available.

The statistical methods used in the present subanalysis were the same as those applied in the primary study 16) . In summary, odds ratios (ORs) of non-HPR and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) on Day 5 after starting treatment with prasugrel vs clopidogrel were analyzed in patient subgroups. An OR ＞1 suggested that prasugrel-treated patients were less likely to experience HPR, and an OR ＜1 indicated that clopidogrel-treated patients were less likely to experience HPR. The adjusted mean PRU and its 95% CI, the between-group difference in adjusted mean PRU (prasugrel group minus clopidogrel group) and 95% CI, and exploratory p-values were estimated using a linear mixed model in each patient subgroup. The results of these analyses were interpreted descriptively because of the low reliability of inferential statistics for small sample sizes.

Unless otherwise specified, the significance level used in hypothesis testing was 5% (two-sided), and CIs were calculated at the two-sided 95% level. No adjustment for multiplicity was conducted, as the analyses reported herein were strictly exploratory. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 or later (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

A total of 186 patients were enrolled in the primary study, with 176 included in the full analysis set (88 patients in each treatment group) 16) . In the prasugrel and clopidogrel groups, respectively, the mean±standard deviation (SD) age was 70.4±10.8 and 71.1±11.3 years; mean±SD BMI, 24.54±4.15 and 23.60±3.24 kg/m2; proportion of patients with CKD, 36.4% and 35.2%; proportion of patients with DM, 44.3% and 42.0%; proportion of patients with hypertension, 85.2% and 84.1%; proportion of patients with dyslipidemia, both 76.1%; proportion of patients with time from stroke onset to treatment ≤ 24 hours, 78.4% and 79.5%; median (interquartile range) NIHSS score, 2.0 (0.0, 3.5) and 1.0 (0.0, 3.0); and the proportion of patients with history of ischemic stroke, 15.9% and 5.7% 16) . Generally, background characteristics were similar between the prasugrel and clopidogrel groups.

Prasugrel-treated patients with CKD (eGFR ＜60 mL/min/1.73 m2) appeared to have a numerically lower risk of HPR than clopidogrel-treated patients (8.06 [1.58, 41.04], p = 0.012), but the risk was not different in those without CKD ( Fig.1 ). Prasugrel was also associated with a numerically lower risk of HPR compared with clopidogrel regardless of stroke severity (NIHSS score ≤ 3: OR, 2.77 [1.07, 7.19], p = 0.036; and NIHSS score ≥ 4: OR, 9.00 [1.32, 61.13], p = 0.025). Similarly, the following factors were also associated with numerically lower HPR risk in patients treated with prasugrel vs clopidogrel: age ＞75 years, BMI ＜25 kg/m2, presence of DM or dyslipidemia, ≤ 24 hours from stroke onset to treatment, and no prior ischemic stroke. The OR of HPR numerically favored prasugrel in both ABCD-GENE subgroups (score ＜10: OR = 3.33 [0.82, 13.60], p = 0.093; score ≥ 10: OR = 2.73 [0.90, 8.30], p = 0.076), but the exploratory p-values did not suggest a significant difference. Importantly, there was no subgroup in which prasugrel-treated patients appeared to be more likely to experience HPR than clopidogrel-treated patients.

PRU at Baseline and Day 5 Post-Treatment Initiation in Patient Subgroups

PRU at baseline and Day 5 after treatment initiation in patient subgroups are shown in Fig.2. Prasugrel numerically reduced PRU compared with clopidogrel regardless of the ABCD-GENE score: adjusted mean difference −24.4 (95% CI, −45.1, −3.7), p = 0.021 for ABCD-GENE score ＜10 and −38.3 (−60.5, −16.1), p＜0.001 for ABCD-GENE score ≥ 10 ( Fig.2A and 2B ). Prasugrel numerically reduced PRU compared with clopidogrel regardless of age: adjusted mean difference for age ≤ 75 years, −28.0 (95% CI −47.4, −8.7), p = 0.005 and for ＞75 years, −42.0 (−67.7, −16.4), p = 0.001 ( Fig.2C and 2D ). Prasugrel numerically reduced PRU compared with clopidogrel in individuals with BMI ＜25 kg/m2: adjusted mean difference, −35.9 (95% CI −55.7, −16.1), p＜0.001, but not in those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (−23.4 [−48.9, 2.1], p = 0.072) ( Fig.2E and 2F ). Likewise, adjusted mean differences (prasugrel vs clopidogrel) indicated substantial reductions in PRU for individuals without and with CKD −22.9 [−42.3, −3.5], p = 0.021 for eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and (−50.3 [95% CI −75.5, −25.1], p＜0.001 for eGFR ＜60 mL/min/1.73 m2 ( Fig.2G and 2H ). Similar results were found for DM status: −28.0 (95% CI −48.5, −7.4), p = 0.008 (without DM) and −39.9 (−63.5, −16.3), p = 0.001 (with DM) ( Fig.2I and 2J ).

The apparent advantage persisted regardless of hypertension status: −53.9 (95% CI −96.6, −11.2), p = 0.014 (without hypertension) and −29.8 (−46.4, −13.2), p＜0.001 (with hypertension) ( Table 1 and Supplemental Fig.1A and 1B ) and dyslipidemia status: −38.0 (−68.6, −7.4), p = 0.015 (without dyslipidemia) and −32.8 (−50.0, −15.6), p＜0.001 (with dyslipidemia) ( Table 1 and Supplemental Fig.1C and 1D ). Among those treated within 24 hours of stroke onset, the PRU-lowering effect of prasugrel appeared to be favorable over that of clopidogrel: adjusted mean difference, −34.9 (95% CI −51.9, −18.0), p＜0.001, whereas no apparent difference emerged for treatment initiated after 24 hours (−29.6 [−64.8, 5.6], p = 0.099) ( Table 1 and Supplemental Fig.1E and 1F ). Prasugrel consistently reduced PRU numerically more than clopidogrel for both mild stroke (NIHSS score ≤ 3, adjusted mean difference, −32.6 [95% CI −49.9, −15.3], p＜0.001) and moderate stroke (NIHSS score ≥ 4, −40.2 [−79.2, −1.1], p = 0.044) ( Table 1 and Supplemental Fig.1G and 1H ). Finally, prasugrel was numerically more effective than clopidogrel in patients without a history of ischemic stroke (adjusted mean difference, −34.9 [95% CI −51.5, −18.3], p＜0.001), but not in those with a prior ischemic stroke (−15.4 [−66.1, 35.2], p = 0.548) ( Table 1 and Supplemental Fig.1I and 1J ).

Discussion

This exploratory subanalysis of the ACUTE-PRAS study compared the pharmacodynamic response to prasugrel versus clopidogrel at 5 days post-treatment initiation in 176 patients with acute atherothrombotic stroke or high-risk TIA. Given that this was a post hoc analysis, the findings are limited to hypothesis generation, and any conclusions require confirmation in prospective, validated clinical outcome studies. Overall, prasugrel elicited numerically greater reductions in PRU and a lower frequency of HPR than clopidogrel; however, because no formal interaction test was performed, comparisons of the magnitude of treatment effect cannot be made. Nevertheless, the direction of benefit was uniform across the main clinical subgroups we examined, including ABCD-GENE score, age, CKD, DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Absolute differences tended to be greater in older patients and in those with CKD or DM, who have previously been reported to be prone to clopidogrel hyporesponsiveness 7 , 25 , 26) . In our study, larger differences were observed among patients who initiated treatment early (≤ 24 hours from stroke onset to treatment). These data suggest that prasugrel provides rapid and consistent platelet inhibition across a broad patient population, supporting its potential role as an alternative to clopidogrel in acute cerebrovascular event settings.

Rapid and sufficient PRU reduction is important in patients with LAA and high-risk TIA, including patients with CKD. Therefore, identifying patient characteristics that predict significant benefit from prasugrel is of major clinical relevance. Personalized treatment approaches that incorporate the use of prasugrel may be particularly advantageous in high-risk groups. In this subanalysis, prasugrel showed numerically lower PRU/HPR rates in some high-risk subgroups, especially those with elevated ABCD-GENE score or CKD. These results align with previous work examining P2Y12 inhibitor responsiveness in high-risk populations 27 - 30) . Our findings suggest that there may be a benefit to using prasugrel in highly vulnerable individuals based on factors like ABCD-GENE score and kidney function, as well as patients with LAA or TIA who might otherwise show inadequate response to conventional therapies.

In conditions where platelet activity is prone to increase (such as CKD, DM, and LAA), prasugrel, a potent P2Y12 inhibitor, demonstrated consistent therapeutic effects. Initiating prasugrel at an early stage of acute stroke may augment its prophylactic benefit. In individuals aged ≥ 75 years, increased platelet reactivity and vascular inflammation may predispose such patients to recurrent events 31) . The previous ACUTE-PRAS subanalysis 17) and the present study both suggest that prasugrel’s PRU-lowering effect intensifies when multiple risk factors are present. Conditions like CKD, DM, and hypertension frequently co-occur in older patients and may affect clopidogrel’s efficacy 32) . Furthermore, prasugrel’s pharmacodynamic profile appears relatively stable in mild to moderate kidney dysfunction 33) , whereas clopidogrel’s efficacy may decline with CKD 34) . Our data suggest that patients with an eGFR ＜60 mL/min/1.73 m2 on prasugrel may be able to achieve both lower PRU and lower HPR risk compared with clopidogrel.

In individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, the efficacy of prasugrel, both in terms of PRU reduction and HPR lowering, appeared to be similar to that of clopidogrel. Differences in body composition or metabolic changes may affect plasma levels of antiplatelet drugs, and dose adjustments or combination therapy may be needed in obese patients. As obesity often coexists with other comorbidities, careful consideration of bleeding risk is warranted. This study had few individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, so larger or dedicated analyses are needed to clarify the efficacy of prasugrel in this population.

Previous studies (CHANCE 10) and POINT 35) ) demonstrated the clinical benefit of clopidogrel-based DAPT in patients with minor ischemic stroke (NIHSS score of ≤ 3), using recurrent stroke as the primary outcome. By contrast, our analysis enrolled patients with an NIHSS score up to 10 and relied on Day 5 PRU as a pharmacodynamic surrogate rather than on clinical events. These methodological differences preclude direct comparisons. Nevertheless, two observations are noteworthy: (i) prasugrel achieved rapid and consistent platelet inhibition, even in patients with moderate stroke (NIHSS 4–10), and (ii) when therapy was initiated within 24 hours of symptom onset, the magnitude of PRU reduction with prasugrel matched or numerically exceeded that observed with clopidogrel. Whether this enhanced pharmacodynamic profile translates into fewer ischemic events in moderate stroke remains unknown and will require confirmation in adequately powered outcome-driven trials that include rigorous safety assessments.

Regardless of baseline ABCD-GENE score (＜10 and ≥ 10), prasugrel elicited a numerical reduction in PRU compared with clopidogrel, and the corresponding ORs for HPR were all in the direction of benefit for prasugrel. Although these differences cannot be judged as statistically significant in the context of this exploratory study, the consistent trend across strata suggests a potential advantage of prasugrel in patients with higher genetic or clinical risk of clopidogrel hypo-responsiveness. One possible factor that could influence the determination of a significant effect is that HPR was defined dichotomously as PRU ＞208; this cutoff can mask moderate but clinically relevant shifts in the overall PRU distribution. Additionally, the sample sizes for each category of the ABCD-GENE score may not have been sufficient to clearly capture the proportion of patients falling below (or above) the HPR threshold. Therefore, while the overall reduction in PRU appeared to be stronger with prasugrel compared with clopidogrel, a larger study population or more focused analysis of distribution patterns in a study including a prespecified null hypothesis is required to definitively demonstrate a significant reduction in HPR incidence. In addition, the applicability of the ABCD-GENE score to Japanese populations remains uncertain. The score was derived predominantly from Western cohorts that include a larger proportion of individuals with obesity (BMI ＞30 kg/m²), a characteristic that is relatively uncommon in Japan. Moreover, racial differences in CYP2C19 allele frequencies may also be a confounding factor, especially considering the disproportionate weighting of CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles in calculating the ABCD-GENE score 18) . Consequently, some components of the score, particularly the BMI variable, may have limited discriminatory power in our study population, potentially diluting its ability to stratify patients accurately according to clopidogrel responsiveness. Further validation of the ABCD-GENE score, or development of an ethnicity-specific modification, is therefore warranted before it can be used with confidence in Japanese patients.

Caution is needed in the application of antiplatelet therapy in real-world settings, as older patients or those with multiple comorbidities may have an inherently higher bleeding risk, requiring close monitoring in clinical practice. In the previous ACUTE-PRAS subanalysis 17) , the incidences of adverse events and adverse drug reactions within 90 days after starting treatment were similar between the two treatment groups (adverse events: 50.0% and 40.9%, and adverse drug reactions: 9.1% and 6.8%, in the prasugrel and clopidogrel groups, respectively). In the clopidogrel group, the cumulative incidence of International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis major bleeding was 2.7% and that of Management of Atherothrombosis with Clopidogrel in High-risk Patients (MATCH) life-threatening bleeding was also 2.7%. There were no severe bleeding events in the prasugrel group. The incidence of MATCH clinically significant bleeding was the same in both treatment groups (both 2.5%) 17) .

PRU is a surrogate marker; whether early pharmacodynamic superiority translates into fewer ischemic events without excess bleeding remains to be proven in adequately powered clinical outcome trials. Nevertheless, in the TRITON-TIMI 38 trial, the prasugrel group exhibited significantly lower PRU values compared with the clopidogrel group, which was associated with a 19% relative reduction in the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke (p = 0.0004) 36) . Furthermore, subgroup analyses suggested that greater reductions in PRU were associated with more pronounced suppression of ischemic events. Additionally, the CHANCE-2 trial 37) demonstrated that in patients with CYP2C19 genetic polymorphisms, the more potent P2Y12 inhibitor ticagrelor was more effective than clopidogrel. Prasugrel and ticagrelor have been reported to reduce PRU by approximately 30–40 units, and a PRU reduction of 30–40 units that remains within the established optimal therapeutic window (typically 85–208 units) can be considered clinically meaningful from a risk–benefit perspective.

Regarding clinical implications, the rapid and consistent platelet inhibition achieved with prasugrel across multiple high-risk strata suggests that prasugrel may serve as a practical alternative to clopidogrel for acute secondary prevention in patients with atherothrombotic stroke or high-risk TIA, particularly in cases with advanced age, CKD, low BMI, metabolic comorbidities, or very early treatment initiation (≤ 24 h).

The present study has some limitations. First, although the primary ACUTE-PRAS trial achieved its overall target enrollment 16) , this exploratory subanalysis was not prospectively powered for subgroup effects. Consequently, some strata contained very few patients, limiting the ability to determine clinically meaningful differences in PRU or HPR, and increasing the likelihood that nominally significant findings, especially those with marginal p-values, reflect chance. Second, PRU data were analyzed with linear mixed models that assume approximate normality, yet formal tests of distributional adequacy were not performed; deviation from this assumption could bias effect estimates in small groups. Third, we conducted numerous subgroup comparisons without adjustment for multiplicity, so the overall type-I error rate is inflated, and positive results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Fourth, pharmacodynamic follow-up ended at Day 5; therefore, durability of platelet inhibition and its relation to clinical outcomes remain unknown. Finally, participants were exclusively Japanese, which may limit generalizability to other ethnicities with different genetic backgrounds, risk profiles, and dosing regimens. In particular, the applicability of the ABCD-GENE tool, which incorporates BMI and CYP2C19 loss-of-function allele data into the score calculation, has not been confirmed in exclusively Japanese populations. A sensitivity analysis (e.g., excluding the BMI component), may have provided more reliable results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results suggest that prasugrel may elicit more consistent PRU reduction by Day 5 compared with clopidogrel in patients with acute atherothrombotic stroke or high-risk TIA, even in high-risk groups characterized by advanced age, CKD, or DM. However, questions remain regarding its efficacy in obese individuals, optimal bleeding risk assessment, and long-term outcomes. Additional investigations using larger, adequately powered and longer-term prospective study designs are needed to confirm our findings. Validated clinical outcome studies that build on our exploratory results are warranted.
