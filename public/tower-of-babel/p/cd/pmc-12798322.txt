Radiomorphometric indices and bone findings on panoramic images of childhood cancer survivors
Authors: Ana Maria Ipólito Barros, Ana Paula Veras Sobral, Luiz Pedro Mendes de Azevedo, Cleomar Donizeth Rodrigues, Híttalo Carlos Rodrigues de Almeida, Márcia Maria Fonseca da Silveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to assess radiomorphometric indices in panoramic radiographs of pediatric cancer survivors.Materials and MethodsThis population-based case-control study included 31 patients who were 18 years or older at the time of the radiograph and had a history of cancer diagnosed during childhood or adolescence (between 0 and 19 years of age), treated with chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. The radiomorphometric indices mandibular cortical width (MCW) and mandibular cortical index (MCI) were assessed in panoramic radiographs to determine whether there was evidence of a reduction in bone mineral density (BMD). A 95% confidence level (P<0.05) was used for all statistical tests.ResultsIn the MCW analysis, the case group showed a variation between 2.2 mm and 4.6 mm, while the control group showed a variation between 2.9 mm and 5.2 mm (P<0.05). For MCI, most (54.8%) of the images in the case group were classified as C2, and 16.1% as C3, whereas none in the control group were classified as C3, demonstrating a statistically significant difference (P<0. 05).ConclusionChildhood cancer survivors are at an increased risk of long-term reduction in BMD, and panoramic radiomorphometric indices represent an accessible and reliable screening tool for predicting this risk.Keywords: Bone Density; Cancer Survivors; Child; Radiography, Dental; Radiography, Panoramic

Introduction

Childhood cancer is the leading cause of disease-related death among patients aged 0 to 19 years in Brazil,1 with leukemia, central nervous system tumors, and lymphoma being the most common neoplasms.2 It is estimated that, at present, up to 80% of children with cancer can be cured, given the advances in available treatment modalities.3,4,5 However, cancer survival is frequently associated with several late effects related to treatment administered at an early age, resulting in chronic physical and psychological complications that persist or emerge 5 years after diagnosis.6,7

The skeletal system is among the most affected sites by late treatment effects in cured cancer patients, compounded by nutritional deficiencies, corticosteroid use, and the malignancy itself.8,9 These patients may develop alterations in bone metabolism and face an increased risk of bone mineral density (BMD) deficiency and, consequently, bone fractures.10,11,12 The gold-standard test for evaluating and diagnosing BMD is dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). However, the high cost limits its regular application.13

Quantitative indices such as mandibular cortical width (MCW) in the mental foramen region, and qualitative indices such as the mandibular cortical index (MCI), which assesses cortical erosion in the region between the mental foramen and the third molar, are radiomorphometric parameters used in panoramic radiographs. These indices may indicate alterations in mandibular bone and contribute to identifying patients with undiagnosed low bone density.14

Despite substantial advances in survival rates for childhood and adolescent cancer patients, the long-term assessment of bone health remains a significant clinical challenge. Although DXA is the gold standard for diagnosing BMD, its inherent limitations—including high cost and radiation exposure—make its routine, widespread use unfeasible as a screening method in this vulnerable population.13

As a result, a critical gap persists in clinical practice. There is a pressing need for accessible and reliable screening tools to promptly identify childhood cancer survivors at risk of reduced BMD. MCW and MCI obtained from panoramic radiographs have demonstrated correlation with bone density and show potential for identifying patients with undiagnosed low bone density.15,16 However, their specific application and systematic validation as a noninvasive, low-cost screening tool for predicting BMD alterations in childhood cancer survivors—a population with distinct and increasing osteometabolic risks—have not yet been comprehensively investigated.

Therefore, this study sought to address this gap by evaluating the usefulness of radiomorphometric indices in panoramic radiographs of cured pediatric cancer patients. The objective was to propose an innovative approach for the early detection of long-term bone health risks, thereby contributing to improved monitoring and management in this patient population.

Materials and Methods

The research protocol was submitted to and approved by the Ethics Committee (number: 205/06). This retrospective case-control study analyzed radiographic findings in children and adolescents diagnosed with cancer and treated with various therapeutic protocols early in life, in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement.

A total of 53 patients of both sexes, aged 0 to 19 years, had been diagnosed with a malignant neoplasm during childhood or adolescence and treated with antineoplastic therapy, including chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, with or without surgical resection, and subsequently cured.

Patients were evaluated at a single time point, with a minimum follow-up of 5 years after completion of treatment. The control group was selected from medical records at a private radiology clinic in the same region as the case group. Controls were chosen through simple random sampling, with an estimated case-to-control ratio of 1 : 1, and matched by sex and age according to eligibility criteria.

The inclusion criteria for the case group were as follows: cured patients of both sexes who underwent chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, with or without surgical treatment, during pediatric age; had survived for at least 5 years; and were in their second decade of life at the time of panoramic radiograph acquisition. The control group consisted of normoreactive patients of both sexes, in their second decade of life or older, who had not undergone medical treatment that could interfere with the study, were not on continuous-use medication, did not have metabolic bone disease affecting BMD, and did not present with pathological bone alterations. Exclusion criteria for the case group included patients treated exclusively with surgery and those lacking a posttreatment panoramic radiograph. For the control group, normoreactive patients with panoramic radiographs who could not be matched to the case group were excluded. Clinical data collected from medical records included age at radiograph and diagnosis, sex, malignant neoplasm diagnosis, anatomic site, staging, and treatment type.

Images from the case group were acquired using the same X-ray unit, Rotograph Plus (Vila System, Buccinasco, MI, Italy). Exposure parameters were 65–75 kVp, depending on the patient, and 10 mA. After acquisition, the radiographs were digitized using the CamScanner app (INTSIG Information, Shanghai, China) and stored in JPEG format. Control group images were obtained as digital panoramic radiographs on the ORTHOPHOS XG 3D ready/Ceph unit (Dent sply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany), using exposure settings of 65–75 kVp, depending on the patient, and 10 mA, and were saved in JPEG format after processing.

Panoramic radiographs were evaluated and measured by a trained observer in a predetermined environment under dim lighting, using a computer with Microsoft Windows 7 Professional, Intel Celeron processor, 4 GB RAM (Intel Corporation, USA), and a 14-inch LED screen with HD resolution (1024×768 pixels). The observer was trained by a radiologist with over 20 years of experience. In cases of uncertainty, 2 additional radiologists, each with more than 10 years of experience, were consulted independently.

To ensure validity and consistency in radiomorphometric measurements and manual image assessments, observer reliability was analyzed. Intraobserver agreement was assessed by having the primary observer repeat MCW and MCI measurements in 20% of the sample after a 2-week interval. Agreement was evaluated using the Cohen kappa coefficient for categorical data (MCI) and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous data (MCW). Results indicated excellent reliability, with a Kappa value of 0.88 for MCI and an ICC of 0.91 for MCW. Because of the retrospective study design and single-observer assessment, interobserver reliability was not tested. However, cases of classification uncertainty were reviewed by consensus with 2 experienced radiologists, reducing the likelihood of classification bias. Together with the excellent intraobserver reliability, this approach supports the consistency of the data presented.

Panoramic radiographs from the case group were measured using ImageJ software version 1.4.3.67 (US National Institutes of Health, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) with an average magnification correction of 19%. Control group radiographs were measured using RadioLaudo software, which automatically applied a 17% magnification correction. The differences in magnification correction reflected the specifications of each X-ray machine.

The mental foramina were identified bilaterally, and parallel lines were drawn along the mandibular inferior and superior cortices. From the midpoint of each mental foramen, a perpendicular line was drawn intersecting the parallel lines (Fig. 1). MCW was defined as the distance between the parallel lines delineating the mandibular cortices. Bilateral measurements were obtained, and the mean value was calculated for each patient.17

MCI was classified according to the method proposed by Klemetti et al.16 This qualitative index assesses mandibular cortex bone quality in the region between the mental foramen and the third molar. Classification was bilateral, and the side with the greatest degree of erosion determined the patient’s MCI category. Patients were classified as C1, C2, or C3 (Fig. 2). C1: mandibular cortical bone without erosion, with an even, sharp endosteal margin bilaterally. C2: mandibular cortical bone showing superficial erosions, with the endosteal margin presenting semilunar defects on one or both sides. C3: mandibular cortical bone clearly porous, with numerous erosions compromising more than 2-thirds of the cortical thickness.

Panoramic radiographs of patients cured of cancer classified according to the mandibular cortical index (MCI) in the region between the mental foramen and the third molar. A. C1. B. C2. C. C3.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics for both groups included absolute and relative frequencies, measures of central tendency (mean and median), and standard deviation. Associations were tested using the chi-square test and Fisher exact test. Quantitative variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using the Student t-test, and 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons across more than 2 groups. When ANOVA indicated statistical significance, the Tukey honest significant difference post hoc test identified intergroup differences. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. A 95% confidence level was adopted, and P-values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In the analysis of age, sex, and MCI (Table 3), no statistically significant difference was observed between sexes (P>0.05), indicating an equal distribution of men and women across the groups. Regarding age at radiograph, the control group had a mean age of 23.87±4.02 years, comparable to that of the case group (23.54±3.71 years), with no statistically significant difference (P>0.05). However, MCI analysis revealed a statistically significant difference between the groups (P<0.05). Specifically, in the case group, most images (54.8%) were classified as C2, and 16.1% as C3, whereas none in the control group were classified as C3. This significant finding suggests that childhood cancer survivors are at an increased risk of reduced bone mineral density, as indicated by qualitative changes in mandibular cortical bone. Within the case group, analysis of MCI by cancer staging, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and sex showed no statistically significant differences (P>0.05 for all; Table 4).

As shown in Table 5, there was a statistically significant difference in MCW between the groups (P<0.05). The case group exhibited a mean MCW of 3.15±0.58 mm, while the control group had a mean MCW of 4.00±0.58 mm. This significant difference, with the case group showing a thinner mandibular cortex, indicates that childhood cancer survivors are at higher risk of reduced bone mineral density and osteoporosis compared with controls. Discussion

Cancer remains the leading cause of disease-related death among children and adolescents.4 The patients included in this study were considered cured, having survived between 5 and 15 years after completing treatment. Long-term survivors of childhood cancer represent a vulnerable population exposed to potential late effects of treatment, which compromise quality of life.18,19,20 These effects may affect multiple organ systems, with the most frequent complications involving endocrine, metabolic, and skeletal functions.21,22 Because approximately 60% of maximum BMD is acquired during childhood and adolescence, disorders directly or indirectly related to cancer treatment may prevent the attainment of an optimal peak bone mass.21,22

Monitoring bone health in childhood cancer survivors has been widely discussed and recommended.18,22 In this study, the lack of detailed information on cumulative doses and duration of chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens was an inherent limitation due to its retrospective design. Such heterogeneity in treatment protocols is a common feature of long-term studies involving pediatric cancer survivors and reflects the evolving therapeutic approaches over the decades in which patients were treated. Despite this variability, the present study’s findings for MCW and MCI within the case group, when analyzed according to treatment type (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery), revealed no statistically significant differences (P>0.05 for all). This suggests that the impact of cancer and its treatment on long-term bone morphology may be a broader systemic effect that transcends specific therapeutic modalities, thereby justifying analysis of the survivor group as a whole for BMD screening.

Although DXA remains the most widely recommended examination for evaluating BMD, its routine use is limited by high cost and exposure to radiation.18,24 By contrast, radiomorphometric indices derived from panoramic radiographs offer a lower-cost and routinely performed alternative that can predict low BMD and indicate the need for more detailed investigation.24,25

Mandibular cortical indices were developed to quantify and assess mandibular bone mass by detecting signs of cortical resorption in panoramic radiographs, thereby aiding in the identification of osteopenia.26 This study utilized 2 such indices, MCI and MCW, both of which have demonstrated moderate to high diagnostic accuracy in previous studies when compared to DXA-based diagnoses of low BMD.24,27,28,29 MCI, a qualitative index established by Klemetti et al.,16 assesses the degree of cortical resorption in the mandibular basal bone, while MCW is a quantitative measure of cortical thickness and has been correlated with BMD in the lumbar spine and proximal femur. Marandi et al.30 found statistically significant results for both MCI and MCW when comparing panoramic radiograph indices with DXA. Similarly, Mansour et al.25 reported positive correlations between lumbar spine t-scores and both MCW and MCI. Other studies have suggested that MCI may demonstrate superior predictive value for osteopenia compared with other indices.27,28

The present study’s findings regarding MCI indicate that childhood cancer survivors who underwent antineoplastic treatment are at increased risk of presenting with low BMD compared to controls. The observed prevalence of C3 cases in the case group aligns with findings by Yalcin et al.27 in other at-risk populations. The degree of bone porosity associated with the C3 classification supports its potential relationship with osteopenia and osteoporosis.29,30 Likewise, the lower MCW values observed in the case group suggest a heightened risk of reduced BMD and osteoporosis, consistent with previous studies that established MCW thresholds (3.0–3.5 mm, or below 3 mm) as indicators of osteoporosis risk.31,32,33 These conclusions are consistent with those reported by Balto et al.,34 who found positive associations between MCW and altered bone mineralization in postmenopausal women.

This study found no statistically significant differences within the case group regarding primary tumor type, anatomical site, or treatment type in relation to radiomorphometric variables. This supports the notion that both malignancy and treatment during childhood exert systemic effects on bone morphology, irrespective of specific clinical variables. Nonetheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size.

One important limitation of this study lies in the heterogeneity of imaging acquisition protocols between the case and control groups. While control group images were obtained digitally, case group radiographs were originally printed and later scanned using a mobile application (Cam Scanner), which may have affected image quality, resolution, and sharpness. Furthermore, radiographs were acquired using different machines, each with distinct magnification factors (corrected by 19% for the case group and 17% for the control group). Although calibration and magnification adjustments were applied, methodological differences may still have introduced measurement bias.

Furthermore, the use of different software tools (ImageJ and RadioLaudo) for image analysis across groups may have influenced measurement consistency. These factors could have affected the direct comparability of radiomorphometric indices between groups. Future studies should employ standardized imaging protocols and analysis methods to minimize variability and improve reproducibility and accuracy.

Another limitation was the heterogeneity of the case group itself. The cohort included a wide range of cancer types (hematologic and solid tumors), treatment modalities (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and/or surgery), and ages at diagnosis. These variables may have influenced bone metabolism differently and thereby affected radiomorphometric indices. However, the relatively small sample size precluded subgroup analyses to explore these effects in greater depth.

This study included both hematologic and solid tumors because the focus was on evaluating radiomorphometric indices. While this broadened the scope of the analysis, it also represented a limitation, as treatment characteristics vary by tumor type. It is likely that separate analyses by cancer type would yield different results. Even so, the childhood cancer survivors in this sample demonstrated an overall increased risk of low BMD.

The exploration of specific subgroups defined by detailed treatment regimens (e.g., chemotherapy type, cumulative dose, and radiotherapy field) would help clarify their individual effects on BMD. The retrospective design and sample size of this study precluded such analysis. Nevertheless, the identification of increased risk for low BMD among childhood cancer survivors, regardless of tumor type or treatment regimen, underscores the importance of developing accessible screening strategies for this vulnerable population.

In conclusion, the assessment of BMD in childhood cancer survivors is essential for monitoring bone growth and development. Radiomorphometric indices from panoramic radiographs represent a practical and reliable screening tool for oncopediatric patients. Survivors treated with chemotherapy or radiotherapy require pediatric dental follow-up as a preventive measure. Such follow-up can improve dental care for this population and support the multidisciplinary team dedicated to optimizing their quality of life.
