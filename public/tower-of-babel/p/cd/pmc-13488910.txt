Systemic proportional pulse index is associated with incident probable dementia: a post-hoc analysis of the SPRINT-MIND trial in the United States
Authors: Sula Mazimba, Newton Nyirenda, Olayiwola Bolaji, Nicholas Ashur, Patrick Stafford, Joseph Phiri, Jane Chanda Kabwe, Younghoon Kwon, Laprincess C Brewer, Belinda Chihota, Mwenya Mubanga, Selma F Mohammed, Khadijah Breathett, Allana T Forde, Manjunathan Raju, Agrawal Akanksha, Andrija Vidic, Nishaki Mehta, Emily Lin, Kenneth C Bilchick, Deana Saylor, Amber Johnson, Daniel Addison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Pulse pressure (PP) is associated with cognitive decline but does not account for inter-individual differences in body size that may influence vascular load. We evaluated whether the systemic proportional pulse index (SPI), a body size–indexed pulsatile measure, is associated with incident probable dementia in the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial Memory and Cognition in Decreased Hypertension (SPRINT-MIND).Methods:We conducted a post-hoc analysis of SPRINT-MIND participants with complete baseline blood pressure and anthropometric data. SPI was calculated as PP indexed to the body surface area. The primary outcome was incident probable dementia adjudicated using standardized SPRINT-MIND protocols. Cox proportional hazards models evaluated associations between SPI and dementia after adjustment for demographic and clinical covariates. Incremental prognostic value was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and Akaike Information Criterion.Results:Among 6,070 participants (mean age, 63.2±6.0 years; 65.7% male), 110 (1.8%) developed probable dementia during follow-up. Higher SPI was independently associated with increased dementia risk in models adjusting for PP and body surface area (p=0.031). Participants in the highest SPI quartile had greater risk compared with the lowest quartile (hazard ratio, 2.36; 95% confidence interval, 1.27–4.39). Addition of SPI modestly improved model discrimination and fit beyond PP alone.Conclusions:SPI is independently associated with incident probable dementia and provides modest incremental prognostic information beyond standard clinical factors and PP, suggesting that incorporating body size into assessment of pulsatile load may refine vascular risk stratification for cognitive outcomes, pending external validation.Keywords: Blood pressure, Body surface area, Cognitive dysfunction, Cognition, Cardiovascular diseases, Prognosis

Introduction

With advancing age, the high prevalence of neurocognitive decline has become a major health concern. Cognitive decline contributes to increased morbidity, mortality, and significant health care cost. The prevalence of dementia is expected to triple by 2050 from an estimated global prevalence of 50 million cases in 2020 [1]. Mild cognitive impairment is a precursor to dementia and is associated with arterial stiffness of the central arteries, which is closely correlated with systolic hypertension. Vascular factors are the second leading cause of cognitive decline and dementia after degenerative causes [2–5].

Pulse pressure (PP), defined as the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure has been associated with dementia [6,7]. The mechanisms by which PP could lead to cognitive decline are varied, ranging from metabolic alterations, tissue injury, cellular oxidation, and inflammatory injury to cellular senescence leading to structural remodeling and white matter rarefaction. Central to the pathophysiology of vascular-mediated cognitive decline is the role PP plays in initiating and sustaining injury of the blood brain barrier [5]. With advancing age, central arterial stiffness results in proportional elevation in systolic blood pressure relative to diastolic blood pressure.

However, PP does not account for inter-individual differences in body size that may influence the effective hemodynamic load transmitted to the microcirculation, and prior studies have demonstrated that blood pressure indexed to body surface area (BSA) differs across individuals, with higher relative vascular load observed in smaller-bodied individuals and in women. To address this limitation, we defined the systemic proportional pulse index (SPI) as PP indexed to BSA, conceptualized as a scaling index reflecting pulsatile load relative to body size rather than a direct biomechanical parameter. To our knowledge, the association between SPI and incident probable dementia has not been well characterized in hypertensive adults. Leveraging adjudicated cognitive outcomes from Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial Memory and Cognition in Decreased Hypertension (SPRINT-MIND), we evaluated whether SPI is independently associated with incident probable dementia and whether it provides incremental prognostic utility beyond PP and standard clinical covariates. We hypothesized that higher SPI would be associated with increased risk of incident probable dementia and may provide incremental prognostic information beyond PP alone. Our conceptual framework was based on the hypothesis that pulsatile hemodynamic load relative to body size may better capture inter-individual differences in vascular stress.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study utilized data from the SPRINT-MIND trial (NCT01206062), a multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted at 102 North American sites between 2010 and 2015 [8]. The data is publicly available from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute BioLINCC data repository, which can be accessed at https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home/.

SPI calculation

Baseline PP was calculated as the difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressure (PP = SBP–DBP). BSA was estimated using the Mosteller formula: BSA = √[(height (cm) × weight (kg))/3,600] [9].

SPI was defined as PP indexed to body size: SPI = PP/BSA. SPI was treated as a composite scaling measure reflecting pulsatile load relative to body size. SPI was analyzed as a continuous variable and in quartiles (Q1–Q4) [10].

Cognitive assessment

The methods for cognitive ascertainment have been previously published in the original SPRINT-MIND study [8]. Briefly, participants completed a Cognitive Screening Battery at baseline and follow-up, and those scoring below prespecified thresholds underwent further evaluation using the Extended Cognitive Assessment Battery [8]. The primary outcome for the present analysis was incident probable dementia, adjudicated using standardized SPRINT-MIND procedures.

Covariates

Prespecified covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included age (years), sex, race/ethnicity (Black, White, Hispanic, or Other), smoking status (never, former, current), chronic kidney disease (CKD), history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and subclinical CVD. PP and BSA were additionally included in selected models to evaluate the independent and incremental contribution of SPI beyond its individual components. CKD was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline. CVD history reflected prior clinical cardiovascular events documented at baseline. Subclinical CVD was defined as objective evidence of cardiovascular pathology in the absence of prior clinical events, consistent with SPRINT definitions (e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy on electrocardiography or abnormal ankle–brachial index).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared across SPI quartiles using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for dementia-free survival across SPI quartiles, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between SPI and incident probable dementia. SPI was modeled both as a continuous variable and in quartiles (Q1–Q4). All primary models were adjusted for age, sex, CKD, CVD history, subclinical CVD, smoking status, and race/ethnicity. To assess whether SPI provides information beyond its individual components, additional models were constructed including PP alone, SPI alone, and combinations of PP and BSA. A fully adjusted model including PP, BSA, and SPI simultaneously was used to evaluate the independent contribution of SPI beyond its constituent variables. Incremental prognostic value was evaluated by comparing nested models using likelihood ratio tests. Model discrimination and fit were assessed using Harrell’s C-index and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). A trend test across quartiles was performed by modeling SPI quartile as an ordinal variable; and results interpreted with consideration of potential threshold effects. Analyses were conducted in R ver. 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using tidyverse and survival packages.

Ethics statement

This secondary analysis of the publicly available SPRINT trial dataset was deemed exempt from Institutional Review Board review by the University of Virginia. The original SPRINT trial was approved by institutional review boards at all participating sites, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Study population

Among 6,070 SPRINT-MIND participants included in the analytic cohort, all had complete baseline blood pressure and anthropometric data enabling calculation of SPI. The cohort included 3,988 (65.7%) male participants. A total of 3,038 (50.0%) participants were randomized to the intensive blood pressure control arm and 3,032 (50.0%) to the standard control arm. Primary outcome analysis

During a median follow-up of 5.20 years (interquartile range [IQR], 3.95–6.08 years; mean, 4.93 years; range, 0.75–7.43 years), 110 participants (1.8%) developed probable dementia. Baseline SPI values ranged from 8.33 to 79.37, with a median value of 27.58 (IQR, 23.27–33.14). Participants who developed probable dementia had higher baseline SPI values compared with those who remained dementia-free, and outcome stratification by SPI quartiles demonstrated increasing risk primarily in the highest quartile, suggesting a threshold pattern rather than a graded linear association.

Event rates for incident probable dementia increased progressively across SPI quartiles, from 0.99% in Q1 (15/1,518) to 3.69% in Q4 (56/1,517) (Supplementary Table 1). This pattern of elevated risk in the highest SPI quartile was consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, including sex, CKD status, CVD history, subclinical CVD, smoking status, and race and/or ethnicity, with statistically significant trends observed in most subgroups (Supplementary Figures 1–3). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated lower dementia-free survival among participants in the highest SPI quartile (Figure 1).

Association between SPI and incident probable dementia

In the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, higher SPI was independently associated with increased risk of incident probable dementia. When modeled continuously, each 1-unit increase in SPI was associated with a higher hazard of probable dementia (HR, 1.15; p = 0.031 in models adjusting for PP and BSA). Per standard deviation increase in SPI (SD, 7.89), the adjusted HR was 1.50 (95% CI, 1.27–1.78), and per 10-unit increase the HR was 1.67 (95% CI, 1.35–2.07) (Table 2).

When SPI was analyzed in quartiles, participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher adjusted risk of probable dementia compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (HR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.27–4.39), while Q2 and Q3 were not significantly different from Q1 (Table 2). Event rates for incident probable dementia were similar across lower quartiles but increased markedly in the highest quartile (Q4), consistent with a threshold effect (Table 2).

Treatment arm stratification

The association between higher SPI and incident probable dementia was consistent across treatment arms. In both the intensive and standard blood pressure control arms, participants with high SPI (above the median) experienced substantially higher event rates compared with those with low SPI (Supplementary Table 2), with no evidence of effect modification by treatment assignment (p = 0.95), indicating consistency of the association across treatment groups.

Incremental prognostic value of SPI

To evaluate whether SPI provides prognostic information beyond its individual components, additional models were constructed including PP and BSA. In fully adjusted models including both PP and BSA, SPI remained independently associated with incident probable dementia.

In nested model comparisons, the addition of SPI to a model including PP and BSA significantly improved model fit (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.045). Model discrimination was modestly improved with SPI compared to PP alone (C-index, 0.756 vs. 0.746) and compared to PP and BSA combined (C-index 0.756 vs 0.754), with corresponding improvements in AIC (1,737 vs. 1,739), indicating that SPI captures additional prognostic information beyond its individual components. Discussion

In this post-hoc analysis of the SPRINT-MIND trial, we found that SPI was independently associated with incident probable dementia, including in models adjusting for PP and BSA. SPI demonstrated modest but statistically significant improvement in model performance compared with PP alone and when added to models including PP and BSA. The findings showing the strong relationship between SPI and incident probable dementia remained consistent irrespective of the treatment arm (standard vs. intensive), (p = 0.95). We observed that increased risk was primarily concentrated among individuals in the highest SPI quartile, consistent with a threshold effect rather than a graded linear relationship. These findings suggest that SPI may identify a subgroup of individuals with disproportionately elevated pulsatile load relative to body size, representing a potential high-risk vascular phenotype not fully captured by PP alone.

These findings are consistent with literature implicating pulsatile hemodynamic load and arterial stiffness in vascular cognitive impairment. High PP is a marker of vascular stiffness which in turn is also associated with neurocognitive decline with demonstrably increased beta amyloid deposition in the brain [11].

The relationship between PP and probable dementia is complex. However, previous studies have demonstrated that PP is strongly linked to the integrity of vascular health and specifically, arterial stiffness. PP has been described as a vascular index of aging [12]. With advancing age, the elastic components of the central arteries are replaced with collagen fibers resulting in increased arterial stiffness. The increased arterial stiffness leads to a predominant elevation in the systolic component of the blood pressure in tandem with the PP. The increased pulsatility and load is transmitted deeper into the cerebral vasculature leading to small vessel disease and rarefaction of the cerebral gray matter. The cerebral vasculature is vulnerable to pulsatile load because it is constitutively characterized by a microcirculation of low-resistance, high-flow state [13]. Several studies have associated peripheral arterial stiffness with the sustained increase in PP leading to brain micro-vascular diseases such as cerebral small vessel disease, cortical gray matter thinning, white matter atrophy, and cognitive dysfunction in older individuals and prematurely in hypertensive as well as diabetic patients. In animal models arterial stiffness and the subsequent excessive pulsatility lead to cognitive dysfunction through putative mechanisms involving vascular injury, oxidative stress and inflammation [14,15].

Although PP is strongly correlated with cognitive decline, the relationship between PP and whether body size modulates this relationship has not been rigorously studied. Prior studies have shown that blood pressure load on blood vessels may differ based on body size, with smaller sized individuals experiencing greater blood pressure load compared to individuals with larger body mass [16]. We extended this conceptual rationale by examining whether indexing pulsatile load to body size could better capture inter-individual variability in vascular stress. Rather than representing a direct biomechanical parameter, SPI should be interpreted as a pragmatic scaling index reflecting pulsatile load relative to body size. In this study, we confirmed that SPI, a vascular metric that incorporates PP indexed to BSA, is significantly associated with probable dementia and demonstrated modest improvement over PP alone. The role of pulsatile load on the vasculature may provide a potential mechanistic perspective on observations such as the “obesity paradox,” phenomenon wherein, although obesity is associated with clustering of vascular risk factors that may be associated with neurocognitive decline (hypertension, diabetes and other metabolic disorders), the presence of higher body mass index (BMI) appears to confer some protection from developing dementia [17]. Furthermore, some studies have demonstrated an association between obesity measures and arterial stiffness [18].

Given the shared susceptibility of low-resistance, high-flow organs such as the brain and kidney to pulsatile load, future work should examine whether SPI also relates to longitudinal renal outcomes.

Limitations

Some limitations in this study merit consideration. This post-hoc analysis of the SPRINT trial tempers the causal inference that could be made between SPI and neurocognitive decline. These findings could be considered more appropriately as hypothesis generating. There are also complex interactions at play between genetic and acquired factors that influence blood pressure, body size and arterial stiffness that cannot be explored by this database. For example, in monozygotic twin studies body size is correlated with blood pressure variables attributable to genetic and unique environmental factors [19]. In addition, deeper phenotyping, such as longitudinal vascular imaging and molecular profiling, will be important to clarify biological pathways linking pulsatile load to neurocognitive outcomes and to evaluate how SPI relates to direct measures of arterial stiffness. Finally, we could not fully determine whether associations differ by specific antihypertensive medication classes or treatment strategies beyond randomized BP target assignment.

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. We leveraged data from SPRINT-MIND, a large, rigorously conducted randomized clinical trial with standardized blood pressure measurements and centrally adjudicated cognitive outcomes, minimizing outcome misclassification. The availability of detailed baseline hemodynamic and anthropometric data allowed derivation of SPI using routinely collected clinical variables. We also evaluated SPI using complementary parameterizations (continuous scaling and quartiles) and assessed incremental model performance, supporting robustness. Furthermore, consistency of the association across treatment arms strengthens the internal validity of the observed relationship.

From a clinical perspective, SPI represents a simple, pragmatic metric derived from routinely available clinical measurements that may help identify individuals at higher vascular risk for neurocognitive outcomes. Incorporating BSA into assessment of pulsatile hemodynamic load may improve individualized risk stratification for vascular-mediated cognitive decline among hypertensive adults. If externally validated, SPI could serve as an accessible tool to identify individuals at heightened risk for neurocognitive outcomes and potentially inform more personalized prevention strategies targeting vascular stiffness and pulsatile load.

In the present post-hoc analysis of the SPRINT-MIND trial, we evaluated the association of SPI (PP indexed to BSA) with incident probable dementia in patients undergoing therapy for hypertension. We found that SPI was independently associated with incident probable dementia and provided modest incremental prognostic information beyond PP. These findings support further investigation into whether incorporating body size into assessment of pulsatile hemodynamic load may improve risk stratification for vascular-related cognitive outcomes.
