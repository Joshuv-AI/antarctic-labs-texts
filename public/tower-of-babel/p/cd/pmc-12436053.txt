Precision Strategy for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Surveillance after Hepatitis C Cure: Debates across Guidelines
Authors: Masaaki Mino, Eiji Kakazu, Tatsuya Kanto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis C virus (HCV) clearance markedly reduces the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); however, HCC continues to develop in a subset of patients, particularly in those with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis. Leading hepatology societies, including Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver, European Association for the Study of the Liver, American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases, Korean Association for the Study of the Liver, Taiwan Association for the Study of the Liver, and Japan Society of Hepatology, have issued divergent guidelines for HCC surveillance after sustained virologic response, which reflects variations in regional patient populations, healthcare infrastructure, and policy priorities. While traditional risk stratification primarily centers on histological staging of fibrosis, an array of additional host-related factors, including age, sex, alcohol use, metabolic comorbidities, and genetic and epigenetic profiles, further influence individual HCC risks. Recently developed predictive models aim to improve risk discrimination and inform tailored surveillance intervals. Concurrently, health economic analyses support the continuation of surveillance in high-risk populations. Nonetheless, the optimal surveillance frequency and criteria for patient selection remain matters of ongoing debates. This review synthesizes current controversies across international guidelines, presents an evaluation of the supporting evidence for varied surveillance strategies, highlights emerging tools for individualized risk assessment, and discusses cost-effectiveness considerations to inform personalized, evidence-based HCC surveillance in the post-HCV cure landscape.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a major global cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1 Chronic HCV infection induces persistent hepatic inflammation, which may progress to cirrhosis or HCC over a span of 10 to 30 years. The recent advent of direct-acting antiviral agents (DAAs) has revolutionized HCV treatment, offering a high likelihood of achieving sustained virologic response (SVR) with minimal adverse effects.2-6 HCV eradication through SVR markedly improves hepatic inflammation and liver function and slows the progression of liver fibrosis.7 Despite the significant reduction in HCC risk following SVR, a residual risk remains—particularly among patients with established cirrhosis.8-12 In the absence of antiviral treatment, annual HCC incidence is approximately 1.3% in patients without cirrhosis and 4.2% in those with cirrhosis.10 Post-SVR, the overall annual incidence declines to 0.43%, yet can still reach up to 1.18% in patients with cirrhosis. Comparable risks have been reported in other liver disease etiologies, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis cirrhosis, where annual HCC incidence ranges from 0.5% to 2.6%.13 In the era of widespread DAA use, the number of individuals achieving HCV clearance has expanded substantially, resulting in a growing population requiring long-term surveillance. This shift has heightened the urgency of developing cost-effective, evidence-based follow-up strategies. Key challenges include: (1) identifying risk factors for HCC in the post-SVR setting; (2) evaluating and refining novel risk stratification tools; (3) optimizing surveillance strategies to ensure cost-effectiveness and resource efficiency; and (4) reconciling variations in HCC surveillance recommendations across major liver societies.

This review addresses these critical issues by synthesizing current evidence and providing an in-depth analysis of emerging precision-based approaches aimed at enhancing HCC surveillance in the post-HCV cure era.

RISK FACTORS ASSOCIATED WITH HCC AFTER SVR

Accurate estimation of HCC risk factors remains critical for optimizing the effectiveness and cost-efficiency of post-SVR HCC surveillance strategies. Although certain HCV genotypes have been associated with HCC risk,14,15 a growing body of evidence indicates that host-related factors exert a more substantial influence, as summarized in Table 1.12,16-36 The following sections highlight key host-related determinants implicated in HCC development after SVR.

1. Advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis

Irrespective of etiology, the extent of liver fibrosis is a well-established and potent determinant of hepatocarcinogenesis.37,38 Among individuals with chronic HCV infection, the severity of baseline fibrosis represents the most robust predictor of HCC risk following SVR.12,39,40 A large retrospective study demonstrated that patients with cirrhosis at the time of SVR (n=9,784) exhibited an annual HCC incidence exceeding 2%.41 In multivariable Cox regression analyses, the presence of cirrhosis was associated with a greater than threefold increase in HCC risk compared with non-cirrhotic counterparts.16-19 Moreover, the posttreatment fibrosis status has been shown to more accurately predict long-term HCC risk than baseline fibrosis severity.42 Several noninvasive biomarkers—including M2BPGi, liver stiffness measurement (LSM), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), serum albumin, and albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade—have been identified as independent predictors of post-SVR HCC.19,24,28,43-45 Recent large-scale evidence underscores the prognostic value of transient elastography after HCV cure. A systematic review of 169,911 patients identified optimal HCC risk thresholds of 12.6 kPa pretreatment and 11.2 kPa post-SVR.23 In a U.S. veteran cohort, every 1 kPa increment in post-SVR LSM translated into 3% increase in HCC hazard.46 The change in LSM has also been evaluated; a reduction in liver stiffness >30% at the end of treatment was associated with a significantly lower risk of HCC at 1 year after end of treatment.47 However, LSM can be influenced by ongoing inflammation and often declines after SVR; therefore, baseline LSM is not a reliable predictor, whereas post-SVR LSM retains prognostic value.47 For M2BPGi, posttreatment value is linked to HCC risk.26,43 In a prospective multicenter cohort of 1,031 patients without prior HCC, higher M2BPGi at the time of SVR was shown to be a sensitive indicator of HCC recurrence, with each 1 cutoff index increase conferring an approximately fourfold higher hazard.25 Liver function has long been recognized as a risk factor for tumor progression.48,49 In multivariate analysis, baseline serum albumin and ALBI grade emerged as independent predictors of HCC development; the 5-year cumulative incidence reaches up 20% in patients with ALBI grade 2 or 3,22,28,50 Likewise, elevated GGT levels are associated with HCC occurrence and recurrence regardless of alcohol consumption or diabetic status;51,52 notably, patients with a baseline GGT >56 IU/L remain at elevated risk for more than a decade following SVR.27

2. Older age and sex

Advancing age is associated with cumulative cellular damage and senescence-related processes, including DNA damage, epigenetic dysregulation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and alterations in metabolic signaling pathways—factors that collectively elevate HCC risk.53,54 As the population of elderly individuals achieving SVR continues to expand, persistent surveillance in this subgroup remains a clinical priority. Numerous observational studies have identified individuals aged ≥60 years as having increased vulnerability to HCC after SVR, particularly when advanced fibrosis coexists.20-22,55 Notably, the risk was significantly elevated among those aged >65 years at the time of initial liver biopsy, with a reported more than 15-fold increase in HCC incidence.56 Sex-based differences have also been observed, with male sex identified as an independent risk factor for post-SVR HCC development.22 Although these findings may be influenced by residual confounding, elevated serum testosterone levels have been proposed as a potential mechanistic link.19,57

3. Metabolic factors

Metabolic factors have garnered increasing attention as pivotal determinants in HCC development, with accumulating evidence suggesting that their influence may surpass that of hepatic fibrosis as the duration following SVR extends.

1) MASLD/SLD

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as a leading etiology of chronic liver disease globally and is now recognized as an important risk factor for HCC.58 Among patients with HCV, hepatic steatosis—independent of hepatic fibrosis—has been identified as a significant contributor to post-SVR HCC development.29,30,59 Notably, individuals with MASLD who also meet criteria for metabolic syndrome and undergo liver resection demonstrate HCC risk profiles comparable to those of cirrhotic patients, despite the absence of advanced fibrosis.60-62 These findings underscore the imperative for long-term surveillance in patients with concomitant MASLD, even after HCV eradication. Beyond steatosis alone, metabolic abnormalities encompassed within the diagnostic criteria for MASLD—including obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome—play a substantial role in hepatocarcinogenesis.31,63,64 Given the multifactorial nature of MASLD, it remains challenging to isolate individual metabolic drivers that disproportionately increase carcinogenic potential.

2) Diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a risk factor for HCC, particularly in the context of chronic liver diseases.65,66 Mechanistically, insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia promote hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis, facilitating oncogenic transformation.67,68 Furthermore, persistent hyperglycemia exacerbates oxidative stress within hepatocytes, thereby enhancing malignant potential. Epidemiologic studies have shown that patients with DM who achieve SVR experience a 1.3-fold increased risk of HCC relative to non-diabetic counterparts, even after adjusting for confounding factors such as obesity and liver stiffness.65 These findings reinforce the critical role of strict glycemic control as a cornerstone of HCC prevention. In support of this, a population-based study from Taiwan reported that metformin use significantly reduced the risk of HCC in individuals with DM after SVR.69

3) Alcohol consumption

Alcohol metabolism results in the generation of acetaldehyde, a toxic intermediate that contributes to hepatocarcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including DNA adduct formation,70,71 generation of reactive oxygen species,72 induction of apoptosis,73 and dysregulation of the gut microbiome.74,75 Among patients with cirrhosis who have achieved HCV eradication, excessive alcohol intake—defined as >30 g/day in men and >20 g/day in women—has been associated with a significantly elevated risk of HCC.21,31,39,76,77

4. Genetic and epigenetic factors

Host genetic polymorphisms—such as those in patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3),78-80 tolloid-like 1 (TLL1),33,81 MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence A (MICA),82,83 HLA complex P5 (HCP5),84 DEP domain-containing 5 (DEPDC5),85 tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)86,87 and interleukin (IL) 28B88,89—have been implicated in the modulation of fibrogenesis and carcinogenic potential. However, these genetic polymorphisms have been primarily associated with the pathogenesis of chronic HCV infection and have not been demonstrated to exert significant influence following the achievement of SVR except for PNPLA3 and TLL1.32,33,81 In contrast, accumulating evidence suggests that epigenetic alterations induced by chronic HCV infection may persist after viral clearance and contribute to hepatocarcinogenesis.34,35 Specifically, HCV-driven acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27ac) has been linked to an increased risk of HCC development. In addition, chronic HCV infection induces enduring immunologic imprints, characterized by long-lasting epigenetic modifications within the immune system.90 Notably, epigenetic signatures in both cytotoxic innate and adaptive immune cell populations demonstrate only partial reversibility following SVR, underscoring their potential contribution to persistent oncogenic risk.

5. Immunological factors

HCV infection disrupts immune homeostasis through immunosuppressive mechanisms,91 and this dysregulation may persist beyond SVR, thereby facilitating carcinogenesis.92,93 In particular, immune exhaustion—marked by impaired cytotoxic functions of CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells—has been associated with an increased risk of post-SVR HCC.94,95 Recent studies have demonstrated substantial phenotypic and functional alterations in intrahepatic and peripheral NK cells derived from patients with HCC after SVR. Specifically, CD56dim NK cells isolated from tumor tissues exhibited elevated expression of exhaustion markers, including TIM-3, ILT2, and PD-L1, consistent with attenuated antitumor immunity.96 Longitudinal profiling of NK cell subsets in cirrhotic patients who later developed HCC revealed progressive divergence in NK cell signatures compared with tumor-free controls; notably, stable or rising frequencies of TIM-3high CD38+ NK cells were predictive of HCC development within an average interval of 11 months, with a predictive probability (88%).36 Furthermore, ILT2+ CD56dim NK cells—whose frequency increases with age—were enriched in cancerous liver tissue and demonstrated reduced cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) relative to their ILT2– counterparts.97

Persistent immune suppression in the post-SVR liver microenvironment is further supported by upregulation of immune checkpoint pathways, including the PD-1/PD-L1 axis,98,99 which may impair effective immune surveillance and promote an immunotolerant milieu conducive to HCC emergence. Integrating these immunological biomarkers into predictive models and surveillance protocols may significantly enhance risk stratification and enable earlier intervention for HCC in the post-SVR population.

6. Amino acid imbalance

Recent advances in metabolomics suggest that amino acid metabolism may play a critically important role in the pathogenesis of HCC among patients after SVR. The integration of metabolomic profiling into clinical practice holds promise for improving risk stratification and could enhance surveillance strategies, particularly in populations with chronic liver disease or following SVR.100 Of particular interest are perturbations in branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and aromatic amino acids, which contribute to hepatic dysfunction and inflammation and are believed to increase the risk of HCC, although the precise causal pathways—whether direct or indirect—remain to be fully elucidated.101,102 In our prior investigation, we demonstrated that the BCAA to tyrosine ratio—an indicator of hepatic dysfunction in Japan103—is significantly reduced in individuals with chronic liver disease compared with healthy controls,104 highlighting the potential influence of amino acid imbalance on disease progression. Moreover, BCAA depletion has been implicated in the development of sarcopenia, a condition commonly associated with HCC recurrence and adverse clinical outcomes.105 Although no study has yet prospectively demonstrated that post-SVR amino acid perturbations predict incident HCC, several longitudinal metabolomic analyses have documented persistent imbalances after SVR, suggesting a possible link to carcinogenesis. For example, glutamate and phenylalanine in post-SVR patients, remain significantly altered compared with healthy controls.106 Similarly, urinary nuclear magnetic resonance in cirrhotic HCV patients showed recovery of valine catabolites but sustained alterations in tyrosine and glutamine through 3 months posttreatment.107 In HIV/HCV-coinfected cirrhotic, at 36 weeks post-SVR identified shifts in BCAA intermediates and N-methylalanine that correlate with improvements in liver function and inflammatory markers.108 Collectively, these findings underscore the multifactorial role of altered amino acid metabolism in liver pathology and tumorigenesis. Although the extent to which these metabolic disturbances result directly from HCV infection or through secondary mechanisms remains uncertain, emerging evidence supports their potential utility as biomarkers for refining post-SVR surveillance and therapeutic strategies.

PREDICTIVE MODELS FOR HCC AFTER SVR

The development of accurate predictive models for HCC following SVR remains a major clinical challenge. Numerous studies have sought to establish scoring systems to stratify HCC risk and optimize post-SVR surveillance strategies (Table 2).21,28,109-115 Traditional tools such as the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index offer simple, practical clinical utility;109 however, multivariable regression-based risk calculators provide greater predictive accuracy for HCC development. A large retrospective cohort study involving 45,810 HCV-infected patients treated with antiviral therapy, of whom 1,412 subsequently developed HCC, led to the creation of a model incorporating four parameters—age, platelet count, serum aspartate aminotransferase/√alanine aminotransferase (AST/√ALT) ratio, and albumin. This model demonstrated robust discriminatory power, with a Gönen and Heller κ-statistic ranging from 0.70 to 0.77, and is publicly accessible via an online platform (www.hccrisk.) to assist clinicians in individualized risk estimation. The age-male-albumin-bilirubin-platelets (aMAP) score, developed from a multinational cohort of 17,374 patients with viral and non-viral hepatitis, provides etiology- and ethnicity-independent risk stratification for HCC. Notably, 44% of patients with an aMAP score <50 had an annual HCC incidence of <0.2%. The 3A model—comprising age at baseline, ALT at SVR, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) at SVR—has also demonstrated utility in identifying low-risk individuals without cirrhosis for post-SVR surveillance. Additional noninvasive biomarker-based models have been proposed to further delineate individuals at minimal risk of HCC development.

DEFINING A LOW-RISK GROUP FOR POST-SVR HCC

Although the long-term risk of HCC following SVR is not fully understood, identifying a subset of patients with low risk remains a key objective in refining surveillance strategies. Comparative assessments of various risk models, including the aMAP and modified PAGE-B scores,116 in a Korean cohort revealed no incident cases of HCC among patients categorized as low risk.117 Similarly, patients scoring 0 on the 3A model demonstrated no HCC occurrence, reinforcing the feasibility of defining a low-risk population. However, a comparative analysis involving six predictive models found that none achieved a Harrell’s C-index exceeding 0.8, highlighting the limitations in current risk stratification capabilities post-SVR.118 Concordant findings in Egyptian cohorts further underscore the ongoing challenge of achieving reliable and complete risk discrimination in this setting.

PERSONALIZED RISK ASSESSMENT USING MACHINE LEARNING MODELS

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has led to the emergence of machine learning-based models that surpass traditional regression approaches in predictive performance. In particular, deep learning architectures such as recurrent neural networks have demonstrated superior accuracy compared with conventional logistic regression for identifying individuals at high risk of developing HCC (Table 2).113 Furthermore, AI-driven decision tree algorithms that integrate both clinical and genetic data have been introduced to enhance individualized HCC risk stratification and inform tailored surveillance protocols.114 One such example is the SMART model, which incorporates seven readily available clinical parameters—age, platelet count, serum AFP, GGT, albumin, AST, and body mass index—to generate personalized risk curves for HCC development.115 Additionally, models that predict broader liver-related outcomes rather than HCC alone, such as the Ai-Safe-C score, have demonstrated clinical utility in non-cirrhotic patients following SVR.119

OPTIMAL DURATION OF SURVEILLANCE AFTER SVR

Evidence regarding the appropriate duration of HCC surveillance after SVR suggests that the risk persists in high-risk populations. For instance, patients with cirrhosis and post-SVR FIB-4 scores ≥3.25 continue to exhibit HCC incidence rates exceeding 1.5% even 7 years after achieving SVR.41,120 In a retrospective cohort of 75,965 patients who achieved SVR, non-cirrhotic individuals demonstrated a consistently low annual HCC incidence (0.23–0.27 per 100 person-years), with no significant correlation between the time since SVR and HCC risk. In contrast, among patients with cirrhosis, the risk gradually declined over the first 6 years but remained above the accepted thresholds that justify ongoing surveillance.121 The need for routine surveillance in low-risk patients without advanced fibrosis remains uncertain and warrants further investigation, especially in those with concurrent risk factors for HCC.

COST-EFFECTIVENESS ANALYSES

Ongoing surveillance in high-risk individuals following SVR has been shown to be a cost-effective strategy, largely given the benefits associated with early detection and the potential for curative interventions in HCC. Nonetheless, the determination of optimal surveillance intervals and appropriate patient stratification remains contentious, primarily because diagnostic accuracy and economic efficiency are closely interdependent, complicating efforts to optimize both parameters concurrently. A microsimulation model indicated that biannual ultrasound surveillance for HCC remains cost-effective up to 75 years of age in patients with cirrhosis and up to 60 years in those with stable advanced fibrosis.122 In Japan, treatment with DAAs across all fibrosis stages in patients with HCV is found to be cost-effective, irrespective of disease severity.123 A similar conclusion was drawn in an Italian cohort study, in which a universal treatment approach using DAAs demonstrated superior cost-effectiveness compared with a fibrosis-stage–based prioritization strategy.124 Furthermore, another investigation from Japan reported that initiating DAA therapy in cirrhotic patients yielded greater cost-effectiveness than deferring treatment until after liver transplantation, due to a significant reduction in the risk of HCV recurrence following transplantation.125

Looking ahead, the integration of AI with genomic, proteomic, and immunologic data platforms—beyond reliance on fibrosis staging alone—may enable more precise therapeutic approaches and further improve cost-effectiveness.

SURVEILLANCE STRATEGIES FOR HCC AMONG REGIONAL GUIDELINES

There is broad consensus across major liver disease societies that patients with cirrhosis should undergo continued surveillance for HCC following SVR. However, notable discrepancies exist among regional guidelines—such as those of the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL), European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), Korean Association for the Study of the Liver (KASL), Taiwan Association for the Study of the Liver (TASL), and Japan Society of Hepatology (JSH)—reflecting differences in healthcare infrastructure, priorities, and resource allocation (Table 3).126-133

A principal area of divergence lies in defining the target population for surveillance. Both APASL and TASL advocate for broad-based screening that includes all patients with chronic HCV, irrespective of fibrosis stage. In Japan, routine clinical practice involves surveillance of HCV-infected individuals, with HCC screening every 6 months in patients with chronic hepatitis and every 3 to 4 months in those with cirrhosis. However, the JSH guidelines do not explicitly specify surveillance recommendations post-SVR. These approaches are particularly applicable in regions with a high HCV prevalence, where early detection and timely intervention remain critical. Conversely, EASL, AASLD, and KASL recommend targeted surveillance limited to high-risk groups, including individuals with advanced fibrosis (F3) or cirrhosis (F4), to optimize the cost-effectiveness of surveillance and ensure efficient resource utilization. Surveillance intervals also vary across guidelines. While biannual screening is commonly endorsed for patients with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis, TASL and JSH recommend shorter intervals. Although this may improve early-stage detection rates, it can impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems with limited resources. Furthermore, EASL and AASLD explicitly recommend indefinite continuation of surveillance following SVR, whereas guidelines from societies that advocate broader screening do not delineate an optimal surveillance duration. Regarding surveillance modalities, abdominal ultrasound combined with AFP testing is widely accepted as the standard approach owing to its accessibility, non-invasiveness, and cost-effectiveness. However, APASL and JSH guidelines incorporate additional biomarkers, such as PIVKA-II (protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II), which improve diagnostic sensitivity but incur higher costs. This reflects an emphasis on precision medicine in regions such as Japan, where these costs are generally covered by the national health insurance system. Conversely, EASL and AASLD prioritize streamlined, lower-cost strategies to maintain equitable access to surveillance in high-risk populations without exceeding healthcare system capacities.

Despite these ongoing efforts, significant limitations in surveillance strategies persist. Sampling variability associated with liver biopsies and imaging techniques may result in misclassification of fibrosis stage, potentially excluding high-risk individuals from appropriate surveillance. Furthermore, a subset of HCC cases continues to arise in patients outside the established high-risk criteria, highlighting limitations in current stratification frameworks. Incorporating individualized risk factors—such as older age, alcohol use, metabolic syndrome, and comorbid conditions—may improve surveillance precision and clinical outcomes. Tailored approaches that account for these variables are essential to enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of HCC surveillance in the post-SVR population.

CONCLUSIONS

The advent of curative therapies for HCV has markedly reduced the incidence of HCC and liver disease-related mortality. However, a residual risk of HCC remains, particularly among individuals with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis. Existing surveillance recommendations vary across liver society guidelines and are primarily based on the degree of fibrosis. Recently, predictive models have been developed to improve risk stratification, offering the potential to inform more individualized surveillance strategies. Future approaches should prioritize the implementation of personalized surveillance frameworks that integrate predictive algorithms with comprehensive datasets encompassing genomic, metabolic, immunological, and clinical variables—augmented by AI-driven analyses that incorporate cost-effectiveness considerations (Fig. 1). A key objective will be the precise identification of patients at both minimal and elevated risk to allow for surveillance interval adjustments based on individual risk profiles and the establishment of robust tools for early cancer detection. Ongoing data acquisition and rigorous evidence-based validation will be critical for refining risk stratification strategies, optimizing resource utilization, and improving clinical outcomes in the post-SVR landscape.
