Steatotic Liver Disease Predicts Lower Likelihood of LDLR Gene Mutations in Young Korean Patients with Suspected Familial Hypercholesterolemia
Authors: Chang In Han, Sung Hyun Cho, Keungmo Yang, Hyun Yang, Si Hyun Bae, Jaejun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is underdiagnosed in young adults, and universal genetic testing is costly. We evaluated whether steatotic liver disease (SLD) could predict low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) mutations in suspected FH.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 111 Korean military personnel aged 19–30 years who underwent LDLR sequencing and SLD assessment using controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and hepatic steatosis index.ResultsPathogenic LDLR variants were identified in 23.4% of participants. Compared with non-carriers, carriers had lower CAP values (245.2 dB/m vs. 273.9 dB/m, P=0.085) and a significantly lower prevalence of SLD (52.9% vs. 82.0%, P=0.025). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed SLD was independently associated with lower odds of variant carriage (adjusted odds ratio, 0.11; 95% confidence interval, 0.02 to 0.56; P=0.009). Clustering analysis showed the highest variant prevalence in lean, non-SLD individuals.ConclusionSLD was inversely associated with genetically confirmed FH. Incorporating SLD assessment may help prioritize genetic testing in populations with a high prevalence of SLD or limited access to sequencing resources.

INTRODUCTION

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) caused by low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) variants results in lifelong low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) elevation and premature cardiovascular risk, affecting approximately 1 in 313 individuals worldwide but remaining underdiagnosed in >99% of cases.1 Although clinical scoring and genetic testing aid diagnosis, sequencing costs (US dollar 200–500/person) limit broader use, underscoring the need for simple biomarkers to better target testing.2

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is increasingly prevalent in adolescents and young adults.3 In South Korea, >90% of military personnel are aged 19 to 22, a group with rising MASLD prevalence.4 In the limited military healthcare setting, hepatic steatosis may serve as a useful proxy to prioritize genetic testing. However, the relationship between steatotic liver disease (SLD) and genetically confirmed FH remains poorly understood.

We evaluated whether SLD is linked to the presence of LDLR variants in young adults with suspected FH to assess if SLD is a cost-effective biomarker for phenotype-guided triage.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at the Armed Forces Goyang Hospital, Republic of Korea, by reviewing records of military personnel aged 19 to 30 years evaluated for suspected FH between January 2022 and December 2023. All participants underwent LDLR genetic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Institutional Review Board approved the protocol (AFMC 2024-01-010-001) and waived the requirement for informed consent.

Definition of suspected FH

Criteria included: (1) lean: LDL-C ≥131 mg/dL and body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m2 (95th percentile for Korean 19-yearolds); 5 (2) obese: LDL-C ≥160 mg/dL and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (approximate 99th percentile for Korean young adults); (3) obese with borderline LDL-C: BMI ≥25 kg/m2, LDL-C <160 mg/dL, plus triglycerides ≥133 mg/dL or total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL; or (4) 2022 Korean Consensus Statement criteria (premature coronary artery disease, LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL, xanthomas, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, or first-degree relative with FH).6 Participants on lipid-lowering therapy or lacking complete fasting lipid profiles were excluded. Lower LDL-C thresholds were used to reflect age-specific norms in Korean young adults and to include a broader group with suspected FH.

Genetic testing

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood, and LDLR coding regions and exon-intron boundaries were analyzed by polymerase chain reaction and direct sequencing. Variants were classified per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines.

Hepatic steatosis assessment

The term SLD is used throughout this manuscript for consistency, as it encompasses both MASLD and steatosis without complete metabolic dysfunction criteria. Steatosis was assessed by transient elastography using the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), with thresholds of ≥238 dB/m (S1: 238–258; S2: 259–291; S3: ≥292 dB/m).7 For sensitivity analysis, the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) was calculated as 8×(alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase)+BMI, with steatosis defined as HSI ≥36.8

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were compared using t-tests or analysis of variance and categorical variables with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Trends were assessed by the Cochran–Armitage test. K-means clustering (k=3) used BMI, LDL-C, and steatosis status. The optimal number of clusters (k=3) was determined using both the elbow method and silhouette analysis, which consistently supported the presence of three phenotypic groups. Logistic regression identified factors associated with LDLR variants. P<0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (R version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Of 111 participants (110 male; mean age 21.6±2.2 years), 26 (23.4%) carried pathogenic LDLR variants (Supplementary Table 1). Carriers had higher LDL-C (232.5±58.8 mg/dL vs. 197.6± 40.9 mg/dL, P=0.001) and total cholesterol (301.9±56.0 mg/dL vs. 281.0±43.3 mg/dL, P=0.048) levels, but lower fasting glucose (93.4±5.8 mg/dL vs. 97.2±8.2 mg/dL, P=0.032) and CAP (245.2±51.5 dB/m vs. 273.9±56.6 dB/m, P=0.085) than non-carriers.

Hepatic steatosis and LDLR status

Carriers had lower SLD prevalence than non-carriers (52.9% vs. 82.0%, P=0.025) (Fig. 1A). Carrier prevalence decreased with steatosis severity (42.1% in S0 vs. 11.8% in S3, P-trend=0.073) (Fig. 1B). Sensitivity analysis using HSI ≥36 showed similar trends (23.1% vs. 41.2% in carriers vs. non-carriers, P=0.109) and moderate CAP–HSI concordance (κ=0.466) (Fig. 1C).

Correlations of DLCN score with clinical parameters

The Dutch Lipid Clinic Network (DLCN) score was positively correlated with LDL-C (r=0.76, P<0.001) and total cholesterol (r=0.72, P<0.001), and inversely with fasting glucose (r=–0.34, P<0.05) (Fig. 1D). CAP and liver stiffness showed weak inverse correlations with DLCN score (r=–0.21 and r=–0.08, respectively).

Phenotypic clustering

K-means clustering using BMI, LDL-C, and hepatic steatosis identified three phenotypic clusters (Fig. 1E): Cluster 1 (n=37) had a BMI 23–25 kg/m2 with SLD (13.5% carriers); Cluster 2 (n=22) included obese participants with SLD (18.2% carriers); and Cluster 3 (n=52) represented lean, non-SLD individuals with the highest carrier rate (32.7%). This supports the inverse association between hepatic fat and genetic FH.

Factors associated with LDLR variant carriage

In regression, SLD (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.11; 95% confidence interval, 0.02 to 0.56; P=0.009), impaired fasting glucose (adjusted OR, 0.28; P=0.032), and CAP (adjusted OR, 0.98 per dB/m; P=0.026) were inversely associated with variant carriage (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study demonstrated a strong inverse association between SLD and pathogenic LDLR variants in Korean young adults with suspected FH. Variant carriers showed lower SLD prevalence and CAP values despite higher LDL-C levels than non-carriers. This relationship was consistent across definitions and methods, supporting the use of SLD as a negative predictor of FH. To our knowledge, this is the first report of such an association in young adults.

The inverse association may be attributed to the distinct pathophysiologic mechanisms underlying LDLR-mutation-driven dyslipidemia and SLD. LDLR mutations cause lifelong LDL-C elevation independent of insulin sensitivity, whereas SLD is primarily driven by insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, which promote hepatic fat accumulation through increased lipolytic flux and impaired insulin signaling.9-11 This divergence may partly explain the lower SLD prevalence in genetically confirmed FH, as non-carriers are more likely to exhibit metabolic or environmental risk factors that predispose to steatosis.

Because universal FH genetic testing remains costly with low yield, incorporating SLD into pre-screening may improve efficiency, particularly in high-SLD or resource-limited populations.12,13 CAP-based stratification could identify individuals more likely to harbor mutations, with lean, non-SLD individuals showing the highest prevalence.

Key limitations of this study include its retrospective, single-center design, predominantly male cohort, and missing CAP data in 39.6% of participants, although HSI-based analyses showed consistent findings. The male predominance was largely attributable to the military hospital setting, making further validation in female patients necessary. Genetic analysis was restricted to LDLR, excluding apolipoprotein B (APOB), proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), and polygenic factors, which may have resulted in underestimation of FH burden. As APOB and PCSK9 are important contributors to the genetic spectrum of FH, inclusion of these variants could further strengthen the comprehensiveness and robustness of phenotype-based triage strategies. Moreover, some PCSK9 variants have been linked to steatosis risk, highlighting the need for broader genetic assessment. Lastly, SLD-related variants such as transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2) E167K and patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3) I148M were not assessed; including these factors in future studies could provide a more complete evaluation of genetic modifiers.

Nonetheless, this study demonstrated for the first time an inverse relationship between SLD and LDLR mutations in young adults. Incorporating SLD assessment into pre-screening may enhance the cost-effectiveness of genetic testing, especially in high-prevalence or resource-limited settings. Further studies are needed to confirm this association and evaluate steatosis-guided triage in clinical practice.
