Diagnosis and minimally invasive surgical treatment of peritoneal dialysis–associated pleuroperitoneal fistula: A case report and literature review

Published: 2026

Central Message

Peritoneopleural fistula associated with peritoneal dialysis: diagnosis and fluorescence-guided minimally invasive surgical treatment.

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a frequently used renal-replacement intervention for patients with end-stage renal disease. However, PD-related complications can lead to treatment interruption, among which the complication of pleuroperitoneal communication (PPC) is relatively rare (incidence approximately 1.6%-10%) but serious.E1,E2 Its characteristic feature is the leakage of dialysate through diaphragmatic defects into the pleural cavity, resulting in significant pleural effusion, often causing dyspnea and ultrafiltration failure, and potentially forcing a switch from PD to hemodialysis.E3 Since its first report by Edwards and Unger in 1967,1 diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for PPC have continuously evolved. Patients who undergo traditional nonsurgical treatments (such as temporary PD cessation, thoracentesis, and chemical pleurodesis) have high recurrence rates,E4 and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), because of its minimally invasive, intuitive, and efficient nature, has become the mainstream choice for treating PPC.2 However, because diaphragmatic defects often present as microscopic pores or areas of tissue thinning that are difficult to identify under white-light thoracoscopy, precise intraoperative localization remains a key challenge for surgical success.3 In this case report, we demonstrate a case in which intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) was used to confirm the diagnosis and guide the selection of VATS as the surgical approach for the treatment of pleuroperitoneal fistula and discuss optimized management strategies in conjunction with recent studies.

Case Report

A 43-year-old woman underwent PD catheter placement for stage 5 chronic kidney disease. Preoperative assessment indicated a low-average transporter type, meaning slower glucose absorption and prolonged osmotic gradient maintenance, which facilitates fluid removal (peritoneal equilibration test: 4-hour D/P creatinine ratio of 0.47), with a urea clearance index of 2.06, indicating adequate small solute clearance (target >1.7). She was managed with a manual intermittent PD regimen, which achieved a daily ultrafiltration volume of approximately 50 mL while maintaining a 24-hour urine output of 1500 to 2000 mL. After 4 months of regular PD, the patient developed progressive ultrafiltration failure. The ultrafiltration volume turned from positive to negative and rapidly deteriorated from approximately −120 mL to approximately −500 mL within a short period, accompanied by exertional dyspnea. Findings on computed tomography (CT) of the chest were highly suggestive of a large right-sided pleural effusion consistent with a PPC.

To alleviate symptoms, right-sided thoracentesis and catheter drainage under local anesthesia were performed, yielding clear fluid. The glucose concentration in the drained pleural fluid exceeded the diagnostic serum glucose concentration threshold, suggesting the presence of a pleuroperitoneal fistula. The glucose concentration in the drained pleural fluid was 7.4 mmol/L, and the glucose gradient between pleural fluid and serum was 3.0 mmol/L, which was slightly above the previously proposed diagnostic threshold of 2.78 mmol/L (50 mg/dL). However, given that this threshold was derived from a case series with a limited number of patients and there is no universally accepted standard, the diagnostic evidence for PPC in this case was considered insufficient. Therefore, CT peritoneography was subsequently used to obtain a definitive diagnosis. The specific method involved mixing 100 mL of a nonionic contrast agent (iohexol) with 1.5 L of dialysate, which was subsequently administered into the peritoneal cavity through the PD catheter. The patient was instructed to ambulate and change positions appropriately to promote an even distribution of contrast. CT scans of the chest were performed at 1 hour and 3 hours after infusion (Figure 1). The results revealed a greater volume of the right pleural effusion than before, and the CT value (Hounsfield units) of the effusion area significantly increased over time, confirming leakage of contrast across the diaphragm, thus verifying the diagnosis of a PPC.

Left to right: before contrast injection/1 hour after injection/3 hours after injection.

Given the high recurrence risk with nonsurgical treatment, surgical intervention was selected. The patient underwent VATS under general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation. After the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position, a 1-cm incision was made at the right mid-axillary line, seventh intercostal space, as the observation port for the fluorescent thoracoscope, whereas a 3-cm incision was made at the right anterior axillary line, fifth intercostal space, as the operating port. Intraoperative exploration revealed a large amount of clear pleural effusion. After aspiration, careful observation of the diaphragmatic surface under white-light mode revealed no definitive defects or pores. Subsequently, intraoperative ICG fluorescence navigation for localization was performed. A total of 1000 mL of dialysate containing 25 mg of ICG was rapidly delivered into the peritoneal cavity via the PD catheter, supplemented with moderate manual abdominal wall pressure to increase intra-abdominal pressure. Approximately 10 minutes after infusion, the thoracoscope was switched to near-infrared fluorescence mode. At this point, 4 areas with strong fluorescence signals were identified in the central tendon region of the diaphragm, with fluorescent fluid continuously leaking from 2 of them, allowing precise location of the PPC fistula sites and weak diaphragmatic areas (Figure 2).

A, White-light thoracoscopic view of the diaphragm. B, Near-infrared fluorescence thoracoscopic view of the diaphragm, with arrows indicating the locations of pleuroperitoneal fistula sites and weak diaphragmatic areas.

After the lesions were identified, the fistula/weak areas were sutured using 2-0 ETHICON silk sutures (2-0 silk; Ethicon). A piece of absorbable polyglycolic acid mesh was subsequently trimmed and sutured to cover the repaired area. Finally, a polypropylene hernia mesh was used to overlay the repair site. The mesh was fixed with interrupted 2-0 ETHICON silk sutures. A total of 9 sutures were placed through the mesh and healthy diaphragmatic muscle, avoiding the thin central tendon area. On the basis of physical principles,E5 because the nonabsorbable polypropylene mesh has greater stiffness and lower compliance than the weak tissue, when the 2 are sutured together, the mesh and surrounding normal tissue form a mechanical composite. External forces from the peritoneal dialysate are preferentially borne by the mesh, thereby “unloading” the weak area and achieving local tension reduction (Figure 3). The entire procedure proceeded smoothly. A drainage tube was placed in the right side of the chest after the operation; on the third postoperative day, when drainage was less than 200 mL/d, the tube was removed. The patient recovered well and was permitted to leave the hospital on the fifth postoperative day. PD was temporarily suspended postoperatively, with fluid and sodium restriction and electrolyte monitoring; hemodialysis was not initiated.

A, PGA mesh reinforcement after suture closure. B, Polypropylene mesh overlay with suture fixation for tension reduction.

One month later, a follow-up CT scan of the chest indicated a stable condition (Figure 4, A). PD was gradually restarted, beginning with an exchange volume of 1.0 L. The patient maintained an upright position, and pleural effusion was closely monitored by daily chest auscultation; no routine imaging was performed unless clinical changes occurred. During the 3-month follow-up period, PD treatment proceeded smoothly without recurrence of ultrafiltration failure or pleural effusion (Figure 4, B). Follow-up pulmonary function tests revealed no significant decline (forced vital capacity preoperative/3 months postoperative: 2.68/2.48 L; forced expiratory volume in 1 second preoperative/3 months postoperative: 2.37/2.19 L). The patient reported no dyspnea, full recovery of daily activities to baseline levels (including walking and light housework), and no PD-related readmission. She rated her overall quality of life as 90/100 on the EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale.

Postoperative 1-month CT (A) and postoperative 3-month CT (B). CT, Computed tomography.

Discussion

PPC is a recognized but uncommon complication in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis. A review of the literature published in the last 2 decades revealed that all reports on thoracoscopic treatment consisted of case reports or series, covering a total of approximately 30 cases. Among these patients, only 12 underwent intraoperative fluorescence thoracoscopy for navigation.E2,E6,E7 Except for 1 patient, who experienced recurrent pleural effusion after combined talc pleurodesis leading to treatment failure, the remaining 11 patients successfully resumed their previous dialysis modality. This case report is the first to introduce the concept of tension-reducing repair and to use a combined strategy of “preoperative confirmation via CT peritoneography, precise intraoperative navigation using ICG fluorescence thoracoscopy, multilayer reinforcement repair, and local tension reduction,” achieving safe and effective diagnosis and treatment of PPC.

Evolution and Optimization of Diagnostic Methods

The diagnosis of PPC relies on a combination of clinical presentation, biochemical analysis, and imaging. Pleural fluid biochemical analysis is performed initially; a pleural fluid glucose concentration significantly greater than the blood glucose concentration strongly suggests PPC.4 However, the lack of universally accepted diagnostic thresholds for the pleural fluid-to-serum glucose gradient remains a challenge.E8 A case series proposed a gradient >2.8 mmol/L (>50 mg/dL) as diagnostic, but this conclusion is limited by the small number of cases. When the gradient is sufficiently large, CT peritoneography may not be necessary; however, if the gradient is equivocal or clinical suspicion remains high despite a nondiagnostic gradient, imaging confirmation is required.E9 Because of its high spatial resolution and wide availability, CT peritoneography has become the preferred method.E10 Key points include (1) using an adequate amount of contrast agent mixed with dialysate; (2) instructing the patient to ambulate after the procedure; and (3) performing delayed scans (eg, 1-4 hours after infusion), as small or intermittent fistulas may show delayed opacification.E11 In this case, CT value elevation was observed at the 1-hour delayed scan, with a further increase at 3 hours, fully illustrating the importance of delayed imaging. In addition, Nakayama and colleaguesE8 reported that performing CT scans while the patient holds their breath or performing the Valsalva maneuver can increase the peritoneal-pleural pressure gradient, thereby increasing the detection rate of minute fistulas.

Innovation in Intraoperative Localization Techniques and the Value of ICG Fluorescence

Direct intraoperative identification and management of all diaphragmatic defects/weak areas is crucial for surgical success and prevention of recurrence. Research by Chen and colleagues3 indicated that without intraoperative identification of defects using contrast agents with increased intra-abdominal pressure, 77% of patients undergoing thoracoscopy had no visible diaphragmatic lesions, and the recurrence rate in such cases was 26%. Traditional VATS under white light may reveal only larger defects or bullous lesions, whereas minute pores or areas of simple tissue weakness are easily missed.3 Intraoperative confirmation of the lesion directly affects treatment success rates. ICG near-infrared fluorescence imaging technology provides a powerful tool to address this challenge. Compared with visible dyes such as methylene blue or indigo carmine, ICG offers advantages including real-time imaging, deep tissue penetration, and no staining of the surgical field. Visible dyes can obscure anatomical structures. However, methylene blue remains an effective and less-expensive alternative. Currently, no head-to-head trials have compared these agents, and the choice depends on institutional availability.E6 After binding to albumin and intraperitoneal infusion, ICG leaks from the fistula site, appearing as bright green fluorescence under near-infrared light. The advantages of ICG near-infrared fluorescence imaging include the followingE2,E6,E7: (1) High sensitivity: it has the ability to detect microfistulas (as in this case) or diffuse leakage that is difficult to see with the naked eye; (2) real-time navigation: it has the ability to provide clear targets for surgical repair; and (3) repair verification: observing the disappearance of fluorescence after suturing allows immediate confirmation of repair effectiveness. With respect to the optimal observation time after ICG infusion, the literature suggests a window of 10 to 30 minutes. Too short a time may result in insufficient contrast, whereas too long may cause background interference because of dye diffusion within the pleural cavity.E2,E7

Comprehensive Application of Surgical Repair Strategies

The surgical repair concept for PPC has evolved from simple pleurodesis to a comprehensive reinforcement strategy centered on mechanical repair. (1) Direct suturing: This method forms the basis for repairing definite defects.E12 (2) Mesh reinforcement: The use of nonabsorbable material (eg, polypropylene hernia mesh) to cover the diaphragm not only closes the identified defects but also reduces diaphragmatic tension, strengthens the entire diaphragm, and prevents future leakage from potential weak points. Nemeth and colleaguesE13 used polypropylene mesh to cover the entire diaphragm, achieving a 100% success rate with no recurrences. (3) Absorbable material barrier: Materials such as polyglycolic acid mesh can serve as an additional layer, providing early mechanical support and inducing tissue growth.E14 Regarding the mechanism of mesh reinforcement, although previous literature has primarily attributed its efficacy to pleurodesis, we propose a complementary “load-sharing” hypothesis (as detailed in the surgical technique description of our Case Report). This biomechanical principle is well established in hernia surgery, where mesh reinforcement promotes load distribution and reduces stress on repair sites.E15, E16, E17 However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation in the context of PPC repair. Regarding the necessity of mesh reinforcement, we acknowledge that some cases in Table 1 achieved acceptable results without mesh. However, most of those patients were treated with talc, which can cause serious complications; one patient developed recurrent loculated effusions and dyspnea. Regarding infection risk, we recognize that the use of mesh PD patients with PD carries a risk of infection. We administered prophylactic postoperative antibiotics and suspended PD for 1 month postoperatively to promote tissue healing. The patient showed no signs of infection at 3 months, although long-term risk remains and is acknowledged as a limitation. The “direct suture + absorbable mesh coverage + nonabsorbable mesh reinforcement” multilayer reinforcement method used in this case aligns with successful strategies reported by Wang and colleagues5 and Qian and colleagues,E6 providing redundant safety assurance and constituting an effective strategy for ensuring long-term success.

Postoperative Management and PD Resumption

Postoperative PD cessation for 2 to 4 weeks is recommended to allow adequate tissue healing.E4 Resumption of PD should follow a gradual strategy, starting with low volumes (1.0-1.5 L) and daytime exchanges, with the patient maintaining an upright or semirecumbent position as much as possible to reduce the impact of intra-abdominal pressure on the diaphragm.E18 Close monitoring for pleural effusion via ultrasound is also necessary.E19 In this case, PD was suspended for 1 month postoperatively, and the gradual resumption strategy (low-volume upright PD with clinical monitoring) successfully prevented recurrence at 3-month follow-up (see Case Report for detailed criteria).

Implications and Limitations

The particularity of this case lies in establishing the diagnosis through systematic delayed CT peritoneography and using ICG fluorescence technology to rapidly and precisely identify minute fistula sites that were occult under traditional white light during surgery, thereby achieving minimally invasive, precise, and curative repair. The limitations of this report include its single-institution case report design and its small sample size. The optimal dosage, infusion volume, and observation timing for ICG fluorescence imaging require larger sample studies for standardization.E8 The surgical procedure is demonstrated in Video 1.E11

Conclusions

A pleuroperitoneal fistula is a serious complication of peritoneal dialysis, often leading to treatment interruption. Successful treatment relies on accurate preoperative diagnosis through pleural fluid biochemistry and imaging (particularly delayed CT peritoneography), as well as precise intraoperative identification and thorough repair of all diaphragmatic defects using advanced techniques such as ICG near-infrared fluorescence thoracoscopy. Direct suturing under VATS combined with multilayer material reinforcement constitutes a safe, effective, and curative option that allows patients to return to PD.2,5,E4,E13 Future prospective studies and standardized protocols are needed to further optimize the diagnostic and therapeutic pathway for this rare complication.
