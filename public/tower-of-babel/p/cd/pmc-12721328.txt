DNA extrusion size determines pathway choice during CAG repeat expansion
Authors: Mayuri Bhatia, Ashutosh S Phadte, Anna Lakhina, Anthony R Monte Carlo III, Sarah Barndt, Anna Pluciennik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA triplet repeat expansion causes several primarly neurological disorders like Huntington’s disease, myotonic dystrophy type 1, and fragile-X related disorders. There is general consensus that recognition of extrahelical extrusions or hairpin-loop structures (formed by strand slippage) by the DNA mismatch repair protein MutSβ leads to repeat expansion by a mutagenic process. By contrast, the FAN1 nuclease attenuates triplet repeat expansion, the molecular basis of which was explained by our recent finding that FAN1 nuclease cleaves and initiates removal of extrahelical extrusions. Here we show that extrusions containing two or more triplet repeats are subject to recognition and processing by either FAN1 or MutSβ. However, extrusions containing a single triplet escape FAN1 cleavage and are preferentially processed by a MutSβ-dependent process, leading to repeat expansion. Thus, extrahelical extrusion size determines the ultimate fate of the repeat element, the protective role of FAN1 being limited to removal of extrusions containing two or more triplets. Therefore, repeat expansion is a net consequence of MutSβ-dependent processing of single triplet extrusions and competition between MutSβ and FAN1 for extrusions containing two or more triplets. These findings provide new insights into the role of DNA structural dynamics in determining pathway choice in DNArepair.

Introduction

Tandem DNA repeats are found throughout the genome, and are polymorphic in length due to their genetic instability [1]. These repeat length changes are triggered by the formation of transient unusual DNA structures (extrahelical extrusions) during DNA metabolic processes [2–4]. The detrimental consequences of repeat instability are exemplified by triplet repeat expansions that cause a number of primarily neurological diseases such as Huntington’s disease (HD), myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), and fragile-X related disorders (FXDs) [5, 6]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in HD patients have identified several genetic modifiers of disease onset age [7–16] and somatic CAG expansion in blood [14, 16] that map to DNA repair pathways. These include FAN1 [17] and the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes MSH3, PMS1, PMS2, MLH3, and MLH1 (reviewed in [18–20]). Of these, MSH3, MLH1, MLH3, and PMS1 have been implicated as drivers of somatic triplet repeat expansion [11, 21–33], a counterintuitive finding in light of the critical function of the MMR pathway in mutation avoidance. By contrast, the FAN1 nuclease attenuates repeat expansion [12, 33–35]. Thus, the FAN1 and MMR pathways exert opposing effects on repeat expansion. However, the molecular mechanisms that govern which pathway dominates are not well understood.

There is a broad consensus that the mutagenic role of MMR is triggered by recognition of triplet repeat extrahelical extrusions by the MutSβ heterodimer (MSH2/MSH3) [20, 33, 36], a protein known to be involved in rectification of insertion/deletion loops of 2–10 nucleotides (nt) [37–39]. Repeat expansion also requires the MutL homologs MLH1, MLH3, and PMS1, suggesting a role for the MutLγ (MLH1/MLH3) and MutLβ (MLH1/PMS1) heterodimers in this process. PMS2 [a subunit of MutLα (MLH1/PMS2) heterodimer] has shown mixed effects in different systems on the somatic expansion phenotype, and thus requires further investigation [26, 27, 33, 40]. Recently, we and others have shown that extrahelical extrusions can also be recognized by the FAN1 nuclease [41, 42], an enzyme that possesses a 5′ flap endonuclease and 5′ to 3′ exonuclease activities, and was previously known primarily for its role in repair of DNA interstrand crosslinks [43–47]. FAN1 endo- and exonucleolytic cleavage of CAG and CTG extrusions requires DNA-loaded proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), resulting in the removal of such structures by a short patch excision/repair process [41]. FAN1 action in this manner is highly strand-specific, with activity restricted by the orientation of DNA-loaded PCNA to the strand that contains an extrusion. This reaction requires a physical interaction between FAN1 and PCNA, the nature of which has been revealed by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the FAN1–PCNA–DNA ternary complex [48, 49]. Disruption of the FAN1–PCNA interaction by peptide inhibitors or by mutation of the PCNA-binding interface of FAN1 results in abrogation of PCNA-activated FAN1 cleavage of extrahelical extrusions.

As noted above, triplet repeat expansion is a net outcome of opposing interplay between the FAN1 and MMR pathways. It has been suggested that a physical interaction between FAN1 and MLH1 may inhibit CAG repeat expansion by sequestering MLH1 heterodimers, thereby depriving the MutSβ-dependent MMR machinery of a key catalytic component [50, 51]. Based on biochemical findings, an alternative (and mutually nonexclusive) model posits that competition between FAN1 and MutSβ for CAG extrahelical extrusions might determine this pathway choice [41, 52]. It is therefore plausible that relative protein levels of FAN1 and MutSβ at the relevant DNA loci may dictate which pathway dominates.

Because DNA strand-slippage and misalignment within triplet repeat tracts can result in extrahelical extrusions of varying sizes, we evaluated the effects of extrusion size on FAN1 nuclease activity. We show that FAN1 nuclease displays a strong preference for cleaving extrusions of 2–3 repeats over a single triplet extrusion. This effect correlates with the propensity of the extrusion to induce a kink in the DNA, with a bend angle of >90° being conducive for FAN1 cleavage. By contrast, MutSβ shows no preference for (CAG)2, 3 over (CAG)1, and supports MutLα incision on these substrates with comparable facility. Our observations imply that (CAG)1 extrusions within triplet repeats preferentially provoke MutSβ-mediated MMR, and escape removal by FAN1, thereby leading to expansions.

Materials and methods

Proteins and DNA substrates

Human recombinant full-length FAN1 or its catalytic mutant (D960A) were purified from Escherichia coli cells as described previously [41]. Recombinant human proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) was purified from E. coli harboring plasmid pET11a-PCNA as described [48]. Human replication factor C (RFC), MutLα, and MutSβ were purified from baculovirus infected Sf9 cells according to published protocols [38, 53, 54].

The DNA substrates harboring (CAG)1, 2, or 3 extrahelical extrusions were prepared as described [36], using phagemid constructs that harbor sequences bounding the CTG repeat within the 3′-untranslated region of the human DMPK gene (19 nt 5′ and 40 nt 3′). Oligonucleotide-based DNA substrates were prepared by annealing of HPLC-purified complementary oligonucleotides (Integrated DNA Technologies) (Supplementary Table 1). The annealed substrates were run on 10% polyacrylamide gels in 1× Tris-Borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer to confirm the annealing efficiency. The double-stranded substrates were then radiolabeled using [α-32P] d(GTP) and exo− Klenow at the 3′ end on the extrusion harboring strand (see Fig. 1). For the 5′ radiolabeled DNA substrates, DNA oligonucleotides were labeled with [γ-32P] (ATP) and T4 polynucleotide kinase, followed by annealing with appropriate complementary strands to form double stranded DNAs harboring an extrusion or homoduplex controls. All oligonucleotide sequences used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Results

Extrahelical extrusion size governs FAN1 nuclease activity

DNA extrahelical extrusions are formed within repetitive sequences via slippage of the two complementary strands during DNA metabolic processes [57–59]. Our recent studies have uncovered a novel role for FAN1 in limiting triplet repeat expansion by removal of (CAG)2 or (CTG)2 extrahelical extrusions that form within CAG/CTG repeat tracts associated with several neurodegenerative diseases [41]. The nuclease activity of FAN1 is provoked by such extrahelical extrusions, as judged by the inertness of the enzyme on homoduplex DNAs [41]. However, structural features of the extrusions (such as size or sequence composition) that govern FAN1 nuclease function on such DNAs are not well understood. Therefore, in order to evaluate the effect of the extrusion size within the DNA substrate on FAN1 nuclease activity, we constructed DNA substrates with varying numbers of CAG or CTG repeats forming the extrusion (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 13 CAG-repeats within the extrusion as shown in Supplementary Table 1). The DNA substrates were radiolabeled at the 3′ end and evaluated in FAN1 nuclease assays at low ionic strength (70 mM KCl) (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section) as described previously [41]. We observed that DNA substrates harboring (CAG)2 or (CAG)3 extrusions are cleaved efficiently even at low nanomolar concentrations of FAN1 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), whereas a catalytic mutant of FAN1 (D960A) [45, 47] purified using the same method does not display detectable activity (Supplementary Fig. 1G and H). In comparison, (CAG)1 extrusions are poor substrates for FAN1, with substantially higher amounts of the enzyme required to achieve similar levels of cleavage on (CAG)1 relative to (CAG)2 or (CAG)3 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). This effect is recapitulated on DNAs harboring (CTG) extrusions (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, FAN1 displays substantially lower levels of activity on (CAG)13 or (CTG)13 relative to extrusions containing two repeats (Fig. 1C and F). These observations indicate that the FAN1 nuclease activity is exquisitely sensitive to the size of the extrusion.

In order to further evaluate FAN1 nuclease activity on these DNA substrates, we performed time course experiments (Fig. 2A–C and Supplementary Figs 2 and 3A–F). The observed rates of initiation of FAN1 cleavage under these experimental conditions displayed a ∼2.5-fold preference for (CAG)2 (0.005 s−1) in comparison to (CAG)3,5 (∼0.002 s−1); and a ∼5-fold preference in comparison to (CAG)4, 13 (0.001 s−1). Although the interval (1 min) for measurement of initial rates was not sufficient to distinquish FAN1 preference for (CAG)3 versus (CAG)5 or (CAG)4 versus (CAG)13, the extents of cleavage at saturation indicated the following order of preference (CAG)2>(CAG)3>(CAG)4, 5, 13>(CAG)1. DNAs harboring a (CAG)1 extrusion are poor substrates for FAN1 nuclease: cleavage is below detection limits within the first minute of the reaction. It should be noted that a homoduplex DNA substrate is refractory to FAN1 cleavage (Fig. 2B and C, and Supplementary Fig. 2F) in line with our previously published work [41, 48].

The differences between the observed rates of enzymatic cleavage may be attributed at least in part to the binding preference of FAN1 for these substrates. Surface plasmon resonance experiments (SPRS) clearly established that FAN1 preferentially binds (CAG)2 over all other substrates, with limited binding observed for (CAG)1 (Fig. 2D–G, Supplementary Fig. 3G, and Supplementary Table 4). It should be noted that substantial levels of binding of FAN1 to homoduplex DNA were detected (Supplementary Fig. 3G); nevertheless, as we have noted previously [48], the conformation of the homoduplex is likely not conducive to catalysis (see below).

FAN1 cleavage follows a similar pattern on CTG-harboring extrusions, with substrate preference in the following order: (CTG)3 (0.0034 s−1) > (CTG)2 (0.0025 s−1) > (CTG)4 (0.0014 s−1) > (CTG)5 (0.001 s−1) > (CTG)13 (0.0007 s−1) > (CTG)1 (0.0006 s−1) (Fig. 2C).

We then sought to determine the sites of FAN1 nuclease cleavage. In order to distinguish between the first endonuclease cut and subsequent incisions, we performed FAN1 nuclease assays using a (CAG)2 DNA substrate labeled on the 5′ end of the extrusion- containing strand. Denaturing PAGE sizing analysis of the products of FAN1 hydrolysis indicate that the primary endonuclease cleavage sites are located at positions 27 and 28 (1 and 2 nt past the extrusion on the 3′ side, as measured from the 5′ end) (Fig. 3A), with no endonucleolytic cleavage on homoduplex control (Supplementary Fig. 4A). We also observed a nonspecific exonucleolytic cleavage from the 5′ DNA end on both DNA strands (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary Fig. 4A and B), an activity that was observed previously to yield a 2–4 nt product [45]. Time course experiments using a 3′ labeled DNA substrate revealed cleavage products at positions 18 and 19 as measured from the 3′ end (and in agreement with the 5′ labeled DNA measurements). We also observed subsequent nucleolytic cleavage sites at positions 15 and 13, followed by cuts after every nucleotide, with the cleavage mostly terminating upon removal of 10–12 nt (Fig. 2A). This suggests an exonucleolytic mechanism that ensues following early endonucleolytic incisions.

In order to further understand the interdependence of the FAN1 endo- and exonuclease cleavage reactions, we prepared a DNA substrate that contains a (CAG)2 extrusion and a nick 2 nt past the extrusion on the 3′ side to mimic the DNA intermediate post-endonucleolytic cleavage (Fig. 3B and C). The observed initial rates of cleavage on this DNA substrate are 0.01 s−1 [∼2-fold faster than for the corresponding (CAG)2 substrate that does not harbor a nick; Supplementary Fig. 4C], suggesting that the initial incision by FAN1 might modestly limit the rate of the overall reaction. We also observed exonucleolytic cleavage on nick-harboring homoduplex control (Supplementary Fig. 4D) consistent with previous observations [60], suggesting that nick-directed exonucleolytic cleavage is extrusion independent. The requirement for the extrusion for the initial endonucleolytic incision event implies that this catalytic step is rate limiting for the overall processing of extrahelical extrusions by FAN1.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 3D and E, FAN1 nuclease cleavage of (CAG)2 extrusions occurs in a distributive manner, since challenge of the reaction with an excess of nonradiolabeled DNA substrate quenched the formation of additional cleavage products. This observed distributive behavior of the FAN1 nuclease on (CAG)2 extrusions has been reported previously for other DNA substrates [42, 60].

FAN1 cleaves (CAG)2 extrusions within CTG/CAG triplet repeat tracts

As mentioned above, extrahelical extrusions are formed due to strand slippage within repetitive sequences during cellular events that involve DNA helix opening and re-annealing. The experiments described above were focused on the effect of the DNA structure per se (size and sequence composition of the extrahelical extrusion) within a nonrepetitive sequence context. To better mimic the natural context of the formation of CAG extrusions, we constructed a heteroduplex substrate in which the CAG extrusion is within a CTG/CAG repeat tract. Fig. 4A and B, and Supplementary Fig. 5A demonstrate that the heteroduplex (CTG)11/(CAG)13 (harboring two extra CAG repeats on one DNA strand) is an excellent substrate for the FAN1 nuclease. Unlike the substrates used in Figs 1 and 2, wherein the position of the extrusion is fixed within the overall sequence context, the extrusion within the (CTG)11/(CAG)13 sequence can form anywhere within the span of the repeat tract. While it is possible that the two extrahelical repeats exist as distinct and separate (CAG)1 extrusions, there is evidence suggesting that these extrahelical bases may be accommodated within a single (CAG)2 extrusion [61]. Indeed, the observed initial rates of FAN1 nuclease activity on this DNA substrate were indistinguishable (∼0.005 s−1) from the DNA substrate harboring a (CAG)2 extrusion in the context of a nonrepetitive DNA sequence (Fig. 4A and B and Supplementary Fig. 5B). These data support the idea that the FAN1 nuclease targets the extrahelical extrusion itself rather than recognizing a specific sequence. However, unlike for the (CAG)2 substrate where we observed distinct cleavage sites (Fig. 2A), the FAN1 cleavage on (CTG)11/(CAG)13 occurs throughout the DNA repeat tract (Fig. 4A). This was also evident when we evaluated the FAN1 endonucleolytic cleavage using a 5′ labeled (CTG)11/(CAG)13 substrate: incisions occur throughout the CAG tract (Fig. 4C and D) [unlike the (CAG)2 substrate where two distinct endonucleolytic cleavage sites were observed (Fig. 3A)]. The simplest explanation for this observation is that the (CTG)11/(CAG)13 substrate is composed of a population of DNA molecules wherein the CAG extrusion exists at different loci within the repetitive tract. It is also possible that the extrusion migrates within the repetitive tract [62]. In either scenario, FAN1 activity on such DNA substrates would result in multiple cleavage products in this bulk measurement. FAN1 shows minimal cleavage on the fully paired homoduplex control containing (CTG)13/(CAG)13 repeat tract (Supplementary Fig. 5C).

DNA extrahelical extrusions induce DNA bending

Our FAN1 nuclease assays have revealed a strong preference of the FAN1 nuclease for (CAG)2,3 relative to (CAG)1 or homoduplex (Figs 1–3). We hypothesized that these preferences may be governed by DNA conformational features unique to (CAG)2,3 versus (CAG)1. We therefore used AlphaFold [63] to model conformations adopted by these DNAs. As shown in Fig. 5A, AlphaFold predicts that the extrahelical extrusion would induce a kink in the DNA with the degree of bend correlating with the size of the extrusion. Furthermore, two distinct classes of bent molecules are observed: extrusions containing C, CA, or (CAG)1 cause a modest bend of <45°, whereas (CAG)2 and (CAG)3 result in a significant kink (>90°) of the helical axis (modelling of DNAs harboring >3 CAGs showed no further increase in the bend angle). Because bent DNAs display reduced electrophoretic mobility relative to corresponding linear DNAs [64–67], we experimentally tested the AlphaFold predictions by assessing whether extrahelical extrusions cause electrophoretic retardation. As shown in Fig. 5B, our experiments reveal that first, electrophoretic retardation is a function of the location of the extrusion within the duplex such that extrusions located in the center of the molecule result in slower mobility relative to extrusions located closer to the DNA end. Since differential placement of the extrusion does not alter the molecular weight of these DNAs, effects on electrophoretic retardation may be attributed solely to the conformational features of these molecules. Second, we find that the size of the extrusion governs the extent of gel retardation, with the greatest degree of retardation observed for (CAG)2 and (CAG)3. Interestingly, extrusions containing C, CA, or (CAG)1 do not display appreciable gel retardation under these experimental conditions, consistent with the relative linearity of these molecules as per the AlphaFold predictions. Based on these findings, we propose that an extrusion-induced DNA bend of >90° is a prerequisite for efficient recognition and cleavage by the FAN1 nuclease. This is in line with our recent cryo-EM structures of the PCNA-DNA-FAN1 complex wherein the FAN1 nuclease has an “L-shaped” conformation that can readily accommodate a (CAG)2 extrusion-harboring DNA that is bent by ∼100° [48].

Extrahelical extrusions are efficiently bound by MutSβ

A growing body of evidence suggests that triplet repeat expansions are controlled by two opposing pathways: whereas FAN1 prevents repeat expansions, MutSβ (a heterodimer of MSH2/MSH3 that recognizes insertion/deletion loops [19]) drives this process [22, 33, 34]. Our recent studies have shown that FAN1 can compete with MutSβ for binding to (CAG)2 extrahelical extrusions, and in the absence of MutSβ can efficiently mediate their removal [41]. We have therefore proposed that this competition for CAG extrusion binding may underly pathway choice between MutSβ-driven CAG expansion, and FAN1-dependent CAG stabilization. To further dissect the molecular basis of this pathway choice, we asked whether MutSβ (like FAN1) has a binding preference for (CAG)2 over (CAG)1. SPRS sensorgrams for time-dependent association and dissociation of MutSβ with (CAG)1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 13 and a homoduplex control (Fig. 6) were analyzed by global fit to a 1:1 binding model [according to Eq (3) in see the ‘Materials and methods’ section]. The on rate/off rate correlation plot (Fig. 6C), and steady-state isotherm (Fig. 6D) established that (i) binding of MutSβ to (CAG)1, (CAG)2, and (CAG)3 is significantly tighter than to the homoduplex control (in agreement with our previous findings [36]), and (ii) MutSβ shows no significant preference for (CAG)2 over (CAG)1 or (CAG)3. Further, MutSβ displays a ∼8-fold higher affinity for (CAG)1 (KD = 0.5 0.1 nM), (CAG)2 (KD = 0.5 0.16 nM), and ∼3.5-fold higher for (CAG)3 (KD = 1.2 0.4 nM) over (CAG)13 (KD = 4.2 1.5 nM). Under these conditions, (CAG)4 and (CAG)5 were found to be poor substrates for MutSβ (consistent with previous findings on lack of MutSβ dependence of repair of extrusions larger than 12 nt [38, 68, 69]), since binding was not saturable up to a concentation of 100 nM protein.

Substrate preferences for PCNA-RFC-dependent FAN1 and MutSβ-dependent MutLα nuclease reactions

The physical and functional interactions between DNA and MutSβ or FAN1 described above were evaluated in simple binary reactions. However, we have previously shown that at physiological ionic strength (125 mM KCl) (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section), activation of the FAN1 nuclease on circular heteroduplexes harboring a (CAG)2 extrusion requires interaction between FAN1 and DNA-loaded PCNA (a process catalyzed by the RFC clamp loader) [41, 48]. For these experiments we used a circular DNA harboring a nick to enable efficient PCNA loading by RFC. As shown in Fig. 7A, PCNA-activated FAN1 efficiently cleaves DNAs harboring (CAG)2 or (CAG)3 (appearance of the faster migrating band), but not a (CAG)1 extrusion [no detectable cleavage on the complementary DNA strand was observed (Fig. 7C)]. Thus, the preference for (CAG)2 or (CAG)3 over (CAG)1 is an intrinsic feature of the FAN1 nuclease.

By contrast, MutSβ did not display binding preference between (CAG)1, (CAG)2, and (CAG)3 (Fig. 6). Because extrusion-bound MutSβ initiates DNA MMR by activating the MutLα endonuclease on nicked circular heteroduplexes in a RFC and PCNA-dependent manner [39, 41], we used this reaction as an indicator of initiation of MutSβ-dependent repair of (CAG)1, (CAG)2, and (CAG)3 extrusions. Unlike FAN1, MutSβ-dependent MutLα endonuclease activity occurred with comparable facility on all three extrusions (appearance of a smear) (Fig. 7B). No cleavage was detected on the complementary DNA strand (Fig. 7D). Thus, taken together with the FAN1 nuclease and DNA binding assays, these results suggest that DNA substrates harboring a (CAG)1 extrusion may be preferentially processed by a MutSβ-dependent pathway, whereas (CAG)2, 3 and (CAG)13 are likely subject to competition between FAN1 and MutSβ.

Discussion

Triplet repeat expansion is a net consequence of the interplay between opposing pathways: whereas MutSβ initiated mismatch processing drives repeat expansion, a FAN1-dependent mechanism constrains this process [22, 33, 34]. We have demonstrated that a plausible molecular explanation for the role of FAN1 in attenuating repeat expansion is the ability of the FAN1 nuclease to remove extrahelical extrusions formed within triplet repeat tracts via a pathway that relies on short-patch DNA excision/resynthesis [41]. Because extrahelical extrusions can also be processed by a MutSβ-dependent pathway [36, 41], we have proposed that the competition between FAN1 and MutSβ for such extrusions could determine the choice between repeat stability versus instability. Indeed, relative molar stoichiometries of FAN1 and MutSβ are key determinants that drive extrusion processing in vitro [41]. However, DNA strand mishybridization within repetitive sequences can lead to the formation of a variety of extrahelical extrusions and hairpin-loop structures [2–4, 70–72]. Could it be that the size of the extrusion plays a role in dictating pathway choice?

Therefore, we sought to determine the preferred DNA extrahelical extrusion sizes for processing by FAN1 versus MutSβ. Interestingly, we observed that whereas extrusions of 2–3 repeats efficiently provoke FAN1 nuclease activity, extrusions of one triplet repeat are largely refractory to cleavage by FAN1. This nucleolytic cleavage preference can be explained (at least in part) by the preferential binding of FAN1 to these structures: FAN1 binds to (CAG)2, 3 extrusions efficiently, but very poorly to (CAG)1 extrusions and does not reach saturation (Fig. 2G). By contrast, MutSβ binding is largely agnostic to the size of the extrahelical extrusion; the protein binds with a similar high affinity to extrusions composed of one, two, or three repeats (Fig. 6). We have previously shown that at physiological ionic strength, FAN1 is activated by the presence of DNA-loaded PCNA on a DNA substrate harboring a (CAG)2 or (CTG)2 extrusion (Fig. 7A and [41]). Under these conditions, (CAG)1 is refractory to PCNA-activated FAN1 cleavage (Fig. 7A). By contrast, MutSβ-dependent MutLα endonuclease in the presence of DNA-loaded PCNA processes (CAG)1, (CAG)2, and (CAG)3 with comparable facility. Thus, the overwhelming preference of FAN1 for extrusions containing two or three repeats (over a one-repeat extrusion) is consistent across nuclease assay conditions and binding studies [49]. Likewise, the lack of preference of MutSβ for (CAG)1, 2, or 3 is evident in binding studies as well as MutSβ-dependent MutLα nuclease assays. These observations highlight the fact that extrusion size could be a key driver of pathway choice. It is noteworthy in this regard that the most common length change event in the genome from blood [16], or non-neuronal brain cells of individuals with HD is an expansion of one repeat [52, 73]. Moreover, Handsaker et al. [73] also point out that the large expansions observed in the neurons of individuals with HD can be explained by length change events of one repeat. Likewise, large expansions observed in the blood DNA of individuals with myotonic dystrophy type 1 can also be explained by length change events of one repeat [74, 75]. Similar observations of expansion in single triplet increments have also been documented in animal models of HD and DM1 [22, 76]. Therefore, our findings imply that whereas extrusions containing two or more triplets do not necessarily lead to expansion (due to their efficient removal by FAN1), single triplet (3 nt) extrusions escape removal by FAN1 thereby predisposing them to expansion. Interestingly, hairpin-forming extrusions have also been proposed as intermediates in the expansion process [42, 77]. However, we find that (CAG)13 extrusions are not only poor substrates for FAN1 binding and nucleolytic cleavage in our in vitro studies, but also are bound by MutSβ with a ∼3.5 to 8-fold lower affinity than (CAG)1, (CAG)2, and (CAG)3. Therefore, the role of such hairpin structures in driving pathway choice is unclear.

What might be the molecular basis of the FAN1 binding specificities reported here? A clue comes from our recent cryo-EM studies of the FAN1–PCNA–DNA ternary complex wherein the DNA is bent at an outer angle of ∼102° at the (CAG)2 extrusion site, thus positioning the extrusion appropriately for catalysis [48]. Given that the overall architecture of the FAN1 protein tracks with the DNA bend [48, 49, 60], we hypothesize that the bend angle of the DNA is a key determinant that facilitates efficient FAN1 catalysis. AlphaFold modelling and gel retardation studies indicate that (CAG)1 extrusions induce a very modest kink (∼40°) in the DNA helix, as compared with a significant (>90°) kink for (CAG)2 or (CAG)3. Thus, the intrinsic bendability of the DNA at the extrusion site likely facilitates cleavage by FAN1. Indeed, the crystal structure of FAN1 bound to its bona fide double fork substrate is characterized by a DNA outer angle bend of ∼104° at the strand break [60]. Since FAN1 can bind to homoduplex DNA to an appreciable degree at sufficiently high concentrations [48], why such molecules are nevertheless refractory to FAN1 cleavage has remained a question. This resistance to cleavage by FAN1 could be explained by the lack of an intrinsic bend in homoduplex DNAs. It is therefore tempting to speculate that FAN1 may be a general sensor of DNA lesions as well as unusual DNA conformations that produce a strong kink in the DNA duplex. We are currently investigating these questions.

The overarching goal of our investigations is to enhance our understanding of the protein and nucleic acid factors that drive DNA repeat expansions in human disease. There is general consensus that extrahelical extrusions formed by slippage mediated misalignment of complementary triplet repeat-containing DNA strands are key intermediates in the expansion process. Repeat-containing DNA duplexes can transiently undergo strand separation and reannealing during DNA metabolic processes such as repair and transcription [78], leading to formation of extrahelical extrusions. Additionally, DNA strand separation (and hence strand misalignment) can also be caused by release of accumulated negative superhelical tension either upon nucleosome disassembly or in the wake of a translocating RNA polymerase [79–81]. Such events would result in the formation of complementary extrahelical extrusions on both DNA strands; in principle, these complementary extrusions could contain one, two, or more repeats.

Our observations lend themselves to a model shown in Fig. 8 which integrates current data with previously proposed models [20, 27, 33, 40], such that (i) single repeat extrusions formed by strand slippage within long CAG/CTG repeats in nondividing cells are preferentially processed by a mutagenic MutSβ-dependent pathway (one that is distinct from the mutation avoidance activity of postreplicative MMR in dividing cells). Accordingly, recognition of the extrusion by MutSβ is followed by recruitment of one or more of the known MutL homologs—MutLγ, MutLβ, and MutLα. Although the exact function of these MutL heterodimers in this process is not clear (evidence for involvement of each of them is available) [27, 32, 33, 40, 82], it is thought that MutL catalyzed incisions serve as initiation points for DNA polymerase δ mediated repair DNA synthesis [33], resulting in a net one repeat expansion. If the finding that one repeat extrusions escape removal by FAN1 is recapitulated in vivo, it would imply that therapeutic approaches involving FAN1 activation or augmentation may have limited effect on repeat expansion. In such a scenario, (CAG)1 extrusions will continue to expand by the MutSβ pathway and if somatic expansion is to be halted entirely, it may require an MSH3 downregulating therapeutic agent.

Model for triplet repeat expansion. (A) Strand slippage within long triplet repeat tracts results in the formation of extrahelical extrusions containing one or two and more triplet repeats. The extrusions form on both DNA strands, and if far apart, are likely to be processed independently. (i) Extrusions of one repeat are preferentially recognized by MutSβ, leading to recruitment of MutLγ (and potentially other MutL homologs like MutLβ, and MutLα). MutLγ -catalyzed incisions (pink) opposite to the extrusions or MutLα incisions on either DNA strand (blue) provide entry points for Polδ-mediated repair DNA synthesis (shown in yellow). Completion of the repair process leads to the inclusion of the extrusions on both strands, thus resulting in a net expansion of the tract by one repeat. Extrusions of ≥2 triplets can be processed in multiple ways. (ii) Processing of the extrusions on both DNA strands by the MutSβ-dependent pathway leads to inclusion of both extrusions (as shown for a single repeat extrusion), resulting in a net expansion of ≥2 repeats. (iii) In the event that the extrusion on one strand is processed by the MutSβ pathway, but the extrusion on the complementary strand is subject to removal by a process initiated by PCNA-activated FAN1 nuclease, there is no net change in repeat length. (iv) Exclusive processing of such larger extrusions by FAN1 would result in repeat contractions, although available data suggests that such events are unlikely. (B) Extrusions [as in panel (A)] or oxidative DNA damage could also trigger an incision/excision process wherein strand slippage during DNA repair resynthesis by Polδ leads to formation of new extrahelical extrusions, which either result in a net increase in CAG repeat length (if processed by MutSβ-dependent pathway) or no change in repeat length (if processed by FAN1-dependent pathway). In this scenario, the pathway choice is driven by extrusion size and competition between MutSβ and FAN1 [as shown in panel (A)].

On the other hand, extrusions containing two or three repeats can be processed by either pathway depending on relative protein stoichiometry. Thus, under cellular conditions of spatio-temporal abundance of MutSβ-associated DNA MMR proteins (ii), extrusions harboring two or three repeats on both DNA strands could be processed by the MutSβ pathway on both strands, resulting in expansion. However, under more competitive stoichiometric conditions (iii), MutSβ and FAN1 compete for such extrusions, resulting in the extrusion on one strand being processed by MutSβ, with the one on the other strand removed by the FAN1 pathway. As a result, there is no net change in repeat length. Moreover, in light of previous findings that expansion rates increase in the absence of FAN1, our results imply that extrusions containing two or more repeats are likely to form with a relatively high frequency in vivo. In another scenario (iv), where FAN1 is predominant (e.g. upon downregulation of MutSβ), processing of such extrusions on both DNA strands would be expected to result in repeat contraction. However, genetic ablation of MutSβ in mouse models of HD wherein MutSβ does not result in appreciable repeat tract contractions, suggesting that the scenario presented in (iv) is less likely.

We have considered various ways complementary extrusions that form within resting DNA can be processed. However, extrahelical extrusions can also form on one strand by DNA polymerase slippage during repair DNA synthesis of pre-existing extrahelical extrusions or lesions resulting from oxidative DNA damage (Fig. 8B). Such extrusions can lead to MutSβ-mediated repeat expansion, unless removed by the FAN1 pathway. Given the substrate size preferences of FAN1, it is tempting to envision a role for FAN1 in maintaining the genetic stability not only of other triplet repeat sequences but also of tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats, a line of investigation that we are actively pursuing.

In summary, our work provides the first mechanistic explanation for why triplet repeat expansion in human blood and brain tissue occur in 1 repeat increments [16, 73], and uncovers a key role for DNA conformational dynamics in determining pathway choice in DNA repair.
