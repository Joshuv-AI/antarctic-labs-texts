Beyond accommodation: institutional and policy drivers of healthcare equity for signing deaf adults
Authors: Sarah M Hall, Michael Ballard, Adrianna Watson, Kelton Couperus, Tasha Anderson, Ellie Gardner, Alexis Boseman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeaf and hard-of-hearing individuals who use a signed language experience persistent inequities in healthcare access, communication, and outcomes. Although federal accessibility policies mandate effective communication, implementation remains inconsistent and signing Deaf patients continue to report barriers that compromise safety, autonomy, and trust. Existing literature documents communication breakdowns but offers limited qualitative insight into how institutional structures, administrative practices, and policy implementation processes shape Deaf patients’ healthcare experiences across the continuum of care.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study examined healthcare experiences of culturally Deaf signing adults in the United States using a socioecological analytic framework. Participants were recruited through Deaf-oriented social media groups in Utah. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who self-identified as culturally Deaf and were proficient in American Sign Language (ASL). Data were collected through ASL–interpreted focus groups and individual interviews. Transcripts were verified for accuracy and analyzed using iterative deductive and inductive thematic analysis in NVivo with attention to institutional and policy-level influences on healthcare access.ResultsParticipants described a healthcare system in which effective communication access was inconsistent and frequently required Deaf patients to self-advocate for accommodations. Findings clustered into three interrelated domains: (1) influencing factors, including policy ambiguity, economic constraints, patient knowledge, and advocacy burden; (2) institutional barriers, including capacity limitations, technological and environmental constraints, personnel knowledge and attitudes, restrictive interpreter contracting practices, fragmented scheduling and record-keeping systems, unclear roles and responsibilities, and compliance-oriented institutional culture; and (3) effects on Deaf patients’ lives. Healthcare-specific effects included reduced autonomy, compromised privacy, delayed or inadequate care, healthcare avoidance, trust erosion, and poorer health outcomes. Broader impacts included emotional and mental stress, financial strain, relationship burden, and reliance on litigation or geographic avoidance to secure accessible care.ConclusionsDespite longstanding accessibility mandates, signing Deaf patients continue to experience structurally produced inequities driven by inconsistent institutional implementation. Communication access functions as a core patient safety and equity mechanism rather than a discretionary accommodation. Background

Deaf identity and language context

Addressing inequities in healthcare is an essential area of practice in public health and medicine. One population that remains comparatively understudied is Deaf1 sign language users, whose experiences are shaped not only by communication differences but also by systemic barriers within healthcare systems. In the United States, American Sign Language (ASL) is an important component of sociocultural identity for many Deaf individuals [1]. ASL is a complete language with its own distinct grammar and structure, comparable to spoken languages, while also incorporating unique features such as the use of physical space to establish referents, the ability to convey multiple pieces of information simultaneously, and the use of visual iconicity within vocabulary and structure [2–4]. An estimated 100,000 to 1 million people in the United States use ASL as their primary language [5]. Deaf adults who use a signed language should be understood as a cultural and linguistic minority whose healthcare experiences are often shaped by systemic inequities rather than by hearing status alone [6].

Structural drivers of healthcare inequity

Deaf people who use a signed language experience persistent inequities in healthcare access and outcomes that are increasingly understood as structurally produced rather than inherent to deafness [6, 7]. Prior research has identified several underlying drivers of these inequities, including exclusionary data collection methods, communication barriers in clinical settings, and elevated rates of low health literacy stemming from inaccessible communication about family health history, medical terminology, mass media, and healthcare messaging [5, 8]. These barriers are not isolated to individual encounters but are embedded within broader health system practices. Across settings, institutional failures to ensure effective communication can compromise informed consent, patient safety, privacy, and trust and may contribute to disengagement from care [6, 7]. Importantly, these communication barriers function as patient safety risks rather than solely as access concerns. Although multiple interventions have been proposed, their impact has been limited by inconsistent implementation, suggesting that inequities are driven less by a lack of solutions and more by gaps in organizational policy, administrative practices, and accountability structures [9].

Access barriers and intervention gaps

Evidence syntheses further demonstrate that access barriers are widespread and associated with downstream harms. A scoping review of Deaf sign language users’ experiences highlights recurring challenges, including unreliable access to qualified interpreters, inappropriate substitution of written notes or lipreading, and clinical environments that are not conducive to visual communication [6]. These barriers are associated with reduced understanding of diagnoses and care plans, erosion of trust, compromised privacy, reduced autonomy, and avoidance or delayed use of services [6]. Converging findings from reviews focused on Deaf and hard-of-hearing patients similarly emphasize that communication breakdowns contribute to misinformation, disempowerment, dissatisfaction, and potentially serious safety consequences, reinforcing that inequities are produced by healthcare institutions’ practices, policies, and organizational cultures [7, 10].

A systematic review of interventions aimed at improving healthcare and health education equity for adult Deaf patients identifies multiple actionable approaches, including sign language interpretation, provider training, translated and validated tools, and adapted service delivery modalities, and emphasizes the importance of meaningful involvement of Deaf communities in intervention design and evaluation [9]. However, persistent access barriers across settings suggest that interventions are inconsistently embedded within organizational policy, workflow, and accountability mechanisms, often leaving Deaf patients responsible for initiating accommodations and managing communication risk [6, 9]. This pattern indicates that implementation failures are frequently driven by misalignment between policy requirements and operational practices within healthcare systems.

Implementation challenges

Implementation challenges may be amplified in high-acuity environments, where time pressure and fragmented workflows intensify the consequences of institutional barriers. Qualitative evidence from emergency department settings indicates that Deaf ASL users experience communication access requests as stressful and time-consuming, report concerns about video remote interpreting (VRI), and describe written and oral communication methods as insufficient for complex clinical interactions [11, 12]. Variability across healthcare settings and encounters suggests that access often depends on local administrative practices and discretionary decision-making rather than standardized institutional processes [11]. These findings demonstrate a persistent disconnect between recommended practices and real-world implementation.

While prior research in the United States has documented communication barriers experienced by Deaf sign language users in clinical settings [10–12], fewer studies have examined how institutional structures, administrative practices, and policy implementation processes interact to produce these barriers across the continuum of care. There remains limited qualitative evidence that centers Deaf patients’ perspectives on how accessibility policies are operationalized in routine clinical practice, including during high-risk transitions such as triage, informed consent, and discharge. Thus, the evidence base supports the need for research that examines not only interpersonal communication failures, but also the institutional structures through which accessibility policy and procedures are enacted in everyday care delivery [6, 7, 11, 12].

Policy context

In the United States, barriers and inequities persist despite more than five decades of federal policy mandating equitable healthcare access for individuals with disabilities. Federal mandates, including the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Section 504) [13], the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 [14], and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act [15], require healthcare institutions to ensure effective communication and equal participation for Deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals [16]. In this context, effective communication refers to communication that is timely, accurate, and accessible, allowing patients to participate fully in healthcare decision-making. However, persistent implementation gaps suggest that compliance with policy has not reliably translated into equitable service delivery. While the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services provides guidance through the National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services in Health and Health Care (CLAS) [17], these guidelines do not specifically acknowledge Deaf individuals as a target population [18]. This disconnect between policy and practice reinforces the need to examine how accessibility requirements are implemented at the institutional level.

Study purpose

Examining the policy, economic, and health system influences that produce healthcare inequities is critical to informing accessible healthcare design and decision-making. This study addresses gaps in prior literature by examining how institutional structures, administrative practices, and policy implementation processes shape healthcare experiences for signing Deaf patients in the United States. From a health equity perspective, these findings contribute to understanding how systemic factors influence the distribution, quality, and safety of care within a linguistically marginalized population.

Methods

Design

This study used a qualitative descriptive design employing semi-structured focus groups, supplemented by individual interviews for participants unable to attend a group session. The analytic approach was guided by the Socioecological Model (SEM) to examine barriers and solutions across socioecological levels [19], with the present analysis emphasizing institutional and policy-related influences on healthcare equity for Deaf patients.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

Data collection was co-facilitated by a hearing healthcare researcher and a Deaf Studies researcher who identifies as a member of the signing Deaf community, with doctoral-level training in qualitative health research. The Deaf Studies researcher brought insider cultural and linguistic knowledge, while the hearing researcher contributed public health and health systems expertise. Additional authors were not involved in data collection and had no direct contact with participants.

Participants were informed of the researchers’ professional backgrounds and the purpose of the study. Reflexivity was addressed throughout the study through team-based discussion of positionality, power dynamics, and potential assumptions related to deafness, disability, and healthcare systems. Analytic decisions were reviewed collaboratively to minimize individual bias and to ensure interpretations of participants’ accounts remained consistent throughout the study.

Setting and context

The study was conducted in Utah and focused on healthcare access and experiences among adult signing Deaf individuals. Recruitment and consent processes were designed to support language accessibility for ASL users.

Participants and eligibility

Eligibility criteria required participants to: (a) be aged 18 years or older, (b) reside in Utah, (c) self-identify as part of the Deaf community, and (d) be proficient in ASL. For the purposes of this study, ASL proficiency was based on participant self-report during recruitment. Participant characteristics were collected via an online recruitment survey. Demographic summaries are reported in the Results section.

Recruitment and consent

Participants were recruited using an online recruitment survey disseminated through local Deaf-interest social media groups. The informed consent process was delivered in accessible formats, including an ASL video and written materials in English. Eligible individuals who consented (N = 44) were scheduled for a focus group; those unable to attend a focus group were offered an individual interview. Seven eligible individuals did not participate due to scheduling conflicts or loss to follow-up.

The researchers facilitated focus groups and interviews, with communication access supported by two certified hearing interpreters and two Certified Deaf Interpreters (CDI). CDIs provide services to those within their own Deaf community. Unlike hearing interpreters, CDIs possess extra-linguistic knowledge based on their own Deaf experiences that may influence the interpreting process. The CDIs’ linguistic knowledge is used as a tool to mediate the interpreting process as a supplementary access point for visual language and communication. Thus, the CDIs facilitated communication between Deaf participants and the hearing researcher, providing cultural context and linguistic expertise when needed. No additional non-participants were present during data collection sessions.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected through 90-minute semi-structured focus groups and individual interviews using a guide designed to elicit (a) experiences of institutional and policy healthcare barriers and facilitators and (b) participant-recommended solutions (Appendix A). Focus groups were co-facilitated by a hearing healthcare researcher and a Deaf Studies researcher affiliated with the signing Deaf community. To ensure communication access, sessions included a professional interpreting team comprising certified hearing interpreters and CDIs. Sessions were video recorded to support accurate transcription and analytic verification. Repeat interviews were not conducted. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction. Data collection continued until the research team determined that thematic sufficiency had been reached, such that no substantively new institutional or policy-level themes emerged.

Transcription and data management

Recordings were transcribed into written English from interpreted ASL and spoken English. Participants were each assigned a number for anonymity within the written transcript. A third-party interpreting agency cross-referenced recorded ASL with the written English transcripts to support transcript accuracy. Videos and transcripts were stored on password protected laptops.

Data analysis

Data were managed using NVivo 12. During the initial coding, data were deductively sorted into nodes as informed by the SEM (intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, community, policy). During the second phase of analysis, all data within the institutional and policy nodes were reviewed and inductively sorted into three main emergent themes: influencing factors, institutional factors, and effects on the patient. Data relating to the three main themes were then inductively categorized and sorted into subthemes and developed into an initial codebook. Analysis proceeded through additional iterative cycles combining deductive and inductive approaches. Four additional researchers independently reviewed the full institutional/policy transcript and applied initial codebook codes, or proposed new codes for data that did not fit neatly into existing categories. The team met regularly to compare coding, resolve discrepancies through discussion, and refine code definitions. Subthemes were added, divided, and refined as needed. Study participants did not formally review or provide feedback on the final themes. Instead, analytic rigor was supported through iterative comparison of themes with raw data and team-based reflexive discussion.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Credibility and dependability were supported through (a) team-based independent coding and consensus refinement of code definitions, (b) iterative comparison of themes against raw transcript data, and (c) transcript accuracy verification via cross-referencing ASL recordings with written English transcripts by a third-party interpreting agency.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at a university in the Mountain West region of the United States (ref. 2026-052). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible institutional committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent using accessible ASL video and written English formats.

Results

Participant characteristics

Thirty-seven adults self-identifying as culturally Deaf and proficient in ASL participated in the study. Participants represented a range of ages, genders, and healthcare experiences across outpatient, inpatient, and emergency care settings. Most participants reported being deaf since birth (N = 29, 78.4%), while a smaller number reported onset after birth, either pre-lingually (prior to language acquisition; N = 2, 5.4%) or post-lingually (after language acquisition; N = 6, 16.2%). Approximately half of participants identified as Deaf in a general sense (N = 19, 51.4%), while others self-identified as oral deaf individuals who use ASL (N = 8, 21.6%), hard-of-hearing individuals who use ASL (N = 8, 21.6%), or deaf-disabled (N = 2, 5.4%). These descriptors reflect self-identified communication background rather than mutually exclusive identity categories.

Participants were aged 18–25 (N = 1, 2.7%), 25–30 (N = 7, 18.9%), 31–40 (N = 16, 43.2%), 41–50 (N = 6, 16.2%), 51–60 (N = 2, 5.4%), and over 60 (N = 1, 2.7%), with four (10.9%) not disclosing their age. In terms of gender identity, twenty-one participants identified as female (56.8%), fourteen identified as male (37.8%), and two (5.4%) did not disclose. Participants were primarily white (N = 29, 78.4%), although a few identified as biracial (N = 2, 5.4%) or did not disclose (N = 6, 16.2%), which is reflective of the population of the study region. Three participants (8.1%) identified as Hispanic or Latino and eleven (29.7%) did not disclose. Most participants resided in urban or suburban settings (N = 23, 62.2%) or did not disclose (N = 13, 35.1%), while one participant resided in a rural area (2.7%). All participants were proficient in ASL.

Main themes

Participants described a healthcare landscape in which effective communication access was neither routine nor reliable, requiring Deaf patients and families to continuously negotiate accommodations that they believed should have been standard. Across accounts, experiences clustered into three interrelated domains: (1) Influencing factors shaping the conditions under which access occurs, (2) Institutional barriers that operationalize inequity at the point of care, and (3) Effects on Deaf patients’ healthcare experiences and broader lives (Fig. 1). Within these domains, multiple subthemes were identified that together account for the institutional and policy-level factors shaping Deaf patients’ healthcare experiences. Together, these domains depict a system in which institutions frequently defaulted to minimal or inconsistent compliance, leaving Deaf patients to absorb the logistical, emotional, and clinical consequences. Several subthemes intersected across domains, reflecting how policy, institutional processes, and individual encounters collectively shape communication access rather than operating as isolated factors.

Influencing factors shaping healthcare access for Deaf patients

Subtheme 1.A: Policy context and the absence of enforceable standards

Participants repeatedly located access failures in the limitations of existing law and the absence of standardized procedures across healthcare systems. Many described “hit and miss” practices that varied by institution rather than reflecting clear, enforceable standards. One participant summarized this gap as follows: “there’s no industry standard wide for interpreting and healthcare access for Deaf individuals… There’s the ADA… but I don’t think that there’s any standard operating procedure that all hospitals follow” (Pg. 27, ref. 149).

Several participants described meaningful improvement as requiring legislative change, including amendments to the ADA or creation of state-level patient rights protections: “The only way to get a hospital to really improve is legislatively at the state level and the national level in terms of amending the ADA, passing a Utah State patient Bill of Rights, or passing a state version of the ADA” (Pg. 37, ref. 166).

Policy was also described as insufficiently protective of quality, not merely access in name. One participant noted, “I’m not a lawyer, but the law doesn’t say anywhere that quality healthcare must be provided for any individual, no matter the language… It would be nice if there was a law that said something like that, but there just isn’t” (Pg. 65, ref. 249). Participants linked policy ambiguity and weak enforcement to downstream harms, including litigation following failures in accommodation delivery: “One resulted in a lawsuit due to a misapplication of a VRI” (Pg. 68, ref. 261).

Participants further emphasized the absence of contingency planning within institutional policies, particularly when interpreters became unavailable mid-encounter: “The interpreter [had to] leave… I still have an appointment and need an interpreter. There needs to be a policy set up for situations like this. What’s plan B or plan C?” (Pg. 51, ref. 219). Others argued that written policies should be co-developed with healthcare organizations to align interpreter scheduling requirements with real-world lead times: “rewrite strict hospital policies, working together with them to figure out how many days they would need to know before getting an interpreter” (Pg. 44, ref. 187).

Subtheme 1.B: Economic pressures and cost-driven accommodation decisions

Economic considerations were portrayed as a persistent driver of inadequate access, with interpreter provision framed as a discretionary expense rather than a core component of safe care. Participants perceived institutional cost-saving practices as directly translating into lower quality healthcare experiences: “if they try to save money on an interpreter then they are providing a cheap experience when it comes to our health… if they spend the money to provide good healthcare, then we receive good healthcare” (Pg. 25, ref. 88).

Participants described explicit budget caps on interpreter use, including limits on the number of interpreted visits: “I was limited to two appointments a year with an interpreter” (Pg. 38, ref. 167). Another participant recounted a clinic providing an interpreter only briefly before shifting to written communication: “I was allowed to have an interpreter for the first three appointments because that’s what they had in the budget… and they said ‘well you can just write it down’… the issue was money” (Pg. 9, ref. 45; Pg. 10, ref. 49). Cost-based reasoning was often overt, including staff perceptions that “Interpreters are too expensive!” (Pg. 64, ref. 246).

Subtheme 1.C: Patient knowledge, health literacy, and self-advocacy as compensatory mechanisms

Participants described knowledge of rights, institutional procedures, interpreting agencies, and medical terminology as both necessary for navigating care and an inequitable burden placed on Deaf patients. Many reported assuming responsibility for coordinating accommodations, including repeatedly confirming interpreter requests: “call[ing] and mak[ing] sure that they requested an interpreter” (Pg. 37, ref. 164). Others described the experience as “the process of fighting for an interpreter to fight for accommodations” (Pg. 39, ref. 170).

In some cases, self-advocacy enabled partial resolution. One participant described negotiating appointment reprioritization when an interpreter was scheduled for only one hour: “They ended up moving me up on the list so that I could meet with the doctor while the interpreter was there” (Pg. 51, ref. 217). However, participants also described moments of being compelled to accept inadequate accommodations: “I was silenced. I had to just accept it, I didn’t like that” (Pg. 10, ref. 51–52).

Participants highlighted variability within the Deaf community in individuals’ comfort with self-advocacy and the use of interpreters: “some deaf people need an interpreter, other deaf people do not need one, it really depends on their individual circumstance” (Pg. 12, ref. 60). Knowledge gaps were themselves identified as barriers, particularly for individuals unfamiliar with local interpreting agencies or recourse pathways: “there are a lot of deaf people that don’t know the interpreting agencies in the area… or who to contact if a problem happens… Most people don’t have that kind of access” (Pg. 56, ref. 222).

Subtheme 1.D: Advocacy as shared labor

Advocacy was described as distributed across spouses, relatives, community members, and interpreting agencies. While advocacy sometimes functioned as practical support, such as confirming interpreter bookings, participants also described it as emotionally taxing: “it is one more step that I’m always thinking about and can be frustrating for sure” (Pg. 37, ref. 164).

Several accounts highlighted family members resisting inappropriate role substitution and insisting on proper interpreter access. One participant described a spouse intervening when an interpreter arrived late during radiology: “my wife told the interpreter ‘No! You need to go now to see my husband’… that was an example of an on-site interpreter, my wife got after them” (Pg. 13, ref. 65). Another described an interpreting agency leader confronting clinicians during a miscarriage when interpreter provision was delayed: “The head of the interpreting agency came and rebuked the doctor saying that an interpreter needs to be provided regardless” (Pg. 20, ref. 74).

At the same time, participants emphasized the risks of relying on family members as interpreters, citing role conflict and incomplete information transfer: “parents and spouses should be separate from the role of community interpreter… Those roles should be separate” (Pg. 51, ref. 214). Even when relatives could sign, limitations were noted: “my mom would always interpret, but she wouldn’t interpret all of the information… my mom’s not a certified interpreter” (Pg. 50, ref. 212). Another participant stated: “when I had my babies, I could not rely on my husband for communication. My mom is hearing but I couldn’t rely on her either. I needed a live interpreter there.”

Institutional barriers to healthcare access for Deaf patients

Subtheme 2.A: Capacity constraints and equipment scarcity

Capacity barriers included limited access to VRI equipment in high-demand settings. One participant described a prolonged appointment because “the hospital only had one device for VRI, and that was in use for Spanish interpretation” (Pg. 22, ref. 81). These constraints were not simply logistical; they contributed to delays, awkward waiting, and truncated clinical encounters.

Subtheme 2.B: Time as delay, disruption, and inefficient workarounds

Time costs were pervasive and multi-layered, affecting patients, staff, and the clinical encounter itself. Participants described delays inherent to initiating and re-initiating VRI calls and the compounded burden when clinicians could not wait. One patient recounted: “We waited for about 15 minutes. Once that was done, before the doctor came, the VRI call ended. Then the doctor had to recall, another 15 to 20 minutes of waiting. The doctor couldn’t wait. He told me that he would be back after tending to the other patients” (Pg. 21, ref. 78).

Participants also contrasted efficient interpreted communication with writing-based workarounds, emphasizing the time inefficiency and the distortion of clinical dialogue when communication becomes written note exchange. At the same time, participants observed that routine clinical scheduling often fails to allocate time for meaningful conversation, amplifying inequity: “doctors have you in and out in seconds… They don’t set aside time to really converse with the patient” (Pg. 57, ref. 227).

Subtheme 2.C: Physical environment and technology (room size, positioning, internet instability, and VRI mechanics)

Participants described physical and technological conditions as active determinants of whether communication access was functional. Room size, availability and positioning of electrical outlets for device charging, and device setup were barriers. Positioning errors compromised access, including staff unintentionally prioritizing their own view of the screen. Participants described a recurrent misunderstanding among providers of how VRI should be used in triadic communication. One participant explained that hearing staff would “speak to an iPad screen,” which prevented the Deaf patient from signing effectively, obscured the view of the remote interpreter, and disrupted turn-taking. Participants noted that the proper method for practitioners using VRI is to stand behind the screen while allowing the Deaf person to see the screen and the remote interpreter to clearly see the Deaf person.

Internet failures and freezing were described as common, producing fragmented communication and increased appointment time: “my worst experience of my life was not having an interpreter. They do provide Video Remote Interpreters sometimes, but that’s challenging because the Wi-fi connection breaks often. A live interpreter is much better.” (Pg. 45, ref. 194). In acute care, system-wide connectivity failures created complete access breakdown: “the internet was down… for that entire floor?… yes, the ICU” (Pg. 2, ref. 9). Participants also noted camera framing issues and chaotic turn-taking when multiple hearing staff spoke simultaneously, resulting in the remote interpreter not being able to determine who is speaking or which hearing person to interpret for, causing patient confusion: “the camera is not pointed at the hearing people… everyone is talking at the same time” (Pg. 62, ref. 240).

Subtheme 2.D: Personnel knowledge, attitudes, and treatment

Participants described barriers rooted in staff unfamiliarity with Deaf patient needs, limited understanding of interpreter processes, and disrespectful behavior. Several accounts emphasized education as a primary lever: “They need more education… more training so that the care can be equal rather than different” (Pg. 11, ref. 58). Participants wanted basic operational competence embedded across roles: “I would want all doctors and dentists to have information on how to set up an interpreter, so they don’t have an excuse to say, ‘I don’t know’” (Pg. 11, ref. 55).

Clinical misunderstanding of Deafness was reported to lead to conflict and misattribution: “The doctor assumed that because I could talk, my roommate couldn’t… it became an argument” (Pg. 14, ref. 70). Participants also described serious situations in which care proceeded without an interpreter, requiring hurried, suboptimal improvisation: “The doctor came but there was no interpreter. The doctor scrambled to get the VRI device and set it up… but eventually left!” (Pg. 1, ref. 3). In another account, interpreter absence intersected with clinical risk when the interpreter left mid-course: “the interpreter had another appointment, which is when the health complication happened. It was a mess” (Pg. 51, ref. 216).

Subtheme 2.E: Interpersonal interactions and dehumanization in interpreted care

Participants reported that communication barriers were not solely technical; they were relational and moral. Some described being treated as secondary to the interpreter: “there was a new person… she had talked to my interpreter instead of talking to me, she looked at my interpreter instead of looking at me” (Pg. 44, ref. 186). Others described routine microaggressions and assumptions: “Oh! Can you read lips?” (Pg. 58, ref. 229).

Dehumanization was explicitly named as occurring when providers saw an interpreter and shifted their posture toward the Deaf patient: “they immediately dehumanize the Deaf person” (Pg. 58, ref. 230). Participants emphasized the need for clinicians to address Deaf patients directly and not treat them as incapable: “they learn how to approach deaf people so that you don’t talk directly to the interpreter, they talk directly to the deaf person and that they don’t think deaf people are incapable” (Pg. 11, ref. 56).

Lipreading, used as an accommodation substitute or supplement, was described as exhausting and cognitively burdensome: “lip reading is really exhausting… what my brain is thinking, what my ears are hearing, and what my eyes are catching have to all be connected” (Pg. 47, ref. 199).

Subtheme 2.F: Institutional processes: interpreter scheduling, miscommunication chains, and “no-show” patterns

Participants described routine process failures in scheduling interpreters, including errors, delays, and responsibility shifting back to the patient. Interpreter no-shows were framed as a dominant barrier: “not having an interpreter show up is the biggest barrier… happens often” (Pg. 56, ref. 223). When scheduling failed, patients experienced cascading uncertainty about whether the problem was miscommunication, too many intermediaries, or neglect: “I don’t know if there were too many cooks in the kitchen… miscommunication down the line” (Pg. 56, ref. 221).

Participants also reported situations in which offices claimed they contacted agencies when they did not: “they’re pretending that they called the agency, but in reality, they never really do” (Pg. 64, ref. 244). Several accounts described institutions lacking a clear resource list of Deaf-focused interpreter resources, requiring patients to supply agencies and contact pathways: “Hospitals don’t have a list. I end up having to give them a list of providers. They have companies for German, French, and Spanish but they don’t hire agencies… for the Deaf” (Pg. 36, ref. 163).

Subtheme 2.G: Institutional contracts: exclusivity and lack of patient choice

Exclusive contracting arrangements were described as structurally limiting interpreter access and quality. Participants perceived contract exclusivity as reducing flexibility and forcing acceptance of mismatched or novice interpreters: “There are some hospitals who have an exclusive contract with only one agency… that’s a really bad idea” (Pg. 35, ref. 158).

Participants wanted autonomy in selecting preferred agencies but described being blocked: “I will tell a hospital my preference and they will say they can’t because they have a contract with only one agency. I hate that!” (Pg. 4, ref. 22). Contracting also constrained the ability to route requests to available interpreters across agencies: “I want to request an interpreter for someone who doesn’t contract with that agency… I can’t because it seems like something within the system is preventing that kind of flexibility” (Pg. 35, ref. 157).

Subtheme 2.H: Institutional policy: VRI as default accommodation and refusal of in-person interpreters

Participants described institutional policy positions that treated VRI as a sufficient accommodation regardless of context. One participant described requesting an interpreter for surgery and arriving to find only VRI: “They gave me the iPad for VRI… The hospital said that ‘we have already provided an interpreter and given you reasonable accommodations. VRI is our accommodation for you’… I was silenced. I had to just accept it” (Pg. 9; Pg. 10, ref. 51). Others emphasized the need for consistent interpreter availability for emergencies and high-risk care: “ideally it would be nice to have a 24/7 on call interpreter for emergency care… how can I work with VRI?” (Pg. 45, ref. 192).

Subtheme 2.I: Interpreter availability and quality: qualifications, local fluency, and VRI errors

Interpreter quality was described as variable across settings and modalities. Participants emphasized that both comprehension and expression matter and that agencies’ screening practices were inconsistent: “that agency was not very transparent with their screenings… not very skilled at comprehending sign language” (Pg. 23, ref. 102). Participants also described VRI interpreters coming from multiple states with uneven certification requirements, producing errors in both signing and voicing: “VRI interpreting has a lot of errors… I will catch them voicing the wrong thing… It makes me very frustrated using VRI” (Pg. 34, ref. 176).

Participants noted that interpreters from outside the region may not know local signs, forcing repetition and fingerspelling: “the interpreters are from all over the country, so they sometimes don’t know the signs specific to that area… I’ll have to spell it” (Pg. 43, ref. 184). These quality and availability issues were not merely inconveniences; they were described as determinants of safety, understanding, and dignity. Among institutional barriers, interpreter unavailability, scheduling failures, and restrictive contracting practices were the most frequently described, with technological and environmental factors often compounding these access failures.

Main theme 3: Effects

Participants described the effects of institutional communication barriers as extending beyond discrete clinical encounters, shaping their autonomy, safety, trust, emotional well-being, and healthcare engagement over time. Effects of institutional communication barriers on Deaf patients

Subtheme 3.A: Healthcare effects: loss of autonomy, compromised privacy, avoidance, and trust erosion

Participants described loss of autonomy as both structural and interpersonal, including limited choice of interpreter or agency and limited ability to assert needs in real time: “I would like to have some say… I just want to say that” (Pg. 36, ref. 162). Participants also described difficulty maintaining an equal role in their own care: “I’m not in the situation where I can… be more assertive and say, provider, talk to me” (Pg. 39, ref. 170).

Privacy was compromised when institutions defaulted to untrained family or friends as interpreters. In contrast, direct communication with signing clinicians was experienced as protective and confidential: “the nice thing was that I felt like everything was confidential. There was no third-party” (Pg. 16, ref. 77).

Participants described avoidance and erosion of trust as rational responses to repeated barriers and prior trauma. One participant described an ongoing calculus of whether trying to change providers would worsen the situation: “Is it worth trying to change or will I just make it worse?” (Pg. 41, ref. 174). Others described dropping providers who failed to provide interpreters: “sometimes when they fail to provide an interpreter, I will have to drop that provider… and just try another provider” (Pg. 26, ref. 117). Avoidance extended to entire hospitals: “Most Deaf people avoid going to those hospitals… They’d rather drive 30 or 40 miles to a hospital that will provide accommodations” (Pg. 69, ref. 263).

Trust erosion was also described in more immediate experiential terms: “It turns me off a bit” (Pg. 52, ref. 220), and in broader perceptions that Deaf patients are not prioritized: “I just don’t feel important there… which is the reason I try to avoid going there” (Pg. 6, ref. 34).

Subtheme 3.B: Clinical risk: poor outcomes and delayed or inadequate treatment

Participants linked communication breakdowns to serious clinical risk, including delayed intervention and misunderstanding of treatment plans. One participant described being “seconds from death” and interpreted the slow response as potentially related to communication barriers (Pg. 17, ref. 85). Another highlighted the stakes of major surgery: “I could be paralyzed or I could die on the table… if they try to tell me that my wife can interpret… what if I died on the table, what would you do?” (Pg. 17, ref. 84).

Participants described treatment decisions and procedures occurring without adequate explanation when interpreters were absent. One account described receiving painful injections during an appointment without an interpreter and then experiencing dangerous after-effects at home, including hyperglycemia from cortisone despite known diabetes: “I wish I would have known about that, but I didn’t have an interpreter… my blood sugar was through the roof… their reaction… ‘oh, sorry, we won’t do it again’” (Pg. 8, ref. 40).

Delays were also described as routine outcomes of interpreter unavailability and technological failures: “The Deaf person has to wait for an hour until the interpreter shows up” (Pg. 60, ref. 234), as well as delays when appointments were rescheduled due to interpreter mismatch or VRI failure (Pg. 34, ref. 155).

Subtheme 3.C: Broader impacts: emotional burden, strain in relationships, and downstream consequences

Participants described the cumulative impact of barriers as chronic stress and emotional depletion. Several quotes explicitly named the extra burden of having to manage access: “Yes!… I feel like it’s a responsibility” (Pg. 37, ref. 164). Stress was described as persistent and escalating: “it’s very stressful… to have to fight for initial access” (Pg. 39, ref. 170); “the system just sucks… incredibly frustrating” (Pg. 40, ref. 173). Participants described functional consequences including sleep disruption and social impairment: “it was affecting my sleep… my social life was suffering… I was short tempered and easily frustrated at work” (Pg. 41, ref. 176).

Relationship strain emerged when family members were asked to interpret in situations where they were emotionally or physically unable, such as labor: “while my wife is in labor, they asked her to interpret for me… Isn’t that crazy?!” (Pg. 27, ref. 144). Another participant described discomfort navigating sensitive developmental topics with a child when systems and roles already complicate communication access: “My daughter is going to be 12 soon… puberty… I don’t want her to be uncomfortable” (Pg. 40, ref. 173).

Participants also described downstream consequences that extended beyond the clinic. Some referenced litigation as a response to systemic failure or misapplied accommodations (Pg. 38, ref. 168; Pg. 68, ref. 261). Others described driving farther to avoid retraumatizing experiences and reduce accommodation burden: “I’ll also drive a half hour out of my way… All so I can avoid that extra burden of managing the interpreter piece” (Pg. 37, ref. 165). Finally, participants identified situations where CDI involvement may be necessary, particularly for Deaf individuals with additional disabilities who may not fully understand standard interpreting: “this person is deaf and she has a learning disability… she just nodded her head but didn’t understand… I wonder if she had a certified deaf interpreter if that would have been a better experience” (Pg. 12, ref. 60).

While the themes presented reflect patterns shared across many participants, some variability was observed in the degree of self-advocacy, comfort with technology, preferences for interpreter modalities, and varying healthcare contexts, illustrating the heterogeneity of experiences within the Deaf community.

Discussion

This study reinforces that communication access for signing Deaf patients is not a peripheral “service issue.” It is a core clinical mechanism through which safety, autonomy, equity, and trust are protected or undermined. Participants described barriers that were structural (interpreter availability, time pressure, inaccessible systems), relational (dismissal of stated preferences, stigma, reliance on companions), and informational (incomplete understanding of diagnosis, consent, and discharge instructions). These findings align with prior work showing that Deaf/hearing encounters are frequently mediated through improvised workarounds, including lipreading, written notes, family interpretation, or partial reliance on technology, rather than being intentionally designed for direct, equitable communication [12, 20, 21].

Importantly, the present findings extend current literature by clarifying what communication workarounds do in practice. They function as structural risk transfer, shifting responsibility for comprehension, advocacy, and error prevention from the health system to the signing Deaf patient. In this study, communication barriers also operated as a patient-safety and access problem across the full care trajectory. Emergency care research demonstrates that signing Deaf patients and clinicians perceive communication obstacles as barriers to timely and optimal emergency department access, with downstream consequences for assessment quality and care planning [21]. The current findings deepen this understanding by illustrating how risks accumulate at high-stakes transitions, including triage, procedures, informed consent, diagnostic explanation, and discharge. These are precisely the moments when misunderstanding can change decisions, compromise safety, and erode trust.

Participants’ accounts further demonstrate that inaccessible communication is psychologically consequential, not merely inconvenient. Leite et al. describes the psychological impact of exclusion and the erosion of autonomy when communication is not accessible, particularly during sensitive care processes that heighten vulnerability and dependence [20]. A broader synthesis of signing Deaf encounters in healthcare similarly emphasizes that communication processes, awareness, and policy supports shape trust and empowerment, therefore influencing healthcare experiences [7]. In the present study, the anticipation of misunderstanding or dismissal appeared to function as a deterrent to engagement, increasing the likelihood of delayed help-seeking, passive assent, or incomplete participation, each of which amplifies both clinical and emotional risk.

Technology emerged as a constrained solution rather than a substitute for guaranteed access. While VRI, portals, and telehealth are often positioned as scalable fixes, evidence from Colombia shows that digital health technology use among signing Deaf adults depends on access, usability, and the fit between tools and communication practices [22]. The present findings support a cautious interpretation: digital solutions can reduce barriers in some contexts, but they can also reproduce inequity when platforms are not accessible, connectivity is unreliable, privacy is compromised, interpreters are not qualified, or tools are not co-designed with signing Deaf users. In short, “digital” is not synonymous with “accessible.”

Finally, participants’ accounts indicate that workforce preparation is necessary but insufficient without operational redesign. Educational interventions that include sign language exposure and Deaf-centered communication training show promise for improving readiness [23, 24]. However, the findings suggest that training cannot compensate for absent interpreter infrastructure, misaligned scheduling models, incomplete record-keeping, or weak accountability. This conclusion is reinforced by parallel evidence from intensive care nurses working with patients with Limited English Proficiency (LEP), in which clinicians described strain and risk when language support was unreliable [25]. This illustrates how health systems often depend on clinician improvisation when communication infrastructure is not dependable.

This study extends existing evidence by locating where communication failures become most consequential, not simply that they occur. Participants’ narratives concentrated on high-stakes transitions, including triage, consent, diagnostic explanation, surgery, childbirth, and discharge, where misunderstanding directly alters decision-making and safety. A second contribution is conceptual: common institutional accommodations, such as written notes, lipreading, family interpretation, and default remote modalities, are reframed as risk transfer, not neutral accommodations. A third contribution is implementation-facing: isolated fixes, such as technology adoption or one-time training, are unlikely to produce equity without reliable processes and measurable accountability.

Global implications

These findings are globally relevant to signing Deaf populations. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), Article 25, establishes that people with disabilities have the right to the same range, quality, and standard of healthcare as others [26]. Thus, healthcare inequities for signing Deaf patients are a global health equity concern, as the drivers identified in this study, including limited interpreter availability, inconsistent training, ineffective digital platforms, and weak enforcement mechanisms, recur across countries and settings. Studies worldwide have demonstrated consistent gaps in health knowledge, inadequate communication during healthcare encounters, and dissatisfaction with clinical experiences among signing Deaf patients in both high- and low-income countries throughout Europe, the United States, New Zealand, South America, and Africa [27]. Evidence from emergency care illustrates how time-pressured environments magnify these barriers [20], and international research demonstrates that digital health uptake is shaped by feasibility and usability rather than availability alone [22]. Addressing global inequities will require rights-based policy infrastructure paired with pragmatic service delivery models that match local resource realities. Without both, predictable mechanisms of inequity persist, including delayed care, incomplete understanding, reduced shared decision-making, and preventable harm.

Implications for policy and practice

At the clinical level, frontline clinicians should treat communication preferences as clinically relevant data and avoid ad hoc approaches, such as relying on companions, lipreading, or written notes, particularly for high-risk moments like consent, diagnostic explanation, and discharge teaching [12, 21]. Instead, practice should default to qualified interpretation and incorporate teach-back or comprehension checks at key points in care. Additionally, prior research has found that language-concordant providers are associated with increased use of preventive services [28].

At the health system level, leaders and administrators should operationalize interpreter services as dependable clinical infrastructure, including escalation pathways for nights and weekends, rapid access during triage and diagnostics, and integration into discharge workflows. Accessibility must be designed into patient-facing systems (flagging EHRs, automated interpreter scheduling, portals, signage, discharge materials) and monitored through quality indicators such as interpreter fulfillment rates, time-to-interpreter, and documentation of communication preferences [7, 20].

At the policy level, policymakers should specify enforceable standards for the timeliness and scope of communication access and shift accountability away from individual patients having to self-advocate. Reimbursement and incentive structures should align with interpreter provision and accessible communication supports. Digital inclusion and accessibility requirements are also essential, as access and usability cannot be assumed across contexts [22].

In education and workforce development, professional education programs and credentialing bodies can support training and certification for institutions meeting accessibility standards. Training should be longitudinal and assessed in practice-based formats, including live simulation with signing Deaf actors, emphasizing high-risk clinical transitions and culturally safe [29], Deaf-centered communication. Interprofessional models that include sign language exposure for health professionals alongside Deaf community engagement are supported in the literature [23].

Partnerships with Deaf community organizations are equally essential. Community governance roles are central, including advisory participation, peer navigation, and co-led monitoring of access metrics so reforms reflect lived priorities rather than compliance-driven checklists [20].

Technology developers and vendors also have a role to play in these efforts. Tools should be co-designed with signing Deaf communities and integrated into workflows without increasing documentation burden or compromising privacy. Technology like VRI should augment, not replace, guaranteed access to qualified on-site interpretation, given demonstrated variability in usability and contextual fit [22].

Finally, there is a need for expanded research in implementation science. Future work should evaluate multi-component interventions that combine interpreter infrastructure, workflow redesign, workforce preparation, and accessible technology, with outcomes that capture safety, equity, trust, and patient experience across diverse resource settings.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the study reflects signing Deaf patients’ perspectives within a particular geographic context and participant sample, which may limit transferability to other settings, deaf individuals, and communities. In the United States, ASL is not the only signed language or variety in use, and future research should include users of Plains Indian Sign Language, Black ASL, and linguistically and culturally diverse Deaf communities [30].

Second, transcripts were not returned to participants for member checking, which may limit the accuracy and interpretive validity of the findings. However, this limitation was partially mitigated by video recordings and independent verification by a third-party interpreting agency.

Third, because the study emphasizes patient experiences, it does not fully capture the institutional constraints and decision-making processes that shape capacity (e.g., budgeting, staffing, contracting). Future research should examine healthcare administrators’ and clinicians’ perspectives on feasibility and sustainability of the proposed solutions, paired with implementation-focused evaluation (e.g., effects of EHR flagging alongside automated scheduling on interpreter no-show rates, wait times, patient-reported trust, and safety outcomes). Economic evaluations would also be valuable, particularly given participants’ repeated emphasis that cost-containment strategies may generate downstream harms and inefficiencies.

Conclusion

This study shows that equitable healthcare access for signing Deaf adults depends less on the presence of disability rights legislation than on how health systems operationalize communication in everyday practice. Participants described interpreter access, scheduling, contracting, staff preparedness, and technology use as inconsistent and often treated as discretionary, forcing signing Deaf patients to repeatedly manage and negotiate accommodations that should function as routine clinical infrastructure. These institutional practices produced predictable downstream effects, including loss of autonomy and privacy, erosion of trust, healthcare avoidance, and heightened perceptions of clinical risk, alongside substantial emotional and logistical burden.

A clear call to action emerges. Communication access must be recognized, funded, and measured as a core patient-safety and quality function rather than a compliance obligation. Health systems should embed reliable interpreter pathways across the full care trajectory, establish accountable metrics for access, and design digital tools that are accessible by default, while policymakers and payers strengthen enforceable standards and reimbursement mechanisms that support timely, qualified communication access. Without structural redesign, signing Deaf patients will continue to bear disproportionate responsibility for preventing communication-related harm. With coordinated, community-informed action, healthcare can move beyond minimal compliance toward durable equity that ensures Deaf patients can participate fully, safely, and with dignity.
