Prevalence of genetically diagnosed familial hypercholesterolemia in Vietnamese patients with premature acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Kha Minh Nguyen, Sy Van Hoang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a genetic disorder that results in elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, which manifest early in the first decades of life. It is a major cause of premature coronary artery disease worldwide, leading to significant public health challenges. The prevalence of genetically determined FH in patients with premature coronary artery disease remains underestimated, particularly in developing countries. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of genetically defined FH in Vietnamese patients with premature acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in the Vietnamese population. This cross-sectional study enrolled 218 consecutive patients diagnosed with premature AMI who underwent coronary angiography. The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B, and proprotein convertase subtilisin-kexin type 9 genes were analyzed by next-generation sequencing. FH was diagnosed according to Dutch Lipid Clinic Network criteria. Among the patients with premature AMI who underwent coronary angiography, the mean age was 46.9 ± 6.1 years, with a predominance of males (83.9%). The prevalence of potential FH diagnosed using Dutch Lipid Clinic Network criteria was 14.7% (definite FH, 6.0%; probable FH, 8.7%). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in LDLR, apolipoprotein B, and proprotein convertase subtilisin-kexin type 9 were found in 9 of 218 patients (4.1%), all of which were causative mutations in LDLR. Patients with premature AMI and FH had significantly greater LDL-C levels (217.6 vs 125.7 mg/dL) and more severe coronary artery lesions, as assessed by the Gensini score (100.3 vs 60.5), than did those in the No FH group. The prevalence of genetically determined FH among Vietnamese patients with premature AMI is relatively high. Screening and diagnosis of hereditary conditions in patients with premature AMI are essential to improve early detection and management and reduce the burden of coronary artery disease in this population.

1. Introduction

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by elevated cholesterol levels that frequently leads to premature cardiovascular disease.[1] The prevalence of FH in the general population varies based on its genetic expression; heterozygous FH affects approximately 1 in 250 to 1 in 313 individuals, whereas homozygous FH is significantly rarer.[2,3] The prevalence is notably higher in patients with ischemic heart disease. A recent meta-analysis revealed a wide variation in FH prevalence among patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), ranging from 0.4% to 25.4%.[4] In cases of premature CAD, the prevalence of FH is approximately 30 times higher than that in the general population.[3,5]

In contemporary clinical settings, FH diagnosis primarily utilizes clinical scoring systems such as the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network (DLCN), Simon Broome, and MEPPED criteria.[2,6,7] Various guidelines recommend the DLCN criteria extensively.[8] Identification of pathogenic mutations in low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B (APOB), and proprotein convertase subtilisin-kexin type 9 (PCSK9) genes is considered the gold standard for FH diagnosis.[9] These are the 3 most common dominant mutations causing FH; additionally, FH is also associated with recessive mutations such as LDLRAP1, although with a much lower frequency.[10] Genetic testing for these mutations is becoming increasingly popular and accessible globally. For patients with premature acute myocardial infarction (AMI) (before 55 years in men and before 60 years in women), the 2019 European Society of Cardiology recommends that genetic testing should be performed if conditions allow to screen for FH, with a Class I recommendation.[11] The gold standard for diagnosing FH requires genetic mutation testing; relying solely on clinical parameters may underestimate the prevalence of FH. Moreover, genetic testing enables the screening of family members for the early prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases.[12]

FH remains underdiagnosed and undertreated worldwide, especially in middle- and low-income countries, among patients with premature CAD.[2,5,13] Diagnosing FH in patients with premature CAD allows for the implementation of more aggressive management strategies, particularly in controlling low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels in these patients. Additionally, it enables cascade screening of family members based on the index case, thereby preventing premature CAD in their relatives.[9,11] The reported prevalence of FH in premature CAD patients varies depending on whether the diagnosis is based solely on clinical criteria or includes genetic mutation criteria.[14–16] In Asia, data on FH incidence in premature CAD patients on the basis of genetic mutation criteria and the epidemiological characteristics of FH-associated genes are derived mainly from developed East Asian countries.[17] Conversely, the prevalence data in Southeast Asia primarily rely on clinical DLCN criteria. In 2023, a study in Thailand reported a prevalence of FH in premature CAD patients at 1.36%. In Malaysia, the prevalence of clinically diagnosed FH among premature CAD patients is 16%, with 6% classified as definite FH and 10% as probable FH.[18,19]

In Vietnam, existing studies have only assessed FH in the general population through initial screening via the DLCN criteria[20] with a particular study describing LDLR gene variants in relatives across 3 generations of 4 patients with premature myocardial infarction via Sanger sequencing.[21] To the best of our knowledge, no published studies in Vietnam have reported the prevalence of genetically defined FH in patients with premature AMI. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of FH through genetic testing in a cohort of Vietnamese patients with premature AMI, and to compare the clinical characteristics between patients with and without FH.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and subjects

This observational study was conducted at Cho Ray Hospital, a national medical center in Vietnam, from October 2023 to June 2024. The protocol for this study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam (No. 903/HDDD-DHYD). All the participants provided written informed consent. As reported in a previous study, sample size calculation was based on the prevalence of FH in patients with premature myocardial infarction. The minimum required sample size was 207 patients (estimated prevalence = 16%; d = 0.05).[19]

2.2. Inclusion criteria

The participants included male and female Vietnamese individuals aged ≥ 18 years and diagnosed with premature AMI (males < 55 years old and females < 60 years old). AMI was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.[22] All patients in the study underwent coronary angiography, which revealed that at least 50% of the diameter of 1 of the 3 main coronary arteries had narrowed.

2.3. Exclusion criteria

Patients with advanced liver or kidney disease, pregnant or lactating women, individuals with hypothyroidism, and those with cancer.

2.4. Data collection

Data on demographic features, family history of premature CAD, smoking status, and lipid-lowering therapy were collected. A history of symptoms and signs was obtained from hospitalized patients, and physical examinations, including the Killip classification, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference, were assessed by experienced cardiologists. The diagnoses of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia were based on current national guidelines. Obesity was diagnosed according to Asian criteria, with a diagnostic threshold of BMI ≥ 25 kg/m².[23] Additionally, laboratory tests were performed to confirm the diagnosis of AMI. The results of the laboratory examinations, such as complete blood count, glucose, serum creatinine, cardiac enzymes, and lipid profile, during the first 24 hours after admission were also obtained. The tests were performed at the Biochemistry Department of our hospital. The severity of CAD was assessed according to the Gensini score system based on the results of a coronary angiogram.[24] Pretreatment LDL-C levels will be collected from patients who meet the inclusion criteria for the study. Individuals on lipid-lowering medications without pretreatment LDL-C data had their untreated LDL-C levels conservatively adjusted using relative correction factors based on statin dose and potency. These correction factors originated from analyzing 71 original articles collated before these criteria were established.[25]

2.5. Diagnostic criteria for FH

FH was diagnosed using Dutch Lipid Clinic Network (DLCN) criteria. These criteria included evaluation of personal and family history of CAD, a thorough physical examination, assessment of LDL-C levels, and DNA analysis.[8] Tendon xanthomata were assessed by visually detecting nodules or thickening in the tendons, specifically at the Achilles tendons, dorsal extensor of the hands, elbows, or knees. Premature corneal arcus was identified by the presence of a white opaque ring around the periphery of the cornea in participants under 45 years old. For participants with corneal arcus aged 45 years or older, the age of onset was inquired. If the onset occurred at age 45 or older, it was not classified as premature corneal arcus. Patients with a DLCN score ≥ 6 points, classified as definite/probable FH, were categorized into the “FH group”; otherwise, they were classified into the “No FH group.” The prevalence of FH was defined as those with Potential FH (probable or definite FH).[26] Clinically diagnosed FH is diagnosed based on clinical criteria without genetic mutation testing, while genetically confirmed FH is based on both clinical criteria and genetic mutation testing.

2.6. Genetic analysis

2.6.1. Blood cell sample collection and DNA extraction

Approximately 3 mL of blood was collected and stored in tubes containing anticoagulated K2EDTA (BD Vacutainer, USA), and preservative compounds were used to preserve the blood and prevent cell lysis. The total DNA was extracted using the KingFisher Flex system.

2.6.2. DNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was quantified using fluorescence with a Qubit (or Quantus) instrument. A dsDNA library was then prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit. The genomic DNA was fragmented at random locations within the genome using the Fragmentase enzyme (New England Biolabs, USA), with the fragmentation time optimized to achieve the desired DNA fragment length for sequencing. Following fragmentation, the 5’ and 3’ ends of the dsDNA molecules were modified and sequentially ligated with Illumina adapter sequences through a process involving 4 thermal incubation steps. Purification steps using beads were conducted to remove unused adapter sequences, excessively short library fragments, adapter dimers, and residual buffer solutions. The library was then enriched via PCR using common primers specific to the attached adapters. Next, xGen lockdown probes were designed to include sequences targeting the desired DNA regions. The library samples were pooled and denatured with 0.2 N NaOH solution to generate single-stranded DNA. The library was then diluted to a concentration of 1.5 pM (for the NextSeq550 Mid Output 150 Cycles sequencing kit) before loading into the cartridge. The sequencing process involves 2 main steps: clustering and sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 550 system (San Diego). The sequencing process in this study was conducted at the Medical Genetics Joint Stock Company (MGJ) under the Medical Genetics Institute, Vietnam, with globally qualified laboratory (AOSC ISO 15189:2012) certification and accreditation by the College of American Pathologists (CAP#: 9617604) (https://genesolutions.vn/en/). The 3 main FH-related genes that were sequenced include LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9.

The pathogenicity of the variants was assessed using the Illumina Basespace Variant Interpreter and the 2015 guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics for molecular pathology. The pathogenicity was verified using the ClinVar database.[27]

2.7. Statistical analysis

Data entry and processing were conducted via Stata 14.2 software on a Windows operating system (StataCorp. 2015, Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX, StataCorp LP). Continuous variables are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed or as median (interquartile range) if not. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). Differences in characteristics between the Potential FH and No FH groups were compared using the Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

In total, 218 patients with premature AMI who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. The cohort comprised 183 males (83.9%) and 35 females (16.1%), with a mean age of 46.9 ± 6.1 years. Among the cardiovascular risk factors, hypertension was present in 48.6% of the patients, diabetes in 17.4%, and obesity, based on Asian criteria, in 37.2% (Table 1). Clinically, the incidence of STEMI is higher than that of NSTEMI, with rates of 66.5% and 33.5%, respectively. All the patients underwent coronary angiography, which revealed that each patient had at least 1 major epicardial coronary artery stenosis. Additionally, 65.1% (142/218) of patients had stenosis in 2 or more major coronary arteries. The median Gensini score for the cohort was 62 (range: 42.5–99.5).

In this study, 13 variants of LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9 genes were identified. Among these variants, 7 were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic and were identified in 9 patients (Table 2). All 9 pathogenic LDLR mutations were heterozygous, and each patient carried a single missense mutation. All patients carrying variants classified as likely pathogenic or pathogenic belong to the definite FH group. The findings revealed that FH-related mutations were present in 9 out of 218 patients (4.1%). Additionally, 4 variants of uncertain significance were identified, consisting of 1 variant of the LDLR gene and 3 variants of APOB gene.

3.1. Clinical characteristics of AMI patients according to FH status

There was no significant difference in average age between the FH and No FH groups. However, premature AMI patients with a family history of premature CAD constituted 53.1% of the FH group, which was significantly greater than that in the No FH group. Furthermore, the proportion of patients in the Killip class II or higher group was nearly twice that in the FH group, with this difference being statistically significant (P = .04). The severity of coronary artery lesions, as measured by the Gensini score, was markedly greater in the FH group than in the No FH group (100.3 vs 60.5, P < .001). Additional clinical variables comparing the 2 groups are shown in Table 1.

In our study, serum LDL-C concentration followed a normal distribution (Fig. 2A). 4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Vietnam to report the prevalence of genetically diagnosed FH based on gene mutation testing using NGS in patients with premature AMI. Our findings revealed that the prevalence of genetically diagnosed FH, according to the DLCN criteria, in patients with premature AMI was 14.7%, with definite FH at 6%, and probable FH at 8.7%. Furthermore, the prevalence of likely pathogenic or pathogenic gene mutations among these patients was relatively high at 4.1%. Compared to patients in the No FH group, those in the FH group had a significantly higher incidence of a family history of premature CAD, xanthoma/corneal arcus signs, more severe clinical presentations as classified by Killip, and reduced left ventricular function. Additionally, premature AMI patients with FH present with more severe coronary artery lesions.

The prevalence of genetically confirmed FH in patients with premature AMI who underwent coronary angiography (men < 55 years and women < 60 years) in our study was high, which is consistent with recent global reports. A study in India reported a prevalence of FH diagnosed using DLCN criteria in patients with acute coronary syndrome or chronic stable angina at an age of < 55 years in men and < 60 years in women, with a prevalence of 15% (definite 4%, probable 11%).[28] In Malaysia, the prevalence of clinically diagnosed FH among premature CAD patients is 16% (definite and probable FH are 6% and 10%, respectively).[19] In 2023, a study in Thailand reported a lower prevalence of FH in premature CAD patients (1.36%).[18] These studies primarily used DLCN clinical criteria without routine genetic testing for FH-related mutations, such as LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9. In our study, all premature AMI patients were assessed for mutations in 3 genes, LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9, using NGS. A study on acute coronary syndrome patients in Spain aged ≤ 65 years and with LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL assessed 7 genes associated with FH and reported a genetically confirmed FH prevalence of 8.7%, with a potential FH prevalence of 27.2% according to the DLCN.[16] These higher rates compared to those in our study may be due to the initial LDL-C threshold and the number of genes assessed. Our study set an LDL-C threshold, with an average LDL-C concentration of 139.2 mg/dL. In China, Cui et al reported a pathogenic gene mutation prevalence of 4.4% (10/225 patients) in premature AMI (males ≤ 55 years old, females ≤ 60 years old).[29] Their study revealed a definite/probable FH rate of 8% according to the DLCN criteria, which increased to 23.6% according to China’s modified DLCN criteria. The LDL-C concentration in their study was similar to that of the present study (140.4 vs 139.2). A meta-analysis involving 11 million people reported the prevalence of FH in the general population to be approximately 1:313, which increased to 1:15 (21 times higher) in premature CAD patients.[3] The FH prevalence in Asian premature CAD patients was 29 times higher than that in the general population. The prevalence of FH in patients with premature CAD depends on factors such as race, age threshold, ischemic heart disease presentation, initial LDL-C threshold, clinical criteria used, and genetic testing.[5]

Genetic testing is considered the gold standard for diagnosing FH, a monogenic disorder primarily related to dominant genes, such as LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9. Several other genes, including LDLRAP1, APOE, STAP1, ABCG5, and ABCG8, have been implicated in monogenic cholesterol disorders leading to premature CAD.[30] LDLR gene mutations are the most common (approximately 95%), followed by APOB and PCSK9 mutations. The prevalence of FH-related gene mutations varies by race. In our study of the Vietnamese population, all pathogenic variants were found in LDLR gene. In Taiwan, the FH-related gene variant prevalence was 4.7% (12/256) in CAD patients and left ventricular ejection fraction < 40%, including 9 LDLR variants and 3 APOB variants.[31] In Sweden, the prevalence was 6.9% (8/116), with all pathogenic variants in the LDLR gene.[14] A recent review of the genetic epidemiology of heterozygous FH in Southeast Asia and East Asia highlighted the dominance of LDLR mutations, with data from Southeast Asia primarily from Malaysia.[13] Our study provides additional FH gene data for the Southeast Asia and Asian region.

FH pathogenesis involves elevated blood LDL-C levels early in life, which leads to the early onset of atherosclerosis and arterial blockages, including coronary arteries, resulting in premature myocardial infarction.[32] Our study revealed that LDL-C levels in premature AMI patients with FH were significantly different, whereas high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglyceride levels were similar in both groups. These findings are consistent with those of global studies of premature CAD or premature AMI.[29,33,34] High LDL-C levels from early life can lead to lipid accumulation, which manifests as peripheral signs such as corneal arcus or xanthoma, and contributes to atherosclerosis, including CAD.[35] Our study revealed more xanthoma/corneal arcus signs in the FH group. Notably, the severity of coronary artery lesions assessed by the Gensini score was significantly greater in premature AMI patients with FH than in those without FH group (100.3 vs 60.5), although there was no difference in multi-vessel CAD between the 2 groups (Table 1). Few studies have compared the severity of coronary artery lesions between the FH and No FH groups in terms of the Gensini score. Cui et al reported that patients with FH-related mutations have more severe coronary artery lesions.[29]

Our study had several limitations. First, only patients from a single center were enrolled, and the sample size needs to be larger, which may reflect something other than the general premature myocardial infarction population. Second, some patients needed to be made aware of their family history of premature CAD, which may have led to underestimation of the true prevalence of FH. Secondly, the classification of variants primarily relies on the available genetic database, ClinVar, and does not fully present the combination of pathogenicity criteria according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. Finally, the estimated LDL-C levels, rather than the accurate untreated LDL-C levels, were used for medication-treated patients, which might have led to bias.

5. Conclusion

Our study indicated that the prevalence of FH in patients with premature AMI who underwent coronary angiography was high. In the present study, the prevalence of causative gene mutations associated with FH was 4.1%. This study contributes to the epidemiological mapping of pathogenic FH gene mutations in Asia.
