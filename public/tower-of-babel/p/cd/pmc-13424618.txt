Early childhood caries at its start: oral health disparities in Thai toddlers
Authors: Pattarawadee Leelataweewud, Kitiwadee Choonhawarakorn, Pornpailin Kasemkhun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite a declining trend, early childhood caries (ECC) remains a public health concern, particularly among very young children. In Thailand, ECC prevalence is consistently higher in provincial areas than in the capital city and remains underexplored. This study examined disparities in oral health status among infants and toddlers living in the capital and provincial areas, as well as differences in parental sociodemographic and economic status (SDES), oral health knowledge (OHK), and oral health behaviours (OHB), and identified key factors associated with ECC among Thai children aged 6–36 months. This cross-sectional study included 263 parents of children aged 6–36 months attending a university hospital in Bangkok and two provincial hospitals. Parents completed validated questionnaires assessing SDES, OHK, and OHB. Children underwent oral examinations for caries using the ICDAS scoring system and oral hygiene assessment via the Debris Index-Simplified. Statistical analyses included chi-square, Mann–Whitney U, and regression models to identify factors associated with oral health outcomes. The overall caries prevalence was 39.5%. Children residing in provincial cities exhibited significantly poorer oral hygiene (median [IQR]: 1.50 [1.33] vs. 0.67 [1.16]) and more severe dental caries than those in the capital city (30.6% vs. 9.7% of total tooth surfaces). Parents in the capital city demonstrated significantly higher OHK scores than those in provincial areas (median [IQR]: 8.0 [2.0] vs. 5.0 [4.0]), and unfavourable OHBs were more common among provincial families. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis identified provincial residence, a greater number of erupted teeth, cariogenic snacking, and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption as significant factors associated with both poor oral hygiene and ECC, while lower parental education level was associated with poor oral hygiene only. Significant disparities in SDES, parental OHK, and OHBs were observed between capital and provincial residents, with children in provincial areas exhibiting poorer oral hygiene and a higher prevalence of ECC. Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a highly prevalent global disease. Although its prevalence in Thailand has shown a declining trend1, it remains high and continues to pose a significant concern for its impact on very young children. Untreated caries leads to complicated treatment and jeopardises oral health-related quality of life in preschool children and their families2. Unhealthy oral health behaviours (OHBs) such as prolonged nocturnal breastfeeding or bottle feeding, inadequate oral care, frequent consumption of cariogenic snacks and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB), and inconsistent use of fluoride have been shown to be associated with ECC3,4. These OHBs are established early in life.

The differences in oral health outcomes between urban and rural areas are consistently demonstrated in previous studies5,6. This oral health inequality is seen in the recent Thailand national oral health survey1 with higher caries prevalence observed in rural areas than in the urban areas and the capital city. Meanwhile, areas that appear similar to capital settings—such as urban areas in provincial cities—have been relatively underexplored. Social determinants of children’s oral health in these areas may be different7.

The capital city is characterised by a high concentration of public and private healthcare facilities, university hospitals, and accessible oral health services. Provincial cities in Thailand’s central region are classified as urban municipalities outside the capital. Although they share some urbanisation features with the capital—such as developed infrastructure and public services—they differ substantially in healthcare resource density, specialist availability, and reach of health promotion campaigns. These provincial urban settings are also distinct from rural areas, which face even greater barriers in access to dental care. This three-tier gradient has been underexplored; national surveys have largely compared urban versus rural settings, leaving provincial urban areas inadequately characterised.

Investigating these regions is important, as identifying potential differences may provide deeper insights into population characteristics and oral health-related factors. Furthermore, studies on ECC specifically in very young children have not been updated in Thailand. Despite universal health coverage to ensure accessibility and the ongoing implementation of oral health promotion programmes and policies, oral health practices during the early life of children are underexplored. Therefore, it is important to examine current outcomes and identify additional influencing factors that could inform future oral health promotion development. This led to the following objectives: (1) to compare the oral health status (oral hygiene and severity of dental caries) of infants and toddlers residing in the capital city and provincial urban areas, together with differences in parental sociodemographic and economic status (SDES), oral health knowledge, and oral health–related behaviours that might influence the child’s oral health; and (2) to identify factors associated with early childhood caries among Thai infants and toddlers aged 6 to 36 months.

Methods

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of the Faculty of Dentistry and Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (MU-DT/PY-IRB 2022/042.2608), Samut Prakan Hospital (No.Equation 04165), and Chaophraya Abhaiphubejhr Hospital (No. IRB-BHUBEJHR-199). Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians of participating children.

Study design and setting

This study was conducted and reported following the STROBE guidelines8. A convenience sample of parents who were primary caregivers and children aged 6–36 months with at least one tooth erupted was recruited from families attending a university hospital in Bangkok or two provincial hospitals in Samut Prakan and Prachin Buri provinces in Thailand. As participants were selected based on availability during scheduled clinic visits, families attending hospital-based services may not be fully representative of the general population, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Bangkok is a highly urbanised capital city with a dense population and extensive access to services, whereas Samut Prakan and Prachin Buri are provincial areas. Both provinces contain designated urban municipalities with substantial population density, developed infrastructure, and established community services. Parents who were unable to read and understand written Thai sufficiently to complete the questionnaire and understand the informed consent form were excluded. The sample size calculation was based on a study by Choonhawarakorn et al.9, which examined the effectiveness of two oral health promotion methods in young children, using the formula for estimating an infinite population mean10, Z-score (0.975) = 1.96, standard deviation (σ) = 0.74, error (d) = 0.1, and an alpha error probability (α) = 0.05. A sample size of 211 parents and children was required, anticipating a 20% dropout rate; the total sample size in this study was 263.

Study process

The participating parent, who was the primary caregiver of the child, completed a self-administered questionnaire. To ensure clarity and appropriateness, the questionnaire containing common oral health questions was written in Thai using lay terms suitable for parents and was content validated by two experienced pediatric dentists (IOC = 0.95) prior to the study. The questionnaires consisted of three sections: (1) SDES; (2) OHB, including dietary habits and oral care practices, and (3) ten OHK questions related to ECC.

Prior to data collection, both examiners underwent training and calibration sessions supervised by the senior investigators, who were experienced pediatric dentists with expertise in the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS)11 and the Debris Index-Simplified (DI-S)12. Intraoral examinations were performed on participating children by two well-trained pediatric dental residents with a ball-ended explorer and a flat mouth mirror under headlamp illumination and aided by tactile inspection when deemed necessary, using the knee-to-knee position with the parent actively restraining the child. The oral health assessment included: (1) number of erupted teeth, (2) caries status—reported by number and location of affected surfaces, and severity—according to the ICDAS; only ICDAS codes 2–6 were recorded, as ICDAS code 1 requires air-drying for reliable detection, which could not be consistently applied during knee-to-knee examination of young children in this age group; and (3) oral hygiene status—assessed through soft debris accumulation using the DI-S and categorised as good (0.0–0.6), fair (0.7–1.8), or poor (1.9–3.0).

Statistical analysis

Intra- and inter-examiner reliability for oral health assessments was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise SDES and oral health status, including oral hygiene levels and caries occurrence. The chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U test were employed to examine associations between SDES in different residential areas and oral health outcomes, OHB and OHK, and between OHK and its related OHB. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis with stepwise variable selection was performed to identify factors associated with high debris index scores and caries occurrence, with residential area, SDES, OHK, and OHB entered as candidate variables. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a significance level of 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 263 parents who served as primary caregivers and their infants or toddlers were included in this study, as shown in the STROBE flow chart (Fig. 1). None of the participants withdrew or dropped out during the study period. The intra- and inter-examiner reliability for caries and plaque scoring demonstrated almost perfect agreement, with Cohen’s kappa values ranging from 0.90 to 0.94. The number of children in each age subgroup was 39 aged 6–12 months, 115 aged > 12–24 months and 109 aged > 24–36 months. Of the participants, 43% were from Bangkok, the capital city, and 57% from two provincial cities in the Central region of Thailand (34.2% from Samut Prakan and 22.8% from Prachin Buri). Most parents in the capital city group were in their 30 s, while the majority in the provincial group were in their 20s. There were no differences in child demography between residential areas, whereas all parental SDES variables were found to differ significantly, including age, education, income, and parenting, as displayed in Table 1.

Oral health status

Children in provincial cities exhibited significantly higher debris index scores, which were mostly within the fair level, as well as a greater number and higher severity of decayed tooth surfaces based on ICDAS scores compared with children in the capital city (Table 1). As the study focused on very young children whose caries had not yet been treated by fillings or extractions, ICDAS scores and the number of decayed surfaces were more appropriate measures and better reflected the severity of dental caries in this population. The overall caries occurrence in children was 39.5%, increasing markedly with age: 5.1% in the 6–12-month group, 32.8% in the > 12–24-month group, and 59.6% in the > 24–36-month group. The earliest occurrence of dental caries (ICDAS 2) was in a 9-month-old infant. Teeth were affected in accordance with the sequence of tooth eruption. The labial surface of upper incisors was the first site affected by caries. Following the eruption of molars, caries was observed more frequently on the buccal surface than the occlusal surface, particularly in the upper arch. Caries appeared to extend to proximal surfaces from occlusal lesions in children aged > 24 months and were generally associated with advanced lesions (ICDAS 5–6). The debris score was significantly correlated with the number of carious surfaces (r = 0.478, p-value < 0.001, Spearman’s correlation).

Oral health knowledge

The median OHK score among parents was 6.0 (4.0) from a full score of 10 (range 0–10). Table 2 presents details of OHK questions and the percentages of correct responses. The number of correct responses in the capital group was significantly higher than in the provincial group for most of the questions. The most commonly missed question in both groups was the association between caries experience in mothers and their children. Other frequently missed questions included the association between caries in primary and permanent dentitions, the appropriate age for bottle weaning, and the recommendation of fluoridated toothpaste use in children under three years of age.

The overall data also revealed patterns of OHBs, dietary habits, and oral hygiene practices. Almost 80% of children were introduced to cariogenic snacks at the average age of 18.16 ± 6.65 months, and 57.4% to SSB at the average age of 11.26 ± 4.15 months. A total of 59.7% of parents reported brushing their children’s teeth, while 2.7% allowed their children to brush independently. However, 7.6% had not yet initiated any tooth-cleaning practices. Fluoridated toothpaste was used by 62.7% of parents. Of concern, 24.7% used a wiping gauze or toothbrush without toothpaste, and about 30.8% of children had their teeth brushed only once daily—and irregularly. Moreover, the association between OHK and OHB was analysed at the item level and conducted for conceptually related pairs of knowledge and behaviour items. A significant relationship was found between parents’ knowledge and their actual OHB in two key protective habits: the appropriate age for bottle weaning (p = 0.047) and the use of fluoridated toothpaste in children under 3 years of age (p < 0.001).

Factors associated with ECC

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis using stepwise variable selection was performed to identify factors associated with higher debris scores and caries occurrence. Several factors were significantly associated with poorer oral hygiene and increased caries occurrence (Tables 4 and 5). Factors associated with poorer oral hygiene included residence in provincial areas (2.87 times), parental education lower than a bachelor’s degree (3.92 times), cariogenic snacking (3.48 times), SSB consumption (2.79 times), and an increased number of erupted teeth (1.25 times). Similar factors were associated with caries occurrence, including residence in provincial cities (1.98 times), cariogenic snacking (2.42 times), SSB consumption (2.28 times), and an increased number of erupted teeth (1.16 times).

Discussion

This study investigated early childhood caries patterns and characteristics among Thai infants and toddlers in the capital city and provincial cities of Thailand, as well as their associated factors. Although the two areas had similar caries prevalence among children of comparable ages and numbers of erupted teeth, differences were observed in caries severity, as indicated by the number of decayed surfaces and ICDAS scores, as well as in oral hygiene levels. Provincial residence was identified as a significant factor associated with ECC in this age group. The observed disparities likely reflect underlying differences in SDES and healthcare utilisation between the two populations. Parents in provincial cities were significantly younger and had lower educational attainment and income levels than those in the capital—characteristics that are well-established key factors associated with OHK and OHB. Notably, the younger age of provincial parents warrants consideration, as it may independently influence their OHK and preventive behaviours. Younger parents may have had fewer years of engagement with the healthcare system, less prior exposure to professional dental counselling, and less accumulated health literacy, all of which could contribute to the OHK gap observed in this study. Consistently, parents in provincial areas demonstrated significantly lower OHK scores and less favourable OHBs, which were ultimately reflected in poorer oral hygiene and more severe caries in their children. Furthermore, families in provincial areas tend to access dental services less frequently, relying more on reactive rather than preventive visits, which limits their exposure to professional oral health counselling and early intervention.

Studies of ECC in this particular age group were sparse. The prevalence and severity of the disease, characterised by a higher number of caries-affected surfaces, were found to increase with age, and the number of erupted teeth was consistent with the previous findings of Vachirarojpisan et al.4 in 6–19-months-olds more than 20 years ago. The caries prevalence reported in their study was notably higher than in the present study (20.8–82.8% vs. 5.13–59.63%). The declining trend of caries prevalence is consistent with Thailand National Oral Health Surveys from 2004 to 2023, reflecting the impact of effective national oral health policies over the past two decades. However, the caries prevalence in provincial cities has always been higher than in the capital city. The caries prevalence in the Bangkok group observed in this study is comparable to the more recent report of 54.4% in 2019 by Angkatawanich et al.13. The caries prevalence among children aged 12–24 months is also close to a report in Sri Lanka (32.2%), which shares similar challenges of social inequalities14. However, it is much higher than a report of 17.8% prevalence in the two-year-old age group from a developed country in Southeast Asia like Singapore15. While oral health inequalities are well-documented in particularly vulnerable populations, such as children with special health care needs who face disproportionately high caries burden16 and frequently require dental treatment under general anesthesia17, the present findings demonstrate that such disparities are also evident among typically developing young children across varying SDES and geographic contexts.

Despite many years of intensive oral health promotion efforts, the severity of caries remains largely unchanged. The first occurrence of caries was not delayed, developing shortly after tooth eruption, particularly in anterior teeth. The caries pattern observed in this study aligns with the findings of Escobar et al.18 in preschoolers regarding the labial surfaces of anterior teeth being the most vulnerable surfaces. This highlights the importance of advocating “lifting the lip and retracting the cheeks” in parents’ toothbrushing training. However, the findings for molar teeth differed from their study, which reported caries primarily on the occlusal surfaces. Our study found a higher occurrence on the buccal surfaces of molars, which are smooth surfaces. The discrepancy could also be explained by the older age of children in the previous study, who were more likely to consume a wider variety of cariogenic foods, including sticky or retentive snacks that adhere to pits and fissures and increase the risk of occlusal caries. This caries pattern aligns with the findings of Srivastava et al.19, who reported the highest prevalence of buccal caries at age three, followed by a shift to other surfaces as children grew older.

It is widely accepted that high plaque retention serves as a caries risk indicator in young children as it harbours primary etiological factors of dental caries20,21. For this reason, the present study examined the key factors associated with high debris scores and caries occurrence separately, aiming to generate more insights for practical application. The association between increasing age, higher number of erupted teeth, and increasing dental caries is not surprising. As the number of erupted teeth increases, so does the surface area susceptible to plaque accumulation, along with more sites for food traps22–24. Interestingly, parents’ education below a bachelor’s degree was significantly associated with higher debris scores but not directly associated with caries. As young children are entirely dependent on their parents, parental education is crucial; the immediate impact may be reflected in OHB prior to caries occurrence, which is indirectly influenced and mediated by multiple factors. Practitioners should be cautious, as improving oral hygiene alone without controlling for additional risk factors may not be sufficient to reduce caries occurrence.

In addition, low OHK has been commonly found to be associated with poor OHBs25. This study also found a trend of lower OHK in parents in provincial cities with poorer OHBs. The need to improve essential knowledge and address misconceptions is therefore evident. While early intervention and primary prevention are crucial in ECC, many knowledge gaps among parents were identified, especially among parents in provincial cities. As seen in Table 2, both groups of parents were unaware of the increased risk of ECC related to vertical transmission of cariogenic bacteria26. Other knowledge gaps reflected misconceptions regarding the care of primary teeth and fluoride use in young children. It is possible that widely known oral health information, known to be associated with poor oral health outcomes in children and included in universal guidelines27–30, does not effectively reach populations outside the capital city. Possible obstacles need to be identified and overcome with appropriate strategies accordingly. Moreover, some deleterious OHBs related to commonly known knowledge were practised among most parents, such as high sugary snack and SSB consumption. This underscores the need to improve parents’ oral health literacy.

When compared to the recommended national guidelines31, many high-risk OHBs still fall short of recommendations, particularly regarding consumption of sugary snacks and beverages. SSB was introduced very early during infancy, even before bottle weaning, which may initiate poor dietary habits and early cariogenic bacterial colonisation26. Notably, some SSBs are introduced early as they are often perceived as healthy dietary choices for children while, in fact, they contribute significantly to high sugar intake and the risk of ECC32. This highlights the need for more in-depth parental knowledge on food and beverage choices.

Among the deleterious OHBs, consumption of snacks and SSBs remains a strong factor associated with poor oral health, affecting both plaque accumulation and caries status. This finding is consistent with several previous studies that have confirmed the significant impact of dietary habits on oral health outcomes33–36. A longitudinal study indicated that consumption of sugary drinks at least once a week at an early age has long-term effects on caries development37. These findings highlight the importance of dietary counselling as part of comprehensive health promotion strategies, especially in early childhood, when dietary patterns and OHBs are being established. Moreover, more in-depth knowledge of food choices—such as understanding various forms of extrinsic sugars and how to interpret nutrition labels—could be beneficial in shaping healthier dietary patterns for children, as clustered consumption of cariogenic foods has been shown to negatively impact childhood oral health32.

One of the strengths of our study is its focus on a specific age group—children aged 6 to 36 months—for which only limited and outdated information is available4,13,33. This age group has unique characteristics compared to older age groups; children in this period are entirely dependent on their parents for oral care, and it represents a critical window during which healthy oral health behaviours can be established. Moreover, this study highlights persistent differences between the capital city and urban areas of provincial cities, despite efforts to equalise public services, infrastructure, governance, and population density. Cultural beliefs, values, family-centredness, and a tendency to favour stability over rapid change among provincial residents should be explored to inform the development of effective and culturally appropriate services. Additionally, differences may include variations in health service accessibility, community health resources, traditional dietary patterns, and exposure to oral health promotion efforts. Such variation highlights the need to reconsider a one-size-fits-all approach to oral health promotion, suggesting that uniform strategies may not be equally effective. Tailored key messages and context-specific strategies are essential to address the unique needs and challenges of each community.

Notably, almost half of the children in this study were weaned from the bottle before 18 months of age. This positive trend of favourable OHB reflects growing awareness among parents or the impact of public health messaging related to infant feeding behaviours. Nevertheless, providing knowledge alone may not be sufficient, as only two out of ten knowledge items were significantly associated with actual OHB—specifically, the use of fluoridated toothpaste and appropriate age for bottle weaning. The discrepancies raise concerns about oral health literacy, cultural beliefs, or practical barriers that prevent parents from applying their knowledge to their behaviours. Therefore, knowledge must be delivered in a way that supports real-life application and should be integrated with behaviour-focused oral health strategies. In contrast, the initiation of tooth brushing falls between the rates reported in previous studies, with most parents reporting that they brush their children’s teeth (59.7% vs. 64.7%4 vs. 27.8%33. Similarly, the use of fluoridated toothpaste has increased over the decades (62.7% vs. 41.1%4, indicating a positive trend in key oral health behaviours. From a public health perspective, water fluoridation remains one of the most evidence-based strategies for population-level caries prevention38; however, Thailand relies instead on alternative fluoride delivery channels, including fluoridated toothpaste, school-based fluoridated milk programmes, and professional fluoride varnish application. Given the persistent oral health disparities observed in this study, strengthening and expanding these programmes in underserved provincial communities represents a contextually feasible approach to narrowing the oral health gap.

The limitations of this study include the use of a convenience sampling (non-probability), as the sample was recruited from families attending hospital-based dental services. These families may have higher health awareness or different sociodemographic profiles compared with the general population. This clinic-based selection bias may underestimate the true prevalence of ECC and unfavourable oral health behaviours, particularly in provincial communities. Additionally, the exclusion of ICDAS code 1 lesions, which require air-drying for reliable detection that could not be consistently applied during knee-to-knee examination of young children, may result in an underestimation of the true prevalence of incipient enamel lesions. Furthermore, the primary sample size calculation was based on a formula for estimating an infinite population mean derived from a prior intervention study, which is not fully aligned with the cross-sectional design and multivariable logistic regression employed; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Nevertheless, the sample size of the present participants was considered adequate for the regression analyses performed. Finally, the use of a self-administered questionnaire may be subject to misinterpretation and social desirability bias, potentially leading to over- or underreporting of certain OHBs. Future studies should employ probability-based sampling across a broader range of settings, including community and primary care sites, with larger sample sizes and greater diversity of study locations to improve generalisability.

In conclusion, clear differences were observed between parents residing in the capital city and those in provincial urban areas in terms of SDES, OHK, and OHBs. Regarding oral health outcomes, children in provincial areas exhibited poorer oral hygiene and higher caries occurrence. These disparities were reflected in more severe ECC among infants and toddlers aged 6–36 months in provincial areas. Key factors associated with poorer oral hygiene and ECC included provincial residence, parental education level (only for poorer oral hygiene), increased number of erupted teeth, and early consumption of snacks and SSBs.

These findings suggest that there must be further in-depth research into the health values, culture, and traditions underlying the deleterious oral health behaviours of parents in provincial areas. They highlight the importance of developing tailored, community-specific oral health promotion strategies that consider local SDES contexts and target high-risk behaviours. Additionally, more intensive oral health education programmes specifically designed for parents with lower educational attainment are warranted. Policymakers and health professionals should prioritise early prevention through parental education, dietary guidance, and improved access to pediatric oral health services, particularly in underserved communities. From a clinical standpoint, specific preventive actions should include: scheduling the first dental visit by 12 months of age, caregiver training in the ‘lift-the-lip’ brushing technique, promotion of age-appropriate fluoride toothpaste use, bottle-weaning counselling by 12–18 months, and targeted dietary guidance on sugary snacks and SSBs. Early interventions are critical to reducing childhood caries and promoting long-term oral health equity.
