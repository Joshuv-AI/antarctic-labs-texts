Structural engineering in MOFs and COFs for challenging CO2 adsorption
Authors: Qiao Zhao, Baiyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crystalline porous frameworks, particularly metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) offer unprecedented opportunities for separation applications, yet their implementation under industrially challenging conditions requires sophisticated structural engineering. This perspective addresses the critical need to develop robust design strategies that enhance stability, selectivity, and functionality across different framework types, bridging fundamental research with practical separation challenges. We systematically survey key strategies for CO2 capture under humid environments, dilute concentrations (flue gas and direct air capture), and elevated temperatures/pressures. These include hydrophobic site engineering, humidity-enhanced adsorption, strong physisorption via pore confinement and electrostatic interactions, chemisorption with amines or hydroxides, and designs for thermal/mechanical stability such as metal hydrides, interpenetrated frameworks, and mixed-matrix membranes. This perspective not only advances the fundamental understanding of structural engineering for challenging CO2 capture but also guides the future design of next-generation porous materials toward real-world applications and negative emission goals.

Introduction

Crystalline porous frameworks, particularly metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs), have emerged as a class of highly versatile adsorbent materials, capturing ever-growing research interest over the past two decades.1–5 Their intrinsic porosity, combined with the ability to finely tune pore sizes and geometries, endows them with exceptional structural flexibility and functional tunability. Furthermore, the possibility of creating partitioned pore environments or spatially separated functional domains within a single framework offers a level of control rarely achievable in conventional porous solids such as zeolites or activated carbons.6–10 This unique combination of high surface areas, adjustable chemical functionality, and architectural diversity enables the rational design of adsorption sites tailored to specific target molecules, ranging from small gas species (CO2, CH4, C2Hx) to volatile organic compounds and biomolecules.11–16 Consequently, framework materials have become a cornerstone of modern adsorption-based separation science, with a rapidly growing number of studies focused on their synthesis, regulation, and application in gas adsorption and separation.

The urgent need to mitigate climate change has driven intensive research into carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies. Traditionally, CCS has focused on point-source emissions—such as coal- or gas-fired power plants, cement kilns, and steel mills—where CO2 concentrations are relatively higher than that in ambient air (typically ∼15% for flue gas).17,18 However, the Paris Agreement and subsequent assessments have made it clear that limiting global warming to well below 2 °C will also require negative emissions technologies, among which direct air capture (DAC) stands out. DAC removes CO2 directly from ambient air (currently ∼400 ppm), offering the unique ability to reverse past emissions and offset hard-to-decarbonize sectors.19,20 Nevertheless, whether for post-combustion capture from industrial flue gas or for DAC, the practical deployment of adsorption-based technologies faces a common set of harsh, interconnected challenges that are seldom fully captured in idealized laboratory studies.

Real-world CO2 capture operates under conditions that severely test the stability, selectivity, and capacity of porous adsorbents. First, water vapor is ubiquitous—from humidity in ambient air (up to 100% relative humidity) to the wet flue gas streams of coal-fired plants.21,22 Water molecules often compete with CO2 for binding sites, particularly on open metal centers or polar functional groups, leading to reduced working capacity and, in many cases, framework degradation. Second, the CO2 concentration varies widely depending on the source: ∼15 vol% in post-combustion flue gas and only 0.04 vol% (400 ppm) in air.23,24 Such dilute conditions demand strong yet reversible CO2 binding sites with high selectivity over N2, O2, and H2O. Third, industrial flue gases are often hot (40–200 °C) and, in some cases, pressurized (e.g., natural gas sweetening at 20–60 bar).25–28 High temperatures accelerate desorption but also risk sintering, ligand rotation, or framework collapse; high pressures can induce plasticization in membranes or structural phase transitions in flexible frameworks.

To address these challenges, a new paradigm has emerged that researchers have intentionally designed porous frameworks—MOFs and COFs—to maintain or even enhance their performance under challenging conditions (Fig. 1). This perspective focuses on structural engineering strategies that address three major themes: engineering water-framework interactions for CO2 capture under humid conditions, enhancing uptake and selectivity at low concentrations, and maintaining structural integrity and function at elevated temperature or pressure. By integrating the advanced structural engineering in MOFs and COFs, this perspective aims to provide guidelines for the rational design of next-generation porous adsorbents. Our goal is to bridge the gap between laboratory-scale performance and industrial application, enabling efficient and durable CO2 capture under the challenging conditions that are essential for a sustainable energy future.

Engineering MOFs and COFs for CO2 capture in humid, dilute, and high-temperature/pressure environments.

Engineering water-framework interactions for CO2 capture under humid conditions

Conventional porous frameworks often suffer from structural degradation or loss of CO2 capacity in the presence of water vapor, because polar water molecules compete for adsorption sites and can disrupt metal–ligand coordination.29,30 Early strategies to mitigate this problem focused on introducing hydrophobic groups—such as methyl groups covalently attached to organic linkers, or weakly adsorbed hydrophobic polymer coatings—to create nonpolar microenvironments that repel water while allowing CO2 access to polar or open metal sites.31,32 However, these approaches work reasonably well under low relative humidity but often lose effectiveness as humidity rises. Recently, increasing attention has shifted toward designing framework adsorbents that maintain robust performance even under high humidity or saturated water vapor. Three advanced strategies for engineering water-framework interactions have emerged (Fig. 2)—hydrophobic site engineering, compartmentalized frameworks or composited interfaces, humidity-enhanced CO2 adsorption.

Hydrophobic site engineering

Benchmark MOF CALF-20 is a promising physisorbent for CO2 capture, but it faces a critical challenge that water vapor severely compromises its performance at high relative humidity (above 40% RH), because competitive water adsorption disrupts CO2 uptake and destabilizes the framework.33 Recently, the Farha group demonstrates that introducing methyl groups into the triazolate linker effectively tailors the hydrophobicity and pore environment, which sterically hinder the formation of water hydrogen-bonding networks and increase the distance between water molecules and Zn–N coordination bonds (Fig. 3a). As a result, the methylated variants (CALF-20M-w and CALF-20M-e) retain ∼20% of their initial CO2 capacity even at 80% RH, whereas CALF-20 loses all activity under the same conditions.34 Similarly, a methylated copper MOF (BUT-155) with open metal sites (OMS) exhibits reduced water uptake and slower water adsorption kinetics due to high-density methyl groups.35 Furthermore, a series of MOF adsorbents with finely tuned metal nodes (Zn to Ni) and methyl-functionalized ligands (TM-BDC) achieves highly controllable CO2 capture under varied humidity. The optimal Ni-TM DMOF delivers a CO2 capacity of 5.0 mmol g−1 at 273 K and 1 bar, and shows negligible performance loss under 80% RH in breakthrough experiments, further demonstrating that methyl-enhanced hydrophobicity is a robust route for humid-condition CO2 capture.36 Nevertheless, methyl grafting alone remains insufficient for highly humid flue gas, highlighting the need for exploring alternative hydrophobic functional groups or pore environments that offer stronger water exclusion without compromising CO2 affinity.

Beyond methyl group installation, fluorination has emerged as another effective strategy to create hydrophobic microenvironments.37 As reported, asymmetric trifluoromethyl functionalities installed via ligand exchange enable humidity-resistant CO2 separation (Fig. 3b).38 Similarly, a MOF-based mixed matrix membrane incorporating multiple fluorine sites was recently developed for efficient CO2/N2 separation under humid conditions.39 These fluorinated moieties impart strong hydrophobicity while preserving electrostatic interactions with polarizable CO2 molecules. In addition, in situ polymerization of aromatic acetylene within MOF-5 channels creates confined, hydrophobic polynaphthylene partitions (PN@MOF-5), doubling CO2 capacity and increasing CO2/N2 selectivity 23-fold while retaining >90% dynamic capacity under 65% RH.40 More recently, azine-linked COFs with intrinsically hydrophobic aromatic backbones (BC-Az, Py-Az) exhibit S-shaped water isotherms with minimal uptake below 40% RH, allowing CO2 adsorption without water interference at 40% RH (Fig. 3c).41 These diverse approaches—methyl functionalization, fluorination, and aromatic polymer/backbone design—collectively demonstrate that engineering hydrophobic pore environments is a powerful and generalizable route to enable efficient CO2 capture under humid conditions.

Recently, several new strategies to create hydrophobic sites have also been reported. One approach assembles a hydrophobic ionic liquid together with a fluorinated terephthalaldehyde onto the external surface of a MOF. This creates a water barrier and CO2-permeable channels in the outer shell while preserving pore integrity in the core, directly enabling the one-step separation of 99.999% pure dry CO2 from saturated (RH = 100%) humid flue gas in the breakthrough experiment.42 Similarly, encapsulating a hydrophobic ionic liquid inside MOF cavities also effectively alleviates the competition between water and CO2 in humid gas mixtures.43 In another variation, amino acids (AAs) were incorporated into the ultrastable MIP-206-OH MOF via a simple thermal formate-removal step. The resulting MIP-206-OH-AA frameworks enhance CO2 capture efficiency under both dry and humid conditions, a performance that the dynamic CO2 breakthrough time for MIP-206-OH-Gly increased from 19.8 to 21.8 min g−1 under humid conditions, with stable performance maintained over 10 cycles.44

In contrast to these physisorbents, amine-functionalized MOFs represent a distinct class of adsorbents for post-combustion CO2 capture, yet they face a critical challenge that water vapor can cause diamine leaching or degradation.45 As reported, diamine-grafted Cr-MOFs (e.g., with ethylenediamine, diethylenetriamine, or polyethylenimine) exhibit high CO2 uptake under dry conditions.46 However, in humid environments, these physically immobilized amines are prone to displacement by water molecules, leading to rapid performance decay over cycles. To mitigate this moisture-induced instability, several protection strategies have been developed. The Hong Group found that Boc (tert-butyl dicarbonate) can react rapidly with diamine groups at pore entrances to form bulky secondary and tertiary hydrophobic amines, as supported by een-MOF-Boc1, which retains excellent CO2 adsorption performance even under 10% H2O in simulated flue gas.47 An alternative approach involves reacting diamine with epoxy groups inside the pores, followed by coating with hydrophobic silane and alkyl epoxy moieties. As a result, the een-MOF/Al–Si–Cx exhibit sufficient hydrophobicity to block water vapor entry while reducing diamine volatility, maintaining stable CO2 capacity over 100 cycles under 5% H2O.48 However, these humidity levels (5–10% RH) are substantially lower than those in real flue gas (typically >50% RH), underscoring the gap between laboratory demonstrations and practical operating conditions. Furthermore, integrating triazole derivatives (e.g., 3,5-diamino-1,2,4-triazole) into Ni-MOF-74 via a dual-ligand strategy (Ni-Datz-MOF-74) also boosts CO2 uptake under humid conditions. In contrast to the physical shielding effect of hydrophobic groups, this improvement stems from a shift in adsorption mechanism that the presence of water promotes bicarbonate formation (amine : CO2 = 1 : 1), allowing more CO2 to be captured per amine group under 80% RH.49

Compartmentalized frameworks or composited interfaces

Interfacial chemical modulation works at the molecular scale by actively tuning host–guest interactions through coordination chemistry, polarity gradients, anion engineering, or adaptive framework flexibility, thereby suppressing water competition without relying on thick hydrophobic coatings. Chen and co-workers designed a biomimetic Type III porous liquid by dispersing a Zn2+-coordinated COF in a hydroxyl-functionalized ionic liquid (Fig. 4a). The IL cations coordinate inward to Zn nodes to generate Zn–OH motifs, while hydrophobic alkyl and NTf2− groups point outward, forming a polarity-graded interface that admits CO2 but excludes H2O (Fig. 4b), achieving a CO2 uptake of 4.73 mmol g−1 and a CO2/N2 selectivity of 1273.50 Sun and colleagues constructed ionic hydrophobic gates (IHG) on MOF-808 via dynamic imine assembly of a fluorinated aldehyde and an imidazolium IL, yielding a core–shell MOF@IHG. The fluorine-rich aromatic sites in the IHG shell act as hydrophobic barriers that effectively repel water molecules, as evidenced by the increased water contact angle from 30° to 95° and significantly reduced H2O uptake. As a result, the material maintains a CO2 adsorption capacity of ∼2.51 mmol g−1 under humid conditions (RH = 100%), which is comparable to that under dry conditions, demonstrating excellent water tolerance for practical CO2 capture from wet gas.42 Lim and co-workers rationally substituted anionic pillars (SiF62−, SO42−) into CALF20-type frameworks, generating nucleophilic pore environments that enhance CO2 affinity while suppressing H2O clustering, and the optimized CALF20-SiF6-met-w theoretically retains >92.4% of its CO2 uptake across all relative humidities.51 Beyond surface and pore engineering, the intrinsic flexibility of the framework itself offers an alternative route to humidity-tolerant CO2 capture. A flexible MOF (FJI-H46) was reported to undergo continuous adaptive deformation upon CO2 adsorption, stretching along the b-axis and compressing along the c-axis. Upon H2O expose, however, the framework deforms in the opposite direction, which hinders water diffusion and creates a kinetic barrier. As a result, CO2 diffuses 18 times faster than H2O through the channels, maintaining a CO2 uptake of 3.92 mmol g−1 even at 100% RH, with 97.8% of its dry-state capacity retained.52

Physical barriers and composite shells offer a more direct route by encapsulating moisture-sensitive frameworks with a thin, hydrophobic, yet gas-permeable layer that prevents water ingress while preserving pore accessibility. Li and co-workers applied a “plug-socket anchoring” strategy to grow a hydrophobic NTU-COF shell on the notoriously labile MOF-5, achieving a water contact angle of 136.3° and stable CO2/N2 separation under humid conditions.53 Yang and colleagues developed a core–shell–shell (CSS) nanocomposite in which a MOF-808 core is enveloped by a polyamine network interwoven with a COF outer shell. This architecture provides dual amine chemisorption sites and a protective barrier, delivering a CO2 uptake of 3.4 mmol g−1 at 1 bar and doubling cycling stability under humid flue gas.54 Wu and co-workers grew Mg-MOF-74 on an aminated wood sponge and overcoated it with an ultrathin PDMS layer, in which the composite retained 89% of its dry-state CO2 capacity (1.68 mmol g−1) at 50% RH, and a techno-economic analysis estimated a material cost of ca. US $10.36 kg−1, highlighting its economic viability for industrial scale-up.55

Composite shaping and industrial applicability address the engineering demands of practical deployment—mechanical strength, low pressure drop, ease of handling, and long-term stability—by embedding MOFs into porous carbon hosts or blending them with hydrophobic polymers to form beads or monoliths. Gao and co-workers confined NH2-MIL(Ti)-125 within ordered mesoporous carbon via a “ship-in-bottle” approach, creating a hydrophobic protective carapace that boosted toluene adsorption 7.4-fold at ultra-low pressure while reducing water uptake by 29%.56 Lee and colleagues blended Mg2(hob) with PVDF and shaped the mixture into beads by phase inversion, followed by postsynthetic grafting of N-methyl-1,3-propanediamine. The resulting mpn-MOF@PVDF25 retained 94% of its working capacity after 15 days at 40% RH, demonstrating that polymer encapsulation effectively suppresses amine loss and enhances moisture tolerance in a practical humid condition.57

Humidity-enhanced CO2 adsorption

Although water vapor is usually considered as a rival that occupies adsorption sites and reduces CO2 capture efficiency, increasing research shows that under certain conditions moisture can actually enhance performance. Shi and co-workers discovered that pre-adsorbed water on ZnF(daTZ) (a diaminotriazolate MOF) leads to a 25–30% increase in the isosteric heat of CO2 adsorption (Qst) compared to the dry framework. This enhancement arises from the specific structural feature of metal–triazolate frameworks, where CO2 and H2O occupy spatially separated adsorption sites—CO2 at the channel centers and H2O at the channel corners. The pre-adsorbed water molecules electrostatically polarize the triazolate rings, thereby strengthening the interaction with CO2 without blocking its binding sites. Consequently, rather than competing, water synergistically enhances CO2 physisorption.58 Furthermore, l-lysine-functionalized MOF-808 was found to undergo a conversion from carbamate/carbamic acid formation under dry conditions to bicarbonate (HCO3−) formation under humid conditions (50% RH), achieving a change in the amine : CO2 stoichiometric coefficient from 2 : 1 to 1 : 1, thereby increasing CO2 uptake from 0.612 to 1.205 mmol g−1 under direct air capture conditions (Fig. 5a). The polyamine-functionalized analog MOF-808-TAPA showed a similar but less pronounced enhancement (from 0.498 to 0.872 mmol g−1), highlighting the superior efficiency of the bicarbonate pathway enabled by accessible primary amines in a humid environment.59 Moreover, Li and co-workers recently reported that doping NiCl2 into covalent organic frameworks leads to the formation of confined water with Lewis acidity, which plays a critical role in achieving humidity-enhanced, inflectional CO2 adsorption isotherms (Fig. 5b). DFT calculations further revealed that water molecules coordinated to Ni2+ serve as hydrogen-bond donors for CO2, with binding energies reaching approximately −304 kJ mol−1 for three CO2 molecules, while benzene rings and chloride ions in the frameworks act as secondary binding sites.60 However, such strong binding inevitably makes CO2 desorption energetically demanding, posing a significant challenge for material regeneration and practical cycling. Thus, a trade-off exists between binding affinity and regenerability, which must be carefully balanced in materials design.

More interestingly, the sequence of adsorption also significantly affects humidity resistance. Hastings and co-workers found that in MOF-808 functionalized with trifluoroacetic acid (MOF-808-TFA), the co-adsorption equilibrium of CO2 and H2O is path-dependent—a violation of classical thermodynamic expectations. Preadsorbing just 0.05 mmol g−1 of CO2 completely suppresses subsequent water uptake, whereas preadsorbing water leaves appreciable CO2 capacity intact. This counterintuitive behavior arises from hemilabile modulator dynamics and missing-modulator defects: CO2 binding to open Zr sites renders the framework hydrophobic, delaying water condensation to higher relative humidities. The result demonstrates that mixed-gas adsorption equilibrium in certain MOFs is not a state function, opening new possibilities for eliminating competitive water adsorption through controlled pre-exposure to dilute CO2.61

Enhancing uptake and selectivity at low concentrations

Capturing CO2 from dilute streams such as flue gas (∼15% CO2) and air (∼400 ppm) demands adsorbents with exceptionally high affinity at low partial pressures while maintaining good regenerability. Strategies range from strong physisorption to chemisorption (Fig. 6), highlighting the importance of pore confinement, electrostatic interactions, and functional group engineering. Physisorption relies on reversible non-covalent interactions including van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding, offers the advantage of lower regeneration energy.62 Chemisorption, in contrast, involves the formation of covalent bonds, typically between aliphatic amine groups or hydroxide groups and CO2, yielding carbamates, carbamic acids, or bicarbonates. Both classes have been extensively explored for post-combustion capture and direct air capture (DAC). This section summarizes recent advances in these materials, with emphasis on their mechanism, performance and applicability to flue gas and ambient air.

Strong physisorption

Amine groups have been regarded to enhance the binding affinity of CO2 and promote selective adsorption at low CO2 partial pressures, thereby an amine-functionalized CALF-20 serving as a preferred platform framework material for low-concentration CO2 capture.63 Based on the importance of amine functionalization, Klemenčič et al. further designed a novel zinc oxalate MOF based on guanazole (3,5-diamino-1,2,4-triazole), designated as NICS-24, by introducing two amine groups on one linker. Due to the increase of amine groups concentration within the framework, the physisorption ability under extremely low CO2 concentrations was obviously enhanced, consequently NICS-24 achieving a CO2 uptake of 0.7 mmol g−1 at 2 mbar and 25 °C, significantly outperforming CALF-20 (0.17 mmol g−1) under the same conditions.64

In addition to increasing the number of physisorption sites to enhance low-concentration CO2 capture, constructing a microporous environment also contributes to strong physisorption of CO2.65 For instance, Bhatt et al. reported the deliberate construction of a hydrolytically stable fluorinated metal–organic framework (MOF), NbOFFIVE-1-Ni, with an appropriate pore system (size, shape, and functionality) ideal for the effective and energy-efficient removal of trace CO2. Remarkably, this CO2-selective physisorbent exhibits the highest gravimetric and volumetric CO2 uptake (ca. 1.3 mmol g−1 and 51.4 cm3(STP) cm−3) among physical adsorbents at 400 ppm CO2 and 298 K. The contracted square-shaped channels bring the fluorine centers into close proximity, thereby enhancing CO2-framework interactions and leading to unprecedented CO2 selectivity at very low concentrations. Complete desorption of CO2 from NbOFFIVE-1-Ni can be achieved at 328 K under vacuum with a moderate energy input of 54 kJ mol−1, which is typical for full CO2 desorption in conventional physisorbents but considerably lower than that of chemisorbents.66 Similarly, achieving perfect shape complementarity between the adsorbent and CO2 offers another powerful route to optimal physisorption. Wang et al. developed an ultrastable metal azolate framework, Zn(imPim) (MAF-stu-1), containing imidazole-derived ligands that form well-defined binding pockets, which accommodate CO2 molecules in a “fit-like-a-glove” manner (Fig. 7a). The high degree of shape complementarity, relying on multiple weak C–H⋯O and π–π interactions rather than strong chemical bonds, leads to an optimal balance of properties: high CO2 uptake (3.54 mmol g−1 at 1 bar, 298 K), good CO2/N2 selectivity (106 for 15/85 v/v), a low isosteric heat of adsorption (35 kJ mol−1).67

The construction of ionic sites that electrostatically attract CO2 has proven equally effective for low-concentration capture and high selectivity. Specifically, the NTf2− anions act as electrostatic binding site, combined with imidazolium rings and F atoms of TFTA (2,3,5,6-tetrafluoroterephthalaldehyde) as multiple binding sites that interact with CO2 through hydrogen bonding, exhibiting adsorption enthalpies ranging from −44.90 to −48.70 kJ mol−1, which significantly enhances CO2 affinity while effectively excluding N2. This synergistic interaction enables the material to achieve an exceptional CO2/N2 selectivity of 1780 (15/85, v/v) under low-concentration CO2 conditions.42 Similarly, Zhang et al. reported an anion-functionalized ultramicroporous MOF, ZU-16-Co (TIFSIX-3-Co, TiF62−, pyrazine), featuring one-dimensional channels decorated with abundant F atoms. Moreover, these Cl− sites can adaptively adjust their positions upon CO2 adsorption to optimize host–guest interactions, while the corresponding structural changes consume part of the adsorption heat, maintaining a moderate adsorption enthalpy of 37.5 kJ mol−1—a value that is sufficiently strong for efficient CO2 capture at low concentrations yet low enough to enable facile regeneration under mild conditions.

Collectively, these examples illustrate several strategies for achieving high CO2 selectivity through strong physisorption: maximizing polar binding sites (amines, fluorine, anions), tailoring pore size for confinement/molecular sieving, and introducing adaptive binding sites. The most effective designs synergistically combine these approaches to balance selectivity, capacity, and regeneration energy.

Chemisorption via covalent bonding

Chemical adsorbents form stable covalent bonds with CO2, typically through nucleophilic sites such as amines or hydroxides. Unlike aromatic amines in rigid MOF frameworks (e.g., triazolate-based NICS-24 or CALF-20), which exhibit strong physisorption due to their conjugated structures and restricted lone-pair accessibility, aliphatic polyamines such as tris(3-aminopropyl)amine possess free lone-pair electrons and can undergo nucleophilic addition with CO2 to form covalent carbamate or carbamic acid species—a typical chemisorption pathway. Lyu et al. reported the first covalent incorporation of reactive aliphatic amine species into a COF. Starting from an imine-linked COF (COF-609-Im), the imine linkages were converted to tetrahydroquinoline linkages via aza-Diels–Alder cycloaddition, followed by covalent installation of tris(3-aminopropyl)amine. For the resulting COF-609, carbamate was observed as the major chemisorption product from low-concentration CO2 (Fig. 8a), which exhibits a 1360-fold increase in CO2 uptake compared to the pristine framework.69

Beyond amines, hydroxide-based frameworks offer an alternative chemisorption pathway. Lim et al. demonstrated that the OH−-based framework Rb2CO3 CD-MOF ST captures CO2 from dilute streams such as air and natural gas. Under humid, hot conditions, it displays reversible CO2 chemisorption via bicarbonate (HCO3−) formation (Fig. 8b) – a rare behavior for OH−-based materials. However, under dry, hot conditions, the reversibly formed HCO3− sites convert irreversibly to CO32− species, a phenomenon not previously observed in hydroxide-based sorbents.70 Zick et al. also provided conclusive evidence that CO2 chemisorption in γ-cyclodextrin-based metal–organic frameworks (CD-MOFs) occurs via HCO3− formation at nucleophilic OH− sites within the pores.71

For chemisorbents, selectivity arises from the specificity of carbamate/bicarbonate formation with CO2. The key challenge lies in tuning binding strength to maintain high selectivity at low concentrations while keeping regeneration energy manageable. Yet this balance remains challenging in most reported systems, and future breakthroughs will likely come from integrating chemisorption with reversible framework transformations that lower the energy requirement for regeneration.

Flue gas capture

Flue gas generated from fossil fuel combustion, particularly in coal-fired power plants, typically contains about 70–75% N2, 12–16% CO2, 5–7% H2O, and other trace species (O2, NOx, SOx). The CO2 partial pressure in such streams is approximately 0.15 bar, which is the relevant condition for post-combustion carbon capture. Early studies on CO2 capture from flue gas often relied on adsorption performance at low partial pressures that do not fully represent these real conditions.72 In recent years, research has shifted toward evaluating adsorption capacity and selectivity under simulated flue gas, usually a mixture of ∼15% CO2 and 85% N2. To meet the industrial demands, three main design strategies have emerged: amine-based active sites, synergistic pore-site engineering, and membrane separation.

In addition to traditional monoamine grafting, a “ship-in-a-bottle” strategy was developed to crosslink small-molecule amines (tris(2-aminoethyl)amine (TAEA) or tetraethylenepentamine (TEPA)) with epoxides (trimethylolpropane triglycidyl ether or 1,3-butadiene diepoxide) inside the pores of the robust MOF Cr-BDC (also known as MIL-101(Cr)). The resulting oligomers are permanently locked in the nanospace. TAEA, bearing three primary amine branches, provides abundant active sites for high CO2 capacity (2.2 mmol g−1 at 0.15 bar, 313 K), while TEPA, a linear polyamine with more secondary amines, gives extremely low N2 uptake (0.023 mmol g−1 at 0.85 bar) and thus ultrahigh CO2/N2 selectivity (416).73 Alternatively, a systematic investigation of eleven amino acids grafted onto MOF-808 revealed that glycine (Gly) and dl-lysine (dl-Lys) provide the highest CO2 uptake under flue gas conditions (Fig. 9a), which is attributed to the high pKa of the amine groups (9.60 for Gly, 8.95/10.53 for Lys) and the high amine loading.74

Recognizing that strong adsorption sites alone are not sufficient for efficient flue gas capture, researchers have also explored the role of pore architecture. For instance, an ultramicroporous zinc-aminotriazole MOF (ZnATZoxH2O) with a customized alkaline pore environment (pore size ∼3.6 Å) was developed, which enables size-sieving of CO2 over N2, O2, and CH4, achieving a high CO2 uptake of 2.76 mmol g−1 at 298 K and 1 bar, along with ultrahigh IAST selectivities (CO2/CH4 > 105, CO2/N2 > 103).75 Furthermore, synergy between functional sites and pore confinement has been demonstrated to effectively boost both adsorption capacity and selectivity under flue gas conditions.52 Using a coordination pore-space engineering (CPSE) strategy, the cage-like proto-MFOF-1 was contracted into FJUT-1, which features reduced cage windows (down to 4.5–5.4 Å), preserved cage volume, and a functionalized surface decorated with F−/SO42− anions, pyridine, and benzene rings. Under simulated flue gas conditions, FJUT-1 exhibits CO2 capture capacities of 2.18 mmol g−1 at 298 K, 1 bar and 15% CO2/85% N2.76 Furthermore, a new generation of anion-pillared MOFs (A-Cu-TPA, A = TiF62−, SiF62−, GeF62−) features interconnected small and large cages. The small cages provide strong F⋯CO interactions for high selectivity, while the large cages offer sufficient space for high CO2 loading. Under simulated flue gas conditions (15% CO2/85% N2, 298 K, 1 bar), TIFSIX-Cu-TPA exhibits a high CO2 uptake of 2.1 mmol g−1 and a CO2/N2 IAST selectivity of 910.77

For point-source emissions with lower CO2 concentration, membrane technology that maintains both high flux and high selectivity under humid conditions is highly desired. A polyelectrolyte-mediated assembly (PMA) strategy was used to fabricate ultrathin ionic COF membranes (Fig. 9b). By assembling polyethyleneimine (PEI) with TpPa-SO3H, large COF nanosheets were bridged into a membrane as thin as 8 nm. Under simulated flue gas (20% CO2/80% N2), it achieves a CO2 permeance of 1371 GPU and a CO2/N2 selectivity of 33, surpassing most state-of-the-art COF membranes.78 Additionally, a continuous interface-coupled COF-MOF membrane was prepared by growing a COF (TaPa-1) layer on a PAN support via reverse-induced diffusion, followed by in situ growth of NH2-ZIF-8. The COF layer provides high permeation, while the MOF layer offers selective shielding. For simulated flue gas (20% CO2/80% N2, 2.5 bar, room temperature), the PAN/COF-MOF membrane shows a CO2 permeance of 825 GPU and a selectivity of 29.9 (dry), and even better performance under humid conditions (1346 GPU, 37), demonstrating excellent potential for practical flue gas separation.79

Direct air capture

Direct air capture aims to extract CO2 directly from the ambient atmosphere, where the CO2 concentration is only about 400–420 ppm (0.04%). At such extremely low partial pressures (∼0.4 mbar, 25 °C), most conventional adsorbents show negligible uptake. MOFs with their high structural order and tunable pore sizes, offer an ideal platform to achieve strong host–guest interactions under these trace CO2 conditions.80,81

A recent breakthrough is the development of a new extended MOF-74-type variant, M2(hob) (M = Mg2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Zn2+; hob4− = an azine-linked ligand obtained via room-temperature condensation of 5-formylsalicylic acid and hydrazine). Functionalization of Mg2(hob) with primary-secondary diamines (N-methylethylenediamine, N-ethylethylenediamine) or the secondary–secondary diamine N,N′-dimethylethylenediamine (mmen) enables strong interactions with low-pressure CO2, which deliver top-tier CO2 adsorption capacities of 2.60, 2.49, and 2.91 mmol g−1 at 400 ppm CO2 and 25 °C (Fig. 10a), respectively. This work highlights that the cooperative CO2 insertion mechanism between Mg2+ open metal sites and properly selected diamines (e.g., mmen) shifts the step pressure to as low as ∼0.1 mbar, well below the partial pressure of atmospheric CO2.82 Although this series of materials can capture CO2 from air, the slow adsorption kinetics (∼1500 min for saturation) highlight a major practical limitation, which might originate from the limited number and restricted accessibility of the diamine species appended to the open metal sites within the framework.

In parallel, COFs have emerged as promising platforms for DAC due to their tunable pore environments and robust linkages. Early efforts focused on channel-wall functionalization of a conventional 2D imine-linked COF with carboxylic acid groups, which enhanced CO2 uptake and selectivity for CO2/N2 separation, laying the groundwork for further amine-based modifications.83 A significant advance came with the first covalent incorporation of aliphatic polyamines into a COF, achieved by crystallizing an imine-linked COF (COF-609-Im), converting its linkages to stable tetrahydroquinoline via aza-Diels–Alder cycloaddition, and finally installing tris(3-aminopropyl)amine. The resulting COF-609 exhibited a 1360-fold increase in CO2 uptake over the pristine framework, reaching 0.304 mmol g−1 at 400 ppm under dry conditions and a further 29% enhancement under 50% RH, with carbamate identified as the major chemisorption product.69 To overcome amine leaching and hydrolysis, a polyethylenimine-functionalized COF (COF-709) was developed. By converting imine linkages to stable amides and covalently anchoring polyamines via C–S bonds through aromatic nucleophilic substitution, COF-709 achieved a CO2 capacity of 0.48 mmol g−1 at 400 ppm under dry conditions, which increased to 1.24 mmol g−1 at 75% RH. The material showed no capacity loss over 10 temperature-swing cycles at a low regeneration temperature of 95 °C, demonstrating excellent stability.84

A major milestone was the development of COF-999, an olefin-linked COF with covalently attached polyamines. Under dry 400 ppm CO2, COF-999 captured 0.96 mmol g−1, and under 50% RH the capacity more than doubled to 2.05 mmol g−1. It reached half capacity in only 18.8 min and was regenerated at 60 °C. Remarkably, 100 adsorption–desorption cycles were performed over 20 days using outdoor air in Berkeley, California, with full retention of performance, confirming its exceptional robustness and practicality.85 Most recently, COF-1000 pushed the kinetics even further, achieving a CO2 capacity of 1.31 mmol g−1 under dry conditions (half-capacity in 8.1 min) and 2.19 mmol g−1 at 75% RH (half-capacity in 6.8 min). When tested with outdoor air, it delivered 50 cycles within 3 days, corresponding to a daily CO2 uptake of 22.1 mmol g−1 d−1, surpassing all current state-of-the-art DAC sorbents.86

Membrane-based direct air capture (m-DAC) has also gained attention as an energy-efficient alternative. However, due to the extremely low CO2 concentration in air (∼400 ppm), the need for simultaneously high CO2 permeance and high CO2/N2 selectivity, as well as the challenges of membrane fouling and long-term stability under ambient conditions, only a limited number of membrane materials have been reported for direct air capture to date. A synergistic strategy combining a highly CO2-permeable polymer of intrinsic microporosity (PIM-1) and a moderately permeable but highly selective block copolymer (PolyActive) was developed. By using a two-step solvent-assisted ligand exchange (2SALE) to create a ZIF-94 filler with hydrophilic (aldehyde) and hydrophobic (undecyl) ligands (Fig. 10b), the compatibility with the polymer matrix was improved, minimizing interfacial defects. In a thin-film nanocomposite configuration, this system achieved a CO2 permeance of 1037 GPU with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 16.2 at 500 ppm CO2, and process modeling showed that combining PIM-1 in the first stage with PolyActive in subsequent stages significantly reduces the required membrane area while achieving high CO2 purity.87 Nevertheless, membrane-based DAC faces inherent challenges that the ultrathin selective layers required for high permeance are susceptible to defects and fouling, leading to the CO2/N2 selectivity reported to date remains far below that achieved by many MOF adsorbents.

CO2 capture under elevated temperatures and pressures

Real-world CO2 capture often operates under harsh conditions: high temperatures (post-combustion flue gas, 40–400 °C, though it can cool to ∼50 °C after desulfurization), and high pressures (natural gas upgrading, pre-combustion capture at 15–20 bar or higher).88 While many MOFs and COFs exhibit excellent CO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity under ambient conditions, their performance under such demanding environments is often limited by framework degradation, pore collapse, loss of accessible sites, or unfavorable thermodynamics. Only a limited number of materials have been engineered to retain structural integrity and capture efficiency under these conditions, typically through robust coordination bonds, rigid or interpenetrated frameworks, or unique binding mechanisms that tolerate thermal, mechanical, and chemical stress. This section reviews recent advances in porous adsorbents and membranes that maintain their stability and performance under such challenging environments.

Elevated-temperature CO2 capture

Conventional physisorbents lose capacity at elevated temperatures due to weakened van der Waals interactions. Chemisorbents with strong, reversible binding sites can operate at much higher temperatures. A landmark example is ZnH-MFU-4l, a MOF featuring terminal zinc hydride sites that reversibly capture CO2via insertion into Zn–H bonds (Fig. 11). This material operates between 200 and 400 °C, achieving a remarkable CO2 uptake of 3.52 mmol g−1 at 150 °C and 1 bar, with rapid kinetics and excellent cycling stability.89 This work highlights a general structural engineering strategy that anchoring highly reactive metal–hydride (or metal–alkyl) species onto a rigid, highly connected porous backbone, using elevated temperature to “activate” the chemical reaction while preventing irreversible byproduct formation (e.g., carbonate), thereby enabling reversible chemisorption of specific gas molecules at high temperatures.

Another strategy relies on rigid frameworks decorated with hydrogen-bond donors or electrostatic sites that sustain physisorption at elevated temperatures. An atypical pillar-layered MOF Zn-OX-DATZ (OX = oxalate, DATZ = 3,5-diamino-1,2,4-triazolate) possesses one-dimensional channels decorated with amino groups, in which its rigid Zn–N/Zn–O coordination framework prevents structural collapse, and the dense array of –NH2 groups provides multiple hydrogen bonds with CO2. Consequently, it maintains high CO2 adsorption capacities from room temperature up to 180 °C, with negligible loss after 70 cycles at 120 °C.90 Similarly, CUMT-1a integrates Brønsted acidic –COOH and Lewis basic triazolyl groups within its pores. This dual-functional design enables a high CO2 uptake of 4.6 mmol g−1 at 25 °C and 1 bar, while retaining excellent adsorption capacity even at 60 °C (333 K). The sustained performance originates from the synergistic cooperation of –COOH (hydrogen-bond donors) and triazolyl N atoms (electrostatic interactions), avoiding the steep capacity loss typical of purely physisorptive systems.91 In some cases, elevated temperature can even promote CO2/N2 selectivity. A three-fold interpenetrated Co(ii) framework (FCG-1a) with carboxamide-functionalized channels exhibits strong framework–CO2 interactions and, remarkably, an 82% increase in CO2/N2 selectivity when the temperature rises from 0 °C (selectivity 79) to 40 °C (selectivity 143), which is rarely observed and highly desirable for practical flue gas separation. This can be attributed to the dual effect of the carboxamide groups acting as hydrogen-bond donors that maintain relatively strong CO2 affinity even at elevated temperatures, while the three-fold interpenetration creates a constrained pore environment that minimizes N2 adsorption. As temperature increases, the already weak N2-framework interactions diminish further, leading to a net increase in selectivity.92 Another entangled Co(ii) framework (CSMCRI-25a) decorated with urea groups shows appreciable CO2 adsorption even at 40 °C (313 K), with the selectivity increasing by 40% at that temperature compared to 0 °C, underscoring the potential of hydrogen-bond-donor moieties for high-temperature carbon capture.93

A further approach involves confinement-driven physisorption in contracted ultramicropores with electrostatic potential matching.52 The shrunken windows amplify the confinement effect on CO2, while polar anionic sites offer electrostatic binding—together delivering a moderate Qst that enables effective CO2 capture even at elevated temperatures.76 Furthermore, following an electrostatic potential matching strategy, the anion-pillared MOF HBU-24 (Cd2+, TiF62−, and a tetrapyridyl ligand) achieves a CO2 uptake of 2.67 mmol g−1 at 50 °C and a high CO2/N2 selectivity of 1662.2, with breakthrough experiments confirming its suitability for high-temperature, humid flue gas.94 In a broader context of wide-temperature and high-pressure operation, Zr-based MOF-808 demonstrates stable CO2 adsorption under high-pressure conditions (up to 100 bar) and temperatures of 45–65 °C, with physisorption-dominant behavior, low regeneration energy, and enhanced capacity in humid environments.28 For CO2 adsorption from natural gas mixtures, TAMOF-1, a Cu-based MOF featuring square-planar Cu2+ sites and channel-dangling carboxylate/triazole/imidazole groups, relies on weak physisorption interactions. It separates CO2/CH4 mixtures with high selectivity over a wide temperature (20–80 °C) and pressure (1–6 bar) range, owing to its rigid paddle-wheel framework and multiple weak hydrogen-bonding sites whose interactions with CO2 decay less rapidly with temperature than typical van der Waals forces. Its low-energy regeneration and robust performance under humid and even mildly acidic conditions make it a promising candidate for biogas upgrading.95

Elevated-pressure CO2 capture

Conventional adsorbents and membranes often suffer from CO2-induced plasticization, structural collapse, or irreversible phase transitions under the high pressures required for natural gas sweetening and post-combustion carbon capture. To address these challenges, recent research has focused on three distinct yet complementary structural engineering strategies that preserve integrity and enhance separation performance under elevated CO2 pressures. ZIF-8 has been identified as a potential adsorbent for CO2 removal from natural gas streams under high-pressure operation. Santos and co-workers demonstrated that ZIF-8 retains its crystallinity and chemical integrity after exposure to CO2 and N2 at pressures up to 200 bar across various temperatures, with no permanent structural changes observed.96 Notably, the intrinsic flexibility of MOFs was found to reversibly accommodate CO2 without permanent degradation under elevated-pressure conditions. The flexible MOF Zn2(BDC)2DABCO undergoes an irreversible phase transition under pressure in its activated state, but CO2-loaded samples exhibit continuous unit-cell contraction without phase transition, with two distinct CO2 adsorption sites emerging above 0.79 GPa; moreover, simultaneous high-pressure and high-temperature conditions enhance CO2 uptake, demonstrating that pressure-stimulated structural changes can be harnessed to improve adsorption performance.97

Furthermore, integrating porous fillers into polymer matrices can effectively suppress plasticization, a major issue for polymeric membranes under high CO2 partial pressures. Ahmad and co-workers developed 6FDA-DAM mixed-matrix membranes with up to 16 wt% Zr-based MOFs, achieving stable CO2/CH4 separation up to 20 bar without plasticization, even in the presence of H2S.98 Shahid and Nijmeijer compared MIL-53(Al), ZIF-8, and Cu3BTC2 in Matrimid®-PI and found the neat polymer plasticized above 10–12 bar, all MOF-based mixed-matrix membranes suppressed plasticization up to 40 bar.99 This concept has been extended to more sophisticated architectures, such as polymer-woven MOF glass membranes inspired by spider silk, where the woven polymer prevents lattice deformation during melting and creates continuous subnanometer channels, achieving a CO2 permeability of 1750 Barrer, a CO2/N2 selectivity of 63, and unprecedented pressure tolerance up to 7.5 atm (Fig. 12).100 Most recently, a covalent site-specific polymerization strategy for metal–organic cages (MOCs) was reported, in which amino-functionalized zirconium MOCs are polymerized with poly(ethylene glycol) diisocyanate to yield a continuous membrane with a remarkable MOC loading of 75 wt%, uniform cage distribution, and a CO2 permeability of 577 Barrer with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 50 at 2 bar, surpassing the 2008 Robeson upper bound.101

COFs have also been systematically engineered for high-pressure CO2 capture. Zhang and co-workers synthesized three N-rich 2D COFs (COF-SDU1, COF-SDU2, and COF-SDU3) and revealed that the N-content governs CO2 uptake at low pressures (<1 bar), the BET surface area plays a decisive role at moderate pressures (<25 bar), and a large pore size enhances capacity at higher pressures (>25 bar), with COF-SDU1 achieving a remarkable CO2 uptake of 741 mg g−1 at 298 K and 45 bar.102 A complementary computational study by Fang and co-workers demonstrated that Cl-functionalization of COF10 significantly enhances CO2 adsorption capacity by 75–78% while creating stronger diffusion barriers for CO2 than for CH4, leading to a dramatic increase in adsorption selectivity from 3.75 for pristine COF10 to 13.76–13.77 at 298 K and up to 9 MPa.103 An entirely different but equally promising strategy relies on latent porosity rather than permanent channels. Vitórica-Yrezábal and co-workers developed flexible silver coordination polymers bearing perfluoroalkylcarboxylate ligands, which are non-porous under ambient conditions but undergo a gate-opening mechanism for CO2 above a threshold pressure, comparable to dissolution in fluoroalkanes. Because CH4 uptake is negligible due to alkane–perfluoroalkane immiscibility, nearly 100% CO2/CH4 selectivity is achieved at high pressures, and the gate pressure can be rationally tuned by varying the perfluoroalkyl chain length.104

Conclusions and outlook

In this perspective, we have systematically surveyed the key strategies for engineering MOFs and COFs towards CO2 capture under challenging conditions—humid environments, dilute CO2 concentrations, elevated temperatures and pressures. We highlight how targeted structural engineering—ranging from molecular-level active sites (metal hydrides, amines, hydroxides, hydrogen-bond donors) and pore functionalization (fluorine/chlorine anions, carboxamide) to architectural features such as interpenetration, core–shell composites, and mixed-matrix membranes—enables precise control over framework properties, leading to enhanced CO2 capacity, selectivity, and stability for post-combustion and direct air capture.

In the future, beyond MOFs and COFs, emerging reticular materials such as porous organic cages (POCs) and hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) offer complementary advantages, including solution processability, metal-free composition, and facile recyclability. Future efforts should focus on creating chemical gradients in POCs for sequential separations, and reinforcing HOFs via interpenetration or heterocyclic modules to enhance stability. With rational design and data-driven discovery, the integration of these diverse platform materials can pave the way toward sustainable, cost-effective carbon capture technologies capable of meeting net-zero targets and even achieve negative emissions.
