Understanding the situation and challenges of falls prevention work in clinical settings for geriatric healthcare in China: a mixed-method study
Authors: Qi Zhou, Jiling Liao, Hengshuo Liu, Yuanyuan Jiang, Dingyi Wang, Xinmiao Chang, Juan Wu, Huan Xi, Xiaofen Wu, Haitao Chen, Li Cao, Wenbin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a major public health concern in rapidly ageing countries. While guidelines for preventing falls exist, their application remains arduous. This study aimed to understand the situation and challenges of falls prevention work in Chinese clinical practices for geriatric healthcare.Design and methodsA mixed-methods convergent design was used with a nationwide quantitative survey and qualitative interviews. Geriatricians, general practitioners, and nurses from tertiary/secondary hospitals and community healthcare centres were recruited via purposive and snowball sampling. Domains and themes were developed following the integration of the two results (survey and interview)—focused on falls prevention status and implementation barriers—within the framework of the 2022 World Falls Guideline.ResultsThree domains and nine themes were identified. The domains were (1) The extent of falls prevention practices, (2) Understanding on the clinical importance and effectiveness of falls prevention, and (3) Challenges in implementing falls prevention programs. The themes were (1) Systematic falls prevention was not widely conducted, (2) Fewer practices in community healthcare centres versus secondary and tertiary hospitals, (3) Wards implemented more clinical practices than outpatient settings, (4) Falls were not deemed as a primary concern in clinical practice, (5) Scepticism about the effectiveness of interventions, (6) How to select patients? (7) Inadequate competence in assessment and intervention, (8) A lack of integrated care and pathways, and (9) Financing challenges.Conclusions and ImplicationsThis study concluded that while the importance of falls prevention was widely recognized in both outpatient and inpatient settings, its implementation remained unsystematic. Narrowing the knowledge-to-practice gap is a key priority for geriatric healthcare settings when rolling out evidence-based falls prevention initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07317-w.

Background

Falls have become a major public health concern in China due to the rapidly growth of the ageing population. The incidence rate of falls among older adults reached 3799.4 per 100,000 population, and the rate of mortality caused by falls was 39.2 per 100,000 population in 2019 [1]. In a recent study, China was found to bear a substantial burden of falls in 2021; particularly, the prevalence, incidence, and mortality rates rose sharply among individuals aged 75 years and older [2]. Falls are described as a geriatric syndrome and require multifactorial intervention to prevent further incidents [3].

Older outpatients and inpatients are often at risk of falls. They are more likely to be frail, suffer from malnutrition, sarcopenia, and multiple comorbidities, and experience physical and functional impairments [4]. Many of these issues are risk factors for falls; therefore, addressing these factors appears more useful for falls prevention when older adults visit outpatient clinics or reside in wards [5].

The Chinese government has issued several policy documents to address the healthcare issues arising from the rapid growth of the ageing population. This has opened opportunities for implementing falls prevention guidelines in hospitals. For instance, The National Health Commission of China (NHCC) mandates that more than 60% of the public hospitals at the secondary level and higher should establish geriatric medicine departments by 2025 [6]. According to the National Essential Public Health Service Specification (2013 Edition) [7], annual health management is required at community healthcare centres. These policies will increase the number of older adults visiting geriatric and general medicine clinics daily. Consequently, more older adults will benefit from the integration of falls risk screening, patient education, and interventions into routine clinical practices.

Implementing falls prevention guidelines into practice often proves challenging due to the misalignment between current guidelines and local conditions [8]. Previous studies identified multiple barriers to implementing falls prevention, mostly in primary healthcare settings [9, 10]. A common finding was the importance of considering the stakeholders' views on falls prevention. Researchers posit that elements such as insufficient financial backing, a dearth of medical practitioners, and restricted human and temporal resources could potentially obstruct the execution of falls prevention guidelines. However, the majority of previous studies were conducted in Western countries, raising uncertainties about the generalizability of these findings to Chinese hospital settings. Furthermore, most prior interview outlines were developed independently without including the context of falls prevention guidelines. As a result, we could not identify detailed factors associated with the implementation of a specified guideline.

Generally, the implementation of falls prevention guidelines in hospitals should harmonize with the conditions of the hospital and the beliefs of healthcare practitioners. Nevertheless, this issue has been somewhat overlooked in studies conducted in Chinese hospitals. The 2022 World Falls Guideline (WFG) considers the feasibility of implementation in resource-constrained settings, such as low- and middle-income countries [3]. However, the factors related to implementation have not been sufficiently studied yet. Against this background, this study presents the following research questions:To what extent are falls prevention measures implemented in geriatric medicine wards and outpatient clinics?What challenges should we address when implementing falls preventions in outpatient and inpatient settings?

Methods

Design

A mixed-methods convergent design (a quantitative phase and a qualitative phase) was conducted from July 28 to December 31, 2023 (Fig. 1). The quantitative phase of the study included a nationwide survey, which was designed to preliminarily understand the status of falls prevention practices across different clinical settings. The qualitative phase included semi-structured interviews with health professionals to deeply understand their beliefs and views about falls prevention practices and the WFG. The determinants to implementing WFG were synthesized and interpreted based on the integrated findings of both phases.Fig. 1Overview of the study design

Overview of the study design

Pragmatism served as the guiding philosophy of this study because all of the findings were intended to propose practical methods for putting WFG into practice [11].The research materials, including the survey and interview topics and questionnaires, were developed based on the WFG theoretical framework, combined with the clinical experiences of frontline nurses and physicians.

COREQ-checklists were used to ensure all the items relevant to qualitative research were included [12]. This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was granted by the Beijing Hospital Research Ethics Committee (2021BJYYEC-173–02).

Settings

This study focused on both outpatient and inpatient settings. Geriatric medicine department in tertiary and secondary hospitals as well as general medicine department in community healthcare centers were included because they were important places for primary and secondary prevention among older adults.

Settings were selected by recruiting related healthcare providers using purposive and snowball sampling methods (Supplementary file 1). Briefly, four researchers (Wenbin Wu, Li Cao, Haitao Chen, and Xiaofen Wu) initiated participant recruitment via the networks of the National Centre of Gerontology of the National Health Commission and the Chinese Association of Geriatricians – both with members across most Chinese provinces. They contacted one geriatrician per Chinese mainland province, enrolling 32 tertiary hospital staff. These staff participated in the survey and served as provincial contacts for further recruitment. Finally, a total of 2351 participants agreed to participate in the survey; 2163 respondents answered the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 92.0%. They were in 218 out of 333 (65.5%) prefecture-level regions and 32 out of 34 (94.2%) province-level regions in China.

Research group and participants

A research group comprising the authors experienced in falls prevention practices and WFG was established. It consisted of seven geriatricians (Wenbin Wu, Xinmiao Chang, Jiling Liao, Li Cao, Haitao Chen, Huan Xi, and Xiaofen Wu), one nurse (Juan Wu), one geriatric researcher (Qi Zhou), and one graduate student in geriatric medicine (Yuanyuan Jiang). They provided their knowledge and expertise to both the quantitative and the qualitative study.

In the quantitative study, geriatricians, general practitioners, and nurses were asked to describe the current status of falls prevention in their outpatient clinics and wards. In the qualitative study, interviewees were purposively contacted if (1) they reported familiarity with falls prevention practices in the baseline questionnaire; (2) their department was conducting falls prevention; and (3) they were administering some of WFG tests, such as comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), as part of their daily works. We called 20 participants and all of them finished our interview.

Qualitative study: semi-structured interviews

The interviews were performed in an online meeting room (Tencent Technology, Shanghai Co., Ltd) from October 2023 to December 2023. An interview guide was developed based on the algorithm of WFG and a modified executive framework described before [13]. The micro-, mid-, and macro-level components that comprised the executive framework were included in the guide as keywords (Supplementary file 3). These factors included policies, financial resources, human resources, the clinicians’ competency in learning WFG, and collaboration between different departments and health professionals. The data of interviews were simultaneously analysed, allowing the interview guide to be update whenever new themes merged.

The interview started after a verbal consent to participate among interviewees. It was video- or audio-recorded and lasted between 30 and 60 min. Wenbin Wu, Qi Zhou and Yuanyuan Jiang conducted the interviews. The interviewees were supposed to be relatively homogenous according to the inclusion criteria; a narrow range of interviews (n = 9 ~ 17) and focus group discussions (n = 4 ~ 8) can reach saturation in qualitative analysis [14]. Data collection continued after 15 interviews to ensure no new codes emerged, and data saturation was reached at the 20th interview.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse participant characteristics and clinical settings. The Chi-square test was also employed to examine conditional differences between clinical settings that performed fall prevention practices and those that did not perform them. The relationship between fall prevention practices and the conditions of the studied clinical settings was examined by logistic regressions analysis (R × 64 for Windows, version 4.0.0). Qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis based on a framework method [15]. Audio records of interviews were transcribed in verbatim, and then Qi Zhou and Wenbin Wu independently coded every line of the interview content based on deductive and inductive approaches in MAXQDA 2020 (VERBI Software, 2019). Codes were used to generate domains and themes.

Data integration procedures were 1) obtaining results by analysing quantitative and qualitative data, 2) comparing the results and looking for each concept, 3) interpreting differences and similarities through group discussion, 4) generating domains and underlying themes.

Domain 1: The extent of falls prevention practices in clinical practices

Theme 1.1 Systematic falls prevention was not widely conducted

Table 1 shows that 1283 (71.4%) settings hold at least one relevant training each year. About half of the investigated settings (49.5%) were conducting falls prevention practices. Most tools had a utilization rate of under 50%; several WFG-recommended tools (e.g., the 3KQ) exhibited an even lower rate of roughly 25% (Fig. 2C, D). The in-depth interviews showed that the clinical settings conducted fewer multifactorial assessments and interventions compared to risk factor screening, which indicated a lack of systematic practices. Falls prevention practices were serious matters in the wards, but most of the measures focused on risk stratification and primary patient education. Efforts to identify the potential causes of patient falls and eliminate them remained insufficient. Similar results were found in outpatient settings.

It helps us to find patients who is at risk of falls, we will remind the doctors and caregivers to pay more attention to them. We did not used the WFG, since the application of current standards isn't obligatory.” (Participant 12, nurse, female, 38 years old, tertiary hospitals).

Theme 1.2 Lower practices in community healthcare centres versus secondary and tertiary hospitals

Compared to tertiary (60.0%) and secondary hospitals (56.3%), community healthcare centres (30.5%) carried out less falls prevention practices. This could be resulted from the inadequate medical resource in community healthcare centres (Table 1). Community healthcare centers hold less trainings (54.7%) than secondary (75.3%) and tertiary hospitals (82.8%) in our data (Supplementary file 6).

Falls prevention practice was also significantly associated with sufficient and skilled human resources, strong leadership support, practices of CGA, and more related research in this area (all P < 0.05) (Tables 1 and 3). Theme 1.3 Wards implement more clinical practices than outpatient settings

The practice rate in the wards was significantly higher (81.2%) than that in outpatient clinics (31.0%) (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Most interviewed clinicians acknowledged that, according to department regulations, falls prevention is obligatory for inpatients but not for outpatients. The rate of utilization of falls prevention tools was also higher in wards compared to outpatient clinics (Fig. 2CD). This may also correlate with more frequent training, enhanced leadership support, and greater knowledge availability in wards (Supplementary file 7). Equipment and space factors may additionally contribute to the observed results. In-depth interviews revealed that outpatient departments, especially in tertiary hospitals, have insufficient space for mobility testing.

Domain 2: Understanding on the clinical importance and effectiveness of falls prevention

Theme 2.1 Falls was not deemed as the primary concern in clinical practices

Although 1142 participants (63.5%) agreed their department heads were very concerned about falls prevention, and 1077 (59.9%) reported familiarity with it (Table 1), falls prevention was not a central concern in clinical practices. Healthcare professionals prefer to treat diseases such as sarcopenia and malnutrition or address acute conditions in clinical practice. Patients' attitudes also play a crucial role in determining clinicians’ approach to falls prevention. Fewer patients realize they are at risk of falls and should visit clinics. For fallen patients and their families, they simply do not understand why a systematic fall risk assessment is necessary.

“I agree that falls prevention is important for the elderly; however, addressing the acute conditions is a higher priority”. (Participant 8, geriatrician, male, 40 years old, tertiary hospital.)

Theme 2.2 Scepticisms about the effectiveness of interventions

Some interviewees expressed skepticism about the effectiveness of falls prevention interventions. The lack of attention to falls may stem from insufficient confidence in the efficacy of falls prevention strategies, and the multifactorial nature of falls may further contribute to such doubts.

“I read the guidelines and found only a few evidence of interventions to be very strong.” (Participant 4, geriatrician, female, 46 years old, tertiary hospital.)

Domain 3: Challenges in implementing a falls prevention program

Theme 3.1 How to select patients?

Clinicians were uncertain about which older patients should be prioritized for falls prevention due to the limited time and human resources in clinical practices. The WFG recommends identifying opportunistic cases. However, some geriatricians felt that it could consume so much time that they may neglect other patients in outpatient clinics. The same issue occurred in wards, where clinicians struggled to develop comprehensive plans for all inpatients due to their heavy daily workload and the belief that it was not applicable for many inpatients.

“I usually have around 40 to 50 outpatient appointments in the morning. Asking each patient three questions (3KQ) works well, as it doesn’t take too much time. However, if we go a step further to assess their gait, balance, and walking speed, we won’t be able to finish all appointments—since other patients are waiting.” (Participant 4, geriatrician, female, 46 years old, tertiary hospital.)

Theme 3.2 Inadequate competence in assessment and intervention

As a geriatric syndrome, falls intervention requires more extensive knowledge than falls risk screening. 59.9% of participants (n = 1077) stated that they were familiar with falls prevention (Table 1). However, fewer than 40% of the geriatricians or general practitioners were acquainted with the common tools used for falls prevention (Fig. 2AB). The awareness rate was even lower among clinicians in community healthcare centres, all of which indicated insufficient knowledges of falls prevention among the participants. A general practitioner (Participant 19) from a community healthcare centre felt that screening of older adults who at high risk of falls was easy, while the intervention and multifactorial assessment (such as the polypharmacy assessment) seemed not applicable due to her inadequate knowledge.

Clinicians faced greater challenges in intervening in falls than in identifying those at risk. According to the fall risk screening tools recommended by WFG, more than 75% of participants found them feasible for use in both outpatient and inpatient settings (Fig. 2EF). However, when it comes to falls interventions, none of the interviewed participants provided a specified description of intervention strategies for falls.

Theme 3.3 A lack of integrated care and pathways

In wards, geriatricians usually prevailed over multidisciplinary team management, the primary method for multifactorial analysis of falls risk. Conversely, all interviewees expressed uncertainty about collaboration methods in outpatient clinics. All team members concurred that there was a lack of a standard operating procedure, based on WFG, when hospitals implement a systematic falls prevention program, particularly in outpatient clinics.

Theme 3.4 Financing challenges

The last barrier to falls intervention, acknowledged by all interviewed participants, was the financial issue. One geriatrician described the financial shortage as "the biggest barrier" to implementing the WFG in hospitals. Two hospitals tried to charge for multifactorial assessments, such as CGA. However, they were hindered by a lack of national or local policies providing guidance on charging for fall prevention measures.

"Multifactorial assessments consume substantial time and human resources. The more complex the assessment, the more necessary it is to charge. Without charging, we lack the incentive to carry it out, and my department can't hire enough personnel to do so… It is better to complete the test in 5–10 min " (Participant 2, geriatrician, male, 42 years old, tertiary hospital.)

Discussion

This study aimed to comprehend the current state of practices for preventing falls in outpatient and ward settings, and to understand the implementation challenges. The combination of a nationwide survey with in-depth interviews allowed us to pinpoint three domains and nine themes that should be taken into account when integrating a falls prevention program into clinical settings.

One of the main findings of our research indicates that falls prevention for elderly patients did not seem to be systematically executed in the investigated clinical settings. Falls are characterized as a geriatric syndrome, and preventive measures should not only be confined to the stratification of fall risks among the older population but should also extend to identifying multifaceted risk factors to prevent future falls [3]. The current study observed that the risk of falls was routinely screened in wards, yet there was little emphasis on multifactorial assessments and comprehensive interventions. In outpatient settings, both risk screenings and multifactorial interventions were scarcely performed due to the restricted time and human resources.

Our findings also revealed some challenges potentially impacting the implementation of a falls prevention guideline. Some of the China’s healthcare practitioners’ perspectives in this study paralleled those from Western countries [9, 16]. These factors included 1) practitioners’ unawareness of falls when compared with other preventive health measures, 2) the difficulties to implement multifactorial interventions which might reduce the continuity of implementing the WFG guideline,3) lack of financial support, and 4) heavy workload with limited medical resources.

Practitioners' insufficient awareness of falls and skepticism about the benefits of falls prevention may also affect the use of the WFG in clinical practices. They often prefer to treat a 'real disease', like sarcopenia and osteoporosis, rather than focusing on falls prevention. Similar attitudes were noted among clinicians when asked about treating frailty in clinical practices [17]. These findings suggest a lack of understanding and inadequate implementation about the multidisciplinary nature of care for older patients. In fact, implementing the WFG, which emphasizes a multifactorial geriatric assessment, could prevent falls and optimize functional ability among older adults [18]. However, few interviewees in this study realized the relationship between falls prevention and other geriatric syndromes. The Ministry of Education has required colleges to introduce geriatric courses to increase the number of geriatricians [19]. In addition, geriatric medicine faculty training programs leaded by Chinese Association of Geriatricians or some in top-tier teaching hospitals are already underway in China to improve the overall quality of geriatric specialty training across the country. Against the backdrop of geriatric medicine’s rapid advancement in China over the past decade, geriatricians ought to engage widely and deeply in these training programs [20].

Promoting the advancement of geriatric medicine departments in China might expedite the systematic implementation of falls prevention guidelines among older adults in hospitals. Because all the determinants for the implementation of falls prevention are closely related to the core concept of modern geriatric medicine, such as geriatric syndrome, CGA, and functional rehabilitation and maintenance [20, 21]. The Chinese government has implemented a policy to augment the number of geriatric medicine departments [6]. The growth of these departments made the pathways or points of entry to conduct a fall risk assessment clearer. Moreover, linking falls prevention with CGA may be a practical solution to overcome the competing of clinical priorities between falls and other geriatric syndrome, since they share the same core concept and it showed significant association with the falls prevention practices in this result Chinese experts have recently reached a consensus on the applications of CGA [22], which provided standard operating procedures to assessing various facets of geriatric syndrome, such as fall, malnutrition, urinary incontinence, frailty, and sarcopenia. How to integrate the treatment of falls into a general clinical practice such as the treatment of osteo-cardiovascular diseases [23], requires further discussion. In addition, meso-level policies and guidelines for falls and falls prevention remains lacking in China and thus warrant further development.

The efficiency of research translation from geriatric knowledge to clinical practices should also be taken into account. Although the WFG algorithm provides relatively clear tools for falls prevention, the performance of its tools (specificity, sensitivity, and feasibility) is still being validated in clinical practices. Some practitioners of this study expressed skepticism about the effectiveness and practicality of these tools. This could be related to the previous studies which have indicated the challenges associated with generalizing such tools across diverse clinical settings. For example, an Australian guideline-suggested tool (STRATIFY) was found to be significantly correlated with accidental falls among all older adults. However, its specificity is low in acute settings and the performance variated across patient populations with different disease spectrums [24]. Similarly, a recent study reported the WGF falls risk stratification algorithm showed limited sensitivity in an Osteoarthritis Initiative cohort [25]. Thus, the optimization of implementation plans, tailored to the characteristics of older adults and diverse clinical contexts, is of pivotal importance.

Strength and limitations

It is the first study on the current status of falls prevention practices in Chinese outpatient and inpatient settings. A mixed method that integrated a national survey with in-depth interviews ensured the robustness of the results. In addition, a specified guideline (WFG) was employed as a research framework to identify the factors affecting falls prevention, which enabled us to understand more detailed and new themes for future clinical practices. However, this study has some limitations. First, participants such as orthopaedic surgeons, older adults, and hospital managers were not purposively recruited in this study. Moreover, how the falls burden, such as the falls rate, affect their perspectives was neither investigated. Therefore, some perception of these people would be lost. Second, although participants from nearly all provinces of China were included, the readers should judge the scalability of findings when transferring our strategy to other settings. Because large socioeconomic disparities exist among different regions of China, and the purposive and snowballing sample methods could reduce the transferability. Finally, this study did not provide sufficiently specific recommendations for the implementation of falls prevention programs. This limitation might be attributed to the fragmented nature of relevant practices and the challenges associated with the execution of systematic intervention projects.

Conclusions

Our research indicates that systematic falls prevention for elderly patients may not be widely implemented across outpatient and inpatient settings. Gaps from knowledge to practice need to be addressed in future implementation efforts, including low-priority placement of falls prevention initiatives, challenges in patient selection, inadequate understanding of multifactorial assessment and intervention, and ambiguities regarding collaboration, and charge issues.
