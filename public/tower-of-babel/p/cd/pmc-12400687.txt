Relationships between growth rate and Ki-67 and immune indices in ground-glass nodule-featured lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yifan He, Siyu Che, Jiayue Xie, Wen Zhu, Yuxin Niu, Junjie Li, Zhiyong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to explore the correlation between the growth rate of resected ground glass nodule-featured lung adenocarcinomas (GGN-LUAD) and Ki-67, as well as various immune indexes, in patients with a follow-up period exceeding one year.Materials and methodsSixty-seven tumors were divided into a growth group and a non-growth group. The volume doubling time (VDT) and mass doubling time (MDT) of the growth nodules were calculated. Immunohistochemical methods were used to detect the expression of Ki67, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, CD68, and CD163 in pulmonary nodules. The relationships between these immune markers and the growth of GGN-LUAD were explored. At VDT = 600 days, the growth nodules were divided into a fast-growth group and a slow-growth group.ResultsThe median VDT and MDT were 1059.3 and 947.9 days, respectively, for 49 growth GGN-LUAD. Compared with that in the non-growth group, the percentage of Ki67 positive cells in the growth group was significantly greater (p = 0.001). Among the 49 growth nodules, there was a significant correlation between CD3, Ki-67 and the VDT (p = 0.020; p = 0.003), and a significant negative correlation between Ki-67 and the MDT (p < 0.001). In the 32 pathologically proven growth invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC) subgroups, a strong positive association between CD3 expression and VDT was discovered (p = 0.029), and a significant negative correlation between Ki-67 and MDT (p = 0.014). The CD3 of the slow-growth group was substantially higher than that of the fast-growth group (p = 0.034).ConclusionThis study preliminarily explored the relationships among Ki-67 expression, the molecular immune characteristics of lung adenocarcinoma and its growth course. Higher Ki-67 expression is more likely associated with the growth of GGN-LUAD. In addition, higher CD3 expression is associated with the slow growth of GGN-LUAD.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Subsolid nodules, Growth rate, Immunohistochemistry, Ki-67, Computed tomography

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most common malignant tumor and the leading cause of cancer death [1]. In recent years, with the wide application of high-resolution CT (HRCT) and increasing awareness of lung cancer screening, the detection rate of subsolid nodules (SSNs) has increased, attracting widespread attention. SSNs appeared as ground-glass nodule-featured on HRCT. The SSN comprises different categories of diseases, and the risk profile varies according to the size and type of nodules [2, 3]. Persistent SSNs, especially after growth, most pathologically manifest as glandular prodromal or invasive nonmucinous adenocarcinoma (IAC). Only a few are benign lesions, such as those associated with inflammation and focal fibrosis [4]. However, owing to the prolonged growth course of SSNs, managing these nodules remains challenging [5, 6]. Therefore, in clinical practice, accurately identifying stable SSNs and SSNs with different growth rates is crucial for clinical control. Subsequent surgical removal or regular follow-up of persistent SSNs remains controversial for clinicians and radiologists, and the development of precise, individualized diagnostic protocols and optimal management strategies is needed.

Many previous studies have investigated the history of the natural growth of SSNs. After approximately 2.4–4.2 years of follow-up, the frequency of subsequent growth of SSNs ranged from 21.0 to 71.6% [3, 7–9]. Kobayashi et al. reported that the increasing trend in the first 3 years of all cases was apparent [10]. Lee HW and Lee JH reported the incidence of intermittent growth of SSNs after 5 years of stable follow-up, and the results revealed that the frequency of growth of SSNs after 5 years of stable follow-up was 2% (5/235) and 13% (27/208), respectively [11, 12]. The latter suggests that although the SSN is less than 6 mm and has remained stable for 5 years, it should not be ignored. Scholten retrospectively analyzed all persistent SSNS after initial detection and reported that these nodules were rarely pathologically diagnosed as malignant tumors and that close CT follow-up may be a safe choice for monitoring nodular changes [13].

Currently, most lung cancer growth prediction studies are based on imaging features to predict the future growth trend of tumors [14]. However, the biological indicators or detection methods used to grow malignant lung nodules in early-stage lung cancer have not been systematically described and demonstrated. Quass compared the cellular immune characteristics of ground-glass nodule-featured lung adenocarcinoma (GGN-LUAD) and solid lung adenocarcinoma; the authors analyzed the expression of various immune markers and reported that, compared with solid lung adenocarcinoma, ground-glass lung adenocarcinoma has a less active immune profile overall, and tumor-associated macrophages and transforming growth factor β may play a role in the development of GGN-LUAD [15]. Guo et al. aimed to explore the factors affecting the growth of pulmonary nodules in natural processes via immunohistochemical methods [16]. The authors reported that high expression of Ki67 may be closely related to the growth of pulmonary nodules and that vascular endothelial growth factor may be an essential factor affecting the growth of malignant pulmonary nodules. Related studies have shown that Ki67, EGFR, and transforming growth factor β have some clinical significance in tumor growth [17, 18]. However, the role of these immune indicators in early lung cancer growth has not been widely studied or verified. In particular, whether there is variability between the different growth rates of GGN-LUAD is an unknown topic.

The main objective of this study was to combine immunohistochemical methods to analyze the factors by which different immune markers influence GGN-LUAD growth in natural processes and to explore the associations of different immune indicators with the growth rate to gain a deeper understanding of the immune microenvironment of growth-related early lung adenocarcinoma.

Materials and methods

General information

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional review board, waiving the requirement for informed consent. The study subjects were patients pathologically identified with GGN-LUAD who underwent surgical resection in our hospital from February 2017 to July 2019. This study included 67 SSNs from 64 patients, and the pathological findings included 35 IACs and 32 microinvasive adenocarcinomas (MIAs).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the initial thin-layer CT scan showed SSN and preoperative CT follow-up for at least 1 year; (2) the average diameter of SSNs on the initial CT was greater than 4 mm but not greater than 30 mm; and (3) the layer thickness of all baseline and subsequent follow-up CT scans was 2.5 mm, representing nonenhanced scan images.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) CT scans with poor image quality (such as significant artifacts around the nodules); (2) SSNs combined with pulmonary hemorrhage, interstitial lung disease, fibrotic changes, or bronchiolitis; (3) patients treated with radiotherapy or chemotherapy; and (4) recurrent or metastatic tumors in the lung. SSNs were identified primarily on the basis of the initial CT scan images, and subsequent CT was used to confirm persistence. (5) IAC and MIA with mucinous components.

Chest CT scan parameters

As this study is retrospective, the specific CT scan protocol used varied from manufacturer to manufacturer. All patients received multidetector CT scans from different manufacturers (including SOMATOM Perspective or Emotion 16 for Siemens, Optima CT660, Discovery CT750 HD, Revolution CT or LightSpeed16 for General Electric Company, and Brilliance 16P from Philips). The CT scan parameters were as follows: tube current, 120–200 mA; tube voltage, 120 kVp; reconstruction layer thickness, 1.0–2.5 mm; reconstruction interval, 1.0–2.5 mm; reconstruction via the bone algorithm; rotation time, 0.5–0.6 s; and matrix, 512 × 512. Images are viewed in the lung window (1500 HU window width; -600 HU window level) and the mediastinal window (350 HU window width; 40 HU window level).

Imaging and pathological evaluation

We collected the baseline clinical characteristics of the patients included in this study, including age, sex, smoking history, history of malignancy, and history of lung cancer. Imaging features included the lobe location of the lesion, initial and final nodule type, CT follow-up time, diameter, mean CT attenuation (m-CTA) of the lesion, volume, mass, and tumor histological type. Pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs) were defined as those in which there was no solid component within the lesion, showing only a uniform increase in density and not masking the bronchial vascular structure. Part-solid nodules (PSNs) are realistic components of lesions and cover normal lung tissue and bronchial vascular structures. The solid component was measured in the mediastinal window (350 HU window width; 40 HU window level). The average diameter (maximum and maximum vertical orthogonal diameter) was calculated in the lung window (1500 HU window width; -600 HU window level). The development of new real solid components includes increasing the real solid component of the PSN by more than 2 mm or producing a realistic solid component within the pGGN. All these features were evaluated by the consensus of two chest radiologists (a junior radiologist with 3 years of experience and a senior radiologist with 25 years of experience) on initial thin-section CT examination, and SSNs on initial thin-section CT were identified.

Nodule segmentation

After the patient information was blocked, initial and subsequent thin-layer CT images were transmitted to software (UAI-ChestCare, R001, Shanghai Lianying Intelligence Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and semiautomatic nodule detection and segmentation were performed under the lung window (1500 HU window width; -600 HU window level). Automatic segmentation was successful when each layer of the nodule was completely outlined without any segmentation errors. When the outlined region of interest (ROI) included blood vessels and vacuoles, the radiologist manually adjusted the margin of the nodules to exclude them. The degree of adjustment was assessed by two thoracic radiologists in consultation, who reviewed the SSNs outlined on the cross-sectional CT slices. After semiautomatic segmentation, the software automatically calculates the maximum diameter and maximum vertical orthogonal diameter, m-CTA, and overall nodule volume of the SSN. The nodule mass was calculated via the following formula [19]: M = V× (A + 1000) ÷ 1000. M is the mass in mg, V is the volume in mm3, and A is the mean CT attenuation in Hu.

Immunohistochemical analysis

At least 2 experienced pathologists reviewed the pathological sections of all surgically resected SSNs and classified the pathological subtypes of lung adenocarcinoma according to the 2011 IASLC/ATS/ERS classification [20]. Any disagreements between the pathologists were resolved via discussion. All the tissue samples were fixed with formalin and stained with hematoxylin‒eosin. The pathological results included IAC and MIA. The DAB method was used to observe the immune response.

The antibodies used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis were as follows: CD3 (MX 036, Beijing, China), CD4 (EP204, Beijing, China), CD8 (SP 16, Beijing, China), CD20 (L26, Beijing, China), CD163 (10D6, Beijing, China), and CD68 (KP 1, Beijing, China). Ki-67 protein antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotech, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) were used to stain for Ki-67 at a 1:100 dilution. The percentage of Ki-67-positive cells was determined. The procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The number of immune cells (CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, CD68, and CD163) was defined as the proportion of positive cells to total immune cells in the tumor stroma. The percentage of ki67-positive nuclear tumor cells in all tumor cells (tumor cell count ≥ 500). The details are as follows:

CD3 is mainly positive for cell membrane expression, and cell membrane staining represents these immune cells as positive. In tumour cells, the proportion of CD3 is recorded as the number of CD3-positive cells in the total number of immune cells.

The proportions of CD4, CD8, CD20 and CD163 were scored as CD3.

CD68 was mainly expressed in the cytoplasm, and cytoplasmic staining represented that the immune cells were positive. In tumour cells, the proportion of CD68 was recorded as the number of CD68-positive cells in the total number of immune cells.

Growth analysis

The growth of SSNs was defined as follows [21, 22]: (1) the volume of the SSN increased by more than 25% between the first and last CT scans, (2) a solid component appeared inside the pGGN during follow-up, or (3) the maximum diameter of the solid component of the PSN increased by more than 2 mm. For SSNs that grew during follow-up, the time interval from baseline detection to the onset of growth was recorded. The volume doubling time (VDT) and mass doubling time (MDT) of the growing SSNs were subsequently calculated according to the modified Schwartz formula [23]: DT = (Log2×T) ÷ [Log (Xf ÷ Xi)]. Xf and Xi are the final and initial volumes (or masses), respectively, and T is the interval between the initial and final CT scans (in days). The follow-up time was the interval between observing the same SSN during the first and final CT scans. We divided the growth nodules into a fast-growth group (n = 6) and a slow-growth group (n = 43) on the basis of VDT ​​= 600 days. We analyzed the differences in immune infiltration between the fast-growth and slow-growth subgroups [24].

Statistical analysis

The discrete trends of continuous variables are described with the mean ± standard deviation when they are normally distributed; when they are not normally distributed, the discrete trends of continuous variables are described as the median (interquartile range, IQR); and the categorical variables are represented by the number (percentage, %). Continuous variables were analyzed via the t test or Mann‒Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed via the Pearson chi‒square test or Fisher exact test to analyze differences between the growth and nongrowth groups. Similarly, differences between the fast-growth and slow-growth groups were analysed via the methods described above. The correlations between the growth rate and the levels of Ki-67 and different immune markers were explored via Spearman correlation analysis. All the statistical analyses were performed with the IBM Company SPSS software (v.26.0) or GraphPad Prism (v.9.0), with significant differences at p < 0.05.

Results

The histopathology can be divided into IAC (n = 35) and MIA (n = 32). Among the 67 tumors with baseline CT SSNs, 51 (76.1%) were pGGNs, and 16 (23.9%) were PSNs. The mean initial age of the 64 patients was 58 ± 10 years, and most were female (47,73.4%). A total of 7 patients (10.9%) had a history of smoking and lung cancer. The median follow-up period for the 67 tumors was 713 (IQR, 556–1061) days. Table 1 shows the clinicopathological and radiological features of the included patients.

Assessment of GGN-LUAD growth

During CT follow-up over 1 year before surgery, in 67 patients, including those with GGN-LUAD, 49 (73.1%) had subsequent growth, and 18 (26.9%) had no definite growth. The baseline CT findings for 49 growing lung adenocarcinomas included 34 pGGNs and 15 PSNs. Four of the 34 initial pGGNs were considered newly formed solid parts, and one developed a solid nodule. One of the 15 PSNs was converted to solid nodules. Therefore, 29 pGGNs, 18 PSNs, and 2 solid nodules were included in the final CT scan before surgery. The median VDT for the growth nodules was 1059.3 (IQR, 703.1–1479.8) days, and the median MDT was 947.9 (IQR, 665.6–1513.6) days. The baseline diameter, volume, and mass of the growth group (n = 49) were significantly greater than those of the nongrowth group (n = 18) (Table 2). Figure 1 shows representative pathological images and Ki-67 staining images of the growth and nongrowth groups. Ki-67 expression was significantly greater in the growth group than in the nongrowth group (p = 0.001, Fig. 2-a). However, the numbers of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (CD68, CD163) (Fig. 2-b) and CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, and CD4/CD8 (Fig. 2-c) were not significantly different between the two groups.

Discussion

From a pathological perspective, especially from an immunopathological perspective, this study revisited the pathological basis affecting the different growth conditions of GGN-LUAD. The associations between the growth rate of GGN-LUAD patients and different immune indices, such as Ki-67, were analyzed. We first compared the differences in Ki-67 and immunopathology between growing and nongrowing GGN-LUAD and demonstrated that higher Ki-67 expression favors the growth of GGN-LUAD. Moreover, this study suggested that higher CD3 expression is associated with the slow growth of GGN-LUAD, which initially explored the relationship between the molecular immune properties of lung adenocarcinoma and its duration of growth. This study lays a foundation for further expanding the sample size, exploring the specific regulatory mechanism and regulatory factors of SSN growth from a pathological perspective, and providing new ideas for further scientific management of SSNs.

Ki67 is a macromolecular protein reflecting cell proliferative activity and is a widely used cell marker throughout the proliferation phase. The expression of all proliferative cycles was observed except in the G0 phase. It is one of the best indicators for assessing active tumor proliferation, and it has an important role in determining cell proliferation, analyzing cell growth and metastasis, and predicting patient prognosis [17]. Malignant lung adenocarcinoma shows more robust cell proliferation, faster tumor growth, and a corresponding increase in Ki-67 expression [25, 26]. Related studies have also shown that Ki-67 can promote cell proliferation by regulating the cell cycle [27–29]. Guo et al. explored the factors affecting the growth of pulmonary nodules in natural processes via immunohistochemistry [16]. They reported that high expression of Ki-67 may be closely related to the growth of pulmonary nodules. Our study first compared the differences in Ki-67 and immune characteristics between the growth and nongrowth groups and revealed that only the growth group had significantly greater Ki-67 levels than did the nongrowth group, with no differences in the remaining immune indicators. Moreover, Ki-67 expression was significantly negatively associated with VDT and MDT in the 49 growing GGN-LUAD patients, and Ki-67 expression was significantly negatively associated with MDT in the 32 pathologically proven growing IAC patients. This finding confirms that with increasing Ki-67 expression, tumors are more inclined to grow. However, the specific regulatory mechanism and regulatory factors of Ki-67 themselves need to be clarified, which can be confirmed experimentally in subsequent studies.

GGN-LUAD is a common, radiologically distinct clinical entity with an indolent clinical course and a high survival rate, implying a unique underlying biology. The tumor immune microenvironment, especially that of tumor-infiltrating T cells, plays a critical role in the development of tumors. In recent years, some studies have combined the pathology and imaging of pulmonary nodules to explore the molecular characteristics and immune characteristics of GGN-LUAD [15, 30, 31]. A prospective study by Chen et al. included 105 cases of GGN-LUAD; the results showed that, compared with lung cancers presenting with solid lung nodules, GGO-associated lung cancers are characterized by a less active metabolism and a less active immune microenvironment, which may be the mechanisms underlying their indolent clinical course [32]. However, the relationship between the molecular immune properties of GGN-LUAD and its growth course has yet to be systematically studied, and the biological indicators or detection methods that can be used to predict the growth of pulmonary nodules have yet to be systematically elaborated and demonstrated. Our study analyzed the correlation between different immune indices and the growth of GGN-LUAD (49 patients with GGN-LUAD growth and 32 patients with pathologically proven IAC growth). Only CD3 + T cells were significantly positively correlated with the VDT of growing GGN-LUAD.

In the NELSON screening test, growth nodules were stratified by the VDT (high risk < 400 days; medium risk 400–600 days; low risk > 600 days) [24], and in this study, VDT = 600 days was selected to divide growth nodules into fast- and slow-growth groups to analyze the associations between different growth speeds and immune markers. SSNs represent slow-growing cancers that exhibit a longer VDT. These results revealed that CD3 expression was significantly lower in the fast-growth group than in the slow-growth group, confirming the association between CD3 expression and the lung adenocarcinoma growth rate. Altorki et al. there was no difference in the intratumoral density of CD3 + cells between the part-solid nodule (PSN) group and the solid-nodule group [33]. Nevertheless, Ren et al. analyzing the microenvironment of ground-glass nodules (GGNs) and PSNs, found that T cell infiltration was significantly reduced in the PSN-IAC group compared to the PSN-MIA group [34], which is consistent with our findings. Almost all T lymphocytes express CD3 T cells, and CD3 + T cells are biomarkers of T lymphocytes with antitumor activity. In our study, most lesions were GGNs, and the majority of rapidly growing lesions were invasive adenocarcinomas (4 cases, 66.7%). Therefore, we hypothesize that in the early stages of tumorigenesis, a substantial accumulation of T cells in tumor tissues may actively resist cancer cell infiltration and inhibit tumor growth. Moreover, CD3 + T cells can transduce the antigen stimulation received by T-cell antigen receptors (TCRs) into cells and then activate T cells to participate in the antitumour immune response. Ravi et al. reported that DLBCL patients with a relatively high percentage of CD3-positive T cells were significantly associated with a complete response to treatment [35]. Tian et al. reported that the infiltration of CD3 + T cells in tumor tissues was positively correlated with the 5-year overall survival rate of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, revealing that CD3 + T cells had a positive effect on the prognosis of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients [36]. These studies have shown that CD3 + T cells have antitumour activity, which is related to the overall survival rate and recurrence rate of patients and is an important prognostic predictor [36–37]. The lower the CD3 expression level is, the faster the growth rate of GGN-LUAD, and the level of CD3 cells within the tumor helps to regulate the tumor growth course.

Ki-67 expression was not significantly different between the fast growth group and the slow growth group. Part of the reason may be that the sample size of the fast growth group and the slow growth group is quite different, which leads to the deviation of the statistical results. From the graphic distribution in Fig. 6c, it can be seen that Ki-67 in the fast group is mostly concentrated at about 10%, while Ki-67 in the slow group is mostly less than 10%, and as Ki-67 increases, the number of lesions decreases. The sample size needs to be increased to further verify the association between Ki-67 and growth rate.

We did not find significant differences between the growth and nongrowth groups of GGN-LUAD patients in terms of CD4+, CD8+, CD4+/CD8 + T-cell numbers, or CD20 + B-cell numbers, and there was no correlation between the growth rate and these immune markers. In a retrospective study comparing the cellular immune signatures of GGN-LUAD and solid nodule-like lung adenocarcinoma (SN-LUAD) paired cohorts, the authors reported a significantly lower proportion of TAMS and Treg cells in only the GGN-LUAD cohort. In contrast, the remaining immune cells, including CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells, in GGN-LUAD patients were not significantly different [15]. Poorly differentiated tumors are associated with increased T-cell clonality and cytotoxic CD8 + T-cell infiltration, activated CD4 + T cells, and proinflammatory macrophages [38]. Since the patients included in this study had early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, the results differ from those of previous studies, and the specific immune mechanism needs further analysis. TAMs are classified into classically activated M1 macrophages and alternatively activated M2 macrophages on the basis of their function and phenotype [39]. In lung cancer, TAMs usually function as M2 macrophages [40]. TAMs are closely related to angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis and are involved in the development of non-small cell lung cancer. Moreover, an elevated M2 ratio (CD163+/CD68+) indicates a poor prognosis in NSCLC patients [41]. In this study, we found that the expression of CD68 + TAMs and CD163 + TAMs in the growth group was greater than that in the nongrowth group, but the difference was not statistically significant.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the sample size of this study needed to be larger, which could lead to questions about selection bias. These results should be interpreted carefully, and studies with larger sample sizes should be conducted. Second, our interpretation of the immune indicators was the proportion of positive cells to total immune cells in the tumor stroma, which may not reflect the complete immune landscape. Analysis of the association of the growth course of GGN-LUAD with the immune microenvironment is needed in future studies by increasing the sample size, including more lung cancer patients with more pathological types, and adding more clinical data. Third, the study is limited to the relationship between tumor-related immune indicators and lung nodule growth, which should be further investigated at the molecular level. Fourth, the value and mechanism of immune cells, such as Ki67 and CD3, in the growth of lung nodules need to be further explored. Large-scale, multicenter, prospective clinical studies are needed to validate their clinical value. Fifth, the small sample size of the fast-growth group (n = 6) raises concerns about statistical power. We should increase the sample size of the rapid growth group, balance the data offset between the two groups, and increase the reliability of the statistical results. Sixth, there was no refinement of clinical factors in this study (e.g., treatment history). Seveth, the follow-up period of some patients with slow growth is not sufficient. With increasing understanding, follow-up management has become more scientific and perfect.

Conclusion

This study suggested that higher Ki-67 expression favors increased growth of GGN-LUAD. In addition, this study suggested that higher expression of CD3 was associated with the slow growth of GGN-LUAD and explored the relationship between the molecular immune properties of lung adenocarcinoma and its growth course, providing new ideas for further scientific management of SSN.
