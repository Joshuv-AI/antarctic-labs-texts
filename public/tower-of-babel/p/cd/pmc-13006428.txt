An integrative multi-omics approach identifies microbiome alterations linked to pathological and behavioral features in autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Kai Mi, Miao Cao, Lingli Zhang, Qianlong Zhang, Wei Zhou, Chijun Deng, Yue Zhang, Qing Zhao, Yusen Wei, Xingyin Liu, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study employs a multi-omics approach to investigate the gut-brain axis in 326 children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and 169 typically developing (TD) controls, aged 0–10 years. By analyzing neuroimaging, gut microbiome, and plasma metabolome data, we find that microbial features could accurately distinguish ASD from TD children. A key finding is that gut microbial abundance, particularly an increase in Clostridioides difficile, serves as the strongest predictor of both ASD symptom severity and brain structural variations. Crucially, these gut and brain differences are age dependent, diminishing as children with ASD aged and converging toward TD patterns. A mediation model suggests a potential pathway where specific microbes influence brain structure and behavior via metabolites. The findings establish the gut microbiota as a robust predictor of brain and behavioral phenotypes in pediatric ASD, underscoring the necessity for early, age-stratified therapeutic strategies via modulating the composition of the gut microbiome.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, multi-omics, neuroimaging, metabolome, gut microbiota

Introduction

The etiology of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is highly heterogeneous, involving a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, environmental factors, and early developmental processes.1,2 While extensive research has focused on identifying specific genetic variants3 and brain abnormalities,4 a holistic understanding of ASD pathophysiology necessitates a broader perspective that integrates multiple biological systems.5 Emerging evidence increasingly points toward a significant role for the gut-brain axis, highlighting the intricate bidirectional communication between the gut microbiota and the central nervous system.6,7 This connection is particularly pertinent in ASD,8 where gastrointestinal issues are frequently reported and gut microbial dysbiosis is a consistent finding.9

Traditionally, neuroimaging studies of ASD have revealed a myriad of brain structural and functional anomalies, including altered brain volumes,10 cortical thickness,11 and atypical functional connectivity patterns.12,13 Specifically, brain regions including the prefrontal cortex,14 temporal lobe,15 amygdala,16 and cerebellum17 consistently show structural and functional alterations in individuals with ASD. These abnormalities are thought to contribute to deficits in social cognition, emotion processing, and executive functions. These brain differences often exhibit age-dependent trajectories, with early brain overgrowth in infancy, followed by more complex changes in adolescence and adulthood.18 However, these prior investigations have employed siloed analyses of neuroimaging data, often neglecting the critical interplay between the brain and peripheral biological system (e.g., metabolic and microbiological ecosystems). The reductionist approach limits our understanding of how systemic dysregulation may shape neurodevelopmental outcomes, highlighting the need for integrated muti-omics frameworks that account for cross-system interactions.

Recent advancements in multi-omics technologies, including metagenomics for microbial profiling and metabolomics for comprehensive biochemical assessment, offer unprecedented opportunities to bridge this gap.19 The gut microbiota produces a vast array of metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, neurotransmitter precursors, and bile acid derivatives, which can directly or indirectly influence brain physiology, neuroinflammation, and neurodevelopment through various pathways.20,21,22 Conversely, brain activity and stress can modulate gut motility and microbial composition.23

From the perspective of omics data analysis, correlation analysis serves as the foundational approach for integrating gut microbiota and neuroimaging data.24 To further explore potential causal links between gut microbiota and brain structure and function, more advanced methods like Mendelian randomization25,26,27 and mediation analysis27 are increasingly employed. For instance, Hu et al. utilized a large-scale Mendelian randomization study and identified two significant causal associations between the gut microbiota and subcortical brain volume. Their findings revealed that the genus Parasutterella was causally correlated with a decrease in intracranial volume and the genus FamilyXIIIUCG001 was associated with a decrease in thalamus volume.26 Meanwhile, Zheng et al. utilized brain structures as mediating variables to explore the links between gut microbiota and psychiatric disorders through a mediation analysis framework.27

Furthermore, previous study has firmly established characteristic alterations in the gut microbiota and serum/fecal metabolome profiles of children with ASD, linking these biological changes to core behavioral symptoms.28 Specifically, numerous studies have documented the deviated and early unsustainable stunted development of the gut microbiota in ASD children,29,30 identifying specific multi-kingdom and functional gut microbiota markers that may serve as non-invasive markers of prediction.29,31 These microbial shifts are often accompanied by significant alterations in host metabolism.32,33,34,35 Research focusing on the metabolome has identified wide-ranging dysfunction, including changes in amino acid profiles,32 homocysteine metabolism,36 and cholesterol metabolism,37 with metabolic network analyses tracing these deviations back to pre-ASD newborns.35 While these findings establish strong associations, they also provide crucial therapeutic insights. Specifically, targeted modulation of the gut microbiome emerges as a promising strategy for ameliorating core behavioral manifestations of the disorder.38

Despite these advances, integrative studies that systematically link gut microbial, plasma metabolic, and neuroimaging data to core behavioral phenotypes of ASD remain limited, leaving critical gaps in our understanding of ASD’s pathophysiological mechanisms. This present study aims to leverage a comprehensive multi-omics dataset, encompassing neuroimaging, gut microbiome, and plasma metabolome data from children with ASD and typically developing (TD) controls, to gain a more integrated understanding of ASD pathophysiology. Within our established cohort, we will utilize Multi-Omics Factor Analysis v2 (MOFA2) to identify latent variables within the multi-omics data and explore their association with ASD symptom severity. Specifically, we investigate the relationships between distinct microbial taxa and their functional pathways, specific host metabolites, and quantifiable brain morphometric variations, ultimately linking these biological insights to observed behavioral patterns. Through this integrative analysis, we seek to elucidate potential mediation pathways within the microbiota-metabolite-brain-behavior axis, offering insights into ASD etiology and facilitating the development of age-specific, biologically informed diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Results

Cohort construction and latent factor identification in multi-omics data analysis

This study enrolled a cohort comprising 326 children diagnosed with ASD and 169 age-matched TD controls (Figure 1A). The age range of the recruited children was 0–10 years. The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) and the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) were employed to assess the severity of symptoms in the cohort. Brain imaging was conducted. Plasma and fecal samples were collected from the participants. The median interval between brain imaging and biological sample collection was 0.573 months. With respect to the age at biological sample collection (Figure 1B), the median age was 4.425 years for children with ASD and 5.415 years for TD controls, a statistically significant difference (t = −2.59, p = 0.010). The proportion of males was significantly higher in the ASD group (85.2%, 278 of 326) compared to the TD group (46.7%, 79 of 169) (chi-squared = 80.276, p < 2.2 × 10−16) (Figure 1C). Subsequent to plasma and fecal sample collection, metagenomic sequencing and targeted omics analyses were performed. The distribution of samples for which structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), metagenomic, and targeted omics data were available, contingent upon individual participant consent, is presented in Figure 1D. The distribution of ADOS and CARS scores across different age ranges is detailed in Table S1.

We initially employed MOFA2 to elucidate the driving sources of variation across data modalities by identifying 15 latent factors (LFs) from the comprehensive multi-omics dataset, which comprised metagenomic, metabolomic, and brain imaging data. These 15 LFs accounted for a significant portion of the variance in microbial functional pathways, microbial species abundance, and sMRI-derived brain anatomy, with comparatively less variance explained in the metabolomic data. Notably, over 60% of the variance in microbial functional pathways was captured by these 15 LFs (Figure 1E).

A detailed examination of these factors revealed that LFs 1, 2, and 5–8 were primarily associated with microbial species abundance and functional pathways, whereas LFs 3, 4, and 9–11 predominantly explained variance derived from sMRI-derived brain morphometric measures (Figure 1F). We then constructed a Pearson correlation matrix between the 15 LFs and host metadata (Figure 1G). This analysis readily showed a significant negative correlation between LF2 and sample group (ASD vs. TD), while LFs 3 and 4 both exhibited positive correlations with ages. These findings suggest that age and ASD diagnosis are important sources of variation within the multi-omics data. Meanwhile, LF4 and LF9 exhibited certain correlations with gender information. LF5 and LF11 were found to be correlated with behavioral scores (Figure 1G), suggesting that they capture variance related to symptom severity or specific behavioral domains rather than mere diagnostic status.

We utilized k-means clustering on the LFs to delineate potential subgroups within the cohort. The silhouette score metric (Figure S1A) indicated that the optimal number of clusters was 2. This clustering separation was visually confirmed by dimensionality reduction of the LFs (Figure S1B). Our analysis showed that while the children in Clusters 0 and 1 did not differ significantly in autism severity (Figure S1C and S1D), Cluster 1 was associated with a slightly younger age at the time of both biological sample collection (Figure S1E) and sMRI acquisition (Figure S1F). This observation compellingly suggests that the LFs extracted successfully captured age-related variation present in the multi-omics data. Significant differences in sex distribution were also observed between the two identified clusters (Figure S1G).

Based on the correlation matrix, LFs 1, 2, 3, 8, and 9 exhibited significant associations with ASD status, with LF2 showing the most robust correlation. Consequently, to verify that this association was not driven by demographic factors, we first regressed out the effects of age and gender from LF2 using a linear model. We then constructed a logistic regression model using the resulting LF2 residuals to predict ASD diagnosis. This model achieved an area under the curve of 0.831 in differentiating between the ASD and TD groups (Figure 1H). Together, these findings demonstrate that the LFs identified by MOFA2 capture biologically relevant variations that effectively distinguish children with ASD from TD controls.

Among the microbial species abundance data, the relative abundance of Clostridioides difficile was found to be significantly elevated in children with ASD compared to healthy controls (T-value = 8.84, p value = 4.91 × 10−16). Furthermore, Clostridioides difficile showed the strongest positive correlation with LF2, with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of r = 0.71 (p value = 5.2 × 10−16) (Figure 2A). Regarding microbial functional pathways, the enrichment of the PWY-5199 pathway was also significantly increased in the ASD group (T-value = 11.06, p value < 2.2 × 10−16). Moreover, PWY-5199 (factor 420 biosynthesis) exhibited the highest correlation with LF2 (r = 0.96, p value = 8.8 × 10−194) (Figure 2B). sMRI-derived brain morphometric measures and plasma metabolomics data generally contributed less to LF2 compared to the microbiome layer. Illustrative features include the cortical folding in the right prefrontal cortex, which correlated negatively with LF2 (r = −0.31, p value = 4.6 × 10−07) (Figure 2C), and the metabolite sarcosine also negatively correlated with LF2 (r = −0.18, p value = 5.0 × 10−04) (Figure 2D).

In addition to LF2, factors 1, 3, 8, and 9 were also found to be correlated with the ASD classification. We visualized the omics features with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.3 with these latent variables (Figure 2E). LF1 was associated with Bacteroides caecimuris, the microbial functional pathway L-threonine degradation II, and gray matter volume in the left inferior lateral prefrontal cortex. LF3 was linked to microbial pathway sulfur reduction III and size of the left superior frontal gyrus. LF8 showed associations with the metabolite 3-ureidopropionic acid and standard deviation of normalized intensity (normStdDev) of the brain region Left-Cerebellum-White-Matter. LF9 was associated with Brainvol of CortexVol. In conclusion, the integrated multi-omics analysis indicated a particular emphasis on the critical role of microbiome and metabolites features in distinguishing ASD.

Gut microbiome features are associated with the severity of behavioral symptoms in children with ASD

Through comparing the predictive performance of models across diverse omics datasets, we found that those based on microbial abundance offered the most precise predictions for both ADOS (Figure 3A) and CARS scores (Figure 3B). For ADOS prediction, a linear model incorporating the abundance of the top 14 correlated microbial taxa achieved a significant correlation between predicted and observed scores (r = 0.59, p < 2.2e−16; Figures 3C and S2A). Similarly, the model for CARS prediction, utilizing the top 13 correlated microbial taxa, yielded a correlation coefficient of r = 0.66 (p < 2.2e−16; Figures 3D and S2B).

Five microbial species were identified as common predictors across both ADOS and CARS. Among these, Thermomonas sp. HDW16 (chi-squared = 75.783, p < 2.2 × 10−16) and Rathayibacter rathayi (chi-squared = 76.781, p < 2.2 × 10−16) demonstrated a significantly higher detection rate in children with ASD compared to TD controls (Figure S3A). For the remaining three species, Enterobacter roggenkampii showed significantly higher abundance in the ASD group compared to neurotypical controls specifically within the 0- to 3-year age bracket (t = 3.296, p = 0.002) (Figure S3B). Pantoea phytobeneficialis exhibited significantly higher abundance in ASD children vs. TD controls within the 3- to 6-year age group (t = 2.092, p = 0.045) (Figure S3C). Rhodococcus opacus, across the entire observed age range (0–9 years), was significantly more abundant in children with ASD compared to TD (t = 5.376, p = 1.966e−07) (Figure S3D).

Utilizing the abundance profiles of these five identified microbial species, we were able to successfully infer the enrichment of 66 metabolic pathways, achieving an adjusted R2 greater than 0.7 for each inference (Table S3). Several of these inferred pathways are known to be closely related to processes implicated in neurological impairment, inflammation, and autism (Figure 3E). Specifically, these encompass the following: (1) pathways involved in neurotransmitter metabolism (e.g., PWY-6334, L-dopa degradation I), (2) those mediating oxidative stress response (e.g., PWY-5923 and PWY-5924, limonene degradation), (3) pathways for short-chain fatty acid synthesis (e.g., PWY-5366, palmitoleate biosynthesis II), and (4) the metabolism pathways of environmental toxins or pro-inflammatory compounds (e.g., PWY-8292 and PWY-6968, trimethylamine N-oxide biosynthesis and degradation; PWY-6200, 2,4,5-trichlorophenoxyacetate degradation; and PWY-5488, 4-nitrophenol degradation II).

To further dissect these associations, we investigated which specific enzymes within these pathways were significantly related to the abundance of the five common microbial species. This analysis identified twelve specific enzymes (Table S4; Figure S4). Pantoea phytobeneficialis and Rhodococcus opacus individually accounted for over 30% of the explained variance in the abundance of these twelve enzymes. In contrast, Ruminococcus rathayi and Tyzzerella sp. HDW16 primarily influenced the abundance of the catechol O-methyltransferase (COMT) (EC 2.1.1.6) enzyme, specifically within the PWY-6334 (L-dopa degradation I) pathway (Figure 3E).

Using the annoTree tool, we then explored the evolutionary distribution of microbial species possessing the genetic capacity to encode these 12 enzymes (Figures 3F and S5). Focusing on EC 2.1.1.6, COMT, which catalyzes the transfer of a methyl group from S-adenosyl-L-methionine to a catechol substrate, producing S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine and a guaiacol, as an illustrative example, microbes encoding this enzyme were predominantly distributed within the phyla Actinobacteriota (48.4%) and Proteobacteria (15.0%) (Figure 3G). In summary, the analysis indicated that gut microbial data outperform other omics for predicting autism-related severity; moreover, key autism-linked microbes show distinct patterns, connecting to vital metabolic pathways and enzymes, with specific microbial phyla driving these relationships.

Microbial abundance demonstrates predictive capacity for brain structural changes in children with ASD

To identify structural brain correlates of ASD, we first assessed sMRI-derived morphometric features that displayed a statistically significant difference (p value < 0.01) between children with ASD and TD controls, accounting for the effects of MRI acquisition age and gender. Concurrently, within the ASD cohort, we identified morphometric features that exhibited a significant correlation (p value < 0.01) with both ADOS and CARS scores, also controlling for MRI age and gender. A Venn diagram approach was employed to analyze the overlap among these filtered sets of brain morphometric features (Figure 4A). Our subsequent analyses focused on the 19 morphometric features that were present in the pairwise intersections of these selection criteria (i.e., features showing case-control differences and correlating with symptom severity) (Table S5). These 19 morphometric features encompassed brain regions including the prefrontal cortex, lateral orbitofrontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, posterior cingulate cortex (PCC)/precunues, postcentral gyrus, temporal pole, primary somatosensory cortex, Brodmann area 44 (BA44), and inferior temporal gyrus (Figure 4B). These brain regions are critically involved in functions including language processing, theory of mind, spatial attention, face recognition, and social cognition.

We then investigated the degree to which these 19 brain structural features could be predicted by data from different omics layers. For each omics layer, we selected the top 8 features demonstrating the strongest Pearson’s correlation with the 19 brain structural features and performed linear regression analysis. This analysis revealed that gut microbial abundance data offered the most robust predictive performance for these 19 morphometric features compared to other omics types (Figure 4C). Specifically, using microbial abundance profiles, we were able to predict five of these brain structural features with an adjusted R2 exceeding 0.36. These five highly predictable features (Figure 4D) were mean curvature of the left PCC/precunues (7Networks_LH_Default_Par_3_MeanCurv_lh, Pearson Cor: 0.623, p value = 2.587e−12, Figures S6A and S7A), cortical thickness of the ventral suborbital portion of the left orbitofrontal cortex (S_suborbital_ThickAvg_lh,Pearson Cor: 0.684, p value = 3.179e−15, Figures S6B and S7B), mean and Gaussian curvature of the right temporal pole (temporalpole_MeanCurv_rh, Pearson Cor: 0.617 p value = 1.096e−12, Figures S6C and S7C), size of the left dorsal medial prefrontal cortex (7Networks_LH_Default_PFC_5_NumVert_lh, Pearson Cor: 0.586 p value = 2.228e−11, Figure S6D and S7D), and temporalpole_GausCurv_rh, Pearson Cor: 0.589, p value = 6.525e−12, Figures S6E and S7E). Considering the median interval between biological sample collection and acquisition date, we performed a sensitivity analysis to formally assess the potential impact of this temporal gap on the observed associations between the gut microbiota and brain structure. To achieve this, we stratified the entire cohort into two subgroups based on the median interval: a low-interval group (≤median) and a high-interval group (>median). Then, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between microbial and neuroimaging features within each subgroup (Table S6). Besides, we also calculated correlation within the subgroups derived from MOFA2 LF clustering (Figure S1B). As detailed in Table S6, the significant associations identified between microbial taxa and neuroimaging metrics remained robust across the subgroups. Notably, the stratified analysis generally yielded higher correlation coefficients than the overall cohort analysis, strongly suggesting that this segregation approach successfully mitigates residual temporal variance and enhances the detection of true biological signals.

We further examined the developmental trajectories of these five brain structural features across age (Figure S8). The mean values plotted against age indicated that the differences of the five brain structural features between the ASD group and TD controls progressively diminished over time. This observation supported the hypothesis that structural MRI differences between children with ASD and TD controls tend to decrease with age. To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed the number of brain features showing significant differences between ASD and TD controls using 2-year age bins (Figure 4E). This analysis demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in the number of differential brain morphometric features as age increased. We also provided the sample sizes for both the ASD and TD groups across age bins at Table S7.

In a related previous investigation,30 we reported an age-dependent convergence of gut microbial ecological associations in children with ASD. Building upon this finding, we re-evaluated the disruption of gut ecological relationships in the current cohort within different age windows (0–3 years, 3–6 years, and 6–9 years) compared to TD controls. This analysis revealed that in the 0- to 3-year group, the ecological network imbalance in the ASD group was predominantly centered on Bifidobacterium species, including Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Bifidobacterium catenulatum (Figure 5A). In the 3- to 6-year age group, the most prominent disruptions in ecological relationships were associated with Bacteroides uniformis and Bacteroides sp. M10 (Figure 5B). Importantly, parallel to the brain findings, the degree of gut ecological relationship imbalance in children with ASD significantly lessened with advancing age, gradually approaching the state observed in TD children (Figure 5C). Collectively, the analysis further confirms that the degree of gut ecological network imbalance in these children significantly diminishes with increasing age (with distinct core bacterial genera driving the imbalance across different age groups), which is consistent with the age-related changing trend of brain structural differences.

Gut microbiota influences ASD-related behaviors through mediation pathways involving fecal metabolites and brain structure

Based on emerging evidence of gut-brain axis interactions in neurodevelopmental disorders, we propose the hypothesis that the gut microbiota dysbiosis is linked to neurodevelopmental abnormalities through metabolite imbalance, and structural brain remodeling may serve as the biological substrate for behavioral symptomatology in ASD. Building upon this framework, we constructed a second-order mediation model, i.e., Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior. The mediation modeling framework designates microbiota abundance as the initial exposure variable, metabolites as the first-level mediators, brain morphometric features as the second-level mediators, and core behavioral phenotypes (ADOS/CARS scores) as the ultimate outcome (Figure 6A). Prior to model construction, age and gender effects were regressed out from all omics datasets. We subsequently identified paths exhibiting significant indirect effects (p < 0.05). This involved identifying significant mediation along the Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI segments (Table S8) and along the Microbiota → sMRI → Behavior segments (Table S9). These significant paths together constituted the mediation pathway network visualized in Figure 6B.

The identified path network highlights its impact on various brain structures, encompassing regions such as the lateral orbitofrontal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, temporal pole, superior frontal sulcus/middle frontal gyrus, and suborbital cortex. A consistent finding across these implicated sMRI-derived features was their negative correlation with either ADOS or CARS scores. The network also involved a diverse set of metabolites, including bile acids and their derivatives (e.g., deoxycholic acid and taurocholic acid), compounds with known neurological relevance (e.g., citicoline, N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid), and amino acids and their derivatives (e.g., sarcosine and taurine). The microbial taxa implicated in these relationships primarily belonged to the dominant gut phyla, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, but they also included opportunistic pathogens from the Proteobacteria phylum, specifically Klebsiella pneumoniae and Raoultella ornithinolytica. In the mediation network shown in Figure 6B, edge thickness corresponds to the strength of correlations between nodes. Significant correlations were observed between the microorganism and the metabolite, exemplified by the pronounced positive correlation between Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and deoxycholic acid.

We use taurine and deoxycholic acid to illustrate the second-order mediation model. [Ruminococcus] lactaris abundance was negatively correlated with taurine levels (Pearson Cor: −0.193, p value: 0.023). In turn, taurine levels were positively correlated with cortical thickness of the left postcentral gyrus (Pearson Cor: 0.218, p value: 0.010). Interestingly, the direct correlation between [Ruminococcus] lactaris and cortical thickness of the left postcentral gyrus was positive (Pearson Cor: 0.206, p value: 0.027), indicating that the indirect effect mediated by taurine had an opposing direction to the total effect, suggesting a suppressive mediating role for taurine in this specific pathway segment. Furthermore, the cortical thickness of the left postcentral gyrus was negatively correlated with ADOS scores (Pearson Cor: −0.280, p value: 0.002) (Figure 6C). Another illustrative path involved Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which positively correlated with deoxycholic acid levels (Pearson Cor: 0.250, p value: 0.003). Deoxycholic acid, in turn, was negatively correlated with cortical thickness of the left postcentral gyrus (Pearson Cor: −0.187, p value: 0.027), subsequently influencing behavior (Figure 6D). In short, the “Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior” pathway model for ASD suggests that microbe-derived key metabolites may mediate this relationship.

Discussion

In this study, we collected multi-omics data from children with ASD and TD controls, encompassing neuroimaging, gut microbiome, and plasma metabolome profiles. We utilized MOFA2 to explore the sources of variance within this multi-omics dataset and elucidate their associations with ASD. Our analysis inferred connections between brain structural variations, microbiome variations, and ASD behavioral scores. Furthermore, we constructed a two-stage mediation model (Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior) to identify potential pathways linking the microbiota to ASD behaviors. offering insights into the complex interplay among microbiota, metabolites, brain structure, and behavior in ASD.

MOFA2 successfully identified LFs 1, 2, 3, 8, and 9, which were significantly associated with ASD diagnosis. The multi-omic features loading onto these LFs included the abundance of specific microbial species such as Bacteroides caecimuris, Clostridioides difficile, and Staphylococcus agnetis. Notably, Bacteroides caecimuris and Clostridioides difficile exhibited significant alterations in ASD. Clostridioides difficile toxin TcdB is known to activate enteric neurons and pericytes, triggering a cascade release of neuropeptides and cytokines, thereby inducing neurogenic inflammation.39 Vinithakumari et al. reported that Clostridioides difficile produces the toxic metabolite p-cresol. This production leads to a significant elevation of p-cresol concentration in the serum, subsequently resulting in altered dopamine metabolism in the murine brain regions.40 Furthermore, Clostridioides difficile-associated enteritis is a recognized condition,41 which might be linked to the high prevalence of gastrointestinal issues9 frequently observed in children with ASD. Bacteroides caecimuris regulates carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, significantly altering host metabolism and immune responses.42 It also competitively inhibits the co-colonization of Akkermansia muciniphila, exacerbating inflammation through the release of toxic metabolites like lipopolysaccharide.43

Microbial functional pathways implicated by the above-mentioned MOFA2 LFs included PWY-7976 (sulfur reduction III), THREONINE-DEG2-PWY (L-threonine degradation II), PWY-7374 (1,4-dihydroxy-6-naphthoate biosynthesis I), and PWY-7373 (superpathway of demethylmenaquinol-6 biosynthesis II). Both PWY-7373 and PWY-7374 are intimately involved in the biosynthesis of microbially derived vitamin K2 (menaquinone [MK]). Specifically, 1,4-dihydroxy-6-naphthoate is a crucial intermediate in MK synthesis, while demethylmenaquinol-6 is a direct precursor to MKs (particularly long-chain MK forms like MK-6).44 Vitamin K2 plays a vital role in brain health45 and exerts neuroprotective effects.46 Consistent with these findings, cohort studies have reported significantly lower blood concentrations of vitamin K2 in children with ASD compared to TD children.47 PWY-7976 is involved in the conversion of sulfur to sulfide, which is subsequently converted to sulfite and sulfate through enzymatic actions of thioredoxin reductase. Several studies have provided evidence for dysregulated sulfur amino acid metabolism in children with ASD,48 and an inverse correlation has been reported between sulfate levels and ASD severity.49 Furthermore, urinary threonine levels are elevated in children with ASD compared to healthy controls,50 indicating an imbalance in threonine metabolism.51 This aligns with our observation of THREONINE-DEG2-PWY (L-threonine degradation II) being associated with ASD diagnosis.

The comparatively lower variance explained in the plasma metabolomics data by MOFA2 LFs might be attributed to the high intrinsic temporal variability and technical noise inherent to metabolomic measurements. Furthermore, host physiological buffering of microbial signals diminishes the linear correlation between the circulating metabolites and the extracted LFs. Among the metabolites linked to MOFA2 LFs, 3-ureidopropionic acid was identified. The pathological accumulation of 3-ureidopropionic acid is known to correlate with severe neuropathology affecting both gray and white matter, as well as dystonic movement disorder.52 Brain structural data also contributed significantly to the identification of MOFA2 LFs, particularly in aiding ASD diagnosis. The implicated brain regions were predominantly located in the left superior frontal gyrus and left prefrontal cortex, areas associated with emotional regulation and social cognitive functions.

In the multi-omics data, the gut microbiota demonstrated the highest predictive capability for both the severity of autism symptoms and brain imaging abnormalities in children with ASD. The abundance of specific microbial taxa, including Thermomonas sp. HDW16, Rathayibacter rathayi, Enterobacter roggenkampii, Pantoea phytobeneficialis, and Rhodococcus opacus, exhibited strong correlations with ASD behavioral severity. We further investigated the microbial functional pathways associated with these microbes, which included neurotransmitter metabolism and oxidative stress response. Notably, the abundance of these microbes could predict the activity of EC 2.1.1.6 COMT within the PWY-6334 (L-dopa degradation I) pathway with a Pearson correlation of 0.87. Dysregulation of the dopamine system is a common feature observed in syndromic forms of ASD.53 The precise computation of a reward-prediction error is critically dependent on the coincident activity of dopamine and acetylcholine neurotransmission within the striatum.54 The human homolog gene, COMT, encoding COMT, plays a significant role in modulating catechol-dependent functions, including cognition, cardiac activity, and pain processing. Pertinently, single-nucleotide polymorphisms at loci rs8192488, rs4680, rs165774, and rs4818 within the human COMT gene have been reported to be significantly associated with dopamine levels and risk for autism.55,56 In the prefrontal cortex neurons, the mechanism for dopamine clearance is primarily mediated by uptake via the norepinephrine transporter and subsequent metabolism by COMT.57,58 The critical role of activity in neurobehavioral regulation is further underscored by the findings of Simpson et al.,59 who utilized COMT-overexpressing mice. These mice exhibited significant cognitive deficits, particularly in stimulus-response learning and working memory, accompanied by altered presynaptic dopamine release capacity in the striatum. Given the strong functional and chemical parallel between host activity and our observed dysregulation in microbial-like pathways, this finding strongly suggests that modulating the activity of microbial and its related metabolic pathways represents a significant and important potential therapeutic strategy for ASD.

The aforementioned work highlights the close relationship between the gut microbiota and both ASD diagnosis and behavioral severity. We further examined the association of brain imaging features with ASD. Nineteen sMRI-derived features were identified as being associated with ASD diagnosis and behavioral severity. These features primarily involved regions such as the prefrontal cortex, lateral orbitofrontal gyrus, PCC/precunues, postcentral gyrus, temporal pole, primary somatosensory cortex, Brodmann area 44 (BA44), and inferior temporal gyrus. Among these 19 sMRI-derived features, five showed strong correlations with microbial abundance: mean curvature of the left PCC/precunues, implicated in theory of mind; cortical thickness of the left suborbital cortex, linked to adaptive social behavior; mean and Gaussian curvature of the right temporal pole, which is crucial for processing emotional cues from faces and voice prosody; and size of the left medial prefrontal cortex, known for its role in mentalizing (differentiating self/other intentions) and social reasoning. Our findings indicate a direct interplay between specific brain structural features and the gut microbiome, highlighting that particular structural brain alterations in individuals with ASD are closely associated with their gut microbial profiles. Notably, the receptor and transporters profiles of key neurotransmitters, such as serotonin and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), are intimately linked with both overall brain structure and its structural connectivity.60 The gut microbiota can directly influence the synthesis of neurotransmitters like serotonin,61 or indirectly impact neurotransmitter synthesis through mechanisms involving short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs).62 This close association with neurotransmitters63 is likely one of the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota influences brain structure. Beyond neurotransmitter-mediated effects, the gut microbiota may also affect brain structure and neurodevelopment by influencing microglia cells through pro-inflammatory signaling pathways.64,65

To investigate the possible mediation pathways within the multi-omics data, we constructed a two-stage model (Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior). While a two-stage mediation model simplifies and assumes certain conditions compared to the complexity of biological systems, it still allowed us to identify key metabolites and brain regions that play significant roles in the microbiota-behavior association in ASD. Metabolically, the associated metabolites were enriched in bile acid metabolism or neurological relevance pathways. Bile acid metabolism dysregulation has been linked to ASD etiology and represents an important potential therapeutic target for ASD.37,51 Among neurologically relevant metabolites, citicoline possesses comprehensive neuroprotective properties.66 In a clinical trial, autistic features improved in seven pervasive developmental disorders (PDD) patients with ASD after citicoline treatment.67 Another neurologically relevant metabolite, N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid, is a key brain metabolite involved in neuronal health68 and is significantly reduced in the brains of individuals with ASD.69 Other metabolites, such as S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine, are considered biomarkers for ASD diagnosis.70,71 Sarcosine and other metabolites also show significant differences in ASD compared to healthy controls.72 The brain regions influenced by metabolites were distributed in the middle frontal gyrus, lateral orbitofrontal cortex, and temporal lobe, which are closely associated with social cognition, emotional processing, autobiographical memory, and language comprehension (especially semantic information).

Plasma metabolites can influence the brain through multiple mechanisms. This includes diffusion across the blood-brain barrier (BBB),73 transport via specific transporters,74 and indirect effects. Some metabolites can alter the permeability of the BBB by inducing peripheral inflammation or releasing cytokines,75 can modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis,76,77 or can affect the synthesis/release of hormones that subsequently impact brain function.78,79 While the second-order mediation model identifies statistically significant sequential pathways linking the gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, and brain structure to behavior, these findings are derived from cross-sectional observational data and must, therefore, be interpreted with caution. In the context of the gut-brain axis, communication is inherently bidirectional.80 We recognize that our model cannot definitively exclude the possibility of reverse causality. In other words, structural brain abnormalities could influence the gut microbiome via neural or endocrine pathways (e.g., the vagus nerve or the axis). To rigorously confirm the directional flow of effect and establish a causal relationship, experimental validation using longitudinal designs or targeted intervention studies is required to substantiate the potential associations indicated by the model.

The mediation model highlighted the involvement of several intestinal disease-associated microorganisms, including Clostridioides difficile, Campylobacter coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and [Ruminococcus] gnavus. Conversely, we observed a significant positive correlation between Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and deoxycholic acid. This finding is particularly notable given that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii intervention has been shown to restore reduced deoxycholic acid levels following enteritis in mice.81 This bacterium is also widely known to be significantly depleted in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients.82 As a major butyrate producer, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii depletion in neuropsychiatric disorder patients is associated with increased intestinal and neuroinflammation.83,84,85 Similarly, the Coprococcus genus is recognized as a butyrate producer and has been proposed as a potential biomarker for neurological disorders.86 Furthermore, Bacteroides species, such as Bacteroides ovatus, metabolize complex carbohydrates and generate various metabolites, including precursors for GABA.87 Considering the high prevalence of gastrointestinal comorbidities among children with ASD, the microbiota signals identified via our mediation model likely reflect the underlying gut pathology frequently encountered in this cohort.

Previously, we reported that gut microbial dysbiosis in children with ASD gradually ameliorates with age.30 Consistent with this finding, this present study further highlights the critical role of Bifidobacterium, which we identified as a hub-node in the gut microbial ecology of children with ASD under 3 years. The significance of Bifidobacterium is underscored by numerous reports of its altered abundance in children with ASD. In early life, Bifidobacterium, along with Prevotella, is crucial in shaping the developmental trajectory of the infant gut microbiota.88 Its functional importance is extensive, influencing antimicrobial resistance, virulence factor genes, carbohydrate metabolism, and overall metabolic signatures,89 with some strains persisting in the gut for up to 6 years.90 Mechanistically, Bifidobacterium has been positively correlated with fecal GABA levels91 and has been shown in germ-free mice to affect early synapse formation and microglial function92. Collectively, our research provides additional evidence for the intimate link between Bifidobacterium and the neurodevelopmental processes associated with ASD.

A finding is that the structural abnormalities in the brains of children with ASD also progressively diminish with age. This conclusion is subject to the age range of our collected samples (0–10 years). Published work has reported that children with ASD aged 6–24 months exhibited characteristics of early brain size overgrowth,18,93 followed by accelerated structural volume decline and even degenerative changes.94 Additionally, in line with our findings, Gonzalez et al. demonstrated greater differences for the children group, with a reduction for the adolescent group, and a new increase in the adult set via investigating ASD-control brain folding/shape differences across ages (6–12, 13–18, and 19–44 years).95 Together, brain structural changes in ASD children are dynamic and age related throughout the lifespan, coupled with the observed delayed development in the microbiota, which suggests the necessity of age-dependent interventions for ASD diagnosis and treatment.

Recent advances in integrative multi-omics have revolutionized the study of the microbiota-gut-brain axis, establishing it as a fundamental mechanism underlying complex neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders. Across diverse conditions, this approach has successfully mapped microbial-metabolite-brain cognition pathways in the Alzheimer’s disease continuum,96 linked Alistipes and their related metabolites to major depressive disorder,97 and revealed microbiota involvement in chronic conditions like cerebral small vessel disease.98 These integrative analyses consistently demonstrate that gut-derived factors sculpt central nervous system function, often mediated by structural or functional brain biomarkers. For instance, multi-omics fusion biomarkers have been shown to mediate the association between the gut microbiota and general cognition,99 while large-scale functional network connectivity mediates the gut microbiota’s influence on sleep quality and executive functions.100

Multiple studies utilizing diverse animal models of ASD (e.g., genetic, maternal immune activation models) have demonstrated that interventions such as specific probiotics (Bacteroides fragilis101 and Limosilactobacillus reuteri102) or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)103 can effectively ameliorate the core ASD-like behavioral and metabolic deficits. Specifically, these interventions have been shown to improve social interaction deficits, reduce repetitive behaviors, and normalize anxiety-like states. Mechanistically, these therapeutic effects are often mediated by the restoration of altered metabolite profiles (e.g., short-chain fatty acids104 or amino acid derivatives105) and the subsequent normalization of neuroinflammatory or HPA axis signaling.106 Our finding that microbe-derived metabolites participate in a Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior pathway aligns closely with existing literature. Collectively, these studies establish a convergent mechanistic framework where the microbiota-gut-brain axis is crucial not only for the etiology of neurodevelopmental disorders but also for identifying gut-targeted therapeutic avenues across the entire spectrum of neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric illness.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that gut microbiota data have strong predictive capabilities for both brain structural variations and the severity of behavioral symptoms in children with ASD. Compared to TD children, both brain structure and gut microbiota development in children with ASD exhibit age specificity, further emphasizing the necessity of age-dependent therapeutic strategies and early intervention. More critically, the Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior mediation pathway pinpoints specific, modifiable biological targets (e.g., bile acids and producers) that operate along the gut-brain axis, supporting the development of highly targeted therapeutic interventions based on dietary changes, personalized probiotics, or pharmacological modulation of key microbial metabolites. In the future, we aim to rigorously confirm and extend our findings by utilizing independent external replication cohorts and establishing longitudinal cohorts of children with ASD and TD individuals, providing further evidence for our constructed Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior two-stage model, and elucidate how gut microbiota influences ASD development.

Limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations. To ensure the acquisition of high-quality data, a subset of children required sedation during the scanning procedure. While sMRI measures static anatomical structure, and the acute influence of common anesthetics on quantitative structural metrics (e.g., brain volume and cortical thickness) is generally considered minor, we did not include sedation status as a covariate in the statistical models to rigorously control for this potential source of bias. Furthermore, several other potential confounding factors, including parental socioeconomic status, maternal pregnancy history, and child dietary habits, were not incorporated as covariates in the statistical models. Consequently, these factors represent a source of residual confounding in the present study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants were recruited through the neurodevelopmental project—the Shanghai Autism Early Development Cohort (SAED)—at Xinhua Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. This study included 326 children diagnosed with ASD and 169 age-matched typically developing (TD) children. Clinical ASD diagnoses were made by experienced clinicians based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and were subsequently confirmed using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS). The ADOS calibrated severity scores, including the social affect calibrated severity score (SA-CSS) and the restricted and repetitive behavior calibrated severity score (RRB-CSS), were calculated to standardize severity comparison across participants tested with different ADOS modules. The Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS) was used to assess social-related clinical phenotypes. Typically developing children were recruited from community resources and pediatric health checks and were screened by clinicians to ensure the absence of any history of psychiatric illness or developmental delay. We applied rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize confounding factors, particularly those affecting the gut microbiota and neuroimaging data integrity. All participants were excluded if they presented with known genetic disorders, epilepsy, other neurological conditions, or suspected vision/hearing problems. To ensure the integrity of the gut microbiota data, participants were stringently excluded if they had a history of acute or chronic gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., Inflammatory Bowel Disease). Specifically, participants were excluded if they had any history of antibiotic, probiotic, prebiotic, or immunomodulator use within one month prior to fecal sample collection. Only sMRI scans deemed technically adequate (e.g., not corrupted by excessive motion) were included in the final analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (XHEC-C-2019-076) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04358744). Informed consent was obtained from the parents or the guardians of each participant.

Method details

sMRI acquisition and analysis

Participants received MRI scans on a Siemens Verio 3.0T MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil. High resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired collection with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.28 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 192∗192 mm2; voxel size = 1∗1∗1 mm3; slice thickness = 1 mm, and slice number = 170. To ensure collection, uncooperative children were sedated with chloral hydrate orally or by enema. Highly cooperative children were scanned in a resting state. The whole scanning procedure was conducted under the supervision of medical staff.

Raw structural images were independently manually inspected by two staff members to exclude those with obvious motion artifacts. After quality control, all structural MRI scans were processed with FreeSurfer using the standard recon-all pipeline based on the different atlases to estimate brain morphometric measures, including cortical thickness, surface area, gray matter volume, mean curvature, Gaussian curvature and local gyrification index. Briefly, the processing stage of the recon-all pipeline included: motion correction, non-uniformity intensity correction, projection to Talairach space, intensity normalization, skull-stripping, tissue segmentation, surface interpolation, tessellation, registration, and cortical parcellation. In this study, we utilized six atlases: the Desikan-Killiany atlas, the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville atlas, the Destrieux atlas, the Broadmann atlas, and the Yeo atlas (with two resolutions). The use of multiple atlases was motivated by two key considerations. First, it allowed us to capture a broader range of neuroanatomical information. Second, the multi-omics factor analysis method we employed is robust to multicollinearity, making it suitable for integrating data from diverse parcellation schemes. The quality control protocols of T1w images and surface reconstruction were applied using MRIQC (https://github.com/nipreps/mriqc)117 and FSQC (https://github.com/Deep-MI/fsqc). Our analysis involves metrics such as MeanCurv (Mean Curvature), ThickAvg (Average Thickness), NumVert (Number of Vertices), and GausCurv (Gaussian Curvature), in addition to normStdDev (Standard Deviation of normalized intensity). Specifically, the metric normStdDev is derived from the surface-based processing pipeline and is calculated as follows: The raw T1-weighted intensity values are first subjected to Field Inhomogeneity Correction and Intensity Scaling to correct for global variations and signal bias and normStdDev is then defined as the standard deviation of these resultant normalized intensity values.

Metabolite profiling: Sample collection, execution, and analysis

Plasma samples were collected from peripheral blood by centrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. For extraction, 50 μL of thawed plasma was deproteinized using an acetonitrile-methanol (1:1, v/v) solution containing isotopically-labeled internal standards. The mixture underwent vortexing, sonication, and incubation at −40°C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm. The resulting supernatant was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in 60% acetonitrile, and centrifuged again to remove particulates. The final supernatant was transferred to vials for analysis. Quality control (QC) samples, created by pooling aliquots from all study samples, were prepared and analyzed alongside the experimental samples to monitor system stability.

Metabolite profiling was conducted using a targeted multi-reaction monitoring (MRM) method on a UPLC-MS/MS platform. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters Atlantis Premier BEH Z-HILIC Column(1.7 μm, 2.1 mm ∗150 mm)using a gradient of (A) 8:2 water:acetonitrile and (B) 1:9 water:acetonitrile, both containing 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate (pH 9.0). The analysis was performed on an AB Sciex QTrap 6500 plus mass spectrometer operating in both positive (+5000V) and negative (−4500V) ion modes. Key ion source parameters included a temperature of 500°C and curtain and nebulizer gas pressures set to 35 psi and 50 psi, respectively.

Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed with SCIEX Analyst and Data Driven Flow (v-1.0.1) software for peak integration and metabolite quantification. The resulting data matrix was subjected to a rigorous preprocessing pipeline: metabolites with poor detection rates (<80%) were filtered out, and data were normalized using Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN) to correct for analytical variance. Subsequently, the data underwent log transformation and auto-scaling to prepare for statistical analysis. The high quality and reproducibility of the dataset were confirmed by the tight clustering of QC samples in a Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

Sample collection for gut microbiome metagenomic assay processing, and sequencing

Stool samples were collected and kept in sterile microcentrifuge tubes. After collection, the specimens were immediately frozen at −80°C until further processing. Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from 200 to 500mg stool samples using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals,CA,USA) following the manufacturers’ instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. DNA samples meeting quality control criteria were utilized for library construction at a concentration of 10 nM. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit by Shanghai Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd., and sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq platform with paired-end (2 × 150bp) sequencing.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Gut microbiome metagenomic data analysis

Sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC v0.12.1.108 Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed with Trimmomatic v0.39.109 Contaminating reads, including those from Homo sapiens (GRCh38 + major SNVs), were filtered using bowtie2 2.3.5.1.107 Taxonomic profiling, based on the Kraken2 algorithm, was performed using Kraken v2.1.2110,111 and Bracken v2.8110,112 with Kraken Standard plus protozoa & fungi 3/14/2023 Refseq indexes. Microbial pathway abundance and enzyme abundance were estimated using RFW.113

Multi-omics data preprocessing

Microbial species abundance, pathway abundance, and enzyme abundance data underwent centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation using the R package compositions (version 2.0–8). Metabolite profiling data were subjected to normal transformation. sMRI data were standardized using StandardScaler() from the Python scikit-learn package (version 1.0.2).

Latent factor extraction and analysis by MOFA2

Multi-Omics Factor Analysis v2 (MOFA2_1.12.1)114 was applied to four distinct data views: sMRI, metabolite, microbial pathway, and microbial species abundance. The microbial pathway and microbial species abundance views were CLR-transformed. Metabolite data underwent normal transformation, and sMRI data were standardized. The number of latent factors to extract was set to 15. plot_variance_explained() was used to visualize the proportion of variance explained by the latent factors across the multi-omics data, and correlate_factors_with_covariates() was used to analyze Pearson correlations between the latent factors and sample meta-information.

MOFA2 latent factor clustering

Following the removal of and effects from the MOFA2 Latent Factors, the data were standardized. We then utilized the silhouette_score from python’s sklearn package to determine the optimal number of clusters. Subsequently, K-means clustering was performed using the package sklearn, and the resulting clusters were visualized through UMAP dimensionality reduction.

Age and gender effect removal

Except for the MOFA2 analysis, we further removed the effects of age and gender from all omics features before preceding analysis. For each omics feature, a linear regression model (omics_feature ∼ age + gender) was fitted using statsmodels.api.OLS() with the least squares method in Python, adding a constant term via add_constant(). This model was trained on the TD group’s omics feature data (as the dependent variable) and their corresponding age and gender information (as independent variables). Subsequently, the residuals, representing the difference between the actual and estimated omics feature values from this regression model, were calculated for both ASD and TD groups and used for subsequent analyses.

The effects of age and gender were residualized from LF2 using the identical linear regression approach. Utilizing the resulting LF2 residuals, R glm() was used in to examine the association with ASD status, and classification Area Under the Curve (AUC) analysis was subsequently performed using the function roc () from the R package pROC.

Variance explained percentage assessment

We assessed the correlation between microbial species abundance and microbial enzyme abundance by evaluating the variance explained by the former. First, a linear regression model (enzyme_abundance ∼ microbial_abundance) was fitted using statsmodels.formula.api.ols. An ANOVA table was then generated using statsmodels.api.stats.anova_lm with typ = 2 (Type II sum of squares). This table decomposes the total variability in the dependent variable into portions attributable to the independent variable and to the residuals (unexplained variance). Finally, the percentage of variance explained (variance_pct) was calculated by dividing the sum of squares explained by the independent variable (ss_explained) by the total sum of squares (ss_total), and multiplying by 100.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was conducted using the R mediation package (version 4.5.0). To construct the proposed two-stage mediation model (Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI → Behavior), we sequentially performed two separate mediation analyses: Microbiota → Metabolite → sMRI, and Microbiota → sMRI → Behavior. Our primary focus was on identifying interconnected pathways where the average mediation effects (ACMEs) were statistically significant in both analyses. For all mediation analyses, parameters were set to boot = TRUE with sims = 1000 for robust bootstrapping. The analyses included the top 199 most abundant microbial species, all identified metabolites, 19 ASD-associated brain morphometric features, and ADOS and CARS behavioral scores. Prior to analysis, all data were preprocessed to remove the confounding effects of age and gender.

Microbial ecological imbalance analysis

Gut microbial ecological imbalance between ASD and TD groups was analyzed using PM2RA,118 with parameters set as minimum_coexist_taxa_number = 15, minimum_taxa_number = 10, minimum_taxa_prevalence = 0, and a Gaussian kernel. Ecological imbalance networks were visualized using Cytoscape. For clarity, only nodes with a degree greater than 2 and their immediate neighbors, and edges with a PM score greater than 0.3, are displayed.

Statistical analysis and visualization

Linear regression analyses, including associations between MOFA2 latent variables and ASD diagnosis, MOFA2 latent variables and omics features, and sMRI-derived features and microbial features, were performed using lm() in R 4.3.2. Forest plots illustrating the estimated coefficients for individual independent variables were generated using forest_model() from the forestmodel package (version 0.6.2). Unless otherwise specified, Pearson’s correlation coefficients between features were calculated using cor.test() in R. Group differences were compared using Welch’s Two Sample t test (R t.test()). Gender differences between ASD and TD samples were assessed using Chi-squared test (chisq.test()). Most visualizations were completed using the R package ggplot2(). For testing significant sMRI differences between ASD and TD groups, ANOVA was used to remove the potential confounding effects of age and gender. Similarly, for analyzing the linear correlation between sMRI-derived features and ADOS/CARS scores in ASD/TD groups, ANOVA was employed to control for age and gender. Visualization of brain regions corresponding to sMRI-derived features was performed using R ggseg and ggsegExtra.116 Microbial enzyme phylogenetic trees were analyzed and visualized using annoTree.115 Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.001; ns, non-significant in all figures.
