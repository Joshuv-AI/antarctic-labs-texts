Refractive outcomes after cataract surgery using swept-source OCT biometry and image-guided toric IOL alignment: a prospective comparative study in normal versus long axial length eyes
Authors: Kuo-Chi Hung, Tzyy-Chang Ho, Pi-Jung Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This prospective comparative study evaluated the refractive accuracy of the ARGOS® swept-source OCT biometer combined with the VERION™ Image-Guided System in cataract surgery using the Barrett Universal II formula. Ninety eyes were divided into Normal Axial Length (AL) (22.5–26.0 mm, n = 50) and Long AL (≥ 26.0 mm, n = 40) groups. All underwent phacoemulsification with PanOptix® Toric IOL implantation. The primary outcome was mean refractive prediction error (RPE) at 1 month. The mean RPE was − 0.07 ± 0.18 D in the Normal AL Group and − 0.05 ± 0.23 D in the Long AL Group (P = 0.375). The percentage of eyes achieving an absolute RPE within ± 0.50 D was 98.0% and 95.0%, respectively (P = 0.574). Mean absolute residual refractive astigmatism was 0.56 ± 0.32 D in the Normal AL Group and 0.47 ± 0.25 D in the Long AL Group (P = 0.110). Visual acuity outcomes were comparable between groups. In conclusion, the combination of ARGOS® segmental biometry and VERION™ digital image guidance yielded high-precision refractive outcomes in long AL eyes that were comparable to normal AL eyes.

Introduction

In recent decades, the demand for cataract surgery has steadily increased, driven by an aging global population and rising expectations for postoperative spectacle independence. Concurrently, the global prevalence of high myopia is escalating, particularly in East Asia1. High myopia poses significant challenges for cataract surgery, as accurate biometric measurement and intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation are historically more prone to error in eyes with long AL2. A thorough preoperative assessment is essential for successful refractive outcomes3. Traditional optical biometry methods, such as partial coherence interferometry (PCI), often assumes a single refractive index for the entire eye, which can lead to hyperopic prediction errors in long AL eyes due to the greater proportion of vitreous humour.

To address this, the ARGOS® biometer (Movu, Inc., CA, USA) was introduced. It utilizes swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) with a 1060 nm wavelength to measure the optical path length of each ocular segment (cornea, anterior chamber, lens, and vitreous) and applies specific refractive indices (SRI) to calculate the “sum-of-segments” AL4–6. This approach has been shown to improve accuracy in long AL eyes. Furthermore, precise management of astigmatism is critical for multifocal IOL performance. The VERION™ Image-Guided System (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) assists surgeons by creating a digital reference of the eye, ensuring precise incision placement and toric IOL alignment7,8.

Although both technologies have independently shown clinical value, only a limited number of clinical studies have evaluated their combined performance, and existing reports primarily involve general cataract populations or retrospective datasets9,10. Importantly, few studies have prospectively examined the integration of ARGOS® and VERION™ specifically across different axial-length categories, and evidence focusing on long AL or highly myopic eyes undergoing astigmatism management remains scarce. This gap underscores the clinical need to better characterize outcomes in these more challenging subgroups. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to prospectively evaluate and compare the refractive and visual outcomes of the ARGOS® biometer integrated with the VERION™ system in cataract patients with normal versus long AL.

Results

Demographics

A total of 90 eyes from 62 patients were analyzed. Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics. The Long AL Group demonstrated a significantly longer mean AL (27.40 ± 1.15 mm) compared to the Normal AL Group (24.03 ± 0.95 mm, P < 0.001). Consistent with the phenotype of high myopia, the Long AL Group had a significantly more myopic baseline spherical equivalent (-8.97 ± 3.17 D vs. 0.28 ± 5.82 D, P < 0.001), deeper anterior chamber depth (3.40 ± 0.28 mm vs. 3.14 ± 0.38 mm, P < 0.001), and flatter steep keratometry (K2) (43.32 ± 2.20 D vs. 44.56 ± 1.38 D, P = 0.0019). Patients in the Long AL Group were also younger (P < 0.001), consistent with the clinical profile of high myopia. Gender distribution and laterality showed no significant differences between groups.

Refractive outcomes

At 1 month postoperatively, the refractive accuracy was comparable between the two groups (Table 2). The mean RPE was − 0.07 ± 0.18 D in the Normal AL Group and − 0.05 ± 0.23 D in the Long AL Group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.375). Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of absolute RPE. The proportion of eyes achieving ≤ 0.25 D of absolute RPE was high in both groups — 86.0% (43/50) [95% CI: 73.3%-94.2%] in Normal AL and 80.0% (32/40) [95% CI: 64.4%-90.9%] in Long AL (P = 0.597). Accuracy further improved at the ≤ 0.50 D threshold, reaching 98.0% [95% CI: 89.4%-99.9%] and 95.0% [95% CI: 83.1%-99.4%], respectively (P = 0.574). Importantly, all eyes (100%) in both groups were within ± 1.00 D of the predicted refraction, demonstrating consistently strong refractive predictability.

Astigmatism and visual outcomes

Preoperative corneal astigmatism was comparable between the Long AL Group (1.86 ± 1.25 D) and the Normal AL Group (1.49 ± 0.94 D, P = 0.642). Postoperative mean absolute residual refractive astigmatism was nearly identical between groups (0.56 ± 0.32 D vs. 0.47 ± 0.25 D, P = 0.110) (Table 3), as also demonstrated in Fig. 2, where the cumulative proportion of eyes achieving ≤ 0.50 D of residual astigmatism was similarly high in both groups. Visual outcomes were excellent overall (Table 4). The mean postoperative logMAR UDVA was 0.01 ± 0.03 in the Normal AL Group and 0.00 ± 0.02 in the Long AL Group (P = 0.521). Regarding intermediate and near vision, the mean logMAR UIVA (60 cm) was 0.07 ± 0.11 vs. 0.04 ± 0.09 (P = 0.104), and mean logMAR UNVA (40 cm) was 0.12 ± 0.11 vs. 0.12 ± 0.11 (P = 0.843), showing no significant difference.

Relationship between axial length and prediction error

As shown in Fig. 3, RPE values across the entire AL spectrum (22.5 mm to > 30 mm) were evenly distributed without clustering toward hyperopic or myopic error in long AL eyes. The regression line exhibited an almost flat slope (R² = 0.005), indicating no significant association between AL and prediction error. These findings confirm that the ARGOS® segmental biometry maintains its refractive accuracy even in eyes with extreme axial elongation.

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrates that the integration of the ARGOS® biometer and VERION™ Image-Guided System yields high refractive accuracy in cataract surgery across different AL. A key finding is that prediction accuracy in long AL eyes was statistically indistinguishable from that in normal AL eyes, with more than 95% of eyes in both groups achieving a residual error within ± 0.50 D of the target.

Historically, calculating IOL power for long AL eyes has been challenging due to limitations of traditional biometers that apply a uniform refractive index across the ocular media, often leading to overestimation of AL in myopic eyes and subsequent hyperopic shift post-operatively2,3,11. Our results support the efficacy of the ARGOS® device, which employs segmental refractive indices (for cornea, lens, and vitreous) and thus corrects for vitreous liquefaction and anomalous geometry in highly myopic eyes12, effectively eliminating the so-called “long eye penalty” in our cohort4–6,9,13.

Additionally, despite the Long AL Group presenting with significantly higher preoperative corneal astigmatism — an acknowledged attribute of high myopia — the postoperative residual refractive astigmatism was identical to the Normal AL Group. This highlights the clinical utility of the VERION™ system. In large myopic eyes, where capsular bag size and IOL stability pose greater risks14, the precise intraoperative alignment enabled by digital image guidance appears to secure robust toric IOL performance7,10.

Importantly, this study is innovative in its institutional capability: we deployed both ARGOS® and VERION™ plus the premium multifocal/toric IOL platform (Alcon PanOptix® Toric) in a cohort with high AL and astigmatism15. The use of advanced biometry and image-guided surgery, applied in high myopia cases that are traditionally excluded or considered high-risk, distinguishes our work from prior literature and supports the notion that combining top-tier instrumentation and advanced formulas (e.g., Barrett Universal II) permits successful multifocal/toric IOL implantation even in long AL eyes.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, the follow-up period was limited to 1 month; therefore, our findings should be interpreted strictly as early postoperative outcomes. While we demonstrated high initial refractive accuracy, this duration is insufficient to assess long-term IOL rotational stability, late capsular bag contraction, or long-term refractive shifts in long AL eyes. Literature indicates that rotational instability in long AL eyes is primarily driven by the mismatch between the IOL haptic size and the larger capsular bag diameter. This anatomic discrepancy can reduce the friction between the haptics and the capsular equator, thereby predisposing the lens to rotation14,16. Furthermore, zonular weakness, often associated with high myopia, may further compromise IOL stability17. Longer follow-up and analyses that account for within-patient correlation are needed. Second, although the image-guided system was utilized for alignment, we did not routinely document the postoperative physical axis orientation of the IOL; therefore, specific vector analysis of rotational stability could not be performed. Third, we did not include patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to evaluate subjective visual quality (e.g., halos, glare) or satisfaction with the trifocal optic. Fourth, a formal pre-study sample size calculation was not performed, as the sample size was determined by the continuous recruitment of eligible patients during the study period. Future multicenter studies with follow-up of at least 6 months are necessary to validate long-term stability and patient satisfaction. In our setting, refractive accuracy in long AL eyes was comparable to normal AL eyes when using ARGOS® and VERION™. This suggests that high AL should not be an absolute contraindication for premium toric IOLs when appropriate biometry and guidance systems are utilized. Our work highlights the feasibility of leveraging advanced instrumentation to expand premium IOL indications even into challenging anatomical contexts.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective comparative observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Medical Research Ethics Foundation Joint Institutional Review Board Taiwan R.O.C. (approval No. 23-S-007-1) and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study cohort consisted of 90 eyes from 62 patients diagnosed with age-related cataracts. Patients were divided into two groups based on AL: the Normal AL Group (22.5 mm ≤ AL ≤ 26.0 mm, n = 50) and the Long AL Group (AL ≥ 26.0 mm, n = 40). Inclusion criteria were patients undergoing routine cataract surgery with implantation of the AcrySof® IQ PanOptix® Toric IOL (Models T2–T6). Exclusion criteria included history of ocular trauma, prior corneal or refractive surgery, active ocular disease (e.g., uveitis, proliferative diabetic retinopathy), or advanced glaucoma. Specific to high myopia, all patients underwent dilated fundus examination and OCT macula scanning preoperatively. Patients with significant myopic maculopathy (e.g., foveoschisis, macular atrophy) or posterior staphyloma involving the fovea were excluded to ensure that visual potential was not compromised by retinal pathology.

Preoperative assessment and surgical technique

All patients underwent preoperative biometry using the ARGOS® biometer. Measurements followed the standard device protocol, obtaining three consecutive scans per eye. Only acquisitions with a high signal-to-noise ratio validated by the device’s internal quality check were accepted; measurements with poor signal quality were repeated until valid data were obtained. The sum-of-segments AL was used for IOL power calculation. The Barrett Universal II formula was utilized for spherical power, and the Barrett Toric Calculator (v2.0) was used for cylinder power and axis18,19. The manufacturer’s recommended constants optimized for the ARGOS® biometer were applied, with a Lens Factor (LF) of 1.94 (corresponding to an A-constant of 119.1). A standardized surgically induced astigmatism (SIA) of 0.1 D was entered into the calculator for a 2.2 mm temporal clear corneal incision.

All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon using a standardized phacoemulsification technique. Regarding image guidance, a high-resolution reference image of the limbal vessels and iris features was captured preoperatively using the VERION™ Reference Unit. Intraoperatively, the VERION™ Digital Marker was used to register the eye and compensate for cyclotorsion in real-time. This digital overlay guided the capsulorhexis, incision placement, and final toric IOL alignment. Intraoperative aberrometry was not utilized in this study.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the mean refractive prediction error (RPE) at 1 month postoperatively. RPE was defined as the difference between the postoperative spherical equivalent (SE) refraction and the predicted SE (Postoperative SE - Predicted SE); thus, a negative value indicates a myopic shift and a positive value indicates a hyperopic shift. Secondary outcomes included the mean absolute error (MAE), the percentage of eyes within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, and ± 1.00 D of the target refraction, and mean absolute residual refractive astigmatism. The latter was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the absolute magnitude of the postoperative cylindrical error, without regard to axis, to prevent the canceling effect inherent in averaging signed cylinder values. Additionally, uncorrected distance (UDVA), intermediate (UIVA), and near (UNVA) visual acuity were measured using a standard Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (Statsmodels library). Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since several variables exhibited non-normal distributions, and to simultaneously account for the inter-eye correlation from bilateral cases, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with robust standard errors were used for comparing continuous variables and categorical outcomes between groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method.
