Extracellular Vesicle-Based Strategies for the Prevention of Chemotherapy-Induced Cardiotoxicity
Authors: Zhaoqin Shen, Jie Qiu, Wei Yu, Rumeng Chen, Yuancong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity (CIC) is a major limitation of modern anticancer therapy, particularly with anthracyclines and targeted agents, and contributes to long-term cardiovascular complications in cancer survivors. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale lipid-bilayer particles that mediate intercellular communication through the transfer of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. Emerging evidence indicates that EVs play a dual role in CIC. On one hand, EVs released from tumor cells or stressed cardiomyocytes can propagate cardiotoxic signals by modulating pathways related to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses. On the other hand, EV-associated molecular cargo, including specific microRNAs and proteins, shows promise as minimally invasive biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of cardiac injury during chemotherapy. In addition, EVs derived from stem or progenitor cells, as well as engineered EVs with modified cargo or surface ligands, have demonstrated cardioprotective potential by attenuating oxidative damage, suppressing apoptosis, and modulating immune and inflammatory signaling in the injured myocardium. Despite these advances, several barriers hinder clinical translation, including vesicle heterogeneity, limited targeting specificity, challenges in standardized isolation and characterization, and insufficient safety and efficacy validation. This review summarizes the mechanistic roles of EVs in the development of CIC, highlights their emerging value as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic carriers, and discusses current technical challenges and future directions for translating EV-based strategies into clinical cardio-oncology practice.Keywords: cancer, extracellular vesicles, chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity, precise prevention

Introduction

Advancements in molecular biology, genomics, and precision medicine have substantially transformed chemotherapy for malignant tumors, leading to a more personalized cancer treatment. The use of molecularly targeted drug selection, personalized dosing strategies, and real-time therapeutic monitoring has significantly improved the efficacy of chemotherapy.1 However, despite these advances, the clinical use of chemotherapy is still limited by systemic toxicities, with CIC being among the most severe and dose-dependent side effects.2,3 CIC is characterized by structural injury to cardiomyocytes and a progressive, largely irreversible damage to cardiac function.4 This condition manifests as arrhythmias, myocardial hypertrophy, and congestive heart failure, leading to a substantial decline in patient quality of life and, in severe cases, increased mortality. At the molecular and cellular levels, CIC involves a complex network of pathogenic mechanisms, including excessive oxidative stress, mitochondrial impairment, dysregulated inflammatory signaling, aberrant cardiomyocyte apoptosis, PANoptosis, and endothelial dysfunction.5,6 Current clinical surveillance approaches, such as echocardiographic assessment and circulating cardiac troponin measurements, have limited sensitivity and are often insufficient for early detection.2 Moreover, delayed biomarker elevation or false-negative results may occur in specific patient populations, hindering timely risk stratification and dynamic disease monitoring.7,8 Pharmacological interventions for the prevention of anthracycline-associated cardiotoxicity, including dexrazoxane, have shown partial cardioprotective effects; however, their clinical use remains limited by concerns regarding potential attenuation of antitumor efficacy and by their reduced effectiveness against cardiotoxicity induced by non-anthracycline agents and targeted therapies.9 Despite these advances, current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for CIC remain suboptimal. Conventional imaging techniques and circulating biomarkers typically detect cardiac injury only after structural or functional damage has occurred, limiting their value for early prediction and timely intervention.10 In addition, existing cardioprotective strategies are largely nonspecific and provide incomplete protection against the diverse cardiotoxic effects of modern chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, highlighting the need for more sensitive biomarkers and targeted therapeutic approaches.1 Therefore, identifying innovative strategies that combine early diagnostic capability with therapeutic efficacy is critically important for achieving precision prevention and management of CIC.

The rapid development of precision medicine has made liquid biopsy a promising alternative to traditional diagnostic methods due to its noninvasiveness, feasibility for long-term monitoring, and ease of sample acquisition.10 Liquid biopsy-based approaches provide a novel avenue for early detection and prognosis of CIC. In this case, EVs have emerged as a focus of biomarker research.11 EVs are lipid bilayer-enclosed nanoparticles released by nearly all cell types under both physiological and pathological conditions.12 Although liquid biopsy technologies have been extensively investigated in oncology,10,11,13 increasing evidence indicates that they also have important applications in cardiovascular diseases.14,15 Circulating EVs released from cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells carry molecular signatures that reflect myocardial stress, vascular dysfunction, and inflammatory activation.16,17 In the context of CIC, these EV-associated molecular signals may provide cardiac-specific information that cannot be captured by tumor-focused liquid biopsy approaches alone.2,18–20 Therefore, integrating EV-based liquid biopsy with cardio-oncology research provides a promising strategy for early detection and mechanistic investigation of CIC.5,21 According to the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) 2023 consensus, EVs are cell-derived lipid bilayer membrane particles without nuclei.14 EVs are mainly classified into three subtypes, including small EVs (exosomes, 30–200 nm, derived from endosomal compartments), microvesicles (200–1000 nm, shed directly from the cell membrane) and apoptotic bodies (>1000 nm, released during cell apoptosis). In this review, the umbrella term EVs is uniformly adopted with consistent nomenclature for all specific subtypes.14 The structural integrity of EV membranes protects their molecular cargo (proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids). This stability allows EVs to circulate in biological fluids and facilitates their role in intercellular communication. They act as crucial mediators (molecular messengers) of cellular signaling, accurately mirroring the physiological or pathological state of their originating cells.13,15 Studies increasingly show that, while chemotherapeutic agents are cytotoxic to tumor cells, they also trigger intracellular stress responses that stimulate cardiomyocytes, immune cells, and other cell populations to secrete EVs with distinct molecular signatures.22 These chemotherapy-associated EVs are predominantly disseminated via paracrine or systemic pathways and influence distant target cells through membrane fusion, receptor-mediated uptake, or endocytosis. This process contributes to the initiation and progression of CIC.23,24 Figure 1 depicts the endocytic–exocytic trafficking pathway involved in EV biogenesis and release in cardiomyocytes, together with representative molecular cargoes packaged within EVs. In addition to their potential diagnostic value, EVs exhibit favorable biocompatibility and cargo-delivery capability and have therefore attracted increasing interest as candidate therapeutic delivery platforms. Ronan et al25 demonstrated that plasma EV miRNA profiling of blinded patient samples revealed distinct clustering by doxorubicin-cardiotoxicity risk, with high-risk patients exhibiting miRNA signatures similar to those from doxorubicin-treated tissue-engineered models. Pathway analysis of the most distinguishing miRNAs linked them to cardiac homeostasis and cardiotoxicity-related mechanisms, supporting the potential of plasma EV miRNAs as noninvasive biomarkers for early risk stratification and personalized cardioprotective interventions in oncological care.25 To improve the rigor and reproducibility of EV research, the recent MISEV has provided standardized recommendations for EV isolation, characterization, and functional analysis. Adherence to these guidelines is essential for ensuring methodological consistency and facilitating cross study comparisons in EV research. In addition, the ISEV “massivEVs” workshop26 report has highlighted key challenges for the large scale production and clinical translation of EV based products, including issues related to manufacturing scalability, quality control, purification strategies, and regulatory considerations. These standardization efforts provide an important framework for advancing EV based diagnostics and therapeutics toward clinical application. In addition to their diagnostic value, EVs exhibit good biocompatibility, natural targeting properties, and effective cargo-delivery capacity, making them attractive candidates for therapeutic applications.7,10 As an important carrier of liquid biopsy, EVs have significant application value in the early warning, dynamic monitoring, and risk stratification of cardiac diseases and CIC. The specific molecular information they carry can sensitively reflect subclinical myocardial damage, making up for the shortcomings of traditional biomarkers and imaging in early monitoring, providing a new means for the precise diagnosis and treatment of CIC and efficacy evaluation, and is also highly consistent with the direction of subsequent targeted intervention research.10,26 EVs possess favorable characteristics, such as biocompatibility, biological stability, and intrinsic cargo-delivery capacity, which make them attractive candidates for therapeutic applications. Increasing evidence from preclinical studies suggests that EVs can serve as delivery platforms for cardioprotective molecules and may contribute to the modulation of cardiotoxic signaling pathways during chemotherapy.21,27

Biogenesis, release, and molecular cargo composition of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in cardiomyocytes. EVs are heterogeneous membrane vesicles that are commonly described as including exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies according to their proposed biogenetic origin, although these categories may partially overlap. EVs carry a broad spectrum of bioactive cargoes, including proteins, nucleic acids, and signaling molecules. In the context of chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity, EVs can mediate bidirectional molecular exchange between cardiomyocytes and surrounding cells in the cardiac microenvironment, potentially influencing downstream signaling pathways involved in cellular stress responses and myocardial injury.

In summary, EVs play a dual role in CIC: on the one hand, EVs can act as damage mediators, carrying chemotherapy-related toxicity signals, inflammatory factors, and damage-related molecules, participating in cardiomyocyte apoptosis, inflammation amplification, and myocardial microenvironment dysregulation, directly promoting the occurrence and development of cardiotoxicity; on the other hand, EVs can play a protective and repairing role, inhibiting myocardial inflammation, reducing oxidative stress damage, and improving cardiomyocyte survival and repair by transporting functional nucleic acids, proteins, and antioxidants, thereby exerting a cardioprotective effect.18,28 The discussion highlights the molecular mechanisms underlying EV-mediated cardiac damage, evaluates the clinical potential of EVs as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, and explores strategies to optimize EV-based therapeutic interventions. Besides this, current challenges and future perspectives associated with the clinical translation of EV-centered approaches are also summarized to establish a theoretical basis for the precision prevention and treatment of CIC.

Molecular Correlation Between EVs Loading Components and CIC

Extracellular vesicles involved in CIC originate from multiple cellular sources. Among these, two major EV populations are particularly relevant: tumor-derived EVs released from cancer cells during chemotherapy and cardiomyocyte-derived EVs produced by stressed cardiac cells. Tumor-derived EVs can mediate pathological tumor–heart communication by transferring oncogenic or stress-related cargoes that aggravate cardiac injury, whereas cardiomyocyte-derived EVs primarily reflect the intracellular stress status of the myocardium and may propagate injury signals within cardiac tissue.13 In addition, EVs released from other tissues, such as skeletal muscle, can participate in systemic inter-organ communication that influences susceptibility to CIC. The molecular composition of EV cargo serves as a high-fidelity reflection of the cardiomyocyte’s internal state, directly linking the intracellular stress response to the progression of CIC. The pathogenesis of CIC involves multifactorial interconnected processes, ie., redox imbalance, mitochondrial damage, apoptosis, and inflammation.5,16 EVs act as a dynamic messenger “molecular mirror” of intracellular status. They exhibit quantitative and qualitative alterations in response to chemotherapeutic stress. Changes in EV abundance, size distribution, and molecular cargo composition mirror cardiomyocyte injury and provide valuable mechanistic insights and diagnostic potential for early CIC detection.17,29 Cardiomyocyte-derived EVs represent a primary source of circulating vesicles during chemotherapy-induced cardiac stress and provide insight into myocardial injury mechanisms. Exposure to chemotherapeutic agents, especially doxorubicin, induces cardiomyocytes to secrete EVs enriched in damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which potentiate myocardial injury by activating innate immune responses. Yarana et al observed a significant increase in circulating EV levels after doxorubicin administration, with EV-associated brain/heart glycogen phosphorylase (PYGB) detectable in plasma before increases in cardiac troponin. PYGB abundance within EVs was modulated by manganese superoxide dismutase and cardioprotective treatments, indicating that PYGB-positive EVs could serve as an early, highly sensitive marker of cardiomyocyte damage.30 Doxorubicin has also been shown to increase cardiomyocyte exosome secretion by suppressing SIRT1 (silencing information regulator 1) expression, thus aggravating cardiotoxicity. Similarly, this agent induces the formation of mitochondrial-derived vesicles, a process that may expand multivesicular body formation and accelerate exosome secretion.19 Moreover, senescent cardiomyocytes triggered by doxorubicin release exosomes containing components of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. These vesicles transmit pro-senescent signals to nearby unaffected cardiomyocytes, accelerating cardiac damage through paracrine effects.31 Doxorubicin can alter cardiomyocyte function and paracrine signaling pathways. Its abnormal paracrine miRNAs and related factors can reproduce cardiotoxic effects in in vitro models. The extracellular vesicle miRNA profile of patients’ plasma can achieve early risk stratification of doxorubicin cardiotoxicity, providing an important basis for personalized cardioprotective intervention and mechanism research.25,32 A study has shown that EV-associated miRNAs, including miR-144-3p and miR-423-3p, are closely associated with echocardiographic parameters and may participate in the regulation of myocardial damage-associated pathways. Differences in the abundance of these and other miRNAs contained within EVs are strongly associated with echocardiographic measurements and with the modulation of myocardial injury-related signaling pathways. Chemotherapy-induced redistribution of miRNAs between plasma and EV compartments indicates that doxorubicin-related transcriptomic alterations are reflected in circulating EVs and may occur before the development of late-stage cardiomyopathy.20,33 Furthermore, changes in cardiac functions are better indicated by EV-associated miRNA levels than by total circulating miRNAs, likely due to the selective EV cargo sorting and target-cell-specific uptake mechanisms.33 In addition to cardiomyocyte-derived EVs, systemic inter-organ communication via EVs may further affect CIC susceptibility. Ugwu et al suggested that skeletal muscle–derived EVs, released during chemotherapeutic stress in sarcopenic patients, are enriched in mitochondrial DNA, mitoproteins, and DAMPs. These vesicles activate the TLR9/cGAS-STING pathway, promote pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-6 and TNF-α), disrupt insulin signaling, and worsen metabolic dysregulation in cardiomyocytes, ultimately increasing the risk of cardiotoxic injury. This hypothesis indicates that circulating EVs may serve as potential prognostic indicators and therapeutic targets in CIC and highlights the need for experimental validation of this proposed inter-organ communication mechanism.34 Research also indicates that non-coding RNAs within EVs are key mediators of CIC. Circular RNA circ-SKA3 has been shown to function as a competing endogenous RNA for miR-1303, increasing doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity by activating the miR-1303/TLR4 signaling pathway. circ-SKA3 is selectively packaged into exosomes released from doxorubicin-treated AC16 cardiomyocytes and transferred to nearby cells, which worsens cardiomyocyte injury through intercellular communication.35 Tumor-derived EVs also contribute to CIC through pathological cross-talk between cancer tissue and the heart. In an orthotopic breast cancer mouse model, EVs secreted by doxorubicin-treated breast cancer cells significantly worsened cardiac injury. Suppressing EV biogenesis through Rab27a knockdown or Dicer deletion attenuated this deleterious effect. Exosomal miRNA profiling identified miR-216a-5p as significantly upregulated in tumor-derived EVs after doxorubicin exposure. Mechanistic analyses revealed that its expression is driven by AMP-dependent transcriptional activation mediated by transcription factor 3 and that it is selectively incorporated into exosomes via splicing factor 3b subunit 4.36 Similarly, small EVs released from doxorubicin-treated breast cancer cells were enriched in the ferroptosis-associated miR-338-3p, further enhancing myocardial damage.37 These findings suggest that EV-mediated molecular communication among tumors, cardiomyocytes, and peripheral tissues is pivotal to the initiation and progression of CIC. EV-cargo alterations therefore provide important mechanistic clues for CIC; however, several limitations remain in the current body of evidence. Most mechanistic studies rely on cell culture systems or small animal models, and the extent to which these EV signatures accurately reflect human cardiotoxicity during chemotherapy remains insufficiently validated.13 In addition, the molecular mechanisms governing selective EV cargo sorting under chemotherapeutic stress are still poorly understood, and it remains unclear which EV subpopulations are primarily responsible for transmitting cardiotoxic signals. Future studies integrating multi-omics profiling, lineage tracing of EV origins, and longitudinal clinical cohorts will be essential to clarify the causal role of EV-mediated signaling in CIC progression. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that modulate EV release, cargo loading, or uptake by target cells could be a valuable approach to reduce CIC. However, although EV-mediated molecular signaling plays an important role in the progression of CIC, accumulating evidence indicates that unmodified EVs exhibit limited efficiency in crossing the myocardial endothelial barrier and achieving sustained retention within cardiac tissue. Experimental studies have shown that systemically administered EVs often display low cardiac homing and rapid clearance, which restricts their ability to reach cardiomyocytes in sufficient quantities and exert durable therapeutic effects.28,38 These limitations highlight the importance of developing strategies that enhance EV targeting and myocardial retention. Ultimately, these molecular alterations demonstrate that EVs are not merely passive byproducts but active participants in the pathophysiology of CIC, where their specific cargo loading dictates whether they function as mediators of damage or signals for repair.

Clinical Translational Potential of EV Biomarkers

Translating the molecular signatures of EVs into clinical practice provides a paradigm shift for liquid biopsy in cardio-oncology, offering a non-invasive means to detect subclinical myocardial injury. Compared with traditional circulating biomarkers, EVs may provide enhanced molecular context because their lipid bilayer protects diverse biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, collectively reflecting the physiological state of the parent cells. However, the concept of “greater molecular specificity” remains debated, as circulating EV populations originate from multiple tissues and cell types, making it challenging to attribute detected signals exclusively to cardiac injury. Therefore, improving cell-of-origin identification and subtype-specific EV profiling will be critical for increasing the diagnostic precision of EV-based biomarkers in CIC. A clinical study involving canine patients receiving doxorubicin chemotherapy reported that levels of EV-encapsulated miR-502 decreased significantly before detectable changes in cardiac troponin I or echocardiographic parameters, highlighting the potential of EV-associated miRNAs as early indicators of cardiac injury.39 Furthermore, previous reports have also demonstrated that PYGB⁺ EVs are regulated by manganese superoxide dismutase and cardioprotective therapies, serving as early, sensitive markers of cardiomyocyte injury.30 EV-associated molecular signatures have emerged as promising indicators for early detection of chemotherapy-induced cardiac injury. A clinical study involving canine patients receiving doxorubicin chemotherapy demonstrated that EV-encapsulated miR-502 decreased significantly before detectable alterations in cardiac troponin I or echocardiographic parameters, highlighting the sensitivity of EV-derived miRNAs for identifying subclinical myocardial damage.34 Similarly, circulating EV levels of miR-107 and miR-146a exhibit dynamic changes following doxorubicin treatment and correlate with the progression of cardiac injury, suggesting that EV-miRNA profiles may provide greater sensitivity than conventional biomarkers. Further research35 has demonstrated that EVs released from doxorubicin-treated breast cancer cells exhibit distinct inflammatory molecular profiles associated with cardiotoxic signaling. Osorio-Méndez et al reported that EV-associated factors were specifically enriched in GRO, VEGF, IL-8, MCP-1, angiogenin, and SDF-1, whereas IL-6 and TNF-α were predominantly detected in the soluble fraction rather than within EVs.36 Moreover, EVs derived from doxorubicin-exposed cells have been reported to contain elevated levels of multiple bioactive mediators, including TGF-β, VEGF, CXCL1, CXCL12, CCL5, IL-3, IL-4, IL-10, galectin-3, and KIT ligand (KITLG).40 Therefore, longitudinal assessment of EV abundance and their specific molecular cargo may improve therapeutic safety monitoring during doxorubicin-based chemotherapy.22

Moreover, changes in specific EV-miRNAs further emphasize their diagnostic importance in CIC. Some EV-miRNA profiles show higher sensitivity in detecting cardiotoxicity than traditional troponin measurements.39 Besides anthracycline-related toxicity, EV-based biomarkers also have potential for detecting cardiotoxic effects caused by other drugs. For example, isoproterenol hydrochloride, a β-adrenergic receptor agonist commonly used to treat hypotension, shock, and bronchospasm, is limited in clinical use due to its risk of myocardial injury. Zhao et al found that exosomal lncRNAs are highly stable in plasma. While much of the evidence stems from chemotherapy models, studies of other cardiotoxic agents, such as isoproterenol, provide critical proof-of-concept for the stability and tissue-specificity of exosomal lncRNAs as early diagnostic markers of drug-induced cardiac damage.41 Furthermore, circulating microparticles are not only markers of endothelial damage caused by VEGF inhibitor (VEGFi) therapy but also active players in endothelin-1-driven, redox-sensitive pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. Beyond small EVs, larger circulating microparticles (a distinct EV subtype) serve as dynamic markers of endothelial dysfunction during VEGFi therapy, reflecting real-time vascular stress in cancer patients.42 Another important limitation is the lack of large-scale prospective clinical studies validating EV biomarkers specifically in cardio-oncology populations. Many currently reported EV-associated miRNAs or proteins have been identified in small cohorts, and their predictive value across different chemotherapy regimens, cancer types, and patient comorbidities remains uncertain. Establishing multicenter validation studies and standardized analytical pipelines will be essential before EV biomarkers can be integrated into routine clinical monitoring. Variations in EV isolation methods, such as size-exclusion chromatography and density-gradient ultracentrifugation, along with inconsistencies in sample handling and storage, can introduce artificial changes in EV cargo profiles.43 Moreover, reliable detection of low-abundance EV subpopulations requires advanced analytical platforms, such as single-molecule fluorescence assays and nanoflow cytometry. EV biomarkers have significant clinical translational potential, enabling early, non-invasive, and dynamic monitoring of chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity via liquid biopsy. They can be used for patient risk stratification and personalized medication guidance, optimize the timing of cardioprotective interventions, and promote the development of precision cardiac oncology, providing important support for clinical translation and novel diagnostic and treatment strategies.14,43 Therefore, developing standardized protocols for EV isolation, characterization, and quantification is a crucial prerequisite for successful clinical translation.14,26 In summary, EVs and the various biomolecules they carry have the potential for earlier and more sensitive early diagnosis of various chemotherapy-related cardiotoxicities compared to traditional biomarkers. However, there is currently a lack of standardized protocols for EV detection, and establishing a standardized separation and detection system is crucial for its clinical translation and application. In summary, while the clinical translation of EV biomarkers faces technical hurdles, their superior sensitivity over traditional proteins positions them as the cornerstone of future risk-stratification and dynamic monitoring strategies in CIC.

Therapeutic Cardioprotection Mediated by Source-Specific EVs in CIC

Beyond their role as indicators of injury, the unique biological properties of EVs, such as low immunogenicity and innate targeting, allow them to be repurposed as therapeutic vehicles to counteract the pathogenic mechanisms of CIC. Exosomes generated through the endosomal pathway possess distinct surface proteins and lipid compositions that mediate cell recognition, membrane fusion, and tissue tropism. These features enable EVs to act as natural nanocarriers capable of delivering therapeutic molecules while reducing systemic toxicity, making them attractive platforms for mitigating CIC.7,10 Studies have demonstrated that exosomes derived from human osteosarcoma MG-63 cells can be effectively loaded with doxorubicin using electroporation. This exosome-based delivery method significantly increased intracellular drug accumulation and induced apoptotic signaling in osteosarcoma cells. This therapeutic potential is particularly evident in the ability of stem-cell-derived EVs to deliver regenerative signals that directly neutralize the oxidative stress and apoptotic pathways identified in Section 2, thereby bridging the gap between molecular understanding and clinical intervention. Mechanistic studies show that inhibiting AKT1 in the AKT signaling cascade activates the downstream GSK-3β and mTOR pathways, leading to cardiomyocyte damage. The exosome-mediated delivery system demonstrated improved cardiac safety profiles in animal models, suggesting its potential to reduce CIC while enhancing antitumor effects in osteosarcoma treatment.26 Consistent with these findings, Wang et al demonstrated that EVs derived from 293T cells significantly enhanced intracellular doxorubicin accumulation and antitumor activity both in vitro and in vivo. This effect was attributed to a distinct EV internalization pathway that also stimulated IFN-γ and TNF-α signaling in the tumor microenvironment, thereby improving tumor targeting and therapeutic efficacy.44 Cell-specific tropism of exosomes has also been exploited to enhance therapeutic precision. This tropism is largely mediated by membrane molecules on EV surfaces. For example, tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81) regulate vesicle docking and membrane fusion, while integrins, such as α6β1 and αvβ3 influence organ-specific biodistribution through interactions with endothelial ligands.45 In addition, lipid components including phosphatidylserine, sphingomyelin, and cholesterol-rich lipid rafts contribute to membrane stability and facilitate receptor-mediated uptake by recipient cells.24,27 These molecular features collectively determine EV biodistribution and cellular targeting efficiency.13,15 Exosomes derived from MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells preferentially target the lungs. Using this property, co-culture systems with A549 or 293T cells were developed to deliver lung-targeted doxorubicin. This approach significantly reduced A549 cell migration and increased caspase-3 activation and cleavage, while having minimal impact on non-target 293T cells, highlighting its selective anticancer effect.45 In addition to mammalian sources, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles have emerged as alternative therapeutic platforms. Nanovesicles isolated from bitter melon (Momordica charantia L.) show intrinsic antioxidant properties and have been shown to attenuate doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity by stabilizing the autophagy adaptor protein p62.46,47 Similarly, exosome-like nanovesicles derived from Beta vulgaris have shown cardioprotective efficacy in chronic doxorubicin exposure models by suppressing ferroptotic cell death pathways, highlighting their potential as bioactive therapeutic materials.48

Along with tumor-derived vesicles, stem cell-derived EVs are emerging as a promising approach for cardioprotection. Beyond their application in tumor-targeted drug delivery, EVs derived from regenerative cell sources have shown direct cardioprotective properties. Exosomes from embryonic stem cells show multiple favorable effects in CIC, including reducing inflammation-related pyroptosis, inhibiting pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, modulating immune pathways, and enhancing cardiac remodeling.49,50 Systemically administered mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) serve as endogenous secretory reservoirs, releasing cardioprotective EVs continuously that facilitate inter-organ communication and reduce doxorubicin-induced heart damage.50 Exosomes derived from bone marrow MSCs have been extensively studied in preclinical models, demonstrating reparative effects in myocardial infarction, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and doxorubicin-associated heart failure in mice and rabbits.51–54 Similarly, adipose-derived MSC exosomes have been shown to target cardiac tissue in cyclophosphamide-induced cardiotoxicity models, improving cardiac function, reducing damage biomarkers, and myocardial fibrosis by suppressing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and autophagy.55 EV-enriched secretomes from adipose-derived stem cells further protect cardiomyocytes from doxorubicin-induced apoptosis by upregulating clusterin expression and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.56

Recent studies also highlight the role of non-coding RNA cargo within stem cell-derived exosomes. MSCs preconditioned with macrophage migration inhibitory factor secrete lncRNA-NEAT1-enriched exosomes, which exert cardioprotective effects by suppressing miR-221-3p activity and reducing doxorubicin-associated myocardial injury.57 Zhai et al reported that bone MSC-derived exosomes carrying lncRNA GHET1 attenuate doxorubicin-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis via modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.58 Other stem cell sources further expand the therapeutic potential of EVs. Exosomes from cardiac progenitor cells are enriched in redox-regulatory proteins and miR-146a-5p, which suppress doxorubicin- and trastuzumab-induced cardiotoxicity by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and downregulating targets like Traf6 and Mpo.59 Similarly, exosomes from trophoblast stem cells exhibit strong cardioprotective effects against doxorubicin, decreasing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and promoting mitochondrial fusion by upregulating mitofusin-2 (Mfn2) expression.60,61 Figure 2 illustrates EVs as mediators of intercellular communication, transferring diverse molecular cargoes that contribute to signaling interactions between cardiomyocytes and other cell types in the cardiac microenvironment. Nevertheless, several translational challenges remain unresolved. The optimal EV dose, administration route, and treatment timing for preventing or reversing CIC have not yet been clearly defined, and the long-term biological fate of therapeutic EVs in human tissues remains largely unknown. Furthermore, heterogeneity in EV cargo composition between donor cell sources may lead to variable therapeutic efficacy, highlighting the need for standardized manufacturing and potency assays. Collectively, the use of cell-derived EVs represents a dual-track therapeutic strategy: utilizing tumor-derived vesicles for safer drug delivery while employing stem-cell or plant-derived vesicles to actively repair chemotherapy-induced cardiac damage.62 In addition, EVs derived from tumor cells, plants, and stem cells represent versatile therapeutic platforms capable of both targeted drug delivery and direct cardioprotection in CIC. Their biological membrane composition enables selective cellular interactions, while their cargo, including regulatory RNAs, proteins, and lipids, modulates oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes.

Source-Specific Engineered EV Modification and Targeted Delivery Optimization

Although EVs possess inherent cell-recognition properties and partial tissue tropism, their natural targeting efficiency and cargo-loading capacity are often insufficient for precise therapeutic applications. Engineering approaches have therefore been developed to enhance EV biodistribution, targeting specificity, and payload delivery.47,63 Physical and external stimuli-responsive guidance strategies provide spatial control over EV localization, allowing for targeted delivery to the myocardium or tumor tissue. Studies show that structural modification, surface functionalization, and external guidance strategies can improve EV biodistribution, payload delivery, and therapeutic outcomes. Sun et al reported that ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction significantly enhanced exosome-mediated cardiac delivery of miR-21, reducing cardiomyocyte death and restoring cardiac function. This approach introduces a promising strategy for cardioprotection against chemotherapy-induced damage and provides a basis for gene-based interventions in other cardiovascular disorders.63 Similarly, ultrasound-assisted microbubble disruption was shown to enhance the delivery of siHmox1-loaded exosomes, effectively suppressing doxorubicin-induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes.64 Surface functionalization via peptides and antibodies remains the most widely explored method for enhancing EV target specificity and reducing off-target cardiotoxicity. Exosomes modified with the tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD showed enhanced tumor targeting both in vitro and in vivo, delivering antitumor effects similar to those of trastuzumab but with a much lower risk of cardiotoxicity.65 Another strategy involved engineering exosomes to co-deliver doxorubicin and carvedilol directly into the mitochondria of breast cancer cells, which significantly reduced CIC while potentiating antitumor activity.66 Innovative EV retargeting strategies based on bioorthogonal metabolic engineering have further expanded the therapeutic potential of EVs. For example, one approach uses the immunosuppressive properties of programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) on tumor-derived EVs, which can deactivate T cells. By selectively enriching azide-modified EVs within injured myocardial tissue, targeted delivery was achieved, effectively reversing immune checkpoint inhibitor-associated cardiotoxicity without compromising anti-tumor immune response.67 The integration of EVs with synthetic nanomaterials or the use of bioorthogonal chemistry creates hybrid systems that combine biological biocompatibility with enhanced structural stability. Advances in nanostructure-assisted EV modification have demonstrated significant potential. Exosomes derived from MSCs, functionalized with “G-C” tetrahedral DNA nanostructures, showed promising results in reducing doxorubicin-induced liver toxicity. Further genetic engineering with cardiotropic peptides produced Exo-TDN-PCM constructs with enhanced cardiomyocyte targeting and tissue repair potential.68 Marques et al found that incorporating connexin-43 (Cx43) into EV membranes significantly improved fluorescein delivery efficiency in vitro and in vivo. When loaded with doxorubicin, the Cx43-enriched vesicles decreased cardiac toxicity while maintaining their anti-cancer effectiveness.68 Biopolymer-based surface modification has also been explored to promote EV functionality.

Wen et al developed a chitosan-functionalized exosomal drug delivery system that effectively reduced anthracycline-associated cardiotoxicity while preserving potent antitumor activity. Chitosan modification conferred enhanced structural stability, improved tumor-targeting capacity, and pH-responsive drug release. Through CD44 receptor–mediated endocytosis, this system efficiently delivered therapeutic agents to tumor cells, significantly suppressing tumor growth and reducing cardiotoxic risk.69 Hybrid nanovesicle systems integrating EVs with synthetic nanocarriers have also shown therapeutic advantages. Zhang et al encapsulated the traditional Chinese medicine compound Rg3 within PLGA nanoparticles and coated them with tumor cell–derived microvesicle membranes. This platform showed controlled drug release, high loading efficiency, and dendritic cell activation. In combination with doxorubicin, it significantly inhibited 4T1 tumor progression while reducing cardiotoxicity, providing a novel strategy for chemo-immunotherapy integration in breast cancer treatment.70

Research on scalability has led to the large-scale production of small EVs derived from human umbilical cord MSCs using three-dimensional bioreactor systems. When loaded with ginsenoside Rg1, these EVs showed enhanced therapeutic efficacy in CIC models.71,72 Further targeting accuracy was achieved using Exo-Dox-NP systems, in which doxorubicin-loaded exosomes from placental MSCs were modified with carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The use of an external magnetic field allowed precise spatial control of drug delivery, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes in breast cancer treatment.73 Furthermore, genetic modification of EV-producing cells has enhanced targeting accuracy. While exosomes from immature dendritic cells lack innate tumor specificity, engineering them to express the exosomal membrane protein Lamp2b allowed for the formation of exosomes that can precisely target tumors. These modified vesicles successfully delivered doxorubicin to tumor sites, reducing tumor growth without causing significant cardiotoxicity.74 Similarly, exosomes derived from A33-positive LIM1215 cells were loaded with doxorubicin and combined with surface-carboxylated superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles conjugated to A33 antibodies, resulting in selective targeting of A33-positive colorectal cancer cells with minimal cardiac side effects.75

In addition, MSC-derived exosomes have also shown unique chemotactic properties mediated by the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis, allowing targeted and efficient delivery of doxorubicin to osteosarcoma cells while minimizing cardiac toxicity.76 Furthermore, incorporation of miR-499a-5p mimics into cardiac-homing peptide-engineered bone marrow MSC exosomes through electroporation significantly reduces doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity through modulating the CD38/MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway.77 Doxorubicin-loaded EV formulations derived from diverse cellular sources have consistently shown enhanced therapeutic efficacy and improved cardiac safety profiles. These findings highlight the potential of EV-encapsulated chemotherapeutics as a viable and advantageous alternative to conventional drug delivery strategies in oncology.78–80 In general, various engineering modification strategies have effectively compensated for the inherent defects of natural EVs, significantly improving their targeting, drug delivery efficiency, and treatment safety, thereby alleviating cardiotoxicity in anti-tumor chemotherapy.

Safety and Therapeutic Efficacy of EV Therapy

The transition of EV-based therapies from preclinical models to the clinic requires a rigorous balance between therapeutic efficacy and long-term safety, particularly regarding off-target effects and immunogenicity.75 Exosomes exhibit notable biological properties, including low immunogenicity, good biocompatibility, and intrinsic cargo-delivery capacity, which have attracted increasing attention for drug delivery applications.36 Besides their role in diagnosing CIC, the intrinsic biological traits of EVs make them attractive candidates as therapeutic carriers or even as independent cardioprotective agents. By precisely controlling EV-producing cell sources, cargo composition, and targeting specificity, EV-based strategies enable targeted modulation of cardiotoxic pathways while preserving the anticancer effects of chemotherapeutic drugs. Despite these advantages, the translational application of EV therapies requires careful consideration of potential safety concerns. For example, EVs derived from fibroblasts are enriched in cytokines (TGF-β1).81,82 Study shows that activation of downstream signaling cascades, including the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway, may promote excessive protein synthesis and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, ultimately contributing to maladaptive cardiac remodeling and progression to heart failure under severe conditions.81,83 Moreover, the application of mouse adipose-derived MSC-associated factors to human cardiomyocytes may provoke species-specific immune responses and compromise biocompatibility, thus influencing therapeutic reliability and clinical safety.

Therefore, a functional analysis of human cardiomyocytes is needed for a comprehensive safety assessment. Advancements in intracellular engineering now allow for organelle-specific targeting, such as mitochondrial delivery, to maximize therapeutic outcomes at the subcellular level. Such evaluation should extend beyond standard endpoints, such as apoptosis and mitochondrial ROS generation, to include detailed examinations of myocardial contractility and electrophysiological stability. Moreover, engineered exosomes, due to synthetic or genetic modifications, may induce unexpected pharmacodynamic changes or tissue-specific toxicity, highlighting the importance of rigorous preclinical safety validation. Significant interspecies variability has been observed in the molecular composition and biological effects of EV cargo (miRNAs, proteins, and cytokines) derived from conditioned media across various sources. These differences could significantly impact therapeutic outcomes in human cells and may cause unintended biological responses.56 Of particular concern are tumor cell–derived EVs, which pose a potential therapeutic paradox. Surface integrins, such as α6β4 and α6β1, help EVs target specific organs through interactions with endothelial ligands.84 Meanwhile, EV-contained miRNAs can compromise vascular barrier integrity by downregulating tight junction proteins, such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), facilitating tumor cell migration across endothelial layers.85,86 Furthermore, tumor-derived EVs can promote metastasis by transferring immunosuppressive molecules such as PD-L1, which inhibits T-cell activity and alters the tumor immune microenvironment.87,88 These observations emphasize the need for in-depth elucidation of EV-associated tumorigenic mechanisms and developing strategies to maximize therapeutic benefits while reducing oncogenic risks.

To improve the safety of EV-based therapies, it is crucial to carefully select source cells and enhance targeted delivery methods. Priority should be given to donor cells (ie., MSCs and dendritic cells) with low tumorigenic potential and high biocompatibility.34 A comprehensive evaluation of cell purity (without tumor cell contamination), biological activity, and oncogenic risk (eg., proto-oncogene expression) should be conducted using techniques, such as flow cytometry and PCR.17 Using autologous cells for exosome production can reduce immune reactions, improve compatibility, lower the risk of adverse immunogenic responses to non-biological materials, and facilitate efficient crossing of biological barriers.89 Genome-editing tools like CRISPR/Cas9 can also be used to delete high-risk genes, such as TGF-β1 and PD-L1, to further enhance safety during manufacturing.90 Precision targeting is possible through surface functionalization with ligands like peptides, antibodies, or aptamers, or by adding magnetic nanoparticles and photoresponsive agents to minimize off-target accumulation. The integration of external physical signals, such as magnetic-field guidance and near-infrared irradiation, can further improve delivery accuracy and reduce unintended toxicity to healthy tissues.91 Besides, EV distribution and clearance in vivo are closely related to their size, surface properties and administration route. Most EVs are mainly cleared by the liver and spleen, while incomplete clearance may lead to unexpected tissue accumulation. Additionally, engineered EVs after chemical or genetic modification may induce potential immune responses due to foreign surface structures. Moreover, tumor-derived EVs carry oncogenic cargos and immune-regulatory molecules, which may promote tumor progression and metastasis. To alleviate such risks, selection of low-oncogenic cell sources, genetic editing of harmful genes and optimized surface targeting modification are considered feasible strategies. In addition, MISEV2023 standardized the basic identification, separation, purification and quality control standards for EVs, requiring strict characterization of particle size, morphology, biomarkers and purity, while the massivEVs workshop report put forward standardized requirements related to process stability, batch consistency, quality control and clinical translation for the large-scale production of EVs.14,26 By integrating the standardized guidelines of MISEV2023 with the translational insights of the massivEVs workshop, the field can move toward a new era of precision cardio-oncology. Overcoming the safety and manufacturing hurdles will finally allow EV-centered approaches to provide the “early warning and targeted protection” necessary to improve the cardiovascular outcomes of cancer survivors. The implementation of standardized characterization and reporting criteria, as outlined in the MISEV2023 guidelines, is critical to ensure reproducibility, purity assessment, and functional validation of EV preparations. Moreover, insights from the massivEVs workshop underscore the current challenges in large-scale production, batch consistency, and regulatory compliance that must be addressed before EV-based therapies can achieve widespread clinical adoption.

Conclusion

EVs are a highly promising platform for diagnosing and treating CIC; however, their clinical translation and implementation still face substantial technical and regulatory challenges.15,18 Firstly, while preclinical studies such as those by Hsu et al have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of MSC-derived EVs in mitigating chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity and identified potential biomarkers of treatment efficacy, rigorous standardization remains necessary for clinical translation.52,56 Secondly, large-scale manufacturing processes for EV production remain insufficiently standardized, and consistent quality-control metrics are still needed to ensure batch-to-batch reliability.14,75 Furthermore, EVs often exhibit limited targeting specificity toward diseased cardiac tissues and relatively short retention times at target sites in vivo, which may compromise therapeutic efficacy.14,15 Finally, despite preclinical evidence of EV biocompatibility and safety, comprehensive risk assessments (including long-term effects) and large-scale, multicenter clinical trials are still required before clinical application.13 Currently, most studies only focus on the therapeutic effects of EVs derived from different sources. However, unified quantitative data regarding their preparation difficulty, drug loading efficiency, targeting performance and safety are still insufficient. Besides, there is no widely accepted golden standard for systematic comparison among various EV sources. Thus, objective and comprehensive comparative evaluation remains a major challenge, which will be an important direction for future research. Therefore, selecting and characterizing EV source cells and optimizing delivery methods are needed to improve safety and reliability, paving the way for the successful clinical translation of EV-based strategies in CIC treatment.
