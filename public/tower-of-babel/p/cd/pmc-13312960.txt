Interpreting the Interpretive Obligation: Empirical Insights into the Use of Section 3 of the Human Rights Act 1998
Authors: Lewis Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract—Section 3 of the Human Rights Act provides courts with a distinctive, and controversial, power to (re)interpret legislative provisions, where ‘possible’, in a manner which is compatible with the rights under the European Convention on Human Rights. However, much of the commentary on this provision relies disproportionately on a small handful of early landmark cases, which, whilst important, risks obscuring how section 3 is routinely applied by courts, including lower courts, in reality. This article provides an empirical account of how section 3 has been interpreted and applied in practice, drawing on qualitative and quantitative measures and a bespoke dataset compiling all successful uses of section 3 in the case law. It brings insights into how judges conceptualise their duty under section 3; which factors influence and control its use; and the relationship between section 3 and other methods of interpretation. Ultimately, it will be argued that section 3, however radical it may be in theory, is subject to significant judicial constraints in practice.

Introduction

Section 3 of the Human Rights Act (HRA) is one of the most important, and most controversial, provisions of UK human rights law. Its use is the subject of numerous articles, textbooks and lectures.1 Yet the actual, practical workings of this power are not well known, with most accounts beginning and ending at an account of what some of the leading case law instructs. An account of section 3 which does not consider its practical application is deficient, and it is this deficiency which this article seeks to remedy.

Section 3, perhaps more than any other provision in the HRA, has been subject to serious criticism in the literature. Critics allege that the provision has in effect green-lit the undermining of the sovereignty of Parliament insofar as it allows judges to replace ‘the rights-based judgments of parliament’2 with their ‘own moral views’.3 This is particularly problematic, they say, because of the political context of human rights litigation,4 where a lack of democratic legitimacy is particularly dangerous.5 Further, the lack of transparency and predictability regarding section 3 gives rise to rule of law-based concerns.6 The critique is typically made with reference to the leading early case law. But implicit in much of this critique is the suggestion that problems are widespread, or at least arise frequently enough to warrant concern. Is the rule of law to be threatened by one or two errant cases? How would section 3 undermine ‘stability’7 if its apparent dangers were limited to a single example? There have always been cases with bad legal reasoning and cases which lead to bad legal outcomes, but much of the familiar criticism of section 3 goes beyond criticism of individual cases and is instead directed at the impact of section 3 as a whole and its effects on targeted structures—democratic legitimacy, the rule of law and so forth—in general terms. It therefore becomes very important to determine what the use (and possible misuse) of section 3 is across the whole corpus of cases, rather than select passages of judgments. That is what this piece seeks to do.

The terms of the HRA are ambiguous, so the finer details of its operation have been left to the judges to determine.8 After an initial period of instability, the approach espoused in the leading case of Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza has emerged as the leading authority on section 3 matters,9 and it continues to be the most widely cited section 3 case some 20 years after it was decided.10 The judges in Ghaidan advocated for a relatively ‘expansive reading’ of the section 3 power.11 The contours of the case and possible controls on the use of section 3 are explored in more detail below, but the approach allows for European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR, the Convention)-compliant interpretations to be adopted in the absence of any linguistic ambiguity, and perhaps even in a manner which undermines or upsets the intention of Parliament in passing the legislation in question. The interpretive obligation has been described variously as ‘a strong one’12 and a ‘broad power’13 which is ‘very wide’.14 It empowers judges to go ‘well beyond the normal canons of … statutory interpretation’15 and to do ‘something very close to re-writing’ the legislation.16 It is expressed in mandatory terms, meaning that it ‘must … be adopted unless it is plainly impossible’ to do so.17 All of this gives the impression of an extensive power, and in the view of some, a dangerous one, giving credence to some of the concerns sketched out above. The Ghaidan approach was never intended to lay down iron-clad rules, but it has nevertheless had considerable influence.

However, judicial passages and quotable dicta, though influential, do not determine case outcomes. Rather than exclusively relying on what some of the judges have said about section 3, it makes good sense to investigate what they have actually done with it. The remainder of this article examines the case law in full, using quantitative and qualitative measures and a bespoke dataset of section 3 cases to assess the empirical reality behind the rhetoric. This will help to inform debates about the provision’s merits, demerits and dangers.

Indeed, there is a real need for such an analysis. It has been noted that, in contrast to section 4 of the HRA, there is very little empirical evidence as to the extent of the use of section 3 in practice.18 Certainly, until very recently there were no formal databases cataloguing the use of section 3 in the same manner as the government’s annual reports chronicling the use of section 4.19 In 2023, the Ministry of Justice helpfully published a list of section 3 cases which it used to feed policy, and, as explained below, academics have published their own case lists and databases.20 All are partial and cover only certain time periods, but it is a positive step, considering that the government rejected recommendation of the Independent Human Rights Act Review (IHRAR) of compiling a public database of relevant section 3 cases.21

In order to attempt to make sense of the ‘confused and inconsistent’ case law in this area,22 a full database attempting to capture all successful judicial uses of section 3 was constructed. The dataset spans from October 2000 to December 2023 and contains 107 successful invocations of section 3.23 The author is responsible for its compilation, but is indebted to three sources which were of particular assistance. The first is the Ministry of Justice database noted above.24 Although the authors of that data state that it is not intended to be an exhaustive document, it provided a helpful reference point for the bulk of the cases used in the present dataset. A second source was JUSTICE’s submission to the IHRAR’s call for evidence,25 which sets out a large number of section 3 cases decided over the last 10 years. To compliment this, a third source, an annex to an article by Christopher Crawford, which covers section 3 cases decided during the HRA’s first decade, proved valuable.26 The author independently verified all of the information in those databases by checking and inputting the data into the dataset from scratch. Further additions were also made, from combing through case law databases, and some of the cases included in the other datasets were modified or removed to suit the parameters of the current enquiry.27 This resulted in the 107 cases which made up the final dataset. Of course, this dataset cannot hope to be comprehensive, not least owing to the large number of unreported cases, particularly at lower levels of the court hierarchy. Nevertheless, this is the most complete and thorough database on section 3 available thus far.

For each case, a number of details were recorded, including: the basic details about the case, including the parties (their names and what kind of parties they are); the judgment (its date, and the name and jurisdiction of the court deciding it); the issues involved; the rights (successfully and unsuccessfully) invoked; how section 3 was used (for more on this, see section 3 of this article); whether section 3 was used alone or in conjunction with other tools; whether ordinary interpretation was mentioned or considered; whether section 4 HRA was mentioned or considered; whether the use of section 3 had an actual or hypothetical impact on the case; and whether certain landmark authorities were cited or applied. For each case, additional qualitative comments were added to record anything of particular interest in the judgment or its reasoning.

The aim was not only to capture accurate data about the use of section 3, but to determine consistencies (or, indeed, inconsistencies) in the case law, to uncover the factors which influence the use of section 3 and to identify any changes occurring over time. A detailed look at how section 3 has been used in practice allows us to get a better understanding of its operation in the ‘real world’ and to test the accuracy of the impressions which might be drawn from reading only the headline cases and most famous dicta. Some of the ‘headline numbers’ to come out of the analysis are detailed in the next section.

Headline Numbers and the Use of Section 3

As noted above, there are no official statistics on the use of section 3 in practice, therefore we lack even the basic numbers about its use. There is uncertainty about how frequently section 3 is used. The Ministry of Justice’s data suggests that there are around 3.5 cases involving a section 3 interpretation (henceforth ‘section 3 judgment’) per year.28 Crawford’s data puts the figure at just over 4.8 cases per year,29 whereas JUSTICE proposes a figure of around 6.75 cases per year.30 The dataset used in this article, which I submit involves more rigorous methodology and a wider timeframe than these comparator datasets,31 involves a figure falling near the middle of these estimations: there are roughly 4.6 cases involving a successful invocation of section 3 HRA per year (107 cases over 279 months, or roughly 4.6 over a 12-month period).

Figure 1 sets out when each qualifying case was delivered. As can be seen, after an initial (perhaps understandable) enthusiasm for section 3 at the beginning of this period—four of the five years featuring the most successful section 3 cases fell between 2001 and 2005—there can be seen a gradual evening out of the data, with cases flowing fairly steadily from that point onwards. There are, it is suggested, no obvious later periods which saw section 3 falling either in or out of favour with judges. The use of section 3 has, it seems, been relatively consistent across time.

Uses of s 3 HRA over time (all cases).

Figure 2 turns to the rights of the ECHR which were found to be breached (or, rather, would have been breached but for the use of section 3) in qualifying cases.32 By far the most likely right to be invoked was the right to a fair trial under article 6 ECHR, which featured in over one-third of all relevant cases. There are a few reasons why this might be the case. Many article 6 complaints involve issues relating to procedural fairness, and aggrieved parties may seek only technical changes to the legislative scheme, something judges may generally be willing to do under section 3. There is also an aspect of judges feeling empowered to sort out their own wheelhouse; many article 6 cases involve challenging judicial or quasi-judicial procedures, which reduces the risk of judges improperly trespassing upon the terrain of other constitutional actors.33 Other frequently breached rights include article 8 (right for family and private life); article 14 (freedom from discrimination); and article 1 of Protocol 1 (right to peaceful enjoyment of possessions). The most serious and unqualified rights, such as the right to life and freedom from torture, have featured in successful section 3 cases only very rarely. This suggests that section 3 interpretations are more likely to be applied in cases involving purported breaches of qualified, rather than absolute, ECHR rights.

Reading In, Reading Out and More?

This section moves from raw numbers to considering what actually happens in section 3 judgments.35 Section 3 allows courts to do a number of different things with the text of legislation. It is suggested that the case law reveals six main methods which are deployed when section 3 is applied, each of which are slightly different from one another.

Firstly, and perhaps most obviously, section 3 can be used to ‘read in’ words which are not there, allowing judicial additions to be made to the text (‘words read in’).36 Secondly, it can be used to delete or remove words (‘words read out’).37 Logically, the first and second uses can be combined, creating a third method: replacing certain terms with others (‘words replaced’).38 A fourth method keeps the language of the statute as it is, but insists upon a strained meaning to be ascribed to a term or phrase (‘provision read in specific way’).39 A fifth method foregoes linguistic certainty and instead simply allows judges to read passages in a certain way, as if they had a different meaning, so as to achieve Convention compliance (‘provision read with ECHR breach exception’).40 A sixth option is to simply imply a kind of Convention-proofing clause, importing into the statute a condition that the power or discretion must not be exercised in a manner which would breach the rights in the HRA (‘provision read expansively’).41

In Ghaidan, the court confirmed that the choice of which specific method to adopt under section 3 will depend on the context of the case and the nature of the statute in question, rather than being subject to some strict formula.42 As such, it is unsurprising that judges have not tried to offer any general prescription as to whether one method should be employed over another. Generally, the case law suggests that no method is prima facie more appropriate than any other.

An exception perhaps can be found the case of JR111.43 In that case, Scoffield J accepted that both reading in (the first method) and reading out (the second method) are ‘well-recognised tools in the section 3 toolkit’, though he suggested that the latter—reading out words so that the terms of the statute may be ‘ignored or given no effect whatsoever’—may be ‘more problematic’.44 But even this passage recognises that the appropriateness of reading out words will depend on the statute being interpreted in each instance.45 Another indication that ‘reading out’ ought to be approached cautiously may be found in parts of Lord Millett’s speech in Ghaidan; he dissented from the views of his colleagues that the Rent Act 1977 could be interpreted compatibly with the Convention using section 3. In doing so, he warned that section 3 should not be used to ‘repeal’ or ‘delete’ the language in the statute.46 Whilst Lord Millett’s view is influential and remains widely cited, it was, of course, a minority view, and later judges have questioned whether the particular language used in his speech advocates too narrow a view of section 3.47

Figure 5 details how often each of the specific uses of section 3 were deployed across the dataset. Each of the cases in the dataset was marked according to which of the six methods of interpretation set out above was applied by a majority of judges in the case. As can be seen, the most common use of section 3 was simply to read and interpret a legislative provision or passage in a particular way. This is revealing—adopting this approach obviates the need for any explicit addition or deletion of the legislative text. Rather, the provision is read ‘as if’ it meant something else: provision X, which if read naturally would mean Y, should instead be read as if it means Z.

Use of s 3 HRA (all cases).

The next most popular use is the straightforward ‘reading in’ method (‘words read in’). This was a far more popular method than ‘reading down’. The fact that cases in the latter category are so rare perhaps suggests some truth to Scoffield J’s argument that reading out words might generally be considered a more severe exercise than reading them in. It is easy to see why judges would be attracted to the reading in method: deleting legislative language feels more disruptive an exercise than adding something to it. Further, if seen through the lens of legislative intent, it could be said that it is far more likely that Parliament neglected to add something to a provision to make it Convention-compliant than it would have specifically included a Convention-breaching provision intending it to be ignored.

Of note, two section 3 cases involved judges adopting an unusual method: interpreting a discretionary power normally, but ordering that any exercise of that discretion must be done in a Convention-compliant way (‘provision must be exercised in compliant way’). To construe this as a use of section 3 is unusual, given that in such circumstances the statute itself is upheld as compatible with the Convention48 and is not interpreted in a stretched way at all, and the exercise of statutory discretion in this way will in any case be governed by section 6 of the HRA. Nevertheless, judges described this as an exercise of the section 3 power in two of the cases in the dataset.49 They were therefore marked and represented in Figure 5 accordingly.

What can we make of the above? Ultimately, judges seem to prefer to adopt methods which do the least damage to the legislative text. Provisions may simply be read expansively, or read subject to an implicit boilerplate proviso reading ‘except where the ECHR would be breached’. The dicta in Ghaidan and other cases shows that judges can do serious violence to the language of legislation under section 3. Figure 5 shows that, in practice, they do not.

Controlling and Enabling Factors

Given the extent of concerns about the potential for section 3 to bring about judicial overreach, it is important to note that judges have reminded themselves of the limits to using section 3 in practically every case in which it is considered. Academics have debated what the source of these limits is. Some have identified the source of these controls as the terms of the Act itself,50 others have identified them as part and parcel of the judicial role in the context of constitutional adjudication,51 and still others have suggested that the vagueness of the language used and the breadth of the powers conferred by the Act meant that any controls must be judicially self-imposed.52 Whatever their source, it is clear that judges take these limits seriously.53 They have recognised that to go beyond the proper scope of section 3 would be to intrude in the legislative realm; the limits in section 3 are essential for safeguarding respect for sovereignty.54

Judges have adopted various shorthand tests and phrases, to try to clarify the applicable threshold for determining whether section 3 can be used or not. Section 3 cannot be used to erase or undermine some ‘fundamental’55 or ‘cardinal’56 feature of the legislation, nor can it sanction the adoption of an interpretation which would go ‘against the grain’ of the legislation.57 ‘The grain’ has been taken to mean ‘the essential meaning of principle which Parliament intended’ by passing the legislation.58 The courts have drawn distinction between interpretation on the one hand, which is legitimate, and interpolation on the other, which is not,59 and have elsewhere distinguished the permissible act of interpretation from the impermissible act of legislation.60

As Lord Bingham has noted, each of these phrases and ideas try to get at roughly the same point: section 3 is powerful, but there are limits to its use, and the essential core of the legislation must not be undermined.61 Helpful though the above phrases may occasionally prove to be for explaining how section 3 ought to be used in theory, they are often of quite limited utility in practice: reminders that judges should interpret rather than legislate does not indicate where the real difference between the two concepts lies.62 The fact that the use of section 3 may have more to do with practical matters than steadfast principles63 is part of the impetus for writing this section: which kinds of experiences seem to have made an impact and which have not? What practical considerations might guide the exercise of section 3? Which are important, which are less important and which are irrelevant?

Intent

Perhaps the most controversial passages from seminal authorities are those which suggest that judges can use section 3 to go against not only the terms of a statue, but also the intent of the legislature in passing them.64 In Sheldrake, Lord Bingham stated, without qualification, that section 3 ‘may require the court to depart from the legislative intention of Parliament’.65 Such stark admissions have understandably perturbed some commentators.

The concept of the ‘intention of Parliament’ is, of course, no straightforward thing.66 For one, it must be remembered that it was Parliament that passed the HRA, including section 3,67 meaning that judicial manoeuvring around the intention of the legislature (that which is being interpreted under section 3) may itself have been intended, and authorised, by the legislature at a different point in time.68 This still, of course, presumes that Parliament did, in fact, intend that judges should discard legislative intent when using section 3, and it is not immediately clear that this is in fact the case.69 To argue that the intent of Parliament in 1998 can be used to undermine the intent of Parliament manifested in legislation, both before and after that time, raises knotty constitutional questions about Parliament’s (in)ability to limit its own competence.70 Fortunately, an examination of the case law reveals very few instances where judges even contemplate departing from what they consider to be the express intent of the legislature, never mind completely undermining it. Therefore, whilst certain passages of dicta might ring alarm bells, the danger being signalled almost never arises in practice. It is acknowledged, however, that the continued possibility of section 3 being used in a liberal manner, when in fact it is being used much more moderately in practice, may itself raise rule of law concerns.71

Far from jettisoning considerations of legislative intent, the case law is replete with examples of judges seeking to identify and preserve the intention of the legislature.72 Although some cases do make express reference to the fact that section 3 is being used to authorise a departure from a statutory purpose,73 in most cases the judges are clearly concerned with preserving the intent or purpose behind the legislation in question. In this respect, there are three broad positions which can be found in dicta from the case law.

The first, and the position which views legislative intent as a significant limit to the exercise of section 3, seems to stand in direct contrast to passages suggesting that section 3 allows courts to undermine the intent of Parliament. In some cases, judges have positioned the search for legislative intent as the core principle underlying the use of section 3.74 In Wilkinson, Lord Hoffmann suggested that when using section 3 judges must seek ‘some general concept implied rather than expressly mentioned in the language of Parliament’75 rather than providing their own preferred alternative. In other cases, the intention of the legislature has been cast as some hard limit on the use of section 3: in Wood, Rix LJ was explicit that whilst the provision can authorise an expansive interpretation of legislation, it could not be used to bring about one which would fall out of alignment with Parliament’s intention.76

A second position, softer than the first, is that the courts should respect the intention of Parliament where possible. The case law is replete with references emphasising the importance of respecting, where possible, the purpose behind the legislation being interpreted and what the legislature was trying to do when enacting it.77 In Vining, the court confirmed that its section 3 interpretation was permissible because it would align with the ‘primary statutory intention’ behind the provision in question;78 and a similar consideration encouraged the use of section 3 in the case of EG.79 In both cases, alignment with legislative intent added legitimacy to the exercise of section 3; equally, in neither case did the court suggest that it would not be able to use the provision if alignment were not considered possible.

A third position, softer still, is that courts can use section 3 to undermine legislative intent, but it is generally beneficial that they do not do so. In Thomas, Carnwath LJ suggested that, when considering whether a section 3 interpretation can be adopted, it can be useful to ask what the legislature would have wished for the court to do with the problem identified.80 Similarly, in Bosher, the tribunal considered it helpful, when considering the use of section 3, to consider what the ‘mischief’ behind the legislation might have been.81 If nothing more, the intention of Parliament can act as a useful cross-check as to the appropriateness of using section 3, once a preliminary view has been taken as to whether a construction is possible.82 An example here is Pomiechowski, where the court used section 3 because it was believed that Parliament had not ‘foresaw or intended’ the specific problem arising in that case, and would have intended for a Convention-compliant solution if it had.83

So, are the early dicta indicating that section 3 may permit courts to ‘depart’ from legislative intent misleading at worst or a dead letter at best? If the first position canvassed above is correct, there does seem to be some conflict. But the correctness of that position is doubtful, not least because there remain cases, however rare, in which the court explicitly undermines the intent of Parliament in pursuit of a Convention-compliant interpretation.84 Presuming, then, that either the second or the third position most accurately describes the role of intent in section 3 cases, a coherent picture emerges: section 3 allows courts to go against the intention of Parliament, but courts should nevertheless strive, where possible, to act in a way which accommodates, rather than undermines, this intention.85 This is the best way to make sense of the otherwise inconsistent passages in the case law.

Language

Well-known passages from some of the seminal section 3 cases insist that the language used in a statute is no bar to the use of section 3. In Ghaidan, the judges cautioned against placing undue weight on the language of a statute when determining whether an alternative reading can be provided using section 3. They each confirmed that the use of the section is not dependent on some textual ambiguity and can be applied even if the linguistic meaning of a statute is clear.86 Lord Nicholls emphasised that a statute’s language is not ‘determinative’;87 Lord Steyn cautioned against an ‘excessive concentration on linguistic factors’;88 and Lord Rodger argued that the ‘linguistic form’ of the original statute is unimportant.89 Even Lord Millett, dissenting in that case, conceded that section 3 allows judges to do ‘considerable violence to the language [of a statute] and stretch it almost (but not quite) to breaking point’.90

It might be thought, then, that the language used in the statute plays only a very minor role in determining whether it is appropriate to use section 3. Indeed, it is possible to read some of the early cases as authorising an approach which ‘dismiss[es] … paying attention to statutory words’.91 However, it is rarely the case; in practice, the language of the statute often militates against the use of section 3. The court in PW & Co warned of using the provision in a way which constitutes a ‘misuse of language’.92 In Wilkinson, Lord Hoffmann declined to use section 3 because of the strength of the ‘indications in the language’93 of the statute, suggesting it was inappropriate to do so. In Anderson, Lord Steyn refused to use section 3 to bring about a change which was ‘contrary to [the] express statutory words’ used.94 The express language used in a statute has prevented the courts from using section 3 in other instances, including in the cases of Fox,95 Smith96 and AS,97 and in some other cases where section 3 was used, this was only, apparently, because doing so involved ‘no real violence’ to the language of the statute in that case.98

Language, it seems, matters. As to whether these cases are inconsistent with the speeches in Ghaidan, the answer is probably no.99 It has never been the case that the language of the statute is an irrelevant factor—as the court confirmed in Vanriel, with even the most compelling case for the use of section 3, ‘linguistic … boundaries’ will remain.100 The language of a statute may not determine the applicability of section 3, but the case law strongly suggests that the more specific, explicit and precise the language adopted, the more likely it will be that the use of section 3 would be considered inappropriate.101 In the case of Dutch, for example, the Court of Session suggested that whilst language is not determinative, language which is ‘unequivocal and unyielding’ and whose message ‘could not have been put more plainly’ would present a barrier to the use of section 3.102 Similar language was used by the House of Lords in AS.103 This makes good sense. The key dividing line between permissible and impermissible uses of section 3, as explored above, relates to the core intention of the legislature. In this light, the importance of the language used becomes apparent. If the legislature decides to use very specific and precise language, or sets out the statutory scheme in meticulous detail, it is more likely that it intended that weight should be placed on those specific terms because they are important. If, by contrast, terms are vague and open-ended, it is less likely that the legislature wished for a specific word, phrase or term to be central to the statute’s interpretation. Therefore, language can and does play a role in the court’s assessment of whether to employ section 3, insofar as the language used reveals something about the intention of Parliament. The fact that examination of statutory language is a frequent feature of section 3 cases means that courts try hard to take legislative intent seriously.

Answers from Elsewhere

A useful factor which might encourage a judge to use section 3 is the availability of some kind of importable solution—an interpretation which can be borrowed from another statute or another part of the same statute—as to how to remedy potentially incompatible legislation. For example, judges have considered aspects appearing in one (Convention-compliant) part of the legislation as being interpreted and have inserted duplicate provisions in order to remedy the incompatibility of another section.104 Similarly, if two different statutes feature similar provisions and one is Convention-compliant but the other is not, passages from the compliant statute can be imported into the offending one to cure the incompatibility.105 Finally, if it has been deemed possible to apply a conforming interpretation to similar passages, either under the HRA or under obligations arising under EU law, this can provide support for the use of an analogous conforming interpretation to sufficiently similar provisions.106 The presence of an ‘importable’ solution appears to encourage judges to use section 3.

Provenance of Passages

One potential factor to be taken into account is how lengthy or significant the offending passages are, where in the statute they appear and perhaps how extensive any replacement wording might need to be. Deleting or replacing a single word might be seen as more appropriate and easier to accomplish using section 3 than reformulating or reconfiguring entire sections of an Act. The courts have not, however, been drawn to this factor specifically. Of course, if the offending provision is only an inconsequential part of the statutory scheme, this will probably make it more likely that a court will find that it is not central to the statutory scheme and modifying it will not go against its grain. But the courts have recognised that essential concepts can be encapsulated in a small number of words, and that much verbiage can corrode vast swathes of legislative text.

Lord Rodger was clear about this in Ghaidan, where he suggested that the number of words being read in or read out will generally be irrelevant, insofar as this consists of a separate exercise to divining the central statutory meaning.107 In Poplar Housing, Lord Woolf refused to accede to the claimant’s proposal to add the term ‘if it is reasonable to do so’ to a statutory power relating to possession under the Housing Act 1988. He recognised that this would be a very modest change in terms of the number of words being added, but argued that by adding them it would significantly change the scope of the statutory power in a way which was impermissible under section 3.108 The length, extent and placement of words in offending passages therefore appears to be an irrelevant consideration for judges considering the use of section 3.

However, Kavanagh notes that courts may be reluctant to intervene where the ‘legislative provision is deeply imbricated in a complex legislative scheme whose provisions are all interlinked’, meaning that the length, extent or placement of words might have some influence on whether the courts invoke section 3 in practice, albeit indirectly.109 This limit still appears to have more to do with the complexity of the statutory scheme as a whole, however, than the number of words involved per se.

Hypotheticals and Lost Cases

Another factor to be taken into account is whether the use of section 3 is a ‘live’ issue in a given case, in that it would affect the outcome of the case in some way. Naturally, if an issue is no longer determinative, it may be considered inappropriate to deal with it in a judgment, as the courts are generally reluctant to determine matters on a purely academic basis.110 However, this has not stopped judges, at least on occasion, from applying section 3 to provisions which did not apply to the case at hand. In Lambert, a number of judges provided an important ruling on the compatibility of legal and evidential burdens of proof despite the majority finding that the court technically did not have jurisdiction to decide the case.111 In Havering Magistrates, the court found that the issue complained of did not arise on the facts, but considered that it could be ‘usefully dealt with’ to aid future judges.112 And in X v Y, the court found that there was no engagement with any ECHR right in the claimant’s case, but set out how section 3 would apply in the case of a future, hypothetical breach of the Convention.113 Courts have also sometimes confirmed that section 3 could not be used to bring about a Convention-compliant solution in certain hypothetical or academic situations, despite this being strictly unnecessary for disposal of the case at hand.114

Relatedly, in a number of cases, judges have agreed to a claimant’s argument to invoke section 3 in circumstances where it is found that this nevertheless does not help them succeed on the facts. Therefore, perhaps owing to the mandatory nature of section 3 and the perceived usefulness of providing a compliant interpretation for future judges, neither the fact that an issue is hypothetical nor that the claimant would not succeed on the facts has proven a barrier to the use of section 3, as Figure 6 shows.

Live and hypothetical cases.

In a majority of section 3 cases, there is an actual, rather than hypothetical, dispute; similarly, the use of section 3 makes a difference to the parties’ individual positions in most cases. But in a sizeable minority of judgments, this is not the case. A dispute being hypothetical, or the invocation of section 3 being unhelpful, does not present a barrier to its use in practice.

Precedent

As with any other area of law, the existence of persuasive precedent will make it much easier, if not mandatory, to adopt a conforming interpretation under section 3. Of course, there will be no occasion to ‘reuse’ section 3 on a legislative passage which has already been reinterpreted under this provision; the judge in the latter case would simply apply the interpretation provided in the first.115 But, given that many legislative provisions are similar to one another, the use of section 3 in one case might provide a compelling reason for a judge to apply the same interpretation in a later case concerning a provision which is similar in wording, intent or effect. In short, existing case law might help to convince a judge that it is ‘possible’ to interpret a particular provision compatibly.116

The interpretation in the case of Hand117 was emulated in the later case of JC Druce;118 the case of Re W119 seemingly provided the answer as to whether section 3 could be used in the later case of Pierhead;120 and the outcome of Connolly121 provided a complete answer to the interpretation of a similarly worded provision in Scottow.122 Indeed, in at least two cases, the presence of precedent was the only reason a judge adopted a Convention-compliant interpretation under section 3. In the case of Daniel, Auld LJ confessed that he was only able to adopt a novel interpretation of provisions of the Insolvency Act 1986 because binding authorities on similarly incompatible provisions required this of him.123 Similarly, in Jwanczuk, the Court of Appeal upheld an interpretation of the Pensions Act 2014 adopted by the court below, but confessed that it found the issue ‘less straightforward’ than the lower court had, and but for the strength of precedent concerning the interpretation of equivalent provisions in force in Northern Ireland, it may not have arrived at the same conclusion.124 Precedent, therefore, can not only influence the exercise of section 3 HRA, but can, in some cases, practically dictate its use.

Party Views

The views of the parties to the case as to the suitability of a section 3 interpretation are very influential.125 Despite the fact that it has been recognised that the specific arguments relied on by parties ‘cannot modify the task of the court’,126 which is to determine for itself whether a Convention-compliant interpretation ‘must’ be adopted in a given case, often when parties are aligned as to what the specific remedy ought to be in the event of a prima facie incompatibility, this will influence the court significantly.

In several cases, where all parties agreed that section 3 would provide an appropriate means of redress, the court simply followed.127 In others, the parties agreed that recourse to section 3 would not be possible. Again, the court accepted the parties’ positions in each instance.128 The fact that in some cases all sides advocated for the use of section 3 helps shed some light on some of the more far-reaching decisions in the dataset. In the case of Hammond,129 the House of Lords authorised a significant change to very clear legislative text when it interpreted, under section 3, a provision which expressly removed the right to oral hearings as being subject to exceptions. The judges accepted that the use of section 3 in that case was ‘bold’130 and emphasised that the appropriateness of using section 3 was not a live issue between parties, meaning the court was happy to proceed without probing the issue too much.131 One judge, Lord Rodger, stated on record that despite acceding to the parties’ requests, his judgment should not be taken as approving their use of section 3.132 Therefore, one of the most far-reaching interpretations under section 3 was in fact adopted, in large part, because the government defending the case urged the court to accede to that route rather than having to respond to a declaration of incompatibility down the line.

Something similar occurred in the case of TTM. Provisions which set out the contours of qualified immunity in the context of mental health detention were practically ripped up by the judges, but this was done with the consent of both parties, with no judicial comment being made on whether, but for the parties’ agreement, a radical interpretation would have been arrived at.133 A final example is AF (No 3),134 where Lord Scott was openly sceptical of the interpretation adopted using section 3 in an earlier case.135 However, he noted that the use of section 3 had been urged by the secretary of state in that case and was not challenged thereafter. Whilst strongly suggesting that such a reading was improper, he resigned himself to acceding to the interpretation which all parties accepted with a pithy ‘so be it’.136

These cases show that party argument, and especially party agreement, is a very serious influence on the judicial use of section 3, and can sometimes authorise some far-reaching, head-scratching interpretations.137

Secondary Legislation

The obligation arising under section 3 applies to all legislation, primary and secondary. Of course, judges have additional tools when it comes to secondary legislation which breaches the Convention: they can simply declare it invalid, or, depending on the legislation in question, can declare the exercise of its power unlawful under section 6 of the Act.138 But this has not stopped judges from using section 3 to construe secondary legislation in a Convention-compliant way where possible. Indeed, they have a legal duty to do so. This raises the question as to whether it is less objectionable for judges to apply section 3 to modify secondary legislation compared to primary legislation. In other words, is the fact that legislation is not passed by Parliament a factor which might make it more likely that a section 3 reading is ‘possible’ to adopt in practice?

Scant judicial attention was paid to this issue until 2021, when the High Court delivered its judgment in the Trinity House Deptford Strond case.139 In that case, the court was being asked to interpret provisions which essentially put in place a moratorium for private evictions during the most severe parts of the coronavirus pandemic. It said plainly that ‘the court may take a more interventionist approach in general with subordinate legislation’ because ‘the grant by Parliament of the right to make it is, at least impliedly, on the basis that it will be Convention compliant’.140 The court therefore linked the subordinate nature of the legislation with the court’s ability to infer that there is sufficient space for a section 3 interpretation, or at least that such an interpretation would not patently go ‘against the grain’ of it.

The court went on to identify, in addition to primary and secondary legislation, ‘an intermediate situation where … subordinate legislation has obtained … positive approval from Parliament’141 and suggested that in such cases, it will be more difficult to find the requisite space for a Convention-compliant interpretation. The regulations in question in the case fell into such a category, therefore a higher threshold for the use of section 3 applied.

Therefore, just as the courts reserve the right to take a more interventionist approach when it comes to secondary legislation more generally, the court in Trinity seemed to suggest that the level of intervention will also depend on what kind of secondary legislation is involved. Presumably, under section 3, subordinate legislation passed, for example, via the affirmative resolution procedure will be subject to greater deference than, say, a Henry VIII power which was passed without any legislative scrutiny whatsoever, mirroring the position the courts have tended to adopt in judicial review cases more generally.142 In more recent judgments, when courts and tribunals have been asked to apply section 3 to secondary legislation, the subordinate nature of that legislation has been explicitly noted as a relevant consideration in the court’s overall process of deciding whether a compliant interpretation is possible.143

Interestingly, despite these comments, the majority of cases in which the courts deploy section 3 have related to primary rather than secondary legislation. As noted above, there may be other reasons for this, likely including the fact there are alternative public law and statutory remedies available to claimants when it comes to secondary legislation which breaches the Convention. Are Judges ‘Equipped’ to Identify a Solution?

Dicta in the seminal Ghaidan case suggest that there are two central limits on the use of section 3: the first is that an adopted interpretation should not go against the grain, or undermine the fundamental purpose, of the legislation, bringing considerations relating to language and intent into play; the second is a slightly more ambiguous condition that judges must not use section 3 in an area in which it is unsuitable for the judiciary to venture.144 As Kavanagh puts it, judges undertake a ‘rounded assessment of what is … constitutionally appropriate’ for them to do in each case.145 This raises the question of what constitutes, to use the language of the case law, a ‘decision … for which [judges] are not equipped’ to make.146

I suggest there are five classes of case where, despite recognising an incompatibility, and finding that an alternative reading would not go against the grain of legislation or be otherwise improper, judges have refused to invoke section 3 because they felt they were ‘not equipped’ to provide a solution. In such a situation, it would not be ‘possible’ to adopt an interpretation under section 3. Naturally, these classes of case will overlap significantly, as judges have been reluctant to spell out the contours of this particular condition.

The first class of case is when the proposed solution involves considerations of policy.147 The term is unhelpfully ambiguous, and it is sometimes difficult to know exactly what judges mean by it. It is possible that reference to ‘policy’ simply signals engagement with one of the four classes of case set out below. But ‘policy’ has been invoked as a consideration in its own right in cases like Dutch (where it was inappropriate to provide a solution to an incompatibility because changing the law would require grappling with ‘questions of policy’),148 Mercer (where the court could identify ‘no formulation that does not involve making a series of policy choices’)149 and Bellinger (where the court refused to use section 3 to provide a reading of a statute which would require it to wade into ‘questions of social policy’).150 The term must mean something. ‘Policy’ might simply be used as shorthand to signal that a case involves issues which are particularly politically important or controversial; the cases of Dutch, Mercer and Bellinger involved issues relating to prisoner rehabilitation, trade union rights and recognition of sex changes, respectively. Where an issue has political salience, judges may simply feel it best to leave the determination of an appropriate solution to Parliament. Of course, many cases where the courts have used section 3 have involved deeply political issues and controversial policy choices.151 Indeed, almost all issues involving human rights considerations will have some kind of policy dimension to them.

The second class of case involves issues which are too ‘fundamental’ for judges to consider. This seems to involve considerations beyond mere political controversy; in Bellinger, the court suggested that questions about what constitutes a valid marriage are simply too ‘fundamental’ for the courts to determine, and that questions of such weight should be left to the legislature.152 Some questions may simply be too significant or monumental, regardless of the extent of any political heat surrounding them, to be determined judicially.

The third class of case involves issues which judges lack requisite expertise to determine. These issues need not be controversial or even important, but they are necessarily complicated and niche. In JR111, the court found that the law which required a medical diagnosis of gender dysphoria before a Gender Recognition Certificate (GRC) could be applied for was incompatible with the Convention. But, owing to the complicated relationship not only between the law’s treatment of sex and gender, but also inherent in the mechanisms for obtaining a GRC and the social and medical complexities therein, the court considered that it lacked the required expertise to be able to proscribe a better solution which would satisfy scientific, social and medical experts.153 A specialist solution was needed, and the court could not provide one. Concerns relating to ‘administrative feasibility’ have also prevented judges from using section 3; the legislature, of course, could marshal such experts and make decisions as to feasibility, including in highly contested policy areas.154

The fourth class of case involves judges being asked to provide a reading of a statute which would be suitable for the case at hand, but which might have significant and unintended repercussions in other cases. In Re S, Lord Nicholls was clear that this kind of consideration could affect the question of whether it is ‘possible’ to use section 3 in a given case;155 and, indeed, in other cases the courts have been explicit about their concern that the use of section 3 would give rise to ‘ramifications’156 and ‘repercussions’157 across different contexts which they would be ill-equipped to predict and control.

The final, fifth class of case involves judges being presented with multiple competing options, where any one would cure the incompatibility, but there being no objective reason to prefer one over another.158 In Ghaidan, the court confirmed that ‘there may be several ways of making a provision Convention-compliant’ and that this choice may often require ‘legislative deliberation’.159 Similar passages feature prominently in, and play a big part in the reasoning of, other cases.160 In Aviva Insurance,161 the High Court linked the presence of multiple options with the language of ‘possible’ in the HRA:

when considering whether a Convention-compliant reading is ‘possible’, the court must keep in mind that section 3(1) mandates … a reading down only to the extent that it is necessary in order to make the legislation Convention-compliant … a reading down will not pass that test if it pre-empts alternative ways in which the court might reasonably anticipate the legislature could choose to render the provisions compliant.162

In Mercer, the Supreme Court declined to use section 3 in the absence of ‘a single, obvious legislative solution’,163 where it was ‘far from obvious … what the nature, scope and structure of the requisite protection should be’164 and where the court could not ‘assume that Parliament would necessarily choose’ one solution over another. The approach in Mercer showcases not only a great reluctance on the part of judges to invoke section 3 where there are multiple available solutions, but also an insistence on linking ‘possible’ interpretations to that which might be contemplated by Parliament.

What can be gleaned from these five (admittedly overlapping) classes of case? Judges are not necessarily using section 3 in a radical way. Judges are reluctant to use section 3 where they have identified a problem but feel they cannot bring forth an adequate solution. They are loathe to proscribe solutions to policy questions for which they feel they should not be taking responsibility. In addition to factors such as legislative intent, party arguments and precedent, the issue of whether judges are ‘equipped’ to deal with certain issues arises as a relevant consideration for, or against, the judicial exercise of section 3.

Relationship with Other Powers of Interpretation

This section looks at the judges’ approach to section 3 alongside, and in comparison to, similar powers and provisions, beginning with the relationship between ordinary interpretation and the special method of interpretation under section 3.

Relationship with Ordinary Interpretation: Ordering of Operations

The remedial provisions under the HRA are subject to an ordering of operations. When encountering a provision which is alleged to give rise to an incompatibility with the Convention, judges should approach matters in three steps: first, they should determine how the provision is to be construed using ordinary methods of interpretation; second, supposing that interpretation produces a result which is incompatible with the Convention, it should be determined whether the adoption of a special section 3 interpretation is possible;165 and third, only if the second step is found not to be possible, the court should consider whether to issue a declaration of incompatibility under section 4 HRA.166

Why should courts consider ordinary interpretation as being ‘logically prior’167 to any consideration of section 3? Section 3 interpretations and ordinary interpretations are different methods, and, crucially, because section 3 is exclusively suited to remedying otherwise incompatible legislation, it only becomes relevant if utilising ordinary methods of interpretation brings about a result which is clearly incompatible with the Convention.168 Lord Millett in Ghaidan said that ‘the obligation arises (or at least has significance) only where the legislation in its natural and ordinary meaning, that is to say as construed in accordance with normal principles, is incompatible with the Convention’.169

If there is no incompatibility when applying these ‘normal principles’, the courts lack the jurisdiction to go on to consider section 3.170 In the case of Bosher, the tribunal rejected the claimant’s argument that section 3 should be used to provide legislation with the most Convention-compliant meaning possible; upon an ordinary construction, the legislation did not breach the Convention, so section 3 was not considered relevant at all.171 Something similar happened in the recent Friends of the Earth case. The High Court rejected an attempt to invoke section 3 in the abstract; the judge said this:

it is only if the ordinary interpretation of a provision is incompatible with a Convention right that section 3(1) is applicable. Otherwise [it] can be safely ignored … section 3(1) does not allow a court to adopt an interpretation of a provision different from that which would otherwise apply in order to be ‘more conducive’ to, or ‘more effective’ for, the protection of a Convention right.172

These cases make it very clear, in principle, that there is a clear and structured order to the use of section 3, and that it should not be considered absent the presence of an incompatibility under ordinary interpretive principles. However, it is not necessarily clear that this limitation has been routinely adhered to, as analysis of Figure 7 will show.

Alternatives to ordinary interpretation (all cases).

Once it has been determined that a section 3 interpretation cannot be adopted, the courts can then—and only then—go on to consider whether a declaration of incompatibility under section 4 would be suitable. Because section 3 is ostensibly the ‘primary’ remedy under the HRA173 and the use of section 4 is a ‘last resort’,174 an assessment as to whether section 3 can be used to cure an incompatibility is a ‘preliminary step’175 before any consideration as to whether section 4 should be used. Indeed, Lord Steyn spoke of a ‘a strong rebuttable presumption’176 in favour of deploying section 3 over section 4. Although not all cases in which section 4 is invoked expressly consider whether section 3 should be used, and some judgments jump from assessing the Convention-compatibility of a provision to the question of whether a section 4 declaration ought to be issued,177 in such cases it might be considered obvious, or agreed between the parties, that section 3 could not be used in such instances.

Therefore, section 3 operates as the second of three remedial steps (even if these steps are not always adhered to rigidly): consideration of ordinary interpretation is a condition precedent for its use, and consideration of section 3 is itself a condition precedent for the use of section 4 HRA.

Relationship with Ordinary Interpretation: Substance

What, then, of the substantive difference between interpretations adopted under section 3 and that under the rules of ordinary interpretation, which of course involves, in modern times, consideration of a purposive approach?178 Section 3 creates a power which is, on its face, stronger than even the most liberal use of purposive interpretation.179 Nevertheless, in a number of cases in which section 3 was used, the judges signalled the possibility that a purposive interpretation may yield similar results in practice.180 In Cachia, Brooke LJ admitted that he ‘might have been tempted’ by recourse to ordinary purposive interpretation to adopt an interpretation equivalent to that adopted under section 3 if the provisions of the HRA were not available to him.181 In Culnane, Eady J adopted a particular interpretation using section 3 on the basis that he was bound to do so by precedent, but opined that, in the absence of authority, he would have thought that the same conclusion could be arrived at via ordinary interpretative principles.182 In multiple high-profile judgments involving panels of judges, certain judges, dissenting or concurring, have suggested that they would have come to the same conclusion as their colleagues who invoked section 3, but would have done so using ordinary interpretation instead.183

Indeed, sometimes in judgments the use of section 3 is not really distinguished from the use of wider purposive considerations, and sometimes section 3 is itself framed as a kind of purposive consideration to be weighed amongst others. It is often unclear where purposive interpretation ends and a section 3 interpretation begins.184 Clearly, judges are not always adhering to the ordering obligation set out above, although it should be noted that on many occasions judges will confirm that because ordinary interpretation provides a solution, recourse to section 3, however attractive, is barred.185

Figure 7 sets out how often judges viewed section 3 as a mere alternative to ordinary interpretation and how often they viewed section 3 as providing a more powerful remedy, distinguishing it from ordinary interpretation.

As can be seen, a significant minority of cases where section 3 was successfully invoked featured implicit (21 cases), and sometimes explicit (nine cases), suggestions that ordinary methods of interpretation would have sufficed and resulted in the same conclusion.186 This shows that in most cases, judges consider section 3 to bring something unique to the case at hand, but that this is certainly not true in all cases. If judges are adhering to the strict ordering requirement set out above, these statements—certainly the explicit ones—should not be made. There would be no possibility of considering whether ordinary interpretation and a special section 3 interpretation would yield the same result if the proper ordering of operations was complied with, because if the application of ordinary interpretation does not result in a breach of the Convention, there should be no recourse to section 3. Equally, if section 3 is invoked, it should only be because the application of ordinary interpretation has yielded a result which breaches the Convention. The figures therefore suggest that despite repeated judicial pronouncements urging judges to adhere to a strict ordering of operations in section 3 cases, these warnings are not being adhered to consistently in practice.

Relationship with Other Methods of Interpretation

Otherwise, judges have mooted the possibility of their section 3 interpretation being reached by different methods. Specific examples include recourse to the Interpretation Act,187 common law interpretive principles and presumptions,188 or the need for the court to interpret provisions in a way which preserves fair trial rights and procedural fairness.189 Often, judges will note the overlap between section 3 HRA and the need to adopt a conforming interpretation under EU law.190

Outside of the realm of interpretation, there may be alternative remedies available to claimants seeking to convince the court to use section 3. The case law is replete with examples where section 3 was seriously considered and either used in tandem with, or as an alternative to, remedies, including: the disapplication of primary or secondary legislation under EU law;191 invalidation of secondary legislation via judicial review;192 a finding of unlawfulness under section 6 of the HRA;193 and the court exercising its inherent jurisdiction to provide an alternative means of providing a remedy.194

If alternatives are available, what might the utility of using section 3 be? In some cases, the courts might question the jurisdiction to use an alternative remedy, so invoking the much more certain section 3 would provide a more solid basis for the decision.195 Section 3 can also be used to allow judges to get around precedent in a way which other remedies might not.196 Otherwise, section 3 allows judges to work with a significant degree of precision: it can be used to specify, where appropriate, exactly what is going wrong and exactly what needs to be done to fix it. Therefore, section 3 might be invoked, notwithstanding the availability of other remedies, to make things crystal clear or to ‘make … plain’ the statute’s alternative reading.197 Section 3 also creates a more permanent mark than some other remedies: once interpreted using section 3, the new meaning of a statute remains effective indefinitely unless expressly departed from, resolving residual problems arising from the former incompatibility.198

One alternative interpretive tool which does not feature in the case law (at least where section 3 is chosen as the interpretive tool to be deployed), perhaps to the disappointment of some, is the so-called principle of legality, whereby specific meanings of legislation are adopted in order to conform with common law rights.199 To be fair, references to this tool appear in both Thomas v News Group200 and Punch Partnerships,201 but in neither case did it carry particular weight. A simple explanation can be provided for the lack of references to the principle of legality: it is simply significantly weaker, and operates very differently, from the interpretive obligation under section 3. Although some have sought to argue that common law rights have, or ought to have, significant impact on the interpretation of primary legislation,202 their arguments fail to convince.203 Section 3 applies in the absence of ambiguity; common law rights do not. Section 3 can allow courts to question legislative intent; common law rights cannot. Section 3 invites judges to consider a specific and sophisticated set of rights; common law rights are vague and ill-defined.204 Despite some academic (and judicial) enthusiasm for common law rights, it should not be surprising to find that they are rarely canvassed as an alternative for the use of section 3 interpretations in practice.205

A ‘Minimum Necessary’ Principle?

Finally, it is useful to consider the arguable existence of a kind of minimum necessary principle in the case law when it comes to finding a suitable interpretation to be applied using section 3. Shortly put, supposing the court finds an incompatibility, and that it can be cured using section 3, it should only do so to the minimum extent necessary to secure compliance in the case at hand. This is often put forward as a way of reconciling the mandatory nature of the section 3 obligation with the inherent limitations of the judicial role vis-à-vis the legislature.206 As was straightforwardly said in Friends of the Earth, ‘if the court does have to rely on s.3(1), it should limit the extent to which the ordinary interpretation of the provision is modified to that which is necessary to achieve compatibility’.207

In Adesina, Kay LJ stated that any changes brought about by section 3 ‘must be [to] the minimum extent necessary to secure ECHR compliance’ and rejected the argument of the claimant that he should adopt the most compliant interpretation possible in the case before him.208 In other cases, judges have considered and rejected the claimants’ proposed remedies on the grounds that they reached too far and, if adopted, would go beyond the minimum necessary to cure the incompatibility arising in the case before them.209 In ABX, the tribunal rejected the argument that it should impute a kind of force majeure clause, considering that however useful this may be, it was not necessary to do so on the facts, adding: ‘our power begins and ends with reading the Order in a way which is compatible with Convention rights. Any words to be read in to the legislation can do no more, and no less.’210 In Beaulane, the judge rejected as inappropriate the inference of a far-reaching limitation to adverse possession and refused to imply a duty of equity into the statute—both would cure the incompatibility identified in that case, but both were too radical an approach.211

There is at least one exceptional case where this principle has not been applied: in the case of K, the Supreme Court rejected an interpretation put forward by the parties on the basis that whilst it may remedy the incompatibility in the case at hand, it would not cure the incompatibility in other hypothetical cases.212 A slightly more expansive reading of section 3 was adopted as a result. However, this single case does not detract from the general trend showing strong judicial adherence to a practical kind of ‘minimum necessary’ principle in section 3 cases.

Conclusions

This article has surveyed, using quantitative and qualitative methods, the practical reality of the court’s use of section 3. It has sought to map out how the judges see their role in practice in this respect, and has noted, in relation to the use of section 3: after an initial period of enthusiasm, its use has become steady over time; article 6 is by far the most likely right to be successfully invoked; cases are determined by a mix of both appellate and lower courts; criminal cases are disproportionately represented in the case law corpus; and the courts are reluctant to modify the language of the statute unless they feel they must, preferring instead to read sections ‘as if’ they meant something different to their ordinary meaning.

The article has also looked at the controlling factors which guide judicial discretion as to whether to invoke section 3. Factors which encourage the use of section 3 include: the presence of ‘importable’ solutions; persuasive or binding precedent; agreement of parties that section 3 would be appropriate; the fact that the legislation being interpreted is secondary rather than primary legislation; the presence of vague rather than specific words; and the lack of any fundamental issues, policy concerns, topics which require expertise or issues which could be remedied in multiple ways. By contrast, the use of section 3 would be discouraged if doing so would: go against the wording of the statute (the more explicit the language, the more difficult this would be); undermine the intent of the legislator; involve reinterpreting primary rather than secondary legislation; or require judges to make a decision which they are not ‘equipped’ to take. The nature of the solution required, the provenance of the offending passages and the fact that a dispute is hypothetical appear to be neutral factors.

The relationship between section 3 and other interpretive methods has also been analysed; it is not a straightforward one and cannot be easily summarised, but it is clear that section 3 is both distinct from, and in some cases more useful than, related methods. It is notable, however, that in a distinct minority of section 3 cases, judges suggested that the operation of ordinary interpretative methods might have produced a similar result to the section 3 interpretation adopted in practice. This suggests that the gulf between ordinary and Convention-compliant interpretation may be real, but not always as significant as is sometimes assumed. Finally, it has been recognised that judges often apply a kind of ‘minimum necessary’ principle in practice, where they will generally only use section 3 to the barest extent required to achieve Convention compliance in each case. In practice, this operates as another limit on the potential for judicial abuse of section 3.

It would appear, from all of this, that in many ways the court’s use of section 3 does not match with some of the content of the criticism of this provision.213 It is suggested that this article provides some evidence to assuage the fears of such critics. Section 3 is powerful, but is perhaps not as radical as it is sometimes portrayed as being. Indeed, some of the cases in which section 3 is invoked appear very unradical indeed. The provision has been used, for example, in order to correct drafting errors214 and lacunas215 which escaped the legislature’s attention, and the provisions are not being used—at least, on a regular basis—to radically depart from the intention of the legislature, or to completely rewrite the meaning of a statute (it is telling that on the rare occasions where the courts have adopted a particularly radical interpretation, this has been at the insistence of all parties to the dispute). Finally, it is noteworthy that many of the limits to the use of section 3 recognised by the judges are entirely self-imposed. Where section 3 is not being used outside of its proper constitutional boundaries, the reason for that is largely because of the limits drawn up and adhered to by the judges themselves.

Of course, critics may allege that so long as a more permissive, activist approach is possible we should remain worried. Ekins and Gee rightly note that ‘courts have not always applied the section 3 duty as fully as their own case law permits’216 and that passages from the arguably more radical earlier case law remain authoritative. But if this is true, does the court’s actual practice not affect, in and of itself, the judicial understanding of how these earlier pronouncements should be understood? Does the practical reality of widespread reticence not contribute towards the forming of a new standard, one which dilutes some of the more radical sentiments in the older case law? It is suggested that reality, rather than rhetoric, should provide the primary guide as to how best to understand section 3.

The most realistic picture of section 3 reveals neither reticence nor radicalism. We continue to see the repetition of fairly expansive rhetoric from the early case law, but we do not see that approach being applied in practice. We see some cases where section 3 is used to depart from the intention of Parliament, but many more cases where departing from legislative intent is portrayed as some Rubicon which cannot be crossed. As has been shown, the judicial approach to section 3 has not been shown to be consistently radical in practice.
