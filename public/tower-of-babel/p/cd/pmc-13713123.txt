Pattern of children death in Sfax, Tunisia: A 14-year autopsy study
Authors: Wiem Ben Amar, Mariem Grayaa, Fatma Daoud, Hanen Dhouib, Karim Derbel, Ahmed Chakroun, Samir Maatoug, Zouhir Hammami, Malek Zribi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aim: Child mortality is a critical indicator of public health. Identifying the causes and circumstances of death among children is the first step in preventing this tragedy. The purpose of this study was to investigate and analyse the epidemiological, social, and forensic characteristics of children deaths in Tunisia over a 14-year period and identify the leading causes of death.Methods: We conducted a transversal descriptive study in the Department of Forensic Medicine at Habib Bourguiba University Hospital of Sfax over 14 years (January 1 st, 2008-December 31st, 2021) including all forensic autopsies carried out on children.Results: A total of 436 cases were examined with a male predominance (Sex ratio:1.5) and a mean age of 8 ± 6 years. Accidents were the most prevalent manner of death (70%) followed by natural deaths (16%), suicides (7%) and homicides (2%). A significant association between sex, age group and the manner of death (natural/violent deaths) was observed (p<0.001). The majority of accidents were due to road traffic accidents (48%), followed by drowning (20%). For suicidal deaths, hanging was the most common method (65%) followed by self-immolation (16%) and, finally, ingesting pesticides (13%). The most frequent method of homicide was sharp force.Conclusion: This study provides valuable data regarding child mortality patterns in Tunisia, which can help to implement adequate preventive measures.

INTRODUCTION

Child death is a tragedy that affects both families and society. According to the WHO (1), child mortality rates have shown a global decline since 1990 reflecting significant global progress. However, in some countries, especially sub-Saharan African and South Asian countries, children face alarmingly high mortality rates. In Tunisia (2), the child mortality rate decreased between 1985 and 2000. Since then, it has remained relatively stable ever since. Between 1972 and 2006, there was a six- fold decrease in child mortality rates for children under 5-years of age and a five-fold decline for infants under one year. However, disparities exist between regions based on rural/urban area, socioeconomic status and access to healthcare services. Globally, the leading causes of death for children under 5-years include preterm birth complications, birth asphyxia/trauma, infectious diseases and congenital anomalies (1). For older children, road traffic injuries and drowning are the leading causes of death (1). The purpose of this study was to investigate and analyse the epidemiological, social, and forensic characteristics of children deaths in Tunisia over a 14-year period and identify the leading causes of death in order to guide preventive strategies and activities.

METHODS

It was a transversal descriptive study conducted in the Department of Forensic Medicine at Habib Bourguiba University Hospital of Sfax over 14 years (January 1 st, 2008-December 31st, 2021). Forensic autopsies in Tunisia, are required by law in cases of sudden, suspicious or violent deaths. Our department performs approximatively 700 medicolegal autopsies annually covering almost all the southern part of Tunisia. It is located in the district of Sfax (second city of Tunisia) and it used to cover the medicolegal activity of 7/24 districts of Tunisia (Sfax, Mednine, Sidi Bouzid, Gafsa, Tatouine, Tozeur, Kebili). Since 2016, other department of forensic medicine have been opened (Mednine, Sidi Bouzid, Gafsa). Our study included all forensic autopsies carried out on children who died during the study period. According to the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the tunisian’s Child Protection Code (3,4), a child is defined as a human being under the age of 18 years. Data were collected from police investigations reports, medical records, deceased relatives’ commemoratives, and autopsy reports. We excluded cases of children’s deaths related to illegal immigration and infanticides due to their specific characteristics. We analyzed socio-demographic variables (age, sex, nationality, origin), medical history, circumstances of death, the cause, and the manner of death. The victims were divided into five age groups: <1, 1–4, 5–9, 10–14, and 15–17. The manner of death (natural, accident, suicide, homicide, undetermined) was assumed based on the context, autopsy findings and confirmed by the judicial authority. Manner of death remains undetermined when the circumstances were unknown or insufficient, or when the cause of death was not specified. Statistical analyses were performed using statistical package SPSS for Windows, version 22.0. Descriptive statistics including numbers and percentages were calculated for qualitative variables. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean. The Student t-test was used for means comparison, the Chi-squared test to compare qualitative variables, and non- parametric tests in cases of non-applicability of the previous tests. A P value ≤0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

All procedures involving human participants in this study were performed in accordance with ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. All data were anonymized.

RESULTS

During the study period, a total of 9235 autopsies were conducted, with 436 cases concerned children aged between 0 and 17 years (4.5%). On average, 31 cases of child death per year were reported during the study period. The distribution of cases showed two peaks in 2017 (60 cases) and 2020 (58 cases).

One third of child deaths occurred during the summer. There was a male predominance with a male-female ratio of 1.5 with the mean age of 8 ± 6 for both sexes. Roughly 33% of the victims were aged between 1 and 4 years. Tunisian nationality accounted for most of the cases (96%) and most of them were from rural areas (66%). Only 26% of the deaths occurred in hospital and merely 12% of the victims had antemortem medical histories. Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of the study population categorized by the manner of death. Accidental deaths were the most common (70%), followed by natural deaths (16%), suicidal cases (7%) and homicidal cases (2%). The manner of death remained undetermined in 19 cases (4%). Male predominance was observed in all manners of death except for natural deaths.

The manner of death (natural/violent) varied significantly with the victim’s sex (p<0.0001). Age also showed significant variations in terms of natural/violent death (p<0.0001). However, there were no significant variations in the manner of death (natural versus violent) to the season, origin(urban/rural) and the place of death (hospital/elsewhere) (Table 2).

For accidental deaths, there was a male predominance (sex ratio of 1.9). Only 13 cases had antemortem medical histories. The majority of these deaths were attributed to traffic accidents (48%), followed by drowning (20%), poisoning (17%), falls (7%), and burns (6%). Traffic accidents were the leading cause of death for children over one year while two thirds of the victims being boys. Most of cases involved pedestrians (50%), who were hit by vehicles in 80% of cases, or motorbikes in 20% of cases. We noted motorbike victims (drivers or passengers) in 20% of cases. The remaining cases involved car passengers. Most drowning victims were aged between 1 and 4 years (n=35/61) with wells being the most common site of drowning. Toxicological analysis of accidental deaths due to substance inhalation showed that 9 cases were due to CO poisoning, 3 cases were attributed to pesticides, 2 cases resulted from the inhalation of caustic agents, and 3 cases resulted from exposure to other toxic products (Table 3).

For suicidal deaths, no history of diagnosed psychiatric disorders was reported in any case. The male to female ratio of suicide victims was 1.5. Most of victims were lived in rural areas (n=27/32). The most common methods were hanging (65%), self-immolation (16%), and ingesting pesticides (13%) (Table 4). Hanging and self-immolation were more frequent in males, whereas ingesting pesticides and drugs were more common in females.

The majority of homicide victims were male aged between 15 and 17 years. Sharp force, reported in four cases, was the most frequent method of homicide. Other observed causes included burns in two cases, firearms two cases, blunt trauma in one case, and strangulation in one case (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Our study provides significant insight into child mortality in Tunisia offering valuable data for planning preventive strategies. We conducted autopsies on 436 cases of child death, with an average of 31 cases per year. Consistent with previous studies, our findings showed that the majority of victims were male (5–7). This sex disparity was also noticed in the northern of Tunisia, South Africa, and Kuala Lumpur (8–10). This can be attributed to the impulsive behaviour, curiosity, and outdoor activities of boys. Nevertheless, in a separate study carried out in New Delhi, the sex distribution of child fatalities showed variations across years. In 2019, a higher number of deaths were documented among males, whereas in 2020 and 2021, deaths were more recorded in female paediatric autopsies (11). In our series, the most affected age group was children aged 1 to 4 years, which is in conformity with studies from Nigeria (12) and northern Tunisia (8). However, a Turkish study and a Finnish study reported higher incidences among 15 to 18-years-olds and 10 to 14-year-olds, respectively (13,14). The majority of deaths occurred out of hospital. This could be explained by the fact that most of them occurred in rural areas where there was limited access to healthcare services and lack of medical transport. In our study, accidents were the most common manner of child death. This is consistent with previous studies, which have shown rates of 40% to 54% for accidental death (7,10,11). In 2004, according to the WHO, global child violent deaths were distributed as follows: accidental deaths accounted for 84.3%, homicidal deaths accounted for 5.8%, and suicidal deaths for 4.4% (15). The percentage of violent deaths was higher in Tunisian boys compared to girls (p<0.001). These findings are in line with those reported in other countries (7,10). A significant association between age groups and manner of death was found (p<0.001). As the children grew older, the percentage of natural deaths decreased, while the percentage of violent deaths increased. As reported in the literature, the majority of accidental deaths were attributed to road traffic accidents (7,10,11,16). According to the WHO, road traffic accidents are the leading cause of death for children and young adult aged 5-29 years. The rates of traffic- related mortality among children vary between regions and countries depending on the enforcement of strict regulations and the income levels of countries (17). Approximately, one quarter of the deaths in road traffic crashes in Sindh, Pakistan involved children between 2 and 14 years of age (18). This reinforces the view that children are very vulnerable to deaths in road traffic crashes. In line with previous studies, the majority of child deaths resulting from road traffic injuries were among pedestrians (8,13,18). In our study, 61 child deaths were attributed to accidents involving water, which is in accordance with findings in the United States, where water-related accidents were the leading type of nontraffic accidental deaths (19). As noted by other authors (8,10), water deaths occurred mainly in the group 1-4 years. These particular ages reflect the inability of young children to understand and/or deal with potentially hazardous surroundings. The location of child drowning varies between countries. In our study, as reported in Northern Tunisia, Damman and Poland, most of the drowning incidents occurred in natural bodies of water (62%) (5,8,20). Meanwhile, swimming pools were the primary site of drowning in the United States (26–37%), and Australia (19,21). In Japan, drowning in the bathtub was common (28–39%) (22). This variation depends on the geographical area and natural landscape. In this study, suicidal deaths accounted for 7% of all child deaths. It affected adolescents aged between 10 and 17 years. Several behavioral, psychological, family and social factors influence suicidal ideation during adolescence. At this age, adolescents often feel insulted or attacked by family members or friends which can lead to depression and other psychiatric disorders. They may also experience anxiety related to their academic performances (23). In our study, no antemortem psychiatric disorders were diagnosed in any case. This can be explained by the fact that many families concealing psychiatric illness, which is considered a negative label and a societal taboo. In accordance with previous Tunisian studies, most of suicide victims were from rural areas (8,24). Our study showed that suicide occurred more frequently in males with a male to female ratio of 1.5. According to the literature, male teenagers are more likely to commit suicide than females. However, females are generally more likely to experience suicidal ideation and make suicide attempts (25). Worldwide, the suicide rate for males aged between 10 and 19 years was found to be more than twice that of females (26). This higher rate of completed suicide among males, especially in many European and American countries, may be linked to their greater (27–30). In contrast, a higher suicide rate among teenage females was recorded in some governorates of Tunisia, China, and some Middle East countries (24,29,31). According to Petroni et al. (32), the increased number of suicides among adolescent girls is attributed to gender discrimination, sexual abuse, exclusion from education, unemployment, and unequal family responsibilities. Consistent with the literature (10,23,33), the majority of suicide incidents were due to hanging (34 cases, 69%). This can be explained by the easy availability and accessibility of materials for hanging (33). However, there were geographic variations. For example, firearm suicides and jumping from high places were the most common methods of suicide in the USA and Hong Kong, respectively (27,31,34). Gender analysis showed that hanging was the most commonly used method for both boys and girls. Poisoning using pesticide was more common among girls, while self-immolation was committed only by boys. Overall, it has been shown that males are more likely to employ more violent methods of suicide like firearms or hanging, whereas females tend to resort to less violent methods such as pesticide poisoning (23). This distribution of violent and non-violent suicide methods varies between countries. In accordance with our findings, a Turkish study showed that hanging was the most common suicide method among females (23). Comparatively, homicide, the least common manner of death (2.2%), was more predominant among males aged between 15 and 17 years (7 out of 10 cases). A possible explanation for this result is that boys at this age are more exposed to quarrels and other forms of violence due to spending more time outside their homes. In the age group of 15 to 17 years, sharp force injuries were common, as highlighted by other studies of child homicide conducted in Tunisia and other countries (35– 37). In contrast with other reports(35,37), shooting was rare as a method of child homicide. This can be explained by the Tunisian regulations that ban the possession of such weapons. Natural deaths accounted for 12% of all children deaths autopsied in our series, with 30% of them occurring in children under 1 year and 38% occurring in the 1-5- year age group. Van den Ordel (10) has reported natural diseases in 7% of children who were admitted to the medico legal laboratory, with 50% of them occurring in the age of 1-5 years. As indicated by some studies including infants (5,7,38), the majority of this age group die from natural diseases. Respiratory diseases were the major cause of natural death in our study. According to the WHO (39), the leading causes of death among children include respiratory infections, diarrheal diseases, measles, malaria, malnutrition and new-born conditions. However, patterns of child natural deaths varied between regions. In 19 cases, the cause of death could not be established. After the exclusion the intervention of an external factor, this could be explained that some natural deaths could not be identified and not be scientifically ascertained (cardiac arrythmia, genetic disease…). To the best of our knowledge, our study was the first exhaustive chronological report including all cases of child death conducted in southern Tunisia. Although this study was specific to a region, the implications can be generalized to other Tunisian governorates. It allowed us to compare our results with national and international data. However, our study had some limitations as it was retrospective study, which meant that some information was unavailable.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study provides essential data regarding violent and unexpected child deaths in southern Tunisia. The most common manner of death were accidents followed by natural diseases, suicides and homicides. Violent deaths were more prevalent among males and concerned traffic accidents. Many of these fatalities can be prevented through strategic interventions. Recognizing these violent deaths as a substantial factor in mortality and implementing a targeted and preventive plan are crucial at this time.

Key points • Accidents were the most prevalent manner of death followed by natural deaths, suicides and homicides. • A significant association between sex, age group and the manner of death (natural/violent deaths) was observed (p<0.001). • The majority of accidents were due to road traffic accidents, followed by drowning. • Hanging was the most common method of suicide followed by self- immolation and, finally, ingesting pesticides.
