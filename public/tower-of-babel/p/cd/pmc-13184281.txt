Distinct neural substrates of obsessions and compulsions in adolescent obsessive compulsive disorder
Authors: Kun Li, Chenchen Zhang, Rui Li, Xiaochi Yuan, Cong Zhou, Liju Qian, Kexu Zhang, Wei Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by notable clinical heterogeneity, which may limit treatment precision. Although traditional content-based models have improved symptom characterization, they may overlook key pathophysiological features. A complementary process-based framework that distinguishes between obsessions and compulsions offers a promising alternative, but the neurobiological correlates of these dimensions remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we recruited 40 adolescents with OCD and 40 matched healthy controls and conducted connectome-wide association studies (CWAS) using multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR) to identify brain regions whose whole-brain connectivity patterns were associated with obsessive or compulsive symptom severity. Follow-up seed-based analyses were then performed to delineate the relevant circuits, and cross-modal comparisons were further used to examine the alignment of symptom-connectivity association maps with neurotransmitter receptor distributions and gene expression profiles. We found that obsessive symptoms were associated with altered connectivity patterns centered on the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex and Cerebellum Posterior Lobe, whereas compulsive symptoms were linked to the Ventrolateral Prefrontal Cortex. In both cases, connectivity between each symptom-specific target and the default mode network (DMN) was negatively correlated with the severity of its corresponding symptom dimension. Moreover, the symptom-connectivity association maps for obsessions and compulsions showed distinct associations with neurotransmitter systems and transcriptomic signatures. Together, these findings provide novel evidence for distinct neurobiological substrates underlying obsession and compulsion dimensions in adolescent OCD, support the utility of process-based symptom modeling, and suggest potential targets for dimension-specific intervention.Subject terms: Psychiatric disorders, Prognostic markers

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a common and disabling neuropsychiatric condition, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1% to 3% in adolescents [1]. Compared to adults, youth with OCD often show earlier onset, greater familial aggregation, and higher rates of comorbid neurodevelopmental conditions [2, 3]. Despite these developmental distinctions, most neuroimaging research to date has focused on adult populations, leaving the neural substrates of adolescent OCD relatively understudied [4]. Meanwhile, treatment outcomes in adolescents remain suboptimal, with many patients showing residual symptoms despite guideline-recommended interventions [5]. One contributing factor may be the heterogeneity of symptom presentation, which could reflect distinct pathophysiological mechanisms [6]. Identifying the neural correlates of specific symptom dimensions may inform personalized, circuit-based treatment strategies, including refinement of targets for noninvasive brain stimulation.

Previous efforts have largely focused on content-based symptom dimensions, such as contamination/washing, responsibility/checking, symmetry/ordering, and hoarding [7]. Neuroimaging studies suggest partial dissociations among these dimensions: for example, contamination-related symptoms are often linked to altered Insular activity [8], whereas responsibility-related symptoms engage the Anterior Cingulate Cortex [9]. However, symptom content alone may not fully capture the complexity of OCD. For instance, tasks involving contamination cues elicit increased Insular activation in adults with OCD but decreased responses in youth with OCD [8, 10]. These age-related discrepancies reflect potential limitations in the developmental validity of content-based symptom classification. Furthermore, many studies have failed to determine whether specific treatments are particularly effective for patients with distinct symptom dimensions [11, 12]. These observations suggest that content-based classifications alone may be insufficient, and that alternative dimensional frameworks are needed to better account for the clinical and neural heterogeneity of OCD.

A complementary way to address the heterogeneity of OCD is to consider symptoms along process dimensions. In this view, OCD can be decomposed into obsessions and compulsions as two relatively distinct processes. Although obsessive thoughts may differ in explicit content, they often converge on similar neural systems of threat appraisal and anxiety, whereas diverse compulsive rituals may rely on shared circuits for habit learning and action control [13]. Modeling OCD along thoughts versus actions therefore targets two core processes, namely biased emotional and cognitive evaluation of threat on the one hand and repetitive behavior with reduced voluntary control on the other [14, 15]. This distinction is already reflected in clinical measurement. The Yale Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) and the Children’s Y-BOCS (CY-BOCS) both provide separate scores for obsessions and for compulsions, covering frequency and duration, subjective distress, resistance, and perceived control, and these subscales show good internal consistency and test-retest reliability [16, 17]. Process dimensions may also have implications for treatment response. Patients with predominantly compulsive symptoms often respond more readily to exposure and response prevention, in which blocking the action sequence can rapidly disrupt the symptom cycle, whereas patients with primarily obsessive thoughts may improve more slowly and require greater emphasis on cognitive and emotion regulation strategies [18, 19]. These differences suggest that the neural targets for treating compulsive behaviors and obsessive thoughts may not fully overlap. Thus, investigating the distinct brain circuits underlying obsession and compulsion dimensions may enhance our understanding of OCD subtypes and help identify candidate targets for dimension-specific interventions in adolescents.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) provides a valuable approach for investigating the neural correlates of process-based OCD symptoms, as it characterizes large-scale brain network organization without requiring participants to perform specific tasks [20, 21]. This feature reduces the influence of task performance variability and compliance issues, making rs-fMRI particularly suitable for pediatric populations [22]. Prior studies using rs-fMRI have explored functional connectivity alterations associated with obsessive and compulsive symptom dimensions in OCD, revealing partly dissociable network profiles corresponding to these distinct processes [23, 24]. However, such research in adolescents remains limited. Moreover, most adult studies have relied on hypothesis-driven methods, focusing on a priori defined brain regions. While informative, this region-of-interest approach may overlook distributed or unexpected neural correlates and lacks the capacity to capture whole-brain symptom-network associations. To overcome these limitations, connectome-wide association studies (CWAS) based on multivariate distance matrix regression (MDMR) have emerged as a promising alternative [25]. This data-driven, voxel-wise method enables an unbiased search across the entire brain to identify regions whose whole-brain connectivity patterns vary systematically with individual differences in symptom severity. Unlike conventional univariate approaches, MDMR does not rely on predefined spatial assumptions, making it well-suited to detect diffuse or network-level correlates of complex psychiatric traits. This approach has been successfully applied in previous studies to identify neural signatures related to individual differences in depressive symptoms, schizophrenia related features, cognitive functioning, and other dimensions [26–28]. When applied to OCD, CWAS holds promise for uncovering distinct functional connectivity patterns linked to obsessive versus compulsive symptoms, thereby advancing understanding of their underlying neurobiological mechanisms.

Building on this process-based framework, the present study aimed to dissociate the neural mechanisms underlying obsessive versus compulsive symptoms in adolescent OCD. We conducted an MDMR analysis of rs-fMRI data from adolescents with OCD to quantify how the whole-brain connectivity profile of each voxel covaried with individual differences in obsession and compulsion severity. Brain regions showing significant associations were used to define seeds for follow-up seed-based functional connectivity analyses, allowing us to further delineate specific circuits related to each process dimension. Given the heightened genetic loading and developmental sensitivity in youth with OCD, we additionally examined how the MDMR-derived statistical maps for obsession and compulsion aligned with macroscale neurotransmitter receptor distributions and spatial patterns of whole-brain gene expression. Through this multi-level approach, our goal was to advance the neurobiological understanding of core obsessive and compulsive processes in adolescent OCD and to identify dimension-specific targets that may inform future circuit-guided interventions.

Methods

Participants

This was a data-driven, exploratory, observational study approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Daizhuang Hospital (Approval No. 202410KS-1) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092275). Forty adolescents diagnosed with OCD (OCD group) were recruited from the outpatient and inpatient services of Shandong Daizhuang Hospital. Forty age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC group) were recruited from local communities via public advertisements. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians prior to participation.

Inclusion criteria for the OCD group were: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for OCD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-V); (2) age between 12 and 18 years; (3) a CY-BOCS total score ≥16; and (4) right-handedness. Exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of substance abuse or dependence; (2) any other psychiatric disorder, except for comorbid anxiety or depressive symptoms commonly associated with OCD; (3) a history of neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, or other organic brain conditions); and (4) contraindications to MRI scanning. Healthy controls met the following criteria: (1) age between 12 and 18 years; (2) right-handedness; (3) no personal or family history of psychiatric disorders; (4) no history of neurological disorders or systemic medical conditions; and (5) no contraindications to MRI. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The OCD and HC groups did not differ significantly in age or sex (both p > 0.05), although the OCD group had significantly fewer years of education (p < 0.001). Among OCD participants, 23 were on stable pharmacological treatment and 17 were treatment-naïve. All medicated patients were taking selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including sertraline, fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, and escitalopram. Symptom severity was evaluated using the CY-BOCS, anxiety levels were assessed using the Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale (SCAS), and depressive symptoms were measured using the Children’s Depression Rating Scale (CDRS). The CY-BOCS obsession and compulsion subscores were used as dimensional indices of obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviors, respectively, in subsequent analyses.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

All neuroimaging data were acquired at the Department of Radiology, Shandong Daizhuang Hospital, using a 3.0 Tesla Siemens MRI scanner. Each participant underwent both high-resolution structural and resting-state functional imaging. T1-weighted structural images were obtained using a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (3D-MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.25 ms, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 300 × 320, voxel resolution = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, slice thickness = 0.8 mm, and 192 sagittal slices. Rs-fMRI data were collected using a simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence: TR = 1000 ms, TE = 33 ms, flip angle = 60°, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, matrix = 90 × 90, voxel size = 2.44 × 2.44 × 2.4 mm3, slice thickness = 2.4 mm, with 60 contiguous axial slices and an SMS acceleration factor of 6. A total of 480 functional volumes were acquired per participant.

Data preprocessing was conducted using the advanced version of the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSFA) toolbox [29], based on SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The first 10 volumes were discarded to allow for signal equilibration and participant adaptation. Functional images were then corrected for differences in acquisition time between slices (slice timing correction) and adjusted for head motion using rigid-body realignment. Each subject’s high-resolution T1 image was aligned to the mean functional image and subsequently segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. The T1 image was then normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and the transformation parameters were applied to the functional data, which were resliced to a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3. To mitigate potential confounding effects, voxel-wise nuisance signals including six head motion parameters, white matter signal, and cerebrospinal fluid signal were regressed out from the functional time series. The data were subsequently spatially smoothed using a 4 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel. This relatively small kernel was chosen to preserve fine-grained signals in small subcortical nuclei (e.g., striatum) [30] and to accommodate developmental characteristics of adolescent brains [31]. The data were then temporally band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.1 Hz to suppress low-frequency drifts and high-frequency physiological noise. Subjects with excessive head motion (translation > 2.0 mm or rotation > 2.0° in any direction) were excluded from further analysis.

Connectome-wide association analysis

To identify brain regions where individual differences in obsessive-compulsive symptom dimensions were associated with distributed functional connectivity patterns, we conducted a CWAS using MDMR [25, 32, 33]. The full analysis pipeline is summarized in Fig. 1. To ensure the sensitivity of symptom-connectome associations, analyses were limited to the OCD group, thereby avoiding bias introduced by minimal symptom variance in healthy controls. Preprocessed functional images were resampled to an isotropic resolution of 4 mm to improve computational efficiency. For each voxel, a whole-brain connectivity map was constructed by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between the voxel’s time series and that of every other voxel, yielding a voxel-wise connectivity map for each subject. The similarity of connectivity maps across participants was quantified using a distance metric defined as dij=2(1−rij), where rij denotes the Pearson correlation between the connectivity maps of subjects i and j. This resulted in a distance matrix representing between-subject dissimilarities in voxel-level functional connectome. MDMR was applied to calculate a pseudo-F statistic testing whether inter-individual variability in the voxel’s connectivity profile was associated with the symptom dimension of interest while accounting for covariates. This procedure was repeated independently for all voxels across the brain. Two models were constructed separately: one using obsessive subscale scores from the CY-BOCS as the primary predictor, and the other using the compulsive subscale scores. In each model, the non-target subscale score (compulsive or obsessive, respectively) was included as a covariate to control for shared variance between dimensions. Additional covariates included age, sex, years of education, medication status (medicated vs. unmedicated), and mean framewise displacement (FD). Statistical inference was based on 10000 permutation tests per voxel. Voxel-wise results were thresholded at p < 0.005, and cluster-level significance was assessed using a permutation-based family-wise error correction at p < 0.05. To further assess the specificity of the observed associations, we conducted complementary models in which only the symptom dimension of interest (either obsessive or compulsive) was included as the predictor, without adjusting for the alternate subscale. This approach allowed us to determine whether the symptom-connectome associations remained evident when each dimension was modeled independently.

A Resting-state fMRI data and dimensional scores from the Children’s Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (CY-BOCS), including separate obsession and compulsion subscores, were collected for each participant. B For each seed voxel, C voxel-wise functional connectivity maps were computed across the whole brain for every participant. Seed-based functional connectivity analysis

To further identify functional connections associated with obsessive-compulsive symptom dimensions, we performed seed-based connectivity analyses using MDMR-derived clusters as seeds. Specifically, for each significant cluster derived from the CWAS, we extracted the average time series and computed Pearson correlation coefficients with all other voxels, resulting in individual seed-to-whole-brain FC maps. To characterize large-scale connectivity patterns, we quantified the average FC strength between each seed and eight large-scale functional networks. These included the seven canonical intrinsic cortical networks defined by the Yeo 7-network parcellation: the default mode network (DMN), frontoparietal network (FPN), dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), limbic network (LIM), sensorimotor network (SMN), and visual network (VIS), as well as a subcortical network (SCN) constructed from 36 subcortical nodes in the Brainnetome Atlas [34, 35]. For each participant, seed-to-network connectivity values were then correlated with the corresponding symptom dimension scores (obsessive or compulsive) using partial Spearman rank correlation, accounting for non-normal data distribution, with years of education and medication status included as covariates. Multiple comparisons across networks were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. For networks showing significant symptom-connectivity correlations, we conducted analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) on seed-to-network connectivity with years of education as a covariate. Group comparisons were performed between the OCD and healthy control groups, as well as between medicated and unmedicated OCD subgroups, to determine whether these symptom-relevant connections exhibited group-level differences. All imaging-derived measures satisfied the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance before statistical analyses were performed.

In addition, to localize specific brain regions driving the symptom associations, we performed a complementary whole-brain voxel-wise analysis using the FSL randomise tool. General linear models (GLMs) were constructed separately for each seed region and symptom dimension, controlling for age, sex, years of education, medication status, and mean framewise displacement. Group-level inference was based on 10000 permutation tests, with statistical significance defined at p < 0.001 (voxel-level) and p < 0.001 (cluster-level, AlphaSim corrected).

Neurotransmitter receptor profile correlation analysis

To investigate the neurochemical correlates of brain-wide associations with obsessive and compulsive symptoms, we computed spatial correlations between MDMR-derived association maps and the distribution profiles of six neurotransmitter receptors or transporters [36]. Receptor density maps were obtained from publicly available positron emission tomography (PET) datasets, encompassing serotonin 1 A receptor (5-HT1AR), dopamine D2 receptor (D2R), dopamine transporter (DAT), gamma-aminobutyric acid A receptor (GABAAR), vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), and metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5). All PET maps were aligned to MNI space. To enable spatial correspondence, both the symptom association maps and neurotransmitter maps were parcellated using the Shen 268-region functional brain atlas [37]. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were computed across parcels between each symptom map and each receptor distribution profile. FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons across neurotransmitter systems.

Transcriptomic profile correlation analysis

To explore the molecular correlates of symptom-related connectivity patterns, we assessed whether MDMR-derived whole-brain association maps (for obsessive and compulsive dimensions, respectively) were spatially aligned with regional transcriptomic profiles. Whole-brain gene expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; http://human.brain-map.org), which provides microarray-based transcriptional profiles from six adult human postmortem brains. Preprocessing was conducted using the abagen toolbox [38], involving probe reannotation, filtering based on expression intensity and inter-donor stability, and normalization across donors.

Expression data were parcellated using the Shen 268-region functional atlas, matching the parcellation of symptom association maps. Regional expression values were averaged across parcels and donors, yielding a 268 (regions) × 15633 (genes) matrix. For each symptom dimension, partial least squares (PLS) regression was used to identify genes whose spatial expression patterns tracked the voxel-wise symptom association strength. The MDMR association map served as the response variable, and the regional gene expression matrix as predictors. The first PLS component (PLS1), explaining the largest amount of covariance, was retained for downstream analyses. Gene-wise contributions to PLS1 were converted to Z scores via 1000 bootstrapped resamples. Genes with significant weights (FDR-corrected p < 0.001) were selected as the PLS1 gene set.

To interpret the biological relevance of the PLS1 gene set, we conducted two complementary enrichment analyses. First, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were evaluated using Metascape, applying hypergeometric testing with FDR correction. Second, we tested for enrichment of PLS1 genes within canonical marker gene sets for seven brain cell types (astrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells), derived from prior single-cell transcriptomic studies summarized by Seidlitz et al. [39].

Results

All participants’ MRI data passed quality control criteria for head motion. Consequently, the final analysis included 40 adolescents with OCD (mean age = 16.30 ± 1.14 years, 24 males) and 40 healthy controls (mean age = 16.63 ± 0.83 years, 17 males). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant moderate association between compulsive and obsessional symptom scores in the OCD group (ρ = 0.38, p = 0.015, Figure S1), indicating that these symptom dimensions are related but not redundant. Neither obsessive nor compulsive subscores were significantly associated with age, years of education, illness duration, or medication status (all p > 0.139), suggesting independence from basic demographic and clinical characteristics. Obsessive scores were positively correlated with CDRS scores (ρ = 0.484, p = 0.002) and SCAS scores (ρ = 0.366, p = 0.024), whereas compulsive scores were positively associated with SCAS scores (ρ = 0.409, p = 0.011), reflecting the presence of comorbid affective features.

Functional connectivity patterns associated with the obsessive symptom dimension

The connectome-wide association analysis identified two significant clusters where whole-brain functional connectivity patterns were associated with the obsessive symptom scores. These clusters were located in the left Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC; cluster size = 1647 mm3; peak MNI = −48, 18, 24; Z = 3.322; Fig. 2A) and the left Cerebellum Posterior Lobe (cluster size = 1215 mm3; peak MNI = −42, −60, −57; Z = 4.468; Fig. 2B).

Follow-up correlation analyses were performed to examine how the connectivity strength between these clusters and large-scale resting-state networks related to obsessive scores (Fig. 2C, D). The DLPFC-DMN connectivity showed a significant negative correlation with obsessive scores (partial ρ = −0.41, p = 0.011). However, this DLPFC-DMN coupling did not differ significantly between the OCD and HC groups (Fig. 2E). For the cerebellar cluster, connectivity with the VIS (partial ρ = −0.37, p = 0.022), SMN (partial ρ = −0.49, p = 0.002, FDR-corrected p = 0.016), and DMN (partial ρ = −0.33, p = 0.046) was negatively correlated with obsessive symptom severity. Notably, cerebellar connectivity with the VIS (F [1, 77] = 4.222, p = 0.043, partial η² = 0.052, Fig. 2F) and DMN (F [1, 77] = 4.043, p = 0.048, partial η² = 0.050, Fig. 2H) was significantly reduced in the OCD group relative to HC. No significant differences in functional connectivity were observed between medicated and unmedicated OCD subgroups (all p > 0.318). These findings were further supported by complementary MDMR and seed-based correlation analyses that modeled obsessive symptom dimension independently (see Supplementary Materials Result S1; Figures S2, S3; Table S1).

Functional connectivity patterns associated with the compulsive symptom dimension

The connectome-wide analysis identified one significant cluster associated with the compulsive symptom dimension, located in the right Ventrolateral Prefrontal Cortex (VLPFC; cluster size = 702 mm3; peak MNI = 42, 24, 3; Z = 3.421; Fig. 3A), partially extending into the Insular region.

Follow-up analyses showed that VLPFC connectivity with three networks was significantly associated with compulsive symptoms, including the LIM (partial ρ = −0.38, p = 0.018), DMN (partial ρ = −0.45, p = 0.005, FDR-corrected p = 0.040), and SCN (partial ρ = −0.35, p = 0.029) (Fig. 3B). At the group-level, significant OCD-control differences were observed for VLPFC connectivity with the LIM (F [1, 77] = 6.912, p = 0.010, partial η² =0.082, Fig. 3C) and SCN (F [1, 77] = 4.168, p = 0.045, partial η² =0.051, Fig. 3D), whereas the VLPFC-DMN connectivity did not show a significant group difference (Fig. 3E). A complementary MDMR analysis modeling compulsive symptoms alone also identified bilateral VLPFC clusters, supporting the robustness of the main findings. Detailed results from the complementary MDMR and seed-based correlation analyses are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Result S2; Figure S2, S3; Table S2).

Neurotransmitter signatures underlying symptom-connectome associations

We next examined whether the symptom-related connectome variations were spatially aligned with the distribution of neurotransmitter systems. Spearman correlation analyses revealed that the obsessive dimension was significantly and negatively associated with regional densities of D2R (ρ = −0.30, FDR-corrected p < 0.001) and DAT (ρ = −0.23, FDR-corrected p < 0.001). In contrast, the compulsive dimension showed a positive correlation with mGluR5 (ρ = 0.25, FDR-corrected p < 0.001) (Figure S4; Table S3).

Transcriptomic signatures associated with symptom-connectome associations

We identified transcriptomic signatures underlying symptom-connectome associations by correlating MDMR-derived brain maps for each symptom dimension with cortical gene-expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (Fig. 4B–F). PLS analysis revealed that PLS1 captured 26.6% of the variance in the obsessive dimension and was significantly correlated with the corresponding MDMR map (ρ = 0.331, p < 0.001, Figure S5A). Similarly, for the compulsive dimension, PLS1 explained 22.3% of the variance and showed a significant correlation with its MDMR map (ρ = 0.317, p < 0.001, Figure S5B). Applying a stringent threshold (FDR-corrected p < 0.005), we identified 1796 genes associated with the obsessive dimension and 641 genes associated with the compulsive dimension, with no overlap between the two gene sets. These results highlight distinct transcriptomic signatures underlying the obsessive versus compulsive symptom dimensions in adolescents with OCD. For the obsessive dimension, enriched terms were dominated by nuclear and intracellular regulatory programs, including mRNA and DNA metabolic processes, chromatin remodeling, nucleic acid-related catalytic activity, stress response regulation, and pathways associated with protein and organelle localization (Table S3, Fig. 4C). These enrichments describe a molecular profile heavily involving transcriptional control, epigenetic modulation, intracellular transport, and metabolic regulation. In contrast, genes associated with the compulsive dimension showed strong enrichment in presynaptic organization, synaptic vesicle cycling, endocytosis, Rac and Notch signaling, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, mitophagy, macroautophagy, and terms related to organelle structure and learning processes (Table S4, Fig. 4D). This pattern highlights molecular pathways central to synaptic transmission, membrane trafficking, and degradative cellular systems.

Discussion

This study represents the first connectome-wide investigation to dissociate the neural substrates of obsessive and compulsive processes in adolescents with OCD. Behaviorally, obsessive and compulsive symptom scores were moderately correlated (ρ = 0.38), highlighting that while related, these symptom dimensions may arise from partially distinct neural processes. By modeling symptom dimensions within a unified multivariate framework, we identified distinct regional substrates: obsessive symptoms were associated with altered whole-brain connectivity in the DLPFC and Cerebellum, while compulsive symptoms mapped onto VLPFC. Despite these anatomical distinctions, connectivity between each identified region and the DMN showed significant negative associations with symptom severity, indicating a shared pattern in which stronger obsessive or compulsive symptoms corresponded to weaker coupling with this self-referential network. Furthermore, the multivariate symptom-connectivity association maps exhibited distinct relationships with neurotransmitter receptor distributions and transcriptomic profiles, reinforcing the biological separability of the two process dimensions.

The DLPFC is a key hub for regulating working memory, cognitive flexibility, conflict monitoring, and emotion suppression, and has been repeatedly implicated as an abnormal region in OCD [40]. In the present study, we found that the severity of obsessive thoughts was associated with the whole-brain connectivity pattern of the DLPFC, particularly its reduced coupling with the DMN. This pattern is consistent with previous observations showing abnormal regional activity of the DLPFC during obsession-related tasks in OCD [41], highlighting the DLPFC as a potential target for neuromodulatory interventions aimed at alleviating obsessive symptoms. Existing studies have tested left-sided, right-sided and bilateral stimulation protocols, although the overall therapeutic effects remain modest [42]. Current clinical guidelines for repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation list low frequency right DLPFC stimulation as level C evidence (possible efficacy) [43]. Two explanations may account for these outcomes. First, DLPFC-targeted interventions may be more suitable for patients whose symptoms are dominated by intrusive thoughts. One report found that DLPFC stimulation preferentially reduced obsessive thoughts rather than compulsive behaviors [44]. Second, younger patients may derive greater benefit from DLPFC stimulation. Evidence from obsessive compulsive and related disorders suggests that left DLPFC stimulation is effective, but that treatment response decreases with increasing age [45]. These findings highlight the need for future trials specifically evaluating DLPFC stimulation for obsession dominant adolescents with OCD.

Beyond the DLPFC, the MDMR analysis also identified the Cerebellum as a region strongly associated with obsessive thinking. Follow-up functional connectivity analyses revealed that cerebellar connectivity with the SMN, VIS, and DMN was significantly negatively correlated with the severity of obsessive thoughts. In addition to its role in motor coordination, the Cerebellum contributes to higher order cognitive operations, and its dysfunction has been increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of OCD [46]. Aberrant cerebellar connectivity with the DMN may disrupt the regulation of internally oriented mentation, making intrusive thoughts more difficult to suppress [47]. Converging structural imaging studies further suggest that cerebellar abnormalities are linked to obsessive symptom severity [6]. Causal modeling work has shown that the Cerebellum exerts an excitatory influence on the DLPFC in patients with OCD, indicating that both regions participate in the neural circuitry underlying obsessive thoughts and that the Cerebellum may operate as an “upstream hub” [48]. Neuromodulatory interventions targeting the Cerebellum have emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue for OCD. A recent randomized controlled trial has provided preliminary evidence that cerebellar stimulation using transcranial direct current stimulation can alleviate obsessive symptoms [49]. Given the depth and anatomical constraints of the Cerebellum, traditional stimulation approaches are limited in spatial precision and penetration. Future applications of temporal interference stimulation or transcranial ultrasound stimulation, which offer improved deep and focal targeting [50, 51], may open new possibilities for modulating cerebellar circuits to reduce obsessive thinking.

Beyond obsessive thoughts, compulsive behaviors showed a distinct connectivity signature. We found that inter-individual variability in compulsive symptom severity among adolescents with OCD was associated with distributed connectivity patterns centered on the VLPFC, with symptom-related clusters extending into the Insular cortex. Unlike the DLPFC targets identified for obsessive thoughts, the VLPFC specifically supports inhibitory control over habitual or prepotent actions, making it particularly relevant for compulsive behaviors. From a functional systems perspective, the VLPFC and Insula form a tightly coupled circuit that integrates inhibitory control with salience detection and interoceptive signaling [52]. The VLPFC supports the suppression of prepotent or habitual actions [53, 54], whereas the Insula encodes the subjective salience and urge states that can trigger repetitive behaviors [55]. In OCD, heightened salience and urge-related signals in the insula, together with impaired inhibitory control in the VLPFC, may bias behavior toward compulsive responding. Consistent with this model, individuals with OCD show reduced VLPFC engagement during response inhibition tasks, reflecting weakened top-down control over maladaptive action tendencies [56]. Converging causal evidence further supports the functional importance of this circuit. Disruption of the right VLPFC via repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation impairs stopping performance in healthy individuals, demonstrating its necessity for inhibitory control [57]. In parallel, experimental manipulation of Insular activity in animal models can suppress maladaptive appetitive behaviors, suggesting that modulation of insula-related urge processing may reduce compulsive tendencies [58]. Together, these findings support a circuit-level interpretation in which compulsive behaviors in OCD reflect an imbalance between exaggerated salience or urge signaling (Insula) and insufficient inhibitory control (VLPFC). This framework provides a mechanistic rationale for targeting the VLPFC-Insula circuit in neuromodulation strategies for patients with predominantly compulsive symptoms.

Our analyses indicate a dissociation between prefrontal subregions in relation to OCD symptom dimensions: the DLPFC was preferentially associated with obsessive thoughts, whereas the VLPFC was linked to compulsive behaviors. This distinction suggests that these adjacent prefrontal areas may serve as dimension-specific targets in adolescents with OCD. This observation aligns with prior models of OCD neurocircuitry, in which the DLPFC participates in the dorsal cognitive circuit, supporting higher-order cognitive control and regulation of intrusive thoughts, while the VLPFC is part of the ventral cognitive circuit, mediating response inhibition and control over habitual actions [59]. Moreover, although all identified targets showed symptom-related connectivity with DMN, their network embedding exhibited distinct patterns across symptom dimensions. Connectivity between the obsession-related targets and widespread DMN hubs, including the Precuneus and Angular Gyrus, was associated with severity of obsessive thoughts, suggesting a broad coupling between obsession and self-referential/ruminative networks [60]. In contrast, compulsive behaviors showed a more focal DMN-related profile, with symptom severity primarily associated with connectivity between the VLPFC-Insula circuit and the Precuneus. This more restricted pattern suggests that compulsions depend primarily on the integration of urge-related salience signals with midline self-monitoring rather than diffuse self-referential processing [61]. Together, these dissociable DMN embedding patterns point to partially distinct network-level mechanisms underlying obsessive thoughts versus compulsive behaviors in adolescent OCD.

Accumulating evidence indicates that neurotransmitter dysregulation is a key contributor to major psychiatric syndromes and may shape disorder specific neural signatures [62]. In our study, obsessive and compulsive dimensions showed distinct associations with neurotransmitter profiles. The obsessive dimension was selectively associated with the spatial distribution of mGluR5, a receptor central to glutamatergic signaling and synaptic plasticity. Abnormal glutamate transmission has long been implicated in the pathophysiology of obsessive thinking, particularly through mechanisms linking excitatory drive with cognitive rigidity and intrusive thoughts [63]. By contrast, compulsive symptoms were associated with dopaminergic markers, specifically D2R and DAT, consistent with the well-established role of striato-thalamo-cortical dopamine signaling in habit formation, motor repetition, and reinforcement learning [64]. Our transcriptomic findings suggest a dimension-specific molecular architecture in OCD. PLS analysis revealed that genes associated with the obsession and compulsive dimensions of OCD were non-overlapping using a stringent statistical threshold (FDR-corrected p < 0.001). The obsession-related genes were primarily enriched in microglia [65], whereas the compulsive-related genes were predominantly expressed in excitatory neurons [66]. This dissociation indicates that different cell types may underlie the two core symptom dimensions in adolescents with OCD, highlighting the potential for dimension-specific mechanisms and interventions. However, these findings are correlational and should be interpreted with caution, as gene sets and associations may vary under alternative statistical thresholds or analytical approaches.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size remains relatively modest, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Second, participants were aged 12–18 years, so the findings may not extend to younger children with OCD. Third, the neurotransmitter receptor maps and transcriptomic data were derived from adult samples. Although these datasets provide useful molecular insights, they may not perfectly reflect adolescent-specific expression patterns, which should be considered when interpreting our molecular-circuit associations. Fourth, although our analyses identified potential molecular and circuit-level targets for different symptom dimensions, these targets were not experimentally validated, and future studies are needed to confirm their functional relevance. Fifth, our study focused on gray matter functional connectivity, while emerging evidence demonstrates that white matter also exhibits functionally meaningful BOLD signals [67, 68]. Integration of white matter functional connectivity may help further dissociate the neural substrates of obsessive versus compulsive symptoms.

In this study, we applied a data-driven connectome-wide association study approach to investigate the neural and molecular substrates underlying distinct symptom dimensions of adolescent OCD. Our results indicate that individual differences in obsessive thinking are associated with whole-brain connectivity patterns of the DLPFC and Cerebellum, whereas compulsive behavior is primarily linked to VLPFC-centered networks. Connectivity between these target regions and the DMN was negatively correlated with the corresponding symptom dimension. Furthermore, neurotransmitter and genomic analyses revealed that different symptom dimensions are associated with distinct molecular and cellular profiles, highlighting separate neurobiological mechanisms for obsessions and compulsions. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the heterogeneity of OCD and suggest potential dimension-specific targets for personalized neuromodulatory interventions.
