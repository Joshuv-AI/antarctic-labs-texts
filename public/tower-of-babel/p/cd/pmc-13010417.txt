Response to the letter: “Comment on A cost-utility analysis of pharmacopuncture versus physiotherapy for chronic low back pain: A multicenter, pragmatic randomized controlled trial”
Authors: Ye-Seul Lee, Chang-Hyun Han, In-Hyuk Ha
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Ritika and colleagues for their thoughtful and constructive comments on our study,1 “A cost-utility analysis of pharmacopuncture versus physiotherapy for chronic low back pain: A multicenter, pragmatic randomized controlled trial,” as presented in their Letter to the Editor. We are grateful for their positive appraisal of our work and for highlighting its methodological rigor and clinical relevance.

We appreciate their acknowledgment of our pragmatic multicenter design, use of validated outcome measures, and inclusion of productivity loss from a societal perspective. Their recognition of pharmacopuncture as a potentially cost-effective and opioid-sparing intervention further reinforces the importance of evaluating integrative approaches in chronic low back pain management.

Regarding the follow-up duration, we fully agree that the 25-week horizon of the within-trial analysis primarily reflects short- to mid-term cost-utility outcomes and may not capture the long-term trajectory of this chronic and recurrent condition. In response to this limitation, we have recently published a model-based economic evaluation extending the time horizon to three years using a Markov framework informed by the same pragmatic RCT data.2 In that study, pharmacopuncture remained dominant over physiotherapy from both healthcare system and societal perspectives, demonstrating sustained gains in quality-adjusted life years and reduced overall costs over a longer horizon. We believe this complementary modeling approach addresses the concern regarding long-term cost-utility and strengthens the overall evidence base.

With respect to treatment heterogeneity, we acknowledge that the pragmatic design allowed for flexibility in clinical practice, which may have contributed to variability in treatment delivery. This approach was intentionally adopted to reflect real-world conditions and enhance external validity.3 Nonetheless, we agree that future studies incorporating stratified analyses, standardized core protocols, or component-level cost analyses may further clarify intervention-specific effects while maintaining pragmatic relevance.

We also appreciate the authors’ comments on the potential limitations in generalizability beyond the Korean healthcare context, where pharmacopuncture is integrated into routine practice but not uniformly reimbursed. We concur that cross-national economic evaluations and jurisdiction-specific adaptations of economic models are necessary to inform policy decisions in settings with different reimbursement systems, regulatory environments, and cost structures.

In conclusion, we are grateful to Ritika and colleagues for their insightful discussion and constructive suggestions. Their comments highlight important directions for future research, including continued long-term evaluation, methodological refinement, and international comparative analyses. We hope that our original trial-based analysis together with the subsequent 3-year Markov modeling study will contribute to evidence-based and context-sensitive decision-making in integrative pain management.
