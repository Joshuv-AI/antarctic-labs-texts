Novel indole alkaloids from the flower buds of Panax vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus: Distribution in reproductive organs and integrated biological evaluation
Authors: Thi Hong Ngo, Em Canh Pham, Linh Duy Nguyen, Thanh Thao Vu, Duy Hoang Truong, Ho Phuong Uyen LE, Thi Thu Thao Nguyen, Tuyen Ngoc Truong, Duc Viet Ho, Thi Hong Van LE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the chemical constituents of flower buds from several Panax species have been reported, the phytochemical profile of the flower buds of Vietnamese ginseng remains understudied, particularly alkaloids. This study aimed to characterize alkaloids from the flower buds of Panax vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus (PVF), investigate their distribution across reproductive plant parts, and their biological relevance.MethodsFlower buds were extracted with methanol, then fractionated by liquid–liquid partitioning and chromatography. Structures of isolated compounds were elucidated using NMR and MS. Their distribution in flower buds and various berry stages was analysed by HPTLC, HPLC/PDA, and UPLC-QToF-MS. Anti-bacterial activity was evaluated in vitro, and molecular docking with in silico ADMET analysis was conducted to explore potential targets and pharmacokinetic characteristics.ResultsTwo novel indole alkaloids, panaxindolic acid and pyrindolic acid, with the known alkaloid panaxindole and tryptophan, were isolated. All identified alkaloids were detected in multiple reproductive parts, indicating a broad distribution. Docking simulations predicted strong binding of the isolated alkaloids toward monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) through hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking with key residues, including CYS172 and TYR398. Although panaxindole exhibited limited anti-bacterial activity in vitro, computational analyses suggested potential interactions with bacterial DNA gyrase and the 30S ribosomal subunit. ADMET predictions indicated favorable pharmacokinetic profiles and low toxicity.ConclusionThis study presents the first systematic investigation of indole alkaloids from PVF flower buds and their distribution across reproductive organs. Although in vitro anti-bacterial activity of panaxindole was limited, in silico analyses highlight potential biological relevance to neurodegenerative disease-associated targets.Keywords: Panax vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus, Vietnamese ginseng, Indole alkaloid, Panaxindolic acid, Pyrindolic acid, Flower buds, MAO-B

Introduction

The genus Panax, widely distributed in the Northern hemisphere and mostly in temperate regions, comprises several medicinally important species that have been extensively used in traditional and modern medicine. In 1973, a new Panax species was discovered on Mount Ngoc Linh in central Vietnam and was subsequently identified as Panax vietnamensis Ha et Grushv., commonly known as Vietnamese ginseng (VG), representing the most southerly distribution of the genus. It has long been used by the Sedang ethnic group as a valuable herbal medicine to enhance physical strength and treat various diseases, with uses similar to those of Panax ginseng [1]. Since its discovery, P. vietnamensis has attracted considerable scientific interest, with studies spanning botany, genetics, phytochemistry, and pharmacology. Owing to morphological and geographical variations, this species is currently classified into three varieties: P. vietnamensis var. vietnamensis (PVV, Ngoc Linh ginseng), P. vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus (PVF, Lai Chau ginseng), and P. vietnamensis var. langbianensis (PVL, Langbian ginseng) [2,3].

Similar to other well-known Panax species, such as P. ginseng, P. notoginseng, and P. quinquefolius, P. vietnamensis is rich in dammarane-type triterpenoid saponins with protopanaxadiol (PPD) and protopanaxatriol (PPT) aglycone skeletons. Notably, Vietnamese ginseng is distinguished by its high content of ocotillol-type saponins, including majonoside R1, -R2, and vina-ginsenoside R1, -R2, which are rarely encountered in other Panax species and are considered chemical markers of this taxon. These saponins have been reported to exhibit diverse biological activities, such as anti-oxidant, hepatoprotective, anti-stress, anti-cancer, and neuroprotective effects, thereby contributing substantially to the pharmacological and economic value of Vietnamese ginseng. Consequently, phytochemical investigations of P. vietnamensis have predominantly focused on saponin constituents, whereas non-saponin secondary metabolites have received comparatively little attention.

Among non-saponin compounds, alkaloids represent a structurally diverse class of natural products with broad pharmacological relevance. Although alkaloids were once thought to be absent or negligible in ginseng, subsequent studies have demonstrated that alkaloidal fractions from Panax species possess notable biological activities, including radioprotective, anti-cancer, and lipid-lowering effects [[4], [5], [6]]. To date, only a limited number of alkaloids have been identified from Panax plants, primarily from the roots of P. ginseng and, in rare cases, from ginseng berries or degraded plant materials. The first three alkaloids, N9-formylharman, ethyl β-carboline-1-carboxylate, and perlolyrine, were isolated from the roots of Panax ginseng by Han et al., in 1986 [7]. Subsequently, additional alkaloids were also isolated from the roots of Panax ginseng, including 1-carbobutoxy-β-carboline, 1-carbomethoxy-β-carboline-1-carboxylate, 4-methyl-5-thiazoleethanol, norharman, harman, and spinacine [[8], [9], [10]]. Furthermore, studies on rotten Panax notoginseng roots have reported the isolation of two alkaloid compounds, namely (R)-2-(p-benzoate methyl)-4-(N-methylacetamido)-1-oxotetrahydropyrimidine and (R)-2-(3-hydroxy-4-methoxyl acyl) phenyl-4-(N-methylacetamido)-1-oxotetrahydropyrimidine [11]. Moreover, ginsenine and anoectochine, monoterpenoid indole alkaloids, and β-carboline-1-carboxylic acid were reported from the berry of P. ginseng and P. quinquefolius [12,13]. These findings suggest that alkaloids, although minor constituents, may contribute to the overall bioactivity profile of ginseng and warrant further investigation.

Compared with the extensive research on saponins, studies investigating alkaloids in Vietnamese ginseng remain very limited. To date, panaxindole, which belongs to the indole alkaloid class, is the only alkaloid reported from P. vietnamensis and has been isolated from the leaves [14]. Notably, no systematic study has been conducted on alkaloid constituents from the reproductive organs of Vietnamese ginseng, such as flower buds and berries. The discovery of additional alkaloids from this species, particularly from these parts, may provide access to structurally related metabolites with diverse pharmacological potential. Indole alkaloids constitute a prominent group of natural products known for a wide range of biological activities, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and neuroprotective effects [15,16]. Previous studies have demonstrated that indole-containing compounds can interact with multiple therapeutic targets, such as DNA gyrase and ribosomal proteins involved in antimicrobial activity, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) associated with inflammatory responses, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) implicated in cancer-related pathways, as well as monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B), a target relevant to neurodegenerative disorders [[17], [18], [19]]. Therefore, in addition to experimental antimicrobial evaluation, preliminary molecular docking against selected biological targets was performed to explore the potential pharmacological profiles of the isolated alkaloids and to provide a basis for future biological investigations.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to systematically investigate alkaloid constituents from the flower buds of Panax vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus. Specifically, this work aimed to isolate and structurally characterize alkaloids from flower buds, examine their distribution across reproductive plant parts at different developmental stages, and evaluate their biological relevance using a combination of in vitro assays and in silico approaches. By expanding current knowledge on non-saponin constituents of Vietnamese ginseng, this study provides new insights into the chemical diversity of Panax species and highlights flower buds as an underexplored but valuable source of bioactive compounds.

Materials & methods

Plant materials

Fresh flower buds of PVF (3.0 kg) were collected at a ginseng farm in Lai Chau Province, Vietnam, in June 2024. The materials were washed, drained, and the pedicels were removed, and subsequently dried at 60 °C, yielding 652 g of dried sample.

Different reproductive plant parts, including young, mature, and ripe berries, were collected in Lai Chau at various developmental stages for comparative analysis of alkaloid distribution. All samples were cleaned, dried at 60 °C, and ground into a crude powder before analysis. Photographs of all reproductive plant parts used in this study are provided in the Supporting Information (Figs. S1–S4).

Microorganisms

Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Lactobacillus plantarum were isolated from fermented food provided by the Pharmaceutical Biotechnology Laboratory. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and E. coli ATCC 25922 were purchased from Microbiologics (USA).

Chemicals and equipment

HPLC-grade solvents, including acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH), were purchased from Scharlab (Barcelona, Spain). Other solvents were supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA).

Ginsenoside standards were isolated and structurally characterized from KG and VG in our laboratory [20].

Column chromatography (CC) was employed with normal-phase silica gel (15–40 μm and 40–63 μm), RP-C18 (12–150 μm), and Sephadex LH-20 (25–100 μm), all from Merck. Solid-phase extraction (SPE) was carried out using Waters Sep-Pak Vac C18 cartridges (6 cc/500 mg).

HPLC analysis was performed using a Waters 2695 Separations Module equipped with the photodiode array detector (Waters 2996 PDA) and a Phenomenex C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 4 μm).

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was used to monitor chromatographic separations. Analyses were performed on silica gel F254 plates (Merck), and spots were visualized under UV light (254 and 365 nm), followed by heating after spraying with a vanillin–H2SO4 reagent (VS) or a 5% H2SO4 in ethanol.

Semi-preparative HPLC analysis was conducted on an Agilent 1100 Series HPLC equipped with a diode array detector (G1315 DAD) and a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (250 mm × 10 mm, 5 μm).

HPTLC was performed using the Linomat 5 semi-automatic sampler (Camag, Switzerland) with VisionCATS software. Samples were applied on to HPTLC precoated silica gel plates (20 × 10 cm, Merck, Germany).

UPLC-QToF-MS was conducted on a Waters Acquity UPLC–QToF system (Xevo G2-XS QToF) with Unifi software and UPLC column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) at the School of Pharmacy, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City.

NMR spectra were collected on Bruker Avance Digital 500 and 600 MHz spectrometers (Karlsruhe, Germany) at the Institute of Chemistry, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology, using tetramethylsilane as the internal standard.

Extraction and isolation

Dried flower buds (500 g) were pulverized into a crude powder and extracted under reflux with 80% MeOH (1.8 L × 3), followed by 50% MeOH (1.8 L × 3). The combined extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure to yield 257 g of crude methanolic extract. This extract was suspended in distilled water (1.0 L) and successively partitioned with chloroform (5.0 L), water-saturated ethyl acetate (13 L), and water-saturated n-butanol (9.0 L). Removal of the solvents under reduced pressure yielded a chloroform extract (6.3 g), an ethyl acetate (EA) extract (4.7 g), precipitate from the EA phase (6.0 g), and an n-butanol (Bu) extract (130 g).

The EA fraction was subjected to Sephadex LH-20 column chromatography (CC) using CHCl3–MeOH (8:2) to afford eight fractions (EA.1–EA.8). Fraction EA.4 (1.0 g) was further purified by silica gel CC with a stepwise gradient of CHCl3–MeOH–H2O (85:15:1 → 75:25:2 → 70:30:4), yielding five fractions (EA.4.1–EA.4.5). Fraction EA.4.2 was purified by solid-phase extraction (SPE) with 5% MeOH to afford compound 1 (21 mg). In contrast, fraction EA.4.5 was eluted with 1% ACN to yield compound 2 (18 mg).

The Bu fraction (120 g) was fractionated by silica gel CC using a CHCl3–MeOH–H2O gradient (8:2:1 → 75:25:10 → 7:3:1 → 65:35:10 → 6:4:1; lower layer), resulting in 15 fractions (Bu.1–Bu.15). Fraction Bu.3 (4.2 g) was further separated on Sephadex LH-20 using MeOH as eluent to obtain 5 fractions (Bu.3.1–Bu.3.5). Fraction Bu.7 (11.3 g) was subjected to reversed-phase RP-18 CC, eluted with a MeOH–H2O gradient (10:90 → 20:80 → 50:50 → 60:40 → 80:20 → 95:5), yielding 20 fractions. Recrystallization of Bu.7.1 and Bu.7.3 yielded compounds 3 (30 mg) and 4 (4 mg), respectively. Fraction Bu.7.4 was further purified by semi-preparative HPLC using 20% ACN to yield compound 4 (3 mg).

Computational methods

Conformational searches and geometrical optimization

The compounds were subjected to conformational searches using the Molecular Mechanics Force Field, followed by equilibrium geometry optimization using the PM6 semi-empirical and Hartree-Fock 3-21G methods in Spartan 14. Conformers with Boltzmann distribution values > 1% were then DFT optimized by ORCA 6.1 [21] at B3LYP/6-31G(d) in methanol (for ECD calculations). Harmonic vibrational frequencies were performed at the same level to ensure that no imaginary value was present, confirming the stability of selected conformers. The Gibbs free energy from the frequency calculations was used to calculate the Boltzmann distribution of conformers.

Theoretical calculation of the ECD spectrum

The optimized conformers were then subjected to TDDFT calculations using ORCA 6.1. TDDFT parameters were set at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G (d,p) level and CPCM of methanol. The ECD spectra at 30 excited states for each conformer were collected and summed to obtain theoretical ECD spectra using ChimeraX 1.10.1. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) was taken at ζ = 0.40 eV (for 2a, 2b), 0.8 eV (for 4). Shift excitation energies were applied at −0.6 eV (for 2a), −0.4 eV (for 2b). The ECD spectrum of the enantiomer was composed as the mirror image of that of considering form.

GIAO 13C NMR calculation with sorted training sets strategy

Conformational search of compounds (ent-2a, ent-2b) were performed using the MMFF, followed by equilibrium geometry optimization using the PM6 semi-empirical in Spartan 14. The initial conformers (Boltzmann distribution values > 1%) of ent-2a, ent-2b were optimized at B3LYP-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP/CPCM(DMSO) and tightSCF convergence using ORCA 6.1. GIAO NMR calculation was performed at ωB97X-D3/6-31G(d)/CPCM(DMSO)/VeryTight SCF. The carbon shielding tensors of ent-2a, ent-2b and experimental 13C NMR data of 2 were subjected to STS analysis (in DMSO as experimental NMR solvent) for stereochemical assignation.

HPTLC fingerprinting analysis

Sample preparation: Each powdered sample (100 mg) was extracted with 80% MeOH (10 mL) by sonication for 30 min at room temperature. The extract was centrifuged, and the supernatant was used for analysis. The analysis was performed using three flower bud samples and two berry samples, and the TLC profiles shown represent the average fingerprint patterns.

Ginsenoside and alkaloid standards solutions: Each reference standard was dissolved in MeOH to obtain a stock solution at a concentration of 1 mg/mL.

Chromatographic conditions: HPTLC analysis was performed on silica gel 60 F254 HPTLC plates (Merck). Standard and sample solutions (10 μL each) were applied as 6-mm bands using an automatic TLC sampler. Plate development was carried out in an ADC-2 automatic developing chamber, pre-saturated for 20 min. Two mobile phases were employed: CHCl3-MeOH-H2O (65:35:10; lower layer) and CHCl3-EA-MeOH-H2O (15:40:22:10). Chromatography was conducted to a migration distance of 8 cm at room temperature. After development, the plates were dried to remove residual solvent, then derivatized by spraying with VS reagent and heated at 105 °C until colored zones appeared. The chromatograms were visualized under UV light at 254 and 365 nm, and under white light after derivatization. The HPTLC fingerprint images were recorded using a TLC Visualizer Ultimate Package.

HPLC analysis

Sample preparation: Each powdered sample (100 mg) was extracted with MeOH (10 mL) using ultrasonication at 45 °C for 60 min. The extract was concentrated under reduced pressure to remove solvent, re-dissolved in water (0.5 mL), and subjected to SPE. The SPE cartridge was sequentially eluted with water (5 mL) and 80% methanol (10 mL). The 80% MeOH elute was collected and evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure; the residue was then re-dissolved in MeOH (10 mL). Before HPLC analysis, all samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane filter. The analysis was performed using six flower bud samples and three berry samples, and the chromatograms shown represent the average chromatographic profiles.

Chromatographic conditions: HPLC analysis was performed using a Waters 2695 system equipped with a Phenomenex C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 4 μm); flow rate was set at 1.0 mL/min, column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, the injection volume was 10 μL, and detection wavelength was carried out at 222 nm. The mobile phase consists of solvent A (water) and solvent B (ACN) with gradient program: 0–5 min (2% B); 5–15 min (2–35% B); 15–25 min (35–37% B); 25–45 min (37-48% B); 45-50 min (48-95% B).

UPLC-QToF-MS analysis

Sample preparation: Powdered sample (100 mg) of flower buds was extracted with MeOH (10 mL) using ultrasonication at 45 °C for 60 min. The extract was concentrated under reduced pressure to remove solvent, re-dissolved in water (0.5 mL), and subjected to SPE. The SPE cartridge was sequentially eluted with water (5 mL) and 80% MeOH (10 mL). The 80% MeOH eluate was collected and evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure. The residues were dissolved in MeOH to obtain a final concentration of 2000 ppm. Before analysis, the samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Xevo UPLC–QToF system with UNIFI software, using an Acquity Waters UPLC column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The gradient program was as follows: 0–3 min (2% B); 3–13 min (2–35% B); 13–18 min (35–37% B); 18–28 min (37-48% B); 28–30 min (48-95% B).

Mass spectra were acquired on a QToF mass spectrometer operating in negative electrospray ionization (ESI−) mode over an m/z range of 100–1500. The collision energy was set to 6 eV for low-energy scans and ramped from 40 to 60 eV for high-energy scans. The capillary voltage was 3.0 kV, the sample cone voltage was 40 V, and the cone gas flow was 40 L/h. The desolvation gas flow was set to 800 L/h, with source and desolvation temperatures maintained at 120 °C and 550 °C, respectively.

Anti-bacterial activities

Lactobacillus was cultured in MRS medium at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000g for 5 min, then resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 109 CFU/mL. The prepared bacteria were subsequently added to MRS medium at a final density of 107 CFU/mL, along with panaxindole to reach a final concentration of 200 μg/mL. The bacterial suspensions were fermented at 37 °C for 24 h and 48 h, and then the culture supernatants were collected. The experiment was performed in parallel with a control sample that did not contain panaxindole. The collected culture supernatants were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was harvested and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis of anti-microbial activity using the agar well-diffusion method. Antimicrobial activity was evaluated via the agar well-diffusion method using Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus 29,213. Briefly, 3–5 single colonies from 24-h MHA (Mueller Hinton Agar) cultures were suspended in 0.9% NaCl to a density of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL and swabbed uniformly onto MHA plates. Wells (7 mm in diameter) were punched into the agar, and 100 μL of the test supernatants was loaded into each well. After a 24-h incubation at 37 °C, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured.

Molecular docking

In addition to anti-bacterial and anti-fungal targets, several disease-related proteins, including COX-2, EGFR, CDK4, PI3K, and MAO-B, were selected for molecular docking. The inclusion of these targets was based on previous reports demonstrating that indole alkaloids and structurally related natural products frequently exhibit a broad spectrum of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and neuroprotective effects [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Therefore, molecular docking against these proteins was conducted as a preliminary in silico screening approach to explore potential pharmacological properties of the isolated compounds beyond the antimicrobial activities experimentally evaluated in the present study.

The molecular structures of the indole alkaloids and reference drugs were constructed in ChemBioDraw and subsequently energy-minimized in ChemBio3D to obtain energetically stable conformations. The optimized ligands were then used as inputs for molecular docking simulations performed with AutoDock Vina version 1.2.7. The three-dimensional structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), including DNA gyrase (Gyr; PDB ID: 2XCT), 30S ribosomal subunit (30S, PDB ID: 8CF1), lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51; PDB ID: 5ESF), 1,3-β-glucan synthase (GS; PDB ID: 8JZN), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; PDB ID: 5KIR), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR; PDB ID: 4WKQ), cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4; PDB ID: 7SJ3), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K; PDB ID: 4JPS), and monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B; PDB ID: 2V5Z). Before docking, all crystallographic water molecules were removed, and polar hydrogen atoms, along with Kollman partial charges, were added to the protein structures. Grid box dimensions and coordinates defining the active sites were generated using AutoDock Tools. The reliability of the docking protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized ligands into their respective binding pockets, yielding root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values ≤ 2.0 Å. Subsequently, the synthesized compounds and reference drugs were docked into the active sites of the selected targets using a grid-based docking approach. The resulting ligand-protein complexes were analysed with respect to binding orientation, interaction patterns, bond distances, and key interacting amino acid residues. Two-dimensional and three-dimensional representations of the ligand-target interactions were generated using Discovery Studio Visualizer [[22], [23], [24]].

In silico ADMET prediction

A comprehensive in silico evaluation of the physicochemical properties and ADMET parameters (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) of the alkaloids was conducted using the ADMETlab 3.0 platform [22,24].

Results and discussion

Structure identification

Compound 1 was obtained as a white amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 1 was deduced to be C18H23NO8 (Ω = 8) by HR-ESI-MS negative mode (m/z = 426.14172 [M + HCOO]-, calcd. for C19H24NO10, 426.1400). In combination with its 1H and 13C NMR spectra compared with reference data for panaxindole [14], the structure of compound 1 was identified as panaxindole (Fig. 1). Detailed MS and NMR characterization data, including the corresponding spectra for the four isolated compounds, are available in the Supporting Information (Figs. S5–35).

Chemical structure of the isolated alkaloid compounds from PVF.

Compound 2 was obtained as a white amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 2 was deduced to be C17H21NO8 by HR-ESI-MS negative mode (m/z = 366.12032 [M − H]-, calcd. for C17H20NO8, 366.1189), suggesting one nitrogen and eight degrees of unsaturation. The 1H NMR data of 2 (Table 1) displayed a spin coupling system of four aromatic protons [δH 7.54 (1H, d, J = 7.8 Hz), 7.46 (1H, d, J = 8.2 Hz), 7.08 (1H, t, J = 7.6, Hz), and 7.00 (1H, t, J = 7.4 Hz)], suggesting an orthosubstituted aromatic ring, an aromatic proton singlet at δH 7.25, and signals of a sugar moiety including δH 5.35 (1H, d, J = 9.0 Hz, H-1′), 3.74 (1H, t, J = 9.0 Hz, H-2′), 3.42 (1H, m, H-3′), 3.27 (1H, t, J = 9.0 Hz, H-4′), 3.45 (1H, m, H-5′), 3.92 (1H, d, J = 10.4 Hz, H-6′a), and 3.46 (1H, m, H-6′b). Furthermore, a methylene group at δH 3.14 (1H, dd, J = 14.8; 3.0 Hz), 2.62 (1H, dd, J = 14.8; 8.4 Hz), and an oxymethine proton at δH 3.78 (1H, dd, J = 8.6; 3.0 Hz) were also found in the 1H NMR spectrum. The 13C NMR spectrum that was combined with HSQC data showed 17 carbon resonances attributable to 2 methylenes, 7 methines, 3 sp2 tertiary carbons, and a carbonyl group at δC 176.8. The HMBC correlations from H-2 (δH 7.25) to C-3 (δC 113.5), C-9 (δC 128.8), C-8 (δC 136.4), and C-10 (δC 30.9); from H-4 (δH 7.54) and H-6 (δH 7.08) to C-8 together with the spin system of H-4/H-5/H-6/H-7 in the 1H–1H COSY spectrum indicated the presence of a 3-substituted indole moiety. The appearance of β-D-glucopyranosyl unit in 2 was proven by the large coupling constant (J = 9.0 Hz) of the anomeric proton δH 5.35 (H-1′) and the six carbon signals at δC 84.4 (C-1′), 71.7 (C-2′), 77.7 (C-3′), 70.0 (C-4′), 79.3 (C-5′) and 60.9 (C-6′).

1D and 2D NMR spectral data of compound 2 (DMSO-d6, 600 MHz).

The HMBC cross-peaks from H-1′ to C-2 (δC 123.6), and C-8 revealed the presence of an indole N-β-D-glucopyranoside skeleton. The substituent at C-3 was determined to be a 2-hydroxypropanoic moiety based on the HMBC interactions of H-10 (δH 3.14 and δH 2.62) with C-11 (δC 71.8) and C-3 (δC 113.5); of H-11 (δH 3.78) with C-12 (δC 176.8); along with the COSY correlation of H-10 and H-11. Structurally, compound 2 is an N-glycoside possessing an indole skeleton. Comparison with the reported 1H and 13C NMR data for compound 1 revealed the absence of a methyl signal in the structure of compound 2. Based on the combined analysis of the 1D and 2D NMR spectra summarized in Table 1, together with the 1H–1H COSY and HMBC correlations shown in Fig. 2, compound 2 was elucidated as a demethylated analogue of panaxindole. To clarify the absolute configuration of compound 2, the ECD calculations of 2 were first arbitrarily applied for possibilities, (11R, L-glc)-2a and (11S, L-glc)-2b. The optimized conformers were then subjected to TDDFT calculations at CAM-B3LYP/6-31G (d,p)/CPCM/methanol. The ECD spectra at 30 excited states for each conformer were collected and summed to obtain theoretical ECD spectra. The ECD spectra of the enantiomers (ent-2a, ent-2b) was composed as the mirror images of 2a, 2b. As shown in Fig. 3, the experimental ECD of 2 showed a good agreement with those of ent-2a, ent-2b, supporting the assignment of the sugar moiety as D-glucopyranosyl. This assignment was further corroborated by acid hydrolysis followed by TLC analysis of the liberated sugar, which exhibited chromatographic behavior identical to that of an authentic D-glucose standard (Fig. S36). Therefore, the sugar moiety in 2 was verified as D-glucopyranosyl.

HMBC and COSY correlations of compound 2.

Experimental and calculated ECD spectra of 2.

Owing to the high similarity between the theoretical ECD curves of ent-2a and ent-2b, the absolute configuration of C-11 could not be unambiguously assigned based on comparison of the experimental and calculated ECD spectra. To overcome this challenge, the sorted training sets (STS) strategy has been applied for ent-2a, ent-2b [25]. A comparison of experimental 13C NMR data of 2 with 13C shielding tensors of ent-2a, ent-2b using STS resulted in 100% probability of ent-2b (11R, D-glc isomer) (Fig. S37, S38 and Table S1, S2). Thus, the R configuration was unambiguously assigned for C-11. Compound 2 was elucidated as (R)-β-D-glucopyranosyl-indole-3-(2″-hydroxypropanoic acid) and was named panaxindolic acid.

Compound 3 was obtained as a white amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 3 was deduced to be C11H12N2O2 (Ω = 7) by HR-ESI-MS negative mode (m/z = 203.08117 [M − H]-, calcd. for C11H11N2O2, 203.0821). Structural elucidation was further supported by 1H and 13C NMR spectra, which were consistent with reference data for tryptophan [26]. Based on these combined data, compound 3 was identified as tryptophan (Fig. 1).

Compound 4 was isolated as a white amorphous powder. Its molecular formula is C12H12N2O2, determined by HR-ESI-MS negative mode (m/z = 215.08419 [M − H]-, calcd. for C12H11N2O2, 215.0821), indicating the presence of two nitrogen atoms with eight degrees of unsaturation. The 1H NMR data of compound 4 displayed four aromatic proton signals [δH 7.44 (1H, d, J = 7.8 Hz), 7.33 (1H, d, J = 8.0 Hz), 7.08 (1H, t, J = 7.4 Hz), and 6.99 (1H, t, J = 7.4 Hz)], consistent with an ortho-disubstituted aromatic ring. Two methylene groups were observed at δH 3.18 (1H, dd, J = 16.0, 5.2 Hz); 2.83 (1H, dd, J = 16.0, 10.6 Hz), and 4.22 (2H, m) and a methine proton signal at δH 3.65 (1H, dd, J = 10.6, 5.2 Hz) was also observed. Furthermore, a singlet signal at δH 10.9 attributable to an NH proton. The 13C NMR spectrum combined with HSQC data revealed 12 signals attributable to five methines, two methylenes, and five quaternary carbons, including one carboxyl carbon (-COOH). Comparison of the 1H and 13C NMR data of 4 with those of compounds 1–3 (Table 3) revealed the disappearance of the singlet proton signal at δH ∼7.25 (assigned to H-2), suggesting substitution at this equivalent position.

Compound 4 possesses a tetrahydro-α-carboline skeleton. Compared with β-carboline alkaloids, α-carboline derivatives have been rarely reported, especially from natural sources [27]. To the best of our knowledge, most tetrahydro-α-carboline derivatives reported previously were obtained through chemical synthesis [[28], [29], [30]]. Therefore, this study represents a noteworthy finding of a naturally occurring tetrahydro-α-carboline isolated from a plant source.

Analysis for the presence of indole alkaloids in the reproductive parts of Panax vietnamensis

HPTLC fingerprinting analysis

As shown in Fig. 6, mobile phase A provided satisfactory separation and clear detection of the alkaloid standards. The HPTLC fingerprint revealed intense bands corresponding to panaxindole and panaxindolic acid in flower bud extracts, whereas these compounds were detected only at low levels or were barely visible in berry extracts. This distribution pattern suggests that the accumulation of indole alkaloids varies across developmental stages of the reproductive organs. Because tryptophan showed poor visualization with the vanillin–H2SO4 reagent, an additional TLC analysis was performed using ninhydrin reagent, which is selective for amino acids (Figure S39). The resulting chromatogram confirmed the presence of tryptophan in all flower bud and berry samples. Given that tryptophan serves as a key precursor in indole alkaloid biosynthesis, its occurrence throughout the examined tissues may support the biosynthetic capacity for indole alkaloid production during reproductive development.

Furthermore, the mobile phase B provided satisfactory separation of the major saponin standards. Majonoside R2 (M-R2), ginsenoside Rg1 (G-Rg1), -Rd (G-Rd), and -Rb1 (G-Rb1), which are known to be among the predominant ginsenosides in the roots of Vietnamese ginseng [31,32], were not clearly detected in the reproductive tissues examined. In contrast, vinaginsenoside R1 (V-R1), an ocotillol-type saponin, was consistently observed as a prominent constituent in both flower buds and berries. This distinct distribution pattern suggests that V-R1 preferentially accumulates in reproductive organs, whereas the principal root ginsenosides (M-R2, G-Rg1, -Rd, -Rb1) are present at substantially lower levels. These observations are consistent with previous studies demonstrating tissue-specific distribution of ginsenosides in Panax species. In P. ginseng, roots are characterized by high levels of ginsenosides such as G-Rf, -Rb1, and -Ro, while stems and leaves exhibit distinct ginsenoside compositions, including G-Rg1, -Rh1, -Rc, and berries exhibited the greatest abundance of ginsenosides such as 20-O-glucoginsenoside Rf, G-Re, -Rg2 [33]. Similar organ-dependent accumulation patterns have also been reported in P. notoginseng and P. quinquefolius, where specific ginsenosides are differentially distributed between roots and aerial tissues [34,35]. The appearance of highly polar compounds in ripe berry extracts further suggests an increased accumulation of polar glycosides at later developmental stages. Such tissue-specific accumulation highlights the differential regulation of saponin biosynthesis between underground and aerial organs. Overall, the contrasting distribution of indole alkaloids and saponins among reproductive tissues, as illustrated in Fig. 6, reflects organ-specific and developmental regulation of secondary metabolism in PVF. HPLC/PDA analysis

If a single detection wavelength is selected, 222 nm appears to be the most suitable, as it provides strong absorbance signals for most of the detected compounds (Fig. 7). The detection of tryptophan in the methanolic extracts of reproductive plant parts is consistent with its known role as a biosynthetic precursor of indole alkaloids [36]. Its presence suggests that these tissues may be capable of producing indole derivatives. The accumulation of tryptophan in ripe berries, together with the lower abundance of indole alkaloid, may suggest developmental shifts in indole-related metabolism during fruit maturation. The developmental distribution of panaxindolic acid and panaxindole is noteworthy. Both compounds were clearly detected in flower bud extracts but exhibited substantially lower signal intensities in young, mature, and ripe berries. This trend suggests preferential accumulation of these indole alkaloids in flower buds and a decline in their abundance during fruit development. In contrast, pyrindolic acid was detected only as a minor peak in extracts analysed at concentration of 5000 ppm by HPLC/PDA, indicating that its concentration was below the method's detection limit. Overall, HPLC fingerprinting revealed clear differences in indole alkaloid profiles between flower buds and berries of PVF at different developmental stages, reflecting distinct metabolic roles of these organs. UPLC-QToF-MS analysis

The UPLC chromatograms of the MeOH extracts from different reproductive parts are shown in Fig. 8, and the identified compounds in the extracts are presented in Table 4. Panaxindole and panaxindolic acid were detected in MeOH extracts of flowers, young berries, mature berries, and ripe berries, indicating that these indole alkaloids are widely distributed across the reproductive organs of PVF. In the standards mixture, tryptophan exhibited a low MS response, suggesting that the ionization conditions were not optimal for this compound. This result is consistent with the HPLC results and supports increased tryptophan levels at later stages of fruit development. In general, indole alkaloids are widely recognized as defensive secondary metabolites that protect plants against herbivores and pathogens, and they often accumulate in actively developing tissues such as reproductive organs [40]. Therefore, the detection of indole alkaloids in PVF reproductive parts is consistent with their potential ecological role in plant defense. The biosynthesis of indole alkaloids originates from the amino acid tryptophan through a relatively well-studied biosynthetic pathway, in which two key enzymes are tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC) and strictosidine synthase (STR) [36]. Transcriptomic studies of Panax notoginseng have revealed the presence of the indole alkaloid biosynthetic pathway, and most genes involved in alkaloid biosynthesis show lower expression in roots than in aerial organs such as leaves and flowers [41]. This observation may explain the occurrence of indole alkaloids isolated from PVF flowers and suggests that further comparative studies on alkaloid distribution among different plant parts, as well as the discovery of additional alkaloids from reproductive tissues, are needed.

Anti-bacterial activity

Both L. plantarum and L. rhamnosus inhibited the growth of S. aureus and E. coli, as indicated by the diameters of the inhibition zones (Fig. 9 and Table 5). In general, inhibition tended to increase modestly over time (48 h vs. 24 h). Regarding the combinations, the addition of panaxindole did not significantly enhance the anti-bacterial activity of the tested Lactobacillus strains. Among all tested groups, L. rhamnosus with panaxindole at 48 h produced the largest inhibition zone, and the inhibition of E. coli was weaker overall. The positive control, tetracycline, produced substantially larger inhibition zones than any of the tested combinations. To clarify the direct anti-bacterial activity of panaxindole, the pure compound was evaluated independently against both bacterial strains and compared with the negative control. No inhibition zone was observed for either S. aureus or E. coli, indicating that panaxindole was inactive against both evaluated bacterial strains when used alone under the conditions employed in this study.

Compared with previously reported indole alkaloids, some of which have shown notable inhibitory effects against S. aureus or E. coli, whereas some compounds did not exhibit antibacterial activity against both tested strains. For example, indole alkaloids from Kopsia fruticosa exhibited anti-bacterial activity against several microorganisms, including S. aureus and E. coli, with kopsiafrutine E showing the strongest activity (MIC 0.15–1.14 mM; inhibition zones 23–24 mm) [42]. Similarly, indole alkaloids from a deep-sea-derived Streptomyces sp. showed potent anti-bacterial activity against S. aureus (MIC 0.2–4 μg/mL), with dionemycin and lynamicins B being particularly active against MRSA strains (MIC 1–2 μg/mL) [43]. However, 3-hydroxy-N-methyl-2-oxindole, which was isolated from the marine bacterium Salinispora arenicola, exhibited no inhibitory effects against E. coli and S. aureus [44].

Panaxindolic acid and pyrindolic acid were obtained in only limited amounts; therefore, their in vitro anti-bacterial activities could not be evaluated in the present study. The results obtained for panaxindole may serve as preliminary reference information for this class of structurally related compounds. Future studies are needed to evaluate the biological activities of these compounds (2) and (4) to further elucidate the biological potential of this class of metabolites.

Molecular docking

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the observed anti-bacterial effects - particularly the limited synergistic contribution of panaxindole - and to further explore the putative anti-fungal, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, neuroprotective, and anti-parkinsonian potential of the isolated compounds, molecular docking simulations were performed against nine pharmacologically relevant targets (Table 6 and Table S3, S4). Panaxindole displayed moderate to favorable binding affinities across multiple targets, with the highest scores recorded against CDK4 (−8.9303 kcal/mol), MAO-B (−8.5411 kcal/mol), Gyr (−8.1881 kcal/mol), and GS (−8.1592 kcal/mol). Notably, the affinity for bacterial Gyr (−8.1881 kcal/mol) surpassed that of the reference inhibitor ciprofloxacin (−7.9149 kcal/mol), suggesting a potential for panaxindole to engage the ATP-binding site of gyrase and interfere with DNA supercoiling and replication - a well-established mechanism for fluoroquinolone-class antibiotics. Similarly, the strong predicted interaction with the 30S ribosomal subunit (−6.7183 kcal/mol), comparable to that of tetracycline (−6.7117 kcal/mol), suggests possible interference with protein synthesis, albeit with lower potency than the reference compound. Despite these computationally favorable interactions, the experimental anti-bacterial activity of panaxindole-supplemented probiotic supernatants remained modest and did not exhibit significant synergy. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. First, panaxindole, as an indole alkaloid N-glucoside, likely exhibits limited membrane permeability and intracellular accumulation in intact bacterial cells, particularly in Gram-negative E. coli, where the outer membrane lipopolysaccharide layer restricts access to cytoplasmic targets such as Gyr and the ribosome [45,46]. Second, the docking results reflect optimal binding under idealized conditions, whereas in vitro efficacy is modulated by pharmacokinetic parameters, efflux pump activity, and metabolic stability - factors frequently limiting the potency of natural indole alkaloids against Gram-negative pathogens [47,48]. Third, the primary anti-bacterial components in the Lactobacillus supernatants are likely bacteriocins, organic acids, and hydrogen peroxide rather than panaxindole itself, explaining the absence of marked enhancement upon supplementation [49]. The relatively weaker activity against E. coli aligns with the docking profile, in which affinities for bacterial-specific targets (Gyr and 30S) were moderate compared to those for fungal targets (CYP51 and GS), underscoring a lack of Gram-negative selectivity. In summary, while panaxindole demonstrates promising in silico binding to key anti-bacterial targets, including DNA gyrase and the 30S ribosome, its modest experimental contribution to anti-bacterial activity highlights the challenges in translating computational predictions to whole-cell efficacy. These findings suggest that structural optimization of panaxindole - such as reducing glycosylation or enhancing lipophilicity - could improve membrane penetration and potentiate its activity, warranting further structure-activity relationship studies for the development of novel indole-based anti-bacterials.

In addition, the molecular docking results indicate that the indole alkaloids from PVF, namely panaxindole, panaxindolic acid, and pyrindolic acid, demonstrate modest to moderate binding affinities toward anti-inflammatory (COX-2) and anti-cancer targets (EGFR, CDK4, PI3K), with values ranging from −6.17 to −7.70 kcal/mol for COX-2 and EGFR/PI3K, and stronger but not exceptional scores of −8.50 to −9.11 kcal/mol for CDK4. These affinities are generally less competitive compared to standard inhibitors such as rofecoxib (−10.206 kcal/mol for COX-2), gefitinib (−8.1453 kcal/mol for EGFR), abemaciclib (−10.934 kcal/mol for CDK4), and alpelisib (−9.2739 kcal/mol for PI3K), suggesting limited potential as primary leads for potent anti-inflammatory or anti-cancer therapies targeting these pathways. In contrast, these compounds exhibit notably strong binding to MAO-B, with affinities of −8.5411 kcal/mol (panaxindole), −8.5845 kcal/mol (panaxindolic acid), and −8.8019 kcal/mol (pyrindolic acid), outperforming the reference tryptophan (−7.7560 kcal/mol) and approaching the clinical inhibitor safinamide (−9.0477 kcal/mol). Furthermore, the molecular interaction analysis from docking simulations demonstrates remarkable similarities between the indole alkaloids panaxindole, panaxindolic acid, and pyrindolic acid and the clinically approved MAO-B inhibitor safinamide in their binding modes within the MAO-B active site. All these three derived compounds form multiple conventional hydrogen bonds with key residues such as CYS172 (distances 3.54-3.76 Å in panaxindole and panaxindolic acid; 3.71 Å in pyrindolic acid) and TYR60, alongside carbon-hydrogen bonds involving ILE199 and GLN206 - interactions that align closely with safinamide's engagement of CYS172 via π-sulfur (5.06 Å) and LEU171/ILE199 through π-alkyl and carbon-hydrogen bonds (Fig. 10). Critically, the PVF compounds exhibit robust π-π stacked and T-shaped interactions with TYR398 (e.g., 4.65-5.97 Å in panaxindole, 4.66 Å in panaxindolic acid, and 3.75-3.86 Å in pyrindolic acid) and PHE343, mirroring safinamide's π-π T-shaped interaction with TYR326 (a related aromatic residue) and π-alkyl contacts with ILE199. Additionally, π-alkyl interactions with LEU171 are conserved across panaxindole, panaxindolic acid, and safinamide. These overlapping hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking in the aromatic cage (involving TYR398/TYR435 vicinity), and hydrophobic contacts underscore a highly analogous binding profile, suggesting that the isolated indole alkaloids mimic safinamide's non-covalent stabilization in the substrate and entrance cavities, thereby supporting their strong predicted inhibitory potency and potential as natural lead compounds for selective MAO-B targeting in neurodegenerative therapies.

Furthermore, global comparative research on natural MAO-B inhibitors consistently demonstrates binding affinities that are either comparable to or superior to those of synthetic standards [50,51]. For instance, the isoflavone genistein exhibits a significant docking score of −12.8 kcal/mol against MAO-B, stabilized by crucial hydrogen bonds with the residue THR201 [51]. Similarly, curcumin displays robust binding at −7.80 kcal/mol, driven primarily by hydrophobic contacts with TYR398 and PHE343 - residues that are integral to the enzyme's aromatic cage [51]. These interaction patterns are highly analogous to the binding modes observed in PVF-derived compounds (such as panaxindole, panaxindolic acid, and pyrindolic acid) and the clinical inhibitor safinamide, all of which engage residues such as CYS172, TYR398, and PHE343 to ensure stable complex formation [52]. Further validating this therapeutic potential, desmethoxyyangonin from Renealmia alpinia achieves an affinity of −7.98 kcal/mol through selective π-π stacking interactions with TYR326. This key gating residue that distinguishes the MAO-B isoform [50]. Additionally, (−)-maackiain isolated from Sophora flavescens demonstrates a strong preference for MAO-B by forming specific hydrogen bonds with CYS172, mirroring the selectivity profile and residue engagement seen in our PVF compounds [50]. While direct computational studies on ginsenosides often target the MAO-A isoform - exemplified by ginsenoside Rg1 possessing a high dock score of −216.593 (likely utilizing a non-kcal/mol scoring metric such as that in Molegro Virtual Docker) [53] - these findings collectively underscore the therapeutic promise of plant-derived molecules for managing Parkinson's disease [50,53,54]. Consequently, our results contribute significantly to the established spectrum of affinities reported in the global literature and highlight remarkable methodological consistency in how natural inhibitors stabilize within the MAO-B active site through specific interactions with amino acid residues.

In silico ADMET

The ADMET assessment indicates that these indole alkaloids exhibit acceptable drug-likeness, comply with Lipinski, GSK, and Golden Triangle criteria, and show favorable synthetic accessibility (SA score <6) (Table 7 and Table S5). Panaxindole and panaxindolic acid displayed moderate QED values, whereas pyrindolic acid demonstrated improved drug-likeness, though still inferior to safinamide. In terms of absorption, the three isolated compounds showed low Caco-2 permeability but high passive diffusion in the PAMPA model, comparable to safinamide. None were predicted to inhibit P-glycoprotein, although panaxindole and panaxindolic acid were identified as potential P-gp substrates, which may partially limit oral bioavailability.

Regarding distribution, all three indole alkaloid compounds exhibited acceptable plasma protein binding (<90%) and moderate volumes of distribution. BBB penetration was predicted to be negligible for these subtances, consistent with their physicochemical profiles. Interaction with hepatic transporters (OATP1B1/1B3 and BSEP) was more pronounced for panaxindole and panaxindolic acid, whereas pyrindolic acid showed a reduced transporter inhibition liability. In addition, the metabolic profile suggested minimal CYP450 inhibition for the isolated PVF indole alkaloids, indicating a low risk of drug-drug interactions. However, pyrindolic acid was predicted to act as a CYP2C9 substrate, while safinamide showed broader CYP substrate involvement. Human liver microsome (HLM) stability was highest for panaxindole, supporting its metabolic robustness (Table 7 and Table S5).

For excretion, three indole alkaloids exhibited low-to-moderate plasma clearance and short half-lives (1.6-3.2 h), comparable to safinamide. In particular, the toxicity prediction revealed a favorable safety profile for panaxindole and panaxindolic acid, characterized by low hERG inhibition, low hepatotoxicity, and absence of mutagenic or carcinogenic alerts. In contrast, pyrindolic acid showed higher probabilities for DILI, respiratory toxicity, and acute oral toxicity, indicating a less favorable safety margin. Environmental toxicity indices (BCF, IGC50, LC50) for all compounds were within acceptable ranges (Table 7 and Table S5).

Taken together, the integrated ADMET analysis supports panaxindole and panaxindolic acid as the most promising PVF-derived indole alkaloids, exhibiting balanced pharmacokinetic profiles and safety profiles that are broadly comparable to those of the reference drug safinamide.

Conclusions

In this study, a comprehensive phytochemical investigation of the flower buds of Panax vietnamensis var. fuscidiscus led to the isolation and structural characterization of two new indole alkaloids, panaxindolic acid and pyrindolic acid, together with the known alkaloid panaxindole and its precursor tryptophan. This work presents the first systematic report on alkaloid constituents of Vietnamese ginseng flower buds. The distribution analysis revealed that these indole alkaloids were consistently present across different reproductive plant parts, including flower buds and berries at various developmental stages, suggesting that alkaloid biosynthesis in the Panax species is not confined to underground organs but also occurs in aerial and reproductive tissues. Although preliminary anti-bacterial assays revealed limited in vitro activity of panaxindole under the tested conditions, molecular docking and ADMET predictions suggested favorable interactions with anti-bacterial and neurodegeneration-associated targets, underscoring the potential mechanistic relevance of these compounds. Taken together, these findings broaden current understanding of non-saponin secondary metabolites in Vietnamese ginseng, highlight flower buds as an underexplored yet promising source of bioactive indole alkaloids, and provide a chemical and biological foundation for future investigations into the functional roles and pharmacological potential of alkaloids within the genus Panax.
