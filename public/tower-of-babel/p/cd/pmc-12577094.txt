Oral–Sarcopenia nexus in health disparities: chewing and blood mediated pathways in a Chinese aging cohort
Authors: Yong Xiang, Guosong Wang, Yiting Cheng, Yuhan Zhang, Jialiang Zhao, Birong Dong, He Cai, Li Cheng, Tao Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging affects oral health and skeletal muscle, but the associations between oral diseases and possible sarcopenia remain understudied, especially in low-income regions. Thus, the study aimed to explore this relationship and potential mediating pathways.MethodsData were from the 2021–2022 follow-up of the West China Health and Aging Trend study. Assessments included questionnaires, oral examinations, muscle strength tests, physical performance tests, and routine blood tests. Logistic regression analyses, subgroup analyses, and mediation analyses were conducted.ResultsA total of 532 participants aged ≥ 60 years were included, with a possible sarcopenia prevalence of 50.4%. After adjusting for confounders, those with decayed and filled roots (DFRoot) had a 46% higher risk of possible sarcopenia than those without DFRoot, while those with a number of remaining natural teeth (NRT) ≥ 20 had a 53% lower risk. Participants with number of functional tooth loss (NFTL) > 16 had an 83% higher risk of possible sarcopenia than those with ≤ 16. Mediation analyses indicated that impaired chewing ability, systemic inflammation, and impaired liver function partially explain the oral–sarcopenia nexus. Subgroup analyses showed significant associations between NRT ≥ 20 (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.89), NFTL > 16 (1.90, 1.05 to 3.43), and possible sarcopenia in adults aged ≥ 70 years.ConclusionsOral health emerged as a modifiable risk factor for sarcopenia in vulnerable older adults, with chewing impairment, systemic inflammation, and liver dysfunction partly explaining this association. These findings support integrating oral healthcare into sarcopenia prevention strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal interpretations remain limited, and the identified mediation analyses should be viewed as associative rather than causal. Further longitudinal investigations are warranted to confirm these mechanisms and to evaluate whether oral health interventions can effectively reduce sarcopenia risk.Trial registrationChiCTR1800018895.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07059-y.Keywords: Aging, Sarcopenia, Oral diseases, Mediating effect, Public health

Background

The aging population presents a significant global public health challenge. As of 2021, individuals aged >60 years increased to 11.6% of the global population and were estimated to reach 20.1% by 2050 [1]. Alarmingly, two-thirds of them were estimated to live in low-income regions by 2050 [1, 2], experiencing considerable healthcare inequalities. Sarcopenia, an age-related syndrome defined by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength [3], significantly impairs physical function and quality of life, increases the risk of chronic diseases and mortality. Studies have revealed that sarcopenia patients demonstrated a 50% increased risk of falls [4], a 150% increased risk of all-cause mortality following emergency laparotomy [5], and a 30% higher prevalence rate of mild cognitive impairment [6], compared to their non-sarcopenic counterparts. The prevalence of sarcopenia was estimated to range from 10% to 16% [5], with the rate peaking at 27.9% in low-income regions [7]. Consequently, sarcopenia places a significant economic burden on society and individuals, with sarcopenia-related disability costs estimated at USD 257.1 million for Iran’s population aged 60 and above [8]. Thus, early and effective prevention and management of sarcopenia are of paramount importance.

To facilitate the early identification of older people with sarcopenia or those at risk, the concept of “possible sarcopenia” was introduced by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) in 2019, which was diagnosed by low muscle strength or poor physical performance [9]. Research has suggested that possible sarcopenia can be a reliable indicator of definitive sarcopenia [10], allowing for the identification of older people with, or at risk for, sarcopenia at an early stage, even in the absence of advanced diagnostic equipment.

Indeed, a bidirectional relationship appears to exist between accelerated biological aging and oral diseases, potentially mediated by nutritional deficiencies and shared inflammatory pathways [11, 12]. First, oral diseases may affect food selection and nutrient intake by reducing chewing ability, contributing to malnutrition [13]. In turn, malnutrition, especially inadequate intake of protein and vitamin D, is known to increase the risk of sarcopenia through loss of muscle mass and strength [14]. Chewing ability is critical for nutrient absorption and may play an important role in linking oral diseases and sarcopenia. Recently, functional tooth loss (FTL) has been proposed as a comprehensive indicator of oral function, encompassing not only natural tooth loss but also nonfunctional elements such as residual roots, third molars, and removable dentures [15]. FTL has been confirmed to better indicate the oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in older people and may suggest the sign of sarcopenia, as a study has demonstrated the potential association between sarcopenia and OHRQoL in older dental clinic patients [15, 16].

Beyond the oral dysfunctional pathways, chronic oral diseases such as periodontitis are well-documented contributors to systemic low-grade inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α [17–20]. These inflammatory mediators may disrupt systemic metabolic processes, promote muscle proteolysis, and hinder muscle regeneration, all mechanisms potentially contributing to sarcopenia [17, 21–24]. In addition, long-standing oral infections and the associated inflammation may also induce systemic dysfunction, including impaired protein synthesis and disturbed metabolic homeostasis [25–27]. These processes may reduce production of albumin and insulin-like growth factor-1, which are crucial for maintaining muscle mass and strength [21, 27, 28]. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest a plausible model through which oral diseases may influence sarcopenia risk, involving (1) impaired chewing function, (2) systemic inflammation, and (3) systemic dysfunction pathways. However, the exact interplay among these mechanisms remains incompletely understood, underscoring the need for further mechanistic investigation.

Understanding the oral–sarcopenia nexus and its potential mediators can provide general practitioners as well as clinicians the chance to target and potentially hinder sarcopenia in its early “subclinical” stage and intervene to delay or mitigate aging-related muscle loss. We therefore propose this tripartite pathway as the conceptual basis for the oral–sarcopenia nexus (Fig. 1). This convergence may create a particularly high-risk context for muscle wasting, especially among older adults in low-income settings experiencing delayed oral care. Thus, the present study aimed to investigate the oral–sarcopenia nexus and its mediators among older people in Maoxian, a low-income region of China.

Methods

This study was performed according to STROBE guidelines.

Study design and data collection

Data for this study were obtained from the 2021–2022 follow-up of the West China Health and Aging Trend (WCHAT) study (No. ChiCTR1800018895), a longitudinal cohort initiated in 2018 to annually observe general health outcomes among local participants aged ≥ 50 [29]. In July 2021, a pilot oral health survey was conducted in Maoxian, a low-income and high-altitude (1580 m) area, of this cohort, which subsequently became part of the annual follow-ups starting in 2021, with the 2021–2022 follow-up considered the baseline oral health survey. The ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Approval No. WCHSIRB-D-2021-259).

Eligibility criteria for the current study included: residency in Maoxian for at least 36 months; age ≥ 60 years; provision of informed consent by participants or their legal guardians; and completion of both the oral health questionnaire and detailed oral examination. Exclusion criteria included individuals with severe systemic conditions and those who declined to participate or failed to complete the essential oral questionnaires or oral examination. For the analysis of the current study, data were extracted from structured questionnaires, clinical oral examinations, assessments of muscle strength (e.g., handgrip dynamometry), evaluations of physical performance (e.g., gait speed, timed-up-and-go test), and biochemical and hematological assessments of blood samples collected during the 2021–2022 follow-up of the WCHAT cohort. Among that, demographic information, self-reported chewing ability impairment, comorbidities, nutrition status, and physical activity, were collected by questionnaires, which were completed with researcher assistance when necessary. Oral health assessments were performed by calibrated and accredited dentists using mirrors and community periodontal index probes according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [32], with inter-examiner kappa scores >0.80, indicating excellent consistency. Muscle strength and physical performance tests, including handgrip strength and the 5-time chair stand test, were administered following the AWGS criteria 2019 [9]. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture for comprehensive biochemical and hematological analyses.

Outcome

Possible Sarcopenia

Participants were diagnosed with possible sarcopenia if they met at least one of the following criteria: (1) low muscle strength, defined as handgrip strength < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women; or (2) low physical performance, indicated by 5-time chair stand test ≥ 12 s, according to the AWGS criteria 2019. Each item was tested three times, and the averaged value was used for diagnosis.

Oral diseases

Dental caries

Dental caries were evaluated using the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) index and the Decayed and Filled Roots (DFRoot) index. Each tooth was classified as no decay experience, decayed (DT), extracted or missing (MT), or filled (FT). The DMFT score was calculated as the sum of DT, MT, and FT. Similarly, each root was classified as no decay experience, decayed (DRoot), or filled (FRoot), and the DFRoot score was calculated as the sum of DRoot and FRoot.

Periodontal status

Periodontal status was evaluated by the Community Periodontal Index. Index teeth in each sextant, as shown in Fig. 2, were examined. If no index tooth qualified for examination in a sextant, all remaining teeth in that sextant were assessed. Periodontal parameters, including gingival bleeding on probing (BOP), dental calculus (DC), periodontal pocket (PP), and attachment loss (AL), were recorded for all sextants. BOP was recorded as “0” =no bleeding vs. “1” =bleeding after probing. DC was scored as “0” =no calculus vs. “1” =calculus detected through a gentle tactile and visual examination. PP was classified as “0” =periodontal pocket depth ≤ 4 mm vs. “1” =periodontal pocket depth ≥ 4 mm. AL was recorded as “0” = attachment loss ≤ 4 mm vs. “1” = attachment loss ≥ 4 mm.

The index teeth were examined to evaluate the participants’ periodontal status. The upper and lower teeth were divided into three sextants each, distinguished by shadow and brightness. The index teeth in each sextant used to evaluate periodontal status were highlighted with red arrows

Remaining natural teeth

The number of remaining natural teeth (NRT) was categorized as “more natural teeth” (NRT ≥ 20) vs. “fewer natural teeth” (NRT < 20), in line with the WHO goal that retaining at least 20 natural teeth is essential for maintaining adequate oral function in older adults [33].

Functional tooth loss

FTL encompassed natural tooth loss and nonfunctional teeth (those remaining but with little oral function, e.g., residual roots, third molars). Based on previous OHRQoL evidence in older adults, a cut-off of 16 functional teeth lost was identified as the point at which oral health significantly affects quality of life [15]. Accordingly, the number of FTL (NFTL) was dichotomized as “lower FTL” (NFTL ≤ 16) and “higher FTL” (NFTL >16), enabling more nuanced assessment of oral ageing.

Mediating factors

Chewing ability impairment

The Chinese version of the 12-item Geriatric Oral Health Assessment Index evaluates the impacts of oral conditions on daily activities [15]. This study evaluated self-reported chewing ability impairment via the question: “How often do you experience difficulty biting or chewing food such as firm meat or apples?” with optional responses of “always”, “often”, “sometimes”, “seldom”, or “never”.

Blood Biomarkers

Blood cell counts and derived ratios

Blood samples were analyzed using an automated blood counter (Medonic CA620, Boule Medical AB, Stockholm, Sweden) to measure blood cells, including white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (NEUT), platelet (PLT), and lymphocyte (LY). Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) was defined as the ratio of NEUT count to LY count. Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) was defined as the ratio of PLT count to LY count. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated as follows: SII = P×N/L, where P, N, and L were the PLT, NEUT, and LY counts, respectively [34].

Liver Function Tests

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), AST/ALT ratio, total protein, albumin, globulin, albumin-to-globulin ratio (A/G), and total and direct bilirubin levels in serum were measured to evaluate liver function.

Confounders

The demographic and socioeconomic information (including gender, household type, health insurance, pension, education, and living arrangements) and health-related variables (including comorbidities, nutritional status, and physical activity) were considered as confounders of the study. Among that, demographic and socioeconomic information was collected by questionnaire according to the WCHAT study [29]. The household type was dichotomized as agricultural or non-agricultural family. Health insurance and pension were evaluated by asking participants, “Do you have health insurance/pension?” with the following response options: “Yes” or “Never”. The educational status was recorded as “Uneducated” (illiteracy) or “Educated” (primary school or higher). The living arrangements were measured as “Living alone” or “Living with family or mates”.

The Charlson Comorbidity Index is a weighted assessment tool that evaluates 17 comorbid conditions, with higher scores reflecting a greater comorbidity burden. In this study, self-reported diagnosed chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, arthritis, stroke, and tumor were interviewed [35]. The status of comorbidities was categorized as “Yes” (number of chronic diseases ≥ 1) vs. “No” (number of chronic diseases < 1). Nutrition status was evaluated by the Mini Nutrition Assessment-Short form with 0 to 7 indicating malnutrition status, 8 to 11 indicating malnutrition risk, and 12 to 14 indicating good nutrition status [36]. Self-reported physical activity was evaluated by a validated China Leisure Time Physical Activity Questionnaire, consisting of the frequency and duration of common activities in the last two weeks: walking, indoor housework, outdoor housework, dancing and other regular activities (such as strength exercises, swimming, cycling, jogging, or tai chi). Weekly energy expenditure was calculated by using the equivalents of tasks of each type of activity. Physical activity level was divided into two groups according to the 20th percentile of energy consumption per week, adjusting for sex. Moderate to high physical activity was defined as being ≥ the 20th percentile of energy consumption per week, and the others were defined as low physical activity [37].

Data analysis

Characteristics for participants were described by relative and absolute values for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. The differences between groups were tested by Chi-square test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with possible sarcopenia. All assumptions of logistic regression were examined, including potential collinearity among oral diseases, confounders, and mediators, and were found to be satisfied. The data were presented as crude and adjusted (adjusted for gender, household type, health insurance, pension, education, living arrangements, comorbidities, nutritional status, and physical activity) odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). As possible sarcopenia and oral diseases were both aging-related diseases, the stratified chi-square test was used for subgroup analyses by age. The bootstrap method was used to evaluate the mediating effects.

SPSS Statistics V.26.0 and Amos V.28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) were used for data analysis. The p values < 0.05 were considered to be significant. As the oral health questionnaire and oral examination were complete for all participants, missing data occurred only in other variables (e.g., biomarkers or physical performance measures) and were imputed using the mean or median values for the corresponding variables.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 532 participants were included, with a mean age of 69.14 ± 5.62 years; and 63.0% were female. Overall, 50.4% were identified as having possible sarcopenia. Those with possible sarcopenia tended to be older, and had lower educational attainment and pension income compared to those without possible sarcopenia (all p <.05). Differences were also observed in oral health status: a higher prevalence of possible sarcopenia was seen among participants with root caries or fillings, fewer than 20 natural teeth, or the loss of more than 16 functional teeth. These results indicated a potential association between oral diseases and possible sarcopenia. Further details were presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. Missing data accounted for approximately 3–8% of the sample and were primarily related to physical performance tests and blood markers.

Association between oral diseases and possible sarcopenia

Logistic regression analyses indicated a significant association between oral diseases and possible sarcopenia (Table 2). After adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic and health-related factors, participants with root caries or fillings showed significantly higher odds of possible sarcopenia, while those retaining at least 20 natural teeth were less likely to exhibit possible sarcopenia. Notably, the loss of more than 16 functional teeth was strongly linked to a high risk of possible sarcopenia. These results suggested that maintaining oral function might play an important role in preserving muscle strength and physical performance among older adults.

Subgroup analyses by age

Age-stratified analyses revealed that the association between tooth loss and sarcopenia was particularly pronounced in adults aged 70 years and older, while no significant association was observed among those aged 60–69 years (Table 3). This pattern suggested that longer exposure to oral functional impairment and oral-derived systemic inflammation in later life might intensify the risk of sarcopenia.

Mediation analyses of the association between oral diseases and possible sarcopenia

Mediation analyses suggested that chewing ability impairment, systemic inflammation, and liver function each partially mediated the relationship between oral health and possible sarcopenia (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, chewing ability impairment accounted for a substantial portion of the effects associated with root caries or fillings and tooth loss, while the systemic immune-inflammation index and AST/ALT ratio explained additional variance. Although these mediating effects were relatively modest in magnitude, they shed light on potential mechanisms and suggest promising intervention targets for mitigating oral health-related muscle decline.

Discussion

In order to enhance understanding of early screening and intervention for sarcopenia in the background of health inequality, this study investigated the oral–sarcopenia nexus and potential mediators between oral–sarcopenia in a low-income region. The study showed that over 90% of individuals aged ≥ 60 years in Maoxian had dental caries or periodontitis, and nearly half were diagnosed with possible sarcopenia. Participants with better oral health demonstrated superior muscle strength and physical performance. Specifically, older adults with root caries had a 46% higher risk of possible sarcopenia than those without, those with NRT ≥ 20 had a 53% lower risk, and those with NFTL > 16 had an 83% higher risk. Mediation analyses indicated that reduced chewing ability, systemic inflammatory reaction, and impaired liver function partially explain the oral–sarcopenia nexus.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the oral–sarcopenia nexus and its potential mediators in a low-income region, emphasizing the role of oral health measures in sarcopenia management. However, some limitations should be taken into consideration. One important issue is that, although we adjusted for multiple demographic and socioeconomic and health-related confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. The generalizability of our findings may be greater for older adults in low-income regions with comparable contexts (e.g., parts of Nepal, India, the Andes), where chronic disease patterns often share common environmental drivers like hypoxic stress [38], with potential implications for high-altitude medicine. Another concern is that chewing ability was self-reported, which may introduce recall bias and subjectivity. Even so, it remains a valuable tool for assessing perceived functional impacts in geriatric epidemiology [39, 40]. In future cohort follow-up, we have planned to implement objective masticatory assessments to validate these self-reported measures. Finally, the cross-sectional design using the 2021–2022 follow-up data of the WCHAT cohort precludes causal inference. Accordingly, the mediation analyses involving impaired chewing ability, chronic systemic inflammation, and hepatic dysfunction should be regarded as variance partitioning, rather than proof of causal pathways. Longitudinal follow-up of this cohort is planned to investigate causal sequences and mediatory mechanisms and to track changes in sarcopenia-related risk factors over time.

The prevalence of oral diseases and possible sarcopenia in Maoxian exceeded rates reported in other regions of China [30, 41], underscoring the compounded challenges faced by its residents. Maoxian’s low-income status could be reflected in limited healthcare access, insufficient resources, and pervasive health inequities [42]. High-altitude living there, combined with a characterized diet rich in salt, might exacerbate both periodontal bone and muscle loss [43–45]. Many residents reported relying on subsistence farming and having limited education, which might restrict their hygiene knowledge and preventive care practices [46] and lead to significant disparities. Older adults from non-agricultural families or those with pensions or higher levels of education exhibited markedly lower prevalence rates of possible sarcopenia. These individuals likely benefit from greater health awareness [47], better nutritional choices [48], and improved access to healthcare and recreational activities [49], which might aid in sarcopenia prevention and overall health [50]. These findings highlighted the double burden of environmental and socioeconomic challenges in marginalized populations, calling for targeted efforts to promote healthy diets and balanced nutrition while enhancing hygiene knowledge and behaviors.

After adjusting for confounders, DFRoot, NRT ≥ 20 and NFTL >16 were strongly related to possible sarcopenia. The prevalence of possible sarcopenia on NRT ≥ 20 and NFTL >16 also exhibited remarkable disparities among individuals aged ≥ 70 years. Dental caries was associated with appetite, chewing ability and oral health related quality of life [51, 52], which have been testified to be associated with sarcopenia [16, 53]. Reduced natural or functional teeth could significantly impair chewing ability and nutritional intake [15, 54], which are critical factors for muscle health [55]. Mediation analyses revealed that chewing ability impairment partially explained the association between DFRoot, NRT ≥ 20, NFTL >16, and possible sarcopenia, respectively. Removable partial dentures, often seen as a solution for chewing ability impairment, restore only 10–20% of chewing efficiency, which is insufficient for adequate nutrition [56]. Poorly designed or maintained dentures might further result in periodontitis, denture stomatitis, and even systemic diseases [57]. Our study also highlighted systemic inflammation and liver function as crucial mediators, reinforcing previous evidence that systemic inflammation mediated the link between oral diseases and systemic diseases [26] and providing new insights that oral diseases exacerbate systemic diseases by comorbid diseases. And oral treatment demonstrably lowers systemic inflammation [58]. Thus, oral interventions to preserve functional teeth and restore masticatory function are crucial for older adults.

Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging are regarded as the gold standards for evaluating muscle mass, but these methods are limited in primary care due to their high costs, low portability, and technical requirements [59]. Thus, innovative approaches are needed for the screening of the population with sarcopenia or at risk. Our study indicated that oral examinations in primary care could serve as practical and cost-effective screening tools for identifying the risks of both oral diseases and sarcopenia. It also provided the possibility of preventing sarcopenia by managing oral diseases and improving chewing ability. Integrating oral health management into primary healthcare strategies may reduce sarcopenia prevalence, decrease the incidence and severity of related adverse outcomes (e.g., falls, cognitive decline, mortality, and malignancies), and address health disparities among older adults in low-income regions.

The study linked poor oral health to elevated sarcopenia risk in underserved older, with chewing ability impairment, chronic inflammation, and hepatic dysfunction as key mediators. These findings suggest that integrating dental care into aging-related health initiatives could help prevent sarcopenia. Nevertheless, causality remains unresolved, and longitudinal tracking of our cohort is planned to clarify temporal sequences using lagged exposure models. Further studies should also assess whether oral interventions such as restoring masticatory function and reducing the local inflammatory burden can directly slow muscle loss. In addition, large-scale trials are warranted to explore oral interventions as public health strategies for sarcopenia prevention.

Conclusions

The findings revealed alarmingly high prevalence of dental caries, periodontitis, and possible sarcopenia among older people in Maoxian. Better oral health, as indicated by the absence of root caries and fillings, more natural tooth retention, and less functional tooth loss, was significantly associated with reduced sarcopenia risk, partly through pathways involving chewing ability impairment, systemic inflammation, and liver function. This underscores oral health’s vital role in sarcopenia prevention, particularly in resource-limited regions where health disparities compound vulnerability. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inference is limited; thus, the mediation findings should be regarded as explanatory rather than causal. Further large-scale longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these mechanisms and clarify the impact of oral health interventions on healthy aging.
