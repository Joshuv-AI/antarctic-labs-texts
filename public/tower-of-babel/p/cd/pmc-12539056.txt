Identification and prediction of isolated lateral meniscal lesions in the Chinese population using MRI-based anatomical parameters
Authors: Shuai Xu, Meifeng Lu, Sisi Zhang, Gaobing He, GuiHao Zheng, Yulong Ouyang, Guicai Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated a close association between knee joint anatomical features and lesions of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and meniscus. However, the anatomical risk factors specifically related to isolated lateral meniscus lesions remain inadequately investigated. The aims of this study were to identify anatomical risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions and present a predictive model.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using data from 142 Han Chinese patients, including 71 patients with isolated lateral meniscus lesions and 71 matched controls. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to measure the posterior tibial slope (PTS), posterior slope of the meniscus (PSM), medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), medial tibial depth (MTD), femoral condylar width (FCW), intercondylar notch width (NW), and the lateral angle at the distal end of the popliteus tendon groove (LATG). Demographic and anatomical characteristics were compared between the groups. Parameters showing significant differences were subjected to binary logistic regression to identify independent risk factors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the predictive performance and optimal cutoff values for each risk factor, and a nomogram was developed for model visualization. An additional cohort of 35 patients (17 with injury, and 18 controls) was used for external validation. The model performance was further assessed using ROC and calibration curves.ResultsDiscoid lateral meniscus (DLM), medial tibial slope (MTS), and LATG were independent risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions. ROC curve analysis revealed that LATG had the highest area under the curve (AUC) at 0.766 (95% CI 0.688–0.844), followed by MTS at 0.691 (95% CI 0.604–0.777) and DLM at 0.662 (95% CI 0.572–0.752). The combined model incorporating all three variables yielded an AUC of 0.860 (95% CI 0.799–0.922). Tenfold cross-validation revealed an average AUC of 0.860 (95% CI 0.798–0.922) and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.014 in the training set. External validation produced an AUC of 0.794 (95% CI 0.634–0.954) and an MAE of 0.015.ConclusionDLM, MTS, and LATG are independent anatomical risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions. The combination of the DLM, MTS, and LATG can effectively predict the risk of lesions. However, it should be noted that the ROC curve relies on the current retrospective cohort data, and caution is needed when the model is extrapolated to other populations for validation.

Introduction

The meniscus is a fibrocartilaginous structure within the knee joint that plays multiple physiological roles, including load transmission, joint stabilization, shock absorption, cartilage nutrition, and proprioception [1–3]. Due to its involvement in dynamic knee movement, the meniscus is particularly susceptible to lesions. In particular, the lateral meniscus exhibits distinct anatomical features and attachment patterns: its anterior horn attaches to the anterior intercondylar area of the tibia, positioned anterior to and under the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), whereas the posterior horn connects either via the Wrisberg ligament or directly to the posterior intercondylar area of the tibia. Combined with its unique vascular supply, these characteristics contribute to the distinct lesion mechanisms and incidence rates of the lateral meniscus compared with those of the medial meniscus [4, 5]. Retrospective studies have shown that isolated lateral meniscus lesions (those without concomitant damage to the ACL, medial meniscus, or other structures) account for approximately 17.9% of all meniscal lesions [6]. Notably, 63% of isolated lateral meniscus lesions occur in individuals under 20 years of age, with the incidence decreasing progressively with age [6–8]. Due to the limited intrinsic healing capacity of the meniscus, arthroscopic surgery has become a common treatment modality [9]. However, whether patients undergo meniscal suture or partial resection, they remain at risk of postoperative complications such as repair failure, meniscal retear, and progression of osteoarthritis [10]. Therefore, early identification of risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions and assessment of lesion risk are of significant clinical importance.

In recent years, with the advancement of imaging technology, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been increasingly used for the diagnosis of meniscal and ligament diseases [11–13]. Studies have shown that MRI has excellent diagnostic performance for the diagnosis of meniscal and ACL tears, making it the optimal screening method prior to therapeutic arthroscopy [14]. Concurrently, an increasing number of anatomical studies have demonstrated that variations in knee joint morphology play a critical role in the risk of meniscal lesions. Specific anatomical features, such as a steeper posterior tibial slope (PTS), a wider or shallower intercondylar notch, and a deeper medial tibial plateau, are all associated with an increased risk of meniscal lesions [15–19]. Among these features, an increased PTS has been shown to promote anterior tibial translation during weight-bearing activities, thereby altering the distribution of mechanical stress across the meniscus [15, 16]. A shallower medial tibial plateau may permit posterior displacement of the femoral condyles during knee flexion, thereby increasing relative motion at the tibiofemoral joint and contributing to meniscal strain [17, 18]. Additionally, a narrowed intercondylar notch and reduced tibial spine height have been confirmed to be associated with the risk and severity of meniscal lesions [18, 19]. MRI findings have further corroborated these associations, identifying an increased lateral femoral condyle ratio (LFCR), a steeper notch angle, and a more pronounced medial tibial slope (MTS) as independent risk factors for isolated meniscal tears [20].

However, most studies have focused on the association between a single anatomical parameter and meniscal lesions, with few providing comprehensive analyses incorporating multiple anatomical factors. Moreover, significant anatomical variations in knee joint structure exist among different ethnic populations, yet population-specific and tear type–specific (such as horizontal, radial, or bucket-handle tears) anatomical risk factor studies remain limited. To address these gaps, the present study retrospectively analyzed imaging data and arthroscopic findings from patients with isolated lateral meniscus lesions, integrating anatomical measurements with demographic characteristics. This work systematically explored the relationships between the anatomical features of the knee joint and the occurrence of isolated lateral meniscal lensions to provide a reliable anatomical basis for early clinical identification and disease risk assessment.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Ethics Approval No.: ISL20250591). This was a retrospective case-control study. Patients who presented to our institution with lateral meniscus lesions and underwent surgical treatment between June 2024 and December 2024 were included consecutively based on the order of admission. All patients were of Han Chinese ethnicity. All patients underwent 3.0T MRI and underwent arthroscopic confirmation of isolated lateral meniscal lesions. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) MRI showing a high signal intensity in the lateral meniscus (at least grade II), with no evidence of lesions to the medial meniscus or other ligaments; and (2) arthroscopic confirmation of lesions limited to the lateral meniscus. The exclusion criteria included (1) concomitant lesions such as ligament rupture, tibial plateau fracture, or medial meniscus tear; (2) a history of previous knee surgery or significant trauma; and (3) incomplete or missing knee MRI data. Demographic data including sex, age, height, weight, and affected side were collected from the electronic medical records. All anatomical parameters were measured based on knee MR images.

Anatomical measurements

During MR imaging,, patients were positioned supine with the knee in full extension. Axial, sagittal, and coronal images of the knee joint were obtained. Two experienced sports medicine physicians independently measured the following anatomical parameters using the hospital’s PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System, Shenzhen Gaokaiyue Trading Co., Ltd., Guangdong Province, China): posterior tibial slope (PTS), posterior slope of the meniscus (PSM), medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), medial tibial depth (MTD), femoral condylar width (FCW), intercondylar notch width (NW), and lateral angle of the popliteus tendon groove (LATG). To ensure consistency, when the interobserver measurement discrepancy exceeded 5%, a consensus was reached via a joint review of the original images and clarification of the standards for anatomical landmark localization. The presence of a discoid lateral meniscus (DLM) was also assessed. The diagnostic criteria for DLM were as follows: (1) meniscal width ≥ 15 mm on coronal images; (2) a ratio of meniscal width to tibial width ≥ 20%; (3) continuity of the anterior and posterior horns on more than three consecutive sagittal slices; and (4) a ratio of the sum of anterior and posterior horn widths to the maximum transverse diameter ≥ 75% (Fig. 1) [21].

Measurements of the medial tibial slope (MTS) and lateral tibial slope (LTS) were performed according to the sagittal MRI-based method proposed by Hudek et al. (Fig. 2A, B), which is independent of tibial length [20, 22]. Specifically, on the central sagittal slice showing the intercondylar eminence and the attachment of the posterior cruciate ligament, two circles were drawn within the anterior and posterior cortices of the proximal tibia. The line connecting the centers of these two circles defined the anatomical axis of the proximal tibia (Line C). Next, on the sagittal slice corresponding to the center of the medial tibial plateau, a tangent (Line D) was drawn along the subchondral bone surface from the anterior to the posterior cortical margin. Line C was copied onto this image, and the angle α between Line C and Line D was measured. The medial tibial slope (MTS) was calculated as 90° − α. The same method was applied for calculating the LTS.

Measurements of the PSM and MTD were performed according to the methods described by Yang et al. [20] and Li et al. [23] (Fig. 2C, D). The medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), femoral condylar width (FCW), and intercondylar notch width (NW) were measured based on the techniques described by Liu et al. and Domzalski et al. (Fig. 3) [23, 24]. To measure the minimum lateral angle at the distal popliteal tendon groove (Fig. 4), four coronal MR images were selected: two anterior and two posterior to the fibular head, where the distal groove was most distinct. On each slice, tangents were drawn along the lateral margins of the groove, and the angle θ formed between the two lines was defined as the distal lateral angle. Among the four measured slices, the smallest angle was recorded as the minimum distal lateral angle of the popliteus tendon groove.

Statistical analysis

The KolmogorovSmirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables such as age, BMI, PTS, and PSM. For variables with a normal distribution, independent-samples t tests were applied, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. For non-normally distributed variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used, and the data are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables such as sex, laterality, and the presence of DLM were analyzed using the chi-square test. Variables showing statistically significant differences were included in a binary logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The predictive performance of each risk factor was assessed by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index. A nomogram was generated to visualize the multivariable logistic regression model. To evaluate the generalizability of the model, we performed 10-fold cross-validation using the validate() function. Model calibration was assessed with 1000 bootstrap resamples via the calibrate() function, and calibration curves were plotted to illustrate the agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities. Results

A total of 142 participants were included in the training cohort and divided into two groups based on the presence of lesions: the lesion group (n = 71) and the control group (n = 71). In the lesion group, 21 patients were male and 50 were female, with a mean age of 41.99 ± 16.15 years and a mean BMI of 24.34 ± 3.78 kg/m2. In the control group, 35 patients were male and 36 were female, with a mean age of 40.08 ± 15.14 years and a mean BMI of 23.62 ± 3.53 kg/m2. The demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, lesion location, or BMI. However, there were significant differences in sex and the presence of DLM. In the lesion group, 70.4% of the patients were female, and 49.3% had a DLM, whereas in the control group, the corresponding figures were 50.7% and 16.9%, respectively.

As shown in Table 2, a comparison of anatomical features between the two groups revealed significant differences in the MTS, LTS, posterior slope of the lateral meniscus(LMS), LFCW, LATG, and NWI. However, no significant differences in the posterior slope of the medial meniscus(MMS), MTD, MPTA, FCW, MFCW, or NW were detected between the two groups. As illustrated in Fig. 5, variables with a p value < 0.05 were included in a binary logistic regression analysis. The analysis revealed three significant factors associated with isolated lateral meniscus lesions: DLM (odds ratio (OR) 0.17, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.06–0.48), MTS (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.10–1.59), and LATG (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.97). These results indicate that the presence of a DLM, an excessive MTS, and a smaller LATG are independent risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions (Fig. 6A, B).

The results of the ROC curve analysis (Fig. 6C) revealed that the area under the curve (AUC) for LATG was 0.766 (95% CI 0.688–0.844), that for MTS was 0.691 (95% CI 0.604–0.777), and that for DLM was 0.662 (95% CI 0.572–0.752). The combined AUC for all three variables was 0.860 (95% CI 0.7985–0.9220). The optimal cutoff values for LATG, MTS, and DLM were 87.74 (sensitivity: 61.97%, specificity: 83.1%), 5.465 (sensitivity: 81.69%, specificity: 50.70%), and 0.5 (sensitivity: 49.3%, specificity: 83.1%), respectively. A nomogram was subsequently constructed to integrate the three independent risk factors (DLM, MTS, and LATG). The nomogram provided a visual quantification of the risk for isolated lateral meniscus injury by mapping the values of these factors to corresponding scores and probabilities (Fig. 6D).

The internal validation results based on 10 fold cross-validation (Fig. 7A) revealed an average AUC of 0.860 (95% CI 0.798–0.922) for the model in the training set, indicating good discriminatory ability. After 1000 bootstrap resamples for calibration, the calibration curve (Fig. 7B) demonstrated a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.014, with the predicted probabilities highly consistent with the observed probabilities, reflecting the excellent internal calibration performance of the model. Additionally, we included data from 35 patients collected between March and June 2025 for external validation, comprising 17 patients in the injury group (mean age: 36.94 ± 14.72 years) and 18 in the control group (mean age: 34.22 ± 13.44 years), as shown in Table 3. The ROC curve for the external validation cohort (Fig. 7C) had an AUC of 0.794 (95% CI 0.634–0.954), indicating good discriminatory power. The calibration curve (Fig. 7D) further demonstrated that the MAE was 0.015, with the predicted probabilities closely matching the actual incidence rates. No significant systematic bias was observed, suggesting that the generalizability of the model is acceptable.

Discussion

This study primarily revealed significant correlations between DLM, MTS, and LATG and isolated lateral meniscus lesions. A DLM, an excessively steep MTS, and a smaller LATG were found to increase the risk of isolated lateral meniscus lesions. Among these, LATG exhibited the strongest predictive ability, with an AUC of 0.766 (95% CI 0.688–0.844). Combining these three factors substantially enhanced the predictive capacity, yielding an AUC of 0.860 (95% CI 0.7985–0.9220). Furthermore, the performance of the predictive model incorporating these three factors was excellent through both internal and external validation. The model developed in this study can predict the risk of isolated lateral meniscus lesions based on anatomical data, which is highly important for identifying high-risk patients and implementing early preventive measures.

In this study, DLM, MTS, and LATG were identified as predictive factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions. The DLM is the most common anatomical variation, and is especially prevalent in children and adolescents. The incidence of DLM is significantly higher in Asian populations than in Western populations, with a reported range of 10–17% [25, 26]. Numerous studies have indicated that the abnormal morphology, thickening, disorganized collagen fiber arrangement, and peripheral instability of the DLM make it more prone to tears than a normal meniscus [25–28]. A study involving 1,250 knee MR images revealed that 71% of patients with DLM had meniscal lesions, whereas 54% of patients with a normal meniscus had meniscal lesions (P = 0.01) [28]. Additionally, the incidence of isolated lateral meniscus lesions was significantly greater in the DLM group than in the control group (20% vs. 11%). In this study, 47 out of the 142 patients had DLM, and 35 of them experienced tears, for a tear rate of 74.5%. In contrast, of the 95 patients with normal menisci, 36 had tears, with a tear rate of only 37.9%. These findings align with those of previous research [28].

Several previous studies have highlighted the correlation between knee joint anatomical features and meniscal lesions. For meniscal tears associated with ACL ruptures, an increased PTS has been shown to increase the risk of lateral meniscus lesions [29]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that a steeper medial PTS increases the risk of medial meniscus lesions [30, 31]. The geometry of the tibial plateau directly influences the biomechanical forces transmitted between the tibia and femur. An increased posterior tibial slope results in anterior displacement of the tibia, which in turn increases the forces acting on the meniscus, increasing its susceptibility to lesions [32–34]. Bernholt et al. analyzed 206 primary cases of ACL rupture, 103 of which were accompanied by lateral meniscus posterior root tears (LPRTS), while the remaining 103 cases had no meniscal lesions. They reported that LPRTS was associated with a steeper LTS (9.1 vs. 7.0, P < 0.05) and a steeper MTS (7.0 vs. 6.0, P < 0.05) [35]. Similarly, Kolbe et al. reported that in patients with ACL injury, those who underwent LPRTS had significantly steeper LTS (8.0 ± 3.2 vs. 4.0 ± 2.0, P < 0.001) [36]. In this study, we found that both the MTS and the LTS were greater in the group with isolated lateral meniscus lesions, with values of 8.44 ± 3.89 vs. 5.96 ± 3.11 (P < 0.001) and 7.27 ± 4.92 vs. 4.74 ± 3.50 (P < 0.001), respectively. Furthermore, binary logistic regression analysis revealed that the MTS was an independent risk factor for isolated lateral meniscus lesions, with an AUC of 0.691 (95% CI 0.604–0.777) and an optimal cutoff value of 5.465. When the MTS is steeper, the shear forces in the knee joint increase, resulting in more strain being placed on the lateral meniscus. Additionally, insufficient knee stability leads to increased tension and torsion of the lateral meniscus, further increasing the risk of lesions. Several studies have also shown that asymmetry in the posterior tibial slope of the medial and lateral platforms can increase the risk of posterior lateral meniscal root tears [34, 35].

Several studies have shown that structural abnormalities such as a high femoral condylar ratio, a larger femoral medial posterior condyle radius (RMFPC), a smaller hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, a narrow intercondylar notch, a lower tibial spine, a greater MPTA, and a smaller medial tibial plateau depth are associated with an increased risk of meniscal lesions [36–40]. In this study, however, no significant differences were observed in the medial tibial plateau depth, MPTA, femoral condylar width, medial condylar width, or intercondylar notch width between the injury and control groups. This may be attributed to previous research primarily focusing on medial meniscus tears, whereas this study concentrated on isolated lateral meniscus lesions. The relationships between posterior meniscal horn slope and ACL and meniscal lesions have gained increasing attention in recent years. Zhu et al. demonstrated a close association between the posterior horn slope of the medial meniscus and ACL injury [41]. In this study, no significant difference was found in the posterior horn slope of the medial meniscus between the two groups, whereas the posterior horn slope of the lateral meniscus was significantly greater in the lesion group (2.73 ± 6.79 vs. 0.86 ± 3.57, P = 0.042). Additionally, measurements of femoral condylar width revealed significant differences between the injury and control groups for both the LFCW and NWI, with values of 24.91 ± 2.98 vs. 26.88 ± 3.18 (P < 0.001) and 0.28 ± 0.05 vs. 0.26 ± 0.03 (P = 0.013), respectively. However, another study revealed no significant difference in the NWI between meniscal lesion and control groups [20], which may be due to the inclusion of both isolated lateral and medial meniscal tears in their cohort.

The popliteal tendon groove and its associated structures, such as the popliteomeniscal fascicle and popliteal tendon, play crucial roles in the stability of the lateral meniscus. In clinical practice, patients with lateral meniscus injuries tend to have more acute LATG. However, no studies have previously reported an association between LATG and meniscal lesions. In this study, the LATG was measured by drawing tangents to the most prominent points of the popliteal tendon groove at two levels, anterior and posterior to the fibular head, on the coronal plane. The angle between the two tangents (θ) represents the LATG. The minimum value from four coronal slices was taken as the LATG. The results of this study revealed that the LATG was significantly smaller in patients with isolated lateral meniscus lesions (82.81 ± 18.58 vs. 98.77 ± 11.11, P < 0.001). Furthermore, LATG had the highest predictive ability, with an AUC of 0.766 (95% CI 0.688–0.844). A smaller LATG indicates a sharper angle, which may increase shear forces on the lateral meniscus. Additionally, when the meniscus bulges outward, the LATG can directly apply force to the meniscus, especially during knee flexion, extension, and rotational movements. These factors may explain why a smaller LATG could increase the likelihood of lateral meniscus lesions, although further clinical and biomechanical studies are needed to confirm these findings.

This study focused on isolated meniscal lesions and excluded the confounding effects of concomitant ACL injuries, providing a clearer understanding of the relationship between bone morphology and pure meniscal lesions. The limitations of previous studies that involved mixed patient populations were addressed. Moreover, this study highlights the advantages of multiparameter combined prediction, thus expanding the framework for constructing lesion prediction models. The results of this study can assist in assessing the risk of isolated lateral meniscus lesions and provide a reference for early diagnosis and health education. However, there are several limitations to this study. First, manual measurements in MR images inevitably introduce errors, which may affect the accuracy of the results. Second, subgroup analysis was not performed, and the influence of factors such as age, sex, and lesion location on the outcomes was not considered. Third, the retrospective case-control design limits the strength of causal inference, and the relatively small sample size restricts the interpretability of the findings. Fourth, biomechanical validation was lacking, and the specific mechanical mechanisms through which anatomical features influence isolated lateral meniscus lesions remain unclear. Fifth, both the training and validation datasets were derived from patients who visited our hospital, which may not fully represent the characteristics of a broader population and could limit the generalizability of the model. Sixth, this study did not systematically include data from patients’ contralateral knees, precluding self-control to rule out potential impacts of individual baseline anatomical differences (such as congenital bilateral knee asymmetry) on the results. Despite controlling confounding factors via strict inclusion/exclusion criteria, the lack of contralateral joint comparisons may reduce the precision of demonstrating associations between isolated lateral meniscal lesions and anatomical parameters. Future studies could prospectively collect bilateral data and use self-control or bilateral comparisons to enhance internal validity. Seventh, the ROC curve intuitively demonstrates the model’s ability to distinguish between “diseased” and “non-diseased” individuals under different thresholds by continuously adjusting the judgment threshold, with the true positive rate (sensitivity) as the vertical axis and the false-positive rate (1-specificity) as the horizontal axis. Notably, the “optimal cutoff value” derived from the ROC curve in this study has obvious limitations. First, this value is a post hoc result based on the known sample grouping, determined by maximizing the Youden index, and is not a preset objective standard. Second, its value is strictly dependent on the sample characteristics of this study (such as sample size and population baseline), with sample limitations, and cannot be directly generalized to other populations. Therefore, the cutoff values obtained in this study should be regarded only as hypothesis-generating tools whose value lies in providing verifiable “candidate thresholds” for subsequent studies rather than definitive conclusions directly applicable to clinical decision-making. The true significance and applicability of these cutoff values must be further verified in prospective studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse populations.

Conclusion

DLM, MTS, and LATG are independent anatomical risk factors for isolated lateral meniscus lesions. The combination of the DLM, MTS, and LATG can effectively predict the risk of lesions. However, it should be noted that the ROC curve relies on the current retrospective cohort data, and caution is needed when the model is extrapolated to other populations for validation.
