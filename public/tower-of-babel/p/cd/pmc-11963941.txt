Natural Course of Electrocardiographic Features in Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy and Their Relation to Ventricular Arrhythmic Events
Authors: Anneli Svensson, Henrik Kjaerulf Jensen, Machteld J Boonstra, Marianne Tétreault‐Langlois, Pia Dahlberg, Henning Bundgaard, Alex Hørby Christensen, Rebecca T Rylance, Jesper H Svendsen, Julia Cadrin‐Tourigny, Anneline S J M te Riele, Pyotr G Platonov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrocardiographic abnormalities are common in arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy and are included in the 2010 Task Force Criteria. Their time course, however, remains uncertain. In this retrospective observational study, we aimed to assess the long‐term evolution of electrocardiographic characteristics and their relation to ventricular arrhythmias.Methods and ResultsThree hundred fifty‐three patients with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy as per the 2010 Task Force Criteria with 6871 automatically processed 12‐lead digital ECGs were included. The relationship between the electrocardiographic parameters and the risk of ventricular arrhythmias was assessed at 10 years from the first ECG. Electrocardiographic parameters were compared between the first contact ECG, the ECG at diagnosis, and the most recent ECG. Median time between the first and the latest ECG was 6 [interquartile range, 1–14] years. Reductions of QRS voltage, R‐ and T‐wave amplitudes between the first, diagnostic, and the latest ECGs were observed across precordial and extremity leads. Mean QRS duration increased from 96 to 102 ms (P<0.001), terminal activation duration (V1) from 47 to 52 ms (P<0.001), and QTc from 419 to 432 ms (P<0.001). T‐wave inversions in leads V3 to V6 and aVF at first ECG were associated with ventricular arrhythmias (adjusted hazard ratio [HRadj][V3], 2.03 [95% CI, 1.23–3.34] and HRadj[aVF], 1.87 [95% CI, 1.13–3.08]).ConclusionsDepolarization and repolarization parameters evolved over time in patients with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, supporting the progressive nature of arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Electrocardiographic abnormalities may be detected before diagnosis and might, although not fulfilling the 2010 Task Force Criteria, be markers of early disease. T‐wave inversion in leads V3 or aVF before diagnosis was associated with ventricular arrhythmias during follow‐up.Keywords: arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, electrocardiography, longitudinal follow‐up, natural course, prognosis, ventricular arrhythmiaSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Cardiomyopathy

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) was recognized in the 1970s, with diagnostic criteria proposed in 1994 and revised in 2010 1 (revised Task Force Criteria of 2010 [TFC2010]). These criteria include findings on imaging, histology, electrocardiography, arrhythmia monitoring, and genetic/family history. Electrocardiography is indispensable in the clinical evaluation of ARVC, but the QRS‐T pattern may vary substantially 2 depending on the extent and localization of the disease, with electrocardiographic abnormalities most often progressing over time. 3 , 4 Absence of electrocardiographic changes fulfilling TFC2010 criteria does not, however, rule out structural changes. 5 Extension of T‐wave inversion to inferior leads 3 as well as low QRS voltages in the limb leads 6 (both not part of the TFC2010) are proposed to relate to involvement of the left ventricle, which is more common and appears at an earlier stage of disease development than initially believed. 7

It is crucial to identify individuals with high risk of ventricular arrhythmia (VA), to prevent sudden cardiac death (SCD) by prophylactic treatment with an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator. It has been hypothesized from experimental and clinical evidence that remodeling of the intercalated discs, connecting the cardiomyocytes, and myocyte death/inflammation account for arrhythmias in the early stages of the disease. In addition, in later stages, the replacement of myocytes in the right ventricle (RV) with fibrous or fibroadipose scar tissue leads to conduction delays and forms a substrate for re‐entrant arrhythmias. 8 This risk prediction, however, is complex, 9 , 10 , 11 and the predictive value of diverse parameters is debated.

Our aim for this study was therefore to assess the time course of electrocardiographic characteristics in patients with ARVC and to study early electrocardiographic signs and their relationship to arrhythmic outcomes, including all events occurring after the individual's first ECG was recorded.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. For this study, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) cohort reporting guidelines were used. 12

Patient Population

This retrospective observational study is based on a long‐term follow‐up cohort of 353 patients recruited from 8 tertiary care centers in Sweden (Linkoping, Lund, Gothenburg), Denmark (Aarhus, Copenhagen, Herlev‐Gentofte), the Netherlands (Utrecht), and Canada (Montreal). All patients had definite ARVC according to the TFC2010 and at least 1 ECG recorded in digital format with a raw data file available for analysis. All centers used local follow‐up routines. 13 , 14

ECG Analyses

All ECGs were retrieved from clinical databases (MUSE [GE Healthcare; all centers except Gothenburg] or EC Store [Cardiolex Medical; Gothenburg]), digitally processed applying 150 Hz low‐pass filter, and automatically analyzed using the Glasgow algorithm. 15 The algorithm uses fiducial points to calculate intervals, amplitudes of principal components of QRS‐T, and areas under electrocardiographic signal components. T‐wave morphology is automatically categorized as positive, negative, or biphasic.

Parts of the TFC2010 are depolarization characteristics such as terminal activation duration (TAD) in milliseconds, measured from the nadir of the S wave to the end of the QRS, including R prime, in leads V1 to V3 in the absence of the right bundle‐branch block [RBBB]), and the presence of an ε‐wave (EW) pattern (reproducible low‐amplitude signals between the end of the QRS complex to the onset of the T wave). For repolarization characteristics, T‐wave amplitude measured in microvolts was assessed in all leads; in case of biphasic T‐wave morphology, a composite T‐wave amplitude was calculated as an arithmetic sum of its positive and negative components. T‐wave inversion was defined as the presence of negative or biphasic T‐wave morphology.

Other parameters measured were overall QRS duration in any of the 12 leads (in milliseconds), and for all leads R‐wave amplitudes (in microvolts) and S‐wave amplitudes (in microvolts). In addition, the prevalence of an RBBB and left bundle‐branch block (LBBB) was assessed.

The presence of an EW pattern reported by investigators was further evaluated by the first author (A.S.), using 12‐lead ECG pdfs, for meeting the definition provided in the TFC2010 as reproducible low‐amplitude signals between the end of the QRS complex to the onset of the T wave. 1 In case of discrepancies, a third observer was invited (P.G.P.) for a final decision on classification.

ECG Availability

A total of 9752 digital ECGs from 353 patients were retrieved. After excluding 2881 ECGs due to heart rate <40 or >100 bpm, ventricular pacing, age at time of recording <15 years, or after heart transplantation, 6871 remaining ECGs were used for the analyses. Availability of electrocardiographic recordings varied in number (median, 10 [interquartile range {IQR}, 4–25] per individual) and over time in relation to time of established ARVC diagnosis with median 1 (IQR, 0–6) ECG before diagnosis and 6 (IQR, 2–16) ECGs during follow‐up. The longest time between the first and the latest ECGs for any patient was 31.7 years, with a median of 6.1 years (IQR, 1.2–13.5), and 125 patients had their first and latest ECG >10 years apart.

Control Group

To assess the age‐related ECG characteristics in patients with ARVC, a database of 18 562 anonymized digital ECGs obtained from an unselected sample of 7869 Swedish subjects living in the Scania region who were in contact with health care for any reason during the 12‐month period before the study database lock and initiation of the current analysis (58% men; median age, 41 years [IQR, 32‐52]) was used. The ECGs were exported from a regional clinical ECG archive and contained all ECGs ever recorded both in in‐hospital and outpatient settings since 1988 when the ECG database was introduced in the Scania region, thus matching the principles used for ECG collection in the ARVC group. None of the subjects from the control group had an ARVC diagnosis; however, other medical history is unknown, because the data export and use were allowed only in the anonymized manner. The ECGs were processed using the same exclusion criteria and signal‐processing methodology as in the ARVC cohort. The results of signal processing were used for plotting a reference line in Figures 1 through 4 and Figures S1 through S3.

Outcomes

Information on arrhythmic outcomes was retrieved from the registries. The first documented VA was defined as sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, appropriate implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator therapy (antitachycardia pacing or shock), SCD, or aborted SCD.

Statistical Analysis

All electrocardiographic parameters were calculated and graphically represented using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing, because this allows for modeling nonlinear relationships between electrocardiographic indices and time. To handle the varying number of ECGs at different times and patient‐to‐patient variability, we used a median measurement per patient per year for analysis.

The results are presented as graphs in 2 ways. First, the graphs were plotted in relation to age to illustrate the development of electrocardiographic characteristics over time, evaluate changes possibly due to aging, and visually compare patients with ARVC to the control group. Second, the graphs were plotted in relation to time of diagnosis (marked with a red line in the plots) to visualize changes during the subclinical phase (ie, before diagnosis), compared with changes during follow‐up.

Development of electrocardiographic indices over time was also assessed at 3 time points defined as the earliest available ECG, ECG at the time of diagnosis (±1 year from diagnosis date), and the latest available ECG. If the first or latest recorded ECG was <1 year apart from the diagnosis ECG, only the result from the diagnosis ECG was used. Paired comparisons of median values of electrocardiographic characteristics were performed if time between ECGs exceeded 6 months and was performed for the first ever recorded ECG, ECG at diagnosis (defined as the ECG registered closest in time to the date of diagnosis, ±1 year), and the latest ECG. Test for association of electrocardiographic characteristics with arrhythmic end point was performed in a subgroup of patients with at least 1 ECG taken before or within 1 year after definite ARVC diagnosis date. Comparisons were made using medians for each period and calculated with Wilcoxon matched pair signed rank test for continuous parameters, and with related‐samples McNemar change test for nominal parameters. To correct P values for multiple comparisons we used the Bonferroni correction.

Using Kaplan‐Meier curve analyses and Cox regression analyses, the relationship between the clinical characteristics and electrocardiography indices and the risk of VA during follow‐up was assessed. Patients were censored at the end of follow‐up, death (other than SCD), or heart transplantation. Cox regression analysis was performed for depolarization and repolarization indices univariably, and adjusted for age, sex, and proband status.

A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Plots were made in the statistical environment R version 4.1.2. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed with SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

A total of 353 patients with a definite ARVC diagnosis were included in the study. Median age at diagnosis in the cohort was 42 years (IQR, 29–53), 65% were men, 67% were probands, and 57% were carriers of a pathogenic/likely pathogenic genetic variant. During follow‐up, 16 patients underwent heart transplantation and 24 patients died (6 SCD, 6 deaths by cardiovascular causes, 5 noncardiovascular, and 7 by unknown causes). Death without prior arrhythmic event was uncommon and occurred in only 4 of 232 patients included in survival analysis.

ECG Analysis Results

At the time of first ECG recording, 34% (n=120) of patients did not have any of the TFC2010 electrocardiographic criteria fulfilled. Of them, 81% (n=97) developed any of the TFC2010 electrocardiographic criteria during follow‐up. The length of follow‐up did not differ between patients with any diagnostic criteria positive at first ECG compared with those with a nondiagnostic first ECG (7.3±0.7 versus 7.9±0.5 years, P=0.970). Likewise, there was no difference in the time from the first ECG to the diagnosis between patients with and without diagnostic criteria present at the first ECG (6.3±5.3 versus 7.1±6.4 years, P=0.626). Median values for the studied electrocardiographic parameters and comparisons between the 3 points in time (first at diagnosis and the latest ECG) are presented in the Table S1.

Depolarization

Global QRS duration (Figure 1) demonstrated a continuous increase over time (96 ms at initial ECG versus 102 ms at latest ECG, P<0.001), corresponding to 5 ms per decade. The QRS duration increased both when assessed in relation to age (left panel) and with time point for diagnosis (right panel). It was consistently separated from controls, being broader in patients with ARVC than in controls, already at an early age and further separating after 50 years of age.

TAD in leads V1 through V3 demonstrated gradual increase over time (47 ms at initial ECG versus 52 ms at latest ECG in lead V1, P<0.001, Figure 2), starting before time of diagnosis and continuing afterward, visually separating from controls after 40 years of age when presented in relation to age (left panel). The increase in lead V1 was 4 ms per decade.

At the time of diagnosis, 7 patients demonstrated an EW. In 2 of these who also had an ECG registered at least 1 year before diagnosis, none showed an EW before the diagnosis. At the latest ECG taken >1 year after the diagnosis, 2 patients had a similar ECG pattern, whereas 2 did present with depolarization abnormality but not fulfilling EW definition. In an additional 4 patients, an EW first appeared >1 year after the diagnosis but was not present at diagnosis.

RBBB was observed in 18 patients (5%) at first ECG, in 14 (6%) at diagnostic ECG, and 34 (10%) at the latest ECG after diagnosis. The increase was significant both from first to diagnostic ECG (P < 0.001) and from diagnostic to the latest (P<0.001).

LBBB was present in 43 ECGs from 10 patients. LBBB was not observed in any patient before diagnosis, was present at diagnosis in 3 patients (0.8%), and was observed in 7 (3%) patients during follow‐up. The increase in LBBB prevalence was not significant either from first to diagnostic ECG (P=0.246) or from diagnostic to the latest ECG (P=0.340). In 6 of the 10 patients who demonstrate LBBB at some point in time, however, the latest available ECG did not demonstrate LBBB.

Repolarization

T‐wave amplitude demonstrated consistent decrease over time (Figures 4 and Figure S3) before and after diagnosis, indicating transition to T‐wave inversion (Figure 5). The decrease in lead V3 was −116 μV per decade and in lead V4 –86 μV per decade. The decline was steeper during the period from adolescence to 35 years of age when presented in relation to age (left panel), whereas the decrease was linear when presented in relation to time of diagnosis (right panel). The difference compared with controls was most pronounced in right‐sided precordial leads (Figure 4). For lateral precordial leads and inferior leads (Figure S3), the decline in amplitude demonstrated a pattern similar to controls, but the amplitude was consistently lower in the ARVC cohort. T‐wave inversion fulfilling major TFC2010 criteria at time of diagnosis was more frequent in index patients carrying a pathogenic genetic variant than in index patients with a normal genetic test (60% and 41%, respectively; P=0.003).

Early ECG Markers as Predictors of Risk of VA

Of the 237 patients who had an ECG before or within 1 year after diagnosis (median time before diagnosis 0.6 [IQR, 0–5.4] years), there were a total of 70 patients who experienced VA events during follow‐up. Neither TAD in the right precordial leads, R‐wave amplitude in any lead, nor S‐wave amplitude in any lead was associated with the risk of VA during follow‐up (data not shown).

The presence of T‐wave inversion in precordial leads V3, V4, V6 and limb lead aVF were all significant predictors of VA risk in an unadjusted analysis (Figure 6). T‐wave inversion in leads V3 and aVF were significant predictors of VA risk independently of age, sex, or the proband status: for lead V3 (adjusted hazard ratio [HRadj] 2.03 [95% CI, 1.227–3.343]; P=0.006), and lead aVF (HRadj, 1.87 [95% CI, 1.126–3.079]; P=0.016) (Figure 7) with modest negative predictive value for incident VA (64% for T‐wave inversion in lead V3 and 72% for lead aVF).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest longitudinal multicenter study of the natural course of electrocardiographic parameters in patients with definite ARVC diagnosis using computerized measurements and digital ECGs from regional electrocardiography databases.

As a first‐line screening method of individuals at risk, 12‐lead ECGs are easily accessible and noninvasive, and as demonstrated by Bosman et al, 16 electrocardiographic and arrhythmia criteria alone can be used to rule out ARVC diagnosis with high sensitivity. The value of different electrocardiographic parameters in risk stratification is not entirely clear, although and absence of TFC2010 electrocardiographic criteria does not rule out structural changes. 5 There is a need for further studies and perhaps other parameters for diagnosis and follow‐up. 17 , 18

T‐Wave Inversion in Precordial Leads

In children, T‐wave inversion in right precordial leads is a normal electrocardiographic finding, and the TFC2010 only applies to individuals >14 years of age. In athletes, T‐wave inversion is described to be normal in those who are <16 years of age. 19 During the early subclinical phase of ARVC development, T‐wave inversion might be the only sign raising suspicion of the disease, 8 keeping in mind that isolated finding of T‐wave inversion in V1 and V2 should be considered normal 6 (but still is a minor TFC2010 criterion) and also that the extent of T‐wave inversion may vary over time, from fulfilling major diagnostic criteria to minor or not at all. 4 , 20 Several previous studies have demonstrated progression of T‐wave inversion during follow‐up, extending beyond lead V3, 21 , 22 but also that its presence in precordial leads is associated with RV dilatation, 23 and that T‐wave inversion beyond lead V3 has been associated with more severely reduced left ventricular systolic function. 24 T‐wave inversion was demonstrated to be more frequent in index patients carrying a pathogenic genetic variant than in index patients without genetic findings, 25 and this was also confirmed in our study. T‐wave inversion fulfilling major TFC2010 criteria was associated with VA in a primary prevention cohort. 26 It is, according to a review from 2018, the only ECG criterion consistently demonstrated to be a predictive risk factor for VA 9 and is included in the digital risk factor calculator 10 , 11 (arvcrisk.com). In this present study, T‐wave inversion in lead V3 at the first ECG is associated with VA during follow‐up, corrected for proband status, age at ECG, and sex.

T‐Wave Inversion in Inferior Leads

T‐wave inversion in inferior leads is not included in the TFC2010, but studies have noted it to be present in approximately 1 out of 3 patients, in whom it was associated with extensive disease. 4 Patients with ARVC with signs of heart failure demonstrated T‐wave inversion in leads I and aVL and were more prone to development of LBBB during follow‐up. 24 In our study, the trend of lower T‐wave amplitudes over time and transition to T‐wave inversion was present also in inferior leads but to a lesser extent than in precordial leads. T‐wave inversion in lead aVF at first ECG (taken before or at time of diagnosis) is significantly associated with VA during follow‐up.

TAD, QRS Duration, QT Interval, and Bundle‐Branch Blocks

With progressive RV disease, changes in surface electrocardiography may appear, including widening of the QRS complex. The presence of QRS duration ≥110 ms in V1 through V3 varies in different ARVC series from 26% 27 to 75%. 28 Previous studies in non‐ARVC cohorts have demonstrated a constant QRS duration after 20 to 25 years of age. 29 , 30

Delayed RV activation/conduction may lead to positive TAD (ie, ≥55 ms), which is a minor TFC2010 criterion for ARVC diagnosis. 6 Earlier studies have demonstrated an increased incidence of TAD during follow‐up 31 as well as the possibility of dynamic changes. 20 In our study, TAD in lead V1 demonstrated gradual increase over time starting several years before diagnosis and continuing thereafter, but TAD in the right precordial leads was not associated with the risk of VA during follow‐up.

According to our data, the duration from onset of QRS to nadir of S wave (ie, the part of QRS duration that is not TAD) did demonstrate significant change (Table S1) in V1 from first to latest ECG and from diagnosis ECG to latest ECG. Hence, prolonged TAD may account for the increased QRS duration, but it may also reflect substrate development. The increasing number of RBBBs during the study period may be difficult to interpret correctly, because disease progression may lead to increased fibrosis and impaired cardiomyocyte contact leading to a wider QRS. Patients with ARVC, however, tend to have wider and more atypical RBBBs, indicative of loss of cardiomyocyte integrity. Development of LBBB is not common in ARVC, and the number was low in our study, as expected. However, none of the historical ECGs recorded >1 year before diagnosis had an LBBB.

ε Wave

Earlier studies have demonstrated the EW to be both a constant finding when diagnosed 4 and that its presence may vary. 20 , 22 This major depolarization criterion, according to the TFC2010, is however a subject of debate, because its definition is not free from subjective interpretation 32 and the highly variable reported prevalence in different ARVC registries. 15 It has also been reported in other conditions affecting the RV myocardium. 32 Caution is therefore advocated when evaluating the presence of an EW if no other diagnostic criteria are fulfilled. 6 , 15 EWs have been associated with extensive disease and poor prognosis, 21 but these findings are not consistent, and the EW was not included in the risk calculator (arvcrisk.com) presented in 2019. 10 , 11

In our present study, EW appeared to be a rarely observed electrocardiographic phenomenon that evolved over time. In some patients with a pattern assessed as an EW at the first and/or diagnostic ECG, it was altered during and did not meet the TFC2010 definition criteria at the ECG recorded during follow‐up. Still, although low in number, the EW incidence seemed to increase with follow‐up and hence possibly with disease progression.

Electrocardiographic Amplitudes

Apart from the TFC2010 electrocardiographic criteria, other electrocardiographic parameters that the automatic analyses provide are different amplitudes. Decreasing T‐wave amplitudes are described above, as a first step toward T‐wave inversion. Decreasing QRS amplitudes have been proposed to be an effect of progressing fibrofatty conversion of RV myocardium as well as prolonged conduction in the RV, but also from a change in the electrical vectors when an enlarged RV causes the heart to slightly rotate. 23 A lower sum of R‐wave amplitudes in V1 to V3 than expected has been associated with RV enlargement 23 or both enlargement and dysfunction. 33

As an attempt to explore whether the progression pattern of electrocardiographic indices could be affected by the age at the symptom onset or mutation carrying status, we performed subgroup analyses but were not able to identify notable difference in the progression patterns neither between the genotype‐positive and genotype‐negative patients nor between patients dichotomized by the median age at first ECG (Table S2).

Limitations

Even with an established electrocardiographic algorithm, some electrocardiographic parameters may be difficult to assess, such as the QT interval in flat T waves. As mentioned above, anomalies in the terminal part of the QRS in right‐sided precordial leads due to substrate development may make measurements and evaluation of morphology criteria such as RBBB difficult. For this study, we believe that the high number of ECGs used for constructing graphs illustrating time‐dependent evolution of electrocardiographic parameters in ARVC compensate for these algorithm‐related limitations.

A factor that limits power and the certainty of conclusions in relating early findings in ECGs to ventricular arrhythmic events is that only 31% of the individuals (n=108) had an ECG registered >1 year before the date of diagnosis. The frequency of visits and ECG acquisition might have differed between the participating sites, especially during the period before establishing the ARVC diagnosis, after which annual check‐ups are routinely performed.

Because this study is registry based, detailed information is limited, and methods used for information gathering (such as panels used in genetic testing) are at the discretion of each participating center. Subjects in our study could have had multiple ECGs recorded over a long period of time, and we could not account for potential impact of medications, which might have affected electrocardiographic characteristics. We have not been able to account for historical ECGs that could be available in paper format. We have not observed notable difference in the progression patterns on the patients' countries of origin (data not shown), although subgroup analyses in smaller groups may be underpowered.

Processing of the control group ECGs was possible only in an anonymized format with only the age at ECG and sex information available. For that reason, the age‐related trends could only be presented graphically as reference lines on the illustrative graphs. Nevertheless, we believe that information from the control subjects represents a valuable hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmative addition to the analysis of electrocardiographic characteristics in patients with ARVC.

CONCLUSIONS

Marked and consistent changes are seen in almost all studied depolarization and repolarization parameters over 10 years preceding ARVC diagnosis and continuing afterward. These analyses confirm the progressive nature of ARVC with electrocardiographic substrate developing over the course of decades, as illustrated for 1 patient in Figure S4. The presence of T‐wave inversion in leads V3 and aVF was significantly associated with VA during follow‐up.

This electric ARVC phenotype might potentially be detectable already at a young age; however, additional studies, including application artificial intelligence methodology are needed to improve early diagnostics of the disease, possibly adding new parameters not included in the TFC2010. It also emphasizes the role of electrocardiography in routine follow‐up of patients with ARVC because it is noninvasive, low cost, and quick compared with imaging techniques.
