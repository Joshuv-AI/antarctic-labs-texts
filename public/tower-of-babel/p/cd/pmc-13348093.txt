Struggling to integrate artificial intelligence in prehospital emergency care in a developing country: exploration of the Iranian experts’ views based on qualitative content analysis
Authors: Marziye Hadian, Nader Tavakoli, Mohammadreza Jabbari Khanbebin, Mohsen Nouri, Aziz Rezapour, Tahereh Shafaght, Hojjat Farahmandnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe use of integrated artificial intelligence (AI) in prehospital emergency services can not only increase the quality of services, but also help save patients’ lives and improve treatment outcomes. Given the challenges in emergency medicine, this technology is recognized as an effective tool for increasing the efficiency and accuracy of medical services. The present study aims to determine the barriers to the use of this technology in the provision of Emergency Medical Services (EMS).MethodsThis qualitative conventional content analysis was conducted in Iran using purposive sampling. Data were collected through in-depth interviews with 38 participants, including prehospital managers, Emergency Operations Center (EOC) officers, faculty members, and Information Technology (IT) specialists, conducted between November 2024 and May 2025. Data analysis followed Graneheim and Lundman’s approach, and Lincoln and Guba’s criteria were applied to ensure trustworthiness.ResultsThe qualitative analysis of interviews identified seven main categories and nineteen sub-categories related to the barriers to implementing AI in Iran’s prehospital emergency services. These barriers fell into technical, human, legal, operational, data-related, managerial, and algorithmic domains. Key challenges included inadequate technological infrastructure, poor data quality and completeness, organizational resistance, lack of standardized protocols, legal ambiguities, and technical limitations of algorithms.ConclusionThe implementation of AI in Iran’s prehospital emergency systems is a complex and multifaceted process that requires structural reforms, targeted policymaking, and cross-sectoral collaboration at the national level. Success in this endeavor depends on strengthening technological capacity, enhancing professional digital literacy, establishing data-driven ethical regulations, and adopting an integrated approach to digital transformation in health system governance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-026-01302-1.

Introduction

Prevention and treatment of traumatic injuries are major public health problems in most countries of the world today. The annual costs of traumatic injuries in low- and middle-income countries are about $65 billion, which is more than the annual income of developed countries [1, 2]. The care, treatment, and rehabilitation of injured people impose high direct and indirect costs on society. The estimated costs include lost wages, medical costs, insurance costs, costs from motor vehicle accidents, and indirect costs related to job loss [3, 4]. It is estimated that the financial loss from traumatic brain injuries in the United States is about $100 billion annually [5]. In a 2014 study in Iran by Afandizadeh et al., which aimed to investigate the cost of vehicle damage, the authors reported that more than USD 5.36 million was lost to the country’s economy. This economic damage was more than 0.5% of the gross national product and more than the entire road construction and management budget of the country in that year [6]. The most important way to prevent deaths and reduce costs in traumatic patients is to provide quality pre-hospital care [7]. Although most pre-hospital deaths seem to be unavoidable, preventing even a very small proportion of deaths is very valuable from both a medical and economic perspective [8].

Literature review

In a study conducted by Montazeri, the annual average number of traffic-related deaths in Iran was reported to be 15,482 [9]. Although some of these fatalities could potentially be prevented through improvements in pre-hospital emergency care, a substantial share of cases classified as “unpreventable” stem from the high severity of the crashes, which significantly limits the possibility of effective life-saving intervention. In a similar study conducted in Montreal by Sampalis and colleagues, they found that 62% of the deaths among 360 patients with severe trauma treated by the emergency medical service were preventable [10]. In a study conducted by Papadopoulos and colleagues in an urban region of Greece, 82 trauma-related deaths were examined. The results showed that 4.9% of the deaths were classified as definitely preventable and 42.6% as possibly preventable. These findings highlight the importance of providing rapid and high-quality prehospital care [11]. A review of civilian helicopter programs in the United States suggests that the primary factor in reducing trauma mortality was not the speed of transport but rather the management of care by helicopter personnel or remote hospitals [12]. This is particularly true in situations where primary care is delivered rapidly and appropriately. Evidence shows that timely, life-saving interventions play a more critical role in improving trauma outcomes than transport speed alone [13]. Optimal management of the care and treatment of these patients can greatly prevent secondary injuries and ultimately reduce mortality and disability of these patients, and reduce their economic costs and social problems [14].

Main problem, necessity, and objectives of the study

AI, as a new technology, plays a vital role in providing pre-hospital emergency services. With its capabilities, this technology can help improve the quality of medical services and reduce response times in critical situations [15]. AI can help diagnose diseases and injuries quickly and accurately [16]. Machine learning algorithms can analyze medical data at high speed and accuracy, leading to reduced diagnosis time and faster initiation of treatment [17]. AI can improve the triage process of patients. By analyzing patient data, these systems can prioritize patients based on the severity of their conditions, leading to reduced waiting times and improved treatment outcomes [18]. AI plays an important and growing role in pre-hospital emergency care. This technology is particularly effective in diagnosing and managing crises, improving the efficiency of emergency services, and facilitating rapid decision-making to save patients’ lives [19]. Failure to use AI systems to optimize ambulance dispatch and resource management can increase response times to emergency calls. This can have irreparable consequences, especially in critical situations where time is of the essence [20].

AI can also help medical personnel make faster decisions by analyzing data and providing useful information. Failure to use this technology can lead to greater pressure on medical staff and a decrease in the quality of services [21]. Ultimately, failure to use AI in the pre-hospital emergency care system can have significant negative impacts on the quality of services, patient safety, and the efficiency of medical personnel [15]. However, despite all the above, this technology has not yet been implemented in Iran. This study aims to determine the barriers to using AI in providing pre-hospital emergency services in the Iranian health system.

Methods

Study design and setting, and participants

This qualitative study was conducted from November 2024 to May 2025. The conventional content analysis methodologies were applied in this qualitative study. To extract the main challenges and barriers of using AI in providing pre-hospital emergency services, this study was conducted through interviews with key informants, using purposive sampling with 38 health system experts. These participants included prehospital managers, EOC officers, faculty members, and health information technology specialists. The interviewees were selected from 10 provinces of Iran, including Tehran, Shiraz, Isfahan, Mashhad, Tabriz, Yazd, Bushehr, Golestan, Kerman, and Mazandaran. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, at which point the researcher judged that more interviews would not yield new information [22].

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were having 2 years of work experience in pre-hospital emergency services, minimal familiarity with AI, and willingness to participate in the interview. The lack of consent to participate in the study and the lack of understanding of organizational and management challenges were exclusion criteria.

Data collection instruments

To conduct this study, which was qualitative, in-depth interviews were used. At first, the interview started with open questions and continued with more specific questions, as shown in Table 1. Before starting each interview, the research team explained the importance of this study. Also, the research conditions were explained, and advice was sought on how to conduct interviews that would not endanger the health of the researchers or other participants. The interviews were recorded and lasted between 35 and 75 min. The time and place of the interview were determined by agreement between the researcher and the participants.

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. Depending on the geographical location and participants’ availability, interviews were conducted in person, by telephone, or via online video calls. Participants from provinces distant from the research team’s base were mainly interviewed remotely to ensure feasibility and maintain consistency in data collection. All interviewees were given the option to choose the interview mode that was most convenient for them. During the interviews, field notes were prepared to describe and understand the responses in detail.

Data analysis

The data obtained at this stage were analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s content analysis method (24). In this inductive approach, the researcher reviews the transcribed material several times to fully understand it. Then the units of meaning (words, sentences, or paragraphs) that answered the questions related to the challenges of using AI in pre-hospital emergency care were identified, reduced, and coded. Similar codes were grouped into subsets, which were then combined to form a category (manifest level). Each category derives from a set of related ideas, making the categories internally homogeneous yet externally heterogeneous. A key theme emerged: the interaction between the underlying meanings within the categories, which expresses the latent meaning. The interviews were conducted individually by M.H. and H.F. The data collection process was supervised by D.A.R. MAX QDA 16 software was used to perform coding.

Coding tree development

The coding tree was developed through a stepwise inductive approach. First, all initial codes were grouped at the semantic level. Codes reflecting similar operational, technical, human, managerial, or legal content were merged. For example, initial codes such as “unstable internet,” “low bandwidth,” and “weak communication infrastructure” were merged into the final code “poor network infrastructure.” Similarly, initial codes related to staff fear, distrust of AI, and stress about new technology were combined into the final code “resistance to technological change”. This hierarchical organization enabled the research team to maintain transparency and traceability from meaning units to final themes. Trustworthiness

For reliability and validity testing, this study used Lincoln and Guba’s recommendations [23]. Lincoln and Guba’s evaluation method for validating qualitative research is based on four axes: validity, verifiability, reproducibility, and provability. These criteria are widely used in the validation of qualitative research. For this reason, in internal studies, these two researchers are better known for their work on validity and reliability in qualitative research. To ensure reliability, four requirements of credibility, dependency, conformability, and transferability are required, according to this suggestion. The researchers spent 6 months interacting with data and the environment, constantly making observations and compiling field notes, to improve data reliability. Peer-check procedures were used to assess the data’s reliability. Monthly peer checks were conducted to ensure that the study team had a full discussion of the newly discovered data. Background information, the researchers’ interests in the respective topics, and document upkeep were used to assess data consistency. The context of the interviews, codes, and extracted categories were analyzed by the research team, as well as other experts and three professionals in the field of qualitative research. Using maximum variation sampling, the researchers collected a diverse range of comments, observations, and interpretations.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

There were 38 prehospital managers, EOC officers, faculty members, and information and data technology specialists in the study, with an average age of 38.5 ± 4.7 years and a range of 23–54 years. Their average work experience was 10.57 ± 4.3 years, and all of the participants had worked in EMS for more than 2 years, as shown in Table 2.

Main results

After multiple rounds of analyzing and summarizing the data and taking into consideration similarities and differences, 7 main categories and 19 subcategories were created based on the results of the data analysis, including (1) Technical and infrastructure challenges. (2) Human and cultural challenges, (3) Legal and ethical challenges, (4) Operational challenges, (5) Data-related challenges, (6) Management challenges, and (7) Technical challenges as shown in Table 3. The categories and sub-categories are described in the following sections.

Infrastructure challenges

Infrastructure challenges can significantly affect the efficiency and success of AI projects. Many AI models require processing large amounts of data in a short time, which necessitates advanced hardware such as GPUs (graphics processing units) or TPUs (tensor processing units). The cost of acquiring and maintaining this equipment can be challenging for organizations. After analyzing the interviews, the most common challenges raised by interviewees were technical and infrastructure challenges. Most interviewees stated that the lack of quality medical data, the lack of the necessary infrastructure for the country’s emergency departments to implement AI, the need for large investments to develop and implement AI, and the lack of necessary coordination between existing information systems all prevent the effective implementation and efficiency of using AI in providing pre-hospital emergency services. These challenges were divided into 4 categories: data quality, infrastructure, implementation cost, and system integration.

Data quality

Using inaccurate data can lead to misdiagnoses and, consequently, inappropriate treatment. According to the participants:

Human and cultural challenges

The use of AI in today’s world is accompanied by numerous ethical and cultural challenges that require careful attention and consideration. These challenges not only affect how AI systems operate but can also have profound effects on society and culture. According to most interviewees, one of the main challenges to adopting AI in emergency services is resistance among medical and emergency personnel to change. Many doctors and nurses may be accustomed to traditional methods and feel apprehensive about using new technologies. This resistance can stem from unfamiliarity with the technology, fear of job displacement, or a lack of trust in the accuracy of AI systems. The challenges raised in this dimension were divided into 3 categories: acceptance of change, training, and job status.

Acceptance of change

A change in working methods is always accompanied by resistance. Emergency personnel accustomed to traditional methods may resist adopting new technologies such as AI. This can be due to fear of failure when learning new systems or a lack of control over work processes. According to participants:

We face new challenges every day in our workplace, and the introduction of new technologies usually causes stress and anxiety in employees. As an emergency technician, I feel I can’t adapt quickly to a new system for recording patient information. This fear of not being able to use the technology correctly puts a lot of pressure on me. Legal and ethical challenges

The legal and ethical challenges associated with AI require serious attention from developers, policymakers, and society. Appropriate legal frameworks should be established to manage these challenges, and necessary measures should be taken to protect individual and social rights. International cooperation can also help establish common standards and reduce legal conflicts, enabling the full potential of AI to be realized without violating individuals’ rights. Most interviewees stated that AI and their security may disclose patient data may be compromised. Legal accountability and the lack of clear, well-defined processes hinder the effective implementation and efficient use of AI in the provision of pre-hospital emergency services. This challenge was divided into 3 categories: data privacy, legal responsibility, and transparency and explainability.

Data privacy

The challenge of data privacy in the use of AI in pre-hospital emergency care is significant, given the need to access sensitive patient information and the associated legal and ethical complexities. For the successful implementation of AI in pre-hospital emergency care, it is necessary to strike the right balance between protecting patient privacy and utilizing new technologies. According to participants:

One of the biggest challenges is the collection and analysis of sensitive patient data. We need to collect accurate information from patients, but this information may contain personal and medical details that, if disclosed, could have serious consequences. (Interviewee No. 1)

Operational challenges

Operational challenges in the use of AI are the obstacles organizations and institutions face when implementing and using AI technologies. These challenges require careful planning, training of employees, and development of appropriate infrastructure for the effective use of AI technology. The findings of the present study showed that some reasons for not using AI in pre-hospital emergency services are operational challenges.

The interviewees stated that there is no defined standard for the use of this technology. On the other hand, access to advanced AI technology varies among individuals and groups across different places. They also stated that when a new technology enters the system, it takes a long time for people to get used to and accept that technology. The challenges found in this area are divided into three categories: global standards, resource distribution, and technology acceptance.

Global standards help set clear benchmarks for the design, development, and implementation of AI systems. Data related challenges

AI systems are highly dependent on accurate and clean data. If the input data is incorrect, incomplete, or irrelevant, the results of AI algorithms will also be incorrect, which can lead to poor decision-making. In some cases, there is insufficient data to train AI models. This shortage can be due to legal restrictions, privacy issues, or the lack of appropriate infrastructure for data collection. Without large and diverse data sets, it will be difficult to develop effective models. The findings of the present study showed that some reasons for not using AI in prehospital emergency services are related to data challenges. Interviewees in this study stated that the lack of data diversity and the lack of comprehensive and complete data may cause shortcomings in the use of this technology. The challenges in this area fall into two categories: data diversity and data quality.

Data diversity

The challenge of data diversity in the use of AI technology is a key issue that can profoundly impact the performance and accuracy of AI systems. If the training data lacks sufficient diversity, AI models may respond unfairly to certain groups of people or situations. According to participants:

Management challenges

Managing the use of AI technology in pre-hospital emergency services requires comprehensive, multifaceted approaches. Given the existing challenges, managers should focus on financial and human resource management, staff training, data quality assurance, and change management to realize this technology’s high potential and improve the quality of medical services. Some reasons for not using AI in pre-hospital emergency services stem from management challenges. Interviewees stated that the need for effective management during major changes and developments, as well as the need for proper management of financial and human resources to implement AI technology, are among the challenges of using this technology. The challenges encountered in this area are divided into two categories: data diversity and data quality.

Discussion

The present study aimed to identify the challenges of implementing AI in the provision of prehospital emergency services. Based on the findings of this study, seven categories and 19 subcategories related to the challenges of implementing AI in prehospital emergency care were extracted, which are discussed below:

Category one: infrastructure challenges

The findings of this study indicate that infrastructural challenges in Iran’s EMS extend not only to weak network connectivity and limited hardware but, more importantly, to a lack of data interoperability and incompatibility across existing systems. This observation is largely consistent with the findings of Ahmed et al. (2025), who identified weak digital infrastructure and high implementation costs as major barriers to AI adoption [24]. However, while their study focused primarily on bandwidth, hardware, and security limitations, participants in the present study emphasized the more fundamental issue of “misalignment between existing operational systems and the technical requirements of AI,” a challenge that is less frequently reported in health systems with stronger data standards. Furthermore, Rahimi et al. (2024) showed that the absence of sustained managerial mechanisms can render even available infrastructure ineffective, a finding that aligns well with the EMS experience in Iran, where many technology projects lack continuity and long-term support [25]. Therefore, although infrastructural weakness is a global barrier, in Iran, it is predominantly structural rather than merely equipment-related, highlighting the importance of nationwide system integration.

Category two: human and cultural challenges

The study revealed that concerns about loss of professional identity, resistance to new technologies, and insufficient digital literacy play a substantial role in limiting AI acceptance. These findings are closely aligned with Saadati et al. (2025), who reported that emergency physicians and technicians were concerned about the potential replacement of human decision-making by AI [26]. Sivaraman et al. also demonstrated that even when AI models are explainable, some clinicians intentionally disregard AI recommendations due to fears of reduced professional control or accountability, an attitude echoed in this study by concerns about diminished clinical autonomy [27]. Likewise, Barwise (2024) highlights mistrust in technology and the perception of “role ambiguity” as significant barriers [28]. A key distinction in the current study is that EMS personnel in Iran expressed far stronger fears of “losing professional identity” and “reduced professional status” compared to contexts with more modern educational frameworks. This heightened concern may stem from limited systematic training, minimal exposure to intelligent systems, and a more hierarchical clinical culture. Thus, while human–cultural resistance is widely documented globally, in Iran it not only appears more intense but is also more deeply intertwined with issues of identity and professional legitimacy.

Category three: legal & ethical challenges

The use of AI in prehospital emergency care faces numerous ethical and legal challenges that seem like a complex puzzle. One of the most important ethical challenges is privacy. This technology requires access to sensitive patient data, which must be carefully protected to prevent unlawful disclosure. This is like a red line that should not be crossed. In addition, informed patient consent is also an ethical challenge. Patients must be aware of how their data is used in AI systems and give their consent. This is an ethical challenge that must be carefully managed to maintain patient trust [29]. Justice and equality are also important challenges. AI must serve all patients fairly and without bias. If there are biases in the algorithms, this can lead to inequities in medical services and is like a time bomb that may explode. Finally, liability for errors or harm to the patient is also an ethical challenge [30]. Determining liability is an ethical challenge: does this responsibility lie with the developers, the doctors, or the AI systems? This question remains a puzzle that needs to be answered.

In terms of legal challenges, the laws and regulations governing the use of AI in medicine are complex, and there is a need to develop specific standards for these technologies. These laws are like a roadmap that must be followed carefully to avoid potential risks. Standardization is also an important challenge [31]. Currently, there is no standard framework for the development and use of AI tools in medicine. This can lead to uncertainty in the results of these systems and act as a dark cloud over medical services. Data protection is also one of the most important challenges. Data protection laws must be strictly observed to prevent the misuse of patient data. In a study conducted by Roppelt et al. in 2025 titled “Towards effective adoption of AI in talent acquisition: A mixed method study”, the findings showed that the adoption of AI can lead to potentially harmful actions, such as biased decision-making, data privacy violations, and reduced efficiency, which require preventive mitigation strategies [32].

In a study conducted by Cheng et al. in 2024 entitled “Implementation considerations for the adoption of AI in the emergency department”, the findings showed that the most common obstacle to implementing AI in the emergency department is model interpretability and explainability. More preliminary research should be conducted to examine the implementation of AI tools to help facilitate their successful clinical adoption in the emergency department [33]. In a study conducted by Chenais et al. in 2023 entitled “AI in Emergency Medicine: Viewpoint of Current Applications and Foreseeable Opportunities and Challenges”, the findings showed that AI-based applications in emergency medicine hold promise for the transformation of healthcare, but ethical and risk considerations must be addressed [20].

Category 4: operational challenges

The study found that current EMS workflows in Iran are not designed to support effective AI integration. Personnel face time pressure, system incompatibilities, lack of training protocols, and heavy workload, all of which greatly reduce the feasibility of using digital tools. Saadati (2025) similarly reported that misalignment between traditional workflows and smart technologies leads to operational resistance [26]. Rahimi et al. (2024) showed that many health systems still rely on offline data, limiting the deployment of real-time AI models, a situation also evident in Iranian EMS [25]. Roppelt et al. (2024) emphasized that without frontline involvement in redesigning workflows, AI tools often generate disruption rather than support [34]. The distinctive aspect of this study, however, lies in the intensity and combination of operational barriers in Iran. In many countries, the primary issue is “workflow mismatch,” whereas in Iran, this challenge coexists with staffing shortages, extreme workload, and a lack of continuous training. Therefore, operational barriers in Iran are not only present but multifactorial and more complex than in many international settings.

Category 5: data-related challenges

Medical data are like pieces of a puzzle that need to be connected to create a complete picture. However, this data is often scattered and stored in different formats. This inconsistency acts as a major obstacle to efficient information processing and creates information silos. To solve this challenge, there is a need for appropriate infrastructure for data integration. These infrastructures are like bridges that connect data and help maintain its integrity.

Access to quality data is also a major challenge. AI systems require large, high-quality data to function optimally. Collecting and analyzing high-quality medical data is like searching for gold in a mine that requires the right tools and sufficient experience [35]. This data is like a solid cornerstone that can create a strong foundation for AI systems. Maintaining data privacy and security is also a major challenge. Medical data is like a valuable treasure that needs to be protected. In the absence of an adequate security infrastructure, this data is at risk of disclosure, which can lead to data misuse [36].

Therefore, there is a need for advanced security technologies to protect data. These technologies act as a shield against potential risks, helping keep patient data safe. Incomplete or inaccurate data can also lead to erroneous results in AI systems. Therefore, there is a need for mechanisms to review and correct data. These mechanisms act as filters that can correct incomplete data and improve the accuracy of AI systems.

In a 2024 study by Sarno titled “AI in Pediatric Emergency Medicine: Applications, Challenges, and Future Perspectives,” the findings showed that barriers to widespread adoption of AI in pediatric emergency medicine include technological challenges, ethical issues, age-related differences in data interpretation, and the lack of comprehensive datasets [37].

Category six: management challenges

Management challenges in the use of AI in prehospital emergency care are among the obstacles preventing its effective use. At the heart of these challenges is the organizational structure. Existing organizational structures may not be compatible with the adoption of new technologies. This may be due to resistance to change or a lack of adequate infrastructure to integrate these technologies [38]. Therefore, there is a need for an organizational structure that is flexible and ready to adapt to change. This structure is like a bridge that can take the organization into the future and leverage the benefits of new technologies. Change management is also an important challenge. The use of AI in emergency care requires changes in work procedures and employee training. This change may meet employee resistance and requires effective change management. Change management is like a key that can open the door to employee acceptance and prepare them to use new technologies.

On the other hand, the use of AI in the emergency department requires allocating sufficient resources. These resources include budgets for equipment purchase and maintenance, a trained workforce, and appropriate infrastructure. In many organizations, the allocation of these resources is a major challenge. These resources are like a solid cornerstone, creating a strong foundation for the use of AI. In a study conducted by Biesheuvel in 2024 entitled " AI to advance acute and intensive care medicine”, the findings showed that AI in the emergency department can improve outcomes and simplify clinical workflows. However, widespread adoption is hindered by ethical, legal, organizational, and validation challenges [39].

Category seven: technical challenges

The use of AI in prehospital emergency care faces several technical challenges, including algorithmic accuracy, maintenance, and support. These challenges can directly impact service quality and patient safety.

At the heart of these challenges is data quality and integrity. AI algorithms require high-quality, consistent data to perform accurately. In the emergency department, medical data is often fragmented, incomplete, or recorded in non-standard formats [19]. This inconsistency can make it difficult to process information efficiently. Algorithmic bias is also a major challenge. Algorithms may be trained on biased historical data. Even if an algorithm performs well in a test environment, it may not be accurate enough in real emergencies (with a variety of patients and critical conditions). Some algorithms may overestimate patients’ risk or, conversely, underestimate it [17]. In a study conducted by Mueller et al. in 2022 titled " AI and machine learning in emergency medicine: a narrative review”, the findings showed that AI technologies in emergency medicine are promising, with the potential to increase integration, despite challenges in algorithm accuracy, trust, and patient data security [18].

Limitations

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the experiences and perspectives of health system experts from several provinces in Iran. However, to generalize these findings and apply them more broadly to the prehospital system in other developing countries, it is important to validate them through studies involving other health system populations. Conducting further research in different regions and contexts will enhance the robustness and generalizability of the results.

Practice and policy implications

According to the data obtained from the interviews, to implement AI in pre-hospital emergency care, we should seek solutions such as creating sustainable, secure, and scalable infrastructure that enables system integration and the processing of large volumes of data. On the other hand, international standards should be designed to ensure the quality, security, and transparency of AI and to determine legal responsibilities accurately. Also, to better implement this technology, continuous training courses, practical workshops, and culture-building should be implemented to reduce resistance and increase digital skills among the executive staff. By adopting these comprehensive and aligned solutions, health systems will be able to use AI as a transformative force to serve patients, improve the quality of medical care, and create a smart, sustainable, and humane future.

Conclusion

The implementation of AI in health systems, beyond a mere technological transformation, is a multifaceted challenge that manifests itself in seven key dimensions: technical infrastructure, human and cultural dimensions, legal and ethical frameworks, operational barriers, data quality and quantity, change and resource management, and technical complexities of algorithms and support. The findings of this study show that the real success of AI in health will be achieved only through an integrated, synergistic approach across these dimensions. In the infrastructural dimension, the creation of robust and cost-effective technological platforms is a critical prerequisite without which even the most advanced algorithms cannot achieve the desired efficiency. On the other hand, human and cultural acceptance of technology is key to opening the door to innovation, with continuous training and the management of occupational fears playing a decisive role.

In the legal and ethical realm, ensuring privacy and transparency of AI decisions is not only a legal requirement but also a fundamental pillar of public trust. Operational barriers, the lack of global standards, the uneven distribution of resources, and resistance to change highlight the challenges to the operationalization of these technologies. Data, the lifeblood of AI, must be optimized for diversity and adequacy to avoid biases and inefficiencies. Intelligent change management and resource allocation are the backbone of any successful implementation program, and ultimately, the accuracy of algorithms and ongoing technical support will ensure the security and sustainability of intelligent systems.
