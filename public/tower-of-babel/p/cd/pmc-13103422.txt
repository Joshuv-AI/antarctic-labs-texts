A practical ML framework for biomass torrefaction analysis and simulator deployment
Authors: Sunyong Park, Jiwook Yang, Sungyeol Kim, Kwang Cheol Oh, Kyojung Hwang, JaeJung Lee, Sunhwa Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Torrefaction is an effective thermochemical pretreatment for upgrading agricultural and forestry biomass into solid biofuels; however, selecting operating conditions remains challenging due to trade-offs between mass yield and energy densification. In this study, a comprehensive machine-learning framework was developed to predict biomass mass yield and higher heating value using an experimental dataset collected from diverse torrefaction studies. Multiple regression algorithms—including linear, ensemble, boosting, and kernel-based models—were systematically compared with and without hyperparameter tuning. Domain-informed feature engineering and rigorous data preprocessing were applied to enhance predictive reliability and interpretability. Among the evaluated models, tree-based ensemble and boosting algorithms, particularly CatBoost, showed robust performance and stable feature attribution for both MY and HHV. Rather than treating prediction accuracy as an endpoint, the models were integrated into a GUI-based simulator that enables simultaneous evaluation of MY and HHV under identical operating conditions, visualizes trade-offs, and identifies feasible operating windows without exhaustive trial-and-error. This approach demonstrates how ML can function as a practical engineering tool for torrefaction screening and condition selection, complementing traditional empirical methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40442-5.

Introduction

Due to ongoing environmental development and industrialization, environmental pollution has become increasingly severe. Furthermore, the UN Secretary-General recently stated that we have now entered an era not of global warming, but of global boiling1. As a result, global interest in and regulation of environmental pollution are increasing. In particular, many regulations and studies are underway to reduce the use of fossil resources. Biomass is receiving significant attention as an alternative to the use of fossil resources as fuel2. Biomass refers to biological organic matter such as plants that synthesize organic compounds using solar energy, as well as animals and microorganisms that feed on these plants3. In particular, biomass derived from agricultural and forestry residues has the advantage of being continuously produced throughout human agricultural and forestry activities3. However, such biomass also has several disadvantages, including high collection costs, high moisture and ash content, low bulk density, and low heating value4–6. Various studies have been conducted to overcome these drawbacks, one of which is the torrefaction process. Torrefaction is a thermochemical process carried out at temperatures between 200 and 300 °C for less than an hour under oxygen-free or extremely oxygen-lean conditions7–10. Through this thermochemical process, volatile components in the biomass are removed, the heating value increases, and hydrophobic properties are acquired. In various biomass conversion processes such as pyrolysis, torrefaction, and gasification, the yield and heating value of the final products serve as key criteria for process design, economic evaluation, and assessment of greenhouse gas reduction effects7,11–14. Traditionally, the prediction of these characteristics has relied heavily on experimental methods or simple linear regression equations. Park et al. (2025) developed a predictive model based on mass yield and enhancement ratios, demonstrating that changes in elemental composition, proximate analysis, and energy indices after torrefaction can be accurately estimated for various biomass types with high predictive power (R2 greater than 0.93)15. In a related approach, Chen et al. (2019) introduced the torrefaction severity factor, which integrates process temperature, time, and biomass type into a single parameter, showing that regression with this factor enables reliable prediction of weight loss, higher heating value, and energy yield16. Xu et al.17 further applied a two-stage quadratic regression model by dividing torrefaction into mild and severe temperature ranges, achieving mean absolute errors below 5% when predicting ultimate and proximate analysis as well as yield values.

In real biomass processing, experimental evaluation of fuel properties is time-consuming and often infeasible for rapid process adjustment, limiting the practical applicability of traditional empirical or single-model approaches. Recent advances in artificial intelligence demonstrate two complementary directions in biomass thermochemical research: real-time, fault-aware process monitoring and data-driven prediction of fuel properties. In particular, AI-driven, non-invasive real-time forecasting based on indirect sensing (e.g., thermographic imaging) has been shown to enable process-level prediction without intrusive measurements in biomass gasification systems18. In addition, ensemble-based virtual sensing approaches have demonstrated the ability to accurately infer producer-gas O2 concentrations and detect air leakage under semi-industrial conditions, enabling robust monitoring and control19.

In parallel, machine learning (ML) techniques have been increasingly applied to predict fuel properties in biomass thermal conversion processes. Zhu et al.20 reported that the higher heating value (HHV) of torrefied biomass can be accurately predicted using only solid yield as input, achieving R2 values up to 0.97. Liu et al.21 developed a genetic algorithm–optimized back propagation neural network (GA-BPNN) capable of simultaneously predicting multiple fuel properties, including HHV, fuel ratio, H/C, O/C, mass yield, and energy yield, with R² values exceeding 0.91 across different feedstocks. Li et al.22 further demonstrated that neural network models can jointly predict biochar yield and composition from process and feedstock variables, enabling rapid optimization of biochar production systems.

From an operational and modelling perspective, nonlinear interactions inherent in complex biomass-conversion datasets are effectively captured by ensemble, boosting, and automated model-selection approaches23–25. Nevertheless, despite these methodological advances, few studies have systematically compared diverse ML algorithms using experimental biomass datasets while quantitatively assessing the combined effects of feature engineering and hyperparameter tuning on both prediction accuracy and interpretability. In this study, we address this gap by comprehensively evaluating multiple ML regression models for predicting mass yield (MY) and HHV using experimental data, incorporating domain-informed feature construction and rigorous hyperparameter optimization to assess their practical applicability for data-driven biomass modelling.

Materials and methods

Data and experimental conditions

The total 797 data used in this study were obtained from previous research and consist of actual measured values from torrefaction processes of agricultural and forestry biomass26–73. All source code used in this study is provided in the Supplementary Information. The main input variables include temperature (Temp), reaction time (Time), elemental analysis (C, H, N, O, S, H/C, O/C), and proximate analysis (VM, FC, Ash, FR). Derived variables were additionally applied based on domain knowledge and the correlation structure among variables, including N/C, Ash/VM, Temp×Time, Temp×H/C, Temp×O/C, log(VM), and log(FC). Data pretreatment

In this study, outlier detection and removal were conducted as essential steps in data pre-processing to minimize the negative impact of extreme values (outliers) on the predictive performance and interpretability of machine learning regression models. If outliers are not removed, the model may fail to learn the representative patterns of the data distribution, or prediction results may be distorted76,77. Outlier detection was performed using both the Interquartile Range (IQR) and Z-score methods to ensure robust identification of anomalous values. The IQR method defines outliers as data points outside the range [Q1 − 1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR], where Q1 and Q3 are the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively78. To complement this, the Z-score method was applied to account for potential symmetric distributions, flagging data points with absolute Z-scores exceeding a set threshold (typically |Z| > 3)79,80. The Z-score is calculated by subtracting the mean (µ) from each data point (x) and dividing the result by the standard deviation (σ). In this study, any data point with an absolute Z-score greater than 3, meaning it is more than three standard deviations away from the mean, was defined as an outlier. If a data point was identified as an outlier by either the IQR method or the Z-score method, it was treated as an outlier, and the corresponding row was removed from both the training and evaluation datasets.

Model development and evaluation

For the comparison of machine learning models, a total of nine regression algorithms were applied. In addition to traditional linear regression (MLR) and Lasso, tree-based methods (Random Forest, ExtraTrees), boosting algorithms (XGBoost, CatBoost, GradientBoostingRegressor, HistGradientBoostingRegressor), and the kernel-based SVM (rbf) were included. Tree models use hierarchical splits based on feature importance to model both linear and nonlinear relationships with high interpretability81,82. Boosting models improve prediction accuracy and robustness by sequentially combining multiple weak learners, typically decision trees83,84. SVM identifies the optimal hyperplane for classification or regression, and with kernels such as RBF, it can model nonlinear patterns but offers lower interpretability than tree-based models85,86. Each model was trained in two versions: one using default hyperparameters and the other using hyperparameter tuning based on GridSearchCV with three-fold cross-validation87. The hyperparameter search ranges included representative variables known to affect actual predictive performance, such as the number of trees (n_estimators), maximum depth (max_depth), minimum samples per leaf (min_samples_leaf), learning rate (learning_rate), and kernel parameters (C, gamma, epsilon). The tuned hyperparameters are summarized in Table S1. For MLR, only the default model was used since no hyperparameter tuning is available.

All models were trained on the training set, hyperparameters were optimized using the validation set, and final evaluations were performed on the test set. Variable importance and interpretation

For tree-based and boosting models, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were calculated to quantitatively evaluate the key variables contributing to the prediction of MY and HHV89,90. The SHAP beeswarm and summary plots were used to visually interpret both the magnitude and direction (positive or negative) of each variable’s influence. In contrast, for linear regression (MLR) and Lasso models, SHAP-based interpretation is not directly applicable91. Therefore, feature importance was assessed by directly extracting and analysing the regression coefficients for each input variable. In both MLR and Lasso regression, the absolute value of each coefficient was interpreted as an indicator of the variable’s contribution to the prediction. Since Lasso regression also performs variable selection, some coefficients shrink to zero. For the purpose of interpretation, only the top 10 variables with the largest absolute nonzero coefficients were visualized and discussed. Accordingly, for MLR and Lasso regression models, feature influence was compared based on the top 10 coefficients ranked by absolute value.

Data visualization and diagnostics

The distribution, skewness, variance, and presence of outliers in the data were analysed using violin plots for each input variable92,93. Outliers detected by the IQR and Z-score methods were visually highlighted in scatter plots and other visualizations. The relationship between predicted and actual values was presented using scatter plots with a 1:1 reference line and 95% confidence intervals.

Results and discussion

Dataset and variable analysis

To comprehensively characterize the input and target variables in the dataset used for this study, representative visualization techniques including violin plots, outlier plots, and correlation matrix heatmaps were employed in parallel with statistical analysis. In this process, the distribution of each variable, the presence of outliers, and the degree of linear correlation between variables were systematically examined, as these factors can directly influence the quality of data-driven modelling.

Distribution of input variables (Violin Plot)

The violin plots presented in Fig. 1 simultaneously show the overall distribution, density, median, and interquartile range for each input variable (Temp, Time, C, H, N, S, O, VM, FC, and Ash). For Temp and Time, most data points are clustered within the middle range; however, Time exhibits a long tail at certain values, suggesting that experimental conditions were concentrated within specific intervals. This skew is considered to be a result of the process conditions of torrefaction.

Regarding elemental analysis, carbon and hydrogen generally display distributions close to normal, whereas nitrogen and sulphur exhibit asymmetric (long-tailed) distributions with many extreme values. This reflects the characteristics of biomass, which contains high amounts of carbon and hydrogen but relatively low amounts of nitrogen and sulphur. In proximate analysis, the spread of volatiles, fixed carbon, and ash differs, with some variables showing significant skewness, as indicated by substantial differences between the median and the mean. Notably, volatiles and fixed carbon display opposite tendencies: biomass typically contains high volatile matter and low fixed carbon, resulting in a tendency for volatile matter to be distributed toward higher values and fixed carbon toward lower values. As torrefaction progresses, volatile matter decreases and fixed carbon increases, but the extent of these changes varies depending on the process conditions, leading to the observed distributions.

Detection and distribution of outliers

The outlier scatter plots in Fig. S1 illustrate the distribution of detected outliers (marked as red dots) and normal values for each input variable, based on the IQR and Z-score criteria. In particular, a large number of outliers were concentrated in the variables S, N, and Ash, indicating the need to reconsider the measurement reliability or experimental specificity of these variables. For sulphur and nitrogen, the high number of outliers was mainly due to elevated contents observed in certain biomass types, such as coffee waste. Overall, outliers accounted for approximately 3–10% of the entire dataset. Since including outliers in the machine learning process can negatively affect model generalization and lead to coefficient distortion, these data points were removed during the preprocessing stage.

Correlation analysis between input and target variables

Figure 2 presents the Pearson correlation coefficients to intuitively visualize the linear relationships both among input variables and between input variables and the targets (MY, HHV). Carbon and fixed carbon showed a strong positive correlation (0.69), as did volatile matter and oxygen (0.65), and volatile matter and hydrogen (0.57). This can be explained by the torrefaction process, during which devolatilization leads to decreases in oxygen and hydrogen content, while carbon and fixed carbon increase. As a result of these process dynamics, strong negative correlations were observed between carbon and oxygen (– 0.88), and between volatile matter and fixed carbon (– 0.94). For MY, there were strong negative correlations with temperature, carbon, and fixed carbon (all below − 0.7), and strong positive correlations with oxygen (0.69) and volatile matter (0.71). For HHV, strong positive correlations were observed with carbon (0.81) and fixed carbon (0.62), but the correlation with temperature was relatively lower (0.56). This may be due to the fact that, depending on the biomass type, increases in temperature do not always lead to significant increases in heating value, and the inherent properties of different biomass types also play a role. Negative correlations were observed for oxygen (– 0.69) and volatile matter (– 0.51) with HHV.

Prediction results and performance comparison for biomass mass yield

Linear regression models (MLR, Lasso)

Figure S2 is a list of all the regression values for the MLR and Lasso models. In the MLR model, VM was found to have the most significant positive impact on MY. It was followed by log_VM, H/C, O/C_sqrt, C, and H_sqrt, all of which had positive coefficients. This shows that higher values of these factors are linked to higher mass yield, which is in line with the idea that under mild torrefaction conditions, biomass with higher volatile matter or elemental ratios tends to have higher yield. VM_sqrt, H, VM_sq, and H/C_sqrt, on the other hand, all had large negative coefficients, which means that higher values of these variables lead to smaller mass yields. The fact that similar variables have both positive and negative coefficients (for example, VM vs. VM_sqrt/VM_sq) shows that the relationships might not be linear and that mathematical changes can have an effect.

In the Lasso regression model, the values for H/C, O_sq, N, and S_sqrt were the most positively skewed, indicating a positive link with MY. On the other hand, O/C_sq, H_sq, Temp, N_C, Temp_Time, and FC_sq all had negative ratios, which means they had a negative impact on mass yield. These findings show that both the original variables and the derived variables make a big difference in MY prediction. What’s more, their effect can change direction and size based on how the Lasso model is regularised and what features are chosen.

In Fig. 3, you can see the difference between the predicted and actual mass yield numbers for both models. The training set for the MLR model had an R2 of 0.8206 and an RMSE of 7.2965. With an R2 of 0.5849 and an RMSE of 11.5814, the validation set had worse results than the test set. The test set had an R2 of 0.6581 and an RMSE of 9.8405. The results show a good fit for the training data, but a big drop in accuracy for the validation and test sets. This is because the model is too good at fitting the data, and it can’t be used in other situations. It had an R2 of 0.7442 and an RMSE of 8.7118 for the training set of Lasso regression. It had R2 values of 0.7835 and 0.7495 for the validation set and RMSEs of 8.3639 and 8.4230 for the test set.

The Lasso model was better at predicting the future than the MLR across all splits. It had less overfitting and a slightly higher accuracy on the test set. Overall, though, neither model was very good at predicting what would happen. This is probably because they are linear, which means they can’t fully capture complex relationships, feature interactions, or the effect of outliers in the MY dataset.

Ensemble-based models: random forest and extratrees

This picture (Figure S3) shows the main points of the SHAP study. The H/C group of variables (H/C_sq, H/C_sqrt, and H/C) were always ranked as the most important features in all models, such as ExtraTrees, Random Forest, and their tuned versions. The H/C ratio of biomass is a key factor for predicting the torrefaction mass yield, as shown by this finding. The SHAP value distributions show that as H/C_sq and H/C_sqrt go up, so does the expected yield. The original H/C ratio and its derived variables have a big impact on the model output on their own. Also, temperature was always one of the most important factors. The plots make it very clear that higher temperature values are linked to a lower yield, as shown by the negative SHAP values. Important factors were found to be FC and C, as well as their offspring like FC_sq, C_sq, and C_sqrt. This shows how important both compositional and process-related factors are for figuring out yield. When FC and C percentage changed, along with their nonlinear changes, they had a big effect on yield. This means that these factors might be able to be used as controls to change the results of an experiment.

In the Random Forest model, Temp_Time was also a key feature, showing that the interaction between process conditions further influences yield determination. Even after hyperparameter optimization, the main variable rankings and SHAP value patterns remained nearly the same for all models. In both ExtraTrees and Random Forest, the most important features—the H/C group, Temp, FC, C, and Temp_Time—remained dominant before and after tuning, and the SHAP distributions hardly changed.

These results show strong consistency and robustness in variable interpretation across tree-based ensemble models. This suggests that the underlying characteristics of the dataset and the selection of input variables are the most important factors in determining feature importance. No meaningful differences were observed according to model type or tuning. Instead, the quality and diversity of experimental data and input variables have the greatest effect on interpretive reliability.

The predicted results are illustrated in Fig. 4. For the ExtraTrees model, the training set achieved an R2 of 0.9991 and an RMSE of 0.5129, indicating a near-perfect fit that strongly suggests overfitting. However, the model also maintained strong generalization, with the validation set showing an R2 of 0.8891 and an RMSE of 5.9865, and the test set showing an R2 of 0.8332 and an RMSE of 6.8731.

The Random Forest model also exhibited excellent predictive performance, with an R2 of 0.9764 and an RMSE of 2.6443 on the training data. The validation and test sets achieved R2 values of 0.8601 and 0.8355, and RMSE values of 6.7229 and 6.8253, respectively. While the difference in RMSE between models was significant for the training set, their performance on the validation and test sets was very similar, both in terms of R2 and RMSE. In the predicted versus actual scatter plots, nearly all data points were closely aligned along the 1:1 line and fell within the 95% confidence interval. Except for a few isolated outliers, the deviations between the predicted and observed values were minimal. Considering the experimental errors, measurement deviations, and inherent sample variability in the dataset, these results confirm a high level of predictive reliability. This indicates that both models effectively captured the experimental variability present in the data.

Boosting-based models: CatBoost, GTB, HGBR, and XGBoost

igure S4 shows a summary of the SHAP analysis results for all boosting and ensemble models, along with their tuned forms. The H/C ratio, which shows how many hydrogen-rich volatiles there are, had a good effect on yield. This means that material with a higher H/C content breaks down more slowly during torrefaction. Higher temperatures, on the other hand, had a strong negative SHAP value. This is because thermal degradation at high temps causes volatile loss and less solid recovery. Variables like C, FC, Temp_Time, and O/C were also constantly among the top contributors across all models. This shows how important both compositional and process condition features are for predicting yield. Different variables that are tied to H/C, like H/C_sq, H/C_sqrt, and log_FC, had a big effect on the CatBoost and GTB models. Certain factors, like H, S, N, FR, and Ash_VM, were also important in XGBoost and HGBR. The general patterns of feature importance didn’t change much after the hyperparameters were tuned. In all of the models, the main variables that were changed were H/C, Temp, C, FC, Temp_Time, and other variables that were linked. This high level of consistency in SHAP-based interpretation across boosting and ensemble models shows that the structure of the experimental data and the choice of input variables are the main factors that affect mass yield prediction, no matter the type of model or parameter optimisation.

Figure S5 and Table 1 present the prediction performance of the base boosting and ensemble models. All four models—CatBoost, GTB, HGBR, and XGBoost—showed strong predictive accuracy and robustness. CatBoost achieved a high level of fit for both the training and test sets, with a test R2 of 0.8584 and RMSE of 6.3334, while validation performance was similarly high. GTB and HGBR also maintained stable performance, with test R2 values of 0.8305 and 0.8404, and RMSE values of 6.9295 and 6.7227, respectively, showing little drop-off from training to test data and suggesting minimal overfitting. Although XGBoost achieved the best fit on the training set (R2 0.9991), its generalization to the test set (R2 0.7945, RMSE 7.6295) was slightly lower than the other models. Despite these differences, all models placed the vast majority of predictions tightly along the 1:1 line and within the 95% confidence interval in the scatter plots. Only a few points deviated as outliers, indicating that the models captured the main experimental trends while remaining robust to noise.

Taken together, these results confirm that boosting and ensemble approaches can reliably model mass yield with high accuracy, generalize well to unseen data, and provide stable performance without significant overfitting. This level of predictive reliability makes them suitable for practical applications in biomass process optimization and quality control.

SVM models

SVM-based regression models, particularly those employing the nonlinear radial basis function (RBF) kernel, were used to predict MY. While SVM is known for its strong nonlinear classification and regression performance, it has a fundamental limitation in interpretability when using nonlinear kernels such as RBF. Unlike linear regression, Lasso, or tree-based ensemble models, SVM with an RBF kernel does not provide explicit coefficients for each input variable, and model-based feature importance methods like SHAP are not directly applicable. This limitation arises from the complex high-dimensional mapping inherent to the RBF kernel in the input feature space94–96.

The predictive performance of the SVM with RBF kernel is presented in Fig. 6. For the baseline SVM_rbf model, performance was poor across all data splits, with an R2 of 0.3673 for training, 0.4034 for validation, and 0.3594 for test data. The corresponding RMSE values were all above 13, indicating that the model failed to capture the underlying relationships in the data. The scatter plot shows that the predictions cluster in a narrow band and do not align well with the 1:1 line, resulting in limited explanatory power and a wide spread of residuals. After hyperparameter tuning, as shown in Fig. 6(b), the SVM_rbf_Tuned model showed noticeable improvement. R2 increased to 0.6304 for the training set, 0.6690 for validation, and 0.6112 for test data, with RMSE values reduced to approximately 10.5 across all splits. The predicted values became more closely aligned with the 1:1 line, and the spread of residuals decreased, but the performance still lagged behind tree-based ensemble models such as CatBoost, XGBoost, and HGBR.

Overall, although SVM with an RBF kernel can achieve moderate predictive accuracy when carefully tuned, it remains less effective for experimental data-driven tasks that require high interpretability and insight into variable contributions. For practical applications where process optimization and scenario analysis are important, SVM should be complemented with more interpretable models to provide robust predictions and clear variable impact analysis.

Linear regression models (MLR, Lasso)

Figure S7 shows the important factors and their regression coefficients for predicting HHV with the MLR and Lasso models. The variables with the biggest positive coefficients in the MLR model were VM_sqrt, C, and VM. This means that higher values of these variables are linked to higher HHV. The other hand, C_sqrt, H/C_sqrt, FC_sqrt, O/C, and O/C_sqrt all had big negative coefficients, which means that raising these numbers might lower HHV. It’s worth mentioning that both the original variables (like C and VM) and their derived forms (like VM_sqrt and C_sqrt) were among the most important factors. This shows that HHV forecast is based on nonlinear relationships. In the Lasso regression model, C was the most important variable, with the biggest positive coefficient. N_sqrt, O/C_sqrt, Temp, and C_sqrt all had positive coefficients after it. On the other hand, O/C_sq and N_C had negative coefficients, which means that when these numbers go up, they hurt HHV. Overall, both models showed that variables related to carbon were the main factors that affected HHV. On the other hand, variables related to oxygen level and its derivatives had a negative effect.

Figure 7 summarizes the predictive performance of the linear regression models. For MLR, the model achieved an R2 of 0.8206 and an RMSE of 7.2965 on the training set, but performance dropped substantially on the validation and test sets, with R2 values of 0.5849 and 0.6581, and RMSE values of 11.5814 and 9.8405, respectively. The scatter plot shows that the predictions for the validation and test data were widely dispersed and deviated considerably from the 1:1 line, indicating underfitting and limited ability to capture the underlying complexity of the data. In comparison, the Lasso regression model showed slightly better generalization, with a test set R2 of 0.7495 and RMSE of 8.4230. Both the validation and test sets displayed more clustered predictions than MLR, but a substantial number of points still deviated from the reference line. This moderate improvement can be attributed to the regularization effect of Lasso, which selects key features and reduces overfitting to noise. Nevertheless, compared to ensemble and boosting models, the predictive accuracy and ability to capture nonlinear interactions remained limited for both linear approaches. Overall, both models demonstrated restricted explanatory power and did not sufficiently model the complex relationships present in the experimental data.

Ensemble-based models (Random Forest, ExtraTrees)

The SHAP study results for the Random Forest and ExtraTrees models, as well as their tuned versions, can be seen in Figure S8. The most important factors for predicting HHV were always carbon-related variables, especially C, C squared (C_sq), and C square root (C_sqrt). These variables had the highest SHAP values, which means they had a mostly good effect on HHV prediction. Compositional and derived factors like O/C, O/C square root, N square root, and temperature were also important in the ExtraTrees model. Along with carbon variables, the Random Forest models also showed that Temp_O_C, Temp_H_C, Ash_VM, O_sqrt, and Temp_Time were some of the most important traits. It’s important to note that the general feature importance rankings and the SHAP value distributions didn’t change much before or after the hyperparameter tuning. This shows that feature interpretation in tree-based ensemble models is stable and reliable. It also shows that these models accurately show the relationships between key factors and HHV in experimental datasets.

Both models demonstrated consistently high predictive performance, with test set R2 values around 0.71–0.72 and RMSE values near 1.42 across all cases. Model tuning resulted in only minimal changes, confirming that the initial model structure was already well-optimized for this dataset. In the scatter plots, predicted values were tightly clustered along the 1:1 reference line and fell within the 95% confidence interval for nearly all data points, indicating strong agreement between predictions and actual outcomes. These results highlight the reliability and robustness of both Random Forest and ExtraTrees for regression tasks, as well as their stability against overfitting or noise in the data.

Boosting models (CatBoost, GTB, HGBR, XGBoost)

Figure S9 shows a summary of the SHAP study of the boosting models (CatBoost, GTB, HGBR, and XGBoost). Before and after hyperparameter tuning, C and its derived features (C_sqrt, C_sq) were always the most important factors for predicting HHV across all models. Also, variables like O/C, Temp, FC, and N, as well as relationship terms like Temp_H_C, N_C, and Ash_VM, were consistently ranked as some of the most important factors. However, the size of the SHAP values showed that carbon-related variables typically had a bigger effect than other factors. These SHAP values were mostly going in the right direction for carbon variables, which shows that more carbon directly raises HHV. On the other hand, the effects of process variables and interactions were more situational and sometimes went both ways. The stable rankings of feature importance and SHAP value distributions before and after hyperparameter optimisation show that boosting models are reliable in choosing variables and figuring out what they mean, even when they are tuned in a certain way. This is important for real-world use because it shows how reliable and easy to understand these models are even when the factors are changed to get the best results.

In terms of predictive accuracy (Figure S10 and Table 3), CatBoost achieved the highest performance on the test set, with R2 values approaching 0.80 and RMSE values as low as 1.09. The remaining boosting models—GTB, HGBR, and XGBoost—also exhibited strong predictive capabilities, with only minor differences between models after hyperparameter tuning. For all models, validation and test set results were similar and consistently lower than training set metrics, reflecting a proper fit to unseen data and no major signs of overfitting. Although tuning helped reduce overfitting, the improvement in test set predictive accuracy was generally limited, confirming the already high baseline performance of these ensemble methods. Overall, these results demonstrate that carbon content and its derived variables play a central, reproducible role in biomass HHV prediction, while other compositional and process variables contribute as secondary factors. Among all boosting models, CatBoost provided the best balance of predictive accuracy, generalizability, and feature interpretability. The findings indicate that, for data-driven optimization of biomass conversion processes, the choice of a robust boosting model such as CatBoost, with a focus on carbon-related predictors, offers both high reliability and explanatory power.

SVM models

Figure 9 shows the prediction performance of the SVM_rbf model. Among all tested algorithms, SVM_rbf exhibited the lowest explanatory power. The R2 values for the train, validation, and test sets were 0.6520, 0.6205, and 0.5572, respectively, with RMSEs of 1.7128, 1.7915, and 1.7794. The scatter plots reveal that predicted values are widely dispersed from the 1:1 line, and many points fall outside the 95% confidence interval, highlighting substantial prediction errors. This indicates that, while the RBF kernel SVM can model some nonlinearities, it fails to adequately capture the complex, high-dimensional relationships present in the experimental dataset.

After hyperparameter tuning, the SVM_rbf_Tuned model showed moderate improvement: R2 values increased to 0.7463 (train), 0.6773 (validation), and 0.6171 (test), while RMSEs decreased to 1.4623, 1.6520, and 1.6547, respectively. The scatter plots became somewhat more concentrated along the 1:1 line, but prediction errors and the spread of outliers remained apparent, especially compared to the ensemble models. Overall, even with tuning, the SVM_rbf model could not achieve predictive performance or robustness comparable to tree-based ensemble models, reaffirming the practical limitations of SVM for this type of nonlinear regression task.

Selection of the optimal model

In all tree-based models, SHAP analysis showed that compositional factors, such as C, C_sqrt, C_sq, O, FC, H/C, and O/C, were the most important for predicting MY and HHV. CatBoost and HGBR had the most stable and easy-to-understand importance ratings, even when hyperparameter tuning was used. Random Forest and ExtraTrees, on the other hand, showed similar trends but with more variation in how the variables were ranked. The SVM RBF model, on the other hand, was not good for feature interpretation because of the way its kernel was structured. When it came to precision and generalisation, CatBoost and HGBR always did the best. Their test set R2 values were usually between 0.85 and 0.88, and their RMSE values were the lowest. The results of the prediction scatter plots were very close to the 1:1 line, which shows that they were very reliable. Random Forest and ExtraTrees also gave stable results, but they weren’t able to improve models’ accuracy and generalisation much. It was clear that the SVM RBF model wasn’t very accurate or reliable; most of the test set R2 values were below 0.65. Tuning the hyperparameters didn’t have much of an impact; it did slightly reduce overfitting for all models, but it didn’t make the test results more accurate. The ranking of how important each variable was and the total performance of the model didn’t change much. The SHAP patterns and accuracy of CatBoost and HGBR were almost the same before and after they were tuned. This shows that their built-in design, not fine-tuning, is what makes them better at predicting biomass pyrolysis and making predictions. These results show that CatBoost is the best model for this task, both for making predictions and figuring out what features mean. The results also show that picking the right model structure is much more important than optimising hyperparameters when it comes to getting reliable results and using the model in real-life biomass process data. Although empirical correlations such as Dulong’s formula provide reasonable HHV estimates for raw biomass, their applicability becomes limited for torrefied materials due to non-linear compositional changes induced by thermal severity. In this study, the ML-based HHV model is not intended to replace thermochemical correlations, but rather to function as a consistent predictive component within the simulator, enabling simultaneous evaluation of mass yield and energy densification under identical operating conditions.

Implementation and utilization of a prediction simulator

A simple prediction simulator was included (Fig. 10) to make the machine learning-based biomass property prediction models created in this study more useful in real life. The Streamlit framework, which is based on Python, was used to build the simulator. It lets users change input values and see expected outputs in real time on the web. In the GUI, there are easy-to-use input fields for all the major factors needed for producing torrefied biomass and predicting its properties. Users can directly enter numbers for each variable, and these numbers can be set anywhere in the trial data range. The prediction process goes like this: after the user chooses the input values and clicks the “Predict” button, the chosen inputs are sent to a machine learning model that has already been trained on CatBoost, HGBR, XGBoost, or other optimised models developed in this study. In real time, the target properties (MY and HHV) are shown. The predicted results are shown right away at the bottom of the screen, so users can see how the properties of biomass change and how much they will produce under different situations. In this study, predictions are performed using the trained CatBoost model, identified as the optimal algorithm.

Simulator-based decision-support framework for practical operating condition selection

Building upon the prediction results presented in Sect. 3.5, this section demonstrates how the machine-learning–based predictions of MY and HHV are translated into a simulator-based decision-support framework for practical operating-condition selection. Rather than treating prediction accuracy as an endpoint, the proposed simulator reorganizes the same MY and HHV outputs under identical operating conditions to enable transparent evaluation of trade-offs between material efficiency and energy densification. In this way, the framework bridges data-driven prediction and operational decision-making, providing a structured basis for screening feasible torrefaction conditions under realistic and potentially noisy data environments. The minimum acceptable MY was set to 60%, as yields below this level indicate excessive solid loss that may compromise material efficiency and economic feasibility. In parallel, the target HHV was set to 23 MJ/kg, corresponding to the lower bound of typical commercial sub-bituminous to bituminous coal. This threshold represents an energy densification level at which torrefied biomass can reasonably substitute conventional solid fuels. Importantly, these criteria were selected as practical operational constraints rather than mathematically optimized objectives. Using the simulator predictions reported in Sect. 3.5, MY and HHV were evaluated across the investigated temperature–residence time domain. Figure 12 visualizes this information in the form of two-dimensional decision maps, where the background colour represents predicted HHV values and the temperature–time plane provides a direct representation of operating conditions. Regions satisfying the MY ≥ 60% criterion and those satisfying the HHV ≥ 23 MJ/kg criterion were first identified independently. The overlap of these regions was subsequently defined as the feasible operating window, indicating conditions under which sufficient mass retention and coal-comparable energy content are simultaneously achieved.

The feasible region highlighted in Fig. 12 should not be interpreted as a single optimal condition automatically generated by the model. Instead, it represents a set of candidate operating conditions that satisfy multiple user-defined constraints simultaneously. Within this region, different operating points may be selected depending on practical priorities, such as minimizing residence time, reducing thermal severity, or maximizing energy densification. In this sense, the simulator does not prescribe a fixed solution, but rather provides structured predictive information that supports transparent and flexible decision-making.

By explicitly linking predictive modelling results (Sect. 3.5) to a visual and quantitative decision-support framework (Fig. 12), the proposed approach bridges the gap between model prediction and practical process design. This framework enables informed condition selection without relying on exhaustive experimental trial-and-error or rigid optimization assumptions, highlighting the role of the simulator as an operational decision-support tool rather than a black-box optimizer.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the application of machine-learning (ML) models to predict mass yield (MY) and higher heating value (HHV) during biomass torrefaction, using a large and diverse experimental dataset. Through comprehensive model benchmarking, feature engineering, and interpretability analysis, tree-based ensemble and boosting models—particularly CatBoost—were identified as the most reliable approaches for capturing the nonlinear relationships inherent in torrefaction processes. The results confirm that data-driven models can robustly reproduce complex trends that are difficult to describe using conventional empirical correlations alone.

Beyond predictive accuracy, the central engineering contribution of this work lies in how ML outputs are reorganized and utilized to support practical torrefaction process optimization. Traditional approaches typically rely on single-response optimization or empirical correlations that treat fuel quality and yield independently, often leading to trial-and-error experimentation. In contrast, the proposed GUI-based simulator integrates ML-predicted MY and HHV under identical operating conditions, enabling simultaneous evaluation of material efficiency and energy densification. This structure allows practitioners to explicitly visualize trade-offs and identify feasible operating windows rather than pursuing isolated optima.

By functioning as a decision-support framework rather than a standalone prediction tool, the simulator reduces experimental burden, accelerates process screening, and provides transparent guidance for operating-condition selection. Importantly, the AI approach does not replace conventional engineering principles; instead, it complements them by expanding the accessible decision space and enabling data-driven reasoning where experimental constraints or process complexity limit traditional methods. In this sense, the proposed framework demonstrates how ML can be effectively translated from model fitting into a practical engineering tool for rational and interpretable torrefaction process optimization.
