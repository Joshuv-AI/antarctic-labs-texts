Measuring the Association Between the COVID-19 Pandemic and Cancer Incidence by Sex Using a Quasi-Experimental Study Design
Authors: Kathleen M. Decker, Allison Feely, Iresha Ratnayake, Oliver Bucher, Piotr Czaykowski, Katie Galloway, Pamela Hebbard, Julian O. Kim, Grace Musto, Marshall Pitz, Harminder Singh, Pascal Lambert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThis study examined the association between COVID-19 and cancer incidence by sex in Manitoba, Canada.METHODSWe used a population-based quasi-experimental study design and an interrupted time-series analysis to compare the rate of new cancer diagnoses between males and females before (January 2015 until December 2019) and after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic (April 2020 until December 2022).RESULTSA total of 16,200 females and 20,631 males diagnosed with cancer between 2015 and 2022 in Manitoba were included. Colon cancer incidence decreased by 34% for males and females from April to September 2020. Incidence then remained stable for males but decreased by 22% from October 2021 to December 2022 for females. Brain and CNS cancer incidence decreased by 37% for males during 2021 and 2022 but only for females during the last quarter of 2020 and the first quarter of 2021 (77%). Urinary cancer decreased by 18% for males from April 2020 to December 2022 but was stable for females. Head and neck cancers decreased by 22% for males during 2020, but was stable for females. As of December 2022, the largest estimated cumulative differences in the number of cases occurred for males diagnosed with brain and CNS cancer (31.6% deficit for males, 76 cases), urinary cancer (18.4% deficit, 186 cases), and endocrine cancer (52.4% surplus, 56 cases), and females diagnosed with colon cancer (19.7% deficit, 187 cases).CONCLUSIONSex-based differences in the association between age-standardized cancer incidence and the COVID-19 pandemic exist for several cancer sites. Sex-based differences on postpandemic cancer incidence, especially for brain, CNS, urinary, and colon cancers, need follow-up because of the ongoing deficits documented in this study.

INTRODUCTION

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in the extensive reorganization of health care services and resources across Canada. This reorganization affected all areas of the cancer system from screening and diagnosis to treatment and follow-up care.1 In Manitoba, Canada, these changes contributed to a decrease in the overall age-standardized cancer incidence rates for most cancer sites at the beginning of the pandemic, with longer-term decreases and cumulative deficits observed for several types of cancer.2 This association may differ for subpopulations. For example, we have found that the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on cancer incidence differed by where an individual lived.3 Another important factor that may affect the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and cancer incidence is biological sex. Research has found that males were infected more than females and suffered greater COVID-19 morbidity and mortality,4 but the downstream effects of the pandemic, including an increase in unpaid labor, mental health burdens, and decreased access to health care, affected women disproportionately compared with men.5,6 Sex-based differences that are critical to cancer diagnoses, such as colorectal cancer screening participation and visits to primary care providers,7,8 that existed before the pandemic may have also been exacerbated. To our knowledge, to date, there has been limited research exploring sex-based differences when examining the pandemic and cancer outcomes.9,10 The objective of this study was to examine the association between COVID-19 and cancer incidence by sex in Manitoba, Canada.

METHODS

Setting

The province of Manitoba is located in central Canada and has a population of approximately 1.45 million in 2021.11 The majority of Manitobans live in the capital city of Winnipeg. CancerCare Manitoba is the provincial cancer agency responsible for providing cancer services to all residents. Before COVID-19, approximately 7,000 Manitobans were diagnosed with cancer each year.12 The first COVID-19 case in Manitoba was identified on March 12, 2020. A state of emergency was declared in March 2020 followed by limited access to nonurgent health care services, an increase in virtual care, and large decreases in cancer screening. Some pandemic restrictions were lifted in May 2020 but reinstated in October 2020 during the second wave.13 The third wave in April 2021 resulted in additional restrictions. In June 2021, a three-phase reopening plan was announced and in September 2021, proof of vaccination and indoor mask mandates were implemented.13 By October 1, 2021, approximately 70% of Manitobans were fully vaccinated. The fourth wave began in the fall of 2021 and the fifth wave, driven by the Omicron variant, began in December 2021 and peaked in January 2022. The sixth wave began in March 2022 as COVID-19 hospitalizations began to rise.

Study Design and Study Population

A population-based quasi-experimental study design was used to examine the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and cancer incidence by sex. All lung, colon, rectal, hematologic, unknown primary, head and neck, endocrine, melanoma, brain and CNS not including meningioma, urinary, other digestive, and pancreatic cancers diagnosed between 2015 and 2022 were included. Breast cancers and sex-specific cancers (gynecologic, prostate, testicular, and penile cancers) were excluded from this analysis. Lung included small cell and non–small cell cancers. Rectal included rectal and rectosigmoid cancers. Hematologic included Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, myeloma, ALL, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, AML, acute monocytic leukemia, chronic myeloid leukemia, and other leukemia. Endocrine included thyroid and other endocrine cancers. Other digestive included esophagus, stomach, small intestine, anus, liver, gallbladder, and other digestive system cancers. Urinary included bladder, kidney, renal pelvis, ureter, and other urinary system cancers. Appendix Table A1 lists the cancers in each category and International Classification of Diseases for Oncology Version 3 codes.

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the University of Manitoba's Health Research Ethics Board (project code: HS23979 [H2020:264], approval date: May 5, 2020), Manitoba Health's Health Information Privacy Committee, and CancerCare Manitoba's Research and Resource Impact Committee. Because data were deidentified, informed consent was not required.

Data Sources

Cancer cases were determined using the Manitoba Cancer Registry, which has received the Gold Standard for Registry Certification from the North American Association for Central Cancer Registries for containing population-based, high-quality data that are complete, accurate, and timely.14 Population data were based on estimated Manitoba Health coverage provided by Manitoba Health.

Outcomes

Outcomes included age-standardized cancer incidence rates per 100,000, the estimated cumulative difference in the number of new cancer diagnoses, and the estimated percentage cumulative difference in the number of new cancers diagnosed by biological sex. Incidence rates were age-standardized to the 2011 Canadian population using the direct method. The estimated cumulative difference in new cancer diagnoses was defined as the difference between the quarterly cumulative counterfactual count (ie, the estimated number of diagnoses in the absence of COVID-19) and the quarterly cumulative fitted count (ie, the observed number of diagnoses smoothed). The estimated percentage cumulative difference in the number of new cancer diagnoses was defined as the cumulative difference in the fitted and counterfactual counts divided by the cumulative counterfactual count. Because of the impact of the pandemic on colorectal cancer screening,15 we examined colon cancers separately for individuals younger than 50 years, 50-74 years (ie, eligible in Canada for average risk screening), and 75 years and older. In Manitoba, colorectal cancer screening using the guaiac-based fecal occult blood test Hemoccult II SENSA was recommended every 2 years for individuals age 50-74 years.

Statistical Analyses

We used an interrupted time-series analysis that accounts for baseline trends in cancer incidence.16 We compared cancer incidence rates from April 2020 onward (ie, after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic) to counterfactual rates (ie, estimated rates if the pandemic had not occurred) on the basis of pre–COVID-19 trends (ie, baseline rates) and quantified quarterly new cancer diagnoses by cancer type and sex. This is represented by the following:y=β0+β1T+β2X+log(pop)+Ɛwhere y is the count of cancer cases, β0 represents the baseline level at T = 0 (the intercept), β1 is the slope associated with a one-unit change in time (T), β2 is the slope associated with the change during the pandemic where X = the pandemic period, log(pop) is the offset term that logs the population denominator (to create the rate), and Ɛ is the residual (error).

The pre–COVID-19 period included January 2015 to December 2019. The first quarter of 2020 (ie, January to March) was excluded from the analyses because pandemic restrictions, changes to physician visits, and changes to treatment protocols began to be implemented incrementally throughout March 2020.1,17,18 Generalized linear models (GLM) were selected for each cancer site based on the distribution of the data. Scaled quantile residuals were used to select the distribution and evaluate model fit. Autocorrelation was examined using residuals. Each model included a binary intervention term (pre–COVID-19 period = 0 and COVID-19 period = 1) and a time term defined as the number of quarters since the start of the study period. Fitted values (ie, smoothed estimates from a regression model to predict observed values) were generated using the observed values in the data. Counterfactual values were generated by estimating values using the COVID-19 binary term as 0 rather than 1. After the GLM model selection, the models were fine-tuned by comparing the adjusted McFadden R2 between subsequent models.19

Plots were produced using the observed, counterfactual, and fitted values. When the counterfactual did not follow the baseline trend, we modified the model (ie, the number of df for splines was reduced) until the counterfactual aligned with the baseline trend. When the plotted-fitted values in the COVID-19 period did not fit the observed data well (eg, observed values were below fitted values during the early COVID-19 period but higher during the later COVID-19 period), COVID-19-by-time interactions were used. Although significant time interactions were found for some cancer sites, they demonstrated poor fit (ie, fitted values did not fit observed values well). Therefore, multiple COVID-19 dummy variables were created to represent different time periods during the pandemic to provide a slope for each period, which allowed for more accurate estimates. The analysis used the following R packages: haven, splines, Hmisc, lattice, MASS, ggplot2, car, DHARMa, multcomp, lmtest, glmmTMB, and forest plot.

Ratios between counterfactual and fitted estimates and 95% CIs derived from contrast statements were calculated. The estimated cumulative difference reflects cases up until December 31, 2022, and was plotted using a forest plot. The 95% CIs for the cumulative difference estimates were calculated with parametric bootstrapping and 1,000 replications. Data analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) and R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

A total of 16,200 females and 20,631 males diagnosed with cancer between 2015 and 2022 were included (Table 1). The median (IQR) age at diagnosis was 70 (IQR, 60-79) years for females and 69 (IQR, 60-77) years for males. The largest number of cases for both males and females occurred for lung cancer followed by hematologic cancers. Ratios between the fitted and counterfactual incidence rates for males and females are shown in Appendix Table A2. Colon cancer incidence decreased by 34% for males during the first quarter and also for females during the first two quarters of the pandemic. Colon cancer incidence then remained stable for males but decreased significantly by 22% from October 2021 to December 2022 for females. When examined by age group, colon cancer incidence did not decrease significantly for males age <50 years or age 50-74 years but did decrease by 45% for those age 75+ years from April to June 2020 (Appendix Table A3 and Fig A1). Colon cancer incidence did not decrease for females age <50 years. Females age 50-74 years had a 21% nonsignificant decrease in colon cancer incidence, while those age 75+ years had a significant 24% decrease throughout the entire time period. To help explain these results, we examined the colonoscopy rate per 100,000 by sex for the 50-74 and 75+ age groups (Appendix Fig A2) from January 2015 to December 2022. Colonoscopy rates were significantly below the counterfactual for males and females age 50-74 years and for females age 75+ years. There was no difference in the estimated and counterfactual colonoscopy rates for males age 75+ years.

Rectal cancer incidence was stable for males but was 55% significantly higher for females from January to June 2021. Head and neck cancers decreased significantly by 22% for males during 2020, but was stable throughout the study time period for females. Conversely, melanoma was stable for males but decreased by 22% for females during 2020 and the first quarter of 2021. Brain and CNS cancer incidence decreased by 37% for males during 2021 and 2022 but only for females during the last quarter of 2020 and the first quarter of 2021 (77%). Urinary cancer decreased by 18% for males from April 2020 to December 2022 but remained stable for females. Pancreatic cancer decreased by 32% for males from April to June 2020 then remained stable for male and females for the rest of the time period. Other digestive cancers decreased by 17% for males from April to September 2021 and then remained stable until December 2022. Other digestive cancer incidence remained stable for females for the entire period. Endocrine cancer incidence increased for males by 52%, although this was not significant. The incidence of unknown primary cancers decreased significantly for both sexes (44% from April to December 2022 for males and 39% from July to December 2022 for females). Lung and hematologic cancer incidence remained stable for both males and females.

DISCUSSION

We observed sex-based differences in the association between age-standardized cancer incidence and the COVID-19 pandemic in Manitoba, Canada, for several cancers. Colon cancer incidence decreased at the start of the pandemic for both males and females. This can be explained by reductions to Manitoba's colon cancer screening program during April and May 2020 and a reduction in the number colonoscopies performed in Manitoba.15,20 From October 2021 to the end of 2022, colon cancer incidence was also lower than expected for females leading to a significant deficit in the number of colon cancer cases. When examined by age group, a nonsignificant decrease was observed for females age 50-74 years and a significant decrease was observed for females age 75+ years. A decrease in screening can account in part for this observation, but complex factors also likely overlie this difference. Sex-based differences in primary care utilization, urgency to work up asymptomatic iron deficiency, and gender biases with regard to the validation and workup of nonspecific symptoms may also play a role. Our data do not allow for an in-depth study of such factors but do raise the question that there may be complex health-related sex differences in the care for GI issues.

Brain and CNS cancer incidence decreased for females by 77% from October 2020 to March 2021; this short-term decrease is likely a random effect. Brain and CNS cancers decreased for males by 37% in January 2021 and remained low until December 2022 leading to a significant deficit in cases. A decrease in brain and CNS cancers among both males and females was observed in the United States during March and April 2020, before rising again in May.21 Brain and CNS tumors often cause symptoms that cannot be ignored and result in an emergency department (ED) visit and subsequent computed tomography scan. Studies found considerable reductions in ED visits early in the pandemic22,23 but more recent analyses found that ED visits returned to prepandemic levels by early 2021.24 Hence, we cannot explain why males would have reduced brain and CNS cancer incidence compared with females except for the possible influence of outliers in the baseline period that affected the estimated counterfactual rates.

Urinary cancer incidence decreased significantly for males and the decrease persisted throughout the study time period with a deficit of 186 cases by December 2022. A decrease in urinary cancer incidence was also observed in the United States.25 The decrease may reflect reduced access to cystoscopy,26 challenges diagnosing and managing urothelial tumors throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, and a decrease in the incidental diagnosis of kidney cancers because of reduced abdominal imaging availability. Although research from the United States has found that cystoscopy rates are generally lower for women than men,27 there is no evidence to suggest that reductions in access to diagnostic tests for urinary cancer varied by sex in Manitoba during the pandemic.

A nonsignificant increase in endocrine cancer incidence was observed for males throughout the pandemic leading to a surplus in the number of cancer cases. Because there was an increased number of cases (as opposed to a decrease) and we are not aware of any changes in the diagnosis of endocrine cancers between males and females, it is likely that this observation is random.

Few other studies have examined the association between COVID-19 and cancer incidence by sex. In the United States, Burus et al9 examined observed versus expected cancer incidence rates from 2020 to 2021. Cancer incidence returned to prepandemic levels among females but remained significantly lower than expected among males. Another US study found a larger decrease in cancer incidence for male patients during the first year of the pandemic.10 By 2021, the incidence rate had recovered compared with 2020. Neither study examined incidence by cancer site and sex. It is also necessary to determine if the incidence of late-stage cancers increased for cancer sites that were affected by the pandemic. This is particularly important for colon cancer as there is the potential for delayed diagnoses because of reductions in screening. We are currently examining the impact of COVID-19 on stage at diagnosis.

Our study has several strengths including the use of population-based cancer registry data and the inclusion of a long preintervention period in the interrupted time-series analysis that allowed us to take baseline trends into consideration. Additionally, clinicians who provided care throughout the pandemic were included as authors; they provided content knowledge for what was happening regarding cancer diagnosis and treatment during the entire study period.

The results are also subject to the following limitations. Some cancer sites contained small sample sizes and therefore should be interpreted with caution paying attention to the width of the confidence intervals presented for each site. Since COVID-19 restrictions and cancer incidence may differ geographically, these results should be interpreted within the Manitoba context. Because of data limitations, we were not able to examine the association between COVID-19 and cancer incidence by sex. Research has found that transgender and nonbinary people are less likely to interact with the health care system, which may have been exacerbated by the pandemic.28,29 Potential additional differences could have been demonstrated if gender data were available at the population level.

In conclusion, this study found sex-based differences in the association between age-standardized cancer incidence and the COVID-19 pandemic for several cancer sites. Sex-based differences in postpandemic cancer incidence, especially for brain, CNS, urinary, and colon cancers, need follow-up because of the ongoing deficits documented in this study.
