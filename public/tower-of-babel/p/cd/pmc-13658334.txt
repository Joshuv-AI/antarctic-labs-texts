Early postoperative complication profile and acute hypothalamic dysfunction grading after surgery for adult intraventricular craniopharyngiomas: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Uygun Altibayev, Jakhongirmirzo Yoldoshev, Gayrat Kariev, Bipin Chaurasia, Alvaro Campero, Abhidha Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Craniopharyngiomas of the third ventricle represent a rare and challenging subgroup of craniopharyngiomas that either extend into the third ventricular cavity through destruction of its floor or develop as isolated intraventricular lesions, preserving the ependymal lining and remaining clearly separated from the chiasmatic cistern [1]. Purely isolated third ventricular craniopharyngiomas account for approximately 0.5%–14% of all craniopharyngiomas and differ from suprasellar forms by the absence of extension into the suprasellar cistern, as well as by preservation of the pituitary stalk and the floor of the third ventricle [2, 3]. Suprasellar craniopharyngiomas with secondary extension into the third ventricular cavity comprise approximately 30%–40% of cases, most commonly corresponding to the infundibulo-tuberal type, in which the tumor originates beneath the ventricular floor and subsequently expands upward into the ventricle [4, 5].

Craniopharyngiomas are benign epithelial tumors arising from remnants of Rathke’s pouch and account for approximately 2%–4% of all intracranial neoplasms [6]. In contrast to the pediatric population, in which the adamantinomatous subtype predominates, third ventricular craniopharyngiomas in adults are encountered more frequently and typically present later in the clinical course because of their slow growth [3, 7]. In published series, the mean patient age ranges from 35 to 50 years, with a predominance of the papillary histological subtype, which occurs almost exclusively in adults [2]. However, regardless of histological subtype, contemporary craniopharyngioma management increasingly emphasizes the need to balance tumor control with preservation of neurological, endocrine, cognitive, and overall quality-of-life outcomes [8].

Surgical treatment of third ventricular craniopharyngiomas remains among the most demanding procedures in neurosurgery because of the intimate relationship of these tumors to critical surrounding structures, including the hypothalamus, thalamus, columns of the fornix, and ventricular walls [3, 9]. In current microsurgical practice, the principal approaches remain the interhemispheric transcallosal route and the transcortical transventricular approach [10]. The transcallosal approach minimizes direct cortical injury but carries a potential risk of damage to the corpus callosum, fornix, and interhemispheric connections [11]. By contrast, the transcortical approach may provide a more direct trajectory in patients with marked hydrocephalus or large intraventricular tumors, but it requires corticotomy and has been associated with a higher risk of postoperative seizures [12]. Importantly, approach selection is strongly influenced by tumor size, degree of hydrocephalus, tumor consistency, ventricular anatomy, and surgeon preference; therefore, comparisons between these approaches should be interpreted with caution when baseline anatomical characteristics are not fully balanced.

In recent years, treatment strategies for craniopharyngiomas have shifted toward individualized, morbidity-conscious management rather than uniform pursuit of radical resection in all cases. This trend is particularly evident in cystic craniopharyngiomas, where minimally invasive cyst-directed approaches, including neuroendoscopic fenestration, stereotactic cyst drainage, and Ommaya reservoir placement, have been used to achieve decompression and symptom control in selected patients [13, 14]. Neuroendoscopic strategies have also been described for unusual or acute presentations of cystic craniopharyngiomas, further supporting the concept that treatment should be tailored according to tumor anatomy, cystic component, clinical presentation, and anticipated risk of hypothalamic morbidity [15, 16]. Although these cyst-directed approaches are not directly comparable with the transcallosal and transcortical microsurgical routes analyzed in the present study, they reflect the same contemporary principle of balancing surgical efficacy with preservation of functional outcome.

Despite numerous studies focusing on extent of resection and endocrine outcomes, several early postoperative complications in adults remain insufficiently characterized. Among these, early cognitive change, postoperative seizures, and acute hypothalamic dysfunction are particularly relevant because they directly influence early recovery, intensive care requirements, rehabilitation potential, and quality of life. Cognitive status is often assessed in clinical practice using screening instruments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [17]. However, the MMSE should be regarded as a pragmatic screening tool rather than a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, because it has limited sensitivity for subtle memory impairment, frontal executive dysfunction, and domain-specific cognitive deficits. Therefore, early postoperative MMSE change may provide useful clinical information, but it should not be interpreted as a complete quantification of postoperative cognitive impairment.

The incidence and determinants of postoperative seizures after surgery for intraventricular craniopharyngiomas also require further clarification, particularly when comparing transcallosal and transcortical routes. However, seizure risk may depend not only on the surgical corridor itself, but also on tumor size, severity of hydrocephalus, cortical manipulation, retraction, and postoperative edema. Similarly, acute hypothalamic dysfunction represents one of the most clinically significant early complications after surgery in the third ventricular region. Currently available classifications, such as the Puget scale, were developed primarily in the pediatric population and focus mainly on preoperative or chronic hypothalamic involvement [18]. In contrast, a standardized grading system for the severity of acute postoperative diencephalic dysfunction in adults remains lacking.

The aim of the present study was to characterize the frequency and pattern of early postoperative complications in adult patients with intraventricular craniopharyngiomas, with particular emphasis on three clinically relevant domains: (1) early postoperative MMSE change as a screening-based marker of cognitive status, (2) new-onset postoperative seizures, and (3) the severity of acute diencephalic syndrome based on an original four-stage grading scale. Comparisons between the transcallosal and transcortical approaches were considered exploratory because surgical approach selection was influenced by baseline anatomical and clinical factors, including tumor size, hydrocephalus, tumor topography, and third ventricular floor involvement.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective single-center cohort study of adult patients who underwent microsurgical treatment for third ventricular or predominantly intraventricular craniopharyngiomas between 2015 and 2025. The primary aim was to evaluate early postoperative complications, including early MMSE change, new-onset seizures, and acute diencephalic syndrome. Comparisons between the transcallosal and transcortical approaches were considered exploratory, because the choice of approach was not randomized and depended on tumor size, hydrocephalus, ventricular anatomy, and third ventricular floor involvement.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included adult patients older than 45 years with histologically confirmed third ventricular or predominantly intraventricular craniopharyngioma who underwent primary microsurgical resection through either a transcallosal or a transcortical transventricular approach. The diagnosis and topographical subtype were determined using preoperative MRI, including assessment of the suprasellar cistern, pituitary stalk, sellar changes, tumor relationship to the third ventricular floor, and hypothalamic involvement.

Patients with predominantly suprasellar craniopharyngiomas without a significant intraventricular component, recurrent tumors, age younger than 45 years, combined surgical approaches, biopsy-only procedures, or incomplete postoperative follow-up data were excluded. During the study period, 84 patients were screened. Twenty-two patients were excluded because they did not meet the final inclusion criteria, including 16 patients with incomplete postoperative follow-up data. Finally, 62 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

All early postoperative complications and all-cause postoperative mortality were recorded. Deaths related to progression of severe acute diencephalic syndrome were additionally analyzed as stage IV events; however, deaths from other postoperative causes were not excluded from the overall surgical outcome assessment.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed using standard microsurgical techniques under an operating microscope. Intraoperative ultrasonography and an ultrasonic aspirator were used when indicated, depending on tumor size, consistency, ventricular dilatation, and the degree of adhesion to the surrounding structures.

Interhemispheric transcallosal approach (n = 30): A standard right-sided anterior-to-middle callosotomy was performed. The length of callosal incision was limited to approximately 2 cm whenever possible. In patients with hydrocephalus, the frontal horn of the lateral ventricle was entered and cerebrospinal fluid was released to facilitate ventricular relaxation and tumor exposure.

Transcortical transventricular approach (n = 32): A 2–3 cm corticotomy was made within an avascular zone of the frontal lobe, most commonly through the middle frontal gyrus. This approach was preferentially selected in patients with larger tumors, marked ventricular dilatation, or severe hydrocephalus, where a direct transventricular trajectory was considered technically more suitable.

The choice of surgical approach was individualized and based on tumor size, tumor consistency, degree of hydrocephalus, ventricular anatomy, relationship of the tumor to the third ventricular floor, and surgeon preference. Because approach selection was not randomized, differences in postoperative outcomes between the two approaches were interpreted as exploratory and potentially influenced by baseline anatomical differences.

Intraoperative neuromonitoring was not routinely used in this retrospective cohort. Its absence was taken into account when interpreting postoperative neurological and hypothalamic outcomes.

Outcome assessment

Extent of resection

The extent of resection was determined on the basis of contrast-enhanced postoperative MRI obtained within the first 72 h after surgery. Gross-total resection (GTR) was defined as the absence of visible residual tumor or residual tumor volume ≤ 0.5 cm³. Subtotal resection (STR) was defined as visible residual tumor > 0.5 cm³ left intentionally or because of adherence to critical structures, particularly the hypothalamus, third ventricular floor, fornix, or major vessels. Biopsy-only procedures and partial decompressions without intended tumor resection were not included in the study. In patients with residual tumor, its location and relationship to the third ventricular floor and hypothalamic region were reviewed for the analysis of acute diencephalic syndrome.

Early postoperative MMSE change

Cognitive status was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) at two time points: preoperatively, 1–3 days before surgery, and during the early postoperative period, on postoperative days 10–14 or at discharge. The assessment was performed by the treating neurosurgeon or neurologist. Because of the retrospective design, assessors were not blinded to the surgical approach or postoperative course, and formal inter-rater reliability testing was not available. MMSE scores were interpreted as follows: 27–30 points, normal range; 24–26 points, mild impairment range; 20–23 points, moderate impairment range; and < 20 points, severe impairment range. A decrease of 3 or more points compared with baseline was considered clinically relevant early postoperative MMSE decline. The MMSE was used as a screening instrument and not as a comprehensive neuropsychological battery.

Seizure syndrome

New-onset postoperative seizures were defined as any clinical manifestation of seizure activity, including focal seizures or bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, occurring within 30 days after surgery. Patients with a history of epileptic seizures before surgery were excluded from the analysis of new-onset postoperative seizures. Perioperative anticonvulsant administration, when used, was recorded from medication charts and considered when interpreting seizure outcomes. Because the transcortical approach was more often selected for larger tumors and severe hydrocephalus, seizure rates were interpreted with consideration of potential confounding by baseline tumor and ventricular characteristics.

Acute diencephalic syndrome

To assess the severity of acute diencephalic syndrome (DS), we used an original four-stage grading scale (Table 2). The scale includes five clinical domains: level of consciousness assessed by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), thermoregulatory disturbance, water-electrolyte and endocrine disturbance, autonomic-hemodynamic instability, and respiratory pattern. Water-electrolyte abnormalities included serum sodium level, urine output, urine specific gravity, and clinical signs of diabetes insipidus. Cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) values were considered supportive endocrine markers; however, in patients receiving perioperative glucocorticoids, these values were interpreted cautiously and were not used as isolated determinants of DS stage.

Grading of the severity of early acute diencephalic syndrome

Assessments were performed every 6–12 h during the first 7 postoperative days jointly by the treating intensivist and neurosurgeon. Each patient was assigned the maximum DS stage reached during the observation period. Before assigning neurological, thermoregulatory, autonomic, or respiratory abnormalities to DS, alternative causes were reviewed, including postoperative hematoma, infarction, hydrocephalus progression, infection, medication-related sedation, opioid effect, antiepileptic drug effect, primary cardiopulmonary complications, and unrelated metabolic disturbances. Postoperative CT or MRI and laboratory findings were used when necessary to support this differential assessment.

The development of diabetes insipidus without direct pituitary stalk injury was interpreted as a manifestation of hypothalamic involvement, particularly injury or dysfunction of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. Because the scale was applied retrospectively, formal validation and inter-rater reliability analysis were not performed; therefore, the DS grading results were interpreted as an exploratory clinical severity assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (Q1–Q3), depending on distribution. Categorical variables are reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Because several outcome events were infrequent, particularly new-onset seizures and severe acute diencephalic syndrome, conventional multivariable logistic regression and propensity score matching were not performed because of the limited number of events per variable and the risk of model instability. Therefore, comparisons between the transcallosal and transcortical approaches were interpreted as exploratory. Potential confounding by tumor size, hydrocephalus, and third ventricular floor status was considered in the interpretation of the results.

For analysis of acute diencephalic syndrome, stages I–II were considered mild-to-moderate forms, whereas stages III–IV were considered severe-to-critical forms. Associations between diencephalic syndrome severity, third ventricular floor status, and extent of resection were assessed using Fisher exact test when expected cell counts were small. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Group characteristics

The groups of patients who underwent surgery via the transcallosal approach (n = 30) and the transcortical transventricular approach (n = 32) were comparable in terms of age, sex, and tumor histological subtype (Table 1). However, the transcortical group had a significantly higher proportion of large tumors > 4 cm (44% vs. 13%; p = 0.04) and severe hydrocephalus (84% vs. 60%; p = 0.03). This imbalance reflects the non-randomized nature of surgical approach selection and was considered when interpreting approach-related differences in early postoperative outcomes.

Early postoperative MMSE change

In the overall cohort, the mean MMSE score decreased from 27.4 ± 2.1 preoperatively to 24.8 ± 3.5 in the early postoperative period (p < 0.05). A decrease in MMSE score of ≥ 3 points compared with baseline was observed in 17 patients (27%).

When the two surgical approaches were compared, early postoperative MMSE decline ≥ 3 points was numerically more frequent in the transcallosal group than in the transcortical group: 10 patients (33%) versus 7 patients (22%), respectively (p = 0.29) (Table 3). This difference did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective design and baseline differences between the groups.

The severity distribution of postoperative MMSE scores also differed numerically between the groups. In the transcallosal group, 2 patients had postoperative MMSE scores < 20 and 4 patients had scores in the 20–23 range. In the transcortical group, no patient had a postoperative MMSE score < 20, and mild-range MMSE scores predominated. Among the 10 transcallosal patients with MMSE decline ≥ 3 points, 6 shifted from the normal range to either the mild or moderate impairment range. These findings indicate an early screening-based MMSE change rather than a definitive assessment of domain-specific cognitive impairment.

Seizure syndrome

New-onset postoperative seizures developed in 9 patients (15%) in the overall cohort. Seizures were numerically more frequent in the transcortical group, occurring in 7 of 32 patients (22%), compared with 2 of 30 patients (7%) in the transcallosal group; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.09) (Table 3).

The highest seizure rate was observed after transcortical resection of large tumors > 4 cm, where seizures occurred in 4 of 14 patients (29%). Because the transcortical group included a significantly higher proportion of large tumors and severe hydrocephalus, the increased seizure rate should be interpreted as potentially influenced by both the surgical corridor and baseline tumor-related factors. All seizures were transient and were successfully controlled with antiepileptic therapy.

The severity of acute diencephalic syndrome was closely related to the condition of the floor of the third ventricle. In patients with tumor extension and destruction of the ventricular floor, diencephalic syndrome developed in all cases (19/19, 100%), and severe-to-critical forms, defined as stages III–IV, were observed in 5 of 19 patients (26%). In contrast, among patients with preserved third ventricular floor anatomy, no severe or critical stages were recorded (0/43). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.02).

A combined analysis of extent of resection and third ventricular floor status showed that the most severe postoperative course occurred in patients who underwent gross-total resection (GTR) in the presence of ventricular floor destruction. In this subgroup, severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome developed in 4 of 13 patients (31%), including two fatal stage IV cases. The all-cause early postoperative mortality rate was 3.2% (2/62). Both deaths occurred in patients with stage IV acute diencephalic syndrome, and no deaths unrelated to diencephalic syndrome were recorded during the early postoperative period. By contrast, no severe or critical diencephalic syndrome was observed after GTR when the third ventricular floor was preserved (0/32).

After subtotal resection (STR), severe diencephalic syndrome was observed in one patient, corresponding to 6% of the entire STR group and 17% of the STR subgroup with floor destruction. No severe or critical stages were observed in the STR subgroup with preserved third ventricular floor anatomy (0/11). Therefore, in patients with ventricular floor destruction, severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome was numerically more frequent after GTR than after STR. However, because of the small size of these subgroups, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously.

Summary of results

In summary, early postoperative MMSE decline ≥ 3 points was observed in 27% of patients, new-onset seizures in 15%, and acute diencephalic syndrome in 76%. Approach-related differences in MMSE decline and seizure incidence were numerical and did not reach statistical significance. Severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome occurred only in patients with destruction of the third ventricular floor and was most frequent after gross-total resection in this subgroup.

Discussion

The present study analyzed early postoperative complications in adult patients undergoing surgery for third ventricular or predominantly intraventricular craniopharyngiomas, with emphasis on early postoperative MMSE change, new-onset seizures, and acute diencephalic syndrome. The main findings were as follows: early postoperative MMSE decline ≥ 3 points occurred in 27% of patients, new-onset seizures occurred in 15%, and acute diencephalic syndrome of varying severity was observed in 76%. Severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome was observed only in patients with destruction of the third ventricular floor and was most frequent after gross-total resection in this anatomical subgroup.

Recent literature on craniopharyngioma management increasingly emphasizes the need to balance tumor control with preservation of neurological, endocrine, cognitive, and quality-of-life outcomes [8, 19–23]. This concept is particularly relevant for lesions involving the third ventricular region, where aggressive resection may increase the risk of hypothalamic and neurocognitive morbidity. Recent studies on cystic craniopharyngiomas have also highlighted the role of individualized, morbidity-conscious strategies, including neuroendoscopic fenestration, stereotactic cyst drainage, and Ommaya reservoir placement in selected patients [13, 14]. Although these minimally invasive cyst-directed procedures are not directly comparable with the transcallosal and transcortical microsurgical approaches analyzed in the present cohort, they reflect the broader contemporary trend toward tailoring treatment according to tumor anatomy, cystic component, hydrocephalus, clinical presentation, and anticipated hypothalamic risk [15, 16].

Early postoperative MMSE change

In the overall cohort, early postoperative MMSE decline ≥ 3 points was observed in 17 patients (27%). This finding indicates that early cognitive deterioration after surgery for intraventricular craniopharyngiomas is clinically relevant and should be actively monitored. However, the present results should be interpreted as screening-based early MMSE changes, rather than as a definitive quantification of domain-specific cognitive impairment.

The numerical frequency of MMSE decline was higher after the transcallosal approach than after the transcortical approach: 33% versus 22%, respectively. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.29). Therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence of a confirmed approach-related effect. Rather, it represents an exploratory observation that requires cautious interpretation in the context of a retrospective, non-randomized cohort.

There are anatomical reasons why cognitive vulnerability may occur after a transcallosal route. This approach requires callosotomy and surgical manipulation near the fornix, septal region, and interhemispheric connections, which are relevant to memory, attention, and integrative cognitive functions. In selected cases, enlargement of the callosotomy or traction near the fornices may contribute to postoperative cognitive changes. Conversely, the transcortical approach may avoid direct callosal manipulation but involves frontal corticotomy, which may affect executive function and carries its own cognitive risks.

A major limitation is that the MMSE is a coarse cognitive screening tool. It has limited sensitivity for subtle memory impairment, frontal executive dysfunction, attention deficits, and domain-specific neuropsychological changes. In addition, early postoperative assessment on days 10–14 or at discharge may be influenced by pain, fatigue, sleep disruption, electrolyte disturbances, antiepileptic medication, opioid analgesia, and the general postoperative state. Therefore, the present MMSE results should be interpreted as early postoperative screening data rather than long-term neurocognitive outcomes, particularly because the MMSE has well-recognized ceiling effects and limited sensitivity for mild cognitive impairment and frontal-executive dysfunction compared with more sensitive tools such as the MoCA [24–26]. Future prospective studies should include standardized neuropsychological batteries and later follow-up assessments.

Seizure syndrome

New-onset postoperative seizures occurred in 9 patients (15%) in the overall cohort. Seizures were numerically more frequent after the transcortical approach than after the transcallosal approach: 22% versus 7%, respectively. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.09), and the finding should be interpreted cautiously.

The higher numerical seizure rate in the transcortical group may be partly related to the corticotomy required for this approach, which can create an epileptogenic cortical injury zone. However, the difference cannot be attributed to the surgical corridor alone. The transcortical group had a significantly higher proportion of large tumors and severe hydrocephalus, both of which may increase cortical manipulation, brain retraction, postoperative edema, and metabolic vulnerability. Therefore, the observed seizure difference likely reflects a combined effect of the surgical approach and baseline tumor-related factors.

The highest seizure rate was observed after transcortical resection of tumors larger than 4 cm, reaching 29%. This may be explained by the need for a wider operative corridor, greater tumor manipulation, and more extensive ventricular decompression in large lesions. Nevertheless, seizures were also observed after the transcallosal approach, indicating that this route should not be considered completely seizure-free. Possible mechanisms include frontal lobe retraction, parasagittal cortical irritation, venous congestion, microvascular changes, and postoperative metabolic disturbances.

All seizures in the present cohort were transient and controlled with antiepileptic therapy. Perioperative anticonvulsant use should be considered when interpreting seizure rates, especially in patients undergoing transcortical surgery, patients with large tumors, and those with postoperative cortical irritation or hemorrhagic changes.

Acute diencephalic syndrome

One of the main objectives of this study was to describe the early postoperative severity of acute diencephalic syndrome using a four-stage clinical grading scale. The scale incorporates five domains: level of consciousness, thermoregulation, water-electrolyte and endocrine disturbances, autonomic-hemodynamic instability, and respiratory pattern. These domains were selected because they reflect clinically important manifestations of acute hypothalamic dysfunction and are directly relevant to postoperative intensive care management.

The severity of acute diencephalic syndrome was strongly related to the condition of the third ventricular floor. In patients with tumor extension and destruction of the ventricular floor, diencephalic syndrome developed in all cases, and severe-to-critical stages were observed in 5 of 19 patients. In contrast, no severe or critical stages were observed when the third ventricular floor was preserved. This finding supports the clinical importance of the tumor–hypothalamus interface and the anatomical integrity of the third ventricular floor.

The combined analysis of resection extent and floor status showed that the most severe course occurred in patients who underwent gross-total resection in the presence of floor destruction. In this subgroup, severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome occurred in 4 of 13 patients, including two fatal stage IV cases. The all-cause early postoperative mortality rate was 3.2% (2/62). Both deaths occurred in patients with stage IV acute diencephalic syndrome, and no deaths unrelated to diencephalic syndrome were recorded during the early postoperative period. By contrast, no severe or critical cases occurred after GTR when the third ventricular floor was preserved. After STR in patients with floor destruction, severe diencephalic syndrome occurred in one patient. Because the STR + floor destruction subgroup was small, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously.

It is important to clarify the novelty of the present findings. The concept of tailoring the extent of resection according to hypothalamic involvement and third ventricular floor status has already been emphasized by previous authors, including Pascual, Prieto, Deopujari and colleagues [1, 2, 5, 9]. Therefore, the novelty of the present study does not lie in proposing floor preservation as an entirely new surgical principle. Rather, the contribution of this study is the application of an early postoperative severity grading system for acute diencephalic syndrome in an adult cohort and its integration with early postoperative risk stratification and intensive care decision-making.

Clinical relevance of the grading scale and intensive care management

The proposed grading scale may be clinically useful because acute diencephalic syndrome is a dynamic process. Mild or moderate manifestations may progress to severe or critical stages if water-electrolyte instability, endocrine insufficiency, autonomic dysfunction, hyperthermia, or respiratory deterioration are not recognized early. In the present cohort, two patients with stage IV disease developed fatal multiorgan failure despite intensive care treatment.

However, the scale should be regarded as an exploratory clinical severity tool rather than a fully validated classification. Several parameters included in the scale, such as impaired consciousness, fever, autonomic instability, and respiratory disturbance, may have causes other than hypothalamic dysfunction. Therefore, postoperative hematoma, infarction, hydrocephalus progression, infection, medication-related sedation, opioid effect, antiepileptic drug effect, cardiopulmonary complications, and unrelated metabolic disturbances should be excluded before assigning these abnormalities to acute diencephalic syndrome.

Steroid administration is another important consideration. In patients receiving perioperative glucocorticoids, serum cortisol and ACTH values may be difficult to interpret and should not be used as isolated determinants of diencephalic syndrome severity. In such cases, staging should rely more heavily on clinical status, serum sodium, urine output, urine specific gravity, diabetes insipidus, hemodynamic stability, respiratory pattern, and imaging evidence of hypothalamic or third ventricular floor involvement.

Based on our clinical experience, the grading scale may support a stage-based management framework. Stage I may require close observation and prevention of water-electrolyte imbalance. Stage II may justify escalation of monitoring, including strict urine output and sodium control. Stage III should prompt intensive care management with active correction of endocrine, electrolyte, hemodynamic, and respiratory abnormalities. Stage IV requires maximal intensive support, including ventilatory and cardiovascular support when necessary. This approach requires prospective validation before it can be recommended as a standard protocol.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design carries an inherent risk of selection bias and information bias. Second, the choice of surgical approach was not randomized. The transcortical group included a significantly higher proportion of large tumors and severe hydrocephalus, which may have influenced postoperative seizure rate, cognitive outcome, and overall recovery. Therefore, comparisons between the transcallosal and transcortical approaches should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Third, the number of outcome events was limited, particularly for new-onset seizures and severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome. For this reason, conventional multivariable logistic regression and propensity score matching were not performed because of the risk of model instability and overfitting. Tumor size, hydrocephalus, and third ventricular floor status were therefore considered in the interpretation of the results rather than through formal adjusted modelling.

Fourth, cognitive assessment was performed using the MMSE only. The MMSE is a screening tool and does not allow detailed evaluation of executive function, memory, attention, processing speed, or other domain-specific neuropsychological deficits. In addition, cognitive testing was performed during the early postoperative period, when performance may be influenced by medication effects, fatigue, pain, electrolyte disturbances, and the general postoperative condition. Later follow-up neuropsychological data were not available for all patients.

Fifth, preoperative endocrine and hypothalamic manifestations, including cortisol/ACTH status, thyroid-axis dysfunction, diabetes insipidus, sleep–wake disturbance, and memory complaints, were not uniformly documented in all patients because of the retrospective design. Similarly, tumor consistency and intraoperative neuromonitoring data were not available in a standardized manner across the entire cohort.

Sixth, the proposed diencephalic syndrome grading scale has not yet been externally validated. Formal inter-rater reliability testing was not performed, and assessors were not blinded to surgical approach or postoperative course. In addition, several clinical parameters included in the scale may be influenced by non-hypothalamic causes. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the scale’s diagnostic and prognostic value.

Finally, several subgroups were small, particularly the STR + floor destruction subgroup. Therefore, percentage-based comparisons in these subgroups should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts, standardized endocrine and neuropsychological assessment, predefined imaging criteria for hypothalamic involvement, and external validation of the grading scale are needed.

Conclusion

The early postoperative period after microsurgical treatment of adult intraventricular craniopharyngiomas was associated with early MMSE decline ≥ 3 points in 27% of patients, new-onset seizures in 15%, and acute diencephalic syndrome in 76%.

Approach-related differences in MMSE decline and seizure incidence were numerical and did not reach statistical significance. MMSE decline was more frequent after the transcallosal approach, whereas seizures were more frequent after the transcortical approach; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of baseline differences between the groups, particularly tumor size and hydrocephalus.

Severe-to-critical diencephalic syndrome occurred only in patients with destruction of the third ventricular floor and was most frequent after gross-total resection in this subgroup. No severe or critical diencephalic syndrome was observed when the third ventricular floor was preserved.

The proposed four-stage grading scale may be useful for early postoperative monitoring and risk stratification, but it should be regarded as an exploratory clinical tool requiring prospective validation. Overall, surgical approach, extent of resection, and postoperative intensive care should be individualized according to tumor anatomy, hydrocephalus, third ventricular floor status, and hypothalamic risk.
