Perceptions, practices, and barriers in surgical informed consent: A cross-sectional study from Sudan
Authors: Ibrahim Nagmeldin Hassan, Mohamed Ibrahim, Siddig Yaqub, Muhsin Ibrahim, Haythem Abdalla, Ghada Aljaili, Wafa Osman, Nagmeldin Abuassa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Structured abstractBackgroundInformed consent is a cornerstone of ethical surgical practice, yet significant challenges persist in ensuring patients' comprehension, particularly in low-resource settings. Cultural norms, literacy barriers, and limited institutional support often hinder truly informed decision-making.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from September to December 2024 at a rural surgical center in Omdurman, Sudan. A total of 422 adult patients undergoing elective surgery were interviewed postoperatively using a validated, culturally adapted questionnaire. Descriptive and inferential statistics were applied to assess patient demographics, perceptions of the informed consent process, and associated barriers.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 42.0 ± 14.3 years, with a near-equal gender distribution. Only 17.1% of patients signed their own surgical consent forms, with 80.6% of these self-signers being male. Among those whose consent was signed by relatives (82.9%), females were overrepresented (56.6%). While 91.5% recognized the importance of informed consent, only 33.6% understood its medico-legal significance. Consent explanations were predominantly delivered by residents or house officers (62.1%), and just 20.1% of patients felt that the discussion influenced their surgical decision. Self-signers were more likely to recall discussion of surgical complications (75.0% vs. 51.4%; p < 0.001), less likely to recall expected benefits (61.1% vs. 78.9%; p = 0.001), and showed no significant difference for consequences if surgery was not performed (80.6% vs. 82.9%; p = 1.0). Overall satisfaction with the consent process was high (87.7%), though this did not correlate with comprehension. Educational status significantly influenced autonomy, with illiterate participants disproportionately less likely to sign their own forms and more likely to cite language barriers and lack of information (p < 0.05).ConclusionDespite high reported satisfaction, substantial deficiencies exist in patients’ comprehension and autonomy in the informed consent process in Sudan. Gender disparities, literacy limitations, and systemic reliance on junior staff compromise the ethical validity of consent. Interventions tailored to cultural and educational contexts—such as provider training, simplified materials, and patient-centered communication—are urgently needed to enhance informed surgical decision-making in low-resource environments.

Introduction

Informed consent is a fundamental ethical and legal requirement in surgical practice, ensuring that patients are active participants in decisions about their healthcare. Despite its significance, the process often falls short, with many patients lacking a comprehensive understanding of the procedures they consent to. Studies have shown that patients frequently do not fully grasp the information presented during the consent process, leading to decisions that may not be truly informed [1].

Health literacy plays a pivotal role in this dynamic. A substantial portion of surgical patients possess limited health literacy, which impedes their ability to comprehend complex medical information. Research indicates that low health literacy is associated with poorer understanding of surgical procedures, risks, and alternatives, ultimately affecting patient outcomes [2]. Moreover, the readability of consent forms often exceeds the average patient's reading level, further complicating comprehension [3].

Cultural and linguistic factors also contribute to the challenges in obtaining truly informed consent. Patients from diverse backgrounds may face language barriers and cultural differences that hinder effective communication with healthcare providers. These barriers can lead to misunderstandings and a lack of clarity regarding surgical procedures [4]. The use of medical interpreters has been suggested as a means to bridge these gaps, yet studies reveal that even with interpretation services, patients may still experience inadequate understanding [5].

To address these issues, various interventions have been explored. The "teach-back" method, where patients are asked to repeat the information in their own words, has shown promise in enhancing comprehension [6]. Additionally, multimedia tools and simplified consent forms have been employed to improve patient understanding, with some success [7]. However, the effectiveness of these interventions varies, and more research is needed to determine the most effective strategies for different patient populations.

In low- and middle-income countries, the challenges are often exacerbated by limited resources and educational disparities. Studies have highlighted that in such settings, patients frequently lack the necessary information to make informed decisions about surgical procedures [8]. This underscores the need for context-specific solutions that consider the unique barriers faced by patients in these regions.

Given these complexities, this study aims to assess the effectiveness of current informed consent practices in surgical settings, identify barriers to patient understanding, and explore strategies to enhance the consent process. By addressing these issues, we hope to contribute to the development of more effective, patient-centered approaches to informed consent in surgery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a hospital-based, cross-sectional study conducted over a four-month period from September to December 2024 at a rural surgical center in Omdurman, Sudan. The center serves a large catchment area composed primarily of rural and peri‑urban populations, and offers a variety of elective surgical procedures across general and specialized surgical services. The study was designed to assess perceptions, practices, and potential barriers associated with the informed consent process among patients undergoing elective surgery.

Study population

The study population included all adult patients, aged 18 years and above, who were admitted for elective surgical procedures during the study period. Inclusion was irrespective of gender, educational status, or type of surgery, provided the patient was mentally and physically able to participate. Patients were excluded if they were undergoing emergency surgical procedures, had psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment, suffered from significant hearing impairment, or were experiencing severe pain or distress that limited their ability to comprehend and engage in the interview process.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for estimating a single population proportion, assuming a 95 % confidence level, an expected proportion of patients who consider informed consent important of 91 %, and a 5 % margin of error. This yielded a minimum required sample size of 126 participants. However, to enhance statistical power, allow for subgroup analyses, and account for potential non-response or incomplete data, the final target sample was increased to 422 patients. A consecutive sampling method was used, where all eligible patients presenting for elective surgery during the study period were enrolled until the required sample size of 422 was reached.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a validated and culturally adapted questionnaire, which was slightly modified for local relevance and pre-tested prior to use. Permission to utilize and adapt the original instrument was obtained, and the final version was administered only to patients who met the eligibility criteria and consented to participate. The questionnaire was developed in Arabic and reviewed by a panel of local clinicians and medical ethics experts to ensure content validity and contextual appropriateness. It consisted of three sections: (1) demographic information including age, sex, marital status, educational background, and literacy level; (2) details regarding the informed consent process such as who explained the surgery, who signed the consent form, where it was obtained, and what information was conveyed; and (3) patient perceptions, attitudes, and potential barriers to signing the consent form independently. A pilot test involving 20 patients was conducted prior to the main study to assess clarity, flow, and reliability of the questionnaire, following which minor adjustments were made. In this hospital, informed consent is routinely documented using a standard paper form provided by the Ministry of Health. The form follows a basic template in Arabic, is completed manually by the attending doctor, and includes fields for the patient's or relative’s signature, procedure type, and name of the responsible surgeon. It is typically handwritten, and no institutionalized version of a digital or pre-printed consent form is in use. No blank sample could be attached due to ethical and institutional restrictions regarding hospital forms.

Data collection procedure

Eligible patients were approached during the postoperative period, typically between 48 and 72 h after their surgical procedure. Interviews were conducted in a quiet and private space within the surgical ward to ensure patient comfort and promote honest responses. Data collection was carried out by junior doctors who had been trained in research ethics, interview techniques, and standardized administration of the questionnaire. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min. Responses were recorded on paper-based forms, which were anonymized, securely stored, and later digitized for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/spss-statistics/26.0.0) Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics and key responses. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Chi-square tests were employed to evaluate associations between patient characteristics (e.g., gender, education, signature status) and outcomes related to the informed consent process. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Khartoum. All participants provided written informed consent before being enrolled in the study. The purpose of the study was explained in clear and simple language, and participants were assured that their decision to participate or decline would not influence their medical care. Confidentiality was strictly maintained, and no identifiable information was collected on the data forms. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies (Supplementary Table S1) [9].

Results

Participant demographics and consent form signatory patterns

The mean age of the participants was 42.0 ± 14.27 years (range: 18–85 years). Of the 422 patients included, 210 (49.8 %) were males and 212 (50.2 %) were females. The majority were married (288; 68.2 %), while the remaining 134 (31.8 %) were single. In terms of educational attainment, 60 (14.2 %) were illiterate, 78 (18.5 %) had completed primary education, 114 (27.0 %) had secondary education, and 170 (40.3 %) had attained education at the intermediate level or higher.

A total of 72 (17.1 %) patients signed their consent form themselves, of whom 58 (80.6 %) were males and 14 (19.4 %) were females. Among the 350 (82.9 %) consent forms signed by relatives, the signatories were parents (n = 20; 5.7 %), spouses (n = 160; 45.7 %), their children (n = 60; 17.1 %), and their siblings (n = 110; 31.4 %). Of those whose consent was signed by relatives, 152 (43.4 %) were males and 198 (56.6 %) were females.

Patient perceptions and satisfaction with the informed consent process

A total of 422 patients who underwent elective surgery were included in the study. Of these, 210 (49.8 %) were males and 212 (50.2 %) were females, with a mean age of 42.0 ± 14.27 years. The majority of respondents were married (68.2 %) and had received at least some formal education (74.4 %).

As shown in (Table 1), informed consent was explained by a resident or house officer in 62.1 % of cases, while consultant surgeons were reported as the primary source of consent explanation in 37.9 % of patients. Male and female participants reported similar patterns, and no statistically significant difference was observed between genders (p = 0.233). The location where information was provided varied: the ward was most commonly cited (52.1 %), followed by the clinic (25.4 %) and the operating theatre (22.5 %). Females were more likely than males to receive preoperative information in the operating theatre (30.7 % vs. 14.3 %, p = 0.001). Similarly, consent was most often obtained in the ward (60.7 %), with females more frequently signing in the operating theatre compared to males (44.8 % vs. 21.4 %, p = 0.001). With regard to timing, the majority of participants reported that informed consent was obtained on the same day as their scheduled surgery, often just hours before being taken to the operating theatre. Only a small proportion of patients recalled receiving consent explanations during earlier preoperative assessments. Only 20.1 % of participants stated that the consent process influenced their decision to proceed with surgery, with no significant difference between males and females (p = 0.076). Most participants (85.3 %) indicated that their decision was made independently, though a minority were influenced by doctors (9.5 %) or family/friends (5.2 %). Importantly, 91.5 % of participants considered informed consent to be important, with similar responses from both sexes. However, only 33.6 % were aware of the medico-legal significance of the consent process, and this awareness was higher among males than females (38.1 % vs. 29.2 %, p = 0.054).

When asked about their preferred amount of information, 69.2 % of respondents favored receiving detailed explanations, and a majority (57.3 %) would still prefer detailed information if undergoing the same surgery again. Notably, this preference did not differ significantly by gender (p = 0.059), with males more likely to want detailed information on repeat surgeries. Overall, 87.7 % of patients were satisfied with the information provided, and 95.7 % expressed a favorable view of the consent process. Men were more often in favor of the consent process than women (99.0 % vs. 92.5 %; p < 0.001). Confidentiality and privacy were reportedly maintained in 85.3 % and 73.5 % of cases, respectively. Reports of privacy being maintained did not differ significantly by gender (p = 0.206). Finally, the vast majority (94.8 %) reported that doctors answered their questions satisfactorily.

Regarding information about hospital stay duration, 69.4 % of self-signers and 57.1 % of relative-signers affirmed being informed, though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.053). A higher proportion of self-signers (73.6 %) also reported being informed about the type of anesthesia, compared to 81.4 % of relative-signers (p = 0.271). Discussion

This study provides important insights into the perceptions, practices, and barriers surrounding surgical informed consent in a Sudanese hospital, offering implications for low-resource settings. The findings reflect a complex interplay between patient autonomy, literacy, gender roles, and institutional approaches to consent.

Only 17.1 % of patients signed their own consent forms, while 82.9 % were signed by relatives. This high rate of proxy signing reflects structural and cultural dynamics—likely shaped by literacy barriers, gender norms, and a paternalistic model of clinical decision-making. Among self-signers, 80.6 % were male, highlighting a notable gender disparity. These findings suggest that women may be systematically excluded or discouraged from participating in consent-related discussions. Similar patterns have been reported in Tanzania and Ethiopia, where cultural hierarchies influence who gives consent in surgical settings [10,11]. Although 91.5 % of participants recognized the importance of the informed consent process, only 33.6 % understood its medico-legal implications. This reveals a significant gap between perceived understanding and actual comprehension, raising concerns about the ethical validity of the consent obtained. Comparable findings have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where patients often equate consent with a procedural formality [12].

Patients who signed their own consent forms demonstrated greater awareness of critical domains—such as surgical nature, risks, benefits, and alternatives. For instance, 75.0 % of self-signers understood the complications compared to 51.4 % of those whose relatives signed (p = 0.005). This supports previous evidence that direct patient involvement is associated with improved comprehension and satisfaction [13]. Notably, 88 % of patients expressed satisfaction with the information provided. However, satisfaction does not necessarily imply comprehension. Research shows that trust in healthcare providers can lead patients to report satisfaction despite limited understanding [14], which has ethical implications for evaluating consent quality.

Educational level significantly influenced the consent experience. Illiterate patients reported more barriers—such as language difficulties, time constraints, and lack of interpretable information. Prior studies confirm that low literacy hinders informed consent in LMICs, where verbal explanations often substitute for written documentation [15,16]. Despite comprising only 14.2 % of the sample, illiterate participants were disproportionately unable to self-sign, reinforcing the critical link between literacy and autonomy. Additionally, the issue of insufficient time is particularly concerning, as rushed consent processes compromise the patient’s opportunity to ask questions, process information, and engage in meaningful shared decision-making. When consent is obtained on the day of surgery, patients may feel pressured to comply rather than reflect critically on the information provided.

The challenges uncovered in this study mirror findings from other African contexts. In Uganda, Wismayer reported that nurses, often lacking procedural knowledge, were responsible for obtaining consent—undermining patient autonomy [17]. A scoping review by Pittalis et al. across sub-Saharan Africa identified systemic gaps, including poor documentation, lack of standardized forms, and minimal patient awareness. These studies consistently characterize consent as a legal obligation rather than a shared decision-making process [18]. In Ethiopia, Mihiretu et al. found that only 32 % of surgical patients had adequate knowledge of informed consent, and less than 40 % had a positive perception of it. Education level and previous surgical exposure were key predictors of better understanding—echoing our findings [19]. Similarly, Naidu and Gopalan in South Africa reported that patients often lacked information about anesthesia, alternatives, and surgical risks, and relied exclusively on verbal communication [20]. These parallels highlight the risks of rushed, provider-centered discussions and the benefits of direct patient engagement, as shown by the improved awareness among self-signers in our study.

Globally, challenges persist even in high-income countries. A review by de Costa et al. found that junior doctors in Australia and the UK often lacked the training and confidence to obtain valid surgical consent [21]. In our study, over 60 % of consents were obtained by residents or house officers, raising similar concerns about the adequacy of information conveyed. Fink et al. demonstrated that the “repeat-back” method significantly improved comprehension, especially in patients with low literacy or anxiety [22]. This method could be feasibly adapted in Sudan through verbal scripts and patient recall prompts. Conroy et al. emphasized the value of personalized, empathetic consent discussions, although time pressures and hierarchical systems frequently undermine such interactions [23]. In our study, only 20.1 % of patients felt the consent discussion impacted their decision-making, suggesting a perception of consent as routine rather than informative. Schenker et al., in a review of 44 studies, found that interventions such as multimedia tools, simplified forms, and dedicated sessions improved understanding [24]. While multimedia may not be feasible in low-resource settings, simplified visual aids and structured verbal explanations remain practical. Lin et al. highlighted that trauma patients often fail to recall consent details due to stress and urgency [25]. However, with the increasing penetration of smartphones and internet access even in low-resource settings, the potential of digital consent platforms warrants exploration. These tools—ranging from mobile-based videos to interactive digital forms—can enhance comprehension and standardize content delivery across settings [26]. While our study involved elective cases, this underscores the importance of timing, setting, and a calm environment—particularly for female patients, who reported more discomfort and less engagement.

The predominance of proxy signing underscores cultural and structural barriers to patient autonomy. In many sub-Saharan African societies, family-based decision-making is the norm. While culturally resonant, this can inadvertently exclude vulnerable groups, particularly women and those with lower education. Culturally sensitive consent processes that respect communal values while affirming individual rights are essential [27]. Our data reveal a profound gap in understanding the consent process, especially its legal and ethical dimensions. This gap was widest among patients with limited education. Health literacy interventions—such as simplified language, visual aids, and “teach-back” techniques—have proven effective in improving comprehension in similar contexts [28]. These strategies can and should be adapted for resource-limited Sudanese hospitals.

The reliance on junior doctors for consent discussions raises concerns. Training in communication and ethics—especially in surgical and oncologic contexts—has been shown to improve clinician performance and patient outcomes [29]. Incorporating such training into residency curricula is essential. To address systemic shortcomings, hospitals should implement structured, context-appropriate protocols. The U.S. National Academies recommend strategies such as standardized documentation, verification of patient understanding, and continuous feedback mechanisms [30]. Finally, our findings support the need for further research into culturally adapted, literacy-sensitive interventions. Studies should evaluate outcomes such as comprehension, recall, and satisfaction, particularly in LMICs [31].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its substantial sample size of 422 participants, which enhances the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The inclusion of diverse demographic groups—across age, gender, education level, and marital status—enables nuanced subgroup analysis and improves external validity. Such diversity has been recognized as essential for ethical and equity-driven health research, particularly in informed consent assessments [32]. Moreover, by focusing on elective surgical patients, the study avoids the confounding factors typically associated with emergency settings, such as time constraints and acute stress, which can compromise the validity of consent. This approach allows for a clearer appraisal of routine consent practices and patient comprehension under standard conditions [33].

However, the study is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences or evaluation of temporal trends in informed consent practices. Longitudinal studies would be better suited to assessing the effects of interventions over time [34]. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for recall bias, especially concerning whether specific elements of the consent process were discussed. This issue is well documented in consent research and can skew reported levels of satisfaction and comprehension [35]. Third, the study was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other care settings where resources and consent practices may differ significantly [36]. Additionally, the use of junior doctors as interviewers may have introduced social desirability bias, wherein patients could have overstated their satisfaction with the consent process out of politeness or perceived obligation. Although the study included patients undergoing various elective procedures, specific surgical specialties (e.g., obstetrics, general, orthopedic) were not recorded systematically. This limits our ability to correlate gender-specific patterns (e.g., higher female presence in theatre) with procedure type and should be addressed in future research. Lastly, while the study assessed patient perceptions and understanding, it did not evaluate the actual content or readability of the consent forms themselves. Previous reports have shown that many institutional consent forms lack essential details, use complex language, or fail to present alternative treatment options, thereby undermining their utility as tools for informed decision-making [37].

Conclusion

This study highlights substantial gaps in the surgical informed consent process in Sudan, driven by structural, educational, and cultural barriers. While most patients acknowledged the importance of informed consent, only a minority demonstrated adequate understanding of its components and legal implications. Factors such as limited health literacy, reliance on proxy decision-makers, and inconsistent communication by junior healthcare staff contributed to suboptimal consent practices.

To uphold ethical standards and patient autonomy, informed consent must evolve beyond procedural formalities. Targeted strategies—including provider training, simplified materials, and culturally adapted communication—are urgently needed. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions across diverse healthcare settings, with a focus on improving both patient comprehension and shared decision-making.
