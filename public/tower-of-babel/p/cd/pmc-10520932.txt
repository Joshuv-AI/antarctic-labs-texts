Sustainable conversion of cottonseed hulls to valuable proanthocyanidins through ultrasound-assisted deep eutectic solvent extraction
Authors: Jing Cui, Dan Fang, Xinquan Tian, Jun Peng, Di Chen, Shuangjiao Xu, Lei Ma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Novel UAE-DES converts CSH to PAs via RSM.•UAE-DES had the highest extractability compared with other methods.•MDS shows strong DES-CA interactions, enhancing extraction.•Residues (CSR) form activated carbon (ACCSR) for MB adsorption.•Study guides waste biomass to high-value products.

Introduction

The cotton industry is a highly relevant, long industrial chain with a high degree of commercialization in social industries. However, the development of the cotton industry has led to a large amount of cotton waste being generated during the production and processing of cotton products. Cottonseed, a byproduct of cotton processing, provides a valuable raw material for achieving goals of full utilization and enhancement of value-added products such as cottonseed oil, cottonseed protein, cottonseed hull (CSH), cotton staple fibre, cottonseed oligosaccharides, and cotton acetate. These products are essential in various fields, such as the food industry, livestock feed, and medicine [1].

In China alone, the annual output of cottonseed hulls (CSHs) is 1.9–2.1 million t, of which approximately 400,000 t is used as feed and the remaining 1.5 million t is used as a growth substrate for edible mushrooms. However, the rough processing method restricts the use of cottonseed and results in low comprehensive benefits. Converting biomass into biobased materials can sequester the CO2 fixed by plants and thus is a carbon-negative biomaterial utilization mode [2]. It has been reported that cotton seed hulls can also be prepared into various forms of porous carbon materials, which have broad application prospects in new types of energy and in environmental protection [3], [4]. Cotton seed hulls, also known as testae, are thick, hard, and covered with short-fibred lint. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, the seed coat is the main organ that accumulates proanthocyanidin natural products. Thus, in cotton, with low-cost seed hulls as the primary target, exploring the extraction of bioactive components, analysing their antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, preparing pyrolysis products, and exploring the adsorption and reuse of modified biochar may make it possible to achieve zero-waste multistage utilization of cotton biomass [5]. At present, there are few studies on the bioactive components of cotton waste; however, a previous study showed that flavonoid compounds were extracted from cotton petals, and their composition was identified and quantified, providing new opportunities for the utilization of cotton waste [6]. Cottonseed hulls, as a highly productive but under-utilized biomass resource, can also be utilized to enhance value through the extraction of bioactive compounds.

Proanthocyanidins (PAs) have emerged as significant players due to their potency as bioactive compounds in recent years. PAs are polyphenol compounds that exist as oligomers or polymers of polyhydroxy flavan-3-alcohol (such as (+)-catechin (CA) and (-)-epicatechin) and flavan-3,4-alcohol, which are connected by either a single C4-C8 or C4-C6 bond (B-type PAs) or an extra C2-O-C7 or C2-O-C5 bond (A-type PAs). PAs are widely recognized for their strong antioxidant properties, attributed to the structure of PAs, and are widespread in the plant kingdom, occurring in plant materials such as grape seeds, mangoes, and strawberries [7], [8], [9], [10]. PAs rank as the second most abundant natural phenolic compound after lignin [11]. Moreover, PAs exhibit research-worthy characteristics, such as antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-lowering properties. While grape seeds have been utilized in the development of a range of products, such as grape seed oil and grape seed proanthocyanidin extracts [12], it is important to note that cottonseed hulls have distinct advantages over grape seeds due to their larger size and ease of accessibility. Therefore, exploring the extraction of proanthocyanidins from cottonseed hulls represents a valuable avenue for investigation.

Methods for extracting and isolating bioactive substances from various plant materials are continually advancing. The traditional solvent extraction method, initially utilizing water as the solvent, has evolved to incorporate a wider range of organic solvents. Moreover, auxiliary extraction techniques employing tools, as well as technologies such as heating, pressurization, ultrasound, and microwave, have been introduced. Emerging methodologies, including supercritical extraction, solid-phase microextraction, aqueous enzymatic methods, ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvent (DES) extraction, are also being developed [13], [14].

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) are an emerging class of organic solvents that represent a novel class of compounds formed by combining different hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) [15]. These DES components, such as choline chloride (ChCl), urea, glycerol, lactic acid, carbohydrates, polyols, amino acids, and vitamins, are derived from renewable sources. Consequently, DESs exhibit excellent biodegradability and minimal to no toxicity. Due to the minimal ecological footprint, cost-effectiveness in production, and customizable physicochemical properties of DESs, they are gradually replacing hazardous and volatile organic compounds commonly used across various fields [16], [17], [18]. The physical properties of DESs can be regulated by selecting their components and HBD: HBA ratios. Such eutectic mixtures are characterized by simple preparation, low volatility, strong solubility at high temperatures, and correspondingly tuneable polarity [19]. Therefore, the effects of factors such as the ratio of HBA to HBD, water content, and temperature should be taken into account in the experiments.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) harnesses ultrasound energy to induce mechanical fragmentation and cavitation effects within plant cells, thus accelerating solvent diffusion and reducing extraction time [12]. When combined with a DES, ultrasound promotes the dispersion and penetration of the sample in the solvent, increases the contact area, and promotes the release of the target substance from the sample. Due to the broad polarity range of DESs, they associate with the target macromolecules and form intermolecular hydrogen bonds, which fix them inside the solvent and increase the content of the target substances in the solvent, thus improving the extraction rate. The UAE-DES method demonstrates significantly higher extraction efficacy in extracting active ingredients from liquorice compared to methods such as water-UAE [20]. The extraction of proanthocyanidins by UAE-DES is an attempt to maximize the high value-added utilization of cottonseed hulls. Moreover, the preparation and characterization of the biochar of the extracted residue aim to achieve the comprehensive zero-waste utilization of cottonseed hulls.

To achieve full-value utilization of cottonseed hulls, a specific protocol for the extraction of proanthocyanidins and the preparation of activated carbon from the extraction residue was proposed and optimized in this study. A green and simple method (UAE-DES) for the extraction of proanthocyanidins was optimized by response surface optimization, and the optimized extracts were compared with those obtained by the conventional method to determine the level of effectiveness of UAE-DES. To investigate the solvent–solute interaction, a molecular dynamics simulation study was carried out. Subsequently, the remaining residue was used to produce activated carbon, and adsorption kinetic studies were performed to determine its adsorption capacity. This integrated utilization approach has enabled us to achieve high-value extraction and zero-waste utilization of cottonseed hulls. By implementing this strategy, we are contributing to environmental protection to a large extent.

Extraction of PAs from CSHs

Ultrasonication was performed using an ultrasonic cell crusher (JY98-Ⅲ, power 1200 W; Scientz Biotechnology Co., LTD. China). The other parameters of the ultrasound device were fixed, the frequency automatically tracked the ultrasound power, and the random amplitude variation lever was ϕ20. The extraction process followed these steps: 0.5 g of CSHs was uniformly mixed with 10 mL of DES solution (prepared previously), achieving a liquid-to-material ratio of 20. In the extraction process, the pulse sequence interval was set to 2 s ON and 2 s OFF, and the probe tip was immersed into the solution by 2 cm. The temperature throughout the extraction process was controlled through the use of an ice-water bath. This was done by fixing the beaker containing the sample in a foam box with crushed ice and water and placing it in the apparatus. The obtained extract was transferred to a tube and centrifuged at 4500 ×g for 10 min using an Eppendorf centrifuge 5804. The supernatant was collected and then stored in refrigerated bottles. Filtered liquid portions were further diluted for flavonoid determination by high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS). The CSR was subsequently dried at 105 °C for 12 h and stored in a sealed bag for the preparation of activated carbon.

HPLC–MS/MS analysis

After diluting the extracted samples with methanol and filtering them through a 0.22 μm membrane, HPLC–MS/MS analysis was conducted using a SCIEX Qtrap 5500+ (SCIEX, USA) with sample separation through an ACQUITY BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 100 mm × 2.1 mm) (Milford, MA, USA) maintained at a constant temperature of 40 °C. The mobile phases employed included water with 0.1% acetic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B) and followed the gradient program as described in a previous study [6]. The flow rate was set at 0.25 mL/min, with an injection volume of 2 μL. The negative ion mode was controlled by Analyst version 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex), while MS/MS parameters such as CUR value of 25 psi, ESI temperature of 550°C, MS voltage of 5500 V, optimized DP, and CE (Table A1).

Determination of PAs

To quantify the PAs in the CSHs, a modified approach of the original butanol – HCl assay [23] was used, with a proanthocyanidin B2 standard. In this method, 250 μL of PA sample diluted in methanol (50 μL of PA extract fraction in 200 μL of methanol) and 250 μL of n-BuOH: HCl (95: 5, v/v) were mixed. The mixture was subsequently heated in a covered test tube in a water bath at 95°C for 1 h and cooled in ice water. Absorbance at 550 nm was recorded (TECAN SPARK 10 M Multifunctional Enzyme Labeler), and the PA content was calculated from a calibration curve established with a regression Equation y = 1.5416x + 0.0303 (R2 = 0.9925).

Optimization of PA extraction

Three independent variables, including water content (0, 10%, 30%, 50%, and 70%), extraction time (2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 min), and liquid-to-solid ratio (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mL/g), were determined for the preliminary range. Furthermore, to enhance the extraction yields of PAs, the Box–Behnken design (BBD) was employed to optimize the UAE-DES. The X1, X2, and X3 variables were chosen for further optimization at three levels: −1, 0, and + 1. Design-Expert Version 8.0.6.1 (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used for BBD generation and the assessment of experimental results. The optimal conditions were determined by evaluating the PA extraction yield (mg/g, Y).

After determining the optimal extraction conditions, the responses were measured. To assess the accuracy of the model, the obtained data were compared to the expected values.

MDS analysis

The molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the GRMOACS 2020.6 package [24]. The visualization of structures was performed by VMD software [25]. The molecules were mixed in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions by using the PACKMOL package [26]. The molecular number is displayed in Table A2.

The opls-AA Force Field was selected in this work. To generate the required force field parameters for the molecules, the LigParGen web was used [27], [28], [29].

Before starting the MD simulation, the initial configurations were relaxed using a conjugate gradient minimization scheme. The step size in the simulation was set to 0.01 nm, with a cycle of 5000 steps. Convergence of the minimization was determined by a minimum force of < 50 kJ/mol/nm. To calculate van der Waals interactions, the cut-off method was used, while atomic electrostatic interactions were calculated using PME (particle mesh Ewald). Both the cut-off and PME distances were set to 1.0 nm [30]. To achieve the desired density, the system was equilibrated with a pressure of 1.0 bar. Both the Berendsen and V-rescale methods were employed to control the pressure and temperature. The time constant was set to 1.0 ps, and the compressibility was 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1. For all systems, an equilibrium of 5 ns was achieved using a 0.001 ps time step. The production phase lasted 50 ns, with pressure control switched to the Parrinello-Rahman method. Furthermore, to constrain the hydrogen bond, we utilized the LINCS (Linear Constrain Solver) algorithm [31]. Using Multiwfn, the average noncovalent interaction (aNCI) between CA and various solvents was investigated [32].

The difference between UAE-DES and other extraction methods

To evaluate the effectiveness of the optimized DES extraction method, ChCl-leva was chosen as the extraction solvent, and the traditional solvents of 70% methanol, 70% ethanol, and 70% acetone were used as controls to compare the extraction effectiveness of various extraction methods, including stirring (S) and UAE [7], [33]. Referring to the methods of past research [33], the stirring conditions were kept constant with a solvent-to-solid ratio of 30:1 (v/w, mL/g) and an extraction time of 3 h. Based on the results of RSM, the optimized extraction time for UAE was adjusted to 7 min. The CSR, which included CSR treated with 70% methanol with stirring (S-M), 70% methanol with UAE (UAE-M), DES with stirring (S-DES), and UAE-DES, was dried and analysed.

Determination of antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of PAs extracted using optimal DES and conventional organic solvents was compared by examining the ability of the extracted PAs to scavenge DPPH and ABTS free radicals [34], [35], [36].

Then, 0.2 mL of the sample was added to 0.5 mL of DPPH methanol solution, the mixture was allowed to react in the dark for 30 min, and the absorbance at 515 nm was subsequently measured. To produce ABTS free radicals for the scavenging activity experiment, 7.4 mmol/L ABTS solution and 2.6 mmol/L K2S2O8 were combined in a 1:1 vol ratio. The combination was then allowed to react for 12 to 16 h while being kept in the dark. The solution was then diluted 30-fold with methanol. Next, 0.2 mL of the sample was added to 0.5 mL of the ABTS free radical solution, and the mixture was allowed to react for 6 min. Finally, the absorbance at 734 nm was measured. The DPPH or ABTS radical scavenging capability was calculated using the following equation:clearancerate=A0-A1/A0×100%.where A0 is the absorbance of the blank and A1 is the absorbance in the presence of PAs.

Observation of surface structure

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi, SU3500, Japan) was used to observe the morphology of the powders in this study.

Activated carbon production

The one-step chemical activation method is known for its simplicity and high preparation effectiveness, as reported in previous research [37]. Therefore, for this study, ACCSR was prepared using a one-step phosphoric acid activation process. The CSR (20 g) and the 50% phosphoric acid solution were combined at a mass ratio of 1:2.2, evenly impregnated, and dried until a constant weight was reached. Next, the raw material was heated in a tube furnace (OTF-1200X, China) at a rate of 10°C·min−1 until it reached 350°C and then was kept at this temperature for 60 min. The product was pickled with 35 mL of a 3 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution after cooling, and it was then repeatedly washed at 80°C with deionized water until it reached a pH neutral state before being dried in an oven at 105°C for 24 h to produce the ACCSR. The ACCSR sample was then sealed for storage and further research to study its adsorption performance and characterization.

Adsorption of MB solution

The methylene blue (MB) content was measured by the spectrophotometric method. Briefly, the solution was determined at various time intervals (0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 90, 105, 135, 165, 195, and 1070 min), and the absorbance at 634 nm was recorded. Through the use of a calibration curve created using a regression equation (y = 1.5416x + 0.0303 (R2 = 0.9925)), the MB content was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Standard deviation calculations were performed using IBM SPSS 24.0 statistical software (IBM, USA), and images were created using GraphPad Prism 8 and Origin 2018 software.

Results and discussion

Screening of DESs

To determine the concentration of active substances in CSHs and compare the differences in the capacity of various DESs to extract them, 14 DES extracts were analysed with the methods presented in subsection 2.4, and Fig. 1 depicts the results.Fig. 1Heat map of the yields of the main active compounds in CSH extracted with different solvents.

Heat map of the yields of the main active compounds in CSH extracted with different solvents.

Among the different extracts, 12 bioactive substances were successfully extracted and detected, including phenolic acids (such as protocatechuic acid and caffeic acid), flavonols (such as kaempferol, quercetin, nicotiflorin, tiliroside, and rutin), dihydroflavonoids (such as naringenin), and flavanols (such as catechin and epicatechin). Notably, catechin and epicatechin were the most abundant compounds observed. As essential monomers for the synthesis of PAs, catechin and epicatechin are widely distributed in plants, especially in tea, where they account for 12% – 24% of the plant dry weight [38].

DES-7 exhibited the highest extraction effectiveness for catechin and epicatechin, with values of 101.85 and 58.11 μg/g, respectively. These values were much higher than those obtained from methanol extraction, which yielded 15.24 μg/g of catechin and 8.70 μg/g of epicatechin. DES-12 followed as the next most effective extract for catechin and epicatechin, with values of 88.15 μg/g and 49.90 μg/g, respectively. However, the difference was that the extraction effectiveness of DES-7 was significantly higher than that of DES-12 for protocatechuic acid (48.81 > 20.58 μg/g) and rutin (31.60 > 0.14 μg/g). This distinction may be attributed to the fact that both DES-7 and DES-12 are synthesized from choline chloride and levulinic acid but at different ratios of 1:2 and 1:1.5, respectively. DES-2 and DES-16 also showed higher extraction effectiveness for catechin compared to methanol, with values of 32.04 and 44.04 μg/g, respectively. Moreover, DES-2 demonstrated enhanced extraction capacity for quercetin (49.39 μg/g), while DES-16 exhibited higher extraction effectiveness for protocatechuic acid (32.76 μg/g). These findings can be attributed to the fact that DES is composed of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, which can form hydrogen bonds between DES solvent molecules and phenolic compounds, thus increasing the solubility of phenolics in the solvent [39]. Additionally, the structure and ratio of DES components also affect the binding capacity to phenolic compounds.

Based on these results, DES-7 was selected as the solvent for the extraction of PAs and the subsequent optimization of the extraction process.

Characterization of DESs

DESs are created as a result of hydrogen-bond interactions between HBA and HBD. The FTIR spectra of ChCl, Leva, and ChCl-Leva were measured to examine the hydrogen bonds that exist between the two substances. The results of the FTIR analysis of ChCl, Leva, and their mixtures without water, is displayed in Fig. 2. The O-H or N-H group had a broad peak between 3650 and 3200 cm−1. However, because the O-H and N-H bonds overlapped, only a single combined peak was observed. The C = O bonds of Leva were represented by the peak at approximately 1706 cm−1, which was redshifted due to the influence of the –OH group in DES formation [40]. Both Leva and the DES showed C–O–H vibrations at 1162 cm−1[41]. However, the DES demonstrated several frequency shifts and band width variations when compared to ChCl or Leva alone. For example, for the DES, the stretching vibration absorption peak of the hydroxyl group between 3650 and 3300 cm−1 was wider than that in ChCl or Leva. These spectral observations confirm that DES formation is a physical process and does not involve the creation of new chemical bonds.Fig. 2FTIR spectra of ChCl (a), Leva (b), and ChCl-Leva (c).

Single-factor experiments

The yield of extracted PAs may be influenced by various factors [41]. To establish the linkages between extraction conditions and the desired components, single-factor experiments were conducted to evaluate the impact of sonication time, liquid-to-solid ratio, and water content on the PA yield.

Effect of sonication time

The extraction sonication time is an important parameter to optimize to minimize the process’s energy cost. The extraction time was studied within the range of 2 12 min. The results presented in Fig. 3(1) show that the extraction yield (Y, mg/g) increased from 52.76 ± 3.19 to 75.01 ± 2.46 mg/g within the time range of 2 – 6 min, with only a slight decrease observed between 6 and 12 min. The ultrasonic action induces the DES to form tiny droplets, which accelerates the interaction between the DES and the sample. However, when a certain time point is reached, the number of tiny droplets formed by the DES reaches a maximum, while the UAE-DES extraction effectiveness reaches its peak. At this point, the ultrasonic time becomes optimal [42]. For certain samples, such as those of flavonoid compounds, prolonged extraction times may lead to changes in their internal chemical structures. This instability in the interaction between the DES and the sample results in decreased extraction effectiveness [43]. Conversely, shorter extraction times may result in incomplete extraction, while longer extraction times lead to increased energy consumption. It is worth mentioning that the extracts obtained at 6 min had good flowability and did not suspend excess residue after centrifugation. Effect of water content

As depicted in Fig. 3(2), the ChCl-Leva with 30% water content showed higher PA extraction yields compared to other water contents. This was because adding water to a solution could lower its viscosity and regulate its polarity, which contributed to accelerating the rate at which compounds dissolve. However, too much water reduced the extraction yield, as it diminished the interactions between the DES and target components [44], [45]. After evaluating the extraction PA yield, it was determined that ChCl-Leva with a water content of 30% was the most suitable for the subsequent extraction experiments.

Effect of liquid–solid ratio

The impact of a liquid–solid ratio of 20:1 – 100:1 was investigated. The findings presented in Fig. 3 (3) demonstrate that the extraction yield (Y, mg/g) increased as the liquid–solid ratio increased from 20:1 to 40:1, but there was no visible enhancement when exceeding this ratio. By increasing the amount of extractant, the extraction effectiveness of target compounds in the media was enhanced. However, an excessive amount of solvent may result in wasted resources and potentially have an adverse impact on the extraction process. To strike a balance, a liquid–solid ratio of 40:1 was chosen for further studies.

After analysing the results of the single-factor experiments, the response surface method identified water content (X1), liquid–solid ratio (X2), and sonication time (X3) as the key factors that need to be optimized for achieving the maximum PA yield extraction yield from CSH.

The applicability of the model was assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), as shown in Table 3. The F value obtained from the model was 42.71, and the p-value was determined to be p < 0.0001, indicating excellent accuracy and applicability in explaining the relationship between the predicted and experimental values of the PA extract. The quadratic model's goodness of fit to the data was very high, as evidenced by the high determination coefficient of R2 = 0.9821, indicating that the model could describe 98.21% of the change. The adjusted R2 value (0.9591), which was also very close to the R2 value (0.9821), indicated that the model remained applicable after adjustment, with only a 4.09% change. Additionally, the lack of fit (0.0829, p > 0.05) was not significant. MDS analysis

The extraction effectiveness was affected by the interaction between CA and the solvent molecules. To gain more insight into why DES proved to be a better extractant than other solvents, molecular dynamic simulations were conducted. Three common solvents, namely, ChCl-Leva, methanol, and water, were chosen alongside CA as the compound of interest due to the considerable differences in extraction yields observed with various solvents. Furthermore, simulations were conducted to study the interactions between DES and CA with varying levels of water content.

Fig. 5A illustrated the locations of CA in the solutions between 0 and 50 ns. As seen, CA molecules gathered in water from 0 to 50 ns but remained evenly dispersed in DES and methanol. Although there was a slight variation in the distribution states of CA molecules in ChCl-leva at various water content levels, there was generally no discernible mutual aggregation (Fig. A1). In contrast to water, ChCl-Leva and methanol resulted in greater solubility of those CA compounds. To quantify and compare the solvations, the amount of contact between the dispersed phase and the CA compound was evaluated using the solvent accessible surface area (SASA), which was used as an essential criterion. As shown in Fig. 5B, the SASA values for CA in different solvents reached steady-state levels within 0–50 ns of simulation. In the presence of ChCl-Leva with 50% water, the SASA of CA molecules measured at 29.52 nm2 was found to be slightly higher than that in methanol (29.06 nm2) and significantly higher than that in water (19.11 nm2). These observations suggest that CA molecules have a tendency to aggregate in water, whereas they exhibited a more uniform distribution in methanol or ChCl-Leva solvents, as depicted in Fig. 5A. It was worth mentioning that the SASA of DESs with different water contents was different, consistent with the single-factor results, indicating that the water content does affect the extraction of the target compounds.Fig. 5Investigation of interaction between CA and different solvents. (A) Snapshots of CA dissolved in different solvent systems at 0 ns and 50 ns, respectively; (B) Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of CA in different solvents; (C) average noncovalent interaction (aNCI) analysis: (1)-(4) DES with 10–70% water, (5) methanol, (6) water.

Investigation of interaction between CA and different solvents. (A) Snapshots of CA dissolved in different solvent systems at 0 ns and 50 ns, respectively; (B) Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of CA in different solvents; (C) average noncovalent interaction (aNCI) analysis: (1)-(4) DES with 10–70% water, (5) methanol, (6) water.

Additionally, the average noncovalent interactions (aNCI) between CA and solvent molecules were analysed, with a focus on key interactions, including hydrogen bonding (HB), van der Waals forces (VDW), and steric repulsion (SR) [32]. As shown in Fig. 5C, the colour gradient, ranging from dark blue to red, corresponds to the interactions that range from strong attractive forces to repulsive forces. The findings revealed that CA exhibited a powerful attraction with the blue-green surface in DES with 50% water, suggesting that CA developed a strong attraction with the neighbouring solution molecules. This was particularly evident in the formation of HBs linked with chloride anions, which were more resilient than those formed with other solvents. This was attributed to the unique properties of chloride anions in ChCl-Leva, which acted as bridges, simultaneously connecting the hydroxyl groups in CA with solvent molecules. The formation of the CA cluster (Fig. 5A) caused a stronger SR interaction (red area) between the CA and water, which further limited the contact of the CA with the water. Additionally, it was important to note that the VDW interactions (large green area) between CA and ChCl-Leva were much stronger than in systems containing water or methanol and that the trends shown by different water contents were consistent with SASA. Previous studies have shown that closer and stronger VDW interactions increased the dispersion of solute molecules in DES, promoted their solubility in the solvent, and conferred a more stable state [46]. Overall, it was discovered that CA and ChCl-Leva had an attractive interaction that was more stable and powerful than CA and other solvents, which may explain the high extraction rate of CA.

Comparison of antioxidant activity

This study aimed to evaluate the potential applications of products obtained through two different extraction methods by measuring their antioxidant activities.

Typically, antioxidant components demonstrate potent free-radical scavenging capabilities, thus making them essential in mitigating various diseases. The DPPH and ABTS reagents are representative compounds used to evaluate the free radical scavenging activities of bioactive compounds. The antioxidant activity of PA products was quantified by measuring the IC50, the sample concentration required to scavenge 50% of the free radicals. A lower IC50 value indicates a higher scavenging capacity [48].

The IC50 value of BHT in the ABTS free radical scavenging assay (Fig. 7) was 67.97 μg/mL, and the IC50 value of the 70% methanol extraction solution under stirring conditions was 56.86 μg/mL, which decreased to 49.69 μg/mL after sonication. Similarly, the IC50 value of the DES extract was higher for stirring (43.29 μg/mL) than for sonication (35.64 μg/mL). This suggests that the DES extract exhibited a stronger ABTS scavenging ability than the methanol extract, and ultrasound-assisted DES extraction further enhanced the scavenging ability against ABTS radicals.Fig. 7Antioxidant activity of the Cottonseed Hulls PAs extracted by different solvents. (a) DPPH free radical scavenging activity. (b) ABTS free radical scavenging activity.

Antioxidant activity of the Cottonseed Hulls PAs extracted by different solvents. (a) DPPH free radical scavenging activity. (b) ABTS free radical scavenging activity.

Contrary to expectations, the IC50 of methanolic extracts was lower than that of DES in the DPPH scavenging experiments, both under stirring (108.80 μg/mL vs. 149.10 μg/mL) and sonication-assisted (97.04 μg/mL vs. 113.10 μg/mL) conditions. Four possible explanations were proposed for the lower antioxidant capacity of PAs extracted by DES. First, organic solvents might possess some inherent antioxidant activity that could increase the overall activity of crude PA extraction. Second, DES may inhibit the antioxidant activity of the crude PA extraction solution, thereby reducing its antioxidant ability. Third, the extraction of additional antioxidant components, such as flavonoids, using organic solvents may have raised the antioxidant ability of the PA solution [49], [50]. Fourth, various solvents may extract various PAs that have different compositions. While some ingredients in PAs might have robust antioxidant activity, others might have only moderate antioxidant activity. The ingredients in the PAs that were extracted using organic solvents may primarily have high antioxidant activity [47], [51]. In addition, for both methanol and DES, the IC50 values of the products obtained by UAE were lower than those obtained by stirring. This can be attributed to the effect of ultrasound treatment, which destroyed the plant material and allowed the target substance to be more soluble in the solvent. Of note, BHT had an IC50 value of 178.30 ± 1.28 μg/mL, which was higher than that of all four extracts, indicating that the extracts have higher antioxidant capacity than BHT.

The results above contributed to understanding the potential role of PAs from cotton seed hulls in antioxidants.

Adsorption of biochars

To determine the mechanism of adsorption, it was essential to investigate the kinetics of adsorption. Pseudofirst-order (PFO) and pseudosecond-order (PSO) kinetic models were used to analyse the adsorption characteristics to better understand the adsorption mechanism of activated carbon for MB. A qt versus t nonlinear fitting diagram was created to illustrate different dynamic models, and then parameter values, along with the correlation coefficient (R2), were determined through calculations:

- PFO model: log (qe − qt) = log qe − k1 t.

- PSO model: t/qt = 1/(k2 qe2) + 1/qe t.

where qe (mg/g) and qt (mg/g) are the MB concentrations on the active carbon at equilibrium and at any time t, and k1 and k2 are the rate constants of the Lagergren and pseudosecond order models, respectively.

The adsorption kinetics curves of MB on ACCSH and ACCSR showed an initial rapid increase followed by stabilization (Fig. 10). The PSO models yielded better fitting results for MB adsorption by ACCSH and ACCSR compared to the PFO model (Table 5). Good agreement between the calculated and experimental values was found for the equilibrium adsorption capacity. Based on the PSO model, the expected equilibrium adsorption capacity of MB by ACCSH was 1.0818 mg/g, which was very close to the actual value of 1.0000 mg/g. Similarly, the theoretical equilibrium adsorption capacity of MB by ACCSR was calculated to be 1.0488 mg/g using the same model and was in good agreement with the actual equilibrium adsorption capacity of 1.0000 mg/g. These findings suggested that the PSO model could accurately depict the characteristics of MB adsorption by the two biochars. Chemical processes have been found to primarily control the adsorption of MB, involving the exchange of electrons between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate [58], [59]. In previous studies, Ginkgo biloba branches treated with CMC-Fe3O4-RB exhibited an adsorption capacity with a maximum of 24.109 mg/g [2], and chitosan-modified biochar demonstrated an adsorption capacity of 10.90 mg/g [60], while rice husk showed a capacity of 2.4 mg/g [61]. Conclusion

In conclusion, the extraction of PAs from CSH was accomplished using a sustainable UAE-DES method. A liquid-to-material ratio of 36.25 mL/g, a water content of 33.21%, and an extraction time of 7.4 min were found to be the ideal UAE-DES extraction system. The experiments carried out under ideal circumstances proved that the new method was not only reliable but also more effective than the traditional extraction method. The PA products additionally showed antioxidant activity. The molecular dynamic simulation also identified the mechanism underlying the superior extraction effectiveness of DES in comparison to other solvents. The maximum adsorption capacity of MB by ACCSR was 1 mg/g and followed PSO kinetics. The study objectives were met by successfully demonstrating the high-value comprehensive utilization of waste CSH produced during the production of cotton seed oil.
