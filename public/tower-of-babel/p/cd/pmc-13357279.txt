Outcomes in the Management of Pediatric Retrograde Cricopharyngeal Dysfunction
Authors: Aidan P. Wright, Vivian Jin, Nathaniel B. Hunter, Andrew G. Tritter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo review the presentation and treatment outcomes of pediatric patients with retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction (RCPD).MethodsA retrospective chart review was performed on pediatric patients diagnosed with retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction (RCPD) and treated with cricopharyngeal botulinum toxin injection (CPBTI). Patient‐specific demographic, clinical, and treatment data were collected with a focus on outcomes and complications.ResultsTwenty‐five patients were identified (mean ± SD age, 14.4 ± 3.91; range 4–17). Eighteen (72%) patients were female. Common presenting symptoms included inability to belch (100%), bloating (96%), excessive flatulence (92%), and gurgling sounds from the throat (88%). Patients under 5 years of age were noted by parents to have visible abdominal distention and food aversion. Eleven patients (46%) had social anxiety related to their symptoms prior to intervention. Seven (28%) had alternate diagnoses prior to initial consultation. The average duration of follow‐up was 247 days. All patients (100%) experienced symptomatic improvement, with 88% noting complete symptom resolution and 12% noting reduced symptoms. Only one patient required a second injection to maintain their treatment response.ConclusionRCPD presents similarly in both pediatric and adult patients, though the ability to verbally express symptoms may limit recognition in younger patients. Pediatric patients appear to better tolerate expected side effects of treatment and demonstrate a higher overall long‐term success rate when compared to adult outcomes in the literature. Increased recognition and treatment of RCPD in children may help minimize long term sequelae of the condition while also affording decreased treatment morbidity.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction (RCPD) is a disorder characterized by an inability to burp due to failure of cricopharyngeal relaxation in response to esophageal gas distention. This inability can lead to symptoms including severe abdominal bloating, chest pain or pressure, nausea, excessive flatulence, socially awkward gurgling noises from the throat, difficulty vomiting, and social isolation [1]. Reports of patients with these symptoms date back to at least 1987 [2]. However, the disorder was first formally described in 2019 by Bastian and Smithson, who reported a series of 51 patients with this constellation of symptoms, as well as demonstrated successful treatment outcomes using botulinum toxin (BT) injection into the cricopharyngeus muscle (CPM) [1]. Since then, thousands of patients have been diagnosed with RCPD and treated with this method globally [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Many RCPD patients first experience symptoms during childhood or adolescence, frequently enduring years of initial misdiagnoses and dismissal by the medical community [4]. Despite this, only three peer‐reviewed studies have been published that include RCPD patients under 18 years of age, amounting to only 10 patients in total [11, 12, 13]. Several other articles briefly mention RCPD in children, but lack detailed discussion or data analysis regarding these patients [1, 7, 8, 14]. Alongside the exponential rise in research related to RCPD is a simultaneous increase in public awareness of the condition, with current online communities dedicated to RCPD consisting of over 30,000 members across multiple social media platforms [6]. Despite these advances, significant gaps remain in our understanding of pediatric RCPD.

As an institution with a high volume of pediatric RCPD patients, we seek to present our experience with this population. Here, we provide descriptive and outcomes‐based data for this cohort, while also offering insight into procedural and technical considerations of treatment for RCPD in pediatric patients.

Methods

After obtaining institutional review board approval and a Waiver of Informed Consent from the UTHealth Houston McGovern Medical School, a retrospective chart review was conducted on pediatric patients treated for RCPD after initial evaluation in the otorhinolaryngology clinic at UTHealth Houston between September 2022 and June 2025. Patients less than 18 years old at the time of evaluation, but 18 or older at the time of treatment, were excluded. Clinical and demographic data were obtained from electronic medical records for review, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, zip code, referral source, clinical history, presenting symptoms, treatment details, adverse events, and outcomes data. Treatment success was defined as the development of the ability to belch, with at least subjective partial resolution of presenting symptoms. A response was considered sustained if it was maintained at least 6 months after treatment, with no desire or need for additional treatment.

Operative Procedure

All patients included in this study were treated with cricopharyngeal botulinum toxin injection (CPBTI) under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation in the operating room. For larger pediatric patients, a small Benjamin‐Hollinger or “slimline” diverticuloscope (Karl Storz, Tutlingen, Germany, Product REF# 12068 B) was typically used to expose and suspend the CPM for injection. For smaller pediatric patients that could not accommodate this scope, a more traditional pediatric Parson's laryngoscope was used instead, with the blade suspended in the esophageal inlet to provide a view of the CPM. Cervical esophagoscopy was performed while suspended using a rigid zero‐degree telescope before injecting the BT at three separate sites across the posterior aspect of the muscle. For patients who were post‐pubescent and/or near adult size, a dose of 80 units of onabotulinumtoxin A was injected. This dosage has been arbitrarily established as a standard for adult‐sized patients at our institution. For smaller, or pre‐pubescent patients, a weight‐based dosing protocol of 1–2 units/kg was used [11, 15, 16]. This dosing strategy was chosen based on previously published literature for CPBTI in the treatment of pediatric dysphagia. Results

Demographics

A total of 25 pediatric patients diagnosed with RCPD and treated with CPBTI over a two‐year period between August 2023 and September 2025 were identified. The cohort consisted of 18 (72.0%) females and 7 (17.0%) males. The average age at presentation was 14.4 with a range of 4–17 (Table 1). Of the 25 patients, 21 (84.0%) were from Texas, one (4.00%) was from California, one (4.00%) was from Indiana, one (4.00%) was from Michigan, and one (4.00%) was from Virginia. The average distance traveled for treatment was 339.2 miles (SD: 565.1, range: 3.5–2096). The most common referral sources for the cohort included social media (24.0%), Google and online articles (32.0%), and other healthcare providers (20.0%). For those who were referred by other healthcare providers, two were referred by gastroenterologists (GI), one was from a primary care clinic, one was from a general ENT practice, and one was from a laryngologist who treats RCPD, but was not able to move forward with the procedure due to issues with insurance coverage.

Patient Demographics for Pediatric RCPD Patients.

Before their initial visit to our institution, 17 (68.0%) patients had sought medical attention elsewhere. Among these, seven received alternative diagnoses before presentation; four were still being worked up for a diagnosis, and five were informed that their symptoms were “normal”. Ten patients underwent extensive work‐ups with GI, including blood work and fecal testing. Five (29%) patients underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), with two demonstrating abnormal findings (villous atrophy and an ulcer with positive oral flora culture). Two (12%) patients underwent abdominal x‐rays, which demonstrated the presence of air distended bowels. Three (18%) patients were tested for disaccharidase deficiency, with one positive result. Additional tests included Celiac disease serologies (n = 6; 35%), Helicobacter pylori testing (n = 4; 24%), ova and parasite testing for Giardia (n = 2; 12%), and radioallergosorbent testing (n = 1; 6%). All of these tests were negative. Prior to CPBTI, the most common medications trialed included Proton pump inhibitors (41%), famotidine (18%), ondansetron (18%), cyproheptadine (12%), and simethicone (12%). Diet modification (24%) and activity restriction (24%) were the most frequently attempted conservative therapies. Every patient (100%) in the cohort endorsed an inability to belch, with associated discomfort in either the chest or abdomen. Other presenting symptoms included bloating (96%), excessive flatulence (92%), audible gurgling sounds from the throat (88%), heartburn (32%), nausea (28%), and hiccups that were excessive or painful (28%). Two patients reported dysphagia (8%), one (4%) experienced constant fatigue, three (12%) had to limit their exercise because of symptom exacerbation, five (20%) avoided certain foods because of their symptoms, and two (8%) described breathing difficulties due to pressure in their throat. For patients who were too young to reliably communicate their symptoms with words, parents reported the absence of witnessed belching, as well as visible abdominal distension that culminated by the end of the day and resolved after sleeping overnight. Social anxiety affected 11 (44%) patients, with one individual missing several days of school due to their symptoms. Of those patients whose parents could recall, only seven (33%) patients could not burp as an infant; 14 (66%) patients were able to burp to some capacity as an infant, before eventually losing the ability. Regarding other medical comorbidities, one patient (4%) had a history of subclinical hypothyroidism, and seven patients (28%) had diagnosis of mental health disorders such as anxiety, ADHD, or OCD.

Each patient had at least one follow‐up visit. All 25 patients (100%) gained the ability to belch postoperatively. Postoperative outcomes and complications are detailed in Table 2. Twenty‐two (88.0%) patients experienced complete resolution of original presenting symptoms, and three (12.0%) experienced a partial reduction in symptom severity. One patient (4.00%) could not burp as effectively immediately after their surgery but gained full ability at subsequent visits. Postoperative dysphagia was noted in 21 (84.0%) patients, with seven patients reporting symptoms for 2–7 days and 14 patients reporting symptoms for 2–6 weeks. One patient experienced dysphagia for 2–3 months that was overall manageable despite being much longer in duration when compared to the other patients. These symptoms were managed with dietary modifications until their dysphagia resolved. No types of dysphagia therapy were required in any patients. Regurgitation or reflux due to decreased CPM basal tone was observed in 13 (52.0%) patients; this resolved within 4–6 weeks after treatment in all 13 patients without the need for pharmacologic antireflux/antisecretory therapy.

Respiratory side effects were noted in five patients (20.0%). Patient 10 experienced midline chest discomfort during inhalation at rest for a few days. Patients 18, 20, and 21 experienced a sensation of mild dyspnea with exertion. Each of these patients had symptom resolution within a 2‐week period. One patient (Patient 6) with a history of growth hormone deficiency and PTSD experienced several brief episodes of laryngospasms beginning 72 h postoperatively that prompted a return to the emergency department. He was discharged shortly thereafter but did present again to an outside emergency department for similar symptoms, which seemed to be triggered by episodic regurgitation and reflux. Per reports from his mother, he was admitted for observation for 1–2 weeks with no specific interventions aside from supportive care and respiratory retraining exercises administered by speech language pathology. His symptoms resolved after several weeks. At a follow‐up visit one year later, the child was reportedly doing well, with complete resolution of his original RCPD symptoms. No other adverse events were reported.

The average duration of follow up for all patients was 247 days (SD: 122, range: 31–549). To date, only one patient lost the ability to burp and developed recurrence of his symptoms (Patient 3). A repeat CPBTI was performed 8 months after his initial treatment with an increased treatment dose (60 units compared to initial dose of 25 units). A much more robust treatment response was noted at his initial follow‐up visit 2 months after the second procedure. As of this manuscript's preparation, two patients were lost to follow up after their initial visit within the first 1–2 months of treatment, and one patient is still within the 6‐month postoperative period waiting for their second follow up visit. These patients have only had a single follow‐up visit from the first 1–2 months after treatment.

Discussion

This work represents our institutional experience with management of pediatric RCPD, and to our knowledge, it is the largest series on pediatric RCPD to date. This paper also includes the youngest known RCPD patient successfully treated with BT, at 4 years of age. Only seven other peer reviewed research articles included pediatric RCPD patients prior to this study [1, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Of the four studies with data related to pediatric age and gender, there were 11 total patients included, with ages ranging from 7 to 17 years, and an average age of 15. Nine patients were female (81.8%), and two were male (18.2%) [11, 12, 13, 14]. In three studies that included treatment, all pediatric RCPD patients were treated in the OR with CPBTI [11, 12, 13]. Based on this existing knowledge, our research aimed to contribute to a growing understanding of pediatric RCPD and examine the best approaches to identify and treat these patients.

One of the more interesting aspects of RCPD in general pertains to long‐term outcomes after treatment. While roughly 95% of patients attain the ability to belch once the BT becomes therapeutically active, 70%–80% of patients are able to sustain this ability indefinitely after only a single injection [1, 8, 17, 18]. This suggests a “training wheels” effect where patients are learning this ability from the temporary cricopharyngeal relaxation afforded by BT. Overall, our data demonstrate a similar initial response rate to what is seen in the literature; however, the 96% long‐term success rate we observed in our pediatric population appears to be much higher than what is seen with adult RCPD patients [17]. These findings are similar to those observed in other pediatric‐focused RCPD studies. Of those studies that provided treatment details for their pediatric patients, all but 1 out of 11 total patients experienced symptom relief within 1–4 weeks of CPBTI, and this patient did ultimately achieve a complete response by their 6‐month follow up visit [11, 12, 13]. Similar to our own results, only one patient out of these 11 other patients in the literature required more than one injection [13]. While additional investigation into this idea is needed, we hypothesize that increased neuroplasticity of youth affords patients with an easier ability to acquire and sustain the relaxing function of the upper esophageal sphincter as part of the belching reflex.

The postinjection side effects of temporary dysphagia and regurgitation are well reported in existing adult RCPD literature [17]. These side effects are similarly present in our pediatric series, though subjectively with less severity than seen in adults. This may be related to the physiologic resiliency of pediatric patients, particularly when they are otherwise healthy. Previous studies have suggested that postoperative dysphagia lasts for an average of approximately 16 days [19]. The few patients who experienced prolonged dysphagia were suggested to continue dietary modifications at their one‐month follow‐up until their symptoms were resolved. Those with persistent dysphagia past the 4–6 week mark did not receive swallowing therapy as there was no mention of these symptoms until their next follow‐up date at 6 months. Additionally, a few postoperative complications, such as mild exertional dyspnea and laryngospasm, were observed but not commonly reported in previous literature. Notably, all of the patients with dyspnea in our cohort were student athletes who played competitive school sports and attributed their dyspnea to premature return to strenuous exercise. The patient with recurrent laryngospasms did appear to be an outlier in this study; however, it is worth noting that there is a similar 3%–5% incidence of these episodes in the adult population, which seem to occur most often in the context of laryngopharyngeal reflux. We know this from unpublished institutional data at UTHealth Houston where we observed these episodes in 2 out of 63 RCPD patients being followed for a separate active study. There was also a patient who noted midsternal chest discomfort for several days after their procedure, which seemed more likely to have been attributed to complications from general anesthesia. While most of these adverse effects were mild without serious long‐term morbidity, these outcomes highlight the importance of careful monitoring for pediatric patients receiving CPBTI for RCPD.

When comparing adult and pediatric RCPD patients, there were many similarities in presentation and treatment outcomes, but also notable differences that should be recognized when assessing and treating the pediatric RCPD population. A previous 2024 systematic review of 637 RCPD patients had symptom rates of abelchia (100%), abdominal bloating (83%), and gurgling (75%) that were similar to our own study population [17]. Patients in the early childhood range were exceptions in our study, requiring reliance on parental descriptors to classify these symptoms. These included visible bloating, food aversion, and witnessed lack of burping. Several studies from adult literature also mention an increased incidence of social anxiety seen in the RCPD population [4, 7]. One study observed a high incidence of mood disorders among their patient population [7] and other studies reported patients describing the drastic impact RCPD had on their social life [4, 10]. We found that 44% of patients in this pediatric cohort study attributed their anxiety to RCPD symptoms. Many stated the anxiety was severe enough to cause social isolation, preventing some of them from attending school or socializing with friends. While the association between RCPD and anxiety remains incompletely understood, we do know that treating RCPD with CPBTI can lead to significant and durable improvements in symptoms of anxiety and depression [20]. Identifying and treating pediatric patients who experience mental health concerns at initial evaluation might offer substantial improvement in these symptoms during an otherwise critical time in their social development [21].

Like previous studies in the adult RCPD population, the majority of our patients and their families relied on independent research to get connected with an RCPD provider. They often reported prior unsuccessful attempts at seeking out medical attention for their symptoms [16, 17]. These numbers demonstrate the need for increased awareness of RCPD within the medical community and improved resources for patients and physicians. While there has been an uptrend in the availability of RCPD awareness online, much of this information is lacking in quality [22]. Bridging the gap in education of this condition within the gastroenterology community could also be a vital part in addressing this issue. In our study, GI physicians were the most visited healthcare professionals prior to receiving an RCPD diagnosis from an otolaryngologist. Future efforts should be directed towards improving recognition of this diagnosis and expediting time‐to‐treatment.

When it comes to BT dosing, there is no standardized protocol in the literature, with reported dosages ranging from 10 to 100 units [17]. In pediatric studies, dosages have ranged from 50 to 75 U for those 10–17 years old, and 25 U for those under 10 years old [11, 12, 13, 14]. For our study, dosing regimens were selected using weight‐based dosing of 1–2 units/kg for pre‐pubescent patients, and a standard dose of 80 units for post‐pubescent patients who were essentially adult‐sized. All patients in our study achieved at least a partial, if not a complete response, to treatment, regardless of dosage delivered. Only one patient, a 5‐year‐old male, required a repeat injection after a partial response that waned following an initial 25‐unit injection. A repeat injection was performed 7 months later with 60 units, after which he achieved improved symptomatic relief compared to his initial response. By comparison, a 4‐year‐old female who received 25 units in our study achieved a complete response, and a separate study also reported successful treatment with 25 units of BT in patients as young as 7 [11]. With this degree of dosing variability, it is clear that additional research is needed to optimize dosing strategies for these patients.

Limitations

Since this is a retrospective cohort review primarily seeking to present descriptive information for a population in need of normative data, there is no actual statistical analysis provided with this series. This also remains a relatively small sample set, in spite of being the largest reported series on the subject. The data was also influenced by subjective patient reporting, which inherently leads to potential misrepresentation of patient experiences. This is especially true for our youngest patients, whose experiences were presented by their parents. Lastly, follow up data of at least 6–12 months was available for 88% of patients in this study, effectively allowing us to assess the long‐term treatment response sustained by these patients well after the 3–4 month therapeutic window of BT had passed. While it is not expected to change for the remaining three patients (12%) who have not had a 6 month follow up visit, a lack of sustained treatment response in these patients would lead to a decreased overall long‐term success rate.

Conclusion

With this study, we present the largest series on pediatric RCPD to date, including the youngest known RCPD patient successfully treated with BT at four years of age. RCPD in the pediatric population can have a very similar presentation as compared to adults, though the ability of very young patients to verbally express these symptoms may be limited, and a high index of suspicion is warranted by both clinician and caregiver. Our experience demonstrates a higher overall long‐term success rate for CPBTI in the pediatric population (96%) when compared to the 70%–80% long‐term success seen in the adult population. Side effects from treatment also appear to be comparable, if not subjectively less severe than those seen in adults. Further efforts should be made to increase awareness of RCPD among physicians, as early diagnosis and intervention may lead to decreased overall treatment burden and long‐term sequela from unmanaged symptoms. In terms of treatment, more rigorous investigation into the role of BT dose selection also remains warranted.
