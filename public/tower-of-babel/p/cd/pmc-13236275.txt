Generative Artificial Intelligence Chatbots for Parental Guidance in Pediatric Orthopaedics: A Pilot Feasibility Study
Authors: Wayne Yong Xiang Foo, Kenneth Pak Leung Wong, Arjandas Mahadev, Mohammad Ashik bin Zainuddin, Andrew Chia Chen Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Counselling parents is an integral and time-intensive component of pediatric orthopaedic practice. Supplementary patient education resources play an important role in extending and reinforcing the information conveyed during clinic visits, thereby reducing postconsultation enquiries on clinical staff. A significant proportion of pediatric orthopaedic presentations involve physiological variants such as intoeing, flatfoot, scoliosis, or lower limb alignment differences. Families deserve a detailed explanation of natural history, expected outcomes, and the rationale for either treatment or watchful waiting. Even after a thorough clinic consultation, parents frequently develop further questions at home. These will prompt callbacks, emails, additional return visits to the clinic, or parents may turn to the internet. The internet has become a primary source of health information for parents and caregivers in the pediatric orthopaedic setting, with up to 84% of parents reporting online searches related to their child’s orthopaedic condition, and 97% of those doing so via a general search engine rather than a validated medical resource1,2.

Online resources are, however, highly variable in quality and accuracies. Studies have identified significant quality deficiencies in YouTube videos and other online materials on common pediatric orthopaedic conditions including hip disorders and clubfoot3,4. Misinformation or incomplete information can result in downstream consequences for both families and providers. For the families, it may heighten parental anxiety, delay appropriate care-seeking, and drive unnecessary return clinic visits. Parental health anxiety has been reported in 14 to 52% of cases after online health information seeking5. For the providers, physicians can spend a substantial portion of their working time on administrative work including fielding parental enquiries, documentation, and follow-up of family discussions, with the increased administrative load associated with burnout6,7.

Generative artificial intelligence offers a potential mechanism to address this information gap. In broader healthcare settings, artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot responses to patient questions were frequently rated more favorably than physician responses for quality and perceived empathy8. In pediatric orthopaedics, however, the performance of publicly available models has been less reassuring. ChatGPT and Gemini aligned with the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgery clinical practice guidelines in only approximately two-thirds of responses, with substantial deficiencies in citation accuracy9. Similarly, while ChatGPT answered parental questions appropriately in most cases, it generated unprompted treatment recommendations in more than half of interactions and demonstrated inconsistency across repeated queries10. These limitations suggest that general-purpose models are not yet suitable for unsupervised use in pediatric musculoskeletal care.

To leverage gen AI safely and accurately for pediatric musculoskeletal conditions, a tailored, purpose-built solution is required. To address this need, we developed a gen AI-powered WhatsApp chatbot trained on validated pediatric orthopaedic educational content, designed to be offered to families at the end of the initial consultation. The chatbot serves as a patient education adjunct to reduce unresolved post-consultation enquiries. We evaluated its accuracy, safety, and clinical acceptability among orthopaedic specialists, junior doctors, and allied health staff.

Materials and Methods

Chatbot Development

This pilot feasibility study was conducted at a tertiary pediatric orthopaedic center. Institutional review board exemption was obtained before study initiation. The chatbot was developed in partnership with a local digital health company specializing in AI-powered clinical communication tools. The system employed a hybrid architecture combining 2 complementary approaches. For commonly encountered and predictable questions, the chatbot was configured to deliver preauthored, clinician-vetted responses. This approach minimizes the risk of hallucination (defined as a confident, plausible sounding but factually inaccurate or fabricated output generated by the model) and unprompted treatment recommendations by ensuring that outputs are reviewed and controlled before deployment. For questions outside this predefined set, the system used retrieval-augmented generation, a technique that grounds model responses in a defined knowledge base rather than relying on broad pretrained outputs, with guardrails applied to flag potential hallucinations.

The knowledge base comprised educational materials from OrthoKids (OK), a patient-facing resource developed and maintained by the Paediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America, covering 5 conditions commonly encountered in our clinic: in-toeing, flatfoot, scoliosis, bow legs, and knock knees11. The chatbot was deployed through WhatsApp, which is used by over 80% of Singapore residents, making it the most accessible and familiar communication platform for families in our healthcare context12. The chatbot’s outputs were written in a jargon-free language at a level easily understood by most adult users and was available in multiple languages including English, Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil.

After initial development, the chatbot was tested against 30 commonly asked questions derived from OK resources and selected to reflect the range of enquiries parents typically raise in clinic. Responses were independently reviewed by 2 fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, who assessed accuracy, completeness, the presence of hallucinations, and the appropriateness of any referral or treatment-related statements. The chatbot underwent iterative refinement based on this feedback until both reviewers were satisfied that responses were clinically appropriate and consistent with established guidance.

Clinical Evaluation

Once refined, the chatbot was evaluated by a multidisciplinary group of staff, including pediatric orthopaedic specialists, junior doctors, nurses, plaster technicians, and patient service associates (PSAs). In Singapore, PSAs perform administrative and basic clinical support duties. Race and ethnicity data were not collected as part of this evaluation as the cohort comprised of healthcare staff rather than patients. Participants were encouraged to interact with the chatbot freely by posing questions about the 5 covered conditions (Fig. 1). Participants chose their own queries instead of standardized questions to reflect the range of enquiries they would typically encounter in clinical practice.

Representative screenshots of the generative AI chatbot interface on WhatsApp. AI = artificial intelligence.

Participants were then asked to complete a structured 12-item evaluation form (Appendix 1) adapted from the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Print materials, a validated instrument for assessing the understandability and actionability of patient education content. Items not applicable to a conversational AI format were removed, and additional questions addressing concerns specific to AI-generated content were added, including potential for harm, presence of unprompted treatment recommendations, and citation accuracy. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The 12-item survey covered 3 broad areas. Content quality (Q1-Q3) assessed whether responses were relevant, sufficiently detailed, and clinically correct. Understandability and usability (Q4-Q7) assessed clarity of explanation, logical organization, alignment with treatment protocols, and appropriateness of response length. Safety and clinical acceptability (Q8-Q12) assessed potential for harm, absence of unprompted treatment recommendations, comfort recommending the chatbot to patients, appropriateness of referral suggestions, and citation accuracy. Question 8 (“The answer could be detrimental to the patient’s health”) was scored inversely, with lower scores indicating greater perceived safety.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey responses using SPSS 29 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Likert scale data are reported as mean, SD, and range. The proportion of respondents rating each domain ≥4 (agree or strongly agree) was calculated as a summary measure of positive endorsement. Subgroup analyses by clinician role were performed to explore variation in safety perception and usability ratings. Given the pilot nature of the study and the small sample size, all analyses are descriptive; the study was not powered for formal hypothesis testing.

Results

Participant Profiles

Eighteen participants completed the evaluation between May and June 2025. The cohort comprised nurses (n = 5, 27.8%), junior doctors (n = 4, 22.2%), orthopaedic specialists (n = 3, 16.7%), plaster technicians (n = 3, 16.7%), and PSAs (n = 3, 16.7%). Participants posed questions across all 5 covered conditions; in-toeing and flatfoot were the most frequently queried.

Chatbot Evaluation

Overall ratings were high across most domains (Table I). Protocol alignment (Q6) received the highest mean score (4.50 ± 0.62), followed by logical organization (Q5; 4.44 ± 0.62) and relevance (Q1; 4.39 ± 0.51). Clarity of explanation (Q4) was the only domain in which all 18 evaluators rated the chatbot favorably, with a proportion ≥4 of 100%. Relevance (Q1), correctness (Q3), organization (Q5), protocol alignment (Q6), and response length (Q7) each achieved ≥4 endorsement rates of 94.4%. Depth of information (Q2) and comfort recommending the chatbot to patients (Q10) were rated slightly lower, with 83.3% of respondents scoring ≥4, although mean scores remained above 4.0 for both. Citation accuracy (Q12) received the lowest mean score (4.00 ± 0.77), with 77.8% of respondents rating it ≥4.

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates that a purpose-built, gen AI chatbot trained on validated pediatric orthopaedic content can deliver guideline-aligned responses that are rated as accurate, clear, and clinically appropriate by a multidisciplinary evaluator group. Mean scores exceeded 4.0 of 5.0 across 11 of 12 evaluation domains. Protocol alignment and organization were the highest-rated domains, reflecting the value of training on a curated, condition-specific knowledge base rather than deploying a general purpose model. Clarity of explanation achieved unanimous positive endorsement, which is an essential quality for a tool communicating with nonclinical users. The safety profile was similarly encouraging: 88.9% of evaluators confirmed the absence of unprompted treatment recommendations and no hallucinations were identified. This compares favorably compared with the findings by Zusman et al., who reported unprompted treatment recommendations in over half of its interactions by ChatGPT10. This distinction matters considerably for a tool used by parents seeking guidance on their child’s condition.

The practical value of such a tool in a busy pediatric orthopaedic clinic is considerable. A substantial portion of clinic communication involves answering questions that are important to families but repetitive for providers, including reassurance about a child’s in-toeing gait, explanation of what a flatfoot means at age 5, or clarification of when scoliosis surveillance is warranted. A WhatsApp-based chatbot available around the clock could address many of these queries outside clinic hours without adding to clinical workload, thereby reducing the volume of post-consultation callbacks, emails, and unnecessary return visits to the clinic. This is particularly relevant in a setting like Singapore where tertiary pediatric orthopaedic services serve a large catchment and appointment capacity is finite. Importantly, the chatbot responses are delivered in clear, jargon-free language within a conversational format, which may enhance accessibility and understanding compared with static written materials that frequently contain unfamiliar terminology and require active navigation. There is also a secondary benefit: parents who arrive at the clinic in their follow-up visits having already received accurate, structured information may be better positioned to ask more specific questions, have more realistic expectations, and are generally better prepared to follow through on management plans. The chatbot’s strong ratings for correctness and protocol alignment suggest it can deliver that preparatory function reliably.

For Q8 of the survey, the most striking finding was not a failure of the chatbot but a divergence in how different staff groups perceived it. Orthopaedic specialists gave a mean score of 1.33, reflecting minimal concern about clinical harm, while nurses and PSAs scored the same item of at least 4.00, reflecting greater concern. While we did not formally investigate the reasons behind the elevated harm perception scores, we hypothesize that this gap may be best understood as a difference in baseline familiarity with the source material. Specialists who recognize the OK guidelines in the chatbot's responses can readily verify their accuracy; while nurses and PSAs, who may have less exposure to those guidelines may have understandably more caution toward AI-generated clinical content. It is also worth noting that the chatbot's utility extends beyond parental education. The chatbot may also serve as a useful reference tool for junior doctors and a triage aid for outpatient referrals, helping nonspecialist staff identify when escalation to a pediatric orthopaedic surgeon is appropriate. Importantly, the strong endorsement from orthopaedic specialists and their consensus on the chatbot’s clinical accuracy and safety must be established before broader adoption is implemented.

This study has several limitations. The sample was small (n = 18) and drawn entirely from clinical and administrative staff. While their assessment of the chatbot's performance was encouraging, they tell us little about how parents will experience and use it. The chatbot was assessed at a single time point, and performance may vary as the underlying model evolves over time. Importantly, the 5 conditions selected for this study were deliberately chosen to be low-risk physiological variants, where the consequences of an imperfect response are unlikely to result in clinically significant harm. This was a deliberate approach, and the favorable results from this pilot provide a basis for future expansion to higher acuity conditions. Citation accuracy was the weakest domain and reflects the current design choice to prioritize guideline-aligned answers over academic referencing, although this balance may need to be revisited for more complex clinical queries.

As a pilot feasibility study, the evaluation combined assessment of clinical accuracy and user-friendliness within a single mixed cohort. Future work could incorporate evaluating these dimensions separately, with clinical accuracy benchmarked by specialist clinicians and accessibility assessed directly by parent end-users. With future work on parent users, assessing outcome measures could include comprehension, satisfaction, and behavior change. The impact of the implementation on clinic volumes, email and phone call load, and indirect costs to families such as time away from work or school would provide more visibility on workflow benefit. Multilingual deployment, particularly in Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil—the other principal languages spoken by families attending our institution, would also substantially broaden the tool's utility and access.

Conclusion

A purpose-built gen AI chatbot trained on validated pediatric orthopaedic content was rated as accurate, guideline-aligned, and clinically acceptable by a multidisciplinary evaluation panel. These pilot findings support the feasibility of deploying such a tool as an adjunct to direct parental counselling in pediatric orthopaedic practice. Prospective evaluation with parent end-users and assessment of real-world clinical impact are warranted before broader implementation.
