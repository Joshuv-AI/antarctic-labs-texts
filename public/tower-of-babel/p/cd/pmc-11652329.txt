Impact of colorectal cancer screening programme on survival and employment in Taiwan: A nationwide analysis of real‐world data
Authors: Wei‐Ying Chen, Yi‐Peng Lu, Yu‐Wen Chien, Li‐Jung Elizabeth Ku, Jung‐Der Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) leads to life loss and a significant economic burden, which could be reduced by CRC screening.ObjectiveWe assessed the potential savings of lives and employment to evaluate the effectiveness of the Taiwan CRC Screening Programme.MethodsThrough interlinkages among Taiwan Cancer Registry, National Mortality Registry, Taiwan CRC Screening Database, and National Health Insurance claim data, we enroled patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma, aged 50–74 years and diagnosed during 2004–2017, and followed them up to 2018. Life expectancy (LE), lifetime employment duration (LED), loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED were calculated, compared with age‐, sex‐ and calendar year‐matched cohorts. Assuming no difference within a specific stage for screen‐detected versus non‐screen detected CRC and weighting them by different stage distributions, we compared the total loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED.ResultsThe cohort enroled 77,169 patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma, which included 31,728 women (mean [SD] age, 62.5 [7.1] years) and 45,441 men (mean [SD] age, 62.8 [6.8] years). The mean loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED in women were 6.0 (95% confidence interval [CI] 5.7–6.3) and 1.0 (95% CI 0.8–1.1) year(s), whereas those in men were 5.1 (95% CI 4.9–5.4) and 1.1 (95% CI 1.0–1.2) years, respectively. Among the cohort, 53,678 cases had the screening information. On average, screening potentially saved 2.9 (95% CI 2.6–3.2) years of life expectancy plus 0.5 (95% CI 0.4–0.6) years of employment per case in women and 2.7 (95% CI 2.5–3.0) years plus 0.6 (95% CI 0.5–0.7) years in men, respectively.ConclusionThe Taiwan CRC Screening Programme is associated with the savings of lives and employment duration. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of beginning screening at a younger age after accounting for saving employment loss and possibly adjusting lead time bias.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) affects people worldwide; it has an incidence rate of 23.4 per 100,000 in men and 16.2 per 100,000 in women, respectively, and a mortality rate of 11.0 per 100,000 in men and 7.2 per 100,000 in women, respectively. 1 In Taiwan, it ranked second in cancer incidence and third in cancer mortality. 2 , 3 The influence of CRC leads to not only life loss but also imposes a significant economic burden of treatment, morbidity, and mortality costs. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Screening has been advocated to alleviate the burden of CRC as both CRC incidence and mortality can be reduced through stage shift and polypectomy. 11 , 12 , 13 Screening also effectively reduces the burden of CRC by preventing treatment costs. 14 , 15 However, savings of employment and/or productivity loss are critical from a societal perspective. 16 , 17

Conventionally, two methods have been proposed to estimate productivity loss: the human–capital method and the friction–cost method. 18 , 19 A common method of the human–capital approach is to multiply the number of excess deaths by the present value of future lifetime earnings of the decedents using age‐ and gender‐specific employment rates. 6 , 7 , 8 , 16 , 17 The morbidity costs can also be estimated using questionnaires during the short‐term follow‐up periods. 8 , 9 , 10 Among these studies, one uses the microsimulation screening analysis package to simulate the savings of productivity loss by avoided cases, which relies on model simulation to predict long‐term influences of employment and wages by screening under different scenarios. 17 In brief, no studies have directly quantified the productivity loss of patients with CRC considering dynamic change of employment, nor have they estimated potential savings of productivity loss through CRC screening using real‐world data.

Recently, we developed a method to capture the dynamic change of employment, or the employment‐to‐population ratio (EMRATIO), based on real‐world data to estimate lifetime employment duration (LED) affected by a major disease. 20 As the method has not assumed a retirement age, it might overestimate the employment of the index cohort after the age of 65. In this study, we refined the method by adopting a specified retirement age to estimate CRC‐related life and productivity loss, stratified by stage, sex, and age, and quantified the possible savings by screening. We assumed that there was no difference in health consequences within the same stage for those who received screening versus those who did not and simply weighted them by different stage distributions resulting from screening to sum up the total outcomes. This simplified assumption helps to reduce the potential over‐estimation resulting from generally higher literacy and possibly better survival outcomes among screened cases. While the use of loss‐of‐LE as an indicator cannot comprehensively control for lead time bias, it can help mitigate the potential biases associated with detecting cancer at an earlier age and in different calendar years due to screening.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study commenced after the approval of the ethics review committee of the National Cheng Kung University Hospital (B‐ER‐108‐416) and informed consent was waived in this retrospective research cohort with de‐identified data.

National Health Insurance and CRC screening programme in Taiwan

The National Health Insurance (NHI) is a universal healthcare coverage system, operated under a single payer and administered by the government of Taiwan. Launched in 1995 as a compulsory programme, it achieved more than 99% coverage by 2007. The CRC screening programme in Taiwan is based on the biennial faecal immunochemical test (FIT). Individuals with a positive FIT result are offered free colonoscopy for further diagnosis. Screening was initiated in 2004 for all residents aged 50–69 years, which was extended to people aged 70–74 years in June 2013. By 2014, the screening rate was 40.4% and appeared stable.

Study population

From the Taiwan Cancer Registry, we identified patients with CRC aged 50–74 years and diagnosed from 2004 to 2017, utilising International Classification of Disease (ICD)‐9 codes 153.0–153.9 (except 153.5), 154.0, 154.1, 154.2, 154.3, and 154.8 as well as ICD‐10 codes C18 (except C181), C19, C20, and C21. We only included adenocarcinoma cases (ICD‐O‐3: 81402, 81403, 81413, 81433, 81443, 81453, 81473, 82013, 82102, 82103, 82113, 82133, 82153, 82203, 82213, 82553, 82603, 82612, 82613, 82623, 82632, 82633, 82653, 83103, 83233, 83413, 83803, 84403, 84613, 84703, 84803, 84813, 84823, 84903, 85703, 85753, and 88503), as these are largely detected by CRC screening. We excluded cases of C181 and 153.5 (appendix cancer), as these are rarely diagnosed by CRC screening. The earliest registration was selected for those who had two or more CRC registrations. Patients with metastasis from other organ systems to colon–rectum were excluded. Cases with missing stage information (N = 40,386) or inconsistent pathology records (N = 3086) were also excluded, as summarised in Figure 1. The age and sex distributions of those with missing stage information were almost the same as those included in the final analysis (Table S1). We then followed the CRC cohort up to the end of 2018 to determine if a patient was alive by linkages to the Taiwan Mortality Registry.

Establishment of the study cohort.

Estimation of LE and loss‐of‐LE

We employed the Kaplan–Meier (K–M) method to estimate survival functions for our cohort (2004–2017), stratified by age, sex, and cancer stage. The estimation process of Life expectancy (LE) in these subcohorts involved several steps. We used a Monte Carlo method by repeatedly generating some number of random survival times for each referent based on age‐ and sex‐matched lifetable of the same calendar‐year obtained from Taiwan's vital statistics. With a total of about 100,000 generated survival times of the matched referents, we used K–M method to obtain the survival function of the reference population. Given that the number of new CRC cases aged 50–74 who were within stages 2–4 was about 0.1% of the Taiwan population, 21 we simply applied regular lifetables in simulating referents without subtracting these cases. We then calculated relative survival between each subcohort and its matched referents using logit transformation. This transformation facilitated the construction of a restricted cubic spline model, which extended survival estimation for the index subcohort by one additional month. Subsequently, this extended month was integrated into the original logit relative survival model, enabling us to develop another spline model for a further month's projection. This process was iteratively applied each month, projecting the lifetime survival function for the index subcohort until the survival rate diminished to below 0.1%, thereby estimating LE. These estimation procedures were executed using the iSQoL2 package, freely available at https://sites.stat.sinica.edu.tw/isqol/. The loss‐of‐LE was determined by calculating the area between the lifetime survival curves of the index subcohort and their matched referents. 4 , 20 , 22 Notably, for patients with stages 0 and I CRC, their survival rates closely mirrored those of their matched referents after a 15‐year follow‐up. Consequently, we postulated that these patients had no significant loss‐of‐LE. Therefore, rather than extrapolating further, we directly applied the K–M method to compare these patient groups with their corresponding referents up to the end of our follow‐up period. This approach was undertaken to verify our results.

Estimation of LED and loss‐of‐LED

We stratified the LED by cancer stage, age, and sex. Based on the different ways of monthly premium submission to the NHI, we could determine employment status from the longitudinal database. In general, people who served as both the insured and the proposer (the applicant for insurance) belonged to the employment group, and those who had a different role were considered unemployed. Then, we applied the EMRATIO to estimate the LED. EMRATIO is the ratio of individuals currently employed to the total number of people of their corresponding age‐ and sex‐specific strata in a specific month of a specific calendar year. For simplicity, we assumed that all people retired at the age of 65. As the current screening programme in Taiwan covers people aged 50–74 years, this study limited the estimation of LED to the age of 65. At each time point t, the EMATIO was multiplied by the corresponding survival rate and then aggregated up to the age of 65 to obtain the LED. The loss‐of‐LED was the area between the CRC subcohort and the corresponding matched referents. 20 The whole process is summarised in Figure 2. We applied 100 bootstrap sampling with replacement to estimate the overall mean of each subcohort, standard error, and 95% confidence intervals of LE and LED.

Estimation of total Δloss‐of‐LE and Δloss‐of‐LED because of screening

In the CRC cohort, we identified the screen‐detected cases, who had a positive FIT and received a confirmatory colonoscopy within 6 months as recorded in the Taiwan CRC Screening Database. Others not fulfiling the above criteria were defined as the non‐screening detected cancers. The comparison of loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED between screen‐detected and non‐screen detected CRC would result in a difference‐in‐differences, namely, Δloss‐of‐LE (=loss‐of‐LEns − loss‐of‐LEs) and Δloss‐of‐LED (=loss‐of‐LEDns − loss‐of‐LEDs), respectively, indicating the savings resulting from screening. To obtain the longest follow‐up time, we collected all CRC cases from 2004 to 2017 and followed up to 2018. Although the overall weight for each stage was once reported during 2004–2009, 23 the gender‐ and age‐stratified details were not available. Therefore, we assumed that the stage distributions of the screen‐detected and non‐screen detected cancers during 2004–2009 were the same as those collected during 2010–2017. However, since the health benefit of screening in this study was estimated based on the real‐world results of stage‐shift, we performed sensitivity analysis based on the assumption that the same weight distributions were uniform across different genders and age strata.

RESULTS

There were 169,139 cases with CRC aged 30–84 years in the period of 2004–2017. After excluding missing data and unknown‐stage or non‐target populations, a total of 77,169 CRC cases were included in the study, and the screening status of 53,678 of them was known by linkage with the Taiwan CRC Screening Database (Figure 1). The demographic characteristics are summarised in Table 1. More cases of CRC are found in men than in women across every cancer stage and in each age group; the peak of cumulative incidence rate in men with early CRC (stages 0 and I) was in 2014–2015, whereas that of advanced cancer (III and IV) was in 2010–2011. By contrast, the peak of cumulative incidence rates in women (0.96 of CIR30–84 in 2012–2013 for stage I and 1.22 in 2008–2011 for stage II) were about 2 years earlier than those in men (1.49 of CIR30–84 in 2014–2015 for stage I and 1.83 in 2010–2011 for stage II).

In both sexes within the same cancer stage, LE and LED were longer in the young age groups, whereas the loss‐of‐LE and the loss‐of‐LED also increased (Table 2). Using stage II CRC as an illustration, we found that LEs of women in the age groups of 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, and 70–74 years were 28.1 (95% CI 26.4–29.8), 22.4 (95% CI 19.0–25.7), 20.1 (95% CI 18.6–21.8), 16.7 (95% CI 15.8–17.7), and 12.7 (95% CI 12.0–13.4) years, respectively, whereas the LEDs in the age groups of 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 were 5.9 (95% CI 5.4–6.5), 2.8 (95% CI 2.6–3.0), and 0.9 (95% CI 0.8–1.0) years, respectively. Assuming all people retired after 65 years old, the overall average of loss‐of‐LEDs in women for age groups of 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 were 1.7 (95% CI 1.3–2.2), 1.0 (95% CI 0.9–1.0), and 0.2 (95% CI 0.2–0.3) years, respectively, and those of men were 2.0 (95% CI 1.7–2.3), 1.1 (95% CI 1.0–1.3), and 0.3 (95% CI 0.2–0.3) years, respectively, after weighted the proportions of stages. Similarly, in both sexes with the same age groups, LE and LED were longer in the early stages, whereas the loss‐of‐LE and the loss‐of‐LED decreased (Table 2).

Stage shift after the screening programme

In Table 3, the proportions of early CRC (stages 0 and I) in the screen‐detected group were 51.6% in women and 55.9% in men, respectively, which were much higher than those of the non‐screen detected group. By contrast, proportions with advanced CRC (stages III and IV) in the screen‐detected group were much lower than those in the non‐screen detected group. These findings indicate an association of cancer stage shift with screening.

Effectiveness of the National CRC screening programme

As a result of stage shift of the screening programme, we weighted cancer stage and age distributions to estimate the potential savings of LE and LED of the National CRC Screening Programme. We then estimated that the screening programme potentially saved 2.9 (95% CI 2.6–3.2) years of Δloss‐of‐LE and 0.5 (95% CI 0.4–0.6) years of Δloss‐of‐LED per case in women and 2.7 (95% CI 2.5–3.0) and 0.6 (95% CI 0.5–0.7) years in men, respectively (Table 4). The results of sensitivity analysis under a different assumption were almost the same (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that screen‐detected cancers had a more favourable stage distribution and resulted in smaller overall loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED than non‐screen detected cancers (Table 3). On average, the savings of weighted sums of loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED were 2.9 and 0.5 years in women and 2.7 and 0.6 years in men, respectively, for 2–4 stages of CRC. The above figures resulted from either a better cancer survival or a lower background mortality in screened versus non‐screened cases. The younger the age, the larger the benefit. Given that we did not include the potential savings of polypectomy in the confirmation stage of screening, the above figures would be an underestimation of the screening benefit. However, three common biases may arise in the observational study for comparing screen‐detected and non‐screen detected CRC. First, lead time bias could arise because the time of diagnosis for screen detected individuals was detected at an earlier time or usually younger age, which resulted in prolonged survival outcomes even when the actual time to death remains unchanged. Second, in general, tumours with more aggressive histological features may be less likely to be diagnosed through screening and result in length bias. Third, overdiagnosis occurred when people with cancer may die of other causes if they survived long before progression of the cancer. All three potential biases would make the screening evaluation overestimate the outcomes. 24 , 25 In our study, we did not compare the survival outcomes between the screen‐detected and non‐screen detected CRC directly but made some simplifications to allow us to estimate the effects of interest. We conservatively assumed that all people within the same stage, sex and age would share the same experience of survival, loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED. Then, we used loss‐of‐LE to represent the difference between the LEs of the index subcohort and the corresponding matched referents simulated from the cohort lifetables. Although the comparison between screen‐detected and non‐screen detected subcohorts, or difference‐in‐differences, would reduce the component of lead time bias from detecting higher proportions of early stages at younger ages from CRC screening, 4 , 20 , 22 the exact lead time or preclinical detectable period resulting from a specific screening technology may still exist. In addition, as our study only estimated and weighted the losses related to stages 2–4, there would be no over‐diagnosis bias and the length bias would be minimal. Nonetheless, because our study was not a randomised control trial, residual bias associated with unmeasured confounding(s) other than age‐, sex‐, and calendar‐year, such as higher health literacy leading to detection at an early stage and/or lower comorbidities of other diseases, might still exist and over‐estimated the true screening benefits.

Although this study found reduced loss‐of‐LE and loss‐of‐LED associated with the CRC screening programme, it does not necessarily imply a causal link between the two. However, we have the following arguments to corroborate the above hypothesis. First, Taiwan's NHI system waived all pathologically validated CRC cases from all copayments, so our nationwide collection is comprehensive and representative. Second, although our lifetime extrapolation of survival was based on external additive hazards and considered relatively accurate, 26 , 27 the average follow‐up time was 5.2 [3.7] (mean [SD]) years. Moreover, we paid special attention to validate our estimates of subcohorts by extrapolating the first 8 years of data to 15 years and comparing it with the actual K–M estimate. After excluding those of stages 0 and 1, the relative biases were usually lower than 10% for those with a censored rate below 80% (at the end of the eighth year of follow‐up), except for one out of 30 estimates (Tables S3–S7). This study extrapolated from the end of 15 years, which would be closer to the LE for CRC of stages 2–4 (Table 2), of which the potential biases would be even smaller. Third, we used monthly EMRATIO to represent the true employment condition, which was multiplied by the survival functions and summed up for the LED. The loss‐of‐LED considers the losses due to mortality and morbidity up to the legal retirement age of 65. Considering dynamic change of employment, the estimation of loss‐of‐LED in this study would reflect the real situation, which was different from the conventional estimation of mortality costs using age‐ and gender‐specific employment rates. 6 , 7 , 8 , 16 , 17 Thus, we tentatively concluded that CRC screening in Taiwan led to a cancer stage shift, saving both lives and employment losses.

With a CRC screening rate stagnating around 40% in Taiwan in the last decade, 28 we diagnosed new colorectal adenocarcinoma cases of 3492 women and 5488 men in 2018 aged 50–74 years. 21 Had the screening rate increased from 40% to 80% with 0.80 sensitivity of FIT in women and 0.77 in men, 29 we would have diagnosed CRC by screening from the proportions of 32.0% to 64.0% in women and 30.8% to 61.6% in men, with a potential saving of life loss of 6481.2 (=3492 × 64.0% × 2.9) years plus 1117.4 (=3492 × 64.0% × 0.5) years of loss‐of‐LED in women and 9127.6 (=5488 × 61.6% × 2.7) years plus 2028.4 (=5488 × 61.6% × 0.6) years in men, respectively. The screening programme would reduce 45% of lifetime employment loss per case of screen‐detected CRC in women and 50% in men, respectively. Unfortunately, the COVID‐19 pandemic reduced the screening rate down to 30% recently. 28 Our estimation of the 45%–50% reduction of lifetime employment loss by screening would be an incentive for people, employers, and society to preserve personal productivity in addition to saving potential life losses.

The cumulative incidence rate of early colorectal adenocarcinoma (stages 0 and I) reached its peak during 2014–2015, which was later than that of advanced cancer (stages III and IV) in 2010–2011 (Table 1). Given the stage shift phenomena, the first 8 years of the national screening programme usually detected a high incidence of advanced cases. Women showed peaking of the cumulative incidence rates of stages I and II about 2 years earlier than men, which seemed to be associated with higher participation rates in women than in men when the extensive promotion of the CRC screening programme launched in 2010. These findings corroborated with previous studies that CRC screening is associated with a temporary peak and then a reduction in cancer incidence. 30 , 31

LIMITATIONS

The study had the following limitations. First, some screen‐detected cases who received lower gastrointestinal imaging studies plus sigmoidoscopy for confirmation, instead of colonoscopy, were not included in this study, which may lead to a slight underestimation of screening effectiveness. Second, we did not stratify our cohort by primary tumour locations, which may affect the long‐term survival outcomes. 32 , 33 Future studies are needed to explore how different locations of CRC affect LE and LED. Third, this study only focussed on CRC‐related loss‐of‐LED, not on work efficiency, the costs of disease care, or income deprivation. Further study with social plus personal evaluation is warranted for comprehensive estimation. 34 Fourth, given that the screening data were available for only patients collected during the period of 2010–2017, this study must assume a similar proportion of stage distribution for those collected during 2004–2009. Compared with the stage distribution of CRC during 2004–2009 reported by a previous Taiwan study (stages 0, I, II, III, and IV: 13.3%, 35.0%, 20.9%, 23.7%, and 7.2%, respectively), 23 the distributions in our study during 2010–2017 were very close for advanced CRC, namely, 23.8%, 30.2%, 16.3%, 22.7%, and 6.8% for stages 0–IV, respectively, indicating no major deviation. Fifth, this study did not consider the quality of life and was unable to quantify savings by quality‐adjusted life year estimation. However, as patients with advanced CRC would suffer more than those in early stages, our estimation of savings of loss‐of‐LE would generally underestimate that of the loss‐of‐quality adjusted LE by screening. Sixth, people who opted for screening generally have a higher health literacy, are more likely to be detected cancer at an early stage, and survive longer than those who did not. 35 Using weighted health benefits by stage‐shifting proportions in the final comparison, we were unable to control self‐selection bias because of the nature of the observational study. Seventh, as the labour force participation rates among people older than 65 years were between 7.3% and 8.8% during the study period of 2004–2018, this study underestimated the savings of LED loss by screening. Moreover, our study was mainly concerned with the employment status of a worker, so it did not capture any sick leave or reduced hours and slightly underestimated the same effect of screening.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that CRC screening is associated with the savings of lives and employment duration; however, the impact of lead time bias could not be fully excluded. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of such a programme by considering the influence of LED over productivity and lead time bias, and whether it is worthwhile to begin screening at a young age.
