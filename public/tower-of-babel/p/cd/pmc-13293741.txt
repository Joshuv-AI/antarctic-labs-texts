Ureteral obstruction caused by migration of a renal embolization coil 25 years after blunt renal trauma
Authors: Yitzhak Elkis, Jacob Zac, Vladimir Yutkin, Mordechai Duvdevani, Ofer Gofrit, Liran Zieber, Botrus Srour, Leonid Boyarsky, Guy Hidas, Amitay Lorber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal arterial embolization is a well-established treatment for renal trauma, with most reported complications occurring early after the procedure. We report a case of ureteral obstruction caused by migration of a renal embolization coil 25 years after blunt renal trauma, managed with initial ureteral stenting followed by elective ureteroscopic removal. This case highlights that embolization-related complications may present long after the original intervention and should be considered in patients presenting with renal colic and a remote history of renal embolization.

Introduction

Renal arterial embolization is widely used in the management of renal trauma and selected renal vascular abnormalities, offering effective hemorrhage control while preserving renal parenchyma 1,2. Reported complications typically occur in the early post-procedural period, whereas late complications are considered uncommon 3,4. Migration of embolization coils into the urinary tract has been rarely described and is limited to isolated case reports, most often in the setting of prior percutaneous renal interventions 5, 6, 7, 8. We present a case of ureteral obstruction caused by migration of a renal embolization coil occurring 25 years after blunt renal trauma.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old male presented to the emergency department with acute left flank pain. He denied fever, dysuria, hematuria, or lower urinary tract symptoms. His medical history was notable for blunt trauma to the left flank in 1999, complicated by severe renal hemorrhage that required transfusion of four units of packed red blood cells and two angiographic embolization sessions targeting bleeding vessels in the upper and lower poles of the left kidney. Following these interventions, the patient remained asymptomatic with stable renal function for 25 years.

On presentation, physical examination revealed left flank tenderness. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a serum creatinine level of 1.14 mg/dL. Computed tomography showed migration of a metallic embolization coil into the left ureter, resulting in ureteral obstruction (Fig. 1A). The patient subsequently underwent elective ureteroscopy, during which the migrated coil was visualized within the ureter and successfully extracted using an endoscopic basket (Fig. 3A and B). Discussion

Embolization coil migration is a rare complication of renal arterial embolization and is most commonly reported shortly after the procedure. Very late migration occurring decades after the original intervention appears to be exceedingly rare and, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported.

Most previously published cases of coil migration into the urinary collecting system have occurred following percutaneous renal procedures, such as percutaneous nephrolithotomy or partial nephrectomy with violation of the urinary collecting system, in which direct access to the collecting system is established.9 In contrast, embolization in the present case was performed for blunt renal trauma, without intentional violation of the collecting system. This distinction suggests a different mechanism of migration.

A possible mechanism may involve slow erosion of the embolization coil through the renal parenchyma toward the collecting system over time, eventually resulting in ureteral migration. The selective migration of only one of two coils further supports a localized and progressive process rather than pre-existing anatomic communication.

From a clinical perspective, this case underscores the importance of considering prior renal vascular interventions in the differential diagnosis of renal colic or unexplained ureteral obstruction. A staged approach consisting of initial ureteral stenting followed by elective ureteroscopic removal allowed safe and effective management without the need for prolonged drainage or additional interventions.

Conclusion

Renal arterial embolization coils can migrate and cause clinically significant ureteral obstruction long after placement. Delayed migration should be considered in patients presenting with renal colic and a history of renal embolization, even many years after the original procedure.
