Cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) in Ecuadorian adult women
Authors: Yini Enrique Zambrano-Solórzano, Yovana Huayto-Cupi, Julio Cjuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo adapt the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) to the Ecuadorian context and examine its psychometric properties in a sample of adult women.Materials and methodsA psychometric study was conducted using non-probability sampling. The sample comprised 746 Ecuadorian women aged 18 to 69 years from the Coastal, Andean, and Amazon regions of Ecuador, all of whom had been in a cohabiting intimate relationship for at least one year. Cultural adaptation was carried out through expert judgment by five specialists and a focus group of 10 women, which evaluated item clarity, language comprehensibility, and the adequacy of response options. Subsequently, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), multigroup CFA to test measurement invariance, and internal consistency analyses using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega were performed.ResultsThe Ecuadorian version of the WAST exhibited a unidimensional structure with adequate fit indices (CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.043) and excellent reliability (α = 0.883, ω = 0.902). Measurement invariance was confirmed across age groups, marital status, educational level, and region. In addition, a moderate, positive, and significant correlation with the PHQ-9 was found (Spearman’s rho = 0.543, p < 0.001), supporting the convergent validity of the instrument.ConclusionsThe Ecuadorian version of the WAST demonstrates evidence of internal structure validity, reliability, measurement invariance, and convergent validity. Its implementation is recommended in health care settings for the early detection of intimate partner violence and in scientific research.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence is recognized as one of the major public health problems and a serious violation of human rights worldwide [1]. The World Health Organization [2] estimates that approximately one in three women has experienced some form of physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner at some point in her lifetime. In Latin America and the Caribbean, it is estimated that 43 million women aged 15 to 49 years have experienced intimate partner violence at least once [3]. In the region, prevalence rates remain alarmingly high. The Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean [4] reported that in 2021 at least 4,473 women were victims of femicide across 29 countries and territories, reflecting the magnitude of the phenomenon.

In Ecuador, the situation presents similarly concerning characteristics. According to the National Institute of Statistics and Census (INEC, [5]), six out of ten women have experienced some form of violence during their lifetime, with psychological violence being the most prevalent (56.75%), followed by physical (36.5%), sexual (29%), and economic or patrimonial violence (15.25%).

Furthermore, the Registro Único de Violencia contra las Mujeres, RUV, [6] reports that in 60% of assault cases, the victim maintains an intimate relationship with the aggressor, and that most reports occur within the household (63.09%). Despite this high prevalence, Ecuador still lacks preventive screening instruments that are routinely implemented within health care services.

In contrast, other countries in the region, such as Peru, Chile, Mexico, and Bolivia, have incorporated the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) into their primary health care systems, enabling earlier detection of intimate partner violence cases [7–9].

The WAST has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties across multiple international studies. For instance, in Paraguay, the instrument achieved a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α = 0.909), indicating high internal consistency [10], while in Chile, it showed a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 96.43%, supporting its diagnostic accuracy in clinical settings [7].

In Peru, recent research has confirmed its validity and reliability among women receiving care at primary health care centers in Metropolitan Lima [11, 12].

Theoretically, the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) is grounded in an ecological and multidimensional framework of intimate partner violence, which conceptualizes abuse as a relational phenomenon characterized by tension, coercion, and power imbalances. From this perspective, the instrument assesses two core dimensions: (1) tension and difficulties within the relationship, and (2) the presence of direct abusive behaviors, both emotional and physical. These conceptual foundations are derived from the initial work of Brown et al., [13] and are aligned with the ecological model proposed by Heise [14], which has been widely applied to understand the dynamics of gender-based violence.

Given this context, it is necessary to adapt the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) to the Ecuadorian context and to analyze its psychometric properties in a sample of adult women. This initiative will enable the early identification of intimate partner violence, thereby helping to prevent adverse consequences and potentially benefit Ecuadorian women who are in intimate relationships.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a non-experimental psychometric design [15] with a non-probabilistic sample of Ecuadorian adult women from the Coastal, Andean, and Amazon regions of the country.

Participants

The study included a sample of 746 adult women aged 18 to 69 years. Participants were selected using a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method, as only adult women who met the established inclusion criteria were enrolled. These criteria included age range, Ecuadorian nationality, marital status (married or in a de facto/cohabiting union), and a minimum of one year of cohabitation with an intimate partner. This sampling approach is appropriate for psychometric study designs, as it allows access to a specific population with the necessary characteristics to adequately evaluate the measurement properties of the instrument [15, 16]. Participants of foreign nationality, those who did not complete the questionnaire, or those who provided incomplete responses were excluded from the study.

Among the Ecuadorian adult women who participated in the study, the majority were aged between 18 and 39 years (n = 364; 48.8%). Most participants were married (n = 427; 57.2%), 313 (42.0%) had attained higher education, and 309 (41.4%) had been cohabiting with their intimate partner for 1 to 10 years. Additionally, 422 participants (56.6%) resided in urban areas, and 272 (36.5%) were from the Andean region (Table 1).

Instruments

The Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) was adapted into Spanish in Chile by Binfa et al., [7]. It was later adapted to the Peruvian context [9], which served as the reference version for the present adaptation process. The instrument consists of eight items that assess relationship tension, difficulty in conflict resolution, and the presence of violent episodes, including physical, psychological, sexual, and economic abuse. Responses are rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 to 3, with item-specific response anchors. Item 1 assesses relationship tension (1 = no tension; 2 = some tension; 3 = high tension), Item 2 evaluates difficulty in resolving disagreements (1 = no difficulty; 2 = some difficulty; 3 = high difficulty), and Items 3 through 8 assess abusive behaviors using the response options (1 = never; 2 = sometimes; 3 = many times). In the Peruvian adapted version, adequate content validity was established using Aiken’s V coefficient (V = 0.89–1.00).

Additionally, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that a unidimensional model demonstrated superior fit, with the following indices: χ² = 48.037; GFI = 0.997; CFI = 0.994; TLI = 0.992; SRMR = 0.049; and RMSEA = 0.058. Regarding reliability, the instrument showed satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) = 0.86 and McDonald’s omega (ω) = 0.90, indicating that the instrument is reliable [9].

For convergent validity, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [17] was used. The instrument was adapted into Spanish by Diez et al., [18] and has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties. Multiple studies have confirmed a robust unidimensional structure as well as high levels of internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.80 to 0.89 [18] and omega coefficients exceeding 0.85 in Spanish-speaking and Ecuadorian populations [19]. These findings support the use of the PHQ-9 as a valid and reliable criterion measure for examining the relationship between intimate partner violence and depressive symptomatology in the present study.

The PHQ-9 is a self-administered, 9-item instrument designed for the screening, diagnosis, monitoring, and severity assessment of depression. Participants are asked to report the frequency of symptoms experienced during the two weeks preceding the assessment. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, corresponding to “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “nearly every day,” respectively. Total scores range from 0 to 27, with cut-off points of 5, 10, 15, and 20 indicating mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR, 2022) and American Psychological Association guidelines [20].

Procedure

To adapt the Peruvian Spanish version of the WAST to Ecuadorian Spanish, the items and response options were presented in a validation form to a panel of expert judges in printed format and delivered in person. The experts (i.e., five psychologists) were clinical and family psychologists with at least two years of professional experience in treating cases of intimate partner violence and intrafamilial violence among Ecuadorian women. The experts reviewed the items, provided recommendations for improvement, and evaluated the relevance, clarity, and representativeness of each item.

Following the approval of a preliminary version of the WAST for Ecuadorian women, a focus group was organized with the participation of 10 women who were victims of intimate partner violence. After providing informed consent to participate in the study, the participants completed the questionnaire and were subsequently asked about the clarity and comprehensibility of the items and the wording used in each statement. For this purpose, the following questions will be used: (1) Did you find any unfamiliar words or were poorly used in the survey questions? (2) Was the question difficult to understand? Are there any recommendations for improvement? After this stage, the research team approved the final Ecuadorian version of the WAST.

Subsequently, data collection was conducted by a trained survey administrator using the virtual platform Google Forms. The questionnaire initially presented the objective of the study and the informed consent form; once consent was obtained, participants were able to complete the survey items.

The online form was distributed through various digital channels, including Gmail, WhatsApp, and Facebook. In parallel, the survey administrator monitored responses across these platforms to ensure adequate data collection. Data were collected between June and August 2025.

Statistical analysis

Initially, Aiken’s V coefficient was calculated based on expert judgment from specialists in intimate partner violence, who provided both quantitative and qualitative ratings for each item. Items were considered content valid when V > 0.70. In addition, a focus group with 10 women was conducted to qualitatively assess item clarity, language comprehensibility, and the adequacy of response options in relation to the instrument’s objectives. Participants reported that the items were clear, the language was understandable, and the response options were appropriate, and therefore no substantial item modifications were required.

Subsequently, descriptive analyses were performed. For numerical variables, measures of central tendency and dispersion were estimated, along with skewness and kurtosis, considering values of g₁ and g₂ < 3.0 as indicative of an approximately normal distribution. For categorical variables, absolute and relative frequencies were calculated.

To evaluate the validity of the internal structure, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimation method, which is appropriate for constructs measured with Likert-type ordinal items. Model fit was considered acceptable when CFI and TLI values exceeded 0.90, and SRMR and RMSEA values were below 0.08. In addition, a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) diagram was generated, considering items to be valid when factor loadings were λ > 0.30.

Furthermore, internal consistency coefficients were estimated using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, with values greater than 0.70 considered indicative of acceptable reliability. Complementing these classical reliability estimates, model-based reliability and convergent validity were evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and standardized factor loadings were computed from standardized loadings and error variances, with 95% confidence intervals estimated via bootstrap resampling. These indices were calculated for both the adapted WAST and the PHQ-9 to ensure measurement adequacy within the study sample.

To examine measurement invariance of the instrument across different sociodemographic groups, multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MG-CFA) was conducted. This procedure allows for the assessment of whether the instrument measures the construct equivalently across distinct subpopulations, which is an essential requirement for making valid between-group comparisons [21, 22].

The evaluation was performed sequentially, considering four levels of invariance: configural, metric, scalar (or strong), and strict invariance. To determine whether a more constrained model maintained a level of fit comparable to that of a less constrained model, changes in the Comparative Fit Index (ΔCFI) and/or the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (ΔRMSEA) were examined. Measurement invariance was assumed when these differences were less than 0.01 [23, 24].

Convergent validity, as a form of evidence based on relationships with other variables, was estimated using Spearman’s rho correlation between scores on the WAST and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio statistical software, version 2025.09.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Universidad Peruana Unión under Approval Report No. 2025-CE-EPG-00026. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring ethical standards of justice, confidentiality, and autonomy by obtaining informed consent. Given the sensitive nature of intimate partner violence research and the inclusion of a focus group component, additional violence-specific ethical safeguards were implemented. Participation was voluntary, confidentiality was reinforced through data anonymization and the conduct of group discussions in a private and secure setting, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. In cases where emotional distress or disclosure of ongoing violence occurred, participants were provided with information on local referral pathways, including mental health and social support services.

Results

The descriptive analysis of the WAST showed item means (M) ranging from 1.15 to 1.95, with standard deviations (SD) between 0.45 and 0.82, indicating moderate variability in responses. Regarding distributional properties, skewness (g₁) and kurtosis (g₂) values were below 3.0, supporting the assumption of approximate normality, with the exception of Item 8, which showed a skewness value of 8.20.

With respect to content validity, all items achieved an Aiken’s V coefficient of 1.00 across the three evaluated criteria—clarity, representativeness, and relevance—with 95% confidence intervals ranging from 0.86 to 1.00 (Table 2).

The Ecuadorian adaptation of the WAST demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega both reaching 0.88 (95% CI: 0.87–0.89 and 0.86–0.89, respectively), and high composite reliability (CR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.87–0.90). The PHQ-9 also showed strong reliability (ω = 0.84; α = 0.83) and adequate convergent validity (AVE = 0.52; CR = 0.91). Multigroup factorial invariance of the WAST was supported across age, years of cohabitation, marital status, educational level, place of residence, and region at the configural, metric, scalar, and strict levels. At the configural level, model fit was consistently adequate, with χ²=77.93–120.01 and df = 40–60, together with CFI=0.997–0.998 and RMSEA=0.050–0.063, indicating the same factorial structure across groups. When metric invariance constraints were imposed, models remained stable (χ²=89.61–152.89; df = 47–74; CFI=0.996–0.998; RMSEA=0.049–0.065), with trivial changes (ΔCFI = 0.000; ΔRMSEA=−0.003 to 0.002). Under scalar invariance, model fit continued to be satisfactory (χ²=81.89–136.49; df = 47–74; CFI=0.997–0.998; RMSEA=0.041–0.058), and incremental variation was minimal (ΔCFI = 0.000–0.001), meeting the decision criterion of ΔCFI and/or ΔRMSEA < 0.01. At the strict level, models preserved overall fit (χ²=96.77–178.71; df = 55–90; CFI=0.996–0.998; RMSEA=0.045–0.063) with similarly small changes (ΔCFI=−0.001 to 0.000; ΔRMSEA = 0.000–0.010). Therefore, according to the threshold of ΔCFI or ΔRMSEA < 0.01, the evidence supports full measurement invariance of the instrument across all evaluated multigroup comparisons (Table 5).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to adapt the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) to the Ecuadorian context and to examine its psychometric properties in a sample of adult women. The findings support that the adapted version of the WAST is a valid and reliable instrument for the screening of intimate partner violence within this specific context.

First, content validity, assessed using Aiken’s V coefficient, was excellent (V = 1.00 for all items). This indicates that the panel of experts judged the WAST items to be clear, relevant, and representative of the intimate partner violence construct for the Ecuadorian population. This finding is consistent with results reported in the Peruvian adaptation of the instrument [9], suggesting that the Spanish version of the WAST has a strong conceptual and semantic foundation that is transculturally comprehensible within the region.

Regarding internal structure, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) corroborated the unidimensional structure of the WAST, yielding excellent model fit indices (CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.043). This unidimensional structure, which integrates different forms of violence (psychological, physical, sexual, and economic) into a single latent factor of “intimate partner violence,” is consistent with the original structure proposed by the instrument’s developers [13] and with prior validations conducted in other contexts, such as those reported in Chile [7] and Peru [9].

All standardized factor loadings (λ) were high (λ > 0.71), indicating that each item contributes meaningfully to the measurement of the global construct. Notably, Item 6 (“Has your partner ever hit you?”) exhibited the highest factor loading (λ = 0.96), suggesting that physical abuse may be a particularly strong indicator of the presence of intimate partner violence within this population.

A crucial finding of this study is the evidence of strict measurement invariance of the WAST across multiple sociodemographic variables, including age, years of cohabitation, marital status, educational level, area of residence, and geographic region. This indicates that the instrument measures the intimate partner violence construct equivalently across these distinct subgroups. The absence of significant differences in fit indices (ΔCFI ≤ 0.001, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.001) across configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance models ensures that comparisons of scores—for example, between women from different regions of Ecuador or with different educational levels—are valid and not biased by the measurement instrument.

As noted by Milfont and Fischer [22], establishing measurement invariance is an essential requirement to ensure that a psychological instrument allows for fair and unbiased comparisons across groups. This property is fundamental for both research and clinical practice, as it enables the equitable and comparative use of the WAST within a diverse population.

Regarding reliability, internal consistency coefficients were excellent, with Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.883) and McDonald’s omega (ω = 0.902), both exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. These values are comparable to and slightly higher than those reported in the Peruvian adaptation (α = 0.86, ω = 0.90) [9], further confirming the high reliability and precision of the WAST for consistently measuring intimate partner violence among Ecuadorian women.

Convergent validity of the WAST was confirmed through a moderate and statistically significant positive correlation with the PHQ-9 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.543, p < 0.001). This finding was expected, as extensive literature has consistently documented the strong relationship between intimate partner violence and depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is widely used both as a screening instrument with established cut-off points and as a continuous measure of depressive symptom severity in non-clinical and community-based samples, including cross-sectional studies, which supports the interpretation of this association [25–27] In line with this evidence, the World Health Organization identifies intimate partner violence as a serious public health problem, closely associated with increased rates of depression, anxiety, and suicide among women [28].

Similarly, Sardinha et al., [3], using global and regional data, report a persistently high prevalence of intimate partner violence, which is linked to a greater burden of depressive disorders. Earlier, Devries et al., [29] had already demonstrated at a global level that exposure to intimate partner violence significantly increases the risk of depression and other mental health disorders, further reinforcing the association observed in the present study.

Taken together, these findings confirm that intimate partner violence remains a severe global issue with profound implications for women’s mental health. According to the World Health Organization and the Pan American Health Organization [30], violence perpetrated by an intimate partner is directly associated with deterioration of psychological well-being, constituting both a major public health concern and a violation of human rights. These organizations emphasize that the emotional, physical, and social consequences of such violence substantially increase the risk of anxiety, depression, stress-related disorders, and other high-impact psychological conditions, a conclusion further supported by the evidence reported by Sardinha et al., [3] and previously established by Devries et al., [29].

Accordingly, the results of the present investigation align with this body of evidence. In the Ecuadorian sample, higher WAST scores—indicating greater exposure to intimate partner violence—were associated with higher levels of depressive symptomatology, which not only supports the convergent validity of the instrument but also underscores the urgent need to integrate mental health services into care programs for women experiencing intimate partner violence in Ecuador. From a measurement perspective, these findings should be interpreted within the broader landscape of intimate partner violence screening tools, which is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in instruments, recall periods, and threshold criteria. Recent systematic reviews have highlighted this variability and emphasized the need for standardized, culturally adapted tools to improve comparability and clinical utility across settings [31, 32]. In this context, the validation of the WAST in Ecuador contributes to ongoing efforts to strengthen culturally relevant assessment of intimate partner violence and its mental health correlates in diverse populations.

Among the limitations of this study is the use of a non-probabilistic sample, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of Ecuadorian women. Future research should seek to employ more representative sampling strategies to enhance the external validity of the results.

Another relevant limitation concerns construct validity. Although this aspect was assessed, future studies could further explore it by incorporating additional criterion variables related to mental health or psychosocial well-being, in order to strengthen the existing evidence.

Similarly, the use of a self-administered instrument such as the WAST may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, given the sensitive nature of the topic, which could influence participants’ responses. Finally, the fact that data collection was conducted in health care settings may limit the transferability of the findings to other community-based or non-clinical contexts.

Among the strengths of this study, it is the first to adapt and validate the WAST specifically in Ecuador, taking into account the country’s geographic and sociocultural diversity. The robust psychometric results obtained provide a scientifically sound and culturally appropriate tool for the early detection of intimate partner violence in clinical and research settings in Ecuador.

In conclusion, the Ecuadorian version of the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) demonstrates strong evidence of validity—including content, structural, and convergent validity—as well as excellent reliability (α = 0.883; ω = 0.902) and measurement invariance. Therefore, it can be used by health care professionals and researchers in Ecuador to identify cases of violence against women.
