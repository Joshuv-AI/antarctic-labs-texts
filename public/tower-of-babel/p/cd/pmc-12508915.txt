One-Pot Synthesis of PBAT from PET Waste Using Metal-DES Catalytic Systems for Sustainable Packaging Applications
Authors: Naresh Kathula, Abdul Mannan Mulla, Saleem Mohd, Vineet Aniya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The accumulation of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) waste poses a major environmental concern, highlighting the need for effective recycling methods. Converting PET waste into high-value polymer materials offers a viable strategy to address plastic pollution. This research investigates the one-pot synthesis of poly­(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) using various metal catalysts in combination with deep eutectic solvents (DESs) as cocatalysts. Different metal-based catalystszinc, tin, titanium, and antimonywere tested with DES systems such as choline chloride-glycerol (ChCl-Gly), choline chloride-para-toluene sulfonic acid (ChCl-PTSA), and choline chloride-urea (ChCl-Urea). The antimony trioxide/ChCl-Gly system demonstrated superior catalytic activity, yielding PBAT with molecular weights of 70,000–75 000 g/mol, followed by Ti > Sn > Zn. Optimized reaction conditions (0.25 wt % catalyst and 0.25 wt % ChCl-Gly at 220 °C) improved polymer characteristics over conventional methods. The resulting PBAT was analyzed for its physio-chemical, thermal, and mechanical properties, and PBAT composites were developed for packaging applications. Key thermal and mechanical parameters, including melt flow index (MFI), tensile strength, and elongation at break, were measured. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) confirmed the polymer’s thermal stability, demonstrating its potential for commercial packaging. This innovative method presents an alternative chemical recycling route for PET waste utilization, contributing to sustainable packaging material development.

Introduction

Poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) is one of the most commonly used materials, typically found in various forms, including fibers, films, and bottles. However, its nondegradability poses significant environmental challenges, leading to persistent plastic waste accumulation. Conventional disposal methods, including landfilling and incineration, contribute to pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. Mechanical recycling reduces polymer properties over multiple cycles due to thermal and mechanical stress, necessitating the development of sustainable recycling and upcycling strategies. To overcome these challenges, chemical recycling has been explored to address PET waste accumulation, and different recycling processes are mentioned in Table . Different Chemical Recycling Processes of Waste PET into Valuable Chemicals

Extensive research has documented various chemical recycling approaches for polyethylene terephthalate (PET), including thermal decomposition (yielding oil), hydrolytic breakdown (producing terephthalic acid), alcohol-mediated degradation (generating dimethyl terephthalate or dioctyl terephthalate) , glycolytic cleavage with glycols (forming bis­(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate), and ammonia treatment (resulting in terephthalamide). In a specific application, researchers have converted postconsumer PET waste into polybutylene terephthalate (PBT) copolymers through transesterification with long-chain diols (1,4-butanediol, 1,4-cyclohexanedimethanol, and 1,6-hexanediol) using titanium isopropoxide and antimony trioxide catalyst systems.

A promising approach in PET upcycling involves transforming it into new aliphatic-aromatic copolyesters, such as poly­(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT). This biodegradable polymer combines flexibility with environmental compatibility, making it particularly valuable for packaging solutions and agricultural films. Its compatibility with large-scale processing through conventional extrusion and film-blowing has established PBAT as a strong contender to replace single-use plastics. Originally commercialized as Ecoflex by BASF in the 1990s, PBAT has contributed to the remarkable expansion of the biodegradable plastics sector. Market data reveal substantial growth from 94,800 t in 2005 to 214,400 t by 2010, representing a robust 17.7% CAGR. Packaging dominates sectoral demand (39% share), encompassing rigid containers, flexible films, paper coatings, and disposable foodware. Additional applications include protective loose-fill materials (24%), shopping/carry bags (21%), textile fibers (9%), and specialized uses (7%). PBAT, often blended with other biodegradable polymers like polylactic acid (PLA), polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA), polybutylene succinate (PBS), and starch, enhances mechanical properties while maintaining biodegradability under industrial composting conditions, making it suitable for flexible packaging, films, and disposable items. For instance, Wang et al. (2023) demonstrated that PBAT/nanocellulose composites exhibit enhanced tensile strength and superior water vapor barrier properties, making them promising for food packaging applications. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2024) reported that blending PBAT with thermoplastic starch (TPS) resulted in flexible, compostable films with improved elongation at break. Kumar et al. (2024) further highlighted that lignin incorporation enhances UV resistance and oxidative stability in PBAT composites. Additionally, compatibilizers such as maleic anhydride-grafted polymers have been employed to optimize filler dispersion and strengthen interfacial adhesion, as evidenced by Li et al. (2015).

The industrial production of PBAT typically involves a two-stage polycondensation process. In the initial esterification step, adipic acid (AA) and terephthalic acid (PTA) react with excess 1,4-butanediol (BDO) at 200–220 °C to form PBAT oligomers. This is followed by polycondensation at 250–260 °C to achieve high-molecular-weight polymers. Conventional catalysts for this process include organometallic compounds of titanium, tin, and zinc, typically in oxide, alkoxide, or acetate forms. ,, Recent advances have introduced rare earth metal stearates (Nd, Y, La, Dy) as effective single-component catalysts for PBAT synthesis from dimethyl terephthalate, AA, and BDO, yielding high molecular weight biodegradable copolyesters. , Alternative synthesis routes utilize recycled PET as the aromatic source, combined with AA and BDO using titanium isopropoxide (TIPT) and phosphoric acid catalyst systems. However, this approach typically produces polymers with a melting point of 113 °C and a number-average molecular weight of around 20 000 g/mol, potentially restricting certain applications. Zhenlin Jiang et al. (2024) synthesized PBAT by alcoholyzing polybutylene terephthalate (PBT) to obtain bishydroxybutyl terephthalate (BHBT) and coupling it with bis­(4-hydroxybutyl) adipate (BHAT) via esterification and condensation using a 1:1 mass ratio of antimony ethylene glycol and tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT) as the catalyst. Bingxue Jiang et al. (2023) prepared PBAT via two-step synthesis: PTA, AA, and BDO (1.3:1 diol/diacid) were esterified with 0.15 mol % titanium­(IV) butoxide (TBT) at 180–220 °C under nitrogen until >90% water removal, yielding an oligomer. Polycondensation at 230 °C, 100 Pa for 2.5 h produced PBAT, which was subsequently copolymerized with PGA to form PBAT-co-PGA copolyesters exhibiting high degradability for packaging and agricultural films. Menglu et al. (2022) synthesized PBT and polybutylene adipate (PBA) from discarded PET before reacting to form PBAT using titanium tetraisopropoxide as a catalyst, yielding a product with a molecular weight limited to 45 000 g/mol. It requires additional synthesis steps for PBT and PBA before forming PBA. However, these metallic catalysts can lead to coloration due to various side reactions such as THF formation and polymer degradation over time of reaction at 220–240 °C. DESs (deep eutectic solvents) are emerging as “green” solvents and catalysts for plastic degradation, offering advantages like tunable properties and high thermal stability over traditional methods. They can effectively depolymerize various plastic types along with metal catalysts, making them a promising solution for plastic waste management.

The hypothesis of the work is to develop a sustainable and efficient one-pot synthesis method for upcycling polyethylene terephthalate (PET) waste into polybutylene adipate-co-terephthalate (PBAT), a compostable aliphatic-aromatic copolyester, using a synergistic catalytic system comprising metal catalysts and a deep eutectic solvent (DES). The metal catalyst such as Zn, Sn, Ti, and Sb was selected due to their established roles in transesterification and polycondensation reactions, as well as their favorable catalytic activity, thermal stability, and cost-effectiveness. The combinations in DES are based on their solubility, reducing reaction severity and contributing to a more controlled and efficient polycondensation process. This synergistic interaction between metal catalysts and deep eutectic solvents (DESs) significantly enhances PBAT synthesis by combining their complementary functions. Metal catalysts act as Lewis’s acids, activating PET’s ester bonds for efficient transesterification and polycondensation. Without DES, these catalysts alone led to incomplete PET conversion, lower molecular weights, and product discoloration. The addition of DES improves catalyst dispersion and PET solubility through hydrogen bonding and polarity modulation. DES interacts with metal catalysts and reactive intermediates, stabilizing them and preventing catalyst deactivation under high-temperature conditions. This boosts catalytic activity, enabling rapid and complete PET solubilization within 2 h. It further promotes homogeneous and efficient transesterification while suppressing side reactions like BDO degradation. DES maintains reaction stability by moderating local acidity and improving system homogeneity. The combined effect ensures high molecular weight, thermal stability, and color stability of PBAT.

The overall importance of the present work is to develop an efficient process that aims to offer an alternative route for the preparation of compostable PBAT and tackle the plastic waste management. The synthesized PBAT is evaluated for its thermal and mechanical properties to assess its suitability for packaging applications, with potential for further blending with polylactic acid (PLA) or starch to create eco-friendly composite materials.

Experimental Section

Materials

PET flakes and butyl stannoic acid catalyst were supplied by CDG PET Chem. Ltd. Adipic acid, 1,4-butanediol, and stearic acid were purchased from Finar Chemical Limited. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn­(Ac)2·2H2O), tin acetate, antimony trioxide, zinc oxide, tin oxide, and titanium isopropoxide (IV) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Choline chloride, glycerol, para-toluene sulfonic acid (PTSA), and urea were purchased from TCI Chemicals.

Preparation of DES Catalyst

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) were synthesized by combining choline chloride with hydrogen bond donors (glycerol, urea, or p-toluene sulfonic acid) in a fixed 1:2 molar ratio. The mixtures were homogenized under continuous heating at optimized temperatures (80 °C for glycerol-based systems, 60 °C for urea and PTSA formulations) until complete dissolution was achieved, as evidenced by the formation of transparent, uniform liquids , shown in Figure .

Reaction scheme for the preparation of deep eutectic solvents (DESs). (a) Choline chloride-glycerol DES, (b) choline chloride-Urea, and (c) choline chloride-p-toluene sulfonic acid (PTSA).

The interaction between choline chloride and the other components occurs through hydrogen bonding, where the hydroxyl groups (OH) of glycerol or the amine groups (NH2) of urea and PTSA form hydrogen bonds with the chloride ion (Cl–) of choline chloride. This network of hydrogen bonds stabilizes the mixture, resulting in a uniform, colorless liquid suitable for use as a DES.

Synthesis of PBAT Polymer

Initially, PET bottles are collected and sorted to remove any contaminants. The labels and caps are meticulously removed to separate non-PET components, ensuring only the bottle bodies proceed further. These bottle bodies are then subjected to a comprehensive washing process to eliminate dirt, adhesives, and other impurities that could interfere with subsequent reactions or material quality. Once cleaned, the bottles are fed into a shredding machine, where they are broken down into small, uniformly sized granules shown in Figure . PET (100 g, 0.520 mol) plastic waste flakes are introduced into a 500 mL four-neck glass reactor alongside 1,4-butane diol (140 g, 0.520 mol), adipic acid (76.25 g,1.55 mol), and the catalyst (0.25 wt % of the metal catalyst and 0.25 wt % of the DES cocatalyst to PET) added. The reaction was carried out through a multistage reaction process.

Schematic diagram for the preparation of PBAT from PET waste.

The reaction pathways toward the preparation of PBAT polymer are shown in Figure . Initially, esterification between adipic acid and 1,4-butanediol was carried out at 140–150 °C to form poly­(butylene adipate) (PBA) prepolymeric units. During PET depolymerization at 190–200 °C, the metal catalyst (Sb2O3) acts as a Lewis acid, coordinating with the ester carbonyl groups of PET, thereby weakening the CO bonds and facilitating nucleophilic attack by 1,4-butanediol. This results in transesterification, PET chain cleavage, and formation of PBT oligomers with ethylene glycol release. Simultaneously, the deep eutectic solvent (DES, such as choline chloride–glycerol) enhances the reaction by stabilizing intermediates and transition states through hydrogen bonding and proton transfer, effectively lowering activation energy and improving catalyst dispersion. In the subsequent polycondensation phase at 220–240 °C, the metal catalyst further facilitates ester interchange between PBT and PBA segments, promoting PBAT chain growth. The DES maintains homogeneity and thermal stability while also suppressing side reactions such as tetrahydrofuran (THF) formation by controlling local polarity and solubilizing monomers.

PBAT Composite Preparation

The PBAT granules were produced via a corotating twin-screw extrusion method, as illustrated in Figure , which depicts the extruder’s schematic layout along with the specific screw arrangement employed. The extruder, a Fly Tech Engineering India Model FUE-1LC, featured a throughput capacity of 2–5 kg/h and an L/D ratio of 40. The system included seven heating zones, a die, three volumetric feeders, a water bath, and a pelletizing unit. The twin screws, each with a 22 mm diameter, were designed as parallel, intermeshing, corotating, and segmented to enhance material processing efficiency as shown in Figure . To minimize barrel clogging, 0.1 wt % stearic acid was incorporated into the premix as a lubricant. Processing conditions included a screw speed of 30 rpm and a temperature profile set at 95/110/120/120/120/120/120/120 °C across the eight heating barrels and die. Moisture was eliminated through an atmospheric vent positioned in the fifth heating zone. The extruded strands were subsequently air-dried and pelletized into granules with dimensions of 2–3 mm in diameter and 3–5 mm in length, exhibiting a density range of 1.2–1.4 g/cm3. The developed granules were subjected to hydraulic pressing using a machine (S.C. Dey & Co., India) to fabricate thin films (1 mm) and tensile test specimens. The process was carried out at a pressure of 70 MPa with both upper and lower plates maintained at 130 °C for 5 min, followed by cooling to 30 °C under constant pressure.

Characterization Techniques

The 1H-nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis was performed on a Varian Inova 500 MHz spectrometer to characterize both the synthesized PBAT and the DES catalysts. For the PBAT product, chloroform-d (CDCl3) was used as the solvent, whereas DMSO-d6 was used for the DES catalysts due to their solubility requirement. The chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) and calibrated against known reference compounds for accurate comparison.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 spectrometer, with spectra recorded across the 400 to 4000 cm–1 wavenumber range. Spectra were collected with a resolution of 4 cm–1, averaging 32 scans per sample. Powdered samples were directly placed on the ATR crystal. Baseline correction and normalization were applied for consistency. Measurements conducted in triplicate demonstrated high reproducibility, with peak position deviations of less than ± 2 cm–1.

Molecular weight distribution of PBAT was determined using a Waters GPC system with Empower software and a refractive index (RI) detector. The analysis was performed on a Phenomenex Phenogel column (5 μm, 10E4 Å, 300 × 7.8 mm; code OOH-0445-KO) at a column oven temperature of 30 °C. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) was used as both the mobile phase and diluent, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and an injection volume of 20 μL. The run time was set to 20 min. Calibration was performed using six polystyrene standards with molecular weights of 2880, 6540, 22,800, 50,600, 129,000, and 321,000 Da, each prepared at 2 mg/mL in THF. PBAT samples were prepared at 0.01 g/5 mL THF. All measurements were carried out in triplicate, and the results showed less than 5% relative standard deviation (RSD), confirming reproducibility and reliability of the method.

The crystalline structure of the product was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advanced diffractometer (Germany). The analysis was performed at 40 kV and 30 mA, with diffraction patterns recorded over a 2θ range of 0° to 80° using an X-ray wavelength of 1.5 Å. The crystallinity index was calculated by deconvoluting the crystalline and amorphous peak areas using Origin software. Instrument calibration was carried out using a corundum (α-Al2O3) standard (NIST SRM 1976) to ensure accuracy in peak position and instrument alignment. Reproducibility was verified through triplicate measurements, showing consistent diffraction patterns.

The surface morphology of the samples was analyzed using a Hitachi S-3000 N scanning electron microscope (SEM), which provided high-resolution imaging of the material’s structural features.

The thermal behavior of the samples was investigated using a Q200 differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, TA Instruments, USA) with a constant nitrogen purge of 50 mL/min. A three-step thermal program was employed for all specimens: (1) initial heating from −50 to 200 °C at 10 °C/min (first heating scan), followed by a 5 min isothermal hold; (2) subsequent cooling to −50 °C with a 5 min equilibration period (cooling scan); and (3) final reheating to 200 °C at the same rate (second heating scan). Samples weighing 6–8 mg were analyzed to determine key thermal parameters, including the glass transition temperature (T g), melting temperature (T m), melting enthalpy (ΔH m), and crystallization temperature (T c).

ΔHmo was the crystallization enthalpy of 100% PBAT (1/4 114 J/g).

The mechanical properties of the samples were evaluated through tensile testing using two different methodologies. Initial tests were performed on an Instron 3345 Universal Testing Machine (Norwood, USA) at a crosshead speed of 50 mm/min with dumbbell-shaped specimens. Subsequent characterization followed ASTM D638 standards using a 50 kg capacity computerized testing system (Advance Equipment, India) operating at 10 mm/min, which provided measurements of maximum tensile strength, Young’s modulus, and elongation at break. All specimens were conditioned at ambient temperature before testing, with four replicates analyzed for each sample to ensure statistical reliability.

The melt flow index (MFI) of the polymer blends was determined following ASTM D1238-10 (Procedure A) using a Melt Flow Index Tester (Advance Equipment, India). The measurement was performed at a temperature of 190 °C with a load of 2.16 kg. Before testing, the samples were conditioned at 50% relative humidity for 24 h to ensure equilibrium moisture content. The experimental methodology was adopted from a previously published work (Kesari et al., 2022). Triplicate measurements (n = 3) were performed for each sample, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation in g/10 min to ensure statistical reliability.

Results and Discussion

PBAT Polymer Synthesis and Optimization Studies

We systematically assessed various metallic catalysts for their efficacy in the single-step synthesis of PBAT polymer using recycled polyethylene terephthalate (PET) waste. The catalytic systems examined included zinc-, tin-, titanium-, and antimony-based metal complexes, which were employed in combination with deep eutectic solvent (DES) cocatalysts. Specifically, three DES formulations were evaluated: choline chloride-glycerol (ChCl-Gly), choline chloride-para-toluene sulfonic acid (ChCl-PTSA), and choline chloride-urea (ChCl-Urea).

Reactions conducted in the absence of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) resulted in PBAT with lower molecular weights with different metal catalysts, which are shown in Table . Metal oxide catalysts, such as zinc oxide and tin oxide, failed to achieve complete PET conversion even after 24 h, while titanium oxide facilitated PBAT synthesis but yielded a product with a limited molecular weight of 16,386 g/mol. In contrast, reactions employing with antimony trioxide, zinc acetate, titanium tetraisopropoxide (TITP), and tin-based catalysts, such as tin acetate and butyl stannoic acid (BSA), significantly improved PET solubility and enabled complete conversion. The observed catalytic activity followed the order: antimony trioxide > titanium > tin > zinc. However, these catalysts alone produced PBAT with limited molecular weights and undesirable coloration, as illustrated in Figure . The addition of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) as cocatalysts played a transformative role in enhancing the reaction efficiency. DES improved catalyst dispersion, increased PET solubility, and facilitated the transesterification and polycondensation processes. As an illustration, the use of antimony trioxide combined with choline chloride-glycerol (ChCl-Gly) DES enabled complete PET solubilization within just 2 h during the transesterification stage. This system produced a stable PBAT product with high molecular weight and a white, solidified appearance, even at elevated reaction temperatures (220–240 °C), without significant degradation. The DES not only enhanced the catalytic activity of antimony trioxide but also stabilized the reaction environment, preventing side reactions and degradation.

In contrast, the use of other DES systems, such as choline chloride-p-toluene sulfonic acid (ChCl-PTSA), led to increased side-product formation. ChCl-PTSA promoted the intramolecular cyclization of butane diol, resulting in the formation of tetrahydrofuran (THF) as a byproduct, which adversely affected the polymerization rate and product quality. Similarly, choline chloride-urea (ChCl-Urea) required a longer PET solubilization time (approximately 12 h) and yielded PBAT with a lower molecular weight, demonstrating the importance of selecting the appropriate DES for optimal performance. The results show that attempts to increase molecular weight by raising the reaction temperature to 220–240 °C without DES have negative consequences, such as decreased product molecular weight and discoloration. The significance of DES as a cocatalyst in maximizing the PET-to-PBAT conversion process is highlighted by these findings.

The molar ratio of 1,4-butanediol (1,4-BDO) plays a crucial role in the dissolution of PET and the molecular weight of PBAT under constant catalyst (0.25 wt %) and cocatalyst (0.25 wt % choline chloride-glycerol) loadings in an inert atmosphere, as shown in Figure . The process is inefficient at a 2:1 molar ratio of 1,4-BDO to PET, with prolonged PET solubilization times and low molecular weight (9,200 g/mol). Increasing the ratio to 2:1 improves PET dissolution, reducing solubilization time to 3 h and achieving a molecular weight of 45,286 g/mol.

Effect of 1,4-butanediol molar ratio with different metal catalysts and ChCl-Gly DES.

Further increasing the ratio to 3:1 optimizes the reaction, reducing solubilization time to just 2 h and yielding a molecular weight of 70,000–75,000 g/mol, with improved material properties. However, exceeding the 4:1 ratio causes a decline in molecular weight and stiffness due to tetrahydrofuran (THF) formation, compromising product stability. Thus, while increasing 1,4-BDO facilitates PET solubilization and enhances molecular weight up to an optimal 3:1 ratio, higher ratios introduce side reactions that negatively impact the final product’s quality.

Figure illustrates the yield of PBAT products synthesized under constant metal catalyst (0.25 wt %) and cocatalyst (0.25 wt % choline chloride-glycerol) loadings in an inert atmosphere over 8 h of reaction time. Among the catalysts tested, antimony trioxide (Sb2O3) exhibits the highest catalytic activity, achieving a maximum 92.4% yield. This superior performance is attributed to its high thermal stability and strong Lewis’s acidity at elevated temperatures (200–240 °C), which effectively accelerates both transesterification and polycondensation reactions.

Yield of PBAT at constant metal catalyst (0.25 wt %) and cocatalyst (0.25 wt % choline chloride-glycerol) loadings.

Sb2O3 plays a crucial role in breaking down PET into smaller oligomeric units while simultaneously facilitating ester bond formation, leading to the synthesis of high-molecular-weight PBAT polymer. Additionally, Sb2O3 remains chemically stable throughout the 8-h reaction without undergoing decomposition or deactivation, ensuring consistent catalytic efficiency and enhanced polymer yield under these optimized reaction conditions. Titanium isopropoxide is the second most active catalyst due to its strong Lewis acidity and ability to coordinate with ester groups, which enhances transesterification. However, it is slightly less effective than Sb2O3 because it can undergo partial decomposition or form less active species, such as titanium oxides or hydroxides, at high temperatures. Butyl stannoic acid is less active than Sb2O3 and Ti­(OiPr)4 because it may form stable intermediates or require higher activation energy to initiate the reaction. Additionally, tin-based catalysts can lead to side reactions and discoloration of the final polymer, which limits their efficiency in certain applications. Tin and zinc acetate have moderate activity in this reaction. It can catalyze transesterification and polycondensation, but it is less efficient than butyl stannoic acid due to its lower solubility in the reaction medium. The study reveals that equal loadings of metal catalysts and DES yield higher molecular weights of PBAT compared to unequal amounts. Specifically, an optimized equal loading of 0.25 wt % for both catalyst and DES produces the highest molecular weight of 75,286 g/mol with antimony trioxide (Sb2O3), while titanium oxide results in the lowest molecular weight of 18,233 g/mol. Titanium­(IV) isopropoxide achieves a molecular weight of 74,233 g/mol, closely matching that of antimony trioxide, whereas tin-based catalysts (butyl stannoic acid and tin acetate) and zinc acetate produce moderate molecular weights of 55,233 g/mol, 45,666 g/mol, and 35,666 g/mol, respectively. These results align closely with those obtained using 0.5 wt % of each metal catalyst and DES. However, increasing the catalyst and DES loadings to 1 wt % accelerates degradation at elevated temperatures, destabilizing the product and reducing molecular weight. Conversely, lower loadings of 0.125 wt % result in limited molecular weight production. Therefore, precise control of catalyst and cocatalyst loadings is crucial to balancing reactivity, molecular weight, and thermal stability in PBAT synthesis.

Stage 2, conducted at 170–200 °C for 30 min to 3 h, this process focuses on the transesterification of PET, where extended durations and elevated temperatures (190–200 °C) promote enhanced chain growth. Finally, Stage 3, carried out at 200–240 °C for 2 to 4 h, completes the polycondensation process, with higher temperatures (220–240 °C) and longer reaction times (4 h) significantly increasing the molecular weight of the PBAT product. For example, a combination of 140–150 °C in Stage 1, 190–200 °C in Stage 2, and 220–240 °C in Stage 3 yields the highest molecular weights of 70,000–75 000 g/mol. In contrast, lower temperatures or shorter durations, such as 110–120 °C in Stage 1, 170–180 °C in Stage 2, and 200–210 °C in Stage 3, result in significantly lower molecular weights of 16,000–19 000 g/mol. These results emphasize the critical role of precise temperature and time management across all stages to maximize molecular weight, enhance reaction efficiency, and ensure the thermal stability of the PBAT synthesis process.

Overall, optimized PBAT synthesis is achieved using a 1:3:1 molar ratio of PET:1,4-BDO:adipic acid, with 0.25 wt % antimony trioxide and 0.25 wt % choline chloride–glycerol DES under an inert atmosphere. The ideal temperature profile includes 140–150 °C (Stage 1), 190–200 °C (Stage 2), and 220–240 °C (Stage 3) over a total reaction time of 8 h. These conditions ensure rapid PET solubilization, suppress side reactions, and yield high molecular weights (70,000–75,000 g/mol), which are comparable to commercial PBAT (85,000–92,000 g/mol), with yield (up to 92.4%).

Thermal and Mechanical Properties of PBAT Composites

Figure shows how commercial PBAT (PBAT-C) and prepared PBAT (PBAT-P) composites break down when heated. Both materials stay stable up to around 350 °C, meaning they do not lose much weight before this temperature. After 350 °C, both PBAT samples start to decompose quickly, losing most of their weight between 350 and 450 °C. PBAT-P starts breaking down a little earlier than PBAT-C, which means it has slightly lower thermal stability. One major reason is the difference in molecular weight, as PBAT-P may have shorter polymer chains due to incomplete polymerization or chain scission during synthesis. Shorter chains break down more easily at lower temperatures, leading to earlier degradation.

TGA analysis of PBAT prepared and commercial PBAT.

Additionally, PBAT-P might contain residual catalysts, unreacted monomers, or impurities from the preparation process, which can accelerate thermal breakdown. Structural irregularities, such as branching or defects in the polymer chains, could also create weak points that make PBAT-P less resistant to heat. Another important factor is the absence of thermal stabilizers or antioxidants, which are often added to commercial PBAT to enhance its heat resistance. If PBAT-P is synthesized from PET waste, degraded oligomers or partially hydrolyzed PET fragments may be present, further reducing its thermal stability. These combined factors cause PBAT-P to start degrading at a slightly lower temperature than PBAT-C, making it less thermally stable.

Mechanical Properties of PBAT

The slight reduction in performance of the synthesized PBAT can be attributed to several structural factors, including lower molecular weight, reduced chain entanglement, and possible irregularities introduced during the PET depolymerization and subsequent repolymerization steps. The incorporation of stearic acid as a processing aid during melt extrusion may have further impacted mechanical properties. While stearic acid improves melt flow and facilitates additive dispersion, it can also act as a plasticizer or lubricant, potentially disrupting intermolecular interactions and polymer chain packing. This can reduce cohesive forces and hinder crystallinity development, resulting in diminished tensile strength and elongation at break.

Despite these variations, the synthesized PBAT demonstrates mechanical integrity sufficient for packaging applications, supported by its semicrystalline structure and balanced aromatic–aliphatic backbone. Moreover, the use of a choline chloride–glycerol deep eutectic solvent (DES) during synthesis enhances polymer uniformity and catalytic efficiency, contributing to overall material performance. Collectively, these findings suggest that the synthesized PBAT is a promising biodegradable material suitable for sustainable packaging applications.

The melt flow index (MFI) of the synthesized PBAT was found to be 5.47 ± 1.8 g/10 min, which is slightly higher than the commercial PBAT value of 3.24 ± 3.1 g/10 min. This difference is likely due to variations in molecular weight and chain branching introduced during the depolymerization and repolymerization processes. The synthesized PBAT may have a lower molecular weight or fewer chain scissions, resulting in increased flowability. Additionally, impurities or residual catalysts from the PET-based synthesis could slightly affect the polymer’s rheological behavior.

Conclusion

The study successfully demonstrated a sustainable and efficient method for upcycling PET waste into high-quality PBAT polymer, highlighting the critical role of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) as cocatalysts in enhancing the reaction efficiency and molecular weight of the final product. Among the tested catalysts, antimony trioxide with ChCl-Gly (choline chloride-glycerol DES) exhibited the best catalytic performance, achieving PBAT with a high molecular weight (70,000–75 000 g/mol) under optimized conditions (0.25 wt % catalyst, 0.25 wt % DES, 220–240 °C). This was followed by twin-screw extrusion, which produced uniform PBAT granules with desirable thermal and mechanical properties. The resulting polymer exhibited a thermal stability of 350 °C, a tensile strength of 13.32 MPa, and an elongation at break of 321%, making it suitable for packaging applications. Compared to conventional methods for preparing PBAT using TPA, the present approach using PET offers a more sustainable pathway, as it reduces the environmental footprint of plastic and also provides a valuable side product, ethylene glycol, contributing to the circular economy. Further optimization and industrial-scale validation could enhance the commercial viability of this innovative recycling strategy.
