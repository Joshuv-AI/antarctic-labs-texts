Balloon-Assisted Leaflet Laceration Using Modified UNICORN Technique in Native Valve TAVR
Authors: Charishma S Nallapati, Andres Hughes, Nadeen Faza, Stephen H Little, Marvin D Atkins, Fernando Ramirez Del Val, Michael J Reardon, Neal S Kleiman, Sachin Goel, Joe Aoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCoronary obstruction due to displacement of calcified native valve leaflets over the coronary ostia is a life-threatening complication of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Coronary artery obstruction is a rare but life-threatening complication of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). With a reported 30-day mortality of 40.9%, coronary obstruction remains a catastrophic complication, emphasizing the need for meticulous preprocedural assessment and preventive planning.1 Risk factors for coronary obstruction include female sex, coronary ostial height <10 mm, sinus of Valsalva width <30 mm, and virtual transcatheter heart valve-to-coronary distance <4 mm.1 Current methods for preventing coronary obstruction during TAVR are bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) and chimney or snorkel stenting.2,3 The BASILICA method is technically challenging and at times can cause inadequate leaflet splay requiring bailout coronary reaccess and stent.2 Chimney stenting requires stenting into the ostium of the coronary artery, protruding in the ascending aorta to maintain proximal coronary patency with potential pitfalls of stent distortion, restenosis, and thrombosis and at times is prohibitive for coronary reaccess.3

The undermining iatrogenic coronary obstruction with radiofrequency needle (UNICORN) method is a novel strategy to prevent coronary obstruction primarily in valve-in-valve TAVR. Prior techniques involve traversing with a 0.035-inch radiofrequency wire and at times without coronary protection.4,5 Here, we describe a case of UNICORN performed in native aortic valve stenosis with large, bulky, leaflet tip calcifications that would likely cause coronary obstruction with traditional valve-in-valve TAVR.

Case Summary

An 82-year-old woman with severe symptomatic calcific aortic stenosis (NYHA functional class III; ejection fraction 60%-65%; mean gradient 48.6 mm Hg; maximum velocity 4.1 m/s) who had probable fusion of the left and noncoronary cusps. Computed tomography (CT) showed low left main height and bulky leaflet-tip calcification, predicting high obstruction risk. She was considered high risk for surgical replacement of her aortic valve, predominantly because of frailty.

Procedural Steps

Preprocedural assessment

Preoperative coronary CT demonstrated a low left coronary height (8 mm) with a sinus of Valsalva diameter of 29 mm. Given the heavy left coronary leaflet calcification at the leaflet tip in addition to a low coronary height, there was an elevated risk for left coronary obstruction as well as embolic stroke (Figures 1A to 1D). The UNICORN approach was selected instead of BASILICA because of the extensive calcification of the left leaflet tip.

Step-by-Step Modified UNICORN Procedure

Coronary protection

A 7-F XB 3.5 guide catheter was inserted into the left femoral arterial sheath over the guidewire and oriented to the left coronary ostium. A Sion blue guidewire was advanced to the distal left anterior descending coronary artery, followed by insertion of a 6-F guide catheter extension that was kept in the guide. Before leaflet laceration with the Z-Med balloon (see below), a Synergy Megatron 3.5 × 24 mm drug-eluting stent was advanced over the guidewire to the mid-left anterior descending coronary artery for possible bailout.

Electrosurgical leaflet traversal

Two angiographic views confirmed the position of the AL2 catheter in the middle of the left coronary cusp between the left leaflet calcification and the left main and below the left main coronary artery (Videos 1 and 2). These views are typically selected using CT obtained before TAVR. Transesophageal echocardiography, primarily two-dimensional biplane technique, was essential to guide localization and position of electrosurgery and confirm correct initial wire advancement. The 0.014-inch Astato 20 wire was connected to the electrosurgical pencil and was used (at 70 W) to cross the leaflet while injecting dextrose solution (Figures 2A to 2F, Videos 3 and 4).

Balloon-assisted leaflet laceration

An NC Emerge 4.0 × 20 mm balloon was advanced over the Astato wire. The balloon was inflated to nominal pressure to perform balloon valvotomy at the point of traversal. A second balloon valvotomy was performed with a Nanocross Elite 6 × 60 mm balloon. The Astato wire was exchanged over the CXI crossing catheter for a J-wire. The CXI crossing catheter was then exchanged for a 6-F pigtail catheter. An Safari2 Extra Small wire was inserted through the pigtail catheter, and the pigtail catheter was removed. A Z-Med 16-mm balloon was advanced over the Safari2 Extra Small wire. The balloon was inflated to nominal pressure to perform balloon valvotomy at the point of traversal (Figures 2G to 2I, Video 5).

Valve Implantation

The balloon was then exchanged for a 23-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 Ultra RESILIA valve and delivery system, which had been prepared on the back table before the balloon valvotomy in the event of hemodynamic compromise from aortic insufficiency. The valve was advanced across the native aortic valve, and with rapid pacing, a test injection was performed. A second aortogram was performed with rapid pacing. The valve was deployed in an 80/20 position with a balloon inflation time of 5 seconds (Figure 2J, Video 6). The pigtail was withdrawn during this period. The balloon was deflated and subsequently removed. Final coronary patency post–valve deployment was assessed with a nonselective aortogram (Video 7). There was no evidence of left main obstruction after valve implantation (Figures 2K and 3A to 3C). Post-implantation TEE demonstrated appropriate valve function (Video 8).

Anatomy Considerations

Specific anatomical considerations in implementing the UNICORN technique include bulky calcific masses on the distal leaflet surfaces. This dense calcification likely precludes BASILICA owing to the inability of the guidewire to traverse the calcified leaflet tissue and the higher probability of not splitting or splaying the leaflets reliably.6,7 UNICORN instead uses a radiofrequency needle to undermine the leaflet, creating a controlled hinge or weak zone away from the calcium cap, often with balloon assistance. This avoids relying on tip traversal or long electrosurgical lacerations, which may have been blocked in this case.8

Theoretically, UNICORN reduces the risk of incomplete laceration, leaflet avulsion, and distal embolization that can occur with BASILICA. Specific considerations for balloon-assisted leaflet laceration include careful, case-by-case selection of balloon size. In this case, a 16-mm balloon was selected to ensure complete leaflet laceration before transcatheter heart valve delivery. Use of a smaller balloon risked creating an insufficient defect, potentially precluding SAPIEN valve passage and necessitating repeat traversal of the leaflet with an alternative wire and catheter for additional laceration. The leaflet tip was notably bulky and calcified, further supporting the need for a larger balloon to achieve effective laceration and facilitate delivery of the balloon-mounted SAPIEN valve. However, given concern for asymmetrical leaflet laceration and the associated risk of coronary obstruction, coronary protection was performed.

Safety of UNICORN TAVR in a Native Valve

As far as we know, the safety of UNICORN TAVR has never been established. Also, this is one of the first cases to our knowledge performed in a native valve. The UNICORN technique is comparable to a balloon aortic valvuloplasty in involving short inflations, pacing stabilization, and balloon-centric hemodynamics. Similar to balloon aortic valvuloplasty, there are anatomical factors that suggest the UNICORN has comparable safety. The sinuses of Valsalva and a compliant sinotubular junction offer sufficient volume to accommodate leaflet displacement during inflation, off-loading annular stress, especially when the balloons used are smaller than those used during balloon valvuloplasty.9 In UNICORN, the radiofrequency step is focal and supra-annular (leaflet level). During the brief and rapid pacing inflations, the balloon primarily expands against the thickened, calcified leaflets and fused commissures; the forces produce commissural splitting and nodular calcium fracture at the leaflet plane, rather than at the fibrous annular base.9,10 Nevertheless, because subannular calcification and aggressive area oversizing are key predictors of root/annular rupture during TAVR, a stepwise approach with conservative balloon sizing and brief inflations is generally used.10

Given that this represents one of the earliest described applications of the UNICORN technique in a native valve, formal exclusion criteria have not yet been established. In this case, leaflet calcification was localized to the leaflet tip, without extension into the annulus or left ventricular outflow tract. The UNICORN technique was therefore technically feasible, as the calcific burden was discrete and functionally disconnected, allowing controlled leaflet modification without annular or left ventricular outflow tract involvement. This anatomical configuration may represent an important prerequisite for successful application of UNICORN in native valves and underscores the need for careful preprocedural CT assessment when considering leaflet modification strategies.

Potential Pitfalls

The potential pitfalls of this technique include malalignment of the wire–catheter system relative to the target aortic leaflet during traversal, which may lead to ineffective leaflet splay or damage to adjacent structures. The UNICORN technique is not useful if both coronary arteries are at risk. Proper visualization and position of the catheter in the middle of the left coronary cusp between the leaflet calcium and the left main is crucial. This requires familiarity with anatomy and identification of the structures on both angiogram and transesophageal echocardiogram in multiple orthogonal planes. A similar technique reported by Giustino et al5 uses a 0.014-inch radiofrequency wire for safety in the event of traversal in a suboptimal spot.5

Conclusions

The UNICORN TAVR technique can be considered an alternative approach to BASILICA for leaflet-tip calcification while preserving coronary access more effectively than snorkel stenting in native aortic valves.
