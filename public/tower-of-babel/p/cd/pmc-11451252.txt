The reflux symptom score has good screening value for laryngopharyngeal reflux
Authors: Wenbin Guo, Jinhong Zhang, Huijun Yue, Kexing Lyu, Siyu Chen, Bixue Huang, Yiming Wang, Wenbin Lei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the consistency between the reflux symptom score (RSS) and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test in screening for laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) and the screening value of the RSS for LPR by simultaneously administering daytime multitemporal salivary pepsin test and RSS to patients.MethodsThis was a single-center prospective observational study. All included patients underwent simultaneous daytime multitemporal salivary pepsin testing and RSS. A participant was considered to have LPR when one or more positive salivary pepsin test results or RSS score > 13 were obtained. The consistency between the multitemporal salivary pepsin test and the RSS was compared by the weighted Cohen’s kappa statistic. The screening value of the RSS for LPR was investigated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsA total of 67 patients were included. The positivity rate of LPR was 71.64% according to the results of the multitemporal salivary pepsin test. According to RSS, the positive rate of LPR was 70.15%. The weighted Kappa value between the multitemporal salivary pepsin test and the RSS was 0.675 (p < 0.001). The area under curve of RSS screening for LPR was 0.843 (p < 0.01), and the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of RSS screening for LPR were 89.58%, 78.95%, 91.49%, and 75%, respectively.ConclusionThere is a good consistency between the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test, and the RSS has a good screening value for LPR, which can be applied to screen for LPR in otolaryngologic patients.Keywords: Laryngopharyngeal reflux, Reflux symptom score, Salivary pepsin test, Screening

Introduction

Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) is defined as an inflammatory disease of the tissues of the upper aerodigestive tract caused by the direct or indirect effects associated with the reflux of gastroduodenal contents, which can cause morphological changes in the upper aerodigestive tract [1]. Common symptoms of LPR are frequent throat clearing, globus sensation, regurgitations or burps, heartburn [2, 3]. Common laryngeal signs include subglottic edema, vocal fold edema, posterior commissure hypertrophy, and thick endolaryngeal mucus [4, 5]. LPR has a high incidence in otorhinolaryngology, head and neck surgery patients [6], it was reported that the prevalence of LPR in patients with benign vocal cord lesions, vocal cord precancerous lesions, and vocal cord cancer was 84.62%, 80.00%, and 82.09%, respectively [7].

Common diagnostic and screening methods in clinical practice include 24-hour multichannel impedance pH monitoring (24 h MII-pH), empiric antireflux therapy, salivary pepsin testing, and signs and symptoms scale. 24 h MII-pH monitoring is considered by most specialists as the gold standard for the diagnosis of LPR as it can record the reflux events experienced in real-time, and doctors can analyze them using specific software after the patient’s monitoring is completed [8]. However, 24 h MII-pH monitoring is an invasive and expensive test [9], and only a few large tertiary hospitals in China offer this test, making it difficult to apply 24 h MII-pH monitoring in clinical screening for such a large LPR population. Salivary pepsin assay is an emerging diagnostic method for LPR because it is noninvasive, rapid, and affordable [10]. However, the level of pepsin in saliva is susceptible to feeding as well as other factors [11], and there is still no consensus on its optimal time to collect saliva samples. A study has reported that collecting a saliva sample for testing in the morning is the optimal time [12], but other studies have found that performing only a single test can have a high rate of missed diagnoses and advocate multiple tests throughout the day to improve diagnostic sensitivity [13, 14]. The reflux symptom index (RSI) is currently the most widely used screening method for LPR, where patients are scored only on the severity of their reflux-related symptoms, and are considered to suspected LPR if their RSI > 13 [15]. In recent years, a new reflux symptom score (RSS) has been reported and validated in different regions, and in contrast to the RSI, the RSS incorporates more symptoms associated with LPR (e.g. ear pressure/pain, tongue burning, and abdominal distension/flatus) [16]. In addition, the RSS employs a scoring method distinct from that of the RSI, taking into account not only the severity of reflux symptoms, but also the frequency of occurrence and the impact on quality of life [16]. A study showed better diagnostic LPR concordance between the RSS and 24 h MII-pH monitoring compared to the RSI [17]. However, the RSS is a newly developed scale and its application in clinical practice still requires further exploration and validation. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies investigating the concordance between the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin assay.

The aim of this study was to investigate the concordance between the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test in screening for LPR by simultaneously performing daytime multitemporal salivary pepsin test and RSS in the patients with symptoms of LPR.

Methods

This single-center prospective observational study was conducted from September 2023 to February 2024 at the Otorhinolaryngology Hospital of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. By prospectively enrolling outpatients with symptoms of LPR, the included patients underwent RSS assessment, salivary pepsin testing at multiple time points during the day, and electronic laryngoscopy examination. Inclusion criteria was the occurrence of LPR-related symptoms lasted for more than six months (e.g. heartburn and frequent throat clearing, etc.). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) recently taking proton pump inhibitors, gastrointestinal motility medications and alginate; (2) having a condition that affects the reflux symptom score, such as vocal polyps, vocal leukoplakia, and vocal tumors; (3) history of a major chronic disease, neurological disorders; (4) refusal to undergo salivary pepsin testing, RSS evaluation, or electronic laryngoscopy examination; (5) undergone surgeries related to the ear, nose, throat, esophagus, or gastrointestinal tract, as well as those with trauma to the relevant areas or other conditions (such as acupuncture, suffering from mental illness, currently participating in other clinical studies, etc.; (6) long-term and heavy consumption of alcoholic beverages and tobacco.

Multi-time point salivary pepsin test

The salivary pepsin assay used in this study produced by Jiangxi Nord Medical Equipment Co. Ltd and has been validated in two tertiary hospitals in China. Using 24 h MII-pH as the standard, the sensitivity and specificity of saliva pepsin detection for diagnosing LPR were 86.6% and 80.8%, respectively. [13, 18]. Briefly, the method simply involves collecting a fresh saliva sample from the patient, aspirating an appropriate amount of saliva and adding it to the spiking wells of the kit, and observing the results after 8 min. If two red lines appear in both the T and C areas of the kit, the result is positive; if only one red line appears in the C area, the result is negative; if only one red line appears in the T area, the result is invalid and should be retested. In this study, saliva sample collection and subsequent testing were performed by the subjects themselves. Prior to the study, detailed demonstrations were provided to each subject to ensure their proficiency in the process and answer any questions they may have. All subjects underwent multiple time-point saliva pepsin detection within a single day (the first sample was collected in the morning before brushing teeth and eating, followed by hourly collections starting at 8 a.m. until 6 p.m., totaling 12 times), the saliva samples for the demonstrative procedure were collected and assayed in the outpatient clinic, while subsequent saliva sample collections and tests were conducted by the patients in their home environment. We requested all participants to maintain consistency in their diet and daily routines, while avoiding beverages such as coffee and cola that could affect reflux; if eating coincided with saliva collection, patients were instructed to eat after collecting the saliva sample. One or more positive test results indicated that the patient had suspected LPR.

Scale evaluation

The included patients were assessed for the Chinese (validated) version of RSS (Table 1) [19]. The meaning of each symptom was explained in detail to the patients by the clinicians, and the patients themselves scored the severity of their symptoms (0 means no such symptom, 5 means this symptom is very severe), the frequency of occurrence (0 means it has not occurred in the past 1 week, 5 means it has occurred every day in the past week), and the degree of impact on the quality of life (0 means it has not occurred in the past 1 week, 5 means it has occurred every day in the past week). The scores for the severity and frequency of each reflux symptom were multiplied to obtain a symptom score, and then summed to obtain a final score. If the RSS was greater than 13, the patient was considered to suspected LPR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows (SPSS version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY) and Medcalc. Sample size calculations were conducted using the “Tests for Two ROC Curves” procedure within the PASS software. Anticipating an AUC of 0.60 for the novel method and an AUC of 0.80 for the gold standard, we established a non-inferiority margin of 0.15, with an alpha value set at 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%. The calculations determined that an ideal sample size of 54 would be required, accounting for an estimated dropout rate of 15%. The consistency between the multitemporal salivary pepsin assay and the RSS was compared using a weighted Cohen’s kappa statistic. The screening value of the RSS and RSI for LPR was investigated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The method of Delong et al. was employed for calculating the standard error of the AUC and the differences among those AUCs [20]. A level of significance of p < 0.05 was used.

Results

A total of 67 patients were included, 52 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 39.53 ± 12.18 years and BMI of 25.18 ± 3.52 (Table 2). The electronic laryngoscopic examination results of all patients indicated no functional disorders affecting the reflux symptom scores. All the included patients completed twelve consecutive salivary pepsin tests during the daytime and the results of pepsin tests performed at different time points are shown in Table 3. 71.64% (48/67) of the patients had at least one positive test result. The five time points with the highest LPR positivity in a single test were morning, 8:00, 18:00, 15:00 and 13:00. All included patients completed the RSS assessment, with a mean RSS score of 41.54 ± 41.71 and a quality of life impact score of 12.58 ± 13.90. 70.15% (47/67) of the patients had a positive LPR screen. Detailed scores of the different symptoms are shown in Table 4. The top five highest scores for reflux symptoms were voice disorder, throat clearing, globus sensation, excess throat mucus and halitosis.

Consistency analysis between multitemporal salivary pepsin test and RSS

The Kappa value of the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test for the diagnosis of LPR was 0.675 (p < 0.001, Table 5). In addition, we also analyzed the consistency between the RSS and the single salivary pepsin test separately, and the results showed that only the highest consistency was found between the single morning test and the RSS (Kappa value only was 0.249, p = 0.03), and the Kappa values of the multitemporal tests were significantly higher than those of the single test.

Screening value of the RSS for LPR

With the results of the multitemporal salivary pepsin test as the standard, we calculated the screening value of the RSS for LPR using an ROC curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) of RSS screening for LPR was 0.843 (95% CI 0.733–0.920, p < 0.01, see Fig. 1). The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were 89.58%, 78.95%, 91.49% and 75%, respectively. Additionally, we conducted a further comparative analysis of the AUC values between RSI and RSS in relation to multi-timepoint salivary pepsin measurements. The results indicated that the AUC value for RSS in conjunction with multi-timepoint salivary pepsin detection was significantly higher than that for RSI (RSS vs. RSI = 0.843 vs. 0.690, p < 0.05, see Fig. 1).

Discussion

The incidence of LPR in otolaryngology head and neck surgery patients is increasing year by year, however, there is still no consensus on the diagnosis of LPR. Most scholars now recognize 24 h MII-pH monitoring as the gold standard for the diagnosis of LPR [21–23]. Nonetheless, 24 h MII-pH monitoring is considered an invasive procedure, with patients frequently reporting discomfort throughout the monitoring period. Moreover, the examination’s high cost is also a barrier to its widespread application in clinical practice, as many patients decline the procedure due to financial considerations. The objective of this study is to evaluate the clinical utility of screening tools for LPR. Consequently, the reference standard employed in this study is salivary pepsin testing rather than 24 h MII-pH monitoring. This choice is based on the fact that salivary pepsin testing serves as a more feasible screening instrument in clinical practice, offering the potential for application in large-scale populations. In this study, we first investigated the consistency between the two methods in the screening of LPR and the screening value of RSS for LPR. We found a good consistency between the two methods and that RSS showed good screening value for LPR.

The RSI has been widely used by otolaryngologists in the ensuing decade since it was first proposed by Belafsky et al. [15]. However, as clinicians gain a deeper understanding of LPR, it has become increasingly clear that the reflux symptoms in the RSI do not seem to perfectly cover all symptoms associated with LPR, and that the way the RSI is scored only incorporates the severity of the reflux symptoms, ignoring the frequency of occurrence of reflux and its impact on quality of life [17]. Considering these issues, a new RSS has been reported, which covers a more comprehensive range of reflux-related symptoms and is scored in a more scientific manner, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of the frequency, severity, and impact on quality of life of LPR-related symptoms [16]. The RSS was first validated in France and Belgium, and subsequently, the RSS was validated in different countries [16]. Min et al. translated RSS into Korean and validated it, and their results showed that RSS had good retest validity and internal and external validity [24]. Han et al. translated RSS into Chinese, and they found that RSS had good internal and external reliabilities [25].

Although all of these studies have shown good efficacy of RSS in screening for LPR, RSS is a newly developed scale and its true efficacy still needs to be further validated. To date, only Zhang et al. have reported higher consistency between RSS and 24 h MII-pH monitoring compared to the RSI [17]. Salivary pepsin test is also commonly used in the clinic for the diagnosis of LPR. However, there are no studies investigating the consistency of salivary pepsin testing with the RSS. Through performing simultaneous multitemporal salivary pepsin tests and RSS on patients, we found a good consistency between the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test. The present study is the first to investigate the consistency between the RSS and multitemporal salivary pepsin test, which also limits our ability to make comparisons with other studies. In addition, we investigated the screening value of RSS for LPR, taking the results of multitemporal salivary pepsin test as the criterion, and found that the AUC value of RSS for screening LPR reached 0.843, which suggests that RSS has a good screening value and can be applied in clinical practice. This study did not measure the specific levels of pepsin in saliva samples. The main reason is that our goal was to find a convenient method for rapid clinical screening of LPR. The use of a kit for qualitative detection is a promising method, but obtaining the exact levels of pepsin in saliva in clinical practice is complex and time-consuming. We also recognize that obtaining the specific levels of pepsin could provide more clinical guidance, in future research, we will focus more on the clinical guidance value of the specific levels of pepsin in saliva. In this study, an intriguing observation was noted: pepsin detection in salivary samples, collected prior to morning oral hygiene and breakfast as well as in the evening, demonstrated a higher rate of positivity compared to other time points. The etiology of this phenomenon is likely multifactorial, as the concentration of pepsin in saliva is highly susceptible to various influences, such as salivary secretion, spontaneous deglutition, and dietary factors [10, 26]. It has been documented that pepsin can internalize into cells via endocytosis, where it can retain its activity [27]. Upon a decrease in pH, pepsin can become reactivated, leading to cellular damage [27]. Concurrently, nocturnal saliva production and spontaneous swallowing are significantly diminished during sleep [28]. It is postulated that intracellular pepsin may re-enter the laryngeal mucus or saliva following cell lysis. The accumulation of pepsin throughout the night could result in peak levels in the morning salivary samples, thereby increasing the likelihood of detection. Furthermore, dietary habits can significantly influence the presence of pepsin in saliva [11]. In the Chinese dietary culture, a larger intake is common during the evening meal, often rich in lipids and alcoholic beverages, which are established factors that can exacerbate reflux conditions.

The greatest strength of this study is the first joint application of multi-timepoint salivary pepsin test and RSS to investigate the agreement between the two methods and the screening value of RSS for LPR, which further proves the value of RSS in clinical practice. However, this study still has some limitations: (1) It is a single-center study conducted in China, and the results of the study will need to be validated by collaborating with more centers in the future, (2) the RSS was not compared with the remaining commonly used scales (e.g., RSI and RFS), (3) all patients did not undergo 24-h MII-pH monitoring, (4) pepsin is not a universal biomarker in saliva, future research could further explore the roles of elastase, bile salts, pancreatic enzymes, or other reflux contents in the diagnosis of LPR, and (5) the salivary pepsin detection kit used in this study cannot quantitatively detect the content of pepsin, and this kit has not been compared and verified with the commonly used peptest in Europe and America.

Conclusion

There is a good consistency between the RSS and the multitemporal salivary pepsin test, and the RSS has a good screening value for LPR, which can be applied to the screening of LPR in otolaryngologic patients.
