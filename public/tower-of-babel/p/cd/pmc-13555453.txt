Preoperative Shock in Acute Type A Aortic Dissection: Multicenter Study of Mortality and Transport Time
Authors: Ken Nakamura, Kentaro Akabane, Shusuke Arai, Shuto Hirooka, Atsushi Yamashita, Kotaro Suzuki, Takashi Kawashima, Cholsu Kim, Hideaki Uchino, Tetsuro Uchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), early surgical intervention is critical; however, prognostic determinants in geographically stable populations remain incompletely defined. We sought to identify key predictors of in-hospital mortality in a closed regional cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients undergoing surgery for ATAAD between January 2022 and June 2025 at 3 tertiary centers in Yamagata Prefecture, Japan. Demographics, transport time, admission to surgery interval, preoperative status, and operative variables were evaluated. Independent predictors of mortality were assessed by Cox proportional hazards modeling.ResultsOf 164 patients (mean age, 66.3 ± 11.9 years; 50% male), surgical mortality was 8.5% and in-hospital mortality was 10.4%. Nonsurvivors more frequently presented with preoperative shock (41% vs 12%; P < .001) and malperfusion (35% vs 15%; P = .05). In multivariable analysis, preoperative shock was the sole independent predictor of mortality (hazard ratio, 9.12; 95% CI, 2.88-28.8; P < .001), whereas transport time and operative complexity were not significant.ConclusionsIn a geographically isolated, consistently managed cohort, preoperative shock was the dominant determinant of in-hospital mortality in ATAAD. These findings emphasize the importance of early recognition and stabilization before surgery rather than transport speed alone.

Acute aortic dissection, the most common catastrophic aortic event, occurs in approximately 2.9 to 10 individuals per 100,000 population.1, 2, 3 Autopsy-based studies suggest that up to 60% of patients die before hospital arrival, indicating that mortality is extremely high during the early phase after symptom onset.4 Although advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management have improved outcomes, early mortality remains substantial, particularly in patients presenting with hemodynamic instability.

Several large registries, including the German Registry for Acute Aortic Dissection Type A (GERAADA), have proposed risk stratification scores incorporating clinical factors such as shock, malperfusion, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation to predict early mortality.5,6 However, the relative impact of these physiologic factors compared with transport-related variables, such as transfer distance or time, remains controversial and may vary across regional health care systems.7 Yamagata Prefecture, Japan, represents a unique clinical setting characterized by geographic isolation, minimal population migration, and centralized management of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) within a limited number of tertiary centers. This relatively closed regional system allows evaluation of prognostic determinants with reduced confounding related to access to care or interinstitutional variability. Accordingly, this multicenter study aimed to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality in surgically treated ATAAD patients, with particular emphasis on the relative contributions of preoperative shock and transport time.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective multicenter cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent surgical repair for ATAAD between January 2022 and June 2025 at 3 tertiary cardiovascular centers in Yamagata Prefecture. Patients with chronic dissection or those managed nonoperatively were excluded.

Data Collection

Clinical data were extracted from institutional databases and medical records. Variables included demographics, comorbidities, time intervals (symptom onset to hospital arrival, admission to surgery), preoperative status (shock, malperfusion), imaging findings, operative details, and outcomes.

Preoperative shock was defined as systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg, need for vasopressor support, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation before surgery. Malperfusion was diagnosed on the basis of clinical, laboratory, or imaging evidence of compromised organ or limb perfusion.

Surgical Strategy

All patients underwent emergent open surgical repair through median sternotomy with use of cardiopulmonary bypass and hypothermic circulatory arrest as indicated. The extent of aortic replacement and use of adjunctive procedures were determined at the discretion of the attending surgeon.

Outcomes

The primary end point was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included operative mortality and postoperative complications. Surgical mortality was defined as death within 30 days after surgery according to The Society of Thoracic Surgeons criteria. In-hospital mortality included all postoperative deaths occurring during the same hospitalization, even beyond 30 days.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared by the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared by the χ2 or Fisher exact test.

Independent predictors of in-hospital mortality were assessed by Cox proportional hazards regression. Variables with P < .10 in univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed with JMP software, version 18.0 (SAS Institute).

Results

Yamagata Prefecture, Japan (population: 999,378), has minimal population movement, with a day-to-night population ratio of 99.7%. Of the 164 patients (mean age, 66.3 ± 11.9 years; 50% male) included in this study, postoperative deaths occurred in individuals transferred from both nearby and distant areas (Figure 1). The mean transport time from symptom onset to hospital arrival was 204 ± 299 minutes, and the mean admission to surgery interval was 510 ± 1068 minutes. Preoperative shock was present in 15% of patients and malperfusion in 17% (Table 1). Additional surgical procedures beyond standard ascending aortic replacement were performed in 14% of cases (Table 2).

Early Outcomes

Operative mortality was 8.5%, and overall in-hospital mortality was 10.4%. Nonsurvivors had significantly higher rates of preoperative shock (41% vs 12%; P < .001) and malperfusion (35% vs 15%; P = .05). Transport time and admission to surgery interval did not differ significantly between survivors and nonsurvivors (Table 1).

Predictors of In-Hospital Mortality

In univariable analysis, preoperative shock, malperfusion, and additional procedures were associated with increased mortality. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, preoperative shock emerged as the sole independent predictor of in-hospital mortality (hazard ratio, 9.12; 95% CI, 2.88-28.8; P < .001; Figure 2).

Neither transport time nor the extent of surgical repair was independently associated with mortality.

Comment

This multicenter study from a geographically stable regional cohort found that preoperative shock is the dominant determinant of in-hospital mortality in ATAAD. In contrast, transport-related factors, including prolonged transfer time, were not independently associated with adverse outcomes.

These findings indicate that the physiologic condition at presentation plays a central role in determining survival in an organized regional referral system. Previous studies have consistently identified preoperative shock and malperfusion as major risk factors for mortality,8 and this analysis reinforces these observations using a population-based data set. By directly evaluating transport time alongside clinical severity, this study further clarifies their relative prognostic impact in real-world practice.

The absence of a significant association between transport delay7 and mortality may reflect effective triage, prompt diagnostic pathways, and standardized surgical management across the participating centers. Within such a system, variations in transfer duration appear less influential than the patient’s hemodynamic status at the time of surgical intervention.

From a clinical standpoint, these results highlight the importance of early recognition and active management of preoperative shock during patient transfer.9 In regions where prolonged transport is unavoidable, prioritizing hemodynamic stabilization may represent the most effective strategy to improve outcomes in ATAAD.

Limitations

This retrospective study has a moderate sample size, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Because only surgically treated patients diagnosed after hospital arrival were included, differences from patients who died before diagnosis or transfer cannot be excluded, and the effect of transport-related factors on prehospital mortality could not be assessed.

Conclusion

In a closed, consistently managed regional cohort, preoperative shock was the strongest predictor of in-hospital mortality in ATAAD. Transport time and surgical complexity were not independently associated with outcomes. These findings highlight the paramount importance of early recognition and stabilization of shock before surgery.
