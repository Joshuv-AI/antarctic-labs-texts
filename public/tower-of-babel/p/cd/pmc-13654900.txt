Prevalence and factors associated with depression among junior police officers in Kampala metropolitan area
Authors: Enock Kabanda, Jemimah Kyeyune, Ronald Opito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolice officers in Uganda are at higher risk of depression due to the exceptionally stressful nature of their work and exposure to trauma, yet there is paucity of evidence on depression and its associated factors among police officers in Uganda. This study, therefore, aimed to determine the prevalence of depression and associated factors among junior police officers in Kampala Metropolitan area (KMP).MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study which involved 266 junior police officers in KMP sampled using multi-stage sampling technique and interviewed using a structured questionnaire. Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was used to assess depression, and participants with PHQ-9 scores ≥ 10 were considered to have depressive symptoms. Data was entered into Stata version 16.0 for management and analysis. Descriptive statistics were conducted and presented as means, frequencies and percentages while bivariate and multivariate analysis were conducted using Modified Poisson regression and presented as prevalence ratios (PR) with a 95% interval and a P < 0.05 for statistical significance.ResultsThe mean age of the 266 participants was 36 (SD = 8) years, with prevalence of depression being 31.2% (95% CI 25.9–37.1). The sociodemographic factor significantly associated with depression was working in the field force department, aPR = 2.28 (95%CI 1.17–4.42). The environmental and occupational factors significantly associated with depression were working in rural areas, aPR = 2.11 (95%CI 1.08–4.14) and history of experiencing traumatic incidents, aPR = 2.56 (95%CI 1.45–4.53).ConclusionThis study found the prevalence of depression among junior police officers in KMP to be higher compared to the global prevalence of depression among police officers, and to findings from several studies in other population settings. Working in the field force unit, rural areas and history of experience trauma were significantly associated with depression among the junior police officers. There is a need for the Uganda police force to create friendly working environment with flexible work schedules and put in place mechanisms to support officers experiencing trauma at workplace.Keywords: Police, Depression, Kampala, Junior officers, Environmental stressors

Introduction

A mental disorder is a condition characterized by clinically significant disturbance in an individual’s cognition, emotional regulation or behavior, usually associated with distress or impairment in important areas of functioning [1]. Mental health disorders are increasingly recognized as key determinants of the burden of global health, with depressive and anxiety disorders emerging as primary contributors to this burden [2, 3]. The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 5% of adults worldwide suffer from depression, underscoring the pervasive nature of these mental health challenges [4].

Mental health challenges, particularly depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety disorders are concerning among armed forces such as police officers [5, 6]. A comprehensive analysis and synthesis of existing data investigating the worldwide occurrence and determinants of mental health concerns in police personnel found that the collective prevalence rates were 14.6% for depression, 14.2% for PTSD, 9.6% for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), 8.5% for suicidal thoughts, 5.0% for alcohol addiction, and 25.7% for risky drinking [7]. Preceding studies in Sri Lanka and Tanzania estimated a prevalence of 22.8% and 19.8% respectively [8, 9], while a study in Nepal among 314 soldiers serving in police headquarters within the Armed Police Force showed a prevalence of 42.36% of depression [10].

Many studies across different contexts have identified a wide range of factors associated with depressive disorders among police officers, including occupational, organizational, social, and individual determinants [7, 11, 12]. A study conducted in Sri Lanka found that factors such as satisfactory welfare and workplace facilities were negatively associated with depression [8], while in Tanzania, younger age groups, lack of social support and fewer years of police service were at associated with depression [9]. Furthermore, a systematic review exploring factors associated with depression among police officers identified various determinants of depression, including environmental/occupational factors like exposure to traumatic incidents, organizational factors such as the location of the police station, and individual factors, where some individuals were more susceptible to mental illnesses. Additionally, the study underscored the importance of satisfactory welfare as a significant factor contributing to the development of depression among police officers [11].

Although police face an increased risk of developing mental health problems, reliable estimates of their psychological difficulties remain unknown, however higher occupational stress remains the strongest risk factor suggested for depression [7]. These statistics highlight the pressing need for understanding and addressing mental health challenges. Several studies have been conducted to shed light on the burden of depression. However, most of these studies and efforts target trends of mental health disorders in educational institutions, health institutions, communities and households [13–17]. Limited studies have been conducted among the police officers or other officers in uniform [9–11]. Police officers, due to the exceptionally stressful nature of their work, can be particularly vulnerable to mental health challenges. Research has shown that work-related stress and exposure to trauma can increase the risk of developing depressive symptoms [12].

Despite the critical role police officers play in maintaining public safety and responding to emergencies, mental health among this population remains underexplored in low-resource settings such as Uganda, where occupational mental health services and support systems are often limited. So, there is a paucity of evidence on depression and its associated predictors among police offers in Uganda. In particular, little is known about the magnitude of depression and the occupational, demographic, and psychosocial factors associated with it among junior police officers working in highly demanding urban settings. To address these gaps, we conducted this study aiming to assess the level of depression among junior police officers in Kampala Metropolitan Police (KMP) and the associated factors, to bridge the existing knowledge gap regarding depression among police officers in Uganda.

By conducting this research, our results have contributed to a clearer understanding of the magnitude of depression among junior police officers in KMP and the key socioeconomic, occupational and environmental factors driving it. The evidence generated may guide targeted mental health interventions, inform policy development within the Uganda police force and support the development of support systems that protect officers’ wellbeing and improve public safety and service delivery.

Methods and Materials

Study design

This study utilized a cross-sectional study design to assess the prevalence of depression and its associated factors among junior police officers in KMP, Uganda.

Study site and population

The study was conducted in selected police stations in the Kampala metropolitan area. Kampala metropolitan police (KMP) is made up of three regions, East, North, and South. Kampala East has five divisions which include Jinja road police station, Kira Road police station, Kira division police station, Mukono police station, and Nagalama police station. Kampala South has six police divisions; Katwe police station, Kabalagala police station, Central police station, Kajjansi police station, Entebbe police station, and Nsangi police station. Kampala North police region has six police divisions: Kawempe police station, Wandegeya police station, Old Kampala police station, Wakiso police station, Kasangati police station, and Kakiri police station. All these divisions were eligible for inclusion in this study. The eligible study participants were junior police officers in the selected police divisions within KMP.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria included; all junior policer officers at the rank of constable to sergeant, currently actively employed in the police force, working in Kampala Metropolitan area, has worked as a full-time police officer for at least six months after completion of training and he/she is 20 years and above and is on duty on the day of the interview. The exclusion criteria included; junior contract officers such as special police constables (SPCs), those who were not on duty at the time of data collection, officers who have been on extended leave for more than three months at the time of data collection, those who were mentally incapacitated and unable to respond to the questionnaires or provide informed consent, those who were physically very sick were excluded from the study and officers working in only administrative roles with on operational exposures.

The calculated sample size was inflated by 20% [18] to account for anticipated non-response due to the fact that some of the information being collected are too personal and sensitive, police officers often work irregular shifts and have operational commitments that reduce survey participation rates. The calculated sample size was further adjusted by a design effect of 2, to account for the clustering design of the sample process, to give a sample size of 232, that is 116*2 = 232. Therefore, the calculated sample size was 232.

Sampling was done using multistage cluster sampling method (2-stage). All the 3 police regions within KMP were purposively included to ensure regional representation. The first stage of sampling involved selecting 3 divisions from each of the 3 regions using simple random sampling technique. The names of the six divisions in Kampala South were written on separate pieces of paper, rolled, mixed thoroughly and the three divisions were selected at random using the lottery method. This process was repeated for the remaining two regions resulting in a total of nine selected divisions. The second stage involved selecting participants from each division using simple random sampling. Sampling was done during the Monday parade when all the officers were present at the station. Eligible participants were identified during the parade and invited to participate in the sampling process. Pieces of paper were placed in a box, mixed thoroughly and each willing eligible officer was invited to pick one piece of paper. The papers were numbered according to the required sample size for that division (for example, numbers 1 to 15 where 15 participants were required), while the remaining papers were blank. The participants who picked the numbered papers were then invited to participate in the study. The sample size for each division was determined proportionately based on the population of the study participants in that division.

Data collection tools and procedure

A structured questionnaire was used to collect data on social-demographic, environmental/occupational, and economic characteristics among the eligible participants between November 2024 to March 2025, during Monday Parades. Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), printed in English version was used to assess depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is a self-assessment tool for depressive symptoms and has been used in several studies and validated in at least two study populations in Uganda [19].

Three Research Assistants (RA) were trained for 2 days in the data collection procedures, the consent processes, data validation and other ethical issues involved in the conduct of research among the armed forces. Each research assistant was deployed per region and presented the administrative clearance from the Inspector General of Police (IGP) to the district police commander (DPC) for permission to conduct the study in their respective districts. The DPC then provided the RAs with the list of potential participants where they were sampled using lottery method and interviewed until the divisional/district sample size is reached. The questionnaires were printed and filled by the RAs as they interviewed the participants, then later entered an EPI-DATA version 4.6 for cleaning and management.

Study variables

The study outcome of interest was prevalence of depressive symptoms defined using the PHQ-9 cut off ≥ 10 on the 27-point scale, that is, participants who scored ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9 screening tool were having depressive symptoms.

The independent variables included sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, marital status, education level, police department and years of service. The other factors included the presence of financial stressors, traumatic incidents at work, time on rank, working environment, safety of the workplace, household income and current salary.

Data management and analysis

The field questionnaire and PHQ-9 tool were checked for accuracy and completeness, then entered into EPI-DATA version 4.6 where data cleaning and management of missing variables were performed. The cleaned dataset was then exported to STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) for further cleaning, recoding, and statistical analysis. Baseline characteristics such as age, sex, education status, and marital status were summarized using frequency and percentages for categorical variables and either mean and its associated standard deviation or median and its associated interquartile range for continuous variables. The prevalence of depression at a 95% confidence interval (CI) was obtained as a proportion of the people who were screened positive for depressive symptoms with the PHQ-9 tool out of the entire sample size. To assess the association between depression and independent variables, Chi-square test was conducted at bivariate level.

Effect modification and confounding of the relationship between the dependent and the independent variable was assessed using multivariate analysis, where potential confounders were adjusted with the other predictor variables to obtain and report the adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR). In multivariable analysis, the modified Poisson regression model was used to obtain aPR and their 95% confidence intervals. All significant factors with a p-value of < 0.2 at bi-variate analysis as well as those identified in prior literature as significant predictors of depression were included in the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each variable in the model and those with VIF > 5 were considered to have multicollinear relationship with other variables and excluded from the final model [20]. Factors with a p-value less than the level of significance (5%) or whose confidence interval did not include the null were considered significant at multivariate level.

Discussion

This study aimed at determining the prevalence of depression among junior police officers in KMP and its associated factors. The study found the prevalence of depression among junior police officers to be high at 31% and the factors associated with depression were; working in the field force unit, working in rural areas and history of experiencing traumatic incidents.

The prevalence of depression among junior police officers is higher than that reported in the general population in Uganda. Akena et al. estimated the prevalence of depression in the general population to be 6.8% in central Uganda [14]. Similarly, a study assessing the prevalence and factors associated with depression among school going adolescents in Central Uganda reported a prevalence of 21% [21], while depression among undergraduate medical students in Makerere was found to be 21.5% [22]. In addition, a systematic review on the prevalence of mental disorders in Uganda found the prevalence of major depressive disorders to be 22% [17]. These earlier studies demonstrate that the prevalence of depression observed among junior police officers in the current study (31%) is higher than that reported in most other population sub-groups.

Variations in depression prevalence across the general population have been attributed to differences in socio-economic status and exposure to civil wars and conflicts. Although some studies in Uganda have reported depression prevalence rates higher than the commonly cited 21%, these rates remain lower than the 31% observed in this study. For instance a study by Mugisha et al. assessing depression in Northern Uganda seven years post conflict reported a prevalence of 25% [23], which is higher than average estimates but still below the prevalence found among junior police officers.

Compared to the global prevalence, the depression among junior police officers in Uganda is more than twice as high as the global depression rates among police officers which is at 14.6% [7]. Similarly, the observed depression in our study is higher than what was reported among urban police officers in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, which was 19.8% [9]. The junior police officers in Uganda are therefore heavily burdened by depression, and this poses a very high risk to the civilians they interact with daily.

For public safety, police officers need to be screened for depressive symptoms on a regular basis, say every three to six months before deployment. In addition to screening, there is need to provide a comprehensive psychosocial support program for the officers found with depressive symptoms. This will guarantee personal safety of the officers and increase their productivity in the long run.

In this study, we found that depression among junior police officers was associated with working in the field force unit. Field force officers are deployed in high-risk areas, with very high job demands such as heavy workloads and assaults by citizens and as such they witness several traumatic events which predispose them to depression. A study to assess the role of job demands and job resources in the development of depression and anxiety among police officers in Germany found that high workload and assaults by citizens predicted higher levels of depression and anxiety among police officers, while social support by colleagues, shared values, and positive leadership climate buffered the effect of job demands and were negatively associated with depression [24].

Another study to assess suicidal thoughts and depression among first responder police officers (fire fighters and paramedics) whose work relates with the field force units found elevated risk of suicide among first responders [25].

Furthermore, officers who were exposed to traumatic events were more likely to be depressed as compared to those who did not experience traumatic events at workplace. Job-related trauma and stress leads to the development of PTSD, depression, substance use disorder and suicidality among police officers [23, 26].

These findings call for the implementation of tailored mental health support programs to target police officers deployed in the field force unit who are more likely to experience traumatic events while on duty. The interventions would include deployment for a specified period of 6–12 months, after which the officers would be allowed to recover and then deployed in a less stressful environment for similar period. In addition, there is need for regular mental health assessment, like every 3–6 months for police officers in the field force unit and those found to have depressive symptoms immediately withdrawn for psychological support and rehabilitation.

In addition, officers working in rural areas were more likely to be depressed compared to those in urban and peri urban areas. Rural areas are associated with having a worse working environment than urban areas. Earlier studies have documented satisfactory welfare facilities at work place to be protective against depression among police officers [8]. Improving the welfare of police officers in the rural areas should therefore be given a priority to improve their mental health outcomes.

Study limitations and strengths

This study had some limitations. First, it was conducted only in the highly urban and labor-intensive Kampala Metropolitan area and among junior police officers, thus the findings might not be generalizable to senior police officers and those in rural or less demanding settings in Uganda. In addition, the study excluded officers who were absent during the Monday parade, which introduced selection bias. The study employed PHQ-9 which relies on self-reported symptoms which could have led to stigma related under-reporting and social desirability bias. Some adjusted associations had small sub-groups, more especially rural officers (n = 12), thus, the estimates of prevalence ratios presented here maybe unstable. The study did not explore structural factors such as sleeping hours per day, physical exercise, alcohol drinking habits and history of other chronic diseases such as HIV, cancers which could have association with depression. Finally, the cross-section nature of the design limited causal inferences.

On the other hand, our study had some strengths, including the use of the PHQ-9 screening tool which has been validated in the Ugandan context in at least 2 studies and used prior in several studies makes the findings valid. In the study, we sampled 9 of 17 police divisions within KPM, thereby giving a very good representation of the police divisions participating in the study. In addition, the response rate was more than 100% which enabled us to achieve statistical power for the study.

Conclusion

This study found the prevalence of depression among junior police officers in KMP to be higher compared to the global prevalence of depression among police officers, and to findings from several studies in other population settings. Creation of mental health awareness and accessible support structures in police may help them cope with stressful work environment. In addition, creation of friendly work environment with flexible work schedules, for example, encouraging officers to take leave after a stressful field activity, putting in place mechanisms to support officers experiencing trauma at workplace may be a good strategy to reduce mental health challenges among the junior police officers. There is need to conduct mixed-methods studies to explore structural, organizational, and contextual factors influencing depression that may not have been fully captured in the current study. In addition, there is need to conduct longitudinal studies to better assess causal relationships between occupational exposures and depression among police officers.
