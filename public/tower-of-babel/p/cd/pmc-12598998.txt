Improving staff awareness of sensory aid needs and dementia status in an old age ward
Authors: Bekim Arifaj, Abdullah Shakeel, Limaro Nyam, Karen Sansom-Ninnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaving initially done our critical appraisal of various studies, we found that geriatric wards have a higher prevalence of delirium and dementia. Among this older population, sensory aids were found to be important in orientating delirious patients, and deficits in vision/hearing were associated with an increased risk of delirium. Delirium and dementia are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. This quality improvement project aimed to increase staff awareness of patient sensory aid needs and thus improve patient care as a result.MethodsWe started off with a driver diagram to identify what the primary and secondary drivers are for improving the quality of care for patients with sensory impairment. Of the drivers discussed, we believed that we could have a meaningful impact on improving sensory impairment awareness among the multidisciplinary team (MDT) on the ward. We then went through our Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) cycles, which were as follows: PDSA cycle 1, where I and my two colleagues educated the MDT on how to use the electronic patient records property form checklist, which was being underused. This form tracks whether patients require sensory aids. We did three teaching sessions during board rounds and audited the use of this form over time. PDSA cycle 2 used a laminated bedside checklist that is filled in by the MDT and is used as a visual reminder of the patient’s sensory impairment status.ResultsThe EPR form completion rate increased by 14% over a period of 4 weeks, although this was not statistically significant. 18% of the bedside checklists were filled in, which was statistically significant. Both interventions in combination led to a statistically significant increase in sensory impairment awareness, with a 32% decrease in sensory aid unknown rates, a 40% decrease in dementia unknown rates and a 56% decrease in both sensory and dementia unknown rates. Delirium status was not formally assessed in this QIP due to documentation inconsistencies; however, its relevance remains acknowledged.Keywords: Quality improvement, Healthcare quality improvement, Hospital medicine

Introduction

The aim of our quality improvement project (QIP) was to explore and improve care for patients who use sensory aids, with or without dementia, in an old age ward at King’s College Hospital. We sought to do this by increasing the staff’s awareness of each patient’s sensory needs and dementia status.

Guidelines state that sensory aids (glasses, dentures, braille and hearing aids) are important in orientating patients with delirium and dementia, yet these devices frequently go missing during admission or are not being used appropriately.1 This occurs due to several reasons, including patients arriving without them from accident and emergency (A&E), staff not double-checking possession and items being misplaced or hidden by patients themselves.

This could affect communication and, therefore, overall care, both physical and mental.2 It is widely understood that delirium and dementia are associated with increased morbidity and mortality.3 In this project, we aimed to explore issues around sensory aid use and to identify and implement impactful changes.

Having initially done our critical appraisal of various studies, we found that geriatric wards have a higher prevalence of delirium and dementia. Among this older population, sensory aids were found to be important in orientating delirious patients, and deficits in vision/hearing were associated with an increased risk of delirium. Delirium and dementia are associated with increased morbidity and mortality.3

Our initial baseline data obtained from the ward showed us that nurses were not aware of the sensory aid needs of up to 25% of the patients in the ward, and they were unsure about the dementia status of 46.7% in the ward. These numbers were derived from a baseline survey of named nurses on the ward.

As such, the aim is to improve sensory impairment awareness, as this should theoretically improve long-term morbidity and mortality among this patient group.

Materials and methods

Two Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) cycles were conducted between October 2022 and February 2023. A driver diagram was created following staff interviews on the ward. The driver diagram identified primary and secondary drivers of change. It was informed by qualitative staff interviews (three nurses, one physiotherapist and one doctor) exploring perceived barriers to managing sensory needs.

The following are the comments from the staff interview. “Glasses, hearing aids and dentures often get lost…but it could have happened anywhere, starting in accident and emergency (A&E)”, said a nurse. “We usually check the social history for information on sensory aids, but we don’t often double-check with the patient”, said a physiotherapist. “Information on sensory aids is usually included in property checks when patients are admitted, but when patients don’t come in with them, it can be hard to track them down”, said a nurse. “Some patients also hide their sensory aids, and so it can be difficult for staff to know if they need them or not”, said a doctor.

Of the drivers discussed, we believed that we could have a meaningful impact on improving sensory impairment awareness among the multidisciplinary team (MDT) on the ward.

Plan, Do, Study, Act cycle 1

The first PDSA cycle focused on increasing awareness of a form in electronic patient records (EPRs) and the need for documenting each patient’s sensory aid possessions and dementia status. This was done through bite-size teaching sessions to the team and monitoring of completion of this form (table 1). Baseline EPR completion was low, and so this cycle aimed to increase completion, particularly on admission. We predicted that EPR form completion and bedside checklist usage would increase over time, aiming for 25% and 90%, respectively. We expected this would result in a 20% increase in staff awareness of patient sensory impairment and dementia status.

The MDT on the ward will fill in the sensory aid section of the EPR form more often on admission (aiming for a 25% increase, n=8), so there should be an increase in the number of forms filled in over time and an increase in staff awareness of sensory impairment/needs (20% increased awareness, n=6).

Plan, Do, Study, Act cycle 2

The second PDSA cycle included using a new laminated bedside checklist that was manually filled in and was aimed to serve as a visual cue of the patient’s sensory impairment/dementia status (online supplemental figure 1). There were no quick bedside cues that nurses and other medical staff could use to identify sensory aid needs, so this intervention aimed to tackle this (online supplemental figure 1).

Prediction

These checklists will be filled in more over time (90% filled by the end of QIP, n=27) and will also increase staff awareness over time (by 20%).

Results

The baseline survey showed that staff were unsure of the sensory aid needs (glasses, hearing aids, dentures) of 25% of patients (n=7) and 46.7% (n=14) when it came to dementia status.

Our data collection began on 16 November 2022 and continued through to 15 February 2023. We collected and measured our data over five separate weeks, with the first intervention being implemented on the 14 December (between weeks 1 and 2) and the second intervention put into place on the 25 January (between weeks 2 and 3).

There was a statistically significant increase in sensory aid/impairment awareness as showcased in figure 1, with a 32% (n=9) decrease in sensory aid unknown rates, a 40% (n=12) decrease in dementia unknown rates, and a 56% (n=17) decrease in both sensory and dementia unknown rates.

A graph showing changes in sensory aid/impairment awareness over time (weeks).

Intervention 1 showed an improvement in EPR form completion rates, with a 14% (n=4) reduction in non-completion rates (online supplemental figure 2). However, this was statistically insignificant, likely due to the fact that we were missing some data points due to difficulties accessing the EPR system. As such, it is difficult to elucidate how much of an impact intervention 1 had on the improvements in awareness seen in figure 1. Nevertheless, there was a 13% (n=4) increase in staff answers matching EPR (table 1), which suggests that our intervention has improved awareness to an extent.

These issues were largely due to limited login permissions and availability of computers during data collection times (online supplemental figure 2).

In intervention 2, 18% (n=5) of the bedside checklist strips were being used on the ward (figure 2). Although this intervention resulted in a statistically significant improvement, this fell way short of our 90% (n=27) completion target. Regardless, the improvements in awareness seen in figure 1 suggest that this intervention has played a part in its positive trend.

Discussion

Over the duration of the project, there was a 32% (n=9) improvement in staff awareness of sensory impairment status and a 40% (n=12) improvement in staff awareness of dementia status. The concurrent implementation of increased EPR form completion and bedside checklists therefore seemed to have a positive effect in improving staff awareness in the ward. This could potentially lead to long-term improvements in outcomes of elderly patients due to the mitigation of delirium/dementia risk factors; this has been strongly correlated in the literature.4

The project had some limitations, such as difficulties in contacting the entire medical team and letting them know the details of the project. It is often difficult to change current practices within the National Health Service (NHS).5 Auditing the EPR form over time proved challenging due to access difficulties, which delayed some intervention implementations. In addition, we were only able to come into the wards on Wednesdays, meaning we couldn’t meet the whole medical team. As such, not everyone was aware of our interventions, making staff engagement harder to optimise. Furthermore, we had difficulties contacting the consultants early in the project, delaying the implementation of our interventions. Despite these difficulties, we were still able to implement our changes relatively seamlessly, as our interventions were low maintenance; this further highlights the importance of adhering to sustainable principles.6 7 However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the short time frame and limited number of data points collected preintervention and postintervention.

The main strength of the project was its associated low cost and maintenance. This made the interventions easier to put into place and will also make the project more likely to be sustainable over the long term. However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the short time frame and limited number of data points collected preintervention and postintervention.

Embedding sustainable changes to the Marjory Warren ward was one of our main priorities, as we wanted interventions that could last long term to improve patient care for years to come. To achieve this, we used interventions that had a low burden on staff. For instance, the A4 sheet reminding staff to fill in the EPR form is low maintenance, does not cost much money and is a simple way to elicit change slowly over time. Moreover, the EPR system is used across the NHS. As such, this intervention has a lot of carryover to other departments within King’s College Hospital/other hospitals, making the intervention 1 sustainable for the future. However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the short time frame and limited number of data points collected preintervention and postintervention.

The bedside strips also have the potential to be sustainable. For example, we are working with one of the nurses who is reminding staff to fill them in daily at the morning handovers. There is also potential for these strips to be implemented at a greater scale across other departments, as they are small and able to fit on the whiteboards behind the patient beds. However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the short time frame and limited number of data points collected preintervention and postintervention.

Another strength of this project was the clear objectives put in place. We had a good understanding of what was being measured and how the efficacy of the interventions could be monitored. This allowed for us to collect sufficient data and ascertain the effectiveness of our interventions, which can hopefully be carried forward in future practice.

Additionally, the interventions used in this project can be used outside geriatric wards, as they can easily be introduced into any ward with older age inpatients. This further strengthens this project due to its easy application to any department within a hospital.

Further PDSA cycles may be explored in the future to measure the long-term impacts of this project on patient outcomes, as well as to evaluate whether the changes are maintained.

This QIP highlights the importance of managing sensory impairment within healthcare and also shows that there is still room for improvement in this regard within the NHS in the UK, although other countries do have issues dealing with sensory impairment as well.8 Implementation of cost-effective interventions, therefore, could be of huge benefit to patients, improving outcomes and in turn saving healthcare systems money.9 In addition, educating healthcare professionals on the importance of sensory impairment, and thus increasing general awareness, could have significant positive impacts on health outcomes within the NHS. However, it is often difficult to change behaviours on a large scale, particularly within a healthcare setting, but this can be achievable in the long run if more sensory aid-related QIPs are run across multiple trusts within the UK.

Improving sensory impairment can improve patient outcomes via multiple modalities. For instance, sensory impairment can hinder effective communication and thus make it less likely for patients to understand medical advice and adhere to medical treatment plans.10 Mitigating this by ensuring older patients have their visual and hearing aids present can therefore be of great benefit, further highlighting the importance of engaging in QIPs to ensure patients sensory aid requirements are well managed.

Extending beyond ward-based healthcare management, sensory impairment is also an important issue within the community.11 Subpar management of sensory impairment outside of the hospital setting can lead to increased risk of accidents or injuries.12 For example, poor vision can lead to falls and thus lead to injuries such as hip fractures,13 which increase the risk of delirium while patients are admitted.14 Poor hearing can also prevent people from recognising dangers in their environment, which in turn also leads to an increased risk of accidents.15 This shows that awareness of sensory impairment among the geriatric population is important within the community as well as hospital wards.

Although not mentioned in this QIP, poorly controlled sensory impairment can lead to psychological issues, such as an increased propensity to developing conditions such as depression and anxiety.16 This can be due to reasons such as social isolation, as their sensory impairment can prevent them from going out. This worsening of mental health could also worsen outcomes in delirium patients,17 such is the multifactorial nature of the condition.

This QIP has the potential to extend beyond just ward-based care and can be used to monitor various outcomes and interventions.

Conclusions

Overall, our project has yielded positive results. This project achieved a statistically significant improvement in staff awareness of patient sensory impairment due to the implementation of our interventions. While uptake of the forms and bedside checklist was slow, the project did show an improvement in awareness of staff, and our hypothesis is that this leads to better use of sensory aids. The next step would be to assess whether this leads to better care through further PDSA cycles.
