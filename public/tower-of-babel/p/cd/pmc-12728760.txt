Stimulated Raman Scattering Imaging Enabled Biomarker Discovery for Precision Medicine
Authors: Yuhui Li, Jianlin Liu, Shuhua Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Molecular biomarkers play an essential role in accurate disease diagnosis and personalized treatment. Dysregulated metabolism is closely associated with disease development and progression. The discovery of metabolic biomarkers could remarkably promote precision diagnosis and personalized treatment. Current metabolomics approaches can profile a large number of metabolites but are primarily destructive and lack sufficient spatial resolution, which hinders quantitative measurements of the highly dynamic and heterogeneous intracellular metabolic processes. This further limits the discovery of metabolic biomarkers in these diseases. Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy addresses these gaps by enabling label-free imaging with high sensitivity, molecular specificity, and subcellular resolution. Integrating Raman-active vibrational probes further extends this approach, allowing for real-time tracking of low-abundance biomolecules and metabolic processes. These capabilities have enabled the discovery of biomarkers for disease diagnosis. In this review, we focus on recent advancements in SRS imaging technologies and data analysis methods and their applications in biomarker discovery and precision medicine. Furthermore, future perspectives and emerging trends in this rapidly evolving research area are discussed.

Introduction

Molecular biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity are essential for accurate disease diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies. − Recent advances in omics-based technologiessuch as transcriptomics and proteomicshave significantly advanced our understanding of disease biology by revealing the gene expression level and regulatory mechanisms at the protein level. However, these molecular levels are confined primarily to the delineation of regulatory events. In contrast, metabolism, as the integrated functional output of genetic programs, protein activities, and environmental influences, provides a more direct reflection of the physiological state of cells. ,

Metabolic reprogramming constitutes a hallmark of pathological conditions such as cancers, infectious diseases, and metabolic syndromes. − Notably, metabolism is a highly dynamic and heterogeneous process that varies across spatial and temporal scales. The ability to monitor metabolic biomarkers at the single-cell level has consequently become indispensable for early detection, precise disease management, and improved clinical outcomes. , Traditional metabolomics approaches, which predominantly rely on bulk measurements, have provided valuable insights into global metabolic states but inevitably lack cellular heterogeneity information. Building upon these methods, spatially resolved strategies, such as mass spectrometry imaging, have significantly advanced the profiling of metabolic biomarkers, but they remain constrained by low spatial resolution, destructive sample preparation, and the inability to dynamically monitor metabolic changes in real time. − These limitations hinder their effectiveness in capturing spatially resolved, real-time alterations of disease-associated metabolic biomarkers within biological systems. Therefore, there remains a pressing need for molecular imaging platforms that can achieve high spatial resolution, high sensitivity, and compatibility with live-sample, label-free, in situ analysis.

In addition to mass spectrometry, Raman spectroscopy, which detects molecular-specific vibrational contrasts of chemical bonds, offers high chemical selectivity and has been widely applied in diverse biological studies. , Unfortunately, due to weak signals, spontaneous Raman scattering microscopy (Figure a) has limited imaging speed, which hinders its application in molecular diagnosis. , In order to address the problem, coherent Raman scattering (CRS) microscopy was developed by significantly enhancing vibrational excitation rates by nearly 108-fold, thereby enabling real-time imaging in live biological systems. − There are two main forms of CRS, coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) and stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) (Figure b,c), as nonlinear optical processes of coherent excitation. , Although CARS microscopy has been widely applied in biomedical research in the past two decades, its nonresonant background limits both sensitivity and specificity. To address this challenge, SRS microscopy was introduced in 2008 and subsequently applied to label-free biomedical imaging for the first time. The advantages of SRS over CARS lie in the fact that the SRS signal is completely free of the nonresonant background, the spectral profile is identical to the spontaneous Raman spectrum, and the SRS intensity is linearly dependent on molecular concentration, which enables straightforward generation of quantitative concentration maps of biomolecules. These advantages render SRS microscopy a highly sensitive, chemically selective modality with subcellular resolution for biochemical analysis. ,

Single-frequency SRS microscopy, which acquires a signal at a single vibrational frequency, provides high chemical sensitivity and rapid imaging performance, but it is unable to distinguish overlapping biomolecular signatures in complex biological systems. In order to overcome this limitation, hyperspectral SRS (hSRS) microscopy has been developed to enhance chemical specificity through the acquisition of the Raman spectrum at each pixel, thereby enabling comprehensive molecular characterization and multiplexed analysis of biological specimens. Such functionality is achieved through strategies including wavelength tuning, spectral focusing, and parallel excitation (Figure d). This capability enables in situ visualization of diverse biomolecular species, such as lipids, nucleic acids, proteins, and small-molecule metabolites, within structurally intricate biological systems (Figure e). −

Furthermore, for certain types of biomolecules with relatively low concentrations within cells, in order to enhance the applicability of the SRS microscope, Raman-active vibrational probes have been developed by incorporating small, bioorthogonal chemical groups into metabolic substrates, which can be selectively integrated into endogenous molecules within the cellular “silent region” (1800–2800 cm–1), thereby enabling real-time tracking of metabolic processes with high sensitivity and specificity (Figure f). , In parallel, advances in optical hardware have substantially accelerated imaging speed and thereby permitted the rapid visualization of dynamic biochemical processes in living systems. Moreover, improvements in spatial resolution have been achieved through instrumentation refinements and the integration of computational approaches, thereby enabling the interrogation of subcellular structures with unprecedented precision. Ultimately underscored by these advances is the unique capability of SRS not only to resolve biochemical heterogeneity at both intracellular and intratissue levels, but also to enable the sensitive, specific, and spatially precise identification of disease-associated molecular biomarkers under both physiological and pathological conditions. ,

Numerous studies have demonstrated the utility of SRS microscopy in the detection of disease-associated molecular biomarkers across a broad spectrum of pathological conditions, including cancer, infectious diseases, and metabolic disorders. − Since the technical developments of SRS microscopy have been extensively discussed in multiple reviews, − ,− in this review, we focus on how SRS imaging enables the discovery of disease-associated molecular biomarkers and thereby contributes to the advancement of precision medicine. First, we summarize recent advances in SRS imaging technologies that collectively enhance four critical performance dimensionsmolecular specificity, sensitivity, imaging speed, and spatial resolutionthese improvements expand the applicability of SRS for probing dynamic and heterogeneous biological processes, thereby creating new opportunities for biomarker discovery. Then, we discuss how these innovations support biomarker identification across diverse pathological contexts. Finally, we outline the future directions of SRS-based technologies in precision diagnosis, as well as their prospects for clinical translation.

Technological Advancement for Biomarker Discovery

Specificity Enhancement

In the field of biomedical imaging, the specific detection of biomolecular markers plays a crucial role in disease diagnosis and treatment monitoring. However, due to spectral overlap from similar chemical bondsparticularly in the C–H stretching (2800–3200 cm–1) regionsmost biomarkers still face specificity limitations, which makes it difficult for the initial single-frequency excitation SRS to distinguish molecules with overlapping Raman spectral bands. This limits their application value in complex biological samples. To overcome these challenges, a variety of strategies have been developed to enhance the molecular specificity in SRS imaging, including multicolor SRS, hyperspectral acquisition strategies, and Raman probe-based approaches.

Multicolor Strategies

To quantitatively measure multiple components with overlapping Raman bands, multicolor SRS microscopy (e.g., two-color and three-color SRS microscopy) has been developed by tuning laser wavelengths to match multiple vibrational frequencies. − However, this wavelength tuning approach may lose the colocalization information on different Raman modes, especially when the sample is in a dynamic environment. To solve this problem, Lu et al. proposed spectral shaping, wherein multiple frequency components from broadband pump light are extracted and detected separately through the use of a grating spectrometer. Through this technique, highly specific chemical contrast images can be simultaneously acquired with high speed (typically ∼1.2 s per frame for a 512 × 512 image). This approach enabled the successful mapping of protein and lipid distributions. Similarly, Ji et al. developed a multicolor SRS approach that utilizes an enhanced Optical Parametric Oscillator (OPO) system for rapid wavelength tuning. Combined with line scanning technology, this method can minimize spectral deviation during the movement of the sample. Through linear decomposition of multicolor SRS images, multiple molecular signaturesincluding total lipids, proteins, and unsaturated lipidscan be simultaneously extracted. However, the inherent spectral overlap among metabolites, especially in the strong and crowded carbon–hydrogen (CH) regions, severely hinders the specificity of quantitative chemical analysis and molecular recognition in cells and tissues using multicolor SRS microscopy.

Hyperspectral Strategies

Hyperspectral Setup

To resolve complex biological components, hSRS microscopy with enhanced chemical specificity has been developed to acquire Raman spectra at each pixel, thereby enabling comprehensive molecular characteristic characterization. , HSRS imaging employs advanced techniques such as wavelength tuning, spectral focusing, and parallel excitation to achieve high spectral resolution and imaging specificity. Sequential wavelength tuning is achieved by scanning the vibration frequency step by step for hyperspectral imaging. This process can be controlled by adjusting the temperature of a nonlinear crystal with a large tuning range or by using an electro-optical tunable Lyot filter for fast tuning. In the latter approach, a broadband femtosecond laser pulse is first converted into a narrow-band picosecond pulse. The desired excitation wavelength is then selected during the pulse shaping process to achieve fast and precise wavelength tuning. , However, the sequential nature of acquisition imposes limitations on the imaging speed, making it less suitable for dynamic or large-area biomarker mapping. Another approach, spectral focusing, employs two broad-band femtosecond laser beams to enhance time-domain overlap. Unlike wavelength scanning, which utilizes narrowband sources, spectral focusing leverages the broad spectral range of femtosecond lasers to achieve precise spectral information. , For instance, Liao et al. employed a resonant scanning mirror to rapidly modulate the time delay between broadband laser pulses, enabling the acquisition of a 200 cm–1 spectral window with ∼30 cm–1 resolution in only 83 μs. Moreover, parallel SRS imaging over a large spectral window can be achieved by using a combination of picosecond (ps) and femtosecond (fs) lasers for broadband excitation. The implementation of multichannel array detectors allows for real-time demodulation of SRS signals across multiple spectral channels. , The primary advantage of this approach is its capability for multiplexed biomarker imaging. Collectively, hSRS imaging offers substantial advantages in molecular discrimination by enabling pixel-resolved acquisition of vibrational spectra, thereby allowing for the differentiation of structurally similar biomolecules.

Spectral Analysis

Although widely adopted for biomarker identification, this technique, when coupled with sophisticated analytical methods, holds considerable promise for uncovering subtle biochemical heterogeneities that underlie disease initiation and progression. Nonetheless, extracting clinically meaningful insights from such high-dimensional data sets remains a formidable challenge. Without effective data processing strategies, the diagnostic and prognostic contents encoded within hyperspectral signals may remain largely untapped. Consequently, advanced computational approachesparticularly those based on artificial intelligencehave become essential for realizing the full potential of hSRS imaging in both basic science and clinical contexts.

As reliance on computational methods continues to grow, a range of data analysis techniques have been developed to improve spectral decomposition and enhance the specificity of biomarker identification. Multivariate curve resolution-alternating least-squares (MCR-ALS), a representative hyperspectral unmixing algorithm, has enabled quantitative spatial mapping of select biomarkers, such as the degree of fat unsaturation and cholesterol storage. , However, its performance is constrained under conditions of high spectral overlap or a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). To overcome these limitations, Lin et al. developed a pixel-wise least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) unmixing algorithm, successfully mapping five principal biomolecular constituentsproteins, carbohydrates, fatty acids, cholesterol, and nucleic acidswithin the CH stretching region. Furthermore, the Penalized Reference Matching (PRM) algorithm, devised as a novel strategy for visualizing spatial distributions and abundances of specific molecules, facilitates quantitative spatial mapping of diverse lipid subtypes, including sphingosine and cardiolipin. Although those unmixing algorithms have achieved remarkable performance, the transformative potential of deep-learning-driven unmixing methodologies cannot be overlooked in this field. Despite the underdevelopment of deep learning applications in hyperspectral CRS unmixing, Georgiev et al.’s unmixing autoencoder algorithm represents a noteworthy advancement, enabling unsupervised decomposition of five key biomolecular components in Raman spectroscopic microscopy while demonstrating superior accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency relative to conventional methods.

Importantly, these computational innovations markedly enhance the specificity and fidelity of biomarker identification, thereby broadening the translational applications of hSRS imaging to include early disease diagnostics, live-cell phenotyping, and precision-guided therapeutic monitoring. ,

Raman Probe Strategies

Although hSRS imaging significantly improves chemical specificity by acquiring spectral profiles, it remains challenging to distinguish biomolecules with highly overlapping vibrational signatures or to resolve molecular subtypes within complex biological systems. To overcome these limitations and further enhance the molecular selectivity, various Raman-active vibrational tags have been integrated into SRS microscopy. Small Raman labels, such as alkynes, nitriles, and deuterium-substituted groups, exhibit sharp and intense peaks in the cellular Raman-silent region (1800–2700 cm–1), which are well-separated from endogenous backgrounds. These probes permit dynamic tracking of small molecule metabolism in living systems with minimal biological interference, establishing a foundation for bioorthogonal chemical imaging.

Typically, vibrational probes are categorized by size and functional purpose into three types: vibrational tags for small molecule imaging (<1 nm), a palette of Raman dyes for supermultiplex imaging (several nm), and Raman-active nanomaterials (>10 nm). Among them, small vibrational probes such as deuterium labeling or triple-bond-based groups are particularly effective in displaying the spatial distribution of biomolecules and in specifically tracing the metabolic turnover of labeled species within cells, thereby providing critical support for studying the metabolic dynamics of molecular biomarkers. , For instance, Lee et al. utilized deuterated cholesterol in SRS imaging to specifically track cholesterol distribution and metabolism at the single-cell level. Compared to isotopic labeling, triple bondssuch as alkyne (CC) and nitrile (CN)are larger in size but exhibit distinct Raman peaks within the cell-silent region and more robust signals, even at low concentrations. This enables highly sensitive and specific SRS imaging of a small number of species. Alkyne labels, initially employed by Yamakoshi in spontaneous Raman microscopy and later adopted for SRS imaging, have proven effective in tracking various biomolecules. , Specifically, Lu et al. expanded this application by employing alkyne to label DNA and RNA in live-cell SRS imaging, enabling the specific tracking of dynamic molecular functions.

To further enhance multiplexed specificity, Wei et al. developed Manhattan Raman Scattering (MARS) dyes, which use isotopic editing and bond modifications to generate up to 20 distinct vibrational signatures within the silent region. These probes were validated in live-cell assays to simultaneously distinguish multiple cellular targets, thereby enabling high-content profiling of the molecular phenotypes. Additionally, Hu et al. embedded carbon rainbow (Carbow) probes in polystyrene beads to create supermultiplexed barcodes, achieving up to 60,000 unique spectral signatures for high-throughput and specific labeling of cells. Collectively, these innovations in vibrational probe design not only compensate for the specificity limitations of label-free SRS imaging but also provide access to temporally resolved dynamic molecular processes within living systems. As such, the integration of Raman-active tags into SRS microscopy represents a significant advancement in the molecular imaging of biomarkers, bridging real-time metabolic visualization with emerging needs in clinical diagnostics and precision medicine.

Sensitivity Enhancement

Instrumentation Improvement

As biomolecular markers are frequently present at extremely low concentrations within cellular or tissue microenvironments, the detection sensitivity of molecular imaging platforms is required to be exceptionally high in order to meet the demands of biomedical applications. Although SRS microscopy, when excited by a time-lens laser synchronized with a femtosecond pulse, is capable of achieving mM-level sensitivity, such performance remains inadequate for detecting endogenous biomarkers at trace levels. , To overcome this limitation, a series of methodological innovations have been introduced to enhance SRS sensitivity and thereby broaden its applicability in low-abundance biomolecular analysis. For instance, Lin et al. developed an ultrasensitive reweighted visible stimulated Raman scattering (rvSRS) technique, which integrates delay-modulated laser pulses with multichannel signal processing to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional near-infrared SRS, thereby enabling molecular detection down to ∼4000 molecules (∼2 mM), and offering a promising tool for cellular metabolism research and precision medicine. Moreover, SRS detection sensitivity closely depends on the corresponding excitation spectroscopy, which can be drastically enhanced by coupling to an electronic resonance or localized surface plasmon. By carefully tuning the molecular absorptions of chromophores close to, but not identical to, SRS excitation wavelength, Wei et al. successfully extended the application of electron preresonant SRS (EPR-SRS), thereby pushing the detection limit to sub-μM, while maintaining the sharp vibrational feature with high chemical selectivity. Moreover, Zong et al. achieved single-molecule sensitivity in SRS by leveraging surface enhancement effects at individual hotspots within nanosphere aggregates. Through the application of a customized denoising algorithm, they demonstrated the detection of single adenine molecules via plasmon-enhanced SRS with an acquisition time of 10 μs. This work provides compelling evidence that vibrational signals from single molecules can be detected via SRS, and further identifies the photothermal effect as a major source of background noisesuggesting that improvements in experimental design could further enhance sensitivity.

Raman Probe Strategies

Coupling SRS with strong vibrational tags such as alkynes (CC) allows for effective imaging of diverse biomolecules, but detection sensitivity is limited to about 15 mM for typical chemical bonds such as C–H and 200 μM for the stronger CC bond, leaving many targets (such as metabolites, proteins, and RNA) out of reach. , Alkyne/nitrile vibrational tags establish a quantitative baseline for probe-assisted SRS sensitivity: using EdU-alkyne as an example, the detection limit reaches ∼200 μM. Chemical optimization of the tag further lowers the threshold to ∼35 μM for diyne labels, whereas small alkyne tags remain around 200 μM under nonresonant conditions. , A step-change in sensitivity is achieved by EPR-SRS combined with near-infrared dyes, which boosts vibrational signals by >104 and compresses limits of detection from the nonresonant (35–200 μM) range to submicromolar levels. For instance, ATTO740 can be detected at ∼250 nM. To simultaneously widen target coverage, triple-bond-conjugated dye palettes (e.g., MARS/9-cyanopyronin) place single, resolvable peaks in the cell-silent window, enabling supermultiplex vibrational imaging while preserving EPR-SRS sensitivity gains. , Taken together, these results highlight that rational probe design not only enhances sensitivity but also extends multiplexing capability, thereby overcoming the long-standing limitation of SRS in detecting weakly scattering biomolecules, such as nucleic acids and proteins.

Speed Enhancement

Cellular metabolism is a highly dynamic and heterogeneous process that exhibits substantial variability across both spatial and temporal dimensions. Under pathological conditions such as cancer and infectious diseases, metabolic phenotypes undergo changes in response to microenvironmental stressors, including hypoxia, acidosis, and nutrient deprivation. Consequently, the ability to dynamically track biomolecular markers within biological systems is essential for elucidating the biochemical mechanisms underlying disease onset, progression, and therapeutic response. , To meet this demand, video-rate imaging and rapid hyperspectral acquisition become crucial for capturing rapid biological dynamics in real time. Nevertheless, conventional SRS microscopy has long been constrained by limited imaging speeds, thereby impeding the accurate capture of fast biological dynamics and the ability to distinguish between chemically similar metabolites within complex biological systems.

Single-Frequency SRS Imaging Speed Improvement

By integrating resonant scanning mirrors, researchers successfully developed an external-view synchrotron radiation microscope, which made it possible to perform video-rate SRS imaging in vivo while minimizing motion-induced artifacts. However, due to the difficulty in constructing dedicated detectors, this method has not been widely adopted. Further improvements were achieved through a spatially multiplexed SRS system, which utilizes detector arrays to achieve higher frame rates and enables real-time imaging of chemical kinetic processes. Although these developments represent meaningful progress, technical challenges such as the complexity of beam alignment and the reduction in excitation power per channel have not yet been resolved.

Hyperspectral SRS Spectral Acquisition Speed Improvement

Equally important to spatial imaging speed is the acceleration of spectral acquisition in hSRS, which enables comprehensive molecular fingerprinting. Spectral focusing has emerged as a key technique for this purpose, utilizing chirped broadband femtosecond pulses. By adjustment of the time delay between excitation pulses, vibrational information is time-encoded and reconstructed spectrally. The most straightforward implementation involves using an electrically controlled delay stage. However, its scanning speed remains limited. To address this limitation, high-speed delay modulation methods (including photodriven scanners, polygon mirrors, and acousto-optic modulators) have been adopted to significantly increase the spectral acquisition rate. For example, Wakisaka et al. demonstrated the combination of a high-speed disk scanner with a tunable band-pass filter to achieve high-throughput component imaging of tissues and living cells. , Additionally, combining spectral focusing with fast scanning optical elements enables spectral acquisition at the microsecond level.

Spectral multiplexing is another method for achieving high-speed spectral acquisition, in which a broadband femtosecond laser is combined with a narrow-bandwidth picosecond laser to simultaneously excite multiple vibration bands. Zhang et al. achieved a spectral acquisition rate of 5 μs per spectrum by developing a multiplexed high-spectrum SRS system using a 32-channel tunable amplifier array. Although these technologies have made significant progress, spectral focusing and multiplexing methods are still inherently limited by the bandwidth of the excitation laser source. Although broadband lasers have been explored to expand spectral coverage, issues such as unstable output power and insufficient pulse energy still pose challenges. To overcome these limitations, new tunable laser technologies have emerged. A pulse pair-resolved wavelength-switchable laser has been reported for ultrafast multicolor SRS imaging on flow cytometry. On the laser side, high-speed swept-source techniques using a Fourier-domain mode-locked laser or galvo/resonant scanner-based wavelength sweeping were developed, providing greater spectral flexibility and enabling multiwindow SRS imaging. ,

In summary, these technological advancements in both temporal and spectral dimensions have significantly enhanced the performance of the SRS microscope, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the dynamic spatial distribution and complex spectral signatures of biomolecular markers in living systems. However, a fundamental challenge persists in balancing the spectral resolution, acquisition speed, and molecular specificity. Broader spectral coverage and faster acquisition are often accompanied by compromises in signal quality or spectral specificity, particularly in highly multiplexed or dynamic biological contexts. To address these limitations, future development may rely on advancements in hardware devices, such as lasers, or innovations in computational methods like spectral reconstruction based on deep learning. These efforts are anticipated to yield next-generation hSRS platforms that can achieve real-time, high-specificity molecular imaging without sacrificing throughput or sensitivity.

Spatial Resolution Enhancement

Enhancing spatial resolution in SRS microscopy is of critical importance for the accurate detection of biomolecular markers, especially in demanding clinical contexts such as early stage cancer diagnosis and intraoperative delineation of tumor margins. As a nonlinear imaging modality, SRS microscopy inherently provides optical sectioning and reaches a diffraction-limited resolution comparable to that of two-photon excitation fluorescence microscopy. Using near-infrared (NIR) excitation (typically 800–1064 nm), conventional SRS systems typically achieve lateral resolutions of approximately 300 nm and axial resolutions of 1–2 μm. However, such a resolution is often insufficient for subcellular metabolic imaging or distinguishing spatially adjacent biomolecular features. To overcome this limitation, a range of instrumental innovations and algorithm strategies has emerged.

Instrumentation Improvement

To date, numerous optical engineering strategies have been developed for super-resolution SRS microscopy, including visible-wavelength SRS, , stimulated emission depletion (STED)-based methods, expansion SRS microscopy, and beyond. , Visible-wavelength SRS, for instance, shifts excitation from near-infrared to visible wavelengths, thereby achieving lateral resolutions approaching ∼130 nm. However, this approach introduces inherent phototoxicity risks due to higher-energy, shorter-wavelength lasers. In contrast, STED-based approaches enable approximately 2-fold resolution enhancement while using NIR lasers by depleting nearly all lateral emission, retaining only the focal Gaussian spot with dimensions below the diffraction limit. Critically, STED-like SRS imaging suffers from the inherent sensitivity limitations due to the weak nature of Raman scattering, particularly when achieving super-resolution excitation through subdiffraction-limited focal spots. Without additional optical reconfiguration, expansion SRS microscopy physically enlarges samples via embedding in swellable-polymer hydrogels, thereby achieving <100 nm spatial resolution. However, this approach also presents fundamental limitations: incompatibility with live specimens and the fact that specific reagents used during sample processing induce the loss of biomolecular components, particularly cellular lipids. In parallel, the integration of tailored vibrational probes and enhanced contrast mechanisms has provided further resolution gains.

Algorithm Strategies

To transcend the diffraction-limited resolution (∼300 nm) of conventional SRS microscopy, a series of computational approaches has been successively developed. The Richardson–Lucy (RL) algorithma classical iterative deconvolution methodwas integrated with squeezed light-controlled SRS microscopy by Li et al., achieving an approximate 2.2-fold spatial resolution enhancement over conventional SRS imaging. To further enhance both performance and computational efficiency of deconvolution algorithms, the Adam-optimized pointillism deconvolution (A-PoD) framework was developed, achieving 50–100 nm spatial resolution in SRS imaging. Although these deconvolution algorithms achieve effective resolution recovery in their target applications, their resolution restoration performance is markedly compromised under low-SNR conditions. To address this challenge, Lin et al. devised FURNACE (Fourier reweighting after NACE denoising)a super-resolution technique achieving 86 nm lateral resolution in cellular imaging. Compared to RL and A-PoD algorithms, FURNACE achieves simultaneous noise suppression and preservation of complex structures in low-SNR image processing, significantly enhancing the robustness of resolution enhancement for low-quality images.

Enhancing the spatial resolution in SRS microscopy is essential for resolving subcellular metabolic heterogeneity and distinguishing spatially adjacent molecular features. This improvement also enables real-time and highly specific detection of molecular markers, which is particularly valuable for early cancer diagnosis and precise intraoperative tumor margin delineation. These advancements facilitate the broader implementation of SRS imaging in precision medicine and clinical translation.

Discovery of New Biomarkers for Diseases Enabled by SRS Microscopy

Discovery of Lipid Biomarkers in Diseases

Lipids play fundamental roles in energy storage, membrane structure, and cell signaling. The cellular lipid metabolism network includes exogenous lipid uptake, de novo lipogenesis, lipid droplet (LD) storage, and catabolic breakdown through β-oxidation. Disruptions in any of these processessuch as excessive synthesis, impaired lipolysis, or abnormal storagecan trigger pathological consequences, including metabolic diseases, cancer, atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative diseases, and so on. ,

Fatty acids (FAs) are synthesized mainly through de novo lipogenesis or taken up from extracellular sources and are esterified with glycerol to form triacylglycerols (TAGs), which are stored in LDs. When the energy demand increases, TAGs are hydrolyzed into free FAs and released for mitochondrial β-oxidation. This dynamic network is tightly regulated to maintain homeostasis. Beyond the flux of FAs through anabolic and catabolic routes, another dimension of lipid metabolic remodeling is the alteration of the degree of FA unsaturation within TAGs. Fatty acid desaturation is catalyzed by stearoyl-CoA desaturases (primarily SCD1), and this desaturation modulates membrane fluidity, LD morphology, and mitochondrial function. In cancers, the accumulation of saturated fatty acids can promote the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells and induce endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative stress to promote the resistance of tumor cells to death. Additionally, unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) serve as an important lipid reserve for fatty acid β-oxidation. Recent studies also show that the saturated fatty acids (SFA) /UFAs ratio is a key determinant of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis susceptibility, which is implicated in cancer progression and therapy response. ,

Beyond FAs, cholesterol and its esterified form, cholesteryl ester (CE), represent another pivotal lipid class that profoundly influences cellular physiology. In normal physiology, free cholesterol is distributed within cellular and organelle membranes, where it contributes to membrane fluidity and serves as a precursor for hormone biosynthesis. − The esterification reaction of cholesterol, catalyzed by acyl-CoA cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT1 and ACAT2), represents a key step in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis and preventing the accumulation of free cholesterol at cytotoxic levels. Disruption of this esterification balance can lead to alterations in lipid droplet formation and cholesterol storage, ultimately resulting in metabolic disorders. , For instance, the aggregation of CE during atherosclerosis reflects abnormal cholesterol metabolism. Importantly, cholesterol represents only one category within the broader class of sterols, which also encompasses bile acids, steroid hormones, and vitamin D precursors. Dysregulation of these sterol metabolites, much like cholesterol itself, has been increasingly recognized as a causal factor in diverse pathological states, thereby underscoring the centrality of sterol metabolism in human health and disease.

To assess lipid metabolic alterations in situ, label-free SRS imaging enables real-time visualization of lipid composition based on CH2, CH3, and CC vibrational modes, allowing for quantitative mapping of TAGs (∼2850 cm–1), CEs (∼2870–2900 cm–1), and FA unsaturation (∼3010 cm–1). This approach has been widely applied to characterize metabolic reprogramming in cancer, metabolic disorders, neurodegeneration, and microbial systems. Beyond endogenous contrast, Raman-active lipid probessuch as deuterium-labeled fatty acidsprovide enhanced chemical specificity and dynamic tracing of lipid biosynthesis and trafficking, enabling the functional investigation of lipid biomarkers in complex biological environments.

Fatty Acid Metabolic Biomarkers

Cancers

Fatty acid metabolism is fundamental to cellular homeostasis, and its dysregulation has been increasingly recognized as a hallmark of disease progression. In particular, the synthesis and aberrant accumulation of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) have been linked to tumor development and progression. For example, Yan et al. revealed aberrant accumulation of SFAs in hepatocellular carcinoma tissues by mapping the C–H stretching region (2800–3050 cm–1) using hSRS (Figure a–c), underscoring the link between lipid saturation and tumor progression. To achieve chemical specificity, they applied MCR analysis to deconvolve hyperspectral data sets, thereby confirming the enrichment of SFAs within tumor-associated lipid droplets. Since fatty acid synthesis ultimately feeds into lipid storage pathways, alterations in the composition and dynamics of lipid droplets have emerged as another critical dimension of metabolic dysregulation. Zeng et al. integrated multicolor SRS microscopy with confocal Raman spectroscopy and revealed that triglyceride-rich lipid droplets, characterized by an increased unsaturation of fatty acids, constitute a metabolic hallmark of papillary thyroid carcinoma metastasis. This discovery not only highlighted lipid droplet reprogramming as a driver of tumor aggressiveness but also suggested that aberrant lipid storage may represent a therapeutic vulnerability in metastatic progression.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying abnormal fatty acid metabolism, alkynyl-labeled and deuterium-labeled lipids, including 17-octadecenoic acid, were employed by researchers, whereby high-resolution tracking of intracellular triglyceride biosynthesis was enabled through SRS imaging. By directly visualizing the biosynthetic pathways of lipid species in living cells, mechanistic insights were thereby obtained into how dysregulated lipid synthesis and storage contribute to disease, which in turn facilitated the identification and validation of lipid-based molecular biomarkers. ,

Following SFA synthesis, their metabolic fate is often determined by desaturation pathways that generate monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs and PUFAs), a process that profoundly influences cancer cell survival and plasticity. , Expanding on this concept, Li et al. combined hSRS with lipidomics and found significantly elevated levels of UFAs in ovarian cancer stem cells (CSCs) compared to their nontumorigenic counterparts. Functional studies further demonstrated that fatty acid desaturases are essential for CSC self-renewal and tumor initiation, highlighting lipid unsaturation as a metabolic vulnerability in ovarian cancer. Beyond ovarian cancer, SRS has been applied to the most aggressive primary brain tumor tissue imaging. For instance, Yuan et al. evaluated the therapeutic potential of free fatty acid-induced lipotoxicity for the suppression of glioma growth using SRS and showed that exposure to polyunsaturated fatty acids (EPA) significantly reduced the survival rate of glioma cells by more than 50%, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of PUFA-driven lipotoxic stress.

To further elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of lipid-driven pathology, recent advances have introduced vibrational probes into SRS imaging, offering enhanced chemical specificity and enabling dynamic tracing of lipid metabolism at subcellular resolution. , Among these, several studies have demonstrated how specific alterations in fatty acid composition influence membrane organization and cellular function. For example, Du et al. employed deuterated fatty acids to reveal the formation of solid membrane structures in melanoma cells. Their findings indicated that inhibition of MUFA synthesis induced a transition in the membrane state from fluid to rigid, ultimately impairing cell viability and migratory capacity (Figure d). These findings suggest that regulating lipid metabolism may provide a new therapeutic strategy for melanoma by altering tumor cell behavior. In another study, Lee et al. developed a multimodal Raman platform and discovered that metastatic melanoma undergoes metabolic reprogramming toward a lipid droplet-rich phenotype enriched in cholesteryl esters and unsaturated fatty acids, driven primarily by fatty acid uptake rather than de novo synthesis alone, which promotes tumor progression, highlighting fatty acid metabolic reprogramming as a metabolic signature that can be harnessed for early diagnosis and improved treatment of metastatic melanoma (Figure e).

Beyond their role in metabolic vulnerability, the dynamic rewiring of desaturation pathways has been shown to govern phenotypic transitions in cancer. Chen et al. integrated isomer-resolved lipidomics with SRS microscopy to investigate epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). By directly visualizing lipid unsaturation at subcellular resolution, they uncovered a metabolic switch from stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) to fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2) driven unsaturation, a reprogramming that promoted membrane fluidity, EMT plasticity, and metastatic potential. This study underscored UFAs not only as biomarkers of EMT but also as active mediators of metastatic aggressiveness.

Collectively, these findings delineate a coherent framework in which SFA synthesis, subsequent desaturation, and lipid remodeling converge to shape cancer metabolism and progression, thereby revealing promising avenues for therapeutic intervention and translational diagnostics.

Liver Metabolic Diseases

Liver lipid metabolism plays a central role in systemic energy balance, and its dysregulation has been increasingly recognized as a hallmark of metabolic diseases, such as obesity, insulin resistance, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). In particular, the imbalance between SFAs and UFAs critically shapes hepatic lipid homeostasis, contributing to disease pathogenesis.

Several studies have demonstrated the power of SRS imaging techniques in investigating liver lipid metabolism under pathological conditions. , For example, Fu et al. developed hSRS microscopy for in vivo metabolic fingerprinting of neutral lipids, achieving chemically specific discrimination between triacylglycerols (TAGs) and cholesteryl esters (CEs). By employing ratiometric spectral analysis and isotope-labeled fatty acids, the method enabled single-droplet compositional quantification and dynamic tracing of fatty acid (FA) storage and turnover. The authors revealed that ER stress and obesity promote enlarged unsaturated lipid droplets in hepatocytes. Notably, they showed distinct metabolic fates of fatty acids: unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) were preferentially incorporated into lipid storage, whereas saturated fatty acids (SFAs) accumulated in membrane-like structures, causing cytotoxicity. This mechanistic insight refuted the proposed synergistic incorporation of UFAs and SFAs into TAGs and highlighted lipid remodeling as a pathological driver. Overall, the study established hSRS as a powerful imaging platform for dissecting lipid metabolism with broad implications for metabolic disease research and early diagnosis.

The liver exhibits spatial heterogeneity arising from metabolic zonation along oxygen and nutrient gradients across the lobule, a feature that underlies zone-specific biomolecular alterations and is critical for understanding disease progression. , Jia et al. utilized a label-free multimodal nonlinear optical (NLO) platform, which integrated SHG imaging of collagen fibers, SRS imaging of TGs in LDs, and TPEF imaging of autofluorescent metabolites in intact tissues on the same platform. Their results revealed the selective accumulation of unsaturated triglycerides and collagen fibers within the central vein, particularly during fibrosis progression (Figure f). This redistribution is accompanied by decreased lipid unsaturation and membrane fluidity, attributed to upregulated lipogenesis, desaturation, and lipid peroxidation, which collectively induce ER stress and hepatocyte apoptosis. Together, these studies underscore the power of SRS imaging technologies in elucidating lipid metabolic remodeling during liver disease, offering new avenues for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

Neurological Diseases

Lipid dysregulation plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, where abnormal lipid accumulation is often associated with axonal degeneration, myelin disruption, and neuronal loss. To investigate these pathological lipid changes in situ, Tian et al. applied SRS imaging to visualize lipid distributions in both human and murine models of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Their results revealed pronounced neurodegenerative alterations and extensive oval-shaped lipid deposits within the motor nerve fibers of ALS patients. In mouse models, SRS imaging further demonstrated that peripheral neurodegeneration and lipid accumulation appear as early pathological hallmarks and intensify progressively with disease advancement (Figure g).

Furthermore, there is cumulative evidence that lipid metabolism plays a key role in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Visualizing lipid content in a nondestructive label-free manner can aid in elucidating the AD phenotypes toward a better understanding of the disease. Meng et al. combined multiple optical molecular-specific methods, such as SRS microscopy with multivariate data analysis, to investigate the biochemistry of the brain hippocampus in situ using a mouse model of tauopathy (rTg4510). They observed significant differences in the morphology and lipid content between the AD mouse model and wild-type (WT) samples. Immunohistochemical staining revealed some degree of microglial colocalization with elevated lipids in the brain. These results provide new evidence of tauopathy-related dysfunction in a preclinical study at a subcellular level. Building upon these insights, by employing SRS microscopy in combination with deuterium oxide (D2O) labeling, Li et al. conducted in situ labeling on brains from tauopathy mouse models, Drosophila, human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived neurons, and microglia. In all models, a marked increase in lipid droplet accumulation was observed. In tauopathy brains, neurons under stress or injury accumulated excessive unsaturated lipids, which were transferred to microglia, thereby inducing lipid droplet accumulation. This process triggered inflammatory activation and impaired phagocytic function in microglia, which, in turn, imposed additional stress on neurons and established a self-perpetuating pathological cycle. Through in situ metabolite imaging, the study further validated the pathological accumulation of lipid droplets in microglia within AD brains. It was first elucidated that this phenomenon originated from lipid transfer driven by neuronal metabolic dysregulation, thereby providing evidence that implicates lipid pathophysiology as a therapeutic target and opening new avenues. In conclusion, these findings highlight the potential of SRS microscopy as a sensitive and quantitative platform for monitoring neurodegenerative progression, thus providing deep insights into disease mechanisms and supporting the development of targeted therapies.

Steroid Metabolic Biomarkers

Cancer

The role of altered cholesterol metabolism in cancer has been increasingly recognized. Using a combination of SRS microscopy and confocal Raman spectroscopy, Yue et al. performed quantitative analysis of lipogenesis at the single-cell level in human prostate cancer tissues. They discovered aberrant CE accumulation in lipid droplets of high-grade and metastatic tumors, driven by PTEN loss and PI3K/AKT pathway activation (Figure a,b). Furthermore, they found that such accumulation arose from the significantly enhanced uptake of exogenous lipoproteins and required cholesterol esterification. Functionally, CE depletion suppressed cancer proliferation and invasion and inhibited tumor growth in mouse models with minimal toxicity, suggesting therapeutic potential by targeting cholesterol metabolism. The findings of this study establish SRS imaging as a robust tool for investigating cholesterol metabolism and offer pivotal evidence supporting cholesterol ester accumulation as a diagnostic marker in cancer and related diseases. Moreover, abnormal cholesteryl ester metabolism has also been linked to malignancy in other cancers, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). Applying the same techniques, Zhang et al. investigated CE distribution and metabolic dynamics in human ccRCC tissues (Figure c). These findings highlight the broader significance of cholesterol metabolism in cancer, as similar alterations have been observed in pancreatic cancer, melanoma, and MGMT-methylated glioblastoma (GBM). ,

Beyond SRS-based imaging studies, a variety of complementary approachesincluding large-scale proteomics and functional genomicshave also investigated cholesterol metabolic pathways in cancer. For instance, Jiang et al. employed integrative proteomic analyses to highlight the role of sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1/ACAT1) in promoting the occurrence and development of cancer. Similarly, Cheng et al. demonstrated that targeting cholesterol ester synthase SOAT1 can inhibit lipid synthesis mediated by SREBP-1, thereby inhibiting the growth of GBM. Taken together, these results underscore cholesterol and its esterification products as broadly applicable and tumor-specific metabolic biomarkers, reinforcing their significance in cancer diagnosis and therapy. ,

Liver Metabolic Diseases

In order to explore the mechanism of cholesterol metabolism alteration during the progression from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to fibrotic steatohepatitis (NASH), Xu et al. developed a dual-band hSRS imaging method, through which they characterized and quantitatively analyzed lipid species in liver tissue sections obtained from patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver (NAFL) and NASH. What was revealed is that the birefringent crystals, previously confirmed to be associated with the development of NASH, consisted predominantly of saturated cholesteryl esters. As a result, this study provides both a novel imaging strategy and mechanistic insights into lipid-driven pathology in NASH.

Infectious Diseases

Sterols, such as ergosterol and cholesterol, play pivotal roles in maintaining membrane fluidity, integrity, and intracellular signaling in both host cells and pathogens and are increasingly recognized as key metabolic markers in infectious diseases, particularly in antifungal resistance mechanisms. Aberrant sterol metabolism has been linked to microbial survival, pathogenicity, and development of drug resistance, making it a promising target for diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. Zhang et al. used hSRS imaging to study ergosterol and unsaturated triglyceride distribution in fluconazole-resistant Candida albicans. They found that resistant strains had significantly higher ergosterol levels, linked to de novo lipid synthesis (Figure d). This study not only underscores the diagnostic potential of sterol accumulation as a biomarker of antifungal resistance but also demonstrates the capacity of hSRS imaging to uncover metabolic reprogramming in pathogenic microbes, thereby offering new insights for the development of precision antifungal strategies.

Atherosclerosis

In atherosclerosis (AS), the accumulation and crystallization of cholesterol not only drive plaque formation and progression but also serve as key biological markers of disease severity. Cholesterol crystals, in particular, are closely associated with plaque instability and rupture, underscoring their pathophysiological significance. However, current clinical methods for detecting and assessing atherosclerotic plaques are limited to evaluating plaque morphology and thickness, offering little insight into the plaque structure and composition. SRS imaging enables noninvasive, real-time observation and analysis of cholesterol crystal formation within arterial walls, a critical capability for early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression. −

Suhalim et al. used hSRS microscopy combined with second-harmonic generation (SHG) microscopy to selectively detect cholesterol crystals in atherosclerotic plaques. Their study showed that cholesterol monohydrate crystals could be distinguished from lipids, proteins, and cholesteryl esters, with the additional SHG signal confirming the chiral, noncentrosymmetric nature of the cholesterol crystal lattice. Furthermore, Wang et al. advanced the field by developing an hSRS approach coupled with MCR analysis in the fingerprint region. By exploiting characteristic Raman bands such as the sterol CC vibration at 1669 cm–1, this method enabled the label-free quantitative mapping of free cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, and proteins within intact atherosclerotic tissues, thereby overcoming the spectral crowding and polarization dependence inherent to the C–H stretching region. (Figure e). Collectively, these advances not only provided powerful methodological innovations for cholesterol imaging but also reinforced cholesterol crystals as a critical biomarker in atherosclerosis, with implications for early detection and mechanistic insights into plaque development.

Other Diseases

Dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism and transport is implicated in multiple diseases. including atherosclerosis, Niemann-Pick type C (NP-C) disease, and various cancers. So far, our understanding of cholesterol transport and metabolism is limited, partly due to a lack of suitable tools for imaging cholesterol in a living system. Inspired by the success of fatty-acid-based Raman probes, researchers have developed cholesterol-specific vibrational tags to overcome this gap. Notably, deuterated cholesterol has enabled single-cell SRS imaging of the cholesterol distribution and dynamics. To further enhance chemical specificity and spatial resolution, Lee et al. engineered a bioorthogonal Raman probe, phenyl-diyne-cholesterol (PhDY-Chol), allowing for high-precision intracellular cholesterol tracking (Figure f). This methodological advancement overcomes prior limitations of spectral overlap and poor spatial resolution, providing a powerful approach for dissecting cholesterol metabolism in both physiological and pathological contexts. These innovations establish cholesterol-targeted Raman probes as valuable tools for investigating lipid dysregulation and hold great promise for the diagnosis and treatment of cholesterol-related diseases.

Owing to its high sensitivity, molecular specificity, and subcellular resolution, SRS microscopy has progressed from a method confined to fundamental lipid metabolism studies into a potential platform with broad biomedical applications. Beyond liver disorders, lipid dynamics has been employed to investigate cancer, infectious diseases, metabolic syndromes, and neurodegenerative conditions, where lipid remodeling constitutes a defining hallmark of disease progression. These applications not only underscore the distinct advantage of SRS in enabling label-free and quantitative biomolecular imaging but also highlight its potential to expedite the identification of lipid-related biomarkers and to facilitate early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring.

Discovery of Protein Biomarkers in Diseases

Proteins are fundamental to nearly all biological processes, serving as enzymes, structural components, signaling molecules, and regulators of homeostasis. , Their three-dimensional conformations and metabolic turnover are intricately linked to physiological function and pathological states, rendering them indispensable targets for both mechanistic research and biomarker discovery. − However, conventional imaging techniques such as immunofluorescence are limited by their reliance on antibody labeling, which precludes direct, in situ assessment of protein folding states, aggregation dynamics, and turnover kinetics in complex biological environments. In contrast, SRS microscopy offers a powerful approach to resolving protein structures based on intrinsic vibrational signatures, and when integrated with metabolic labeling strategies using isotopically modified amino acids, it enables subcellular mapping of protein synthesis, degradation, and redistribution in biological systems. Together, these complementary strategies have enabled real-time investigation of protein misfolding in neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic reprogramming in malignancies, thereby establishing a powerful platform for elucidating protein-related pathophysiology in diverse pathological contexts.

Neurological Diseases

Misfolded proteins are implicated in numerous neurodegenerative diseases, including Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, and Huntington’s diseases. However, conventional immunofluorescence-based imaging techniques are limited in their ability to resolve protein conformational states in situ. SRS microscopy overcomes these limitations by enabling label-free imaging based on intrinsic vibrational signals, thus offering a unique capability to visualize misfolded proteins directly within complex tissue environments. Building on this advantage, Ji et al. utilized multicolor narrowband SRS microscopy to distinguish normal proteins from misfolded Aβ plaques by detecting spectral shifts in the amide I band (Figure a), offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the disease. Similarly, Lochocki et al. combined autofluorescence, spontaneous Raman, and SRS imaging to analyze amyloid protein deposition, further validating the efficacy of these spectroscopic techniques in longitudinal tracking of neurodegeneration. Progressing from structural characterization to biomarker evaluation, Oh et al. used high spatial resolution SRS imaging to quantitatively measure proteins and lipids in cell cultures and tissue slices. They explored the utility of protein and lipid measurements as novel quantitative biomarkers in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease, observing that amyloid plaques in the APP-PS1 brain were surrounded by lipid aggregates (Figure b)a feature absent in wild-type brains. This highlights the heterogeneity and disease-related changes associated with Alzheimer’s pathology.

Beyond studying protein misfolding, SRS imaging has also advanced our understanding of neuronal information processing, which critically depends on the ability to measure the membrane potential. Lee et al. utilized hSRS imaging to visualize membrane potential in mouse brain slices by targeting the CH3 Fermi resonance peak of proteins, revealing depolarization across multiple neurons. This technique not only aids in assessing neuronal excitability but also provides insight into electrophysiological abnormalities commonly observed in neurodegenerative conditions such as epilepsy and ALS.

The structure and function of proteins are central to virtually all biological processes, and their perturbation is often directly implicated in the disease onset and progression. Beyond structural alterations, disturbances in protein turnover, including synthesis and degradation, play equally critical roles in the pathogenesis of disorders. To address these challenges, SRS microscopy, particularly when combined with metabolic labeling strategies, has emerged as a powerful tool for noninvasive investigation of protein dynamics in situ. For instance, Shen et al. used 13C-labeled phenylalanine to map proteome degradation in situ. This enabled the early detection of protein disruptions, which are often associated with pathologies such as neurodegeneration. Moreover, by employing two-color pulse tracking of aggregate formation of mutant Huntington proteins using deuterated amino acids (D-AAs), Wei et al. effectively demonstrated the utility of SRS microscopy for studying complex protein metabolism across extensive brain tissue areas. Polyglutamine (polyQ) diseases, a group of neurodegenerative disorders, are marked by toxic protein aggregates containing elongated polyQ chains. However, their cytotoxic mechanisms remain controversial due to a lack of noninvasive detection tools. Wei et al. addressed this challenge by integrating SRS microscopy with deuterium-labeled glutamine, achieving subcellular-resolution imaging and spectral analysis of polyQ aggregates (Figure c). Their findings revealed size-dependent variations in the protein composition within aggregates, offering new insights into their formation.

In summary, in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, SRS imaging enables the direct visualization of protein misfolding and aggregation without the need for external labels, offering new insights into amyloid plaque formation and distribution in brain tissues.

Cancer

Dysregulated protein synthesis and turnover constitute key hallmarks of malignant transformation and progression. Building upon this concept, SRS microscopy has emerged as a powerful modality for the in situ visualization of protein metabolism, thereby facilitating biomarker discovery and metabolic phenotyping. Wei et al. introduced deuterium-labeled amino acids into mammalian cells and achieved live-cell imaging of protein synthesis using SRS microscopy. Their results revealed subcellular regions with rapid protein turnover in HeLa and HEK293T cells, as well as dynamic protein production in neurite outgrowths of differentiating N2A cells. This approach laid the foundation for the sensitive, real-time monitoring of protein biosynthesis in physiological contexts. To extend this approach to in vivo systems, Shi et al. optimized the delivery of isotopic tracers through intra-arterial administration of D-AAs in murine models. This method enabled rapid and homogeneous probe distribution within highly vascularized tissues, permitting time-resolved imaging of protein synthesis dynamics in organs such as the pancreas and brain, particularly the cerebral cortex and hippocampus. Their study further revealed spatially distinct metabolic heterogeneity in liver and colon tumor tissues (Figure d), underscoring the capacity of SRS microscopy to resolve intratumoral variability in protein metabolism. Furthermore, Zhao et al. employed D-AAs in SRS imaging to monitor protein synthesis in various cancer cell lines. This metabolic labeling strategy enabled subtype-specific profiling of protein biosynthesis, thereby providing a molecular basis for cancer classification and precision-targeted therapeutic interventions.

Taken together, these probe-based innovations complement metabolic labeling approaches by equipping SRS microscopy with enhanced specificity, signal amplification, and functional imaging capacity. Such developments further consolidate SRS as a powerful tool for the comprehensive characterization of protein biomarkers across diverse disease contexts.

Discovery of Glucose Biomarkers in Diseases

Glucose plays a variety of important roles in cellular synthesis and catabolism, serving as the primary source of energy for most organisms. Importantly, glucose metabolism is tightly associated with the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cancer, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disorders. Increasing evidence has revealed substantial heterogeneity in glucose metabolism at the single-cell level, particularly during dynamic physiological and pathological transitions. , However, unlike lipids and proteins, glucose lacks strong Raman-active vibrational signatures, rendering it undetectable by label-free SRS microscopy. To overcome this limitation, specialized glucose probes have been developed, such as deuterated glucose and chemically modified analogues (e.g., 3-O-propargylglucose, 3-OPG). These probes have enabled direct visualization of glucose uptake and subsequent metabolic conversion in situ, thereby allowing for the interrogation of disease-specific glucose reprogramming.

Cancer

Glucose metabolism has long been recognized as a pivotal hallmark of tumor biology, where malignant cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to meet the demands of rapid proliferation. This reprogramming, widely known as the Warburg effect, results in increased glucose uptake and redirection of glucose flux toward the biosynthesis of lipids, nucleotides, and proteins. ,− The ability to accurately visualize these metabolic dynamics at the subcellular level is, therefore, essential for advancing the understanding of tumor heterogeneity and progression. Nevertheless, conventional imaging techniques provide limited spatial resolution, thereby constraining the capacity to monitor glucose-derived biosynthesis in biological systems.

In order to address these limitations, SRS microscopy has been integrated with isotope-labeled glucose probes to trace the anabolic conversion of glucose into macromolecules within live cells. Li et al. visualized lipid production in pancreatic cancer cells using SRS in conjunction with isotopically labeled glucose, through which marked differences in metabolic flux were revealed between malignant and normal cells (Figure a). Furthermore, Hou et al. employed SRS imaging in three-dimensional breast cancer spheroids to map glucose-driven lipid synthesis and depletion, through which redox status and lipid indices were identified as potential biomarkers distinguishing malignant from nonmalignant phenotypes. Long et al. further extended this platform by coadministering 3-OPG-13C and D7-glucose in mouse choroid plexus tissue, thereby enabling simultaneous mapping of glucose influx and its subsequent metabolic conversion.

While isotope-labeled probes have been widely employed to trace the anabolic fate of glucose and elucidate its downstream metabolic fluxes, a complementary research direction has emphasized the direct quantification of glucose uptake itself. Given that altered glucose import constitutes a hallmark of metabolic reprogramming in malignancies, the development of probes that reliably report cellular glucose uptake offers critical insights into the upstream determinants of metabolic heterogeneity. Hu et al. introduced 3-OPG, which retains biological transport fidelity and exhibits a distinct Raman shift at 2053 cm–1. This probe enabled precise monitoring of glucose uptake in both tumor and normal tissues, through which glucose uptake heterogeneity was resolved at high spatial resolution. Expanding upon these findings, Tian et al. utilized D7-glucose to evaluate cisplatin resistance in tumor cells by quantifying lipid synthesis and fatty acid uptake (Figure b), whereby metabolic indices indicative of chemoresistance at the single-cell level were identified.

Moreover, glycogen metabolism, which plays a central role in buffering cellular energy fluctuations, remains a critical, yet insufficiently characterized component of tumor bioenergetics. By metabolically incorporating deuterated glucose, Lee et al. revealed the subcellular enrichment of glycogen in live cancer cells and achieved specific glycogen mapping through distinct spectral identification. They found that melanoma subtypes with high glycogen levels were more resilient to glucose deficiency (Figure c), highlighting the potential of targeting glucose metabolism for cancer therapy. Similarly, Sitarz et al. identified that HPV transformation properties correlated with accelerated glycogen metabolism in tumor cells, underscoring the crucial role of glycogen metabolism in the development of cervical cancer. This method opens up the possibility for the noninvasive study of complex glycogen metabolism at subcellular resolution and may help to reveal new features of glycogen regulation in cancer systems.

Infectious Diseases

Glucose serves as the primary carbon source for many infectious bacteria, providing crucial insights into bacterial metabolic activities. To investigate these processes, SRS technology can detect and quantify C–D bond formation in bacteria following incubation with deuterated glucose. This method enables rapid and label-free assessment of bacterial metabolic states. Furthermore, it facilitates the determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values for a broad range of bacterial species, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus. Building on this application, Hong et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of SRS-based metabolic imaging for antibiotic susceptibility testing. By monitoring glucose uptake during a single bacterial cell cycle, they were able to rapidly predict antibiotic resistance profiles. Specifically, significant differences were observed in the SRS signal at 2178 cm–1, corresponding to C–D bond vibrations, between antibiotic-sensitive and antibiotic-resistant strains. For instance, strains sensitive to vancomycin exhibited a substantial reduction in deuterated glucose uptake compared to resistant strains (Figure d), underscoring the potential of this technique for fast and accurate antibiotic susceptibility evaluation.

Taken together, as a central regulator of cellular energetics and biosynthetic pathways, glucose metabolism undergoes profound reprogramming in diverse pathological states. The integration of SRS microscopy with isotopically labeled glucose offers a powerful means of capturing metabolic heterogeneity in situ, thereby advancing our mechanistic understanding and improving the precision of disease diagnosis and treatment evaluation.

Discovery of Nucleic Acid Biomarkers in Diseases

Nucleic acids play a central role in regulating gene expression, cell cycle progression, and tissue homeostasis, and their dysregulated metabolismincluding aberrant synthesis, degradation, and chromatin remodelingis closely associated with a wide spectrum of diseases. Since abnormal DNA and RNA dynamics are hallmarks of malignancies and other proliferative disorders, real-time monitoring of nucleic acid metabolism is essential to advance mechanistic understanding and support translational diagnostics. However, traditional fluorescence-based methods are limited by challenges including nonuniform staining and poor temporal resolution, thereby restricting their utility in live-cell investigations. By contrast, SRS microscopy provides a distinct advantage by enabling high-resolution, high-sensitivity visualization of nucleic acid dynamics, particularly when integrated with metabolic labeling strategies that enhance specificity.

In recent years, SRS microscopy has demonstrated the ability to accurately depict the three-dimensional distribution of chromosomes in live cells without exogenous labels. , For instance, Zhang et al. used SRS microscopy to map nucleic acid distributions in human embryonic kidney cells and breast cancer lines, offering new insights into cellular dynamics during division. Similarly, Samuel et al. combined SRS with multivariate curve resolution to map DNA and RNA in HeLa cells, enabling the identification of chromosomal condensation states during mitosisan application directly applicable to monitoring uncontrolled cell proliferation in tumors. To overcome the challenges of spectral overlap and low nucleic acid abundance, metabolic labeling approaches have been developed. Recent advances have demonstrated the value of this strategy. For example, Hu et al. achieved orthogonal chemical imaging of DNA and RNA in living rat brain tissue by integrating deuterium and alkyne labeling with SRS microscopy. Similarly, given that EdU is a widely used probe for cell proliferation, Yamakoshi et al. demonstrated real-time monitoring of DNA synthesis in live HeLa cells using alkyne-labeled probes and Raman microscopy, highlighting the potential of this technique for studying cell proliferation dynamics. Further advancements by Wei et al. involved the use of alkylated uracil analogue 5-ethyluracil (EU) incorporation to monitor nucleic acid dynamics during mitosis in living HeLa cells, providing insights into RNA turnover rates (Figure a), enabling real-time observation of mitosis.

In conclusion, dysregulated nucleic acid metabolism is a hallmark of malignancies and other proliferative disorders, underscoring the need for accurate monitoring in both basic and translational research. SRS microscopy addresses this challenge by enabling high-resolution, nondestructive visualization of nucleic acid architecture and dynamics in living systems. , Combining intrinsic vibrational contrast with metabolic labeling provides both structural fidelity and molecular specificity, thereby facilitating spatiotemporal tracking of DNA and RNA synthesis. These capabilities expand the utility of SRS in detecting aberrant proliferation, evaluating therapeutic responses, and identifying nucleic acid–based biomarkers across diverse diseases. , Nevertheless, the clinical translation of Raman-active nucleic acid probes remains limited, highlighting the need for systematic integration into diagnostic and therapeutic pipelines to enable early detection, real-time monitoring, and biomarker-driven interventions.

Discovery of Other Biomarkers in Diseases

While SRS microscopy was initially applied to classical biomolecular targets such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, recent advances have significantly broadened its scope to include disease biomarkers, ranging from neurotransmitters to crystalline deposits. This expansion not only enriches the diagnostic repertoire of SRS but also enhances our capacity to investigate disease pathophysiology at the molecular level.

Neurotransmitter Biomarkers in Neurodegenerative Disorders

Among these emerging applications, SRS has shown an increasing utility in functional neuroimaging. The noninvasive quantification of neurotransmitter levels, membrane potential, and synaptic biochemical dynamics has emerged as a powerful application in neuroscience. Fu et al. developed an SRS-based platform to directly image acetylcholine distribution at subcellular resolution within the neuromuscular junction of frog tissue, enabling the precise assessment of neurotransmitter localization under physiological conditions (Figure b). This demonstrates the potential of SRS for elucidating neural function and pathology through molecularly resolved vibrational imaging.

Crystalline Biomarkers in Metabolic Diseases

Structural biomarkers have likewise benefited from SRS advancements. In metabolic disorders such as gout and pseudogout, the deposition of pathological crystalsmonosodium urate (MSU) and calcium pyrophosphate (CPP), respectivelyserves as a direct biochemical hallmark. Leveraging the distinct vibrational signatures of these crystalline species, SRS microscopy enables their label-free and chemically specific visualization in situ. For instance, Zhang et al. integrated SRS with SHG microscopy to simultaneously detect MSU crystals and collagen fibers in inflamed tissues, facilitating the differentiation of gout from pseudogout. Additional studies have revealed that the Raman profiles of MSU and CPP crystals in synovial fluid are spectrally resolvable from noncrystalline backgrounds, thereby supporting time-resolved analyses of pathological deposition windows and guiding tailored therapeutic intervention (Figure c). These applications highlight the potential of SRS as a rapid and precise method for investigating metabolic pathologies.

Calcification Biomarkers in Cancer

In oncology, SRS has been increasingly applied to structural and metabolic biomarkers. One prominent example is microcalcification in breast cancer, which is closely associated with tumor progression and malignancy. These calcified deposits, composed of phosphate, oxalate, carbonate, and proteins, display diverse morphological characteristicsincluding size, shape, and spatial distributionthat serve as diagnostic indicators. Using hSRS microscopy, Yang et al. imaged fresh breast biopsy specimens and integrated the chemical features. By integrating chemical signatures (hydroxyapatite, carbonate, and protein) and morphological descriptors (geometric patterns and textural properties) of the calcified regions through a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm, a high-accuracy classification of benign and malignant breast lesions was thereby achieved (Figure d). This approach provides a promising strategy for the rapid, label-free, and highly specific diagnosis of breast tumors.

In conclusion, the expansion of SRS microscopy beyond traditional biomolecular classes to encompass crystalline, neurotransmitter, and functional metabolic biomarkers underscores its transformative potential in biomedical imaging. SRS microscopy offers a uniquely powerful platform for probing dynamic biochemical processes in vivo, supporting early diagnosis, therapeutic evaluation, and mechanistic discovery in a wide array of diseases. ,

D2O-Based Probes for Metabolic Imaging Across Cancer and Infectious Diseases

Complementing these efforts, deuterium oxide (D2O)-assisted SRS (D2O-SRS) has emerged as a minimally perturbative and functionally informative strategy for metabolic imaging. Unlike conventional Raman substrates, D2O participates in endogenous biosynthesis by incorporating deuterium into newly synthesized macromolecules, resulting in carbon–deuterium (C–D), nitrogen–deuterium (N–D), and oxygen–deuterium (O–D) bond formation. These bonds yield distinct vibrational peaks within the cell-silent region (2000–2300 cm–1), allowing for selective imaging of active metabolic processes with minimal interference. −

D2O-based SRS imaging has emerged as a powerful tool for dynamic metabolic phenotyping across diverse disease contexts. For cancer diagnosis, D2O-SRS enabled the visualization of spatial metabolic gradients within glioblastoma, revealing reprogrammed biosynthetic activity radiating from the tumor core to adjacent normal tissue, thus highlighting intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity. Compared to other deuterated tracers, D2O offers superior biocompatibility, stability, and coverage of biosynthetic pathways. Beyond cancer, D2O-SRS has shown clinical potential in infectious disease diagnostics by enabling rapid, culture-free antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). In both bacterial and fungal models, by detecting reductions in C–D signal intensity following drug exposure, metabolic inactivation could be quantified at the single-cell level within hours, correlating well with standard MIC values. ,

Collectively, these findings position D2O not merely as a tracer but also as a functional metabolic biomarker with broad disease relevance. When integrated with SRS microscopy, it enables real-time, label-free assessment of biosynthetic activity across disease contexts, from malignancies to microbial infections. Despite encouraging results in preclinical models, broader clinical translation of D2O-based imaging remains contingent upon standardized protocols, regulatory validation, and further probe optimization.

Concluding Remarks and Future Perspectives

Molecular biomarkers play an essential role in accurate disease diagnosis and personalized treatment. Label-free SRS microscopy, capable of high specificity, high-speed, and high-resolution chemical imaging, is a novel platform to investigate disease-associated metabolic processes. The integration of SRS microscopy and Raman probes further promotes sensitive and specific biomolecular dynamics imaging in living systems. Together, the SRS imaging system is promising and will become an indispensable tool for linking molecular metabolism with disease pathophysiology.

To fully exploit its diagnostic and mechanistic potential, SRS must overcome inherent trade-offs among imaging speed, spatial resolution, and detection sensitivity. Advances in laser engineering, detector optimization, and computational algorithms are progressively addressing these limitations, enabling high-spatiotemporal-fidelity imaging of dynamic biochemical events. , In parallel, probe innovationsincluding Carbow vibrational palette, Manhattan Raman Scattering (MARS) dyes, activatable enzyme-responsive probes, and ultrabright Raman-active nanoparticles (Rdots)have greatly expanded the chemical and functional space accessible to SRS. − These developments allow for multiplexed and highly specific interrogation of proteins, metabolites, and signaling pathways, thereby broadening the biomedical scope of SRS from structural imaging to functional molecular diagnostics.

Beyond single-modality applications, the integration of SRS with complementary imaging platforms enhances both molecular specificity and diagnostic precision. Fluorescence tagging facilitates the targeted localization of Raman signals within specific subcellular compartments, while expansion SRS microscopy may improve spatial resolution and permit detailed exploration of interactions between small molecules and fluorescently labeled proteins. , Furthermore, integrating SRS with mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) may yield a synergistic platform in which noninvasive vibrational contrast guides the targeted identification of specific molecular species within nontargeted metabolomics. ,,

In modern biology, omics-level analyses have become indispensable, as they no longer rely on predefined molecular targets, but instead adopt comprehensive measurement methods; they can identify the key molecular regulators that are related to the characteristics, states of cells, and their responses to external stimuli. As this paradigm continues to evolve, the integration of SRS imaging with omics frameworks is increasingly recognized as a transformative direction. , Although the spectral information obtained from SRS cannot replicate the molecular depth of genomic or proteomic platforms, it uniquely enables longitudinal, label-free, and nondestructive profiling of cellular and tissue metabolism. By combining high-dimensional vibrational data sets with unsupervised clustering algorithms and deep phenotypic characterization, SRS facilitates the reconstruction of molecular phenotypes and supports the identification of functionally relevant biomarkers. Within this emerging “Raman as omics” framework, SRS microscopy is anticipated to evolve into a system-level analytical tool capable of elucidating complex cellular processes. Owing to its compatibility with longitudinal live-sample imaging, the integration of spatiotemporal Raman data with other modalities may uncover dynamic biological phenomena that even conventional multiomics approaches fail to resolve, thereby advancing our understanding of disease progression.

Although SRS microscopy has already enabled the discovery of numerous metabolic biomarkers with promising potential for clinical application, these biomarkers have not yet been incorporated into routine clinical diagnosis or companion testing. To achieve clinical application, three challenges must be addressed. First, instrumentation: platforms must be miniaturized and cost-effective while ensuring reliable clinical performance; reproducibility across sites and devices depends on automated acquisition, standardized imaging protocols, and harmonized spectral calibration and quality control. Second, large-scale validation: candidate biomarkers should be confirmed in adequately powered, demographically diverse prospective multicenter cohorts. Third, clinical workflow integration: SRS outputs must adopt interoperable data formats supported by validated analytical software to enable seamless use in routine care.

In conclusion, through interdisciplinary collaboration and continued innovation, SRS microscopy is well-positioned to evolve from a powerful imaging modality to a comprehensive platform for molecular diagnosis. By bridging real-time molecular imaging with biology, SRS may contribute not only to improved clinical decision-making but also to a broader understanding of disease mechanisms, paving the way for personalized therapeutic strategies.
