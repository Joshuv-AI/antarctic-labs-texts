Beyond pathogen-specific preparedness: adaptable laboratory systems for emerging infections
Authors: María Paz Sánchez-Seco, Gustavo Palacios
Published: 2026

Global epidemiological changes are placing European countries at growing risk of exposure to pathogens capable of causing major public health concerns. These rare infections require rapid and accurate responses, although sustained preparedness is difficult and costly to maintain.

Laboratory tests are required for case confirmation, underscoring the need for timely and accurate detection and identification of pathogens.1

Over the past two decades, Europe has seen emerging viruses cause unexpected events of local transmission. The 2007 chikungunya outbreak in Italy marked the first autochthonous transmission of a so-called “exotic” arbovirus in continental Europe2 but, since 2010, local transmission of dengue and/or chikungunya are recurring events, and sporadic cases of Ebola infection and sustained mpox circulation has been reported.3 Even this year, cases linked to Andes hantavirus, a New World pathogen with the unusual capacity for person-to-person transmission,4 have raised concern beyond its traditional geographic range. These events illustrate a simple point: infectious microorganisms do not respect geographic boundaries.

This evolving context makes diagnostics one of the central challenges of preparedness, which must address how to be ready for the arrival and subsequent detection of an endless number of pathogens of public health importance (Box 1).

The problem is compounded by clinical presentation. Many of these infections begin as an undifferentiated febrile syndrome.

In 2016 the late suspicion of an imported case of Lassa fever in Germany resulted in secondary transmission to a healthcare worker illustrating the consequences of limited awareness for rare pathogens.5

Early diagnosis is pivotal so clinical awareness and laboratory testing are crucial and this implies preparedness. Assay availability requires validation, maintenance of the techniques, reagents and controls, preservation of technical expertise, and available trained staff when needed. This is expensive and difficult to sustain when the probability of use is low and it becomes impossible if pathogen-specific testing is required for every agent.

The experience with mpox in Spain in 2022 illustrates a more flexible approach. Spain had substantial experience developing and using broad, generic PCR assays for most imported viruses, including orthopoxviruses,6,7 that were developed with the vision that they would be the best way to not only cover for the known, but also for the related viral species of the genus targeted. These pan-orthopoxvirus PCR assays allowed early detection8 before specific commercial assays were widely available. This was not necessarily the fastest possible approach, but it allowed early recognition of cases in a context of uncertainty.

This example suggests that preparedness should not depend only on pathogen-specific tools. It should also rely on adaptable diagnostic strategies. Not every laboratory can, or should, maintain highly specialized methods for every possible pathogen. Instead, Europe needs a tiered diagnostic system.

In the initial phase of an alert, reference laboratories should be equipped with validated broad-range tools as first-line screening tools, guiding subsequent confirmatory testing. At a second stage, once the epidemiological situation requires broader capacity, methods will be transferred close to patients. Later, peripheral laboratories can incorporate more specific tools and reference laboratories would then retain key roles in confirmation, characterization, quality assurance, and complex case management. Generic molecular tools are central to this model. Broad-range PCR assays can detect genetically related viruses and provide a practical balance between preparedness and feasibility. They may require additional steps, such as sequencing, to confirm pathogen identity or, in some cases, nested amplification to reach appropriate levels of sensitivity.

Similarly, serological cross-reactivity within related pathogen groups can provide preliminary diagnostic clues when specific assays are unavailable.

High-throughput sequencing technologies further expand diagnostic capacity. Unbiased metagenomic approaches allow detection of viral agents without prior assumptions. In many respects, these technologies represent the modern equivalent of classical viral culture and electron microscopy: broad exploratory tools capable of identifying unexpected pathogens. Our early work on sequence-based pathogen discovery anticipated that these approaches could transform viral diagnostics.9 That prediction10 is becoming reality. Although still largely restricted to specialized settings, these methods are becoming more common in reference laboratory workflows.

Health systems face a difficult balance: if testing criteria are too strict, important cases may be missed; if they are too broad, laboratories may be overwhelmed. Media attention and public concern can further distort this balance, increasing demand for testing during the early phase of a public health alert, followed by progressive normalization as knowledge improves.

Ultimately, this diagnostic paradox raises a practical question: how can health systems prepare for events that are rare and uncertain, but potentially serious? The answer is not to prepare equally for everything. It is to build flexible systems that can adapt rapidly. This requires highly specialized reference laboratories, predefined networks capable of scaling diagnostic response, and close coordination with national and international surveillance systems.

In an increasingly connected world, the ability to detect the unexpected is a defining feature of resilient public health systems in Europe.
