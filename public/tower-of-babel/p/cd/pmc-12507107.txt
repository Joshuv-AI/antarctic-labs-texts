Understanding postmigration stress in forcibly displaced people in Austria: study protocol of a 14-day ambulatory assessment study
Authors: Rojan Amini-Nejad, Urs M Nater, Ricarda Mewes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction: Postmigration stress has been recognised as a significant factor negatively impacting the psychological well-being of forcibly displaced people (FDP), even independently of pre-migration trauma exposure. However, there is no study yet investigating its detrimental effects on psychological and somatic indicators of mental health in the daily life of FDP. Here, we present a study protocol to investigate the impact of postmigration stress in the daily lives of Arabic- and Farsi-speaking FDP living in Austria, using an ambulatory assessment (EMA) design.Methods: Sixty Arabic- and Farsi-speaking adult FDPs with a maximum of three years of stay in Austria will complete daily questionnaires on smartphones for 14 days. Participants will be prompted to report experienced postmigration stressors, perceived momentary stress levels, transdiagnostic symptoms of psychological distress, and positive and negative affect, at three fixed timepoints each day (i.e. 11 am, 3 pm, and 7 pm). In addition to subjective stress assessment, participants will provide saliva samples to measure somatic stress system functioning, indicated by salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase. Furthermore, participants will be asked to complete a self-initiated end-of-day questionnaire before going to bed. Our hypotheses will be tested using multilevel analysis and biochemical analyses of the human probes.Discussion: This study will advance understanding of how postmigration stress affects psychological and somatic aspects of mental health in FDP. Using a multimodal EMA approach, findings may guide the development of scalable, smartphone-based, just-in-time interventions for this highly mobile, underserved population.KEYWORDS: Ecological momentary assessment, refugee mental health, affect, salivary cortisol, salivary alpha-amylasePALABRAS CLAVE: Evaluación momentánea ecológica; salud mental de refugiados; afecto, cortisol salival; alfa amilasa

Introduction

Forced displacement is characterised by profound psychological and physical stressors, such as witnessing or experiencing violent attacks, extended separation from family, and acculturative stress associated with resettlement in a foreign country (Porter & Haslam, 2005). Consequently, FDP generally show heightened prevalence of psychological distress (Lane et al., 2025), particularly anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Henkelmann et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2022).

While previous research on mental health in FDP has often linked these to FDP’s experiences of pre- and peri-migration trauma (Mesa-Vieira et al., 2022), emerging research highlights that the postmigration context also plays a critical role in the development and maintenance of mental disorders (Hynie, 2018; James et al., 2019; Li et al., 2016). Studies across different FDP populations link postmigration stressors with mental distress (Dangmann et al., 2021; Hynie 2018), and mental disorders such as PTSD, anxiety, and depression (Andisha & Lueger-Schuster, 2024; Dangmann et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2019; Hynie 2018; Schick et al., 2018). A recent meta-analysis found associations between postmigration living difficulties and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and PTSD, and especially transdiagnostic psychological distress (Hou et al., 2019). The authors classified the daily life stressors into four domains: subjective (e.g. worry about the family and friends), interpersonal (e.g. perceived discrimination), material (e.g. employment difficulties), and mixed (e.g. external risk), and found that subjective stressors were related to symptoms of anxiety and PTSD, interpersonal stressors were related to all outcomes, material stressors only showed an association with PTSD, and mixed stressors were associated all examined outcomes (Hou et al., 2019). Other studies found that within the domain of interpersonal daily life stressors, perceived discrimination was particularly associated with mental distress (Hajak et al., 2021; Sifat et al., 2024). Evidence in the domain of material daily stressors is mixed: while Hou et al. (2019) reported links to PTSD, specific stressors such as employment limitations have been identified as robust risk factors for depression (Beiser, 2006; Nutsch & Bozorgmehr, 2020), and financial strain was not associated with distress, anxiety, or depression in a sample of recently resettled Afghan refugees (Sifat et al., 2024).

One proposed mechanism linking exposure to trauma and persistent adversity to psychopathology involves alterations in somatic stress-reactive systems (De Graaff et al., 2023; Hosseini-Kamkar et al., 2023; Zänkert et al., 2019), i.e. the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathico-adrenomedullary (SAM) axis (Ali & Nater, 2020). HPA axis activation, inter alia, results in the secretion of salivary cortisol (sCort), while SAM axis activation can be indexed by heightened concentration of salivary alpha-amylase (sAA), a starch-degrading enzyme (Ali & Nater, 2020).

Much of the previous research on neuroendocrine dysregulation in FDP has focused on altered functioning of the HPA axis in PTSD using hair cortisol concentration (HCC) as a measure of chronic stress exposure (Meyer & Novak, 2012; Schindler-Gmelch et al., 2023). Existing literature generally supports the hypothesis that chronic stress exposure leads to dysregulated functioning of the HPA axis; however, the direction of this dysregulation remains inconsistent, suggesting both hypo- and hypercorticolism (Buchmüller et al., 2020; Dajani et al., 2018; May et al., 2024; Mewes et al., 2017; Steudte-Schmiedgen et al., 2016), or even no association (Dajani et al., 2018; De Graaff et al., 2023).

Compared to HPA axis research, the studies on SAM axis activity among FDP remain scarce. One exception is a study by Thoma et al. (2011) that examined diurnal secretion patterns of sAA in Bosnian war refugees affected by PTSD, reporting an increased sAA awakening response.

To date, very little is known about how postmigration stressors relate to mental health and short-term neuroendocrine functioning in the daily life of FDP. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) addresses this gap by (repeatedly) capturing experiences in near real-time in a context-sensitive manner, thereby reducing retrospective bias and enhancing ecological validity (Müller et al., 2021; Shiffman et al., 2008; Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2013). Despite its methodological advantages in capturing dynamic fluctuations in symptoms, research employing EMA among FDP remains scarce (Eltanamly et al., 2023; Koch et al., 2020). Using EMA, Eltanamly et al. (2023) examined how postmigration stress impacts parenting practices among Syrian refugee parents in the Netherlands, while Koch et al. (2020) assessed daily affect, worry, and sleep among trauma-exposed Afghan refugees in Germany. However, none have integrated somatic markers in their stress assessment.

Addressing this gap, the proposed study will be the first to employ an EMA approach to examine the associations between perceived momentary (postmigration-specific) stress, mental distress, and short-term neuroendocrine activity in the daily life of FDP.

Proposed study

In Europe, Arabic- and Farsi-speaking FDPs have consistently constituted the largest share of asylum applicants within the last decade (Eurostat, 2024). Given their substantial representation in Europe and Austria, the proposed study will focus on Arabic- and Farsi-speaking FDPs in Austria within the early resettlement period (≤ three years), a phase typically marked with ongoing asylum procedures, legal uncertainty, and limited language proficiency (regarding the new country’s language). Building on Hou et al. (2019), we will classify postmigration living difficulties into subjective, interpersonal, and material domains to examine domain-specific links with transdiagnostic psychological distress and disorder-specific symptoms. Critically, the proposed study will distinguish stressor occurrence from perceived stressfulness at each assessment.

The proposed study will employ an EMA design to investigate momentary (postmigration) stress (both occurrence and perceived stressfulness) and stress-associated symptom fluctuations in the natural context of dynamic situational demands in everyday life over a span of 14 days. This multimodal methodological approach allows for high ecological validity and reduced retrospective biases (Mestdagh & Dejonckheere, 2021).

Our primary aim is to investigate the impact of postmigration stressors on momentary subjective and short-term neuroendocrine stress activity as well as on symptoms of psychological distress. Our hypotheses are as follows:

1. Momentary postmigration stress and transdiagnostic psychological distress

1a. Higher momentary perceived stress is associated with stronger momentary symptoms of transdiagnostic psychological distress, depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

1b. Higher average momentary stressfulness of postmigration stressors is associated with stronger momentary symptoms of transdiagnostic psychological distress, depression, anxiety, and PTSD.

1c. The momentary stressfulness of subjective stressors is positively associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety, with boredom being the strongest predictor.

1d. The momentary stressfulness of interpersonal stressors is positively associated with transdiagnostic psychological distress and symptoms of mental disorders, with perceived discrimination being the strongest predictor.

1e. ‘Financial strain’ and ‘communication problems’ are positively associated with symptoms of transdiagnostic psychological distress as well as symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD.

2. Time-lagged associations between postmigration stress and transdiagnostic psychological distress.

2a. Higher perceived momentary stress at time t-1 (earlier on the same day) predicts increased transdiagnostic psychological distress at time t (later on the same day).

2b. The effects of stressor occurrence and stressfulness at time t-1 (earlier on the same day) on transdiagnostic psychological distress at t are stronger when the stressors are interpersonal (e.g. discrimination, isolation) compared to material or subjective stressors.

2c. Higher average momentary stressfulness of postmigration stressors at time t-1 (earlier on the same day) predicts increased transdiagnostic psychological distress at time t (later on the same day).

2d. Higher average stressfulness of postmigration stressors on day d predicts increased transdiagnostic psychological distress on day d + 1 (next day).

3. The relationship between psychological stress and the activity of both somatic stress systems (i.e. hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis and sympathico-adrenomedullary axis).

3a. Higher perceived momentary stress is associated with elevated activity of the HPA and SAM axes, as measured by salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase levels, respectively.

3b. Higher average perceived momentary stress on day d predicts elevated salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase levels on day d + 1.

3c. Higher perceived stressfulness of postmigration stressors is associated with increased levels of salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase.

3d. Higher average perceived stressfulness of postmigration stressors on day d predicts increased levels of salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase on day d + 1.

Further exploratory hypotheses are documented in our preregistration of this study (Amini-Nejad, 2025).

Methods

Eligibility criteria require study participants to be between 18 and 65 years of age and proficient in either Farsi or Arabic; they are required to have flight experience and have been in Austria for less than 36 months. Exclusion criteria encompass a body mass index outside the range of 18-30 kg/m², presence of chronic somatic illness, recent use of psychopharmaceuticals (within the last 14 days), and diagnosis of bipolar disorder, borderline personality disorder, or schizophrenia. Further, the intake of medication known to affect stress-reactive somatic functioning will lead to study exclusion. In women, exclusion will apply in cases of hormonal contraceptive use, pregnancy, and breastfeeding. These exclusion criteria are defined to minimise potential confounding influences known to alter the activity of the somatic stress markers (i.e. sCort and sAA).

To determine the minimum sample size required, we conducted a simulation-based power analysis using the R package lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and simr (Green & MacLeod, 2015). Given three entries per day across 14 days and assuming an average completion rate of 75%, the analysis indicated that a sample of approximately 38 participants would be required to achieve 80% power to detect the target effect size (see preregistration; Amini-Nejad, 2025). Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, we will recruit 60 participants to ensure robust statistical power for the primary analyses.

Procedure

We will recruit participants via advertisement at social institutions and non-profit organisations located in Vienna, Austria, as well as on social media platforms dedicated to FDP. Interested individuals will be asked to complete a short online screening questionnaire to assess whether they meet the eligibility criteria and to provide their contact information. Eligible individuals will be emailed a link to an online booking page to set up an in-person appointment with a member of the study team, and participants can indicate whether they require an interpreter to be present for the appointment. At the appointment, participants will meet with a member of the study team to provide written informed consent and complete a set of questionnaires (see Figure 1).

Overview of Study Design and Assessment Schedule.

Participants will then be given thorough instructions for completing the 14-day assessment, a study manual, a study smartphone with the pre-installed app MovisensXS, version 1.7.1 (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany), as well as sampling tubes for the collection of saliva samples for the analysis of sCort and sAA. Starting the following day, participants will be prompted to complete repeated assessments per day: at (1) 11 am, (2) 3 pm, (3) 7 pm and will also be required to self-initiate an assessment before going to bed (4), using the study smartphone. During assessments (1) – (3), participants will respond to a brief set of items and will be asked to provide a saliva sample to assess the short-term neuroendocrine stress activity. Each of these fixed-time prompts may be postponed up to 60 minutes to ensure study participation will not disrupt daily routines. The self-initiated bedtime assessment (4) only requires participants to provide self-report data, without saliva sampling.

Pilot data showed that each assessment takes approximately 3 minutes to complete (+ 2 minutes for the collection of saliva samples at time points 1-3). After completing the EMA period, participants will be invited for a second meeting to return the study materials and for a short post-participation questionnaire on participants’ acceptance of the survey format and any difficulties they may have encountered. Full study participation will be compensated with 100,00 €. All study materials and procedures are available in German, Arabic, and Farsi.

Measures

The primary outcome measures of this proposed study are described below. The secondary outcome measures and further details are provided in the Supplementary material.

Baseline

The variables assessed at baseline assess sociodemographic information, prior stress experience, postmigration living difficulties, mental and physical health, as well as potential protective factors (e.g. resilience, social support, and habitual emotion regulation) via questionnaires. In the following, we will report the primary variables of interest that will be assessed in the daily lives of our study participants (i.e. EMA items). Whenever the Arabic and Farsi versions of questionnaires and EMA-items are unavailable, the measures will be translated in accordance with the gold standard practices for cross-cultural test adaptation by Beaton et al. (2000).

Daily assessment

During the EMA period, a comprehensive set of items will assess the impact of (postmigration) stress on multimodal indicators of the stress response, transdiagnostic symptoms of psychological distress, and subjective measures of different emotional states.

Momentary subjective stress

Momentary subjective stress will be assessed using the single item ‘How stressed do you feel momentarily?’, to be answered on a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 = ‘not at all’ to 100 = ‘very’, at all three fixed time-points (1-3). Higher values indicate higher momentary subjective stress.

Postmigration stressors and their stressfulness

Following each assessment of momentary subjective stress, participants will be asked to indicate which of the eleven presented postmigration stressors they experienced since the last prompt, e.g. worries about the family, boredom, isolation, or discrimination. Participants may also select ‘none of the above’. For each selected stressor, participants will rate the perceived intensity on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘not at all’ to 5 = ‘very’. For the list of stressors, we will use items from the Post Migration Living Difficulties (PMLD) Checklist (Silove et al., 1998; Steel et al., 1999) and the Daily Hassles (Holm & Holroyd, 1992). The items will be adapted to the daily assessment format as well as to the Austrian context.

At the self-initiated bedtime assessment, participants will be asked ‘Which of the following stress factors have you experienced today?’ to be answered by choosing from a list of six postmigration stressors assumed to show less fluctuation throughout the day, e.g. ‘unsuitable or unpleasant living situation’. Participants may also indicate ‘none of the above’.

For an average stressfulness rating of postmigration stressors, a mean score will be calculated by calculating the sum of stress ratings of all reported postmigration stressors at each measurement time point and then dividing them by the number of reported postmigration stressors at that measurement time point.

Activity of the HPA axis and SAM axis

Complementary to each assessment of subjective stress (i.e. assessment points 1-3), saliva samples will be collected. Participants will be instructed to store their saliva samples in a refrigerator or freezer at home throughout the study period. Samples will be handed over to the study team at the post-assessment appointment, after which they will be stored at −30°C.

Salivary cortisol concentrations (nmol/L) will be analysed using a commercial luminescence immunoassay (IBL-Tecan, Hamburg, Germany). Salivary alpha-amylase activity (U/mL) will be determined by first diluting the samples 1:400 with a 0.9% saline solution, followed by a kinetic colorimetric assay using reagents from DiaSys Diagnostic Systems GmbH (Holzheim, Germany) (Skoluda et al., 2020).

Previous research has demonstrated that somatic stress markers are affected by various biobehavioral factors. Therefore, at each time saliva sampling point, participants will report on the following: intake of medication since last data entry, food consumption within the last hour, physical activity, drink consumption, and sleep within the past two hours. Each variable will be coded dichotomously (0 = no, 1 = yes).

To summarise within-day sCort and sAA, we will compute two trapezoid-based indices per biomarker and per day, following Pruessner et al. (2003): (a) the area under the curve with respect to ground (AUCG), indexing total hormonal output across the day, and (b) the area under the curve with respect to increase (AUCI), indexing time-dependent change relative to the first sample (i.e. 11:00 am).

Transdiagnostic psychological distress

To assess transdiagnostic symptoms of psychological distress, items of the Refugee Health Screener 13 (RHS-13; Hollifield et al., 2013), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001), Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006), and the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013) will be adapted for EMA. The items will be presented at all three fixed-time assessments and rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘not at all’ to 5 = ‘extremely’.

Disorder-specific symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD

Symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD will be assessed via selected items from the Refugee Health Screener-13 (RHS-13; Hollifield et al., 2013), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001), and the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013) will be adapted for ecological momentary assessment (EMA). All items will be rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘not at all’ to 5 = ‘extremely’. These disorder-specific symptom items will be presented daily at each of the three fixed EMA time points.

Emotional states

Subjective reports of emotional states will be documented at all four assessment points. Across the fixed-time assessment points (1)-(3), participants will respond to a set of items capturing both positive (e.g. happy, determined) and negative (e.g. afraid, guilty) affect. The item set will be mainly derived from the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988). Additionally, specific items will be drawn from the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) to capture emotional states related to trauma and depression.

At the end of each day, participants will be presented with an extended list of emotional adjectives including additional PANAS items to provide a more comprehensive assessment of emotional experience. Based on Koch et al. (2020), we will incorporate six additional emotional adjectives that the researchers identified as relevant and applicable within the Afghan language. All emotional state items will be rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘not at all’ to 5 = ‘extremely’.

Data analysis

Analysis will be carried out using the R software (R Core Team, 2021). Given the nested structure of the data, multi-level models will be estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Degrees of freedom will be calculated by applying the Satterthwaite approximation, which yields type 1 error rates comparable to the Kenward-Roger approximation when applied to REML models (Baayen et al., 2008). A three-level model will be specified, with momentary assessments (level 1) nested within days (level 2) nested within participants (level 3). Following Enders and Tofighi (2007), lower-level predictors will be person-mean centred, while predictors on level 3 (between-subjects) will be grand-mean centred.

Guided by Hou et al. (2019), we will differentiate the postmigration stressors by grouping them into conceptually meaningful categories, and a mean occurrence score will be calculated for each category (see Supplementary Material).

For hypotheses 1A to 1E (associations between perceived stress and psychological distress), the analysis will be controlled for relevant covariates such as age, gender, time since arrival in Austria, legal status, and prior trauma exposure.

For hypotheses 2A to 2C (time-lagged associations between subjective stress and transdiagnostic psychological distress), lagged predictors will be computed to model time-lagged associations between stress and transdiagnostic psychological distress, while controlling for the autoregressive effect of transdiagnostic psychological distress at the previous measurement (t-1).

For hypothesis 2D (time-lagged effect of average stressfulness on transdiagnostic psychological distress), average stressfulness of postmigration stressors and average transdiagnostic psychological distress on day d will be modelled as a level 2-predictor and outcome. Time-lagged associations will be estimated while controlling for the autoregressive effect of the average transdiagnostic psychological distress from the previous day (d-1).

For hypotheses 3A-3D (associations between subjective stress and short-term endocrine activity), several covariates will be included. At Level 3, these include sex, age, BMI, and regular medication intake of HPA/SAM-affecting medication. At Level 1, covariates include food intake, drinks, sleep, smoking, physical activity, and use of medication.

For hypothesis 3C, a time-lagged predictor (average perceived momentary stress on day d) will be specified to model time-lagged associations between psychological stress and short-term endocrine and autonomic stress responses. The outcome variables (average salivary concentration of sCort and sAA, respectively, on day d) will be modelled at level 2. Multilevel models will be estimated while controlling for the autoregressive effect of the respective biomarker (sCort and sAA) from the previous day (d-1).

For same-moment hypotheses (3A and 3C), primary analyses will use the raw biomarker levels at each prompt as Level 1 outcomes with concurrent stress predictors and per-prompt covariates (see above). For day-level lagged hypotheses (3B and 3D), daily AUCG (total output) and AUCI (change relative to first sample) will serve as Level 2 outcomes, predicted by day-level stress (mean perceived stressfulness and domain-specific PMLDs) from day d to day d + 1, controlling for the autoregressive term (prior-day AUC) and biomarker covariates. We will compute AUCs only for days with ≥2 valid biomarker samples; days with <2 valid samples will be set missing for AUC analyses. Unequal time intervals are handled directly by the variable-interval formulas.

Anticipated results/discussion

The proposed study aims to investigate the impact of (postmigration) stress on the multimodal stress response, transdiagnostic symptoms of psychological distress, and symptoms related to anxiety, depression, and PTSD in Arabic – and Farsi speaking FDP living in Austria. As indicated by our hypotheses, we expect that experiences of postmigration stress will lead to reports of more negative mental health outcomes and heightened activity of somatic stress systems. We anticipate observing both direct and time-lagged associations between stress exposure and indicators of the multimodal stress response, i.e. emotional, cognitive, and somatic components.

This study builds upon previous EMA research conducted with forcibly displaced study populations (Koch et al., 2020; Müller et al., 2021) by intensively and repeatedly assessing postmigration stressors, affective states, and symptoms of psychological distress throughout the day over 14 days. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to examine the associations between (postmigration) stress and momentary affective and psychological states in the everyday lives of Arabic- and Farsi-speaking FPD. A key strength of this study lies in its comprehensive examination of stress states, by complementing self-reports of subjective stress with objective markers of the activity of somatic stress systems. This multimodal approach will allow for an in-depth understanding of stress levels and variations in the daily life of potentially chronically stressed people affected by forced displacement.

However, we must be wary of biases that could influence the results of our study. The sample will be one of convenience and thus may provide limited representativeness. Moreover, selection bias may occur as our eligibility criteria exclude various health conditions and the use of medication known to affect endocrine functioning. Additionally, women using hormonal contraception or who are breastfeeding or pregnant will be excluded from study participation. While these eligibility criteria are necessary to ensure precision in measuring the activity of the stress-reactive somatic systems, they may reduce the generalizability of the findings.

Another potential hurdle lies within the required language proficiency of our study participants. To address this, all study material will be available in Arabic and Farsi. However, low literacy rates, particularly in female FDPs (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2017), may still hinder participation.

Despite possible limitations, the study will greatly add to our understanding of the impact of postmigration stress on the development and maintenance of mental disorders. By examining both psychological and somatic components of the stress experience, the findings will deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying mental health challenges in FDP. This understanding will allow for the development of informed transdiagnostic interventions tailored to the needs of this population. It can guide the development of smartphone-based ecological momentary interventions (EMIs), which enable just-in-time interventions in real-world settings, regardless of location. Such EMIs offer a promising, scalable approach to addressing mental health needs in this highly mobile and underserved population.
