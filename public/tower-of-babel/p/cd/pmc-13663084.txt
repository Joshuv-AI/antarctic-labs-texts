Characterizing the practice facilitation approach: what facilitators do and how they do it to support quality improvement
Authors: Elizabeth McNeill, Dina Zein, Ji Chang, Anisha Pal, Alden Yuanhong Lai, Ernesto Fana, Jennifer M. White, Sachel Somwaru-Contreras, Mishaal Muqaddam, Donna Shelley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPractice facilitation is an effective multicomponent strategy that focuses on building primary care practice capacity for continuous quality improvement. Despite its growing use, few frameworks empirically specify how practice facilitation strategies are operationalized in resource-constrained settings such as small, independent primary care practices (SIPs). This study aimed to further expand our understanding of core practice facilitation processes and strategies used to facilitate the adoption of evidence-based interventions in SIPs.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative analysis of practice facilitator (PF) field notes from a stepped-wedge randomized controlled trial evaluating the impact of practice facilitation on adoption of team-based care (TBC) for hypertension management in 74 SIPs across New York City. Facilitators conducted 16 structured site visits per practice over a 12-month intervention period and documented implementation activities after each visit using a standardized digital platform. We analyzed 528 visit transcripts using a hybrid deductive–inductive approach. Deductive coding focused on established practice facilitation strategy domains; inductive coding identified novel processes and strategies that emerged from the data. Coding was conducted iteratively with regular team calibration and member checking with the PF team.ResultsWe identified an iterative process in which PFs observe/listen, diagnose, engage, and problem solve throughout the implementation period – a dynamic approach not described in previous frameworks. This process enhanced PFs’ understanding of each practice’s context and guided their selection and application of a core set of practice facilitation strategies. These included previously documented strategies such as training, coaching, educating, and facilitating, as well as a novel strategy we identified: modeling, in which PFs demonstrated specific tasks or workflows in real-time alongside staff, building confidence and reinforcing learning.ConclusionsThis study refines and extends current facilitation frameworks to further define core processes and strategies that drive practice transformation in SIPs. The framework provides practical guidance for implementation efforts targeting small practice settings.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov; NCT05413252; 2022-06-09.

Background

External practice facilitation is a multicomponent strategy that is delivered by trained individuals and designed to build organizational capacity for continuous quality improvement (CQI) [1–4]. Practice facilitators (PFs) use a range of strategies, tailored to practice context, including team building, training, and coaching, to support knowledge acquisition, sustain practice improvements and improved clinical outcomes [1, 5–8]. A growing number of studies provide evidence that practice facilitation (PF) is associated with adoption of evidence-based interventions and improvements in the overall quality of care [9, 10]. More in-depth analyses of large trials also describe the importance of tailoring strategies to practice context to enhance motivation and reduce resistance to change [6–8, 11, 12]. However, there are remaining gaps in our understanding of the facilitation process, how context influences strategy selection and mechanisms that drive facilitation effectiveness [1, 3, 4, 6–9, 12–17]. This is, in part, related to inconsistencies in how the practice facilitator role is conceptualized and defined [1, 3, 4, 6–9, 15].

Practice facilitation is a particularly salient strategy for quality improvement in small, independent primary care practices (SIPs), settings that often lack infrastructure for quality improvement, such as dedicated staff or updated clinical information systems [18–20]. There is a strong rationale for studying effective approaches for supporting small practices (i.e., fewer than five providers), as they account for over half of all primary care office visits and remain a critical component of the U.S. healthcare system [2–4]. The Healthy Hearts Teams (HHT) study examined the impact of practice facilitation on adoption of team-based care (TBC) to improve HTN management and blood pressure outcomes in SIPS located in New York City.

The purpose of this paper was to expand our understanding of the influence of practice context on the facilitation process and the strategies employed in SIPs to increase practice capacity to improve hypertension (HTN) management and clinical outcomes. We applied Baker’s framework, developed through the authors’ experience and literature synthesis, to guide the analysis of practice facilitators’ detailed notes documenting each practice visit [21]. The framework classifies practice facilitation activities by the degree to which the practice relies on the PF to implement practice changes as opposed to having the capacity to lead QI efforts with less PF support [21]. It was designed to provide PFs a guide for selecting and applying strategies that are aligned with practice context and capacity [12, 21]. Our study extends this framework by providing empirical evidence to validate, refine, and enhance the framework’s applicability in real-world settings and provides additional insights into the processes facilitator’s use to adapt strategies and processes to practice context.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative analysis of data collected through structured documentation of PF site visits during the HHTeams study—a stepped-wedge randomized controlled trial evaluating the impact of practice facilitation on implementation of TBC to improve HTN management [22]. We obtained IRB approval from New York University. We followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research when reporting this research [23].

Study setting

The study enrolled 81 small primary care practices that are members of the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene’s (DOHMH) NYC Regional Electronic Adoption Center for Health (REACH) Network which is managed by the DOHMH Bureau of Equitable Health Systems (BEHS). Each practice was assigned one PF who conducted sixteen visits over a 12-month intervention period. BEHS’ core function is to support practice improvement through their practice facilitation program. BEHS equips PFs with knowledge and skills needed to build practice capacity for continuous quality improvement [24]. For this project, four BEHS facilitators received additional training on TBC, how to apply TBC principles to improve implementation of evidence-based HTN management interventions, and how to use the PF toolkit. The toolkit, developed in collaboration with BEHS, outlined key activities and resources for each visit. These included orienting the practices to the goals of the project, redesigning and establishing workflows for new activities, conducting Root Cause Analyses (RCAs) to identify barriers to adopting the HTN management intervention component (e.g., self-blood pressure management), applying principles of TBC, and using practice data to implement “Plan-Do-Study-Act” (PDSA) improvement cycles [25–27]. The practice facilitation protocol included two in-person visits in the first three months, one in-person visit during months four through eleven, and two in-person visits in month twelve. PFs were trained and supervised by the BEHS team throughout the intervention.

The first two visits focused on “Getting Started,” during which the PF established the purpose of the project and met with the practice team. At each of the following visits, the PF focused on establishing TBC workflows for one of five HTN strategies—evidence-based guidelines to improve HTN outcomes [25]. These strategies include: (1) Population Health Management; (2) Panel Management; (3) Measuring Blood Pressure Accurately; (4) Improving Medication Adherence; and (5) Self-Management Support [25]. Practice facilitators remained flexible and adapted their approach to the practices’ specific context (e.g., staffing turnover, different staffing models) and progress. For example, in some practices, PFs needed more time to train staff to adopt new roles and responsibilities related to a specific strategy. The PF closed the intervention on the sixteenth visit.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection

Following each site visit, PFs used Salesforce (a customer relationship management platform) to document both structured and narrative data (e.g., checklists and free-text observations) [28]. The NYC BEHS team provided standardized training to ensure consistent data entry. Documentation included: tasks completed, in-session training, findings from RCAs, details of Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, and noted implementation barriers/facilitators or practice staff task assignments for follow-up. To support longitudinal analysis, these visit notes were organized into de-identified transcripts by practice and visit sequence.

Analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis of 528 visit transcripts from 33 practices that uses both deductive and inductive approaches. First, we developed and applied a codebook based on five predefined practice facilitation strategies from Baker et al. (2017)’s framework [21, 29]. This deductive coding guided the identification of facilitator activities that were aligned with established categories. When data and coded categories did not fit within the five predefined strategies, we included them for inductive analysis to develop new codes grounded in the data, expanding the codebook in the process. With both deductive and inductive approaches, we were able to operationalize Baker et al. (2017)’s framework as well as identify emerging patterns. Three coders (EM, DZ, AP) independently used the codebook that was based on Baker et al. (2017)’s framework to code a transcript that was purposively selected [21]. During this process, in addition to categorizing activities that were aligned with the deduced categories, we also identified frequent activities that were not captured by the existing strategy domains. We then inductively analyzed these excerpts to generate new codes. Through an iterative process that included the research team (JC, DS, EM, DZ, AP) we refined definitions for these emergent codes and integrated them into an expanded codebook. We then jointly coded another transcript to ensure consistency across coders. We resolved discrepancies in our codes via discussion and achieving consensus. Once agreement was reached, we finalized a codebook that included both deductive codes from the Baker framework and inductive codes derived from the data [21]. We applied the finalized codebook to the full dataset using Dedoose software [30]. Three coders (EM, DZ, AP) independently coded transcripts from Wave 1 and Wave 2. We reached saturation after coding visits for 33 sites. There were no significant differences, across 8 practice characteristics (e.g., number of physicians, clinical and allied staff, ownership) between the practices included in the analysis, and those we did not analyze. We held weekly meetings to ensure consistency, resolve discrepancies, and discuss analytic insights. Team members developed analytic memos for each new domain and shared preliminary findings with the NYC DOHMH BEHS team as a form of member-checking at various points of the study (November 2023, March 2024, June 2024) and incorporated their feedback into the final framework.

Results

PFs applied the five strategies identified by Baker et al. [21]. However, our analysis identified an additional strategy, which we defined as modeling, and a distinct, but complementary process not reported in the original framework (Fig. 1). The latter comprised four core actions: observing/listening, diagnosing, engaging, and problem-solving, which facilitators used to assess context, identify barriers, and tailor the change process to the practice context as they concurrently applied the core practice facilitation strategies (Fig. 1). Together, the process and strategies that emerged from the analysis capture both how PFs supported adoption of evidence-based HTN management and team-based care, and the strategies and activities they implemented to achieve practice goals across range of practice settings. Model

A novel strategy emerged during the coding process that we termed “modeling.” Member-checking sessions with the practice facilitators reinforced the distinction between modeling and other Baker defined strategies, including training. PFs consistently described modeling as a situation in which they performed a task themselves — sometimes correctly, sometimes deliberately incorrectly — with the explicit goal of making the process visible for staff observation before or during staff practice [21].

PFs modeled technical tasks, such as generating quality reports to analyze performance and navigating EHR changes: “[I] showed MA how to utilize the PopUp Function to capture multiple BP measurements.” (PF 4, Practice 32). They also modeled clinical practices to improve, for example, the accuracy of blood pressure monitoring: “[The medical assistant] took my blood pressure, and I purposely crossed my legs, talked, and let my arm hang. the MA [medical assistant] did not correct the actions. I reviewed all the mistakes with [the medical assistant] and [then] trained her to do it properly.” (PF 1, Practice 69)

Modeling contrasted with the PF doing a task, as the goal was to transfer expertise to the staff through example rather than completing a task for the practice. In addition, unlike training, as defined by Baker, modeling used the real-time execution of a task as the primary vehicle for learning while staff observe or perform the action and receive immediate feedback. PFs further described modeling as a conceptual bridge between the facilitator executing a task (doing) and facilitating it (building capacity for a new clinical workflow or process).

The practice facilitator process

Our analysis revealed a distinct, macro-level iterative process centered on understanding the broader contextual factors that impact implementation. This overarching process comprised four core domains: observing/listening, diagnosing, problem-solving, and engaging in change management. While Baker categorizes the specific strategies practice facilitators (PFs) use (e.g., coaching, training), and standard quality improvement methods such as PDSA cycles offer tools to support change management, PFs described a process that expands upon this literature by capturing a separate, and additional “outer-setting” mechanism. Rather than focusing on a singular improvement goal, PFs utilized this macro-process as a diagnostic tool. By continuously navigating these four domains during clinical encounters, PFs dynamically assessed practice progress and determined which specific strategies or tools were contextually appropriate for implementing each of the five hypertension (HTN) strategies.

Discussion

This study used primary data to deepen our understanding of how PFs build practice capacity in SIPs. Our analysis suggests two primary expansions to the Baker framework. First, we defined a distinct, macro-level iterative process centered on understanding the broader contextual factors impacting implementation. This emergent model contributes to the literature by empirically mapping the diagnostic and cognitive process PFs use to design context-specific, adaptive change management approaches [6–10, 13]. Previously, Dogherty and colleagues identified—through a content analysis of nursing research—four stages in facilitation-assisted implementation: planning for change, leading and managing change, monitoring progress and ongoing implementation, and evaluating change [3]. While these stages describe the general facilitation process, they primarily capture the interaction between the practice and the facilitator regarding a specific organizational change.

In contrast, the model that emerged from our data provides insight into a higher-level diagnostic process and illustrates how facilitators evaluate baseline gaps in capacity and resources across multiple simultaneous change processes. PFs then use this analysis to determine which strategies (e.g. coaching, training, doing) are most appropriate to facilitate micro-organizational changes to improve HTN management. While the HHTeams toolkit guided the application of specific QI tools (e.g., root cause analysis, PDSAs) across clinic visits, this higher-level process supported facilitators in determining which strategies (i.e., coaching, doing) were most appropriate to build capacity for a given goal. This iterative process characterizes the complex cognitive and relational work applied by PFs to assess practice readiness, align resources, and effectively tailor their capacity-building strategies [6–10].

Baker’s original framework defines strategies or activities that facilitators adopt to support practice improvement. These are organized by the degree to which they build practice self-sufficiency over time - from doing tasks to coaching. In this study, facilitators described an additional strategy, modeling, which was situated between doing and facilitating, reflecting the level of practice or individual staff readiness to complete a specific task or workflow. This strategy, as described, was most similar to training. However, as we observed, modeling operates through a different mechanism. Training, as defined by Baker, “offers content and/or process via an educational model in structured way which allows practice teams to apply knowledge themselves.” When training, the mechanism is the learner’s structured practice with facilitator guidance — the learner is the one performing the task. In contrast, when modeling a task, the facilitator performs the task as the primary vehicle for learning, while the staff observe and receive immediate corrective feedback. This distinction maps onto what Bandura (1977) terms observational learning — learning through watching a competent model perform, not through one’s own guided practice [31]. The pedagogical sequencing is also different: modeling typically precedes independent practice, serving as a bridge between a PF doing a task for the practice (doing) and the practice doing it themselves with PF support (training and facilitating).

We are aware of only one study that applied the Baker framework to analyze associations between practice facilitation and implementation outcomes. Walunas et al. found that practices that engaged in more coaching-based strategies with PFs were more likely to implement more QI interventions [12]. A similar association was not found for the other Baker strategies. The methods applied in this study provide important guidance for conducting rigorous analysis of the association between facilitation strategies and practice improvement outcomes.

The study has several limitations. The analysis was restricted to urban SIPs. However, the strategies and practice facilitation process may have broader relevance. In a previous study conducted in both SIPS and federally qualified health centers (FQHCs), facilitators described applying remarkably similar change processes and practice improvement strategies across these settings. Variations in practice context informed differences in how these strategies were applied [8]. For example, SIPS, with fewer resources and staff, often required facilitators to start with “doing” the tasks. In contrast, in the FQHCs’ which a had more developed QI infrastructure PFs focused more on training and coaching. Additionally, the qualitative data was captured in the field notes written by different PFs. As a result, the data may reflect variability based on PF writing style. Further, our analysis was performed on a small sample of PFs (n = 4), and further research is needed with larger sample sizes. As with any qualitative study, investigator bias may have influenced data interpretation. However, we took steps to minimize this bias by incorporating a team to perform data analysis and validation via member-checking with our study PFs.

Conclusions

Despite the large literature on practice facilitation we lack empirical data on the processes and mechanism associated with this multi component implementation strategy. Practice facilitation is widely use in large health systems, such as the Veterans Administration to support quality improvement goals [32]. These and other study platforms offer opportunities to continue to further develop standard frameworks, definitions, and data systems to facilitate cross study comparisons and greater understanding of the relationship between this complex meta strategy and quality improvement. Additionally, research is needed to expand the theoretical underpinnings of the facilitation process, and similarly, to enhance the rigor of context assessments to guide and further optimize the impact practice facilitation across a range of practice settings.
