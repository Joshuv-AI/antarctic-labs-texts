Subdivision of M1 category and prognostic stage for de novo metastatic breast cancer to enhance prognostic prediction and guide the selection of locoregional therapy
Authors: Lei Ji, Ge Song, Min Xiao, Xi Chen, Qing Li, Jiayu Wang, Ying Fan, Yang Luo, Qiao Li, Shanshan Chen, Fei Ma, Binghe Xu, Pin Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough de novo metastatic breast cancer (dnMBC) is acknowledged as a heterogeneous disease, the current staging systems do not distinguish between patients within the M1 or stage IV category. This study aimed to refine the M1 category and prognostic staging for dnMBC to enhance prognosis prediction and guide the choice of locoregional treatment.MethodsWe selected patients with dnMBC from the SEER database (2010–2019), grouping them into training (N = 8048) and internal validation (N = 3450) cohorts randomly at a 7:3 ratio. An independent external validation cohort (N = 660) was enrolled from dnMBC patients (2010–2023) treated in three hospitals. Nomogram‐based risk stratification was employed to refine the M1 category and prognostic stage, incorporating T/N stage, histologic grade, subtypes, and the location and number of metastatic sites. Both internal and external validation sets were used for validation analyses.ResultsBrain, liver, or lung involvement and multiple metastases were independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS). The nomogram‐based stratification effectively divided M1 stage into three groups: M1a (bone‐only involvement), M1b (liver or lung involvement only, with or without bone metastases), and M1c (brain metastasis or involvement of both liver and lung, regardless of other metastatic sites). Only subtype and M1 stage were included to define the final prognostic stage. Significant differences in OS were observed across M1 and prognostic subgroups. Patients with the M1c stage benefited less from primary tumor surgery in comparison with M1a stage.ConclusionSubdivision of the M1 and prognostic stage could serve as a supplement to the current staging guidelines for dnMBC and guide locoregional treatment.Keywords: de novo metastatic breast cancer, prognosis, staging, surgery

INTRODUCTION

Around 6% of breast cancer patients have distant metastases either at initial diagnosis or within 3 months thereafter, a condition known as de novo metastatic breast cancer (dnMBC). 1 , 2 DnMBC exhibits a high degree of uniqueness and heterogeneity in both clinicopathological and biologic characteristics. DnMBC has distinct prognostic and biologic features compared to early breast cancer or recurrent metastatic breast cancer (rMBC). The 5‐year disease‐specific survival rate for dnMBC was markedly higher than for rMBC (44% vs. 21%), likely due to recurrences and metastases arising from the selection and amplification of drug‐resistant tumor cell clones following the curative treatment of early breast cancer. 3 Additionally, the prognosis among patients with dnMBC can vary significantly, influenced by demographic factors, tumor grade, molecular subtype, and metastatic patterns, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 leading to a median overall survival (OS) of 2–3 years, but ranging from 11 to 55.9 months across different groups. 1

Currently, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems for various cancers, including lung, thymic, colon, and ovarian tumors, have further subdivided patients with distant metastases into different prognostic groups. This enhances the accuracy of prognostic assessments and aids in therapy selection. Furthermore, the 8th AJCC staging system for breast cancer has refined the prognostic stage for early breast cancer by integrating additional biologic factors, such as histologic grade and the status of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), as well as gene‐based assays. 8 Several studies have demonstrated that this updated prognostic stage system offers more precise prognostic differentiation than anatomic staging alone. 9 , 10 , 11 Nevertheless, while dnMBC was recognized as a distinct and heterogeneous disease, the current breast cancer staging system still merged all patients into a single category (M1 or IV stage) without consideration of anatomic and biologic factors.

DnMBC constitutes a significant portion of advanced breast cancer, representing as much as 30.6% of all cases of late‐stage metastatic breast cancer. 12 , 13 , 14 Historically, dnMBC has been considered incurable; thus, systemic therapy (ST) has emerged as the foundational treatment strategy, focusing on symptom alleviation, quality of life enhancement, tumor progression delay, and survival extension. Locoregional therapy (LRT) for primary tumor is primarily palliative, aimed at managing symptoms like skin ulcers, bleeding, and pain. However, distinct characteristics and inherent heterogeneity of dnMBC, coupled with advancements in systemic treatments such as CDK4/6 inhibitors and anti‐HER2 targeted therapies, have enabled a considerable number of patients to achieve long‐time survival. In the CLEOPATRA trial, the combination of docetaxel, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab as a first‐line treatment for advanced breast cancer significantly improved the OS of patients with HER2‐positive metastatic breast cancer, with an 8‐year OS rate of up to 37%, and a progression‐free survival rate up to 16%. 15 Contemporary systemic treatment approaches enhance control over metastatic lesions and extend survival, prompting a reevaluation of the role of LRT in dnMBC management. For the selected patients, who have lower tumor burden or disease with less aggressive tumor biology, LRT may not only be for controlling the local tumor but also for achieving long‐term survival or even clinical cure. We aimed to establish the subdivision of the M1 category (M1a, M1b, and M1c) and prognostic stage groups (IVa, IVb, and IVc) for dnMBC, incorporating anatomic and biologic factors such as T/N stage; histologic grade; subtypes according to ER, PR, and HER2 status; and the location and number of metastatic sites to improve prognosis prediction and assist in determining locoregional treatment strategies.

METHODS

Patients

Patients aged ≥18 years, diagnosed with histologically confirmed invasive breast cancer between 2010 and 2019, were selected from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Upon initial diagnosis, all patients presented with distant metastasis and had a single primary tumor without other malignancies. Patients with missing race, marital status, T/N stage, pathological grade, receptor status (ER, PR, and HER2), metastatic sites (brain, liver, lung, or bone), first treatment course, or follow‐up were excluded. Distant lymph node metastasis (DLNM) has not been collected until 2016; hence, information on DLNM was not included. Patients with additional metastatic sites (other than brain, liver, lung, or bone metastasis) were also excluded. 11 498 eligible patients were grouped into the training (N = 8048) or internal validation sets (IVS, N = 3450) randomly at a 7:3 ratio (Figure 1).

Selection of patients.

In addition, an independent cohort of dnMBC patients who underwent treatment at three hospitals (Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences; Cancer Hospital of Huanxing; Beijing Chaoyang District Sanhuan Cancer Hospital) between January 2010 and May 2023 were enrolled using the generally consistent eligibility criteria, constituting the external validation set (EVS, N = 660) of this study (Figure 1). The pathologic grade of many patients (N = 181, 27.4%; Table 1) was not available; therefore, no restriction on grade was set to this cohort. Ethical approval was obtained by the institutional review board at each center.

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the duration from initial diagnosis to death by any cause. Surviving patients were censored at the most recent follow‐up. Hormone receptor positivity (HR+) was determined by being ER‐positive and/or PR‐positive, while negativity (HR−) was both ER‐negative and PR‐negative. Breast cancer was classified into four subtypes: HR+/HER2−, HR+/HER2+, HR−/HER+, and triple‐negative (HR−/HER−). The number of metastases was divided into single organ metastasis and multiple metastases (≥2 organ metastasis). Visceral metastasis in this study included patients with site of metastases as any of the following: brain, liver, or lung.

Statistical analysis

Two‐sided chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was used to assess categorical variable differences between groups. Unfortunately, information regarding the quantity and diameters of distant metastases (only location) was not recorded in the SEER database. As a result, subdivision of M1 category was attempted on the basis of the location of metastases (brain, liver, lung, or bone) and the presence of visceral metastasis (yes or no) or multiple metastases (yes or no). In the 2016 updated 8th AJCC staging system, nonanatomic factors like pathologic grade, receptor status (ER, PR and HER2), and genomic assays were incorporated for defining the prognostic stage group for nonmetastatic breast cancer, along with local tumor size and regional lymph node involvement. 8 , 16 Contrary to early breast cancer, local tumor burden (T/N stage) was not well‐established risk factors for dnMBC, while distant metastases represented a life‐threatening event and the main cause of death. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Thus, T/N stage was excluded in the final analysis. In this study, prognostic stage was refined according to tumor grade, receptor status (ER, PR, HER2), and M1 stage above.

The backward stepwise selection approach was performed to select the appropriate variables. The multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was developed to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for OS, followed by the construction of the nomogram based on the findings of the model. The accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated through the application of calibration curves. The total risk score for each patient was calculated using the sum of nomogram score for each variable. The X‐tile program determined optimal total risk score cutoff values, dividing the patients into three different risk categories. Afterward, nomogram‐based risk stratification was performed to group patients with similar OS into the same M1 subcategory or prognostic stage, validated based on internal and external validation sets. OS was estimated based on the Kaplan–Meier method and then compared through the log‐rank test. All tests used two‐sided p values of <0.05 suggesting statistical significance. Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM Corp) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

After applying the established eligibility criteria (Figure 1), the final cohort comprised 12 158 patients, with a median follow‐up of 22 months and a median OS of 35 months. Detailed baseline characteristics are displayed in Table 1 and Table S1. The clinicopathologic features exhibited balance between the training and interval validation sets from the SEER database; however, notable discrepancies were observed between the SEER database and the EVS.A higher proportion of patients in the SEER database were aged over 60 (Training/IVS: 47.0%/49.0%) compared to those in the EVS (20.5%). Among the overall population, the majority had T2 (36.0%) or node‐positive (79.9%) disease, with most tumors being grade II/III (91.0%), ER‐positive (75.2%), PR‐positive (61.9%), and HER2‐negative (71.9%). Consequently, the HR+/HER2− subtype was the predominant type among dnMBC patients, accounting for 59.2%. Bone (73.3%) was the most frequently involved organ across all datasets. At initial diagnosis, most patients had only one metastatic site (66.3%). During the first course of treatment, most patients (68.0%) received chemotherapy while nearly one‐third of the patients underwent surgery (33.2%) or radiotherapy (35.0%) for the primary breast tumor. Notably, a higher percentage of patients in the EVS underwent surgery (56.5%) and chemotherapy (93.9%) than those in the SEER database.

Development of M1 category subdivision

With regard to different patterns of distant metastases influencing OS, multivariable analysis in the training set showed that brain (HR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.60–1.98; p < 0.001), liver (HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.19–1.38; p < 0.001), lung (HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.23–1.42; p < 0.001), and multiple metastases (HR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.11–1.31; p < 0.001) were significant independent predictors (Table S2). A nomogram incorporating these factors was developed (Figures 2a and S8), assigning specific scores to each variable (Table S3). The presence of brain metastasis (risk score, 10) contributed the most to the prognosis, which was followed by lung metastasis (risk score, 5), liver metastasis (risk score, 4), and multiple metastases (risk score, 3). Based on the overall scores calculated from the nomogram, patients with dnMBC were categorized into three different risk categories, including low‐ (<4 points), intermediate‐ (4–8 points), and high‐risk groups (>8 points) (Table S3 and Figures 2a and S2). Using the nomogram‐based risk stratification and patterns of distant metastases (Table 2 and Figure S1), patients in the 15 original groups were combined into three categories: M1a for bone‐only involvement; M1b for liver or lung involvement only, with or without bone metastases; M1c for brain metastasis or involvement of both liver and lung, regardless of other locations (Table 3). As the stage increased, the survival rates at 3 and 5 years decreased consistently: 3‐year OS rate, M1a: 56.9%, M1b: 44.9%, and M1c 25.2%; 5‐year OS rate, M1a: 36.7%, M1b: 27.8%, and M1c 14.6% (Table S3 and Figure 3a). After adjustment, the recently established M1 category subdivision remained a significant factor for OS (M1a vs. M1c, HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.44–0.52; M1b vs. M1c, HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.57–0.67; all p‐values <0.001) (Table 5).

Prognostic staging development

After the subdivision of M1 category was constructed, further subdivisions were implemented according to biologic factors to refine the ultimate prognostic stage (IV a/b/c). Initially, a nomogram was established by integrating various anatomic and biologic factors, such as tumor grade, receptor status (ER, PR, HER2), and M1 stage because all these factors showed independent prognostic value (Figure S6). The results demonstrated that M1 stage had the largest contribution to prognosis (risk score, M1b:4, M1c:10), followed by HER2 negativity (risk score, 9) (Table S4 and Figure S6). Given that the combinations of receptor status were too many and complex, subsequent risk stratification analysis could not be conducted (Table S4 and Figure S3). Thus, the receptor status was substituted for breast cancer subtype due to the feasibility of subsequent analyses and possible clinical applications. However, the risk stratification based on grade, subtype, and M1 stage was unable to effectively subdivide prognostic stage into three categories available for clinical application (Table S5 and Figure S4). Further analysis revealed that tumor grade (risk score, II:2, III:3) showed less influence on the prognosis than molecular subtype (risk score, HR−/HER2+:2, HR+/HER2−:4, Triple‐negative:10) or M1 stage (risk score, M1b:2, M1c:7) and there was not much prognostic difference between different pathological grades (II vs. III) (Table S5 and Figure S7). Additionally, the vast majority of tumors were grade II/III (91.0%) in patients with dnMBC.

Ultimately, the nomogram, including subtype and M1 stage only, illustrated that subtype (risk score, HR−/HER2+:2, HR+/HER2−:3, Triple‐negative:10) had a greater impact on prognosis than M1 stage (risk score, M1b:2, M1c:7) (Table S6, Figures 2b and S9). Based on the overall scores calculated from the last nomogram, patients with dnMBC were stratified into three risk categories, including low‐ (<5 points), intermediate‐ (5–7 points), and high‐risk groups (>7 points) (Table S6, Figures 2b and S5). On the basis of the nomogram‐based risk stratification and prognostic groups defined by subtype and M1 stage, patients in the 12 original groups were combined into three categories: IVa, IVb, and IVc (Table 4). The development of prognostic stage remained largely consistent with the risk stratification of total risk scores, while only one adjustment was made to classify patients with HR−/HER2+ and M1b (total risk scores, 4, low‐risk group) into IVb group in consideration of consistency of rules and clinical utility. In general, prognostic stage remained consistent with M1 category subdivision (M1a → IVa). However, patients with triple negative tumors were categorized as IVC owing to their poor survival, but those with HR+/HER2+ subtype showed relatively favorable prognosis and were thus grouped into relatively low prognostic stage compared to M1 stage (M1c → IVb or M1b → IVa) (Table S7). Survival decreased steadily with higher stages, with median OS of 47 months for IVa, 34 months for IVb, and 14 months for IVc (Table 4 and Figure 3d). A multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed notable difference in OS among distinct prognostic stage categories (IVa vs. IVc, HR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.30–0.35; IVb vs. IVc, HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.42–0.49; all p‐values <0.001).

Validation of M1 and prognostic stage

Validation of the M1 and prognostic stage was described in Table 5 and Figure 3. The internal validation cohort consisted of 3450 patients, with a median follow‐up duration of 21 months (Table 1 and Figure 1). By applying the newly defined M1 subdivision model, patients were effectively sorted into three groups, demonstrating significant differences in survival outcomes (median OS for M1a: 44 months, M1b: 32 months, M1c: 14 months; p < 0.001; see Figure 3b). Subsequent adjustments for covariates confirmed the statistical significance of the M1 subdivision's impact on OS (M1a vs. M1c, HR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.39–0.51; M1b vs. M1c, HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.50–0.66; all p‐values <0.001) (Table 5). The external validation cohort comprises 660 patients with a median follow‐up of 31.8 months (Table 1 and Figure 1). After applying the newly defined M1 subdivision model, patients were also successfully grouped into three distinct prognostic groups (median OS, M1a: 60.5 months, M1b: 51.7 months, and M1c: 26.3 months; log‐rank p < 0.001; Figure 3c). After adjustment, the correlation between the recently established M1 category subgroup and OS continued to show statistical significance (M1a vs. M1c, HR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.24–0.49; M1b vs. M1c, HR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.30–0.58; all p‐values <0.001) (Table 5). These findings were consistent across the newly developed prognostic stage groups, as validated by both the internal and external cohorts (refer to Table 5 and Figure 3e,f).

Associations of surgical treatment with survival outcomes across M1 subcategories or prognostic stages

Analysis of surgical versus nonsurgical treatments in the SEER database indicated that undergoing primary breast cancer surgery was associated with a favorable prognosis in a multivariable Cox regression model, especially for M1a patients.

(M1a: HR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.48–0.57; p < 0.001, M1b: HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.56–0.67; p < 0.001, and M1c: HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.53–0.70; p < 0.001) as shown in Table 6 and Figure 4. In addition, patients diagnosed with M1c disease experienced less benefit from surgical intervention than those with M1a or M1b disease in the external validation group (M1a: HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.35–0.76; p < 001, M1b: HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.34–0.64; p < 001, and M1c: HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.46–2.18; p = 1.000) (Table 6 and Figure 4). Surprisingly, the OS advantage of surgical treatment was consistent across all prognostic stage groups, as detailed in Table S8.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the newly subdivisions within M1 category (M1a, M1b, and M1c) and prognostic stage groups (IVa, IVb, and IVc) for dnMBC were proposed, based on key prognostic variables. These included T/N stage, histologic grade, ER, PR, HER2, and the location and number of metastatic sites. Initially, M1 category subdivisions were developed on the basis of the metastatic burden, specifically the location of metastases (brain, liver, lung, bone, and visceral sites) and the number of metastatic sites (either single or multiple metastases). Our analysis identified that the involvement of brain, liver, lung, and multiple metastases was independently associated with OS. The nomogram‐based risk stratification using these factors further subdivided patients with dnMBC into three M1 subcategories with distinct prognoses, providing more accurate prognostic information. Despite numerous variables influencing the prognosis, prognostic stage was subsequently established using prognostic factors in alignment with those used for early breast cancer in the current staging system, namely, T or N stage, tumor grade, and biomarkers (ER, PR, HER2). After multiple analyses, only breast cancer subtype and M1 stage were included to define the final prognostic stage groups with distinctly different survival to summarize the condition and prognosis of dnMBC patients concisely and accurately. Our findings further revealed that patients in M1c stage had shorter life expectancies and might not benefit from LRT, assisting in the tailoring of individualized treatment strategies. Moreover, the robustness and reproducibility of our results were confirmed through validation across multiple datasets.

Generally, our findings align with previous research and clinical observations: patients with less metastatic burden, HR‐positive or HER2‐positive tumor had higher survival rates, while patients with multiple or vital organs involvement, as well as triple‐negative subtype showed the worst prognosis. Specifically, early studies demonstrated that bone‐only or non‐visceral metastases, fewer metastatic sites, HR and HER2 positivity proved to be favorable prognostic factors, which we also identified in this study. 7 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 In a study of 16 702 MBC patients diagnosed from 2008 to 2014, including 4763 dnMBC patients, the median survival for those with HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and HR−/HER2− subtype was 44.91, 42.12, and 14.52 months, respectively. 26 Brain metastases, long considered a catastrophic event in MBC patients in light of an extremely short survival, also emerged as the most significant prognostic factor in our analysis. 27 , 28 Notably, patients with multiple vital organ metastases (the involvement of both liver and lung) carried a dismal prognosis similar to those with brain metastases in this study, underscoring the need for heightened clinical attention to this group. In recent years, numerous studies have highlighted the metastatic heterogeneity of breast cancer with distinct preferences for different organs across subtypes. 29 , 30 Luminal tumors commonly metastasize to bone, while HER2‐positive and triple‐negative cancers tend to have a preference for the liver and lung, respectively. 30 , 31 The survival of liver metastasis was usually worse relative to lung metastasis in previous reports, 29 , 30 but our research revealed that patients with liver metastasis had a comparable or even better prognosis than those with lung metastasis. The organ‐specific metastasis of breast cancer patients may account for this in that the presence of liver metastasis was linked to a higher percentage of HER2‐postive subtype, which has seen a significant improvement in prognosis due to new anti‐HER2 targeted treatments. Both our findings and earlier work demonstrated that the location and number of distant metastases might be critical parameters in defining M1 category subdivisions. Accordingly, patients with bone‐only metastases were grouped as M1a category owing to relatively favorable outcomes, whereas those with brain metastasis or both involvement of liver and lung were classified as M1c.

In terms of prognostic stage, only the prognostic influence of subtype in dnMBC was highly significant similar to those in early breast cancer. Although T/N stage and tumor grade were included for exploratory purposes, they were not as impactful for dnMBC. Notably, our findings indicated that patients with HER‐positive subtype, either HR+/HER+ or HR−/HER− subtype, seemed to have a survival advantage over those with HR+/HER‐subtype, which is generally considered to have the best prognosis in clinical practice and previous research. In contrast, patients with TNBC had the worst prognosis, regardless of M1 category, and were therefore classified within the IVc prognostic group. The nature of intrinsic tumor biology and advancements of systematic therapy determine the role of breast cancer subtype in dnMBC patients. Specifically, first‐line treatments incorporating trastuzumab, either alone or in combination with pertuzumab, have significantly extended median survival to more than 5 years for patients with de novo HER2‐positive MBC, while the survival for advanced TNBC rarely surpasses 2 years, even with the most recent treatment strategies combining immunotherapy and chemotherapy. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38

Various prognostic and staging models have been developed for patients with dnMBC, aiming to enhance prognostic accuracy. 4 , 17 , 18 , 19 A recent nomogram for predicting OS incorporating demographic, clinicopathological, and treatment variables performed moderately well (C‐index, 0.723; 95% CI, 0.713–0.733). 4 The nomogram, however, cannot refine M1 category or prognostic stage for the inclusion of demographic and treatment factors beyond those used in the AJCC breast cancer staging system. A previous study formulated an M1 staging system based on patients with dnMBC diagnosed from 2010 to 2014 and subdivided all patients into three groups: M1a, bone‐only, or lung‐only metastasis; M1b, liver‐only metastasis, or multiple metastases excluding brain or liver metastasis; and M1c, brain metastasis, or multiple metastases including liver metastasis. 17 On comparison, our study used a more contemporary dataset and successfully distinguished patients with bone‐only metastasis (M1a) and those with lung‐only metastasis (M1b) based on the distinct prognostic outcomes of each group, better reflecting current clinical practices and evidence. Additionally, Plichta et al. also developed novel staging systems using recursive partitioning analysis based on anatomic and biologic factors, creating 24 and 53 distinct prognostic subgroups, respectively. This approach highlighted the heterogeneity of dnMBC and suggested directions for future AJCC staging guideline revisions. 18 , 19 While these systems could be simplified into three or four stage groups (IVA‐IVC/IVA‐IVD), their complexity hinders practical application. Additionally, a significant strength of our study is the external validation with a separate Chinese population dataset, enhancing the credibility of our risk stratification and broadening the applicability of our M1 categorization and prognostic staging, in contrast to the studies mentioned.

It is usually considered that palliative ST is the backbone for dnMBC, as a apart of advanced breast cancer. Even so, multiple retrospective studies have showed improved survival with LRT, and some patients with dnMBC pursue complete primary tumor excision for the psychosocial reasons. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 In such studies, selection bias is inevitable as younger patients with better performance status and who respond well to systematic therapy are more inclined to opt for breast surgery. 40 , 41 , 42 Hence, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been performed to assess the efficacy of surgical treatment in dnMBC, yielding conflicting results. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 Notably, four out of five RCTs showed no OS improvement from surgical intervention, leading to guidelines generally not endorsing LRT for dnMBC patients. A comprehensive analysis of all five RCTs concluded that surgical treatment of the primary tumor does not prolong survival in dnMBC patients. 49 Considering the above, evidence from RCTs suggests that LRT might not be applicable to all patients with dnMBC, but may benefit selected patients. At the 10‐year follow‐up, the Turkish RCT (MF07‐01) found that the LRT group had an 11‐month longer survival and a 14% higher likelihood of OS than the ST group (46 vs. 35 months; 19% vs. 5%), particularly in those with solitary bone‐only metastases, ER+/PR+, or HER2− subtype. 46 In addition, multiple retrospective studies have found that patients diagnosed with dnMBC limited to contralateral axillary or sternal regions could experience long‐term survival comparable to those with locally advanced breast cancer following curative‐intent treatment, including LRT. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Similarly, our data revealed that LRT may confer a favorable prognosis for patients with bone‐metastases only (M1a) rather than those with high metastatic burden (M1c). Regarding molecular subtypes, a greater proportion of de novo HER2‐positive MBC achieved durable responses and long‐term survival due to standard dual‐antibody treatments, and were thus likely to be suitable for LRT. 32 , 35 , 54 On the contrary, LRT might not bring survival benefit, even negatively affect prognosis in patients with TNBC. 46 , 47 These results seemed to correspond to our proposed prognostic stage, categorizing HR+/HER2+ patients as IVa/b groups, in contrast to TNBC patients classified as IVc group. Taken together, our proposed M1 category subdivisions and prognostic stage have some implications for clinical practice, aiding oncologists in achieving a better balance between clinical benefit and treatment‐related toxicities when making clinical decisions. The combination of ST and LRT may be preferable for patients with M1a or IVa disease because these patients tended to have a longer life expectancy and better tolerability, more likely to benefit from this treatment strategy. For patients with M1c or IVc disease, ST alone may be a better therapeutic option due to the high tumor burden, aggressive tumor biology, and potential for LRT to reduce quality of life or accelerate disease progression. Despite the observed survival benefit of LRT for all three prognostic groups, it may be due to selection bias. It is reasonable to assume that patients with IVc stage disease receiving surgery may present better performance status and be highly responsive to systematic therapy.

Several limitations were identified in our study. While the SEER database is population‐based and has a large number of patients, some key variables were not available. For example, the size and number of metastatic lesions, other common metastatic sites, including distant lymph node or soft tissue metastases, the evaluation of organ function, and the assessment of the severity of bone metastasis, including stress assessment of load‐bearing bones or bone marrow invasion were not collected in the SEER database. Significantly, the introduction of new treatment options after 2019, like novel targeted agents or antibody‐drug conjugates, has greatly increased the OS of individuals with HR+/HER2− or HER2‐positive advanced breast cancer, changing the treatment landscape rapidly. 55 , 56 As a result, the evolution of systemic therapies during the study period likely influenced the absolute survival rates and could have modified the stratification within the M1 category and prognostic stage. Furthermore, our analysis exclusively focused on dnMBC patients, thereby restricting its applicability to individuals with rMBC.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, the novel subdivisions of M1 category and prognostic stage were proposed, integrating the anatomic extent of distant metastases and biologic factors. These refinements aim to provide more accurate prognostic information and inform locoregional treatment strategies for patients with dnMBC. The staging systems can be used as a supplement to the current staging guidelines, providing an excellent foundation for future research. Even though further refinement and validation in prospective cohorts are required, the systems promise to improve patient selection for treatment in future clinical trials and practice.
