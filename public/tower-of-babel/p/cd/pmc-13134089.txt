Coexistence of pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis following treatment for SARS-CoV-2 infection in a kidney transplant recipient: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Cuirong Hu, Liang Ying, Yaping Zhan, Jinkun Wang, Jinling Ye, Jifang Lu, Haijiao Jin, Xiaoming Tan, Leyi Gu, Yulong Yao, Na Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)- associated pneumonia increases patients’ susceptibility to fungal superinfections, particularly among immunocompromised individuals.Case presentationA 60-year-old woman with history of kidney transplantation was admitted for recurrent fever and positive SARS-CoV-2 antigen on pharyngeal swab testing. The patient developed rapid-onset respiratory distress secondary to progressive pulmonary infection and received assisted mechanical ventilation. Microbiological sequencing and culture of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and sputum, as well as serological tests, revealed the presence of Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus lentulus and Cryptococcus neoformans, along with multiple bacterial and viral pathogens. Following a course of combined antifungal, antibacterial, and antiviral therapies, the patient was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation. The lung exudates and the large cavitary abscess were absorbed completely.ConclusionsWe reported successful treatment of a rare case of concurrent aspergillosis and cryptococcosis following SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia in a kidney transplantation recipient. The severe fungal infection was probably attributed to the immunosuppressive status associated with a history of solid organ transplantation, as well as prolonged administration of glucocorticoid and antibiotics.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Aspergillosis, Cryptococcosis, Pneumonia, Kidney transplantation

Background

Since its first identification in 2019, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has affected over 0.76 billion people worldwide, with more than 6.8 million cases of fatalities [1]. Secondary fungal infections are common in patients with SARS-CoV-2-associated pneumonia, particularly among the elderly, individuals with pre-existing comorbidities and those with immunosuppressive conditions. Candida and Aspergillus species have been reported as the predominant pathogens, whereas Cryptococcus and Mucormycetes infections are relatively uncommon [2]. Superinfection of fungus constituted an additional risk factor for increased mortality in these patients. Herein, we reported a case of concurrent pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis following treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection in a patient with a history of kidney transplantation.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old female patient was admitted to the nephrology ward in December 2022, with chief complaint of recurrent fever and sore throat for 4 days. A self-conducted pharyngeal swab SARS-CoV-2 antigen test was positive. She reported a medical history of kidney transplantation 10 years prior, which was performed for uremia likely attributable to hypertension and diabetes mellitus. At the time of admission, she was receiving prednisone (5 mg qd po.), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF, 0.5 g bid po.) and cyclosporine (CsA, 50 mg bid po.).

Chest computed tomography (CT) at the day of admission did not show obvious exudates (Fig. 1a) and the oxygen saturation was normal. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration was 19.7 mg/dL (normal < 8 mg/dL) and procalcitonin (PCT) level was 0.21 ng/mL (normal < 0.05 ng/mL). Serum creatinine level was 161.65 µmol/L with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, CKD-EPI) of 29.66 ml/min/1.73m2 and her serum creatinine was maintained at approximately 100 µmol/L in the past two years. Prednisone was increased to 10 mg qd and MMF was reduced to 0.25 g bid. Antiviral treatment was not administered owing to the shortage of medicine at the time. Ceftriaxone was given prophylactically and intravenous immunoglobin was administered at the dose of 20 g daily for 5 days.

On hospital day 7, the patient developed respiratory distress and presented low oxygen saturation of 84–90%, accompanied by an elevated serum CRP of 89.8 mg/dL and a serum creatinine level of 348 µmol/L. Chest CT showed ground glass shadows and consolidative opacities throughout both lungs (Fig. 1b) and non-invasive ventilator was applied immediately. Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir was administered for a total of 5 days and the regimen consisted of 300 mg nirmatrelvir plus 100 mg ritonavir on day 1, followed by 150 mg nirmatrelvir plus 100 mg ritonavir once daily for the subsequent 4 days. Methylprednisolone (MP) was applied intravenously at the dose of 40 mg daily, while MMF was discontinued and CsA was reduced to 25 mg bid. Low molecular weight heparin of 2,000IU daily was used as prophylactic anticoagulation. Ceftriaxone was upgraded to imipenem/cilastatin. Breathing distress and hypoxemia resolved soon, and oxygen support was accordingly downgraded from non-invasive ventilation to nasal cannula oxygen therapy. A repeat pharyngeal swab test for SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid turned negative.

However, recurrent chest tightness developed on day 22 with oxygen saturation dropping to 80% despite non-invasive ventilator support. The patient subsequently underwent endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, followed by transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). Intravenous MP was increased to 200 mg per day for 2 days, then tapered down to 80 mg per day. Bronchoscopy was performed on day 26 and culture of the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid demonstrated the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Corynebacterium striatum, and gas-producing Klebsiella (Table 1). Accordingly, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to piperacillin/tazobactam combined with levofloxacin. In response to this treatment, serum infection markers decreased and the lung lesions showed partial resolution. However, serum creatinine increased to 426 µmol/L and 24 h urine output dropped to less than 500 ml. Ultrasound examination showed an increased arterial resistance index in kidney allograft (RI 0.84). In consideration of acute kidney allograft rejection, MP was maintained at the dose of 80 mg qd for 10 days, followed by gradually tapering to oral prednisone. CsA was changed to tacrolimus 0.5 mg qd po. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. Although serum creatinine showed a gradual decline, the patient’s urine output was maintained at only 100 ml per 24 h, and CRRT was therefore continued.

Chest X-ray on day 32 showed the ground glass shadows and consolidative opacities were partially absorbed (Fig. 1c), and endotracheal tube was subsequently removed and the patient was weaned to high-flow supplemental oxygen. However, she demonstrated enhanced breath tightness once again on day 44, and chest X-ray showed increased consolidative opacities in the right upper lobe (Fig. 1d). Tracheostomy was then performed and the patient underwent mechanical ventilation again. Microorganisms sequencing of BAL fluid, and serial culture of BAL fluid and sputum, revealed the presence of Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus lentulus, Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida albicans, Corynebacterium striatum, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and a couple of viruses (Table 1). In addition, serum polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing was positive for cytomegalovirus. Serum CRP rose to 198.5 mg/dL and PCT level increased to 3.86 ng/mL. Serum galactomannan (GM) concentration was 6.09 µg/L (reference range: < 0.5 µg/L) and the serum (1–3)-β-D-glucan (G) level was 417.43 pg/mL (reference range: < 95 pg/mL). Oral voriconazole was commenced at a dose of 200 mg twice daily in combination with intravenous caspofungin for the treatment of fungal infection. The dose of voriconazole was adjusted according to serum trough concentrations, which ranged from 0.5 to 6.0 µg/mL during hospitalization (Table 2). Intravenous imipenem/cilastatin, piperacillin/tazobactam, levofloxacin, cefoperazone/sulbactam, cefepime, meropenem, amikacin, linezolid, vancomycin, and ceftazidime/avibactam were given sequentially for the treatment of bacterial infection. Given the positive serum PCR result for cytomegalovirus, ganciclovir was administered intravenously at a dose of 75 mg three times weekly as a precautionary measure.

On day 54, serum CRP concentration reduced to 141.7 mg/dL and PCT dropped to 0.58 ng/mL. Chest CT revealed increased consolidation in the right upper lobe (Fig. 1e). Nevertheless, the patient maintained stable respiratory status with oxygen saturation kept at 100%. Ventilator parameters were gradually tapered down, and the patient was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation on the same day. Despite continued anti-infective therapy, a large abscess with a central cavity developed in the right upper lobe and the lesion enlarged to 83 × 55 mm, with a thick-walled cavity measuring 75 × 27 mm on day 86 (Figs. 1f-i). On day 75, BAL fluid showed Cryptococcus neoformans growth again with serum cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide antigen testing (Colloidal gold method) turned positive. The dose of voriconazole was increased to 300 mg bid to achieve therapeutic concentration and 200 mg of intravenous fluconazole was administered once daily. The endotracheal tube was removed on day 87 and the patient was subsequently transferred back to the nephrology ward.

Voriconazole and fluconazole were continued for the treatment of pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis, while levofloxacin was administered for bacterial infection control. Serum creatinine levels were maintained at 200–300 µmol/L, while daily urine output persisted at only 300–500 ml. Consequently, the patient underwent an arteriovenous fistula operation and was maintained on stable hemodialysis. On day 101, chest CT revealed regression of the lung abscess (Fig. 1j). The patient was discharged on day 109, with oral voriconazole and fluconazole continued.

One month after discharge, serum CRP decreased to 10.64 mg/dL and serum GM became undetectable. Chest CT showed further absorption of the abscess and cavity in the right upper lobe (Fig. 1k). Voriconazole was therefore discontinued. Six months later, chest CT demonstrated complete resolution of the pulmonary abscess and cavity (Fig. 1l). As serum Cryptococcus capsular polysaccharide remained positive, fluconazole was continued for an additional 5 months with a halved dose until the marker turned negative. To date, the patient continued to receive maintenance hemodialysis at our hospital.

Discussion and conclusions

In the present study, we reported a rare case of severe SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia complicated with pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis in a kidney transplant recipient with a long-term history of diabetes mellitus. She presented with recurrent respiratory distress and required invasive mechanical ventilation due to the rapid progression of lung lesions. A series of antifungal and antibacterial agents were administered in addition to antiviral therapy and respiratory support. Ultimately, the patient was weaned from ventilator successfully. The pulmonary exudates, large abscess and cavity were completely absorbed, with serological markers for both aspergillosis and cryptococcosis converting to negative.

Secondary fungal infection is common in patients with viral pneumonias, including that caused by SARS-CoV-2. Candida has been identified as the most common pathogen and a number of pulmonary aspergillosis cases were reported in high-risk patients. Such infections are termed as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) [3]. The incidence of CAPA was shown to be ranging between 3.3% and 34.4% in critically ill patients [4]. Our patient fulfilled the CAPA diagnostic criteria established by the International Society for Human and Animal Mycology [5]. This diagnosis was supported by BAL fluid microbial sequencing, positive culture of both BAL fluid and sputum and the elevated serum GM level, despite the minor possibility of cross-reactivity with Cryptococcus spp [6]. Moreover, the abscess in the right upper lobe supported the diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Patients with CAPA were older, have a higher prevalence of underling chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and are more likely to have received long-term corticosteroid therapy [4]. Our patient was a kidney transplant recipient. Moreover, she had undergone a prolonged course of broad-spectrum antibiotics treatment and invasive mechanical ventilation, which further increased the susceptibility to develop CAPA. CAPA is associated with more severe comorbidities, higher need for mechanical ventilation and elevated mortality [4, 7]. Indeed, our patient experienced recurrent respiratory failure and stayed at ICU for over 2 months.

Pulmonary cryptococcosis is an opportunistic invasive mycosis that predominantly affects immunocompromised patients [8]. Although several cases of cryptococcosis secondary to SARS-CoV-2 infection have been reported, its epidemiology, clinical manifestations, microbiological features and prognosis remain poorly understood. COVID-19-associated cryptococcosis is more common in the elderly, patients with severe COVID-19 and those who have received systemic corticosteroid therapy [9]. Cryptococcus neoformans has been suggested to be the most common pathogen and most cases of cryptococcosis develop approximately 10 days after the diagnosis of COVID-19 [9, 10]. In the present case, Cryptococcus neoformans was firstly detected in the BAL fluid on day 42 and again on day 75, suggesting the infection was not being effectively controlled by voriconazole monotherapy at that stage. Voriconazole monotherapy has been reported to be effective in patients with concurrent pulmonary cryptococcosis and aspergillosis [11]. It was noted that the trough concentration of serum voriconazole was low at that time, even though the dose had been adjusted to 300 mg bid. However, due to the severe drug shortage during the peak of the COVID-19 epidemic in China at the moment, amphotericin B and flucytosine were not available at our hospital. Notably, serum voriconazole concentrations increased significantly after the addition of fluconazole. So the observed improvement in lung lesions might be attributed to the optimized therapeutic effect of voriconazole, achieved by the elevated serum concentration through the inhibition of CYP450 enzymes by fluconazole. It is worth noting that co-administration of voriconazole and fluconazole is not a classic regimen, but an alternative approach under resource-limited circumstances.

Disseminated infection, defined as either a positive blood culture or cryptococcal involvement of at least two different sites, was reported to be the most common clinical presentation in patients with SARS-CoV-2 associated cryptococcosis, followed by pulmonary and meningeal manifestations [9]. A limitation of the present case is that only serum Cryptococcus antigen testing was conducted, without concomitant serum culture or cerebrospinal fluid examination. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that the positive serum cryptococcal antigen result might indicate isolated pulmonary cryptococcosis, given the patient did not present clinical manifestations of extrapulmonary involvement, such as neurological or cutaneous symptoms. Considering the immunosuppressed status of the patient as a kidney transplant recipient, the antifungal treatment course for pulmonary cryptococcosis was extended beyond the absorption of the lung cavitary abscess, with therapy maintained until the serum cryptococcal antigen converted to negative.

Herein, we report the successful treatment of a rare case of concurrent aspergillosis and cryptococcosis following SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia in a kidney transplant recipient. The only previously reported case in the literature described a patient who progressed to severe pneumonia and acute kidney injury, leading the patient’s surrogate decision-makers to withdraw life support [12]. The patient was a 59-year-old male with multiple medical comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease treated with inhaled corticosteroid, type 2 diabetes mellitus and cirrhosis secondary to hepatitis C virus infection. He was administered high-dose corticosteroids for exacerbated respiratory distress related to COVID-19 infection and was given two doses of cyclophosphamide due to clinical concerns regarding concurrent fibrotic lung disease and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. The BAL fluid culture indicated the presence of both Aspergillus fumigatus and Cryptococcus neoformans [12]. Our patient exhibited a similar clinical profile including senior age, multiple comorbidities, long-term immunosuppressed status and a history of high-dose corticosteroid administration. It should be noted that the clinical manifestations and imaging findings of mixed pulmonary fungal infection are non-specific, the incidence of concurrent aspergillosis and cryptococcosis may be underestimated in COVID-19 patients. Therefore, repeated etiological investigation aimed at early diagnosis and precise treatment are crucial for improving patient outcome.

Several cases of combined invasive pulmonary aspergillosis and cryptococcosis have been reported in non-COVID-19 immunosuppressed patients [13–17]. Moreover, some of these cases necessitated lung biopsy for definitive diagnosis, owing to the difficulties associated with pathogen differentiation [13, 15, 16]. In two cases, Aspergillus was found to infiltrate the pre-existing cavities of pulmonary cryptococcal lesions [15, 17]. Our patient also developed a large cavity within the pulmonary abscess, and microorganisms sequencing of BAL fluid identified concurrent presence of Cryptococcus and Aspergillus. We cannot infer whether the patient initially developed cryptococcal pneumonia and subsequently acquired an Aspergillus infection, owing to the lack of invasive diagnostic procedures including thoracentesis and lung biopsy.

In conclusion, we report the successful treatment of a case of concurrent aspergillosis and cryptococcosis following severe SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia in a kidney transplant recipient. The secondary fungal co-infection was presumed to be linked to the patient’s immunosuppressed status related to a solid organ transplant history, as well as long-term use of glucocorticoid and antibiotics.
