Usage patterns, perceived barriers and perceived impact of mobile medical applications in medical education and clinical practice: a cross-sectional study among medical students and junior doctors in Sudan
Authors: Alemam Awad Alameen Ahmed, Weam Babeker Ahmed Ali, Aliya Elsir Alhaj Mohammedalhassan, Mohamedelborae Abdalgafar Elimam Mohamed, Mohemedelmustafa Yahya Mohamed Eldouma, Amjed Siddig Khalid Mohammed, Abeer Mohammed Sayed Mohammed, Ragaa Mohammed Elshafie, Fal Muawia AbdAlla Hussein, Alaa Azhari Madani Tawfeeg, Ldan Abdelgader Wageialla Ahmed, Raheeg Sayed Mohamed Ali Elkhidir, Saneia Abdalsalm Abakar Abdallah, Mohammed Mahmmoud Fadelallah Eljack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobile medical apps have greatly enhanced access to healthcare information and clinical decision-making globally. However, their use in Sudan faces numerous barriers, despite the growing adoption worldwide. There is limited information on how Sudanese medical students and healthcare providers utilize these apps, the challenges they face, and how these tools affect clinical decisions. This study aimed to examine usage patterns, perceived barriers, and perceived impact of mobile medical applications on clinical decision-making and learning among medical students and junior doctors in Sudan to inform future digital curriculum integration.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among medical students in the clinical phase (4th, 5th, and 6th years), house officers, and general practitioners practicing in Sudan. Data were collected using a self-administered online questionnaire.ResultsA total of 2,945 participants completed the survey, including medical students (66.3%) and house officers or general practitioners (33.7%). Overall, 94% owned a smartphone or tablet capable of running medical applications. 80% reported using mobile medical apps during clinical training or practice, with significantly higher use among house officers and GPs than among medical students (89.0% vs. 75.0%; p < 0.001). Usage patterns differed by academic level, with junior doctors more often using clinically oriented apps for guideline reviews, drug information verification, treatment decisions, and on-call or emergencies. In contrast, students mainly used apps for academic reviews and exam preparation. The most common barrier was the cost of a paid subscription (48.0%). Participants generally viewed mobile medical applications positively, considering them accurate, time-saving, confidence-boosting, and helpful for inpatient care.ConclusionThe widespread use of medical apps, particularly among junior doctors, suggests a high readiness for digital integration; however, addressing subscription costs and providing institutional training is essential to bridge the gap between academic study and clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09528-6.

Introduction

Traditionally, textbooks have served as the main source of medical knowledge for practitioners and students alike [1]. However, there has been a notable shift in access to and utilization of medical information driven by rapid technological advances and the growing popularity of smartphones and other electronic devices [2]. In recent years, mobile medical applications, such as Medscape, UpToDate, and BMJ Best Practice, have greatly improved medical practice and education worldwide [3]. These apps are essential in medical education, as healthcare professionals and students utilize these new technologies throughout their training and practice. It offers numerous features, such as access to clinical guidelines, medication details, diagnostic tools, and medical calculators, while providing benefits over conventional paper resources, including portability, quick search capabilities, and regular updates [3, 4]. Their development has significantly enhanced smartphone capabilities, leading to perceived improvements in medical training, diagnostic accuracy, clinical decision-making, and healthcare delivery [5].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that mobile medical apps can help healthcare professionals and students improve their knowledge and skills [6]. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia showed that residents in clinical practice used smartphone medical apps for clinical communication and counseling (50.0%), interns used them for drug references (56.0%), and external users accessed them for resources and e-books (65.0%) [7]. Additionally, several studies have reported a higher prevalence of mobile medical app usage, especially in high-income countries [8].

In the Sudanese context, preparedness to adopt mobile medical applications is influenced by the existing digital health infrastructure and mobile connectivity. Despite substantial structural and economic difficulties, mobile technologies have been progressively incorporated into healthcare communication and service provision [9]. A recent study indexed in PubMed highlighted the use of mobile phone-based digital health solutions in Sudan, demonstrating their viability for enhancing clinical communication and decision-making, even in resource-constrained and conflict-affected environments [9]. The prevalence of mobile phone ownership far exceeds that of fixed internet access, indicating that smartphones serve as the primary entry point to digital services in the nation [9]. These circumstances facilitate Sudan’s preparedness for the adoption and incorporation of mobile health applications among healthcare professionals. However, despite this potential, the adoption of mobile medical applications in Sudan remains limited due to inadequate infrastructure, insufficient institutional support, high costs, and misalignment with local educational priorities [10]. These challenges hinder the integration of modern digital learning approaches, exacerbate existing educational gaps, and limit opportunities for interactive and experiential learning [11].

Despite the increasing global interest in mobile medical applications, research on their use in the Sudanese context remains scarce. Specifically, there is limited evidence on how these apps are utilized by medical students and healthcare professionals in Sudan, the perceived barriers to their practical use, and their perceived impact on clinical decision-making. This gap in the literature underscores the need for localized studies to understand better the role and significance of mobile medical apps in Sudan’s medical education and healthcare systems. Understanding these usage patterns is crucial for medical education because they show how students move from theoretical knowledge to practical, bedside learning. In resource-limited settings like Sudan, mobile apps can act as a key resource for self-directed learning (SDL), especially when physical libraries are unavailable.

Given the significant role these tools play in bridging the gap between theory and practice, this study aimed to examine how medical students and junior doctors in Sudan utilize mobile medical applications and to identify the primary perceived barriers to their adoption. Specifically, the study sought to answer: (1) What are the current app usage patterns during clinical training? (2) What factors hinder their effective use? and (3) What is the perceived impact of these applications on clinical decision-making and the learning process? These insights are intended to provide an evidence-based foundation for future digital curriculum integration in Sudanese medical schools.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional design to examine the patterns of use, perceived barriers, and perceived impact of mobile medical applications on clinical decision-making among medical students enrolled in Sudanese universities, house officers (interns), and general practitioners working in hospitals in Sudan. The research was conducted across multiple Sudanese universities and hospitals to encompass a wide range of clinical training and practice environments.

Study population and Inclusion criteria

The study population included medical students in their clinical years (4th, 5th, and 6th year) enrolled at Sudanese universities, house officers (interns), and practicing general practitioners in Sudanese hospitals. In this study, junior doctors are defined as medical graduates who have not yet commenced formal residency specialization. This group comprises house officers (interns) during their mandatory clinical training and general practitioners (GPs) serving as medical officers in hospital settings. Eligible participants were those actively involved in clinical training or practice in Sudan. Preclinical students (years 1–3) were excluded because they had limited or no exposure to real clinical environments. Additionally, house officers or physicians practicing outside Sudan at the time of data collection were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using a formula for comparing two independent proportions, appropriate for assessing differences between medical students and junior doctors. With a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and 80% statistical power (β = 0.20), assuming a conservative 10% absolute difference in app usage (e.g., 55% vs. 45%), a minimum of 392 participants per group (784 total) was required. The study’s achieved sample size of 2,945 participants significantly exceeds this minimum, ensuring ample statistical power to detect even subtle, clinically relevant differences between academic groups.

A non-probability convenience sampling method was employed. This method was chosen due to logistical constraints, limited resources, and the lack of a national database for medical students and physicians in Sudan, which made probability sampling challenging. To reduce selection bias and increase sample diversity, participants were recruited from multiple universities and hospitals through various online platforms.

Data collection tool and procedure

Data were gathered through a self-administered online questionnaire adapted from a previous study by Alhusseini et al. (2021) [12]. The questionnaire was modified to fit the educational and clinical context in Sudan. It includes sections on demographic information, patterns of mobile medical app use, perceived barriers to their use, and the self-reported impact of these applications on clinical decision-making. The final version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

A questionnaire was created and shared through Google Forms. To avoid duplicate responses, the “Limit to 1 response” feature was turned on. Branching logic was used to present participants only with questions relevant to their level, which improved response accuracy and completion rates.

Pilot testing and questionnaire validation

A pilot study was conducted with 10 clinical-phase medical students to evaluate the clarity, relevance, and understanding of the questionnaire items. Minor adjustments were made based on participants’ feedback. Data from the pilot phase were not included in the final analysis.

Data collection process

The survey link was distributed to an estimated 3400 potential participants across multiple social media platforms, including WhatsApp, Telegram, Facebook, Instagram, X (Twitter), and LinkedIn. These platforms were chosen for their broad use and accessibility to the target population. Data were collected over five weeks, from August 4, 2025, to September 9, 2025.

A team of trained data collectors distributed the questionnaire link to relevant populations to ensure that a wide range of study participants met the inclusion criteria. Online data collection was especially suitable given the ongoing security challenges posed by the war, thereby ensuring participant safety and continuous data collection.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Health Sector Ethical Review Committee at the University of Gezira, Wad Madani, Sudan. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before administering the questionnaire. The consent process ensured that participants fully understood the study’s purpose, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Additionally, confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study, and all data were securely stored and used only for research purposes. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using R (version 4.4.3). Descriptive statistics summarized the characteristics of the study population and the responses of the study participants. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Grouped pie charts and diverging stacked bars were used for visualization. The data were stratified by academic group (“Medical students” vs. “House officers & GP doctors”) for comparative analysis. The association between these categorical variables and the two academic groups was assessed using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Fisher’s exact test was used as an alternative when the expected cell count was low. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all bivariate analyses.

Results

A total of 2,945 participants completed the survey out of 3400 invitations distributed, resulting in a response rate of 86.0%. The sample included medical students (n = 1,952) and house officers or general practitioners (n = 993). Most were aged 24–26 years (47.0%), female (66.0%), lived in urban areas (76.0%), and had previously taken a course or workshop on evidence-based medicine (57.0%) (see Table 1).

Usage patterns and awareness of mobile medical applications

To address the first research question, we evaluated the foundational engagement with mobile medical applications, encompassing device ownership, awareness of specific tools, and the actual frequency of their use in clinical and academic settings. Overall, 2775 respondents (94.0%) owned a smartphone or tablet suitable for running medical apps, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.2). A total of 2,210 (80.0%) participants reported using a mobile medical app during clinical practice or training, with significantly higher use among house officers/GP doctors (89.0%) compared to medical students (75.0%; p < 0.001). 60% of participants reported that instructors or supervisors had encouraged app use, with higher encouragement observed among house officers/GP doctors (65.0% vs. 57.0%; p < 0.001). Interest in attending a short workshop on the effective use of medical apps was very high (86.0%) and similar across the groups (p = 0.9) (Table 2). Additionally, most respondents primarily or exclusively relied on mobile data (Fig. 1).

Figure 2 illustrates that actual usage significantly increases with clinical exposure and responsibility (Fig. 2). Among the 2,210 participants who used medical apps, Medscape was the most used app (50.0%), followed by anatomy/flashcard apps (47.0%), flashcard-specific apps (31.0%), and UpToDate (20.0%). House officers/GP doctors used Medscape (77.0% vs. 33.0%), UpToDate (30.0% vs. 13.0%), BMJ Best Practice (19.0% vs. 9.5%), and MDCalc (20.0% vs. 5.9%) much more often than medical students did (all p < 0.001). Conversely, medical students mainly used anatomy and flashcard apps (p < 0.001). The most common contexts for using these apps were general revision/academic studies (58.0%), review of clinical guidelines (48.0%), exam preparation (41.0%), and verifying drug dosages/interactions (38.0%). House officers/GP doctors more often used apps to review guidelines (65.0% vs. 37.0%), verify drug information (62.0% vs. 24.0%), select treatments (41.0% vs. 22.0%), and during on-call/emergency shifts (17.0% vs. 7.6%; all p < 0.001). In contrast, medical students used apps more for exam preparation (48.0% vs. 31.0%; p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Perceived barriers and challenges to app adoption

The second research question sought to identify the factors that hinder the effective use of these tools among Sudanese medical learners. The most common perceived barriers to app use (n = 2,775) were paid subscription requirements (48.0%), lack of awareness (26.0%), perceived lack of need (16.0%), and unstable Internet access (18.0%). Subscription cost was the main barrier in both groups, but the lack of awareness and perceived lack of need were significantly higher among medical students (p < 0.001) (Table 5). Among all device owners, the most frequent challenges were high subscription fees (32.0%), unstable Internet connection (30.0%), lack of training (17.0%), and information overload (15.0%). House officers/GP doctors reported significantly more problems with unstable Internet (35.0% vs. 27.0%) but fewer issues with difficult navigation (5.0% vs. 8.8%) and lack of training (13.0% vs. 19.0%; all p < 0.001). Regarding English-language difficulty, 55.0% found the language in the apps easy to understand, 29.0% found it sometimes difficult, and 9.5% found it challenging. Medical students reported more language difficulties than house officers/GP doctors (p < 0.001). Among those reporting any language difficulty (n = 1,060), 27.0% said it significantly hindered their use or understanding of the apps, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.7) (Table 5).

Perceived impact on clinical decision-making and learning

Finally, to evaluate the perceived impact of mobile apps, participants were asked to rate their agreement with statements regarding clinical confidence and patient care. Both students and junior doctors expressed positive views of mobile medical apps, reporting that these tools were perceived as accurate, time-saving, confidence-boosting, and helpful for patient care (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The use of mobile medical apps among Sudanese medical students and practitioners was high: 80.0% of participants reported using them, with higher adoption among junior doctors than among medical students. This level of uptake mirrors adoption rates in Saudi Arabia and other high-adoption contexts (Bahrain, Hong Kong) [12, 13], while exceeding those reported in Egypt, Austria, Ethiopia, and Pakistan [8, 14–16]. This pattern suggests that the use of mobile medical apps may be more closely linked to professional needs and peer influence than to national economic conditions. Despite Sudan’s unique economic challenges, its adoption aligns with regional trends, underscoring the universal appeal of mobile health technologies across diverse cultural and recontexironments.

The differences among the participant groups highlight different uses. Junior doctors showed greater awareness of clinically focused apps such as Medscape, UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, and MDCalc, while medical students preferred anatomy and flashcard tools. This difference reflects the progression from foundational knowledge acquisition to the application of clinical tools, a transition that universities can intentionally support through structures for digital learning tools. Notably, most participants learned about these apps through friends and colleagues rather than official channels; 57.0% learned from peers, compared to only 26.0% from university lectures. Unlike another study, where 55.6% cited friends and colleagues as their main source of information, university faculty were the second most common at 51.7% [17]. This points to a gap in institutional dissemination in Sudan. In Egypt, Alsharif et al. [14] linked increased app use to mandatory e‑learning during the COVID‑19 lockdown, while in Sudan, adoption mainly came from informal peer networks. This contrast shows how different policies influence digital learning behaviors.

Awareness and usage patterns were consistent. Medscape emerged as the most frequently used app, a finding that aligns with a scoping review by Lee et al. [18], which identified it as the most used mobile medical app among physicians. Anatomy and flashcard tools were used, reflecting participants’ reported familiarity. Regional comparisons further highlight contextual differences: in Ethiopia [8], physicians favored UpToDate, Medscape, and MedCalc, whereas in Canada [19], UpToDate, Epocrates, and Medscape were most popular. These variations may reflect differences in the clinical environment and the availability of institutional subscriptions. In Sudan, reliance on free platforms underscores affordability as a likely factor. Similarly to Pakistan, where the cost of medical app subscriptions is a concern in countries experiencing rising inflation rates [16].

The main purposes of app use were academic review, exam prep, clinical guideline review, and verification of drug dosages and interactions. Nearly half of the participants reported paid subscriptions as the main barrier, highlighting how costs limit access. Along with the finding that most awareness comes from peer networks, this emphasizes the lack of institutional support, such as university access. Addressing this gap can improve fairness in digital learning resources.

While a minority of participants reported language difficulties, these appeared to be secondary perceived barriers. Consistent with Boruff et al. [19], the main limitations to mobile resource use are related to access and awareness, rather than technological or intellectual challenges.

Participants reported positive attitudes toward mobile medical applications, which may present a valuable opportunity to integrate digital tools more systematically into medical education in Sudan. Unlike findings from Malaysia (Jembai et al.) [20], positive attitudes did not lead to widespread use in medical training, suggesting that formal strategies are needed to bridge the gap between students’ perceived awareness and structured educational embedding. Providing students and junior doctors with a wider variety of applications, from interactive visual models to evidence-based decision-making tools, could support their academic learning and self-reported educational confidence.

Implications for medical education

Despite participants reporting high awareness of mobile medical apps, their knowledge was primarily derived from peer networks and personal contacts rather than from formal academic channels. This represents a significant opportunity for universities to implement structured digital literacy programs that introduce students to high-quality, evidence-based resources, thereby supporting and enriching their learning process. The most frequently reported barrier was the financial cost of premium app subscriptions, further highlighting a gap in institutional support. Universities can address this directly by negotiating institutional licenses or providing subsidized — or fully funded — access through university email accounts, ensuring more equitable and systematic integration of these tools into medical education.

Strengths and limitations

Our sample size of 2,945 participants is among the largest reported in studies of mobile medical apps in medical education and clinical practice, thereby boosting statistical power and enabling reliable comparisons across subgroups. Including participants from various stages of medical training, such as clinical-phase medical students, house officers, and general practitioners, facilitated meaningful comparisons across different levels of clinical exposure. Additionally, data were collected from multiple universities and hospitals across Sudan, thereby improving the representativeness of the findings and providing a broad national overview of mobile medical app usage. Furthermore, this study employed a comprehensive approach by assessing awareness, usage patterns, frequency, contexts of use, perceived barriers and challenges, attitudes, and the self-reported impact of mobile medical applications on clinical decision-making.

Our study has several limitations; the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between mobile medical application use and clinical decision-making outcomes. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce self-report and social desirability bias, as participants may overestimate their usage of mobile applications or report favorable behaviors. Due to security and logistical constraints, non-probability sampling was employed. While the response rate was high (86.0%), the use of social media for distribution may still introduce selection bias and restrict the generalizability of the findings to the entire population. The questionnaire was distributed via Google Forms and social media, which potentially excluded students without internet access or those in areas with poor connectivity, thereby underrepresenting the most vulnerable. It may have led to the overrepresentation of digitally engaged participants.

Future studies should investigate the factors influencing learners’ preferences for specific applications and use longitudinal or interventional designs to objectively evaluate the impact of mobile medical applications on clinical skills, decision-making, and patient care outcomes in the Sudanese context.

Conclusion

This study underscores the pivotal role of mobile health applications in supporting medical education and clinical practice among students and junior physicians in Sudan, as reported by the participants. Despite high awareness and positive perceptions of tools like Medscape and digital flashcards, significant barriers such as cost and institutional support limit equitable access. Addressing these perceived barriers through targeted, evidence-based strategies has the potential to enhance the integration of m-Health into medical training, thereby potentially supporting evidence-based practice and facilitating clinical reasoning. Implementing systematic interventions will be crucial to fully realize the perceived benefits of m-Health, ultimately aiming to support medical education and contribute to patient care quality in resource-limited settings.
