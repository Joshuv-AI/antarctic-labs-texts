Surgical Strategy for Atrial Functional Mitral Regurgitation With Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Chunrong Bao, Ke Wei, Dongfang Zhao, Junwen Zhang, Ju Mei, Nan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) combined with atrial fibrillation (AF) has become a significant clinical challenge. This study evaluates surgical treatment strategies based on the degree of MR and rhythm control therapy for patients with AFMR and AF.MethodsThis retrospective study included 145 patients with AF and MR from January 2017 to January 2023. Patients were grouped as follows: moderate atrial regurgitation (n=33), severe atrial regurgitation (SAR, n=56), and severe primary regurgitation (SPR, n=56). The group with moderate atrial regurgitation underwent thoracoscopic AF procedure with a unilateral approach, and the SAR and SPR groups received mitral valvuloplasty plus the Cox‐Maze IV procedure. Descriptive characteristics and treatment outcomes were analyzed.ResultsAt an average follow‐up of 2.6±1.1 years, 23 patients in the group with moderate atrial regurgitation maintained sinus rhythm. MR improved in 26 patients, remained unchanged in 6 patients, and worsened in 1 patient. Sinus rhythm maintenance was associated with MR reduction (P=0.0002). The rate of sinus rhythm maintenance in the groups with SAR and SPR was similar (SAR 79.6%, SPR 87.5%). Echocardiography in the group with SAR showed 47 patients with no MR, 4 with mild MR, and 1 with moderate MR. The left atrial diameter in the group with SAR remained larger than in the group with SPR (P<0.001).ConclusionsRhythm control therapy is essential for treating AFMR with AF. The thoracoscopic AF procedure is effective and minimally invasive for moderate AFMR. For severe AFMR, Cox‐Maze IV combined with mitral valvuloplasty provides optimal safety and effectiveness.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, atrial functional mitral regurgitation, Cox‐Maze IV procedure, thoracoscopic ablationSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Valvular Heart Disease, Remodeling, Cardiovascular Surgery, Complications

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Minimally invasive approaches for moderate cases and combined Cox‐Maze IV with mitral valvuloplasty for severe cases may optimize management and improve outcomes in patients with atrial functional mitral regurgitation.

Atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) is receiving increasing attention as a type of functional MR secondary to structural or functional abnormalities of the left atrium. 1 AFMR occurs in the context of left atrial enlargement and myopathy, leading to mitral annulus dilation, while left ventricular (LV) size and systolic function remain relatively preserved. 2 Clinically, it is more common in patients with nonparoxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF). 3 , 4 Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of moderate to severe AFMR in patients with persistent AF is 4% 8%, rising to 28% in those with AF lasting >10 years. 5 , 6 With the growing prevalence of AF, AFMR combined with AF is expected to become a common clinical issue. However, there is still a lack of research (particularly surgical studies) addressing this combination. 7 , 8 Using a single treatment modality for MR with functional regurgitation is often unsatisfactory. Results from catheter ablation studies suggest that restoring sinus rhythm (SR) can reduce valve regurgitation through cardiac remodeling. However, the success rate of catheter ablation and the certainty of cardiac remodeling in reducing regurgitation remain concerning. We have summarized data from patients with AFMR and AF treated at our center, employing different treatment strategies based on the degree of MR alongside rhythm control therapy.

Patient Selection and Characteristics

This retrospective cohort study included 145 patients with AF and MR treated at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from January 2017 to January 2023. Thirty‐three patients with AF and moderate AFMR were designated as the group with moderate atrial regurgitation (MAR); 56 patients with AF and severe AFMR were designated as the group with severe atrial regurgitation (SAR). The remaining 56 patients with AF and severe primary MR were designated as the group with severe primary regurgitation (SPR). All patients received appropriate rate control, antihypertensives, and diuretic therapy before preoperative cardiac echo examination. The diagnostic criteria for AFMR are based on relevant literature: exclusion of organic mitral valve disease, LV ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥50%, no abnormal LV wall systolic activity, and LV end‐diastolic diameter <55 mm. 9 , 10 The degree of MR was diagnosed according to guidelines and confirmed by 2 physicians.

The group with MAR included 20 men and 13 women, with an average age±SD of 70.27±6.46 years. Four cases had persistent AF and 29 had long‐term persistent AF. The history of AF averaged 7.12±4.83 years, and the median CHA2DS2–VASc score was 3 points (0–6). Preoperative echocardiography showed moderate MR in 33 cases. The left atrium diameter was 48.15±4.19 mm, the LV end‐diastolic diameter was 49.91±3.29 mm, and LVEF was 56.39%±4.90%. Preoperative cardiac function was New York Heart Association grade I in 1 case, grade II in 17 cases, grade III in 12 cases, and grade IV in 3 cases. There were 22 patients with tricuspid regurgitation, 8 with coronary heart disease, 19 with hypertension, 10 with diabetes, and 3 with a history of stroke. Three patients had undergone catheter ablation. Baseline characteristics of the group with MAR are described in Table 1.

In the group with SAR, there were 37 men and 19 women, with an average age of 70.54±5.91 years. All patients had long‐term persistent AF with a history of 6.10±4.32 years. The median CHA2DS2–VASc score was 3 points (3–4). Thirty‐eight patients had moderate or above tricuspid regurgitation, 15 had coronary heart disease, 42 had hypertension, 15 had diabetes, and 7 had a history of stroke. Four patients had a history of catheter ablation.

In the group with SPR, there were 36 men and 20 women, with an average age of 66.43±6.39 years. Forty‐four patients had long‐term persistent AF, and 12 had persistent AF, with an AF history of 4.44±2.58 years. The median CHA2DS2–VASc score was 2 points (2–3). There were 23 patients with moderate or above tricuspid regurgitation, 6 with coronary heart disease, 27 with hypertension, 9 with diabetes, and 4 with a history of stroke. One patient had a history of catheter ablation. Baseline characteristics of both groups are described in Table 1.

CHA₂DS₂‐VASc refers to a stroke risk scoring system for AF. New York Heart Association grades classify heart failure severity from Class I (asymptomatic) to Class IV (symptomatic at rest). Persistent AF is defined as AF lasting >7 days or requiring pharmacological or electrical cardioversion to restore normal SR.

Surgical Procedures

All patients in the group with MAR underwent thoracoscopic AF procedure via a unilateral approach as previously described. 11 , 12 After anesthesia, patients were positioned in the right lateral decubitus position. Chest wall incisions were made at the sixth, seventh, eighth, or sixth, eighth, and eighth intercostal spaces near the inferior angle of the left scapula. The ablation routes included ablation of the left and right pulmonary veins, the posterior wall of the left atrium, the ablation line from the left pulmonary vein to the anterior mitral annulus (Dallas lesion), the ablation line from the left pulmonary vein to the resection edge of the left atrial appendage, and the ablation line between the 2 inferior pulmonary veins. The left atrial appendage was either resected with a stapler or clamped with an atrial appendage clip. Marshall's ligament and epicardial autonomic ganglia were ablated by ablation pen. If AF could not be terminated after all steps, cardioversion was performed.

Patients in the groups with SAR and SPR underwent mitral valvuloplasty plus the Cox‐Maze IV procedure (CMP IV). These procedures were performed under general anesthesia and extracorporeal circulation. A minimally invasive approach was used to establish extracorporeal circulation through the femoral artery and vein, with a small incision made in the fourth intercostal space on the right chest wall. All mitral annuloplasties were performed with a prosthetic ring. Additional techniques included artificial chordae, partial excision of the posterior leaflets, junctional suturing, and edge‐to‐edge suturing. If mitral annuloplasty failed, mitral valve replacement was performed. CMP IV included standard ablation routes for both the left and right atria, completed with a bipolar clamp, with isthmus ablation supplemented by cryoenergy. The left atrial appendage was resected or sutured in a double‐layered linear fashion. Concomitant procedures included tricuspid valvuloplasty and coronary artery bypass grafting.

Postoperative Care and Follow‐Up

Postoperatively, all patients received anticoagulation therapy with warfarin. If the patient's heart rate exceeded 70 beats/min, oral amiodarone was to be administered for 3 months. Patients unable to maintain SR were treated with rate control. Recurrent AF was managed with cardioversion under adequate anticoagulation. Follow‐up was conducted through outpatient visits and telephone calls, including symptom evaluation and 24‐hour dynamic electrocardiograms at 3, 6, and 12 months and every 6 months thereafter. Any documented atrial tachyarrhythmia episodes lasting 30 seconds or longer were considered a recurrence after a 3‐month blanking period. Postoperative mitral valve function was assessed by echocardiography at 3, 6, and 12 months and at least annually thereafter.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.2). Measurements are expressed as mean±SD and compared using the t test. Fisher exact test was used to assess categorical data. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate event‐free probabilities, with intergroup comparisons performed using log‐rank tests. The P values for the log‐rank tests were calculated using the logrank_test() function from the ‘coin’ package in R. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed for multivariate risk analysis. We used a significance threshold of P≤0.05, indicating statistical significance at the 95% confidence level. Left atrial diameter values over time are shown in box plots, with median, quartiles, and outliers. Differences were tested using repeated measures ANOVA. Changes in MR severity are displayed in stacked bar charts, indicating patient counts and percentages. Differences across stages were analyzed using χ2 tests, with statistical significance set at P<0.05. Factors associated with recurrence following AF ablation were selected based on an extensive literature review, clinical experience, and univariate analysis, with variables identified using a P value threshold of ≤0.1 for inclusion in multivariate models. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to evaluate risk factors for AF recurrence, providing a comprehensive analysis of potential predictors.

The null hypothesis posited no difference in SR maintenance rates, AF recurrence rates, or changes in MR severity across the groups with MAR, SAR, and SPR. The Fisher exact test and log‐rank test were applied to test these hypotheses. Data were independently verified by 2 reviewers to minimize bias. (For further summary and additional information, please refer to Tables S1 and S2).

RESULTS

Clinical Outcomes of AF Combined With Moderate AFMR

Thoracoscopic AF procedure via a unilateral approach was successfully performed in all patients in the group with MAR. There was no conversion to thoracotomy and no perioperative mortality. No blood products were transfused, and no serious postoperative complications occurred. SR was maintained in 30 patients (90.9%) at discharge. Echocardiography before discharge showed no MR in 18 cases, mild MR in 10 cases, and moderate MR in 4 cases. The left atrium diameter (47.06±3.99 mm), LV end‐diastolic diameter (49.58±3.16 mm), and LVEF (55.73%±5.40%) were not statistically different from preprocedure measurements.

The last follow‐up occurred in June 2023, with durations ranging from 1 to 6 years (average: 2.6±1.1 years). Twenty‐three patients (69.70%) maintained SR, confirmed by 24‐hour eECG. The Kaplan–Meier curve (Figure 1A) demonstrates freedom from AF recurrence over the follow‐up period. At the last follow‐up, 17 patients had no MR, 9 had mild MR, 6 had moderate MR, and 1 had severe MR. The degree of regurgitation improved in 26 patients compared with presurgery levels, remained unchanged in 6 patients, and worsened in 1 patient. The left atrial diameter (46.42±4.40 mm), LV end‐diastolic diameter (49.94±3.32 mm), and LVEF (58.64%±3.88%) were not statistically different from preprocedure measurements.

Effects of Rhythm Control on Cardiac Remodeling and Mitral Regurgitation in the Group With MAR

Patients in the group with MAR were divided into 2 cohorts based on their ability to maintain SR post surgery. SR maintenance significantly benefited MR reduction (P=0.0002) compared with the patients without SR (Figure 1B). The left atrial diameters of patients with and without SR were 47.26±4.06 mm versus 50.20±3.94 mm before surgery (P=0.063) and 46.48±4.00 mm versus 48.40±3.84 mm at discharge (P=0.209), showing no statistically significant difference between the groups. At the last follow‐up, the left atrial diameter was 45.04±3.48 mm in patients with SR versus 49.60±4.81 mm in patients without SR (P=0.004), indicating a statistically significant difference (Figure 2A). Clinical Outcomes of AF Combined With Severe AFMR

All 56 patients in the group with SAR completed the valve procedure, CMP IV, and other concomitant procedures. Two patients died within 30 days post surgery due to postoperative acure respiratory distress syndrome and pulmonary infection. Postoperative complications included bleeding, low cardiac output, pulmonary infection, and acute kidney injury. Three cases (5%) required postoperative electrical cardioversion therapy. At discharge, 50 patients (92.6%) were in SR; 50 cases had no MR, and 2 had mild MR. The left atrial diameter was 48.31±2.60 mm, significantly smaller than presurgery (P<0.001), whereas the LV end‐diastolic diameter (49.41±2.93 mm, P=0.056) and LVEF (54.87%±5.46%, P=0.385) showed no significant changes from presurgery.

The mean follow‐up period was 2.6±1.1 years. At the last follow‐up, 43 patients (79.6%) in the group with SAR maintained SR (Kaplan–Meier curve shown in Figure 3). Echocardiography showed no MR in 47 cases, mild MR in 4 cases, and moderate MR in 1 case. The left atrial diameter was 48.46±3.80 mm, significantly smaller than presurgery (P<0.001), whereas the LV end‐diastolic diameter (51.52±3.54 mm, P=0.225) and LVEF (54.80%±4.93%, P=0.310) showed no significant differences from presurgery measurements.

Analysis of Risk Factors for Recurrence of AF

Cox multivariate analysis indicated that AF recurrence in the group with MAR was associated with the absence of regurgitation reduction (hazard ratio [HR], 0.0369 [95% CI, 0.0026–0.5156]; P=0.0142) and with a decrease in left atrial diameter (HR, 0.01865 [95% CI, 0.0007–0.5016]; P=0.0177). Those associations were independent of age, CHA₂DS₂–VASc score, and both preoperative and postoperative left atrial diameter. In severe MR, the risk factors for AF recurrence in the group with SAR included a postoperative left atrial diameter >50 mm (HR, 4.081 [95% CI, 1.109–15.016]; P=0.0344). No association was found with age, AF duration, CHA₂DS₂–VASc score, preoperative left atrial diameter, or residual MR. In the group with SPR, AF recurrence was associated with a postoperative left atrial diameter >50 mm (HR, 12.480 [95% CI, 1.288–120.898]; P=0.0294) and residual MR (HR, 16.358 [95% CI, 1.604–166.850]; P=0.0183). No association was found with age, AF duration, CHA₂DS₂–VASc score, or preoperative left atrial diameter. This study did not identify risk factors for the recurrence of MR in either group.

DISCUSSION

AF and MR are progressive conditions that exacerbate each other, leading to more severe symptoms, rapid disease progression, and serious consequences such as heart failure. 13 Timely and effective treatment is particularly important when AF is combined with MR. 14 We selected different treatment strategies based primarily on the degree of MR, with a primary focus on treating AF. Our data suggest that for moderate AFMR combined with AF, rhythm control via thoracoscopic AF procedure yields satisfactory results. Maintenance of SR improves cardiac remodeling and reduces MR.

For severe AFMR combined with AF, we employed a combination of rhythm control and valvular intervention using the classic CMP IV surgical technique in conjunction with mitral valvuloplasty. This approach aligns with standard surgical guidelines for treating AF combined with severe MR. However, most previous evidence pertains to primary MR combined with AF, not AFMR combined with AF. 15 , 16 Our study demonstrates that the safety and efficacy of CMP IV combined with mitral valvuloplasty for severe AFMR with AF are comparable to the outcomes in primary MR with AF. AF is the initiating factor of AFMR, making it the primary target for treatment. In cases of functional MR, merely treating the regurgitation without addressing the primary disease often fails to yield favorable outcomes. 17 In cases of moderate MR, the structural changes in the atrium and mitral valve are reversible. 18 , 19 Although rhythm control may reduce or eliminate MR through cardiac remodeling, treatment efficiency remains unsatisfactory. 20 , 21 The effectiveness of rhythm control is crucial in the management of AF. However, a large proportion of patients with AFMR have long‐standing persistent AF, significant left atrial enlargement, high CHA₂DS₂–VASc scores, and other factors that impede successful treatment or maintenance of SR. 22 Previous research has shown that thoracoscopic surgery may be more effective than catheter ablation in SR maintenance. 23 The thoracoscopic technique provides high‐quality pulmonary vein ablation, directly ablates the Marshall ligament and autonomic ganglia from the epicardium, and enables left atrial appendage excision. Our modified procedure includes isolation of the left atrial posterior wall to further improve success rates. 24 Our data show favorable results for patients with AF and moderate AFMR, although subsequent catheter ablation may be required for patients with unsuccessful outcomes. This approach is reasonable for patients with AFMR who require a more robust method to maintain SR and interrupt the cycle of AF, MR, and heart failure. 25

Patients with severe AFMR present greater management challenges for clinicians. We therefore used CMP IV, a classic surgical technique for treating AF, which has the highest single success rate in clinical practice. Despite their increased age, complexity, and potential complications, patients with severe AFMR still require treatment options that offer a high degree of success in maintaining SR. As such, these patients would benefit the most from individualized ablation strategies or further minimally invasive procedures.

AFMR is MR secondary to structural or functional abnormalities of the left atrium, leading to mitral annulus enlargement and an imbalance between annulus and leaflet area. Although functional in nature, AFMR may still involve abnormal mitral valvular structure. 26 , 27 Studies suggest that there is potential for mitral valve with structural abnormalities to recover with improved cardiac remodeling. 28 However, for most patients with severe AFMR, surgical valvuloplasty is a necessity due to underlying structural abnormalities. 29 , 30 In patients with AFMR, even with successful AF treatment, cardiac remodeling alone may not achieve the necessary changes in the left atrium and mitral valve. The extent of cardiac remodeling by SR maintenance may be a key factor. From the perspective of AF management, patients with AFMR and AF are often refractory to catheter ablation, thoracoscopic AF procedure, and even hybrid procedures. Therefore, treatment with CMP IV should be prioritized, as it is more reasonable to undertake concomitant mitral valvuloplasty for its more definitive effect, rather than relying on cardiac remodeling with uncertain outcomes in MR. 31 According to current guidelines, severe MR should be treated from a valvular management perspective. Without treatment, residual mitral regurgitation will self‐progress and increase the inducibility of AF in the long run. 32

Limitations

Although this study addresses an important topic with a paucity of data on the impact of therapy, it is constrained by its relatively small sample size and single‐center retrospective design. Potential bias includes selection bias inherent to the single‐center retrospective design, which may limit the generalizability of findings. Subjective bias in data interpretation was minimized through independent confirmation by 2 reviewers. The sample size was limited due to the exclusion of patients with organic mitral valve disease (such as prolapse, flail leaflet, rheumatic disease, or mitral leaflet calcification) and the selection of patients with normal LV morphology and function to minimize the interference caused by ventricular functional regurgitation. Propensity score matching could not be applied to compare the groups with SAR and SPR due to the limited sample size. Patients with AFMR tend to be older, frailer, have more comorbidities, and have a longer AF duration. These characteristics may be less apparent after propensity score matching.

CONCLUSIONS

Because AF is the primary cause of AFMR with AF, rhythm control therapy is the cornerstone treatment for these patients. Thoracoscopic AF procedure is an effective and minimally invasive treatment option for patients with AF and moderate AFMR. Moderate AFMR can be potentially reduced by SR restoration, which promotes cardiac remodeling.

For patients with severe AFMR and AF, we recommend concomitant CMP IV with mitral valvuloplasty. This approach is both safe and effective, increasing the success rate of resolving both AF and MR.
