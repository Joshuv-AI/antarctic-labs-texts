Patterns and clusters of diet in relation to metabolic syndrome among adults in Ubungo (urban) and Kilindi (rural) districts, Tanzania: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Linda S. Paulo, Gonçalo J. Piedade, Simeon Mayala, Vanessa Harris, Virissa C. Lenters, Daniel R. Mende, Appolinary Kamuhabwa, Gideon Kwesigabo, Folkert W. Asselbergs, Pilly Chillo, K. Klipstein-Grobusch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) is rising worldwide, with particularly high rates in regions undergoing rapid urbanization and dietary change, such as Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). However, few studies have examined the relationship between dietary patterns, dietary clusters, and MetS in these transitioning communities.ObjectivesTo examine the associations between dietary patterns, dietary clusters, and MetS in urban and rural Tanzania.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 379 adults in urban (Ubungo, Dar es Salaam) and rural (Kilindi, Tanga) districts. Data on demographics, medical history, and lifestyle factors were collected using validated tools. Anthropometric, blood pressure, and biochemical measurements followed the WHO STEPS survey protocols. Dietary habits were assessed using a food frequency questionnaire; dietary patterns were derived by principal component analysis (PCA) and dietary clusters by hierarchical clustering on the principal components (HCPC). Logistic regression models were used to assess associations between diet, lifestyle factors, and MetS/MetS components.ResultsPrincipal components analysis identified eight dietary patterns, including mixed, plant-rich, and processed diets. Hierarchical clustering on principal components classified participants into three dietary clusters: rural non-diverse, mixed unhealthy, and urban mixed diets. The prevalence of MetS was 47% (95% CI: 39—54) in urban areas and 38% (95%CI: 32 – 46) in rural areas (p = 0.11). High adherence (quartile 4) to the mixed diet—pattern 1 was associated with MetS. While dietary clusters were not significantly associated with MetS overall, the mixed unhealthy diet (cluster 2) was associated with two MetS components—central obesity and dyslipidemia.ConclusionThe prevalence of MetS is high in both urban and rural Tanzania, with no statistically significant difference between the two settings. Specific mixed unhealthy dietary patterns and clusters were associated with MetS, dyslipidemia and central obesity. Public health measures to address unhealthy dietary patterns and the burden of MetS are needed in both rural and urban Tanzania.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25653-1.

Background

Metabolic syndrome (MetS)—defined as the presence of any three of five risk factors -hypertension, central obesity, high triglycerides/low high-density lipoproteins, impaired glucose regulation, or microalbuminuria—increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and other chronic conditions [1–4]. While its clinical and epidemiological value is still debated, it remains a useful tool for assessing population risk of cardiovascular diseases [5–7].

The prevalence of MetS is rising rapidly worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including South Asia, China, Brazil, and sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [8–12]. In SSA, studies published between 2006–2019 estimate a prevalence of 11.1—23.9% using International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria [10]. A more recent review from 2023 estimated a prevalence of 32.4% for African countries using data collected between 1985 and 2021 [13].

Urbanization in SSA is a major driver of this increase, as it is associated with hypertension, dyslipidemia, central obesity, and type 2 diabetes [14]. Urban populations consistently show a higher burden of MetS than rural populations, but this gap is narrowing due to rural—urban migration, technological advancement, and dietary transitions [15]. These transitions – marked by a shift from minimally processed, fiber-rich foods to energy-dense, highly processed foods [15–19] are a major contributor to non-communicable diseases (NCDs) risk. However, in SSA countries such as Tanzania, empirical evidence directly linking dietary patterns or clusters to MetS remains scarce.

Dietary patterns are often derived using PCA, which reduces dietary data into a smaller set of components. However, PCA-based approaches face challenges, including subjectivity in component retention, low variance explained in heterogeneous diets, and loss of information when few components are selected [20–22]. Hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC), as proposed by Maugeri et al. (2022), integrates PCA with clustering to improve interpretability and capture more variability [23]. While HCPC has been used in other fields, it remains underutilized in nutritional epidemiology, particularly in SSA. Other dietary assessment methods, such as dietary quality indices are increasingly used in nutrition research [24]. However, these indices have not been validated for use in Tanzania and may not adequately reflect local variations shaped by cultural, economic, and geographical factors [25]. Therefore, PCA and HCPC were applied in this study to identify dietary habits that capture population differences between the two districts.

Few studies have examined how dietary patterns or clusters relate to MetS in both rural and urban African settings. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by assessing the association between empirically derived dietary patterns and clusters and MetS and its components in rural and urban Tanzania. We hypothesised that MetS prevalence and its components would be higher in urban areas and differ by dietary habits.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The data for this study was collected cross-sectionally using a two-stage sampling approach in January and February 2022. First, 4 wards were randomly selected (2 wards in Kilindi and 2 wards in Ubungo districts) and thereafter one person at the household level. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with respondents. Kilindi and Ubungo districts were randomly selected from Dar es Salaam and Tanga regions in the east and northeastern parts of Tanzania respectively. Ubungo district is found in the South-Eastern part of the city at 6°47′20″S 39°12′20″E and borders Kinondoni district on the South-East and the Pwani region on the West and North. It has 14 wards and 90 streets (sub-wards) with a population of 1 million people (499,161 men and 532,188 women). Residents in Ubungo districts like other urban districts of the Dar es Salaam region are mostly migrants from other regions of Tanzania and have lost their traditional lifestyles including diets.

Kilindi district in the Tanga Region represents a rural population where the dietary and lifestyle transition is at an intermediate level compared to urban or the hunters and gatherers communities. It has a total population of 398,391 according to the 2022 census spread over 21 wards. It borders the Handeni district on the East, Kiteto district on the North, and the Morogoro region on the Southern side [26]. Kilindi rural district was selected due to its relatively preserved dietary pattern and peasants’/cattle keepers mix which represents other rural areas in Tanzania.

This manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology – Nutritional Epidemiology (STROBE-nut): An Extension of the STROBE statement guideline for reporting cross-sectional studies [27]. Dietary data was collected using a previously validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [28] while interviews, anthropometrics, physical activity, and biochemical measurements were done using WHO STEPS survey guidelines previously used in Tanzania [29–32].

Study population and eligibility

Men and women aged 44–65 years were recruited at household level. For households with two or more eligible participants, priority was given to the male family member. This was done purposefully as females were overrepresented. Where more than one respondent of the same sex qualified for the interview, each was given a number, and a random number generator was used to select the respondent. Recruitment of the study participants was done on the first day where demographic, medical history, lifestyle, 24-h dietary recall, and FFQ data were collected. Blood pressure was also measured on the first day. Those with chronic diseases not related to MetS were excluded from the study including malignancies and Tuberculosis.

Variables

Primary outcome variable

The primary outcome variable for this study is MetS defined as a combination of central obesity and any two out of the following factors as per the IDF criteria i. raised triglycerides level: > 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L), or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality ii. reduced High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol: < 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in males and < 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) in females, or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality iii. raised blood pressure (BP): systolic BP ≥ 130 or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mm Hg (considered the 3rd measurement only), or treatment of previously diagnosed hypertension or iv. raised fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or previously diagnosed type 2 diabetes.

Secondary outcome variables

Blood pressure and hypertension

Blood pressure was measured following the steps outlined in the WHO STEPS survey guidelines [33], using the Omron M2 Basic automatic upper arm blood pressure monitor. Respondents were instructed to remain seated quietly for 15 min before initiating measurements. The measurements were done 3 times, each taken at an interval of 3 min. The mean of the second and third values was recorded as the final BP level for each respondent. The classifications were based on WHO criteria, with the following categories: normal – systolic blood pressure (SBP) < 120 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) < 80 mmHg, elevated blood pressure—SBP 120 mmHg – 129 mmHg and DBP – less than 80 mmHg, stage 1 hypertension—SBP 130–139 mmHg & DBP 80–89 mmHg, and stage 2 hypertension SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. The Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) was calculated by adding the DBP to the one-third of the difference between SBP and DBP i.e. DBP + 1/3(SBP – DBP).

Biological samples—fasting blood glucose and lipids

Other outcomes included diabetes – defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L, dyslipidemia—high triglycerides > 2.30 mmol/L and/or low HDL < 1.04 mmol/L or previous diagnosis and treatment of any of these abnormalities. Respondents were instructed to do an overnight fast, refraining from eating after dinner or on the morning of blood sample collection. A total of 4 ml of blood were drawn, and a drop was used to test fasting blood glucose levels using a glucometer. The remaining sample was put in a vacutainer and stored in a cooler box at 8℃ and transported to the laboratory. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged and then stored at −20 ℃ until the time of testing. Lipid measurements were done using the Cobas Integra 400 Plus Chemistry Analyser.

Physical measurements—weight, height, waist and hip circumference

Anthropometric measurements including weight, height, waist, and hip circumference followed procedures outlined by the WHO STEPS survey and as previously used in the Tanzanian STEPS survey [32, 33]. Central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 94 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on weight and height (weight in kilograms/height2 in meters). The BMI was categorized as per WHO classification into; less than 18.5 – underweight, 18.5–24.9 normal, 25–29.9 overweight, and more than 30 – obese.Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was used to reflect the health risk based on sex-specific criteria as specified by the WHO. For females, WHR was categorized as ≤ 0.8 – low risk, 0.81–0.85 – moderate risk, and ≥ 0.85 – high risk. Among males, the values were ≤ 0.90 – low risk, 0.91–1.0 – moderate risk, and ≥ 1.0 – high risk.

Other variables

Demographic and lifestyle variables

Demographic and lifestyle variables were measured including age, sex, marital status, education level, income, alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking. Income was assessed by asking participants the amount of their weekly, monthly, or yearly monetary income as previously used in Tanzania’s STEPS survey. The reported income was classified into 5 levels; 1 – less than 250,000 (~ 100 USD) per year, 2—> 250,000–499,999, 3- 500,000–749,000, 4–750,000–999,999, 5—≥ 1,000,000 per year, 6- Don’t know or refused to answer. The final levels were obtained by recategorizing 1–2 – low, 3–4 – middle, and 5- high-income levels. Physical activity was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) previously adapted for use in Tanzania [29].

Dietary patterns/clusters assessment

Diet was assessed using an existing validated 30-day Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) with 179 food items [28]. Respondents were asked to recall if they ate the listed food items, the frequency of consumption per week, the usual amount consumed per meal, and supplement use. To assist with the estimation of portion sizes, participants were shown a physical utensil model to select the usual one they use at home. The utensil models were sampled from the studied community in a pilot study. The total daily energy intake was estimated using portion sizes and corresponding energy values obtained from the Tanzania Food Composition Table (TFCT) multiplied by the reported frequencies [34]. All participants with an average energy intake exceeding 5000 or below 500 kcal were excluded from the analysis in line with the original study that validated the FFQ [28]. The matrix of the group frequencies was used to derive dietary patterns using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.85, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for PCA. The frequency counts were normalized using the total energy intake, standardized, and then analysed by PCA using the “prcomp” R software statistical package [35] with varimax rotation. We retained 8 principal components (PCs) based on Eigenvalues > 1 for the dietary patterns/clusters analysis (Supplemental file 1—Fig. 1).

The first three components, identified by the elbow method, explained 30.7% of the total variance in dietary intake. To capture more variability, we retained the first eight components with eigenvalues > 1, increasing the cumulative variance to 55%. Dietary patterns were named based on food groups with absolute factor loadings ≥ 0.2 (Supplemental file 1—Fig. 2), while dietary clusters were named using the mean z-scores of energy-standardized and normalized food group frequencies.

Maugeri et al. (2022) proposed the use of HCPC, which groups individuals based on their PC scores [23]. This approach has been applied in various forms in previous studies [23, 36, 37]. In this study, individuals were clustered based on Euclidean distance on the scores from all eight PCs. The elbow method indicated three optimal clusters. Cluster contents were defined by the mean z-scores of standardized food group frequencies, with positive mean z-scores used for naming. Associations between dietary patterns, dietary clusters, and MetS and its components were examined using logistic regression.

Data analysis and software

Data were downloaded from REDCap software [38, 39] and analyzed using R software statistical package [35]. Respondents missing all biochemical measurements (n = 74) were excluded from the analysis, while multiple imputations were used for those with fewer than two missing values. Continuous variables were assessed using mean and standard deviations while Pearson's Chi-squared test, Fisher's exact test or Wilcoxon rank sum test were used to compare the frequencies between categorical variables. Bivariate analysis was used to assess the relationship between the prevalence of MetS/MetS components with demographic factors and other outlined covariates. The selection of variables for inclusion in the final model was done based on having a p-value of < 0.2 in the univariable analysis or biological plausibility. Logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between MetS, its components, and dietary patterns/clusters. Results were reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Significance was considered present in case of a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

The overall prevalence of MetS/MetS components

There were no differences in the prevalence of MetS between rural and urban residents. Similar results were observed for hypertension and diabetes. The prevalence of dyslipidemia was higher in rural (63%, 95%CI: 56, 70) compared to urban (49%, 95%CI: 42, 57) residents (p = 0.006). All forms of obesity were more common in urban than rural residents. Central obesity affected 72% (95%CI: 65, 79) of urban vs 52% (95%CI: 45, 59) rural residents (p < 0.001). Association between dietary patterns and MetS/MetS components

Eight dietary patterns were identified using the first 8 PCs including pattern 1 (mixed diet), pattern 2 (plant-rich diet), pattern 3 (processed diet), pattern 4 (fish, potatoes and tubers rich diet), pattern 5 (alcohol and potatoes rich diet), pattern 6 (dairy, poultry, and sugar rich diet), pattern 7 (whole grains and meat rich diet), and pattern 8 (tubers, refined grains, and alcohol rich diet) (Supplemental file 1—Fig. 2). PC scores were divided into quartiles, with each compared to the first quartile (Q1) i.e. Q2/Q1, Q3/Q1 and Q4/Q1 in regression analysis. In multivariable analysis, those in the 4th quartile of dietary pattern 1 had higher odds of MetS [OR = 2.59, 95%CI: 1.34—5, p = 0.005] and central obesity [OR = 2.73, 95%CI: 1.26—5, p = 0.011] compared with Q1 (Fig. 3A). High adherence to dietary pattern 2 (Q4 vs Q1) was associated with higher odds of dyslipidemia [OR = 3.39, 95%CI: 1.77—5; < 0.001] (Fig. 3B). Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the associations between dietary patterns, dietary clusters, and Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) and its components among rural and urban populations in Tanzania. Dietary habits, assessed using PCA and HCPC, revealed eight dietary patterns and three distinct dietary clusters. Both approaches identified significant associations between diet and MetS or MetS components. High adherence to the mixed dietary pattern (DP1, Q4) was associated with both central obesity and MetS, whereas adherence to the plant-rich dietary pattern (DP 2, Q4) was associated with dyslipidemia. Of the dietary clusters, the mixed unhealthy diet dietary cluster (DC2) was associated with lower odds of dyslipidemia and higher odds of central obesity.

The overall prevalence of MetS was high, with no significant difference between urban and rural participants. Earlier studies in SSA, including East Africa, have reported higher MetS prevalence in urban than rural populations, contrary to our findings [10]. One explanation may be differences in diagnostic criteria. The IDF definition, used in this study, mandates central obesity as a prerequisite for MetS diagnosis, potentially underestimating prevalence in urban settings where obesity may not be accompanied by other MetS components. We also observed marked variation by district and sex, suggesting further caution in applying uniform diagnostic thresholds. In relation to diet, existing evidence has linked “healthy” dietary patterns with lower odds of MetS and to meat-based or western dietary patterns with increased risk [41]. In contrast, dietary pattern 1 in the present study—a mixed diet comprising both plant and meat-based foods was associated with higher odds of MetS among participants in the highest quartile of adherence.

The prevalence of hypertension exceeded 35% in both districts. The lack of difference in hypertension prevalence between districts contrasts with most SSA studies, which typically report higher rates in urban settings [42, 43]. But, compared with past surveys in the same regions of Dar es Salaam and Tanga (1988 and 2003), there is clear evidence of a rising and largely unaddressed burden of hypertension [44–46]. Previous studies undertaken in SSA including Zambia, Kenya and Tanzania reported an inverse relationship between hypertension and adherence to plant-based dietary patterns [47]. However, in the present study, despite the rural population showing strong adherence to a plant-rich pattern, no significant associations were observed between dietary patterns or clusters and hypertension. This suggests that other unmeasured factors beyond diet such as e.g. salt intake and sleep patterns may contribute to hypertension in both settings.

Consistent with prior SSA studies [48], central obesity and overall overweight/obesity were more common in urban than rural populations. High adherence to the mixed dietary pattern—high in refined grains, fried foods, and sugary foods was marginally associated with central obesity. Cluster 2 membership was also associated with central obesity, whereas no such relationship was found for cluster 3. This is likely explained by the urban dominance of cluster 2, in which many individuals also fell into the higher quartiles of adherence for the mixed dietary pattern. These results highlight the calorie-rich nature of certain urban dietary profiles, which may drive obesity without necessarily increasing other MetS components.

In rural areas, blood pressure showed a stronger correlation with waist circumference, while this relationship was weaker in urban populations, especially among women (Supplemental file 1—Fig. 3). Similar rural–urban and sex-based differences were observed for HDL, triglycerides, fasting glucose, and age. These patterns question the reliability of using uniform waist circumference cut-offs in diverse and transitioning populations. The lack of Africa-specific central obesity thresholds may have led to both misclassification and inaccurate prevalence estimates [11, 49]. Moreover, IDF criteria do not account for risk factors such as smoking, which while harmful to cardiovascular health can lower blood pressure [42].

The high prevalence of dyslipidemia observed in this study is consistent with findings from a systematic review of East African countries [50]. Similar to results from Western Tanzania [49], rural participants had higher rates than their urban counterparts, primarily due to low HDL levels, a trend repeatedly documented in rural–urban comparisons. Notably, dietary cluster 2, characterized by higher intakes of fried potatoes and tubers, poultry, juices, pasta, and bread were associated with lower odds of dyslipidemia in adjusted models. Comparable associations were found for dietary pattern 4 (rich in tubers, pasta, bread, fish, and fried potatoes) and 5 (potatoes and alcohol), despite their high caloric content. In this population, dyslipidemia was predominantly driven by low HDL, and adherence to these dietary patterns was linked to reduced odds of low HDL. While high carbohydrate intake has been associated with lower HDL and high fat intake with elevated LDL [51], LDL levels were not measured in this study, thus, the relationship observed remains inconclusive.

The low prevalence of type 2 diabetes observed in both districts (prevalence < 4%) aligns with earlier SSA studies using fasting blood glucose (FBG) measurements [52], though it is lower than the 2012 STEPS survey estimate for Tanzania of 9.1% [32]. In both rural and urban settings, FBG was positively correlated with waist circumference, with the relationship reaching statistical significance in urban participants. The rarity of diabetes despite high obesity prevalence may suggest protective factors such as higher physical activity levels or that the obesity threshold triggering diabetes onset is higher in this population. Due to limited data, the relationship between diabetes and dietary patterns/clusters was not assessed.

Study strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study in SSA to combine PCA and HCPC to derive dietary clusters and examine their associations with MetS. PCA is widely used to reduce dietary data into interpretable patterns, but it can lose important variability depending on the number of components retained. By applying HCPC to the first eight PCs, we grouped individuals based on both their dietary patterns and intake levels, capturing more variability and enabling clearer interpretation of diet-disease associations. This approach also addresses some limitations of PCA alone, such as the subjective selection of components. However, it cannot overcome the inherent limitations of the FFQ, including recall bias, misreporting, and the limited reproducibility of PCA-derived patterns.

Several other limitations should be noted including an incomplete lipid panel lacking LDL and total cholesterol measurements, which would have allowed a more comprehensive lipid profile assessment. Other key risk factors such as salt intake and sleep patterns were not measured, limiting the ability to fully explore hypertension determinants [53]. Additionally, the absence of validated waist circumference cut-offs for SSA populations may have influenced estimates of central obesity and MetS prevalence. Lastly, conducting multiple statistical tests, as done in this study, increases the likelihood of false-positive findings. However, given the exploratory nature of the analysis, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied consistent with methodological recommendations for hypothesis-generating studies [54].

Implications for nutrition policy, programs, and future research

The findings suggest a need for context-specific health and nutrition programming and policies. In urban areas, interventions could focus on improving the food environment by reducing production and consumption of processed foods. For rural areas, efforts should be on preventing the penetration of unhealthy foods and improving access to diverse and nutrient-rich foods. Given the diverse nature of diets in Tanzania, initiatives are needed to develop and validate more robust dietary assessment methods. Future population-wide representative longitudinal studies and interventions are needed to explore and address the primary causes of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and central obesity (in urban women).

Conclusion

The burden of MetS in Tanzania remains high across both rural and urban settings despite variations in dietary habits, with central obesity and dyslipidemia as key drivers. Further research and interventions are needed to address the growing prevalence of hypertension and other MetS risk factors – dyslipidemia and central obesity in both rural and urban settings. The associations between specific dietary patterns and clusters with dyslipidemia or central obesity suggest potential intervention targets, particularly in urban settings where high calorie and processed food consumption is common.
