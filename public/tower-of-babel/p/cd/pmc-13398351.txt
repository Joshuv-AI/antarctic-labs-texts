The pectoralis muscle orientation as an indicator of the modes of wing‐propelled locomotion in birds
Authors: Takumi Akeda, Shin‐ichi Fujiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the diversification of wingbeat utilisation for body propulsion among birds, it is essential to understand the relationship between skeletal morphology and flapping ability, depending on the wing depression exerted by the pectoralis muscle. The path between the origin and insertion of the pectoralis, which can be identified on the skeletal surface, indicates the orientation of the force it exerts. In this study, line segments were made within the pectoralis to represent the lines of muscle action (LoA). We then compared their overall orientation and distribution among different modes of wing‐propelled locomotion (WPL) using three‐dimensional skeletal models constructed from computed tomography and surface scans of 79 neornithine bird specimens. The species were selected to represent a diverse range of taxonomic groups, WPL modes, and body masses. The pectoralis LoAs in flap‐flight and wing‐propelled diving birds were oriented craniolaterally, whereas those in soaring birds were oriented more laterally. These differences in pectoralis profiles among species suggest a putative adaptation to generate forces more efficiently in the direction necessary for typical flapping styles. Therefore, the overall morphology of the thoracic skeleton, which reflects pectoral muscle orientation, can be an indicator of WPL mode. It can also be utilised as a more robust approach for reconstructing the locomotor capabilities of fossil taxa with avian‐like musculoskeletal systems.Keywords: 3D model, directional statistics, line of muscle action, neornithes, wing‐beat frequency

INTRODUCTION

Some animals that engage in forelimb fluid propelled locomotion, or wing‐propelled locomotion (WPL), such as birds (Neornithes, Theropoda), bats (Chiroptera, Mammalia), sea‐ and pig‐nosed turtles (Chelonioidea and Carettochelyidae, Testudinata), sea lions (Pinnipedia, Mammalia), and the extinct pterosaurs (Pterosauria, Archosauria) and plesiosaurs (Plesiosauria, Sauropterygia) have established ecological niches in both aerial and aquatic environments (Benton, 2014; Biewener & Patek, 2018). Birds are a particularly diverse group that acquired the ability of WPL during their evolutionary transition from terrestrial bipedal dinosaurs, subsequently diversifying WPL modes (Gatesy & Middleton, 1997). While many birds possess the ability to fly, some diving birds also use their wingbeats for swimming. In addition, several large raptors and seabirds utilise sea winds and updrafts to soar with minimal flapping. However, many bird lineages have ceased to use wingbeats for locomotion (Bruderer et al., 2010; Roots, 2006).

Modes of WPL for varying niches influenced diversification of morphological traits in birds. Previous studies have demonstrated that WPL modes generally depend on wing morphology, based on comparisons across broad phylogenetic groups of birds (Baliga et al., 2019; Rader et al., 2020; Rayner, 1988; Taylor & Thomas, 2014; Waldrop et al., 2020; Wang & Clarke, 2015). However, wingbeat motion is driven by the musculoskeletal system at the forelimb locomotor module; therefore, WPL modes may also depend on the musculoskeletal morphology of the shoulder girdle and forelimbs.

During bird wingbeats, the downstroke generates propulsion, primarily powered by contraction of the pectoralis muscle (Biewener, 2011). The pectoralis originates from the area surrounding the margins of the furcula, keel of the sternum, and caudolateral part of the sternum; runs along the ventral side of the coracoid shaft; and inserts into the deltopectoral crest of the humerus (Ashley, 1941; Berger, 1954, 1956; Dial et al., 1991; Fisher, 1946; Fisher & Goodman, 1955; Forbes, 1882; Fürbringer, 1902; Hudson & Lanzillotti, 1955; Matsuoka & Hasegawa, 2007; Vanden Berge, 1970). Consequently, some studies have focused on the furcula, sternum, coracoid, and humerus to attempt functional morphological interpretations through interspecific comparisons (Akeda & Fujiwara, 2023; de Margerie et al., 2005; Deeming & Da Silva, 2025; Habib & Ruff, 2008; Hui, 2002; Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021; Nudds et al., 2007; Simons et al., 2011). These studies have shown that the morphology of the bones to which the pectoralis attaches has evolved to meet the mechanical demands of the diverse wingbeat patterns of neornithines.

The shape and spatial distribution of the muscle attachment areas largely determine the potential muscle volume, muscle paths, and spread of muscle fibers, which, in turn, influence the magnitude and orientation of the forces generated by the muscles. Based on this premise, researchers have modelled muscles on the skeletal systems of tetrapods to estimate muscle orientation patterns and moment arms, examine correlations with ecological factors (Dickinson et al., 2024; Sellers et al., 2022), evaluate muscle function (Allen et al., 2017; Rankin et al., 2016), construct highly accurate musculoskeletal models of extinct taxa (Bishop et al., 2021; Nyakatura et al., 2019), and explore the evolution of limb postures (Allen et al., 2021; Brocklehurst et al., 2022; Dempsey et al., 2023). Therefore, the morphology of the flight muscles' origin/insertion can serve as an indicator of the magnitude and orientation of the forces they generate. These morphologies may vary depending on the orientation of the muscular forces required to encounter forces on the wings from the surrounding fluid during WPL.

This perspective provides a framework for understanding the evolution of wingbeat propulsion within a lineage from dinosaurs to neornithines via (in order) pennaraptorans, avialans, pygostylians, ornithothoracines, and ornithurines (Xu et al., 2014). By focusing on morphological changes in the pectoral region throughout this lineage, researchers have investigated how the morphologies necessary for flapping ability in neornithines were acquired (Baier et al., 2007; Jenkins, 1993; Mayr, 2017; Ostrom, 1976; Vazquez, 1992; Voeten et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2024). For instance, in the evolutionary steps from pennaraptorans to ornithothoracines, the open margins of the saddle‐shaped glenoid rotated from craniocaudal to dorsoventral orientations, enabling dorsoventral movements of the arms necessary for wingbeat (Jenkins, 1993). Consequently, the origins of the ligaments connecting the shoulder and upper arm shifted dorsally relative to the glenoid, allowing them to counteract the forces exerted by the pectoralis (Baier et al., 2007). However, the presence of a prominent sternal keel is characteristic of ornithothoracines, indicating that other clades lack the muscle volume necessary for powered flight (Mayr, 2017). Recent studies have suggested that even without the powerful flapping required to support the body in air, wingbeat could have assisted in other types of locomotion, such as for ascending steep/overhanging slopes (Dial, 2003). Therefore, it is essential to investigate whether these taxa could perform effective wingbeat propulsion even if they did not achieve flight. The transformation of the origin/insertion morphology of the pectoralis muscle into a shape suitable for wing downstroke may indicate the acquisition of auxiliary wingbeat propulsion ability.

Thus, the skeletal morphology of the attachment areas of the pectoralis can serve not only as a morphological indicator of wingbeat in birds but also elucidate its evolutionary history. However, few studies have estimated the orientation and form of flapping muscles from the muscular attachment areas (Agrawal et al., 2022; Deetjen et al., 2023; Heers et al., 2018). Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have conducted interspecific comparisons across broad phylogenetic groups to discuss their functional characteristics. Therefore, this study focused on the three‐dimensional (3D) morphology of the origin/insertion of the pectoral muscles. We hypothesised that the orientation of the pectoralis differs according to the mode of WPL.

Mechanical model and hypothesis

Assuming that the downstroke of a wing is a rotation around an axis parallel to the body axis, the resultant muscular force acting on the wing is expected to depress the wing. Subsequently, the aerodynamic/hydrodynamic force acting on the wing is oriented dorsocranially (Harada et al., 2021; Norberg, 1990; Rayner, 1989; Tobalske, 2022) and its cranial component functions as a propulsive force for flapping animals. Therefore, to counteract the cranial component of the fluid force, the pectoralis must exert a force that includes both a depressing and retracting component (Bannasch, 1994; Gatesy & Baier, 2005; Sy, 1936).

Furthermore, as the downstroke speed increases relative to the cruising speed, the angle of attack in the incurrent fluid on the wing increases, resulting in a greater cranial component of the aerodynamic and hydrodynamic forces acting on the wings (Tobalske, 2022). Therefore, more vigorous flapping requires greater wing retraction. By contrast, soaring birds rarely flap their wings; therefore, the aerodynamic force acting on the wings has fewer cranial components (Cone, 1962; Tobalske, 2022). Instead, the wings must withstand the upward aerodynamic forces generated by updrafts or sea winds using their pectoralis. Consequently, the role of the wing depressor is more critical in soaring birds than in other flapping birds. Based on these descriptions, it is expected that the lines of action (LoA) within the pectoralis vary in orientation among modes of WPL and according to wingbeat frequency. For example, it is expected that the pectoralis in birds that employ powered flapping for propulsion, such as flap‐flight and wing‐propelled diving birds, is dorsocranially oriented, whereas in soaring birds, it is oriented closer to the transverse plane to effectively depress the wing. It is also anticipated that the higher the flapping speed, the more cranially the muscle fibers are oriented, whereas the pectoralis in soaring birds is oriented more laterally.

Due to the shallow nature of the glenoid joint in birds, it is necessary to keep pressing the humerus toward the glenoid during the downstroke of the wing to prevent shoulder dislocation (Baier et al., 2007). In addition, the chordwise angle of the wing relative to the body axis changes during the downstroke (Clark & Bemis, 1979; Dial et al., 2008; Goodge, 1959; Warrick et al., 2009; Warrick & Dial, 1998). Consequently, the orientation of the aerodynamic and hydrodynamic forces acting on the wing relative to the body axis varies during the wingbeat (Chin & Lentink, 2019; Deetjen et al., 2020; Ros et al., 2011). Therefore, the pectoralis must have the potential to counteract such a dispersed force orientation. Moreover, in maneuvering and hovering flights, the orientation of the force acting on the wing is likely to change more drastically (Cheng et al., 2016; Chin & Lentink, 2022; Ros et al., 2011; Song et al., 2014). Considering these factors, it is anticipated that the pectoralis LoA in flapping birds has diverse orientations, and that there is a positive correlation between wingbeat intensity and the angular dispersion of LoA orientation.

By contrast, non‐flapping birds, such as kakapo (Strigops, Strigopidae), kiwi (Apteryx, Apterigidae), and the flightless cormorant (Nannopterum, Phalacrocoracidae), do not generate aerodynamic forces on their wings, which are effective for body propulsion; thus, their pectoralis does not need to counteract these forces. Consequently, pectoralis LoA orientation in non‐flapping birds is not constrained by the aerodynamic/hydrodynamic factors described above, potentially resulting in a greater variety of LoA orientations than in flapping birds.

This study aimed to assess whether the trends in the muscle orientation characteristics of each WPL mode align with the hypotheses proposed above. To achieve this, the three‐dimensional (3D) orientations of pectoralis LoAs were estimated from 3D avian skeletal mesh data and quantified as bivariate angular data using 3D CAD software. Furthermore, statistical methods were employed to compare muscle orientations among different modes of WPL.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

We used 132 specimens, representing 123 species, 109 genera, 69 families, and 33 orders (Table S1). The species were selected to be as diverse as possible in terms of taxonomic group, WPL mode and body mass (Table S1, Figure S1). This study focused on the differences in WPL mode among adult birds, rather than on the ontogenetic shift of WPL. Therefore, juvenile specimens were excluded.

Among the studied specimens, 3D skeletal data of 79 specimens were obtained from the online databank, such as Morphosource (https://www.morphosource.org/) and the Yamashina Institute for Ornithology Specimen Database (https://decochan.net/index.php?p=1). 77 specimens are stored as computed tomography (CT) images, and 2 specimens were stored as surface‐scanned images of the skeletal specimens (Table S1). In addition, 19 fresh and 35 skeletal specimens were used for anatomical observations of the musculoskeletal system (Table S2).

Categorisation of WPL mode

Following the previous study (Akeda & Fujiwara, 2023), the 79 studied species were categorised into four different WPL modes based on the use of their wings for locomotion: predominantly flapping flight birds (“flap‐flight,” n = 46), wing‐propelled diving birds (“wp‐diving,” n = 8), flightless birds in which flapping ability is not effective for body propulsion (“non‐flapping,” n = 10), and thermal and dynamic soaring birds (“soaring,” n = 15) (Table S1, Figure S1). Categorisation of these modes was accomplished based on the literature (Bruderer et al., 2010; Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021; Roots, 2006; Taylor & Thomas, 2014) and the website “Birds of the World” (Billerman et al., 2022).

The “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” birds generate cranially oriented fluid‐dynamic force by flapping their wings, which can propel their body for locomotion in the air (former) or water (latter). The “soaring” birds also use wingbeat propulsion for locomotion, but frequently utilise atmospheric air flow such as thermal updrafts, ridge lifts, and wind gradients to gain altitude or keep aloft. Birds that fly with alternating active flapping and passive gliding periods (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021; Viscor & Fuster, 1987) are categorised as “flap‐flight” birds. Sustained hovering birds such as hummingbirds (Trochilidae) were also included in the “flap‐flight” category. This is because their high‐frequency wingbeats generate cranially oriented aerodynamic force analogue to other “flap‐flight” birds, and they can switch between hovering‐ and forward‐flights by tilting their body (Tobalske et al., 2007). We also added mostly grounded birds, which are relatively reluctant to fly (Roots, 2006), such as tapaculos (Rhinocryptidae), rifleman (Acanthisitta, Acanthisittidae), hoatzin (Opisthocomus, Opisthocomidae), and lyrebirds (Menura, Menuridae) to the “flap‐flight” category (Table S1) because they fly with wingbeat, albeit for short distances (Roots, 2006). Auks (Alcidae), diving petrels (Pelecanoides, Procellariidae), and dippers (Cinclidae) were categorised as “wp‐diving” birds (Table S1), although many of them engage in both flapping flight and wing‐propelled diving (Raikow et al., 1988).

As mentioned above, the modes of WPL can be represented not only by qualitative categorisation but also by quantitative indices such as wingbeat frequency, although it was difficult to acquire wingbeat frequency data for all bird species used in this study. Therefore, we employed these as supplementary indicators of WPL for 24 out of 79 specimens during level flight, which were compiled from previous study (Altshuler & Dudley, 2003; Askew & Marsh, 2001; Berg & Rayner, 1995; Bruderer et al., 2010; Lapsansky et al., 2020; McGahan, 1973; Pennycuick, 1990, 1996; Tobalske et al., 1999; Viscor & Fuster, 1987). These data were extracted regardless of the modes of WPL, although the “flap‐flight” birds accounted for most of the data.

Identification of pectoralis origin/insertion areas on bones

We identified the attachment area of the pectoralis on bones based on the dissection of bird carcasses, observation of skeletal textures, and the literature (i.e., the origin on a unit of bones composed of sternum, coracoid, and furcula; and the insertion on humerus: Ashley, 1941; Berger, 1954; Canova et al., 2015; Fisher & Goodman, 1955; Forbes, 1882; Fürbringer, 1888; Howell, 1937; Hudson & Lanzillotti, 1955; McGowan, 1982, 1986; McKitrick, 1985; Meyers, 1992; Rosser, 1980; Watanabe et al., 2021; Zusi & Bentz, 1984). The fresh specimens were kept frozen at −20°C prior to observation and were defrosted just before dissection. After removing the pectoralis, the fibrous tissues of the fascicles/tendons were retained at the margin of the origin/insertion on the bone. In skeletal specimens, muscular attachment areas can be identified as roughened textures on the bone and margins, and intermuscular lines can be identified as faint ridges or grooves on the bone surface.

Construction of 3D digital skeletal models

For each CT data point of the 77 bird specimens (Table S1), we segmented the furcula, coracoids, scapulae, sternum, and humeri using a 3D segmentation software (Avizo 8.1: Thermo Fisher Scientific, Zuse Institute, Berlin, Germany). We then created 3D polygonal surface models of these elements.

These 3D models were imported into a 3D CAD software (Rhinoceros 3D ver. 8, hereafter termed Rhino8: Robert McNeel and Associates, Seattle, WA, USA). The 2 surface‐scanned 3D polygonal surface models were imported into Rhino8. If the skeletal elements of the furcula, scapulae, coracoids, and sternum were articulated in the right position to compose a “pectoral girdle,” the positional relationships among the elements were retained in the model. However, if the elements were disarticulated in the 3D model, we reassembled the elements to the right position in the 3D space in Rhino8. Note that there is a range of positions to fit the furcula, scapulae, coracoids, and sternum. However, we verified that the impact of these on the analysis is likely to be very small and negligible. Assuming skeletal bilateral symmetry, subsequent procedures were performed only on the right side. For specimens that were broken or whose surface structures were obscured on the right side, the 3D polygonal surface models were reflected along the median plane from the opposite side.

The plane that passed through both the cranial ends of the coracoids and the caudal end of the sternum was defined as the horizontal plane. The plane perpendicular to the horizontal plane bisecting the sternum was defined as the median plane. The craniocaudal axis was defined as the intersection of the horizontal and median planes. The axes perpendicular to the median and horizontal planes were defined as the mediolateral and dorsoventral axes, respectively.

The right pectoralis origin/insertion on the pectoral girdle and the deltopectoral crest of the right humerus were, respectively, outlined on the 3D model using the “InterpCrv” command in Rhino8. This procedure refers to the anatomical indicators of the bones for muscle origin/insertion as mentioned above.

Subsequently, the 3D model of the pectoral girdle was repositioned on the 3D digital space of the right‐handed x, y, and z coordinate system in Rhino8 (Figure 1a). The craniocaudal, mediolateral, and dorsoventral axes of the pectoral girdle were, respectively, aligned with the x, y, and z axes. Positive x, y, and z correspond to the cranial, right lateral, and ventral sides, respectively. Note that the craniocaudal and dorsoventral axis orientations in this study were not defined based on the orientation of the vertebral column; therefore, these terms do not correspond to the same terms on the entire body.

The 3D model of the right humerus was assembled into the glenoid of the pectoral girdle. The humerus was positioned to maximise the depressor moment arm of the pectoralis (see Appendix S1 and Figure S2 for details on the definition of the humerus posture used in this study). Subsequently, the long axis of the humeral head was roughly aligned with the orientation of the coracoid labrum, where the glenoid opens and the humeral incisure was roughly fitted into the scapular labrum (Baumel et al., 1993). In this posture, the line through the humeral head and centroid of the pectoralis insertion was aligned parallel to the mediolateral axis (y‐axis).

Quantifying the pectoralis orientation

The fascia covering the pectoralis can be modelled as a cylindrical structure that connects the contours of the origin and insertion of this muscle. The force exerted by muscle contraction varies depending on the orientation of each muscle bundle within the pectoralis, pennation angle, and relative length of the tendons (Lieber & Fridén, 2000). However, the net direction of pectoralis contraction should be restricted to the orientation within the cylindrical structure covered by the fascia (Allen et al., 2017; Cost et al., 2022; Demuth, Wiseman, et al., 2022; Wiseman, 2023). Based on this premise, muscle orientation was defined.

We placed two landmarks on the spline curves of the origin and insertion of the right pectoralis using Grasshopper, a visual programming language of Rhino8 (Figure 1b). The landmarks were at the cranial (O1) and caudolateral (O26) ends on the curve of origin, and at the proximal (I1) and distal (I26) ends on the curve of insertion. Thereafter, 24 equidistant semi‐landmarks were placed on the spline curves in clockwise order between O1 and O26, and O26 and O1. Similarly, the 24 equidistant semi‐landmarks were placed on the spline curve in an anticlockwise order between I1 and I26, and I26 and I1. These semi‐landmarks were numbered sequentially in clockwise order along the margin of the origin (O2–O25 and O27–O50) and anticlockwise along the margin of the insertion (I2–I25 and I27–I50).

Thus, 50 line segments connecting the corresponding landmarks/semi‐landmarks to the origin and insertion (O n and I n : n = 1–50) were constructed. The segments constructed using this method were geometrically arranged such that they did not intersect. Hereafter, we termed its orientation (the unit vector of each line segment from the point O n to the point I n on the x, y, and z coordinate space) as P n (n = 1–50), which is equivalent to the decomposed pectoralis LoA orientation to show its distribution and dispersion (Figure 1c). The unit vector of the mean orientations of these P n (n = 1–50) was defined as the P R per specimen, which is equivalent to the resultant pectoralis LoA orientation.

For the visualisation and statistical comparison of P n , each P n was expressed using longitude (ϕ n [°]) and latitude (λ n [°]) by considering the horizontal (x‐y) plane to be an equatorial plane and the cranial half of the median plane to be a prime meridian (ϕ n = 0°: Figure 1d). The ϕ n was defined as the clockwise angle of P n from the prime meridian in dorsal view. Thereafter, we set λ n as the elevation/depression angle from the horizontal (x‐y, equatorial) plane. Therefore, a small (~0°) and large (~90°) ϕ n indicated a more cranially and right laterally oriented P n , respectively. Likewise, a positive and negative λ n indicated a more dorsally and ventrally oriented P n , respectively.

The P R was also expressed as the longitude (ϕ R) and latitude (λ R) to represent the resultant orientation of the pectoralis LoA. Directional statistics on pectoralis orientation

A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) for spherical orientations (Mardia & Jupp, 2000) was conducted to analyse the distribution of P R among the four WPL mode categories (“flap‐flight,” “wp‐diving,” “non‐flapping,” and “soaring”). This analysis tests whether the means of the orientations differed among the groups based on a comparison between the inter‐ and intra‐group dispersions, assuming that the data distribution of each group on the unit sphere followed a von Mises distribution (Mardia & Jupp, 2000).

The P R and WPL mode were set as the objective and explanatory variables, respectively. A pairwise analysis was conducted for all six combinations among the four modes of WPL (“flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving,” “flap‐flight” and “non‐flapping,” “flap‐flight” and “soaring,” “wp‐diving” and “non‐flapping,” “wp‐diving” and “soaring,” and “non‐flapping” and “soaring”) using the “hcf.aov” function in the package “Directional” in R 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024). To prevent type I errors due to multiple comparisons, p‐values obtained from the analysis were corrected using Bonferroni's method (Bonferroni, 1936).

Correlation between wingbeat frequency and pectoralis orientation

We also conducted a regression analysis to assess the correlation between wingbeat frequency and the distribution of P n orientation. The ϕ R and λ R of P R, and the standard deviations of the ϕ n and λ n (σ ϕ and σ λ , respectively) were set as the objective variable, and the wingbeat frequency as the explanatory variable. Because wingbeat frequency data were distributed logarithmically, they were log‐transformed. The analysis was implemented using the “lm” function in R.

RESULTS

Differences in distribution of pectoralis orientation among WPL modes

The distributions of P n expressed as ϕ n and λ n varied among the studied species (Figure S3). On the sinusoidal projection, the 50 P n were widely distributed longitudinally and latitudinally in a convex‐down arc, which was broadened longitudinally to some extent in most specimens.

The average of the P R of all specimens was 34.5° in longitude and −17.5° in latitude (Figure 2), indicating that the entire pectoralis was oriented craniolaterally and inclined dorsally to some extent. However, in some “non‐flapping” birds such as kakapo (Strigops, Strigopidae) and kiwi (Apteryx, Apterigidae), the P n distribution markedly differed from the others. The P n orientations were limited latitudinally in kakapo and were limited longitudinally in kiwi (Figure 2).

Bivariate kernel density distributions (densities: 50%, 75%) and violin plots of ϕ R and λ R are drawn for each mode of WPL.

In the comparison of P R distributions among the four WPL modes, “soaring” birds had the highest ϕ R (45.8° on average), followed by “non‐flapping” (34.9° on average), “flap‐flight” (31.5° on average), and “wp‐diving” birds (30.3° on average), although the differences among them were small (Table 1). This indicates that the pectoralis tends to orient more laterally in “soaring” birds compared with the other WPL modes. However, the λ R of the pectoralis inclined somewhat closer to horizontal on average (−13.5°) in “non‐flapping” birds compared with the other modes (−18.6° on average) (Table 1).

Among the “flap‐flight” birds, Taeniopygia (Estrildidae), Coturnix, Bambusicola, Alectoris (Phasianidae), and Selasphorus (Trochilidae) were in the bottom 10 percentile regarding the ϕ R of P R, whereas Sula (Sulidae), Phoenicopterus (Phoenicopteridae), Phalacrocorax (Phalacrocoracidae), Bubo (Strigidae), and Phaethon (Phaethontidae) were in the top 10 percentile (Table S1). Similarly, Pteroptochos (Rhinocryptidae), Opisthocomus (Opisthocomidae), Acanthisitta (Acanthisittidae), Xiphorhynchus (Furnariidae), and Alectoris were in the bottom 10 percentile regarding the λ R of P R, whereas Oceanodroma (Hydrobatidae), Larus (Laridae), Selasphorus (Trochilidae), Sula, and Phalacrocorax were in the top 10 percentile (Table S1). Among the “non‐flapping” birds, only the flightless cormorant (Nannopterum, Phalacrocoracidae), despite its “non‐flapping” nature, showed an exceptionally large λ n distribution of P n comparable to that of the other “flap‐flight” birds (Figure 2). Additionally, kiwi (Apteryx) had a considerably larger ϕ R than that of “soaring” birds (Figure 2). Among the “wp‐diving” birds, Sphenisciformes tended to exhibit relatively smaller λ R (~ −15°) than other volant “wp‐diving” birds (Figure 2).

The MANOVA of spherical data indicated that the P R of “soaring” birds exhibited significantly different distributions compared to those of other modes (“flap‐flight” birds, F = 23.3, p < 0.001; “wp‐diving” birds, F = 14.6, p < 0.001; and “non‐flapping” birds, F = 10.8, p < 0.001: F indicates F value). Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the distribution of the mean vectors among “flap‐flight,” “wp‐diving,” and “non‐flapping” birds: between “flap‐flight” and “non‐flapping” birds (F = 2.95, p = 5.6 × 10−2), between “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” (F = 9.91 × 10−2, p = 1.00), and between “wp‐diving” and “non‐flapping” (F = 0.96, p = 0.47). This indicates that the description of “soaring” birds tending to have more lateral P R compared with other WPL modes was corroborated statistically.

The angular standard deviations per specimens (σ ϕ and σ λ ) also varied among the studied taxa (Figure 3). The σ ϕ was 0.31° on average in “flap‐flight,” “soaring,” and “non‐flapping” birds, and 0.48° on average in “wp‐diving” birds. This indicates that the P n of “wp‐diving” birds tends to be spread more longitudinally than in birds employing other WPL modes. Among each WPL, foot‐propelled swimming birds such as loons (Gaviidae), grebes (Podicipedidae), cormorants (Phalacrocoracidae), and ducks (Anatidae) (Clifton et al., 2018), tended to exhibit a higher σ ϕ on average (~0.4°) than other species within each WPL mode (Figure 3). Among “flap‐flight” birds, Bubo, Ninox (Strigidae), Alectura (Megapodiidae), Acanthisitta (Acanthisittidae), and Pteroptochos (Rhinocryptidae) were in the bottom 10 percentile regarding the standard deviation of σ ϕ , whereas Gavia (Gaviidae), Phalacrocorax (Phalacrocoracidae), Callonetta (Anatidae), Crypturellus (Tinamidae), and Alcedo (Alcedinidae) were in the top 10 percentile (Table S1).

The σ λ was similar among the four different WPL modes (Figure 3). The “wp‐diving” birds exhibited the highest σ λ on average (0.26°), while the “non‐flapping” birds had the lowest on average (0.15°). The “flap‐flight” and “soaring” birds had a comparable distribution of σ λ on average (~0.2°). The center of the distribution increased in the order of “non‐flapping,” “soaring,” “flap‐flight,” and “wp‐diving” birds. Some mostly grounded birds (Pteroptochos, Opisthocomus, and Acanthisitta), Nestor (Strigopidae), and Phoenicopterus (Phoenicopteridae) were in the bottom 10 percentile regarding the standard deviation of σ λ , whereas Podiceps (Podicipedidae), Bambusicola (Phasianidae), Taeniopygia (Estrildidae), Alcedo (Alcedinidae), and Jacana (Jacanidae) were in the top 10 percentile (Table S1). Among the “wp‐diving” birds, only dipper (Cinclus, Cinclidae) showed similar σ ϕ and σ λ distributions to those of “soaring” and “flap‐flight” birds (Figure 2).

The ϕ R was strongly negatively correlated (r = −0.83) with wingbeat frequency, modelled by a regression analysis in which the slope was −25.3, indicating that if the wingbeat frequency doubles, ϕ R will decrease by 7.62 (Table 2). By contrast, λ R had a negligible correlation (r = −1.96 × 10−2) with wingbeat frequency (Table 2). Thus, the lower the wingbeat frequency, the greater the lateral P R.

DISCUSSION

We estimated P n based on the geometries of the pectoral girdle and humeral skeletons. This study was designed to test the hypothesis proposed in Section 1.1, namely that the decomposed pectoralis LoA orientation (P n ) and dispersion of P n were constrained by the fluid‐dynamic force on the wings depending on the WPL mode or wingbeat frequency. Below, we evaluate these predictions in light of our results and discuss how skeletal morphology constrains pectoralis orientation across WPL modes.

Pectoralis orientation was constrained by the fluid‐dynamic force on the wings depending on the WPL mode

Our results clearly indicate that P n varied in entire orientation (P R: ϕ R and λ R) depending on WPL mode or wingbeat frequency (Table 2). This generally supported our hypothesis: The higher the wingbeat frequency, the more cranially oriented the P n .

Studies using accelerometers have revealed that the fluid‐dynamic force acting on the wings of birds inclines cranially during both flapping flight (Hedrick et al., 2004) and wing‐propelled diving (Harada et al., 2021). Therefore, the cranial P n in “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” birds (Figure 2) may effectively encounter the force orientation required during WPL to retract their wings in order to withstand the cranial component of the fluid‐dynamic force and transmit thrust to their bodies. Note that the P n in “soaring” birds also oriented craniolaterally to some extent, indicating that “soaring” birds exhibit not only soaring but also flapping slightly when maintaining altitude (Williams et al., 2020). Sy (1936) qualitatively described that pectoralis functions not only for wing depression but also for retraction because the sternal keel, where the main part of the muscle originates, is located caudoventrally from the glenoid. A study on the functional anatomy of penguin (Sphenisciformes) wings also indicated that the pectoralis exerts a caudally inclined force on the wings, based on the analysis of fascicle orientation in the muscle (Bannasch, 1994). Electromyographic studies have reported that the caudal part of the pectoralis (pars thoracobrachialis) is activated during humeral retraction during pigeon take off (Dial, 1992; Dial et al., 1988). These studies confirm that the wings are retracted by the pectoralis during the downstroke of the wingbeat, which is consistent with our results showing that “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” birds have a cranial P n to encounter wing motion.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the similarity in P R between “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” birds indicates that “wp‐diving” birds have a large dorsoventral component of lift on the wings generated by wingbeats. Because many “wp‐diving” birds such as alcids, diving petrel (Pelecanoides, Procellariidae), and dipper (Cinclus, Cinclidae) can also fly with wingbeats, this result is likely due to the need to perform both “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving.” However, previous studies have confirmed that the dorsoventral component of the fluid‐dynamic force decreases as the wings of penguins are deformed (Harada et al., 2021). This seems to be consistent with our results that the penguins (Sphenisciformes), which are not capable of flight, have more cranially orientated P R among “wp‐diving” birds (Figure 2). This seems to be consistent with our results showing that penguins (Sphenisciformes), which are not capable of flight, have a more cranial P R compared with other “wp‐diving” birds (Figure 2). This suggests that flapping underwater requires the cranially oriented component of the pectoralis LoA rather than the dorsoventral component compared to flapping flight, although verification is needed for the skeletons of other non‐flying “wp‐diving” bird species such as the extinct great auks (Pinguinus, Alcidae).

The relatively lateral P R in “soaring” birds, which is unique among the other modes of WPL, may contribute to the stabilisation of the wings relative to the body against the aerodynamic force acting on the wings during flight (Meyers, 2019; Pennycuick, 2008). The orientation of the aerodynamic force generated on the wings by soaring depends on the airspeed resulting from the sum of the cruising speed and atmospheric air current, and thus varies depending on the atmospheric environment and flight attitude (Pennycuick, 2008; Sachs et al., 2013). However, because the component of the aerodynamic force that is directed against gravity is always applied to the wings, “soaring” birds have to maintain the wing position horizontally against the upward forces that push the wings into an elevated configuration. In addition, the wing morphology of “soaring” birds allows them to generate larger aerodynamic force for a given airspeed than non‐soaring birds (Rader et al., 2020). Therefore, the pectoralis of “soaring” birds needs to continuously resist the large dorsal ward forces on the wings as well as prevent glenoid joint dislocation.

Electromyography during flight in the American kestrel (Falco sparverius, Falconidae) revealed that the cranial part of the pectoralis (pars sternobrachialis) was active during gliding, whereas the pars thoracobrachialis was inactive (Meyers, 1993). This indicates that “soaring” birds do not engage in wing beating and therefore do not require repeated retraction during flight, in contrast to “flap‐flight” birds. Consequently, the lateral P R in soaring birds is well suited for stabilising the shoulder joint and bracing the wings against upward aerodynamic force. Histological studies have demonstrated that slow muscle fibers in the pectoralis of “soaring” birds are effective at reducing energy expenditure during soaring (Meyers & Stakebake, 2005; Rosser et al., 1994; Rosser & George, 1986). Our study suggests that P n also has a functional correlation with soaring. For instance, in birds such as eagles (Accipitriformes), which possess few slow muscle fibers yet frequently soar (Meyers & McFarland, 2016), a lateral P n may provide a functional advantage.

Although the MANOVA did not reject the null hypothesis of identical distributions since the p‐value slightly exceeded the significance level, “non‐flapping” birds were characterised by a lower λ n than those of “flap‐flight” birds. Their muscular orientation may weaken the dorsoventral component of the net force exerted by the pectoralis, leading to a reduction in the moment arm for the downstroke. This tendency is consistent with our hypothesis that the pectoralis of “non‐flapping” birds does not need to counteract aerodynamic forces. Mostly grounded birds such as moustached turca (Rhinocrypidae), hoazin (Opisthocomidae), and rifleman (Acanthisittidae) also exhibited a lower λ n than other “flap‐flight” birds, indicating that the moment arm for the downstroke may also be reduced in “non‐flapping” birds. Based on these findings, mostly grounded birds have to exert greater muscular force to support their bodies aloft. This is congruent with the observation that mostly grounded birds can fly only short distances (Billerman, 2020; Medrano et al., 2024; Roots, 2006).

Pectoralis orientation was constrained by wingbeat frequency

Based on the above trends, the strong correlation between P R and wingbeat frequency in this study can be interpreted based on a simple aerodynamic model. During flap flight, the resultant airspeed in birds with a high wingbeat frequency is incident on the wing at a larger angle to the chord line of the wing than that in birds with a low wingbeat frequency (Altshuler et al., 2015; Pennycuick, 1990). As the downstroke speed increases when approaching the wingtip, the angle of attack becomes excessively large. Birds overcome this phenomenon by pronating the manus to which the primary feathers attach (Vazquez, 1992), rotating the wing around the spanwise axis (Ajanic et al., 2023), and twisting the separate primary feathers in a nose‐down direction (Norberg, 1990). All these strategies tilt the aerofoil toward a nose‐down direction relative to the body axis. Therefore, the higher the wing‐beat frequency, the more cranially tilted the downstroke‐generated aerodynamic force will be to the body axis. Our findings suggest that birds with a high wingbeat frequency may have a cranial P n to counteract this orientation of aerodynamic force. To verify this, it is necessary to study the relationship between the angular velocity of the downstroke and the orientation of the aerodynamic force on the wing. Further studies of 3D wing movements during wingbeat (Deetjen et al., 2017; Maeda et al., 2017; Wolf & Konrath, 2015) may help to further investigate the correlation between the kinematics of wingbeat and P n among species.

Moreover, birds with high wingbeat frequency can adjust the orientation of the aerodynamic force on their wings and their cruising speed (Tobalske, 2022). Wings are subjected to more cranially oriented lift, especially at low flight speeds (Hedrick et al., 2004). A kinematic analysis of the slow‐maneuvring flight of rock pigeons (Columba livia, Columbidae) showed that strong wing pronation in the downstroke generates lift with a large cranial component, which contributes to the stability of the bird during low‐speed flight (Warrick & Dial, 1998). Rufous hummingbirds (Selasphorus, Trochilidae) during hovering (Tobalske et al., 2007), and zebra finches (Taeniopygia, Estrildidae) during relatively slow‐speed flight (Tobalske et al., 1999), also pronate their wings in downstroke, suggesting that birds with high wingbeat frequency frequently apply lift with a large cranial component to their wings. This is consistent with the results of this study, in which the higher the wingbeat frequency, the more cranial the P n was.

Standard deviations of P n were constrained by WPL mode

Our hypothesis further predicted that the angular dispersion of P n (σ ϕ and σ λ ) was constrained by the fluid‐dynamic force on the wings depending on the WPL mode or wingbeat frequency. This prediction was partially supported: P n varied in the dispersion (σ ϕ and σ λ ) depending on WPL mode, but not wingbeat frequency (Table 2).

The standard deviations of σ ϕ and σ λ constrain the range of force that can be exerted at one time. A higher σ λ means that the bird can depress the wings, as well as pull them towards the glenoid. When birds flap their wings, fluid‐dynamic force constantly acts on the wings (Lentink et al., 2015; Ros et al., 2011; Warrick et al., 2009), and during this time, the pectoralis is continuously activated (Biewener et al., 1992, 1998; Hedrick et al., 2003; Tobalske & Biewener, 2008; Williamson et al., 2001). Additionally, pulling the wing towards the glenoid helps prevent ventral dislocation of the glenoid joint associated with strong wing downstrokes (Baier et al., 2007). Therefore, the relatively large σ λ of the pectoralis in “flap‐flight” and “wp‐diving” birds may be due to the arrangement of the muscle that prevents joint dislocation while allowing for faster wingbeat. Since a high wingbeat frequency requires a greater angular velocity during the downstroke, the positive correlation between wingbeat frequency and pectoralis σ λ can also be interpreted in the same context.

There was no correlation between σ ϕ and wingbeat frequency, which rejected the hypothesis that a high σ ϕ contributes to exerting force over a wide range in the horizontal plane. However, both wing‐propelled and foot‐propelled swimming birds had a high σ ϕ s. These results suggest that the expansion of P n in the horizontal plane is a feature shared by diving birds, regardless of their swimming strategy. As the pectoralis forms a large part of the ventral body profile in birds, a widespread P n distribution may contribute to the formation of a streamlined body profile in diving birds (Lovvorn et al., 2001; Lovvorn & Liggins, 2002; Tickle et al., 2007).

Relationship between skeletal morphology and pectoralis orientation

The P n was established by the 3D morphology of the pectoral girdle, which varies according to WPL mode, as suggested by previous studies (Hui, 2002; Lowi‐Merri et al., 2025). The P n was determined on the basis of the positional relationship between the origin and insertion of the pectoralis. The ϕ R was determined by the ratio of the distance on the mediolateral axis between the origin/insertion to the distance on the craniocaudal axis between the origin/insertion. For instance, a cranial P n indicates that centroids of the pectoralis origin/insertion approach each other in the mediolateral axis and move apart on the craniocaudal axis. However, the λ R was determined by the ratio of the distance on the dorsoventral axis between the origin/insertion to the distance between the origin/insertion when projected onto the horizontal plane. For example, a ventrally tilted P n with a high λ n indicates that centroids of the pectoralis origin/insertion approach each other on the horizontal plane and move apart on the dorsoventral axis.

The standard deviation of P n was significantly influenced by the size ratio between the insertion and origin of the pectoralis. For example, if the origin is elongated craniocaudally relative to the insertion or/and the insertion is shortened mediolaterally relative to the origin, the standard deviation of σ ϕ increases. However, if the area of origin is deeper dorsoventrally relative to the insertion, the standard deviation of σ λ increases. Because the insertion of the pectoralis exhibits little variation in the dorsoventral width among birds (Navalón et al., 2022), insertion morphology variation among birds probably has little effect on σ λ .

The sternum of continuously flapping or burst flying birds has larger caudolateral trabeculae (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021), where the origin of the pectoralis is distributed (Berger, 1954; Hudson & Lanzillotti, 1955). This can expand the origin of the pectoralis caudolaterally and orient the P n more cranially. A study comparing the size of the deltopectoral crest of the humerus among birds suggested that, in flapping birds, the insertion of the pectoralis is closer to the humeral head (Serrano & Chiappe, 2017). This makes the mediolateral component of the P n smaller by closing the origin and insertion mediolaterally, thus resulting in a cranial P n . Further morphological studies of the pectoral girdle and humerus of birds are needed to elucidate these aspects.

In diving birds, including “wp‐diving” and hindlimb‐propelled diving birds (the latter was categorised as “flap‐flight” birds in this study), the pectoral girdle is elongated craniocaudally compared with their close relatives (Bannasch, 1994; Kuroda, 1967; Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2021), contributing to a more cranial P n . Conversely, in diving birds, including both “wp‐diving” and hindlimb‐propelled diving birds, the acrocoracoid process of the coracoid, where the coracoid and furcula form the articulation cranial to the glenoid, is projected more cranially (Bannasch, 1994; Shufeldt, 1888, 1889a, 1889b, 1889c, 1889d; Stegmann, 1964). Among these diving birds, the furcula in alcids extends ventrally from the coracoid‐furcula articular joint (Shufeldt, 1888, 1889a, 1889b, 1889c, 1889d; Stegmann, 1964). Together, these traits contribute to a more cranial P n by locating part of the furcula near the articulation with the coracoid, cranioventrally relative to the glenoid. Consequently, because the pectoral girdle in these diving birds includes the features that contribute to a more cranial and caudal P n , the P R in “wp‐diving” birds did not significantly differ from that of “flap‐flight” birds. Additionally, the elongated pectoral girdle and furcula morphology in these diving birds expand the origin of the pectoralis craniocaudally relative to the insertion, resulting in a tendency of having a relatively large σ ϕ .

The morphology of the pectoral girdle and humerus in “soaring” birds may facilitate a lateral P n . For example, “soaring” birds typically possess a craniocaudally shorter sternum with shorter craniolateral trabeculae (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021; Mayr, 2016), which reduce the craniocaudal component of the P n . The humerus, with its large pectoral origin located far from the glenoid (Serrano & Chiappe, 2017), increases the mediolateral component of the P n . These morphological features result in a more lateral P n . Previous studies have characterised the sternum of “soaring” birds as having a keel that protrudes cranially, which is interpreted to aid in powerful depression (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021). Additionally, another study described the furcula of “soaring” birds as shallow and U‐shaped, which is more advantageous for wing depression by the pectoralis (Hui, 2002). These studies are consistent with our findings, which indicate that “soaring” birds have a lateral P n , facilitating force exertion in wing depression rather than in wing retraction. Moreover, these morphological changes may also contribute to the lateral P n in “soaring” birds.

In this study, we described the skeletal morphology that determines P n ; however, orientation can also affect skeletal morphology (Cost et al., 2020; Engelkes et al., 2020; Toro‐Ibacache et al., 2016). For instance, Akeda and Fujiwara (2023) showed that coracoids in soaring birds exhibited higher bending strength than other birds and considered this to be due to the deviation of P n from the coracoid shaft. Because the shoulder width of soaring birds shows a similar allometric relationship to body mass as that of other birds (Nudds & Rayner, 2006), it is likely that the long axis of their coracoid is oriented in approximately the same orientation as that of other birds. The laterally oriented P n in soaring birds observed in our study corroborates the interpretation of Akeda and Fujiwara (2023). Although further assessment is needed, such as finite element analysis (Cost et al., 2020; Engelkes et al., 2020; Toro‐Ibacache et al., 2016); such insights may also indicate a close association between P n and skeletal morphology.

Limitation and application

In this study, pectoralis LoA orientation could only indicate the potential range of muscle contractile force orientation; it could not precisely determine the orientation of the muscle fascicle force, which depends on the muscular architecture. Although the pectoralis in some birds is composed of superficial and deep layers (Meyers, 2019), these structures were not considered in this study in order to simplify the model. Nevertheless, muscular fascicles converge at the insertion point in the pectoralis of birds, and the LoA of the muscle is generally aligned with the orientation of the force exerted by the muscle (Agrawal et al., 2022; Heers et al., 2018).

The other muscles besides the pectoralis, such as m. deltoideus and m. coracobrachialis caudalis can potentially be involved in the downstroke of the wing (Deeming & Mosto, 2026; Dial, 1992; Dial et al., 1991). Given this possibility, this study opens avenues for future investigations into the relationships between the WPLs and the orientations of these muscles. However, the primary muscle used for flight is the pectoralis, which far exceeds the other shoulder muscles both in mass and in the distance (moment arm) from the shoulder joint to its insertion. Accordingly, prioritising the pectoralis in the present study is well justified, and the roles of the other muscles are deferred to future work.

Future research is needed to clarify the morphological characteristics of the furcula, coracoid, sternum, and humerus, which are essential for achieving the muscle orientation associated with various WPL modes. This would provide crucial information for understanding the morphological evolution leading to WPL mode among extinct species, particularly for taxa where phylogenetic bracketing can be applied using extant species (Witmer, 1995) or where the 3D geometry of the skeleton is preserved as fossils. Some studies have reconstructed the attachment areas of the pectoralis in extinct genera such as Mancalla (basal Pan‐Alcidae) based on osteological correlations in spheniscids and alcids, in order to understand the morphological evolution of muscles reflecting the convergent evolution of “wp‐diving” ability (Watanabe et al., 2021). Additionally, some extinct species of volant lithornithids (basal Palaeognathae) had sustained flapping flight based on their wing morphology (Torres et al., 2020). Our study can help verify these insights from a musculoskeletal morphology perspective and improve the understanding of WPL mode evolution across various taxonomic groups within Neornithes.

Furthermore, these findings provide a preliminary framework for reconstructing the diversification of powered flight in the evolutionary lineage from feathered theropods (through pennaraptorans, avialans, pygostylians, ornithothoracins, and ornithurines) to Neornithes (Chiappe et al., 2019, 2020, 2023; Liu et al., 2017; Lowi‐Merri et al., 2025; Serrano et al., 2017, 2018; Serrano & Chiappe, 2017; Voeten et al., 2018). However, it is important to note that the pectoral girdle morphology changes dramatically within this lineage (Wu et al., 2024). There are still considerable gaps in knowledge concerning muscle attachment areas in the pectoral girdle of fossil birds, in particular, whether the furcula provides the origin for the pectoralis remains controversial (Carpenter, 2002; Elzanowski, 2002; Jasinoski et al., 2006; Mayr, 2017; Olson & Feduccia, 1979). Therefore, careful consideration is needed to determine the taxonomic groups to which the findings of this study can be applied (Xu, 2024). However, ornithurines exhibit a pectoral girdle similar to Neornithes (Wu et al., 2024), and the attachment areas of the pectoralis may also be similar to those of extant birds. Species with ridges or keels along the median of the sternum are likely to have at least a superficial part of the pectoralis attached to the midline of the sternum (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2021).

Another concern in applying our method to extinct taxa is that many fossils are deformed owing to tectonic factors (Hedrick, 2023). However, recent methods for restoring the geometry of deformed fossils have become more sophisticated (e.g., Demuth, Benito, et al., 2022). Studies have reconstructed the 3D geometry of the sternum of the extinct Ichthyornis (Ichthyornithiformes, Ornithurae) and estimated the locomotor ability of this animal based on morphological analysis conducted among extant Neornithes (Lowi‐Merri et al., 2023). Because other bones of Ichthyornis are also available as 3D mesh data (Benito et al., 2022), the present study can provide additional insights into the findings of previous studies. Additional information can also be provided for reconstructing the locomotor function of Gansus (Ornithurae: Nudds et al., 2013) using 3D‐preserved fossils (Wang et al., 2016).

CONCLUSION

In this study, we compared pectoralis LoA orientation among WPL modes in neornithines. The pectoralis LoA were represented by connecting the origin/insertion on 3D skeleton models of 79 neornithine bird specimens, which varied in WPL mode, taxonomy, and body mass. The LoA was oriented more dorsocranially in birds that engage in both flapping flight and wing‐propelled diving, whereas it was oriented more laterally in soaring birds, and was somewhat closer to horizontal in non‐flapping birds. Among birds that use wing flapping to propel their body for locomotion, the higher the wingbeat frequency, the more the pectoralis LoA is oriented cranially, and the wider the dorsoventral LoA distribution. These results can be explained by the fluid‐dynamic force on the wings, which depends on the WPL mode. Furthermore, it is suggested that the skeletal morphology unique to each WPL mode, related to the origin/insertion of the pectoralis, may be associated with the demands of pectoralis LoA orientation. These findings provide a preliminary framework for reconstructing the diversification of WPL modes, including the origin of powered flight, in the evolution of birds from feathered theropods.
