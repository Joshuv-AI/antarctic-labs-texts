Climate change impacts on the distribution of valuable Thelephora fungi in China
Authors: Si-Ao Li, Jia-Rui Yu, Hai-Sheng Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThelephora includes valuable edible ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi in East Asia threatened by overharvesting, climate change, and habitat loss. Using Biomod2 ensemble modeling with bioclimatic variables, elevation data, and occurrence records, this study predicted current and future distributions of four Thelephora species under climate scenarios SSP2-45 and SSP5-85 for the 2050s and 2090s. The results indicate that the potential distribution ranges of these four Thelephora species are primarily influenced by Bio18 (precipitation of the warmest quarter), Bio12 (annual precipitation), elevation, and Bio3 (isothermality), though the relative importance of these factors varies among species. High-emission scenarios predict habitat reduction by 2050, with potential range expansion by 2090. Warming will drive northward habitat shifts, with migration distances increasing under high-emission scenarios. Over half of potential distribution areas contain non-host vegetation. Conservation strategies should prioritize host plant cultivation to expand fungal habitats and mitigate climate impacts.

Introduction

Global climate change has profoundly impacted the distribution, ecological niches, and community structure of fungi. Climate change, particularly rising temperatures and altered precipitation patterns, has markedly influenced the geographic distribution of fungi, leading to shifts in fungal community composition and diversity.1 It has been shown that reduced summer precipitation and increased temperatures are expected to drastically decrease black truffle yields, with climate models forecasting a decline of 78%–100% in Southern Europe from 2071 to 2100, posing a major threat to their growth and economic value.2 Likewise, many other fungal species are anticipated to experience substantial distributional changes by 2050 and 2070, which may have profound implications for global ecosystems and agriculture.3 These findings suggest that as climate change continues, the habitat suitability for fungi will undergo considerable alterations, with rising temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns being crucial factors.

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi of the genus Thelephora are widely distributed across the globe, from tropical to temperate regions, and species within this genus have a close symbiotic relationship with specific coniferous and/or broadleaf trees.4,5,6 Certain species, including T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, are important and precious edible and medicinal fungi in East Asia, particularly in southern China.7,8,9,10,11 The collection of the T. ganbajun generates tens of millions of yuan in annual income for local farmers in Yunnan Province, making it a crucial component of the local economy. However, continuously rising prices have led to overharvesting, resulting in a substantial decline in the populations of these Thelephora species. Additionally, climate change has caused an increase in the frequency of extreme weather events, such as heavy rainfall and droughts, which further disrupt the growth environments of these fungi. Vegetation destruction exacerbates this issue, leading to a reduction in their habitats. These factors collectively have caused a dramatic decrease in the populations of these Thelephora species, posing severe challenges to their conservation. The uncertainty of future climate conditions and habitat stability makes the task of preserving their biodiversity even more difficult.

Species distribution models (SDMs), also known as niche models, are utilized to predict and estimate potential distribution areas of species by using occurrence and environmental data. These models are considered crucial for forecasting the spatial distribution of various species, identifying conservation threats, planning protected areas, and managing biological invasions.12 Among the various SDMs, Biomod2 is recognized as an ensemble model that integrates ten different modeling techniques, and predictive accuracy is enhanced, especially when species distribution data are scarce.13 Biomod2 has been effectively employed to predict the potential distribution of a wide range of taxa, including plants, animals, insects, fishes, and fungi.14,15,16,17,18,19

This study aims to evaluate the changes in the ecological niche distribution of four Thelephora fungal species under global climate change and identify the factors influencing these changes, and the main objectives of this study are as follows: (1) identify critical environmental variables affecting the niche range of these Thelephora species; (2) evaluate changes in distribution areas and the migration direction and distance of distribution centers under current and future scenarios (SSP2-45 and SSP5-85 greenhouse gas emission scenarios for the 2050s and 2090s), thus helping to formulate more precise conservation strategies to address the impact of climate change on their habitats; and (3) analyze abiotic environmental factors and vegetation (biotic) factors to identify regions where fungal area and distribution may increase through future vegetation restoration.

Results

Model evaluation

For these four fungal species, the ensemble model performed exceptionally well across the area under the curve (AUC), Cohen's Kappa coefficient (KAPPA), and true skill statistic (TSS) metrics. Using a 75/25 training-to-testing ratio, the results were as follows: T. austrosinensis (AUC = 0.999, KAPPA = 0.928, and TSS = 0.996), T. ganbajun (AUC = 0.999, KAPPA = 0.987, and TSS = 0.999), T. glaucoflora (AUC = 0.999, KAPPA = 0.937, and TSS = 0.973), and T. pseudoganbajun (AUC = 0.999, KAPPA = 0.937, and TSS = 0.999).

For T. glaucoflora, Bio18 was the dominant variable at 80.2%. For T. pseudoganbajun, the primary environmental variables were Bio18 at 48.3% and elev at 33%. Discussion

Global climate change has greatly affected the potential distribution of economically important Thelephora fungi in East Asia, threatening their survival and niche stability. In this study, 19 WorldClim bioclimatic variables, elevation data, vegetation type information, and 151 distribution records were utilized, and the Biomod2 ensemble model was employed to predict the potential distribution changes of four Thelephora species under current and future climate scenarios. Key climatic variables influencing their distribution were evaluated, and the suitability and associations between their potential distribution and different vegetation types were analyzed. The model performed exceptionally well in terms of AUC, KAPPA, and TSS metrics, with an AUC of 0.999 for T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, along with high KAPPA and TSS values, indicating that the ensemble model significantly improves the accuracy and reliability of fungal species distribution predictions.

This study reveals the close relationship between the distribution of Thelephora fungi and various key environmental factors, particularly Bio18, Bio12, Bio3, and elevation. These factors to some extent affect the potential distribution of these four species. The significance of Bio18 lies in fulfilling the seasonal water and thermal requirements for T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, with a particularly high contribution rate of 80.2% for T. glaucoflora. This strong dependency suggests that T. glaucoflora is highly reliant on optimal hydrothermal conditions during its fruiting period. The hottest season coincides with the peak fruiting phase of T. glaucoflora, during which ample precipitation and suitable temperatures create ideal conditions for the expansion of subterranean mycelial networks. Such favorable conditions enable sufficient biomass accumulation of the mycelium, thereby supporting the development and maturation of fruiting bodies. Bio12 influences T. austrosinensis distribution, with a contribution rate of 48.9%, while its influence on T. glaucoflora is minimal, at only 1.9%. This distinction suggests that T. austrosinensis primarily depends on a consistent water supply throughout the year rather than seasonal hydrothermal peaks.

As ECM fungi, the growth and reproduction of Thelephora species depend on symbiotic relationships with host plants. These plants, in turn, require a stable water supply to synthesize and provide large amounts of carbohydrates for the fungi.20 Steady annual precipitation supports the sustained growth of host plants, enabling continuous carbohydrate synthesis even during non-fruiting periods. This stable carbohydrate availability provides essential nutrients for T. austrosinensis, thereby sustaining its long-term symbiotic relationship with host plants. Bio3 reflects the temperature stability of a region, represented by the ratio between diurnal and annual temperature ranges. Higher isothermality indicates smaller temperature fluctuations, which is particularly critical for the fruiting process of the macrofungi. Stable temperatures can mitigate the adverse effects of extreme temperatures on mycelia growth and fruiting body formation, while also enabling host plants to provide a reliable carbon source to the fungi. Among Thelephora fungi, the contribution of Bio3 to the distribution of T. austrosinensis reaches 32.2%, significantly higher than that for the other three species. This suggests that reduced temperature fluctuations are more conducive to the fruiting process of T. austrosinensis and help maintain the stability of its symbiotic system.21 For T. ganbajun, the contribution of elevation is relatively high, accounting for 43.1%. High-altitude regions typically have lower temperatures and distinct precipitation patterns compared to low-altitude regions. High altitudes may restrict the growth of certain temperature-dependent fungi, while lower temperatures and reduced oxygen availability may further limit the activity range of these fungi.22

Global climate change significantly impacts the distribution of fungi, plants, and animals through various complex mechanisms, posing a major threat to biodiversity. These effects are reflected in rising global temperatures, shifts in precipitation patterns, and alterations in biogeographic patterns. For fungi, temperature and humidity are crucial for growth and reproduction. When climate change causes fluctuations or instability in these conditions, fungal growth can be severely limited. This is especially true for ECM fungi, which are highly sensitive to environmental changes.23 Variations in temperature and humidity can disrupt their symbiotic relationships with plants, impairing ecological functions in certain regions.24 Under future climate conditions, the characteristics of suitable habitat range changes vary across species. T. austrosinensis shows a trend of habitat expansion under both scenarios, with this expansion becoming more pronounced under SSP5-85, reaching 45.32%. This suggests that T. austrosinensis may possess a strong adaptability to environmental changes, allowing it to expand its range in response to increased temperature and precipitation. T. ganbajun experiences minimal habitat loss but shows high potential for expansion into new habitats. For T. glaucoflora and T. pseudoganbajun, the fluctuations in suitable habitat range are more pronounced under SSP5-85; T. glaucoflora experiences habitat loss up to 50.39% under SSP5-85, while its expansion rate reaches 60.16%, indicating habitat instability. This variation may stem from differences in the tolerance of each species to climate fluctuations, particularly changes in temperature and humidity. As symbiotic partners, ECM fungi rely on specific plants for survival, making their distribution closely tied to the biogeographic shifts of host plants. Global climate change can alter host plant distributions, which in turn directly affects the habitat range of ECM fungi, linking their migration patterns to those of their symbiotic plants.25 As climate change drives plants to higher latitudes or elevations, ECM fungi distribution follows, which can limit growth in certain areas and potentially lead to local extinctions.26,27 The trend of northward migration observed in the four Thelephora species under future climate scenarios aligns with the northward shift of their host plants, further illustrating climate change’s influence on ECM fungi distribution. This trend highlights both the role of climate change in driving plant and fungal co-migration and its potential impacts on fungi dependent on symbiotic relationships.

Vegetation types and land-use practices significantly influence the future distribution of ECM fungi. T. austrosinensis and T. glaucoflora primarily thrive in broadleaf forests dominated by Castanopsis spp., while T. ganbajun and T. pseudoganbajun are predominantly found in coniferous forests dominated by Pinus yunnanensis and in broadleaf or mixed forests dominated by the tree species of Fagaceae. Analysis of vegetation types and land use shows that the area of suitable vegetation types for these fungi ranges from a minimum of 26.91% to a maximum of 49.18%. This indicates that within the predicted distribution range, over 50% of the area, although environmentally suitable for these fungi, is limited by the lack of host plants. As land-use patterns change, areas currently unsuitable for ECM fungi may become favorable due to host plant migration or ecosystem restoration. Thus, ECM fungi can successfully establish in specific areas only when suitable environmental conditions coincide with the presence of appropriate host plants. It remains uncertain how effectively the four Thelephora species can migrate to new plant hosts or the extent of their ability to establish symbiotic relationships with alternative hosts; future reforestation efforts should prioritize species such as Pinus yunnanensis and Fagaceae as primary tree species. This reforestation strategy can effectively promote the restoration of habitats for ECM fungi.

This study utilized SDMs based on climate variables, elevation data, and vegetation type data, providing insights for identifying critical environmental variables affecting the distribution of Thelephora fungi, evaluating changes in distribution areas and migration trends under future climate scenarios, setting conservation priorities, and guiding habitat restoration efforts. However, these models have certain limitations when predicting fungal distribution ranges. First, the models did not adequately account for soil physicochemical properties and soil microbial communities, which may lead to biased predictions.28 Second, as ECM fungi, these species have complex interactions with their host plants, and their distribution is closely linked to the distribution of these hosts. Although the models considered environmental variables, they did not fully capture the intricate relationships between host plants and fungi. Future research should incorporate more comprehensive environmental data and ecological factors to improve the accuracy of SDM predictions. Additionally, incorporating field surveys and ecological studies of specific microhabitats, such as wind direction, wind speed, slope aspect, and forest gaps, into the models can help identify and protect critical existing habitats and host plants. This approach not only enhances the effectiveness of fungal conservation efforts but also improves their ecological functions and economic value.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations that should be considered. First, while the models incorporated comprehensive climatic and elevation variables, the inclusion of detailed soil physicochemical properties could further enhance prediction accuracy for ECM fungi distribution. Second, although the models considered plant-type data and environmental variables effectively captured the broad distribution patterns, incorporating more detailed plant-fungus interaction data could provide additional insights into the complex symbiotic relationships that influence Thelephora species distribution under changing climate conditions.

Method details

Taxonomy and habitat of the four ECM fungal species

The four ECM fungal species, T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, belong to Thelephorales, Basidiomycota. In terms of habitat, these species occupy specific ecological environments. T. austrosinensis is found in broad-leaved forests, particularly under trees such as Castanopsis chinensis, C. fabri, C. fissa, C. hystrix, Lithocarpus polystachyus, L. uvariifolius, and Schima superba; T. ganbajun typically grows in pine forests in Yunnan, especially under Pinus yunnanensis; T. glaucoflora is commonly found on the ground in forests of Castanopsis spp., while T. pseudoganbajun inhabits mixed coniferous and broad-leaved forests. These forests are predominantly composed of broad-leaved trees from the family Fagaceae and species of Castanopsis, along with some trees from the family Pinaceae. As ectomycorrhizal fungi, these Thelephora species are likely associated with tree species in Fagaceae and Pinaceae, indicating their ecological preferences and the types of vegetation they were supported. Understanding the specific climate zones and vegetation types in which these fungi grow is crucial for their conservation and management, given their economic and ecological importance.

Occurrence data collection

Data on the locations of the four Thelephora species were obtained from surveys of 151 fruiting body specimens conducted from 2022 to 2024. The latitude and longitude were recorded using the Global Positioning System (GPS) or Baidu Maps by the investigators. The numbers of presences recorded were 15, 87, 35, and 14 for T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, respectively. To avoid sampling errors that generate localized distribution points and overfitting patterns, ArcGIS 10.2.1 was utilized to spatially filter occurrence data using a 2.5-minute resolution grid with 5 km by 5 km cells. Buffer zones of 2.5 km radius were applied around each occurrence point, and cross-tabulation analysis ensured a maximum of one distribution point per grid cell.30 After retaining only one valid point per grid, we collected 15, 81, 33, and 14 distribution points for T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun, respectively, mapping their detailed distributions (Figure 1).

Environmental variables

To analyze the impact of climate change on future species distribution, two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) from the CMIP6 BCC-CSM2-MR model and the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), representing moderate sustainability and high emission scenarios, respectively, were utilized. Nineteen bioclimatic variables and elevation data for the current period, as well as projections for the 2050s and 2090s under these scenarios, were sourced from the WorldClim database (http://www.worldclim.org/). The spatial resolution of the data was set to 2.5 arc minutes (approximately 5 kilometers). All raster-formatted environmental variables were aligned to the WGS84 geospatial datum as the default parameter. To avoid model overfitting, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed on the twenty environmental variables before the model was constructed. Specifically, the distribution points of the Thelephora species were used to create a correlation matrix with SPSS 26.0, and relevant environmental variables were identified. According to the method outlined by,29 if pairs of environmental variables had a correlation coefficient greater than 0.8, the variable with the higher predictive contribution was retained.

Model calculation and evaluation

An ensemble modelling approach was adopted in this study to predict the ecological niche for T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun. The Biomod2 package (v4.3.1, https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/) in R was used to implement the ensemble models.

For each species, 75% of the location point data were used for training, and the remaining 25% were used to test the predictive ability of the model. Additionally, to better simulate the actual distribution and reduce spatial bias, 1000 pseudo-absence points were randomly generated across the study region using the default settings in Biomod2. The modeling process was replicated three times with a 75:25 data partitioning ratio for training and testing to assess model stability and quantify prediction uncertainty. This process was repeated three times during model construction to ensure robustness.

The accuracy of Biomod2 models was evaluated by AUC, TSS, and Kappa values. For AUC analysis, the AUC value was counted as the area enclosed by the ROC curve. AUC < 0.6 indicated failing performance, 0.6 ≤ AUC < 0.7 indicated poor performance, 0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8 indicated moderate performance, 0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9 indicated good performance, and 0.9 ≤ AUC < 1 indicated excellent performance.20 TSS scores ranged from -1 to 1, where values above 0.75 indicated excellent model performance.31 Kappa coefficients were measures of correlation between the model predictions and truth. Kappa values ranged from -1 to 1, where 1 indicated that 100% of prediction agrees with the truth, -1 indicated that 100% of prediction disagreed with the truth.32

Division of suitable habitat grades

The suitability classification of the four Thelephora species was processed using ArcGIS 10.2.1. The potential distribution probabilities (P) of these species were quantified on a scale from 0 to 1 and subsequently classified according to the established method.33

The SDMToolbox in ArcGIS was utilized to visualize habitat changes of the four Thelephora species under different climate conditions, and current and future potential habitat distributions were compared.34,35 The analysis was focused on mapping the suitable habitats under present and projected climate scenarios, allowing shifts in habitat distribution patterns to be identified. The results were categorized into three types: decreased area, increased area, and invariant area.36

Additionally, the centroids of suitable habitats and their migration distances for both current and future climate scenarios were calculated using the Centroid Analysis tool within the SDMToolbox in ArcGIS. This process involved determining the geographic center of the suitable habitat areas by applying the Zonal Statistics tool to extract the central tendency of each habitat cluster. The Centroid Shift tool was then utilized to measure the distances these centroids moved over time, providing detailed insights into potential shifts in species distribution due to climate change.

The vegetation types within the potential distribution ranges of four Thelephora fungi under current climatic conditions were analyzed using 1:1,000,000 vector data of vegetation types in China.37 Initially, the spatial distribution of each vegetation type was analyzed using ArcGIS. Subsequently, the distribution data were processed using Python 3.7.3 to calculate the distribution area and proportion of the four Thelephora fungi across different vegetation types. These vegetation types include subtropical coniferous forests, evergreen orchards, subtropical economic forests, subtropical and tropical evergreen broad-leaved forests, deciduous broad-leaved shrubs, and subtropical and tropical grasslands. Through statistical analysis, the distribution characteristics of each fungus in the various vegetation types were determined, and corresponding area and proportion data were generated.

Species data collection and processing

Occurrence data for four Thelephora species (T. austrosinensis, T. ganbajun, T. glaucoflora, and T. pseudoganbajun) were collected through systematic field surveys conducted between 2022 and 2024 across southern China. A total of 151 fungal specimens were documented during surveys targeting suitable forest habitats. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) were recorded using the GPS or Baidu Maps by investigators with positional accuracy within 10 meters. All fungal specimens were identified through ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) gene sequencing to confirm species identity. Initial occurrence records included 15 locations for T. austrosinensis, 87 for T. ganbajun, 35 for T. glaucoflora, and 14 for T. pseudoganbajun. To minimize spatial autocorrelation and reduce overfitting, occurrence records were spatially filtered using ArcGIS 10.2.1. A grid-based filtering approach was implemented by creating a 2.5-minute resolution grid (approximately 5 km by 5 km cells) with 2.5 km radius buffer zones around each occurrence point. Cross-tabulation analysis ensured a maximum of one occurrence record per grid cell, resulting in 15, 81, 33, and 14 spatially independent distribution points for the four species, respectively.

Environmental data and variable selection

Current and future bioclimatic variables were obtained from the WorldClim database version 2.1 at 2.5 arc-minute spatial resolution. The dataset included 19 bioclimatic variables from Bio1 to Bio19 as well as elevation data, all projected to the WGS84 geographic coordinate system. Future climate projections for the 2050s and 2090s were obtained from the CMIP6 database using two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways: SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. Climate data were sourced from the BCC-CSM2-MR global climate model. To reduce multicollinearity, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed on all 20 environmental variables using SPSS version 26.0. When correlation coefficients between variable pairs exceeded the absolute value of 0.8, the variable with lower contribution to preliminary MaxEnt model performance was excluded. Variable contribution was assessed through single-variable models, with variables showing higher AUC values retained in cases of high correlation.

Species distribution modeling

Ensemble species distribution modeling was implemented using the Biomod2 package version 4.3.1 in R version 4.3.0, integrating ten different algorithms: GLM, GAM, CTA, ANN, SRE, MaxEnt, RF, GBM, MARS, and FDA. For each species, occurrence data were randomly partitioned using a 75:25 ratio for training and testing. To address the absence of confirmed absence data, 1000 pseudo-absence points were randomly generated across the study region using the default settings in Biomod2. The modeling process was replicated three times to assess model stability and quantify prediction uncertainty. Model performance was evaluated using AUC Area Under the Curve, TSS True Skill Statistic, and Cohen Kappa coefficient. AUC values were interpreted as: less than 0.6 indicating failing performance, 0.6 to less than 0.7 indicating poor performance, 0.7 to less than 0.8 indicating moderate performance, 0.8 to less than 0.9 indicating good performance, and 0.9 to less than 1 indicating excellent performance. TSS values above 0.75 indicated excellent model performance. Final habitat suitability maps were generated by calculating the weighted mean of predictions from models with TSS greater than 0.7, with weights determined by individual model TSS scores.

Habitat change analysis

Continuous habitat suitability predictions with values from 0 to 1 were reclassified into discrete categories using established thresholds: unsuitable from 0.0 to 0.25, low suitability from 0.25 to 0.5, moderate suitability from 0.5 to 0.75, and high suitability from 0.75 to 1.0. Spatial changes between current and future climate scenarios were quantified using SDMToolbox in ArcGIS 10.2.1, categorizing habitat changes as stable areas, lost areas, and gained areas. Net change in suitable habitat area was calculated as: Net Change in percentage equals Gained Area minus Lost Area divided by Current Suitable Area multiplied by 100.

Additional spatial analysis

Geographic centroids of suitable habitat areas were calculated using area-weighted mean coordinates of suitable habitat cells with suitability equal to or greater than 0.25. Migration distances were calculated as great circle distances between current and future centroids using the Haversine formula. Vegetation types within predicted suitable habitats were analyzed using the Vegetation Map of the People's Republic of China at 1:1,000,000 scale to determine the proportion of different vegetation categories. Variable importance was assessed using Biomod2 randomization procedure, where each variable was individually randomized and the resulting decrease in model performance indicated variable importance.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and model evaluation

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0 with primary packages: biomod2 version 4.3.1, raster version 3.6-23, sp version 2.1-1, and rgdal version 1.6-7. Spatial analyses used ArcGIS 10.2.1, and statistical summaries used SPSS 26.0. The study utilized 143 spatially filtered occurrence records across four species, with each modeling run incorporating 1000 pseudo-absence points. Sample sizes were determined by available occurrence data following standard practice in species distribution modeling.

Model performance was evaluated using AUC Area Under the Curve, TSS True Skill Statistic, and Cohen Kappa coefficient. AUC values with 95 percent confidence intervals were calculated using bootstrap resampling procedures inherent in Biomod2. TSS values above 0.75 indicated excellent model performance, and Kappa coefficients served as measures of agreement between predictions and observations. Model uncertainty was quantified using coefficient of variation across ensemble predictions from the three replicate runs. Final habitat suitability maps were generated by calculating the weighted mean of predictions from models with TSS greater than 0.7, with weights determined by individual model TSS scores. All analyses followed reproducible research practices with fixed random seeds for stochastic processes and documented parameter settings. Model predictions incorporated cross-validation procedures built into the Biomod2 framework to assess model performance and reduce overfitting.
