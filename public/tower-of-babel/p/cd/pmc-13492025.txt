Insight into the molecular characteristics of community-associated Staphylococcus aureus isolates from pediatric patients in South China
Authors: Huamiao Tang, Cong Li, Yun Zhou, Xiangmin Zhou, Keni Jian, Xing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-associated methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (CA-MRSA) poses a significant global public health threat. However, data on community-associated S. aureus (CA-SA) infections in children in Hainan remain scarce. This study aimed to characterize the molecular characteristics of CA-SA isolates obtained from pediatric patients at a university hospital in Hainan, the southernmost province of China.MethodsA total of 117 CA-SA isolates, including 31 MRSA and 86 methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA), were collected in Hainan between July 2023 and May 2025. These isolates were characterized using multilocus sequence typing (MLST), staphylococcal protein A (spa) typing, staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) typing, and analyses of antimicrobial resistance and virulence gene profiles.ResultsEighteen distinct sequence types (STs) and 49 spa types were identified among the 117 isolates, with ST398-t011(15,12.8%) and ST22-t039(15,12.8%) being the most prevalent. ST398 and ST22 were the dominant clones among MSSA isolates, whereas ST59-SCCmec IV/V(10, 8.5%) and ST45-SCCmec IV(9,7.7%) were the predominant clones among MRSA isolates. The prevalence of CA-MRSA was 26.5%, with SCCmec types IV and V accounting for 71% and 29% of cases, respectively. All isolates exhibited susceptibility to tigecycline, teicoplanin, linezolid, and vancomycin, while 69 isolates (59.0%) were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR). Compared to CA-MSSA strains, CA-MRSA isolates demonstrated significantly higher resistance rates to cefoxitin, oxacillin, erythromycin, clindamycin, and levofloxacin. The predominant resistance genes for erythromycin and tetracycline were ermC (77.2%, 51/66) and tetK (82.4%, 28/34), respectively. Among the 32 virulence genes tested, hla, hld, icaA, and clfA were detected in all isolates, whereas lukM was absent in all. The seb, sel, sek, and seq genes were more frequently identified in CA-MRSA isolates, while sdrD and lukE were more commonly detected in CA-MSSA isolates.ConclusionsThis study represents the first report on the molecular characteristics of CA-SA strains isolated from pediatric populations in Hainan, identifying ST398, ST22, ST59, and ST45 as the predominant genotypes. Notably, CA-MRSA epidemic strains in this region exhibit substantial molecular heterogeneity, reflecting distinct regional epidemiological patterns. These findings provide updated insights into the molecular epidemiology of S. aureus strains, offering critical reference data for clinical management and infection prevention strategies in pediatric settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05362-4.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a leading human pathogen responsible for both community- and hospital-acquired infections, imposing a substantial clinical burden on pediatric populations worldwide [1, 2]. A critical challenge in clinical management stems from its remarkable capacity to develop multidrug resistance, severely limiting therapeutic options [3]. Since its initial identification in a British hospital in 1961, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has disseminated globally as hospital-associated MRSA (HA-MRSA), primarily affecting patients with risk factors such as advanced age, comorbidities, recent surgical interventions, or indwelling medical devices [4]. In the early 1990s, a novel clone emerged, designated community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA), which causes infections in otherwise healthy individuals without recent healthcare exposure or traditional risk factors [5]. CA-MRSA infections predominantly manifest as skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs), with numerous outbreaks reported across diverse community settings [6]. Genetically divergent from HA-MRSA, CA-MRSA is characterized by reduced resistance to non-β-lactam antibiotics, carriage of smaller staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) elements, and distinct exotoxin profiles [7].

The pathogenicity of CA-SA is primarily mediated by a diverse array of virulence factors, including microbial surface component recognising adhesive matrix molecules (MSCRAMMs), hemolysins, bicomponent leukocidins (e.g., Panton-Valentine leukocidin [PVL]), staphylococcal enterotoxins (SEs), toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1), and exfoliative toxins (ETs) [8]. MSCRAMMs constitute a major class of adhesins that facilitate the attachment of S. aureus to host cells and extracellular matrix proteins. Hemolysins and leukocidins are cytolytic toxins that induce lysis of erythrocytes or leukocytes via pore-forming mechanisms in target cell membranes. Both SEs and TSST-1 are classified as staphylococcal superantigens, which induce excessive T-lymphocyte activation and proliferation, contributing to clinical manifestations such as food poisoning and toxic shock syndrome. Exfoliative toxins, as epidermolytic toxins, are predominantly associated with localized skin infections (e.g., bullous impetigo) and systemic conditions, including staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome [9]. Notably, PVL is a well-characterized virulence determinant in CA-MRSA infections, specifically targeting neutrophils and strongly linked to severe clinical presentations, such as necrotizing pneumonia and aggressive skin and soft tissue infections [10]. Therefore, characterizing the toxin gene profile of S. aureus is essential for elucidating its molecular pathogenesis and improving clinical management strategies.

Epidemiological studies have shown that the molecular characteristics of CA-MRSA vary with geography [11]. For instance, ST1-SCCmecIV and ST8-SCCmecIV clones are more common in North America, whereas ST30-SCCmecIV clones are more frequently observed in Australia, ST80-SCCmecIV clones are primarily distributed across Europe [11, 12]. In China, ST59-SCCmecIV/V remains the dominant CA-MRSA clone, with ST30-SCCmecIV, ST88-SCCmecIV, and ST121-SCCmecV also reported regionally [3]. The molecular epidemiology of CA-SA is dynamic, with emerging clones displacing established lineages. ST121 was previously the most frequent clone among CA-MRSA isolates from skin and soft tissue infections in China in 2016 [13]. The ST398 strain has also been reported as the most prevalent CA-SA clone in Beijing and Jiangsu, China [14, 15]. Notably, a recent study identified PVL⁺ ST22 MSSA as the primary epidemic CA-SA clone for SSTIs in Beijing, replacing the previously predominant PVL⁺ ST398 lineage [16]. Molecular surveillance of CA-SA is critical for guiding clinical treatment and infection control strategies. However, pediatric-specific data from Hainan Province remain scarce. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the molecular characteristics, antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes profiles of CA-SA isolates collected from pediatric patients between 2023 and 2025 at a teaching hospital in Hainan, China.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

This study was conducted in the Hainan Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center (SCMC-HN), a university-affiliated teaching hospital situated in South China that offers a comprehensive spectrum of healthcare services, spanning from primary to tertiary care. From July 1, 2023, to May 31, 2025, all the clinical CA-SA isolates from children aged 0–18 years, excluding the colonizing strains, were retrospectively collected in this study. Children were categorized based on internationally recognized age standards: newborn (0 to 28 days), infant (1 month to 1 year), toddler (1 to 3 years), preschool child (3 to 6 years), school-age child (6 to 12 years), and adolescent (12 to 18 years) [17]. Colonizing strains are microbial strains that establish and maintain stable growth on or within a host’s body surfaces or cavities without causing clinical signs of disease or triggering a significant host immune response [18]. They mainly come from the host’s native flora, maternal transmission, person-to-person contact, the environment and healthcare facilities. The patient’s age and gender, admission type as well as the clinical sources, were recorded when the CA-SA isolates were collected.

CA-MRSA has been traditionally defined as any MRSA infection diagnosed for an outpatient or an inpatient within 48 h of hospitalization without the healthcare-associated risk factors, including hemodialysis, surgery, history of hospitalization, residence in a long-term care facility, or use of indwelling catheters in the previous 12 months [19]. For neonates, CA-MRSA infection is defined as culture-confirmed MRSA infection with the first positive culture obtained from outpatients or within 48 h of birth admission, and no evidence of perinatal nosocomial MRSA exposure [20]. It is predominantly caused by maternal-fetal vertical transmission from colonized mothers, with no association with healthcare-associated invasive procedures or prolonged hospitalization.

All S. aureus isolates were derived from different clinical sources, including wound secretion(36,30.8%), abscess(41,35.0%), sputum(33,28.2%), bronchoalveolar lavage fluid(3,2.6%), pharynx swabs(2,1.7%), marrow(1,0.9%), and stool(1,0.9%). These isolates were initially identified by conventional microbiological methods, including Gram staining, catalase, and coagulase tests, and confirmed with a VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France). All isolates were stored at − 80 °C for further experiments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hainan Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of all S. aureus isolates was conducted using the VITEK-2 compact system (bioMérieux, France), incorporating both broth microdilution and disk diffusion methods. The following antibiotics were evaluated: penicillin(PEN), cefoxitin(FOX), oxacillin(OXA), erythromycin (ERY), clindamycin(CLI), tetracycline(TET), gentamicin(GEN), levofloxacin (LEV), moxifloxacin(MXF), rifampicin (RIF), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT), linezolid (LZD), tigecycline(TGC), teicoplanin(TPN), and vancomycin(VAN). S. aureus ATCC 25,923 served as the quality control strain, and results were interpreted in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines [21].

MRSA isolates were initially screened via the cefoxitin disk diffusion method and confirmed by mecA gene detection. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus was defined as resistance to ≥ 3 distinct antimicrobial classes.

MLST, spa, and SCCmec typing

MLST was performed as previously described [22]. Seven housekeeping genes (arcC, aroE, glpF, gmk, pta, tpi, and yqiL) were amplified following the protocol available on the MLST database website (https://pubmlst.org/) [23]. PCR-amplified fragments were sequenced on an ABI 3730xl Genetic Analyzer using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Sequencing Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing data were submitted to the MLST database for sequence type (ST) assignment. Clonal complexes (CCs) were determined based on related STs, as outlined on the MLST website [24].

Spa typing was conducted by analyzing variations in the polymorphic X region of the spa gene [25]. Sequencing data were submitted to the Ridom spa server (http://spaserver.ridom.de) for spa type determination. S. aureus isolates that could not be assigned to any known spa type were classified as nontypable (NT). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA4, based on partial spa gene sequences, with bootstrap analysis performed using 1,000 replicates.

All MRSA isolates underwent SCCmec typing as previously reported [26]. MRSA isolates suspected of harboring SCCmec type IV were further characterized into subtypes IVa, IVb, IVc, and IVd using multiplex PCR, as described by Milheirico et al. [27].

Detection of virulence genes and antibiotic resistance genes

The presence of 32 virulence and 7 antibiotic resistance genes was analyzed in 117 S. aureus strains. The following 32 staphylococcal virulence genes were screened: the staphylococcal enterotoxin genes (sea, seb, sec, sed, see, seg, seh, sei, sej, sel, sem, sen, seo, sep, seq, sek), the adhesin genes (clfA, icaA, sdrC, sdrD, and sdrE), the toxic shock syndrome toxin (tsst-1), the exfoliative toxin genes (eta, etb), the leukocidin genes (lukS-PV and lukF-PV, lukE, lukM), and the hemolysin genes (hla, hlb, hlg, hlg2, hld) as previously described [28].

Additionally, seven antibiotic resistance genes, including the penicillin resistance gene (blaZ), the erythromycin resistance genes (ermA, ermB, ermC), and the tetracycline resistance genes(tetL, tetK, tetM), were examined using PCR assays as previously described [29–31].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test .Categorical variables, including the distribution of virulence genes and phenotypic characteristics among the four dominant ST groups, were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Raw p-values from Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests were further adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control type I error arising from repeated multiple pairwise comparisons. All statistical inferences were based on FDR-corrected p-values, and a corrected p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of pediatric patients with community-acquired S. aureus infection

A total of 117 non-repetitive community-acquired S. isolates were collected from pediatric patients admitted to Hainan Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center between July 2023 and May 2025. Among these, 31 (26.5%) were classified as CA-MRSA and 86 (73.5%) as CA-MSSA.

As shown in Table 1, the median age of the patients was 1.4 years (range: 10 days to 15 years), with 79.5% of patients aged ≤ 6 years. The cohort comprised 53.8% males and 46.2% females. The overall age distribution was as follows: 8 (6.8%) newborns, 44 (37.6%) infants, 24 (20.5%) toddlers, 17 (14.5%) preschool children, 21 (17.9%) school-age children, and 3 (2.6%) adolescents. No significant inter-group differences were found in age distribution between the MRSA and MSSA groups (all p > 0.05).

Regarding admission status, 33 patients (28.2%) were outpatients, all from the otolaryngology department, whereas 84 (71.8%) were inpatients distributed across the following departments: pediatric surgery (39, 36.9%), pediatric internal medicine (25, 23.8%), pediatric intensive care unit (13, 12.3%), neonatology (5, 4.7%), and hematological oncology department (2, 1.9%).

The most common infection type was skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI; 77, 65.8%), followed by respiratory tract infections (38, 32.5%), bloodstream infection (1, 0.9%), and digestive tract infection (1, 0.9%) (Table 1).

Molecular typing of S. aureus isolates

The evolutionary and genetic diversity of 117 Staphylococcus aureus isolates from pediatric patients was analyzed using MLST (Table 2). A total of 18 distinct STs were identified, with ST398 being the most prevalent (23.9%, 28/117), followed by ST22 (17.9%, 21/117), ST59 (12.0%, 14/117), and ST45 (12.0%, 14/117). These four dominant STs accounted for 65.8% of all isolates. Other STs included ST15 (7.0%, 7/117), ST7 (5.1%, 6/117), ST121 (3.4%, 4/117), ST188 (3.4%, 4/117), ST5 (2.6%, 3/117), ST26 (2.6%, 3/117), ST88 (2.6%, 3/117), ST1 (1.7%, 2/117), ST8 (1.7%, 2/117), ST509 (1.7%, 2/117), and four rare STs (ST338, ST537, ST1212, and ST944; each represented by a single isolate). The 18 STs were clustered into 15 clonal complexes (CCs), with CC398 (23.9%, 28/117), CC22 (17.9%, 21/117), CC59 (13.7%, 16/117), and CC45 (12.8%, 15/117) being the most prevalent.

Forty-nine spa types were identified among the 117 isolates. The most common spa types were t011 (12.8%, 15/117) and t309 (12.8%, 15/117), followed by t116 (12.0%, 14/117), t437 (6.8%, 8/117), t034 (3.4%, 4/117), t571 (2.6%, 3/117), t1255 (2.6%, 3/117), t084 (2.6%, 3/117), t091 (2.6%, 3/117), and t1376 (2.6%, 3/117). SCCmec typing was performed on 31 MRSA isolates, revealing that 22 (71.0%) carried SCCmec type IVa, while 9 (29.0%) carried SCCmec type V. Notably, SCCmec IVa was the most prevalent type among pediatric patients in this study.

Antimicrobial susceptibility and antibiotic resistance genes

The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of 117 clinical S. aureus isolates against 20 antimicrobial agents were evaluated (Fig. 2). All isolates demonstrated susceptibility to tigecycline, teicoplanin, linezolid, and vancomycin. Penicillin exhibited the highest resistance rate (95.7%, 112/117), followed by erythromycin (56.4%, 66/117) and clindamycin (53.8%, 63/117). Lower resistance rates were observed for gentamicin (0.9%, 1/117), moxifloxacin (0.9%, 1/117), rifampicin (1.7%, 2/117), levofloxacin (2.6%, 3/117), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (6.0%, 7/117), oxacillin (26.5%, 31/117), and tetracycline (29.1%, 34/117).

Among the 117 isolates, 69 (59.0%) were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), including 31 MRSA and 38 MSSA isolates. The prevalence of MDR was significantly higher in MRSA than in MSSA isolates (p < 0.001). Further analysis revealed that 10 isolates (14.5%) exhibited resistance to six antibiotic classes, 18 (26.1%) to five classes, 8 (11.6%) to four classes, and 33 (47.8%) to three classes. Notably, 89.9% (62/69) of MDR isolates demonstrated concurrent resistance to penicillin, erythromycin, and clindamycin.

The blaZ gene was detected in 116 (99.1%) of the 117 S. aureus isolates, with all penicillin-resistant strains carrying this gene. Notably, four penicillin-susceptible isolates also harbored blaZ. Among the 66 erythromycin-resistant isolates, the ermC gene was the most prevalent (77.2%, 51/66), followed by ermB (22.7%, 15/66) and ermA (1.5%, 1/66). Notably, three of these isolates were found to co-harbor both the ermC and ermB genes simultaneously. The detection rate of ermB was significantly higher in erythromycin-resistant MRSA strains than in erythromycin-resistant MSSA strains (Table 3). Of the 34 tetracycline-resistant isolates, the tetK gene was identified in 28 (82.4%) and tetL in 2 (5.9%) strains. No isolates tested positive for tetM, while four tetracycline-resistant isolates lacked any detectable tet gene.

Distribution of virulence genes among 117 S. aureus strains

The distribution of 32 putative virulence genes varied significantly among the 117 S. aureus strains (Table 3). The total number of virulence genes per strain ranged from 6 to 21, with 72.6% (85/117) carrying ≥ 10 genes. All strains tested positive for hla, hld, icaA, and clfA, while none carried lukM.

Among the staphylococcal enterotoxin (SE) genes, the seg-sei-sem-sen-seo gene cluster was the most prevalent, detected in 43.6% (51/117) of the isolates, followed by sek (32.5%, 38/117), seq (32.5%, 38/117), sel (21.4%, 25/117), seb (13.7%, 16/117), and Sect. (12.8%, 15/117). Other SE genes exhibited low detection rates (< 7% of isolates), with sed, sej, see, and sep absent in MRSA strains. The presence of enterotoxin genes was strongly associated with specific STs. The carriage of SE genes was strongly associated with specific STs: all ST22, ST45, ST121, and ST1212 isolates carried the seg-sei-sem-sen-seo cluster, whereas most ST59 and ST398 isolates harbored the sek-seq genes. The see-sep genes were mainly detected in ST88 strains, seh in ST1 strains, and sed-sej in ST5 strains (Fig. 1).

All S. aureus isolates carried adhesion-related genes, with icaA and clfA detected in all 117 strains (100%), followed by sdrC (76.9%, 90/117) and sdrE (52.1%, 61/117). The hemolysin genes hla, and hld were universally present, whereas hlb was detected in only 45.3% (53/117) of isolates. Exfoliative toxin genes (eta and etb) and the tsst-1 gene exhibited low prevalence. Specifically, eta was identified in four ST121 isolates, while etb was found in three ST121 and two ST509 strains. The tsst-1 gene was detected in 1.7% (2/117) of isolates. In contrast, the genes pvl were relatively common, present in 51.3% (60/117) of the isolates across 11 distinct STs. ST398 accounted for the highest proportion of PVL-positive isolates (36.7%, 22/60), followed by ST22 (35.0%, 21/60); notably, all ST22 isolates in this study harbored the pvl genes (Fig. 1).

Comparative molecular characterization of CA-MRSA and CA-MSSA

High genetic diversity was observed among both CA-MRSA and CA-MSSA strains. A total of 13 distinct STs and 39 spa types were identified among CA-MSSA isolates, while CA-MRSA strains exhibited nine STs and 14 spa types. Among CA-MSSA isolates, ST398-MSSA (31.4%, 27/86) was the most prevalent clone, followed by ST22-MSSA (18.6%, 16/86) and ST15-MSSA (8.1%, 7/86). In contrast, ST59-MRSA-SCCmecIV/V (32.3%, 10/31), the predominant CA-MRSA clone in China, remained the most common among pediatric CA-MRSA isolates in Hainan.

Antimicrobial susceptibility analysis revealed distinct resistance profiles between the two groups. All CA-MSSA isolates were susceptible to cefoxitin, oxacillin, moxifloxacin, and levofloxacin, whereas all CA-MRSA isolates demonstrated resistance to oxacillin, penicillin, and cefoxitin. Furthermore, CA-MRSA isolates exhibited significantly higher resistance rates than CA-MSSA isolates for cefoxitin (100% vs. 0%), oxacillin (100% vs. 0%), erythromycin (77.4% vs. 48.8%), clindamycin (77.4% vs. 45.3%), and levofloxacin (9.7% vs. 0%) (all p < 0.05). (Fig. 3).

Notably, CA-MRSA isolates consistently lacked eta, sed, see, and sep, which were exclusively identified in CA-MSSA strains.

Comparative characterization of dominant strains ST398, ST22, ST59 and ST45

Four predominant types were discerned among both MSSA and MRSA isolates. As showed in Table 2, ST398 and ST22 emerged as the predominant clones within the MSSA isolates, whereas ST59-SCCmecIV/V and ST45-SCCmecIV represented the major clones among MRSA isolates. Stratified stratification by infection site revealed that ST22 (24.7% vs. 2.6%, p = 0.003) and ST59 (13.9% vs. 2.6%, p = 0.033) isolates exhibited significantly higher isolation rates in skin and soft tissue infections compared with respiratory tract infections. By contrast, ST45 isolates were more frequently isolated from respiratory tract than from skin and soft tissue (5.2% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.002). No significant difference in ST398 isolation rates was observed between the two infection sites (24.7% vs. 23.7%, p = 0.907) (Sup. Table 2) .

Notable disparities in antimicrobial resistance rates were observed among the four principal ST strains (Fig. 4). Specifically, ST22 strains exhibited significantly higher resistance rates to erythromycin (71.4% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.047) and clindamycin(71.4% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.047) compared to ST398 strains. Conversely, ST398 strains demonstrated elevated resistance rates to tetracycline relative to ST22 (50% vs. 4.8%, p = 0.001) and ST45 (50% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.007) strains. Furthermore, the resistance rates of ST59 and ST45 strains to cefoxitin and oxacillin surpassed those of ST22(71.4% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.013;64.3% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.017) and ST398 strains(71.4% vs. 3.6%, p < 0.001;64.3% vs. 3.6%, p < 0.001). Notably, all four ST strains harbored the blaZ gene, and ST59 (7/14, 50.0%) and ST398 (14/28, 50.0%) isolates exhibited identical tetK carriage rates. The ermC gene was detected in 71.4%(15/21) of ST22 isolates and 71.4%(10/14) of ST45 isolates, whereas the ermB gene was identified in 71.4%(10/14) of ST59 strains.

Discussion

CA-MRSA has emerged as a significant public health concern, especially among children and young healthy individuals. This study investigated the molecular characteristics, virulence gene profiles, and antibiotic resistance patterns of CA-SA isolates from pediatric patients in a teaching hospital, and represents the first report focusing on the molecular features of pediatric CA-SA isolates in Hainan Province.

The proportion of MRSA among CA-SA infections in Asian countries varies considerably, ranging from less than 5% to over 35% [32]. In China, a multicenter study conducted between 2005 and 2006 involving 4,254 S. aureus isolates collected from five major pediatric hospitals identified 73 (1.7%) MRSA strains, of which 38 (0.9%) were classified as CA-MRSA [33]. Another study at Beijing Children’s Hospital found that 14 out of 351 community-associated skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) (4%) were caused by MRSA [34]. In contrast, the prevalence of MRSA among CA-SA infections in our study was 26.5%, significantly higher than previously reported rates. Notably, MDR-SA was detected in 44.2% (38/86) of CA-MSSA isolates, with the most prevalent resistance phenotype manifesting as concomitant non-susceptibility to penicillin, erythromycin and clindamycin. This high MDR proportion contradicts the longstanding clinical consensus that community-associated MSSA generally harbours limited antimicrobial resistance [35]. From a molecular perspective, plasmid-borne blaZ and ermC predominantly shape the core resistance profile, which are commonly carried by diverse mobile genetic elements (MGEs) [36]. Such MGE-mediated horizontal gene transfer facilitates the rapid accumulation of multidrug resistance in circulating CA-MSSA clones among pediatric populations [36]. Epidemiological comparison with previous studies indicated that the present Hainan cohort exhibits a higher MDR prevalence of pediatric CA-MSSA than most regions across China and Southeast Asia [37, 38], implying distinct regional antimicrobial selective pressure. Clinically, this elevated MDR rate compromises the efficacy of conventional empirical antibiotic regimens for local pediatric CA-MSSA infections. Therefore, empirical use of penicillins, macrolides, and clindamycin should be prudent, and timely antimicrobial susceptibility testing is essential to guide targeted medication and constrain resistance dissemination.

The phylogenetic analysis revealed high genetic diversity of pediatric CA-SA isolates in Hainan. MLST typing further identified four dominant sequence types, including ST59, ST45, ST398, and ST22, which collectively constituted 65.8% of all isolates and shaped the distinct molecular epidemiological profile of pediatric CA-SA identified in our single tertiary hospital in Hainan.

In the present study, ST59-SCCmecIV/V was the most prevalent clone in our pediatric CA-MRSA collection, consistent with the endemic features of CA-MRSA circulating throughout mainland China [39, 40]. A molecular epidemiological study of CC59 MRSA from seven major domestic cities found that children constituted the main population affected by CC59 MRSA infections [39]. Additionally, ST59 was reported to be the dominant lineage, comprising 76.2% of CA-MRSA isolates from adult outpatients in China [40]. Epidemiological surveillance from other Asian regions further confirmed the endemicity of ST59 in Vietnam, Singapore and Hong Kong [41].

ST45-SCCmec IV-t116 was the second dominant clone among CA-MRSA isolates in this study, accounting for 23.1% of strains. Globally, MRSA ST45 is a genetically diverse lineage closely linked to severe invasive infections including bacteremia [42]. ST45-MRSA-IV also known as Berlin-IV or the Berlin epidemic strain, was initially isolated in Berlin hospitals in 1993, and has been a major clone over large areas of Germany, while ST45-SCCmecIV/V has also become endemic across Australia [42]. In China, ST45-SCCmecIV-t116 exhibits distinct geographical distribution: it circulates prevalently in Guangzhou and Hainan, but is only sporadically detected in other regions [43, 44]. Our results are in line with these epidemiological features. Such uneven regional distribution is likely shaped by local environmental conditions [45]. As China’s southernmost province, Hainan features a typical tropical monsoon maritime climate, which differs substantially from inland areas and may contribute to the unique molecular epidemiology of local CA-SA. Accordingly, continuous surveillance of circulating ST lineages is essential to optimize clinical treatment strategies and strengthen targeted infection prevention and control.

ST398 (23.9%) was the dominant CA-MSSA clone in pediatric patients in this study. Traditionally, ST398 is recognized as a typical livestock-associated S. aureus lineage that primarily colonizes pigs and commonly causes infections in individuals with animal exposure [46]. Consistently, this clone is well-documented to transmit zoonotically via direct livestock contact or contaminated environmental sources [47]. However, we did not find any association between livestock contact and ST398 infection. These findings indicate that the widespread dominance of ST398 in local pediatric CA-MSSA is largely attributable to enhanced human adaptability and sustained human-to-human community transmission [48]. In recent years, there has been an obvious increase in the prevalence of ST398, and it has evolved into one of the most prevalent MSSA lineages in China. A Shanghai-based study reported that the proportion of ST398 among CA-SA isolates rose from 1.8% in 2008 to 8.6% in 2018, making it the dominant CA-SA clone in that region by 2018 [49]. Likewise, a prior local investigation in Hainan also documented a notable increase in ST398-MSSA prevalence, from 5.5% during 2013–2014 to 18.4% in 2018–2019 [43]. Collectively, these findings underline the urgent need for long-term epidemiological surveillance to track the transmission dynamics of ST398 in pediatric populations.

ST22 is a typical healthcare-associated MRSA lineage. It was initially identified as EMRSA-15 in England in the early 1990s, and subsequently disseminated widely as a hospital-acquired MRSA clone throughout Europe and other continents, as well as Asian regions including Singapore and India [50]. While prevalent globally, ST22 was seldom detected in China until recently. First reported as the predominant clinical MSSA isolate in Chongqing in 2013 [51], this clone has gradually become prevalent among MSSA or CA-MSSA strains in Urumqi, Beijing, Kunming and other Chinese cities [16, 52, 53]. In the present pediatric cohort from Hainan, ST22 ranked as the second most dominant CA-MSSA genotype. Notably, a prior local survey covering 227 S. aureus isolates from hospitalized patients across three Hainan hospitals between 2013 and 2019 failed to identify any ST22 strains [43]. Considering the dynamic epidemiological changes of S. aureus, sustained molecular surveillance is essential to prevent further spread of this clone.

Notably, the present study observed a relatively high overall pvl rate (51.3%), with a higher prevalence in CA-MSSA (55.8%) than in CA-MRSA (38.7%). This finding is inconsistent with previous epidemiological investigations across China, which have consistently reported a relatively low pvl rate (15%–20%) in CA-MRSA and a higher prevalence of pvl in CA-MRSA than in CA-MSSA [11]. Such discrepancy can be primarily attributed to two inherent biases in the current study. First, the unique ST distribution of clinical isolates contributed to the biased PVL profile. In this study, ST22 and ST398 constituted the dominant CA-MSSA lineages, both of which exhibited high pvl carriage rates. The predominance of these PVL-enriched ST clones substantially increased the pvl positive proportion of CA-MSSA isolates, thereby reversing the conventional epidemiological distribution trend. Second, the high proportion of SSTI-derived isolates led to inevitable sampling bias. It is well recognized that PVL is strongly associated with skin and soft tissue infections [10], and the dominant enrollment of SSTI cases in the present study artificially increased the overall pvl rate. Collectively, the abnormal pvl distribution observed in our study reflects local ST genotype characteristics and sample source bias, rather than representing the general epidemiological characteristics of S. aureus in the overall population.

Some surface proteins, such as the products of the icaA, clfA, and sdrCDE genes, have been implicated in S. aureus biofilm formation. The present results are consistent with previous studies, which have shown that biofilm-related genes icaA and clfA are present in all isolates, while sdrC and sdrE are detected in most strains [54]. Notably, the distribution of virulence genes varies among S. aureus strains. Some virulence genes are ubiquitous in almost all S. aureus, while others are associated with specific molecular types, particularly staphylococcal enterotoxins (SEs). SEs are the main causative agents of food poisoning. All ST22, ST45, ST121, and ST1212 strains carry the seg-sei-sem-sen-seo genes, whereas most ST59 and ST398 isolates harbor the sek-seq genes. The see-sep genes are found in ST7 and ST88 strains, the seh genes are present in ST1 isolates, and the sed-sej genes are observed in ST5 strains.

This study has several inherent limitations. First, this is a retrospective single-center study with a limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader pediatric populations and other geographical regions. Subgroup analyses involving rare ST lineages with minimal isolates may have reduced statistical power and elevated false-negative risk. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are required to validate the findings. Second, 28.2% of the enrolled patients were outpatients with incomplete follow-up data, which precluded comprehensive analyses linking bacterial molecular features to clinical outcomes, such as treatment response, infection recurrence, and mortality. Third, the observed associations between ST lineages, virulence genes, and infection sites are merely epidemiological correlations; further functional experiments are necessary to verify the distinct pathogenic potentials of different ST clones. In addition, phylogenetic analysis based on partial spa fragments provides limited resolution for strain genetic relatedness and microevolution. The absence of whole-genome sequencing also restricted in-depth exploration of strain genomic characteristics and evolutionary patterns. Finally, the conventional 48-hour admission-based CA-SA classification criteria are adult-oriented and not fully suitable for neonates. Future pediatric S. aureus studies should adopt age-specific, neonatal-adapted classification standards to enhance research accuracy and rigor.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated relatively high prevalence of CA-MRSA and MDR-S. aureus among children with CA-SA infections in Hainan, and characterized the dominant molecular clones of MRSA and MSSA circulating locally. ST398 and ST22 were identified as the predominant MSSA lineages, while ST59-SCCmecIV/V and ST45-SCCmecIV represented the most common MRSA clones. The CA-SA strains in this region present remarkable molecular diversity, indicative of unique regional epidemiological features. Collectively, our findings enrich the epidemiological knowledge of pediatric S. aureus and provide valuable baseline data to guide clinical treatment and infection control practices in pediatric settings.
