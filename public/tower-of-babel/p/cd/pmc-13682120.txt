Facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions in the National Defence Forces of Ethiopia: a grounded theory study
Authors: Alemu Tesfahun Fida, Mirgissa Kaba, Alemayehu Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Ethiopian military implements health promotion interventions to improve and maintain fitness, health, and military readiness. However, distinct organizational features of military forces can affect the health of their members. This study aimed to explore facilitators and barriers to implementing health promotion interventions within Ethiopian military settings and develop a conceptual model based on the findings.MethodsA grounded theory approach was used in this qualitative study. We identified 26 participants among health experts engaged in health promotion and disease prevention interventions in Ethiopian military health facilities and health departments. Data were collected using face-to-face interviews with key open questions, which were recorded, transcribed verbatim in Amharic, and translated into English. The English transcripts were analyzed using open, axial, and selective coding with a constant comparative method, leading to the identification of a core category and the development of a conceptual model. ATLAS.ti was used to manage and code the data.ResultsThis study involved 26 experts (21 males and 5 females) working on health promotion interventions in a military context. Five key categories emerged as facilitators: organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors. Barriers were grouped into three categories: organizational, professional, and military factors. Key facilitators included information sharing, the proximity of health personnel at lower levels, sharing best experiences, trainings, working with a shared understanding and with partners, the chain of command, living in mass, and being young. Major barriers included resource constraints, staff turnover, the demanding nature of military work, living in mass, deployment in desert environments, and peer influence. The successful implementation of health promotion interventions in military settings is influenced by the dynamic interaction of organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors.ConclusionsThis study identified key facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions in the military context. The chain of command and the presence of health personnel at lower levels emerged as key facilitators, while resource constraints were important barriers. These findings, along with the conceptual model developed, provide valuable guidance for policymakers and practitioners involved in health promotion efforts within the Ethiopian military context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15193-8.

Introduction

The military is a distinct organization that could be described by its hierarchical structure, laws and regulations governing military personnel, and ethical uniqueness such as courage, dedication, and solidarity [1]. These organizational features have far-reaching implications for the health of military personnel. While military personnel share certain health habits with civilians, health promotion in military settings ought to be specifically tailored and integrated into military health interventions [2]. Health promotion views health as a resource for living productive lives, both individually and socially. This can enable individuals and communities to lead meaningful and socially engaged lives beyond merely the absence of disease. Health promotion involves actions that address a wide range of potentially modifiable health determinants, extending beyond individual behaviors to societal and environmental factors [3]. Health promotion interventions are essential for encouraging healthy lifestyle behaviors, which in turn help prevent and control chronic diseases [4]. Investment in these interventions can lead to reduced long-term healthcare demands and costs [5]. In military settings, such interventions are vital not only for enhancing health but also for maintaining readiness and reducing healthcare expenditures [6].

However, the military health system may face challenges in mitigating health risks while ensuring soldier readiness [7]. Military command and management play key roles in influencing certain health behaviors [8, 9]. Despite this, there are scarce military studies on facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions to inform and guide practitioners and military leaders. In civilian contexts, studies have identified leadership support as a facilitator, while resource constraints and organizational challenges were common barriers [10]. A qualitative study from the United Kingdom also highlighted a hectic work environment and financial constraints as barriers to workplace health interventions. While strategic approach, proper communication, and conducting assessments before, during, and after implementation were indicated as facilitators [11]. The success of health promotion interventions largely depends on the context in which they are implemented, including the availability of resources, organizational structures, and policies [12]. Infrastructure for health promotion, such as human and material resources, organizational and administrative structures, policies, regulations, and incentives, facilitates an organized health promotion response to public health issues and challenges [3].

In the Ethiopian National Defence Forces, health promotion and disease prevention interventions have focused on infectious diseases, but emerging non-communicable diseases have received comparatively less attention. During the study period, the Defence Health Main Department, which is under the National Defence Forces of Ethiopia and is responsible for the health of military personnel, did not have a separate unit/section that focused on non-communicable disease prevention and control in its structure, as it did for communicable disease prevention and control. There is a paucity of research-based evidence that could identify the specific facilitators and barriers to health promotion in resource-constrained military settings. As a result, decision-makers are often overwhelmed by routine health promotion and disease prevention reports that may not adequately address the unique health needs of military personnel. This study aimed to provide evidence-based insights into the facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions in Ethiopian military settings, with the goal of developing a conceptual model to guide practices and policy.

Methods

Setting

The study was conducted in the Ethiopian National Defence Forces. This study took both the ground force and the air force into account. The Defence Health Main Department, the Air Force Health Department, the Air Force Hospital, two air bases, and two divisions were included in the study.

Study design

To address the research gaps, a grounded theory approach was used to identify key facilitators and barriers to implementing health promotion interventions among Ethiopian military personnel and to develop a conceptual model. This approach was chosen due to the relatively unexplored nature of health promotion interventions in military contexts, particularly within Ethiopia. Grounded theory provides a systematic methodology to generate a robust and contextually relevant framework that can guide future interventions and policies [13].

Participants

The sample size estimation was based on conducting interviews with 25–30 key informants, depending on conceptual saturation [14]. This study included 26 key informants. Initially, purposive sampling was used to identify potential participants from military health facilities and health departments for health promotion interventions. Once identified, the interview dates and times were scheduled. The sampling method then shifted to theoretical sampling to refine key categories and expand upon emerging concepts [13]. The study included health professionals who had direct experience in planning, implementing, supervising, or evaluating health promotion interventions within the Ethiopian National Defence Forces. Participants were selected because they possessed rich experiential knowledge relevant to the research objective and represented different levels of the military health system.

Data collection

This study explored the facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions from health professionals in the Ethiopian National Defense Forces. Data were obtained through key informant face-to-face interviews conducted between February and June 2021. The participants included health professionals and experts working in disease prevention and health promotion programs at defense health care institutions and health departments. Interviews were conducted at the participants’ workplaces on their convenient schedule, with no distractions during the interview. After obtaining informed consent, study participants were briefed on the purpose of the study and completed a form with some demographic questions. The first participant interview was analyzed, and additional interviews were conducted with the same participant to refine the questions, which served as a checklist to guide the interview, which primarily included open-ended questions such as “What are the facilitators of implementing health promotion interventions?” and “What are the barriers to implementing health promotion interventions?” The interviewer, the principal investigator, was adaptable in terms of question formulation and sequencing. If more questions were needed, they were asked. The questions were modified during the data collection to refine the collected data [15].

At the end of each interview, participants were invited to share any additional thoughts on health promotion interventions in military settings that had not been addressed. Data collection continued until conceptual saturation was reached. Before each interview, participants were informed that the interviews had been audio-recorded using a digital sound recorder, and recording proceeded only after obtaining their informed consent. Interviews lasted between 30 min and 1 h and 25 min. The principal investigator personally transcribed the interviews verbatim in Amharic and then translated the transcripts into English while preserving their original meaning and context. Each transcript was reviewed alongside the original audio recording to verify completeness and correct any transcription errors. To ensure translation accuracy, the English transcripts were back-translated into Amharic compared with the original transcripts. Identifying information was removed during transcription to protect participant confidentiality. The English transcripts were then used for coding and subsequent data analysis.

Data analysis

The interview data were analyzed using the constant comparative method in line with Strauss and Corbin’s (2015) grounded theory approach, allowing for continuous comparison between units of data throughout the collection process [16]. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection. As a result, after each interview, the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English (available as a supplementary file), and analysis followed a three-stage coding approach that included open, axial, and selective coding after being imported into ATLAS.ti version 7 [15, 17]. During open coding, translated data were broken down into concepts, labeled, and examined to develop initial codes [17, 18]. These codes were compared and categorized based on their similarities. Axial coding involved further organizing these codes into categories and subcategories and identifying relationships between them. In the final stage, selective coding, the core category was established by focusing on a key category and refining the meaning [15, 17, 19]. This process helped integrate and develop the emerging conceptual model. Theoretical sampling was used throughout the study to recruit new participants and explore evolving insights from the data. As data analysis progressed, selective coding allowed for the integration and refinement of categories, ultimately leading to the development of a conceptual model [20]. To ensure the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings and interpretations, participant feedback was sought, with some participants confirming the identified facilitators and barriers [18]. Additionally, memos were captured during the data collection and analysis period to document immediate insight, track the development of categories, and explore connections between them [21].

Reflexivity

The principal investigator is a public health professional with previous experience working in the Defence Health Department ten years prior to this study. This background provided valuable contextual knowledge of the military health systems and enhanced understanding of the study setting. Importantly, the investigator was not directly involved in the implementation of health promotion interventions, thereby maintaining an impartial stance when exploring facilitators and barriers to health promotion practice. Recognizing that prior experience in military health could influence data interpretation, the investigator engaged in ongoing reflexive practice throughout the study. This included maintaining analytic memos to document emerging thoughts and assumptions and seeking participant feedback to ensure that the developing interpretations and conceptual categories remained grounded in participants’ experiences rather than the researcher’s preconceptions and co-authors to examine and challenge emerging interpretations. These reflexive strategies enhanced the credibility and trustworthiness of the study by promoting continual reflection throughout the process of data collection and analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Addis Ababa University College of Health Sciences. Before conducting the interviews, the principal investigator explained the aim of the study and emphasized that participation was voluntary. Participants provided informed consent before proceeding with the interview, which was voice-recorded with their permission. To protect participant confidentiality and anonymity, each participant was assigned an alphanumeric code. The data were securely kept, and access was restricted to the study team. Additionally, special care was taken to maintain the confidentiality of sensitive military-related information. Other ethical principles of autonomy, justice, and beneficence were upheld in accordance with the ethical principles of research involving human participants, as indicated in the Declaration of Helsinki [22].

Results

Demographic information of the participants

A total of 26 experts (21 males and 5 females) working in health promotion interventions at different levels in military settings were included in this study. The age range of the study participants was from 28 to 58 years old. The educational level of the participants varied from bachelor’s degrees to master’s degrees, and their working experience ranged from 1 to 43 years.

Core category

The categories identified through data analysis were used to develop a conceptual model that summarizes the key facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions in the Ethiopian National Defence Forces (Fig. 1). The core category, “improving health promotion interventions implementation in military settings,” reflected the study participants’ main concern: increasing participation in these interventions by enhancing facilitators and minimizing barriers. Facilitators were defined as factors that support the successful implementation of health promotion interventions, including organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors. In contrast, barriers were defined as factors that hinder the implementation of health promotion interventions, primarily related to organizational, professional, and military factors.

Facilitators and barriers to health promotion interventions in Ethiopian military settings conceptual model

The success of health promotion interventions in Ethiopian military settings depends on a dynamic interaction between organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors. Facilitators such as strong institutional support, clear communication channels, and collaboration across hierarchical levels enhance the success of interventions. However, barriers, including weak organizational support, limited professional engagement, and challenges unique to military contexts such as a rigid commanding system and remote and dispersed deployments, can impede implementation progress. Addressing these barriers while reinforcing facilitators is crucial for increasing participation and improving intervention outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained military contexts.

The following paragraphs describe these facilitators and barriers in detail, according to the thematic categories.

Facilitators of health promotion interventions

Organizational factor

Instructions, guidelines/standard protocols, reminders, and information sharing were elements of organizational factors as facilitators. Including instructions or guidelines in the operational plan can facilitate smoother implementation. Regular meetings, whether weekly or monthly, served as reminders and reinforced the importance of following through with health promotion interventions. These meetings also provided opportunities for review and daily communication with health institutions to identify gaps or the need for technical assistance. Participants highlighted the importance of a communication and collaboration system between higher-level and lower-level health professionals within military health. As one participant noted:

We communicate with them [health professionals at a lower level] by phone about the problem they have. Since there are health professionals at the sites, they work on their interventions. Prompt support from us if there is any, and the same thing if they need technical support. [P 13]

We communicate with them [health professionals at a lower level] by phone about the problem they have. Since there are health professionals at the sites, they work on their interventions. Prompt support from us if there is any, and the same thing if they need technical support. [P 13]

This interaction reflected a well-organized structure where lower-level health professionals operated with a degree of autonomy in managing interventions, yet they could still access additional expertise when required. Timely communication plays a critical role in ensuring that interventions are successfully implemented, with higher-level health professionals providing consistent support and oversight. This combination of autonomy and structured communication was key to maintaining the effectiveness of health promotion interventions.

Structural factors

This category encompasses the proximity of health personnel and the integration of a health structure within the defense structure. The military’s hierarchical organization, extending health services to the lowest levels, provided fertile ground for implementing health promotion interventions once they were approved by top commands. Due to the strict adherence to command orders, responsible individuals readily adopted these interventions as part of their duties. Participants described the hierarchical organization of health personnel, from the highest levels down to the company level. As one participant reported:Health personnel are assigned across the organizational structure, extending from defence command down to the company level. The health structure cascades through the defence, command, air force, division, battalion, and company levels, ensuring coverage at all organizational structures from the top to the bottom. [P26]

Health personnel are assigned across the organizational structure, extending from defence command down to the company level. The health structure cascades through the defence, command, air force, division, battalion, and company levels, ensuring coverage at all organizational structures from the top to the bottom. [P26]

This indicated a well-defined and structured health system that could ensure services were accessible and integrated at every level of the military. Such a hierarchical system plays a key role in facilitating the effective implementation of health promotion interventions. It also raised questions about the system’s ability to support communication, resource allocation, and the overall quality of interventions across different levels.

The presence of health personnel at the lowest levels of the military organization, such as frontline health workers, can enhance health promotion interventions. These personnel were strategically positioned to provide immediate technical or professional support. Additionally, the integration of a health structure within the defense system, extending down to the company level, along with the establishment of health committees at the unit level, may further facilitate the implementation of health promotion interventions. Participants provided detailed insights into how this health structure was embedded within the lower tiers of the military’s organizational hierarchy. As one participant indicated:The other is that the health structure reaches the lower level of the defense structure. This reaches the battalion as a health unit in the form of a clinic and may be extended to the platoon level by a junior nurse, who could exist in person. At the ‘gata’ [platoon] and team levels, there is a health committee. The prevention interventions, like the cleaning campaign, are reported by this committee. [P21]

The other is that the health structure reaches the lower level of the defense structure. This reaches the battalion as a health unit in the form of a clinic and may be extended to the platoon level by a junior nurse, who could exist in person. At the ‘gata’ [platoon] and team levels, there is a health committee. The prevention interventions, like the cleaning campaign, are reported by this committee. [P21]

The potential extension of health services to the platoon level, facilitated by the involvement of a junior nurse, underscored efforts to bring healthcare closer to the frontlines, ensuring that personnel have had immediate access to medical support. The existence of a health committee at the platoon and team levels further indicated that health promotion was not just the responsibility of higher-level medical personnel but was also managed at the grassroots level, involving those most familiar with the daily realities of military life. This approach also highlighted the crucial role of lower-level units and committees in maintaining health and proactively promoting and preventing health problems.

Professional factors

This category included being a volunteer and self-discipline, sharing best practices, commitment, and training opportunities; facilitating solutions for existing health problems in consideration of preventive health measures; and the readiness of lower-level personnel. To motivate the best-performing unit personnel, share best practices by depicting and recognizing them during the review meeting. Participants suggested a deliberate and successful effort to address shortcomings and enhance the site’s health standards. As one participant stated:The site achieved recognition because, by changing the weak side of infection prevention to a strength, this site would be taken as a benchmark (model) for other sites. The best experiences and practices will be shared with other sites. The site is expected to maintain the strength achieved. [P12]

The site achieved recognition because, by changing the weak side of infection prevention to a strength, this site would be taken as a benchmark (model) for other sites. The best experiences and practices will be shared with other sites. The site is expected to maintain the strength achieved. [P12]

This approach suggested a systematic method of continuous improvement where effective practices were identified, replicated, and sustained across the organization. It also implied that recognition carried the responsibility to uphold and possibly further enhanced the standards that contributed to the site’s success. This may involve regular monitoring, continued staff training, and a commitment to the best practices. Emphasizing the maintenance of achieved strengths could be particularly relevant when addressing the challenges of sustaining health promotion gains over time.

Once the commandants were convinced, the military community responded positively to upcoming issues. They showed willingness to cooperate, offer support when resources allowed, and welcomed new faces, especially those from higher ranks. Once persuaded, the commandant might commit to budgetary support, and the staff would be ready to participate in activities. This reflected the level of organizational commitment, which was a key facilitator in implementing health promotion interventions. Participants emphasized the multifaceted nature of effective health intervention implementation within the military context. As one participant put it:The other idea is that it is not only the structure but also the leaders’ commitment to convince others. The health professionals coordinate the lower-level personnel’s readiness to accept and accomplish activities. [P26]

The other idea is that it is not only the structure but also the leaders’ commitment to convince others. The health professionals coordinate the lower-level personnel’s readiness to accept and accomplish activities. [P26]

Effective coordination likely involved clear communication, shared goals, and a unified approach to implementing health interventions, preventing efforts from becoming fragmented or redundant. This coordination was essential for the smooth implementation of health promotion interventions, particularly in a complex and hierarchical organization like the military. This could be used to explore the interplay between structure, leadership, coordination, and the attitudes of personnel at various levels in implementing health promotion interventions. This highlighted that while a well-organized structure was important, the human elements of commitment, coordination, and readiness were equally vital for achieving desired outcomes.

Collaboration factors

Collaboration and shared understanding were crucial components of successful health promotion interventions. Collaboration was evident in the integration of health units and programs across various sectors of the defense organization, as well as in partnerships with governmental and non-governmental organizations. This collaborative approach aimed to maximize efforts in promoting health. Achieving a shared understanding, particularly through extensive discussions, was essential for reaching agreements on which problems to prioritize. This process was key to maintaining smooth working relationships, especially with immediate leaders.

Military factors

This category covered the chain of command or commanding system, living together in mass, better education, working with adults, similarity in age at a given level, peacetime conditions, and a new face for health education delivery. The chain of command was particularly important in the military, as it facilitated the effective implementation of health promotion interventions through a clear command structure and organizational discipline. Participants highlighted the unique way health interventions were carried out within the military context, emphasizing the importance of following orders and maintaining discipline. As one participant explained:When the order passes through the structure, everyone who is responsible for it may accept it as their job since they are under command. Perhaps there will be an effective or ineffective implementation process. Some personnel may use a different modality to convince others and implement the order, while others might do little and report it with some changes. The military complies with command orders. [P16]

This reflected the top-down nature of military command, where orders are expected to be followed by all personnel. While this structure ensured compliance, there was potential variability in how effectively these orders were implemented. Compliance was a fundamental aspect of military operations, but the quality of implementation often depended on the attitudes and actions of individual personnel.

Informed and convinced commandants were essential for effectively utilizing the chain of command to facilitate health promotion interventions. In the military, the clear chain of command helped minimize communication barriers, providing a valuable opportunity for efficient information dissemination. Living in close group quarters, such as in a camp, further enhanced the ability to reach soldiers for health education, conduct health inspections, and organize time-intensive activities like environmental clean-up campaigns. The proximity of personnel allowed for swift communication and quick mobilization, which was particularly advantageous in the structured environment of the military. Talking about this issue, a participant said:The military personnel are living together. We could easily reach out for health education provisions. For one thing, it could not take time, and secondly, we can access them when we want to get them. When the command gives the order, we could get the whole thing at once. And this is a great thing. [P16]

This emphasized how the unique living conditions and structured command system within the military were instrumental in facilitating effective health promotion interventions. The communal environment, combined with the authoritative command structure, enabled the swift and comprehensive dissemination of health education, ensuring that interventions reach all personnel efficiently.

Because of the nature of their work, air force personnel generally had a higher academic standing than their ground force counterparts did. While it could facilitate communication with the younger force, challenges may arise in practice. Age similarity among soldiers at a given rank can be advantageous for health education, as it allows for targeted messaging. However, it may also lead to peer influence, which could pose challenges. As one participant expressed:Their [soldiers’] ages are similar at the lower level. When they come to the next level, there is again a similar age. When you go to the brigade level, you get a certain year older than the lower ones do. This similarity would help them in understanding health information at their level of understanding. [P26]

When soldiers are of similar ages, they tend to have comparable levels of health literacy, enabling health educators to tailor their messages to the group’s specific needs and comprehension abilities. This alignment between the audience’s age and the communication approach can enhance the effectiveness of health education efforts, ensuring that the information is both accessible and relevant. As soldiers advance in rank and age, adjustments in health education may be necessary to address evolving health concerns and the need for more advanced or detailed explanations for older soldiers compared to their younger counterparts.

Health promotion interventions are generally easier to implement during peacetime than in wartime. The introduction of new personnel in health provision was met with a positive response, as it signaled a growing concern for health issues within the military. As one of the participants’ words:When health education is provided, the soldiers could have more acceptance for the newcomers from other areas, one step more than those living there. For battalions, if they [educators] come from brigades, for brigades from divisions. Even the leaders in that area have similar perspectives to those of soldiers. The possible reason could be that they accept this issue as having some concern that may need a solution when that person comes from one step above. [P23]

This perception likely stemmed from the belief that if higher authorities were addressing a health concern, it must be important and deserving of serious attention.

Barriers to health promotion interventions

Organizational factors

This included barriers such as resource constraints, procedural inefficiencies in the purchasing process, insufficient follow-up on activities, and overlapping efforts. Resource constraints were categorized into four concepts: budget constraints, inadequate transportation for field activities, material scarcity, and supply problems. Participants expressed dissatisfaction with the insufficient budgetary support, which restricted their ability to carry out essential field activities at military bases. These financial limitations hindered the completion of planned tasks and compromised the quality of work. Logistical and financial challenges were consistently highlighted. One participant commented:A budget constraint prevents us from going immediately if something is wrong. This constraint affects our movement to the units because there is no budget allocated for my activities. This is one of the major issues hindering our effort to solve units’ problems on time. [P22]

A budget constraint prevents us from going immediately if something is wrong. This constraint affects our movement to the units because there is no budget allocated for my activities. This is one of the major issues hindering our effort to solve units’ problems on time. [P22]

This lack of financial support directly affected the military’s ability to address unit-level problems promptly, leading to potential escalation of issues or missed opportunities for early intervention. Recognizing these financial constraints could help inform strategies to advocate for improved resource allocation and more sustainable planning for health promotion interventions within the military.

Limited access to transportation made it difficult to cover dispersed campsites on foot, which hindered the completion of health-related tasks. Adequate transport between sites was crucial, but its absence posed a challenge to providing supportive supervision in person. As a solution, they tried to communicate by phone and followed activities by reports. This problem was emphasized for the remote campsites. Commenting on transport support for field activities, one participant stated:[…] Transportation is needed to move from one site to another. However, there is inadequate transport to go and do the field activities. Campsites are remote and outside of the normal transportation route. Sometimes, there are things you do by getting in person on the sites. …[P16]

[…] Transportation is needed to move from one site to another. However, there is inadequate transport to go and do the field activities. Campsites are remote and outside of the normal transportation route. Sometimes, there are things you do by getting in person on the sites. …[P16]

The remoteness of campsites complicated timely access, potentially delaying critical health interventions. Addressing transportation challenges was essential to expanding the reach and impact of these interventions and improving overall health service delivery in military settings.

Participants highlighted several interconnected barriers to the effectiveness of health promotion interventions in the military, including budget constraints, shortages of health personnel, and insufficient materials. As one participant commented on these barriers:One of the barriers is budget scarcity, which makes our intervention incomplete. The other is a human power shortage. It is difficult to assign the right position. You could serve as soldiers as needed. Wherever you go, there may be material shortages. We use the available materials. [P 8]

One of the barriers is budget scarcity, which makes our intervention incomplete. The other is a human power shortage. It is difficult to assign the right position. You could serve as soldiers as needed. Wherever you go, there may be material shortages. We use the available materials. [P 8]

While an adaptive approach may alleviate some of these barriers, it does not fully address the underlying issues. A deeper understanding of these barriers can help in developing targeted solutions to improve health promotion efforts in the military.

“How can you expect a report without providing the necessary supplies?” one participant remarked. They noted that inefficiencies in the purchasing process might contribute to material shortages at lower levels. Addressing this issue, another participant explained:Perhaps the challenge is in the purchasing process because not all materials requested are purchased. Sometimes the whole request might not be bought out. This challenge has a negative impact on the next activities due to the gap and resulting shortage at sites. [P12]

This can result in unmet needs, disrupt ongoing activities, and create operational challenges. It highlighted the importance of a reliable, efficient purchasing process to ensure the availability of materials and the smooth implementation of health promotion interventions.

Lack of supervision, combined with poor communication and understanding between lower and upper levels of the institution, hindered interventions when activities were only followed up through reports. Overlapping responsibilities among teams led to tasks being left unfinished because some assumed others would complete them.

Professional factors

This category encompasses staff turnover, shortages of health personnel, the history of educators, and differing priorities between health personnel and leaders. Ignoring these barriers might manifest as a lack of support, challenges in completing the task, or a lack of awareness about the problems, as illustrated by one of the participants:In the case of barriers, there may be some ignorance when you say that there is a problem and the activities could not be facilitated due to budget constraints. If a certain problem requires a health professional visit, arranging transport could be difficult. The budget could not be decided promptly. There is also little recognition of the health problem as their own. [P13]

This highlighted several barriers, including a lack of awareness about problems, budget constraints, transport challenges, and delays in financial decision-making. Some services may be provided by staff who have not received proper training and rely solely on readings due to the scarcity of human resources. This shortage also constrained the ability to meet established standards and placed inexperienced health personnel in lower-level roles, potentially compromising the quality of interventions. Because of the shortage of health personnel, it may be difficult to get experienced health personnel in the regiments. This shortage likely had several consequences. One consequence was an increased workload, as fewer health personnel were available, forcing those present to manage additional tasks, which could lead to burnout or reduced quality. Another issue was limited expertise, as the lack of experienced personnel hindered the ability to address complex health problems or implement advanced interventions. A third consequence was delayed response, where fewer staff resulted in slower reactions to health problems and delays in implementing health promotion interventions.

The perception of authority and familiarity affected both the delivery and reception of health education within the military. This issue was further contextualized by the historical background of educators. As one interviewee described:If you are one, you live and eat together, and if you try to provide health education, they [soldiers] could look into your past history. Differences in priorities between health personnel and leaders can pose challenges to both initiating and sustaining interventions, particularly when leaders are not fully convinced of the value.

Military factors

This category includes the nature of work, wartime conditions, the dispersed campsites, and age similarities among personnel at a given level. The nature of work involves living in groups, engaging in military missions, and being stationed in remote or desert areas. While living in groups can facilitate health promotion interventions, it can also pose a risk for the spread of infectious diseases. Hence, living in groups is a mixed concept with both facilitator and barrier roles. As one informant reported:[…] To keep a person’s privacy, a separate living room is needed. This is good for disease prevention and personal comfort. In a military setting, they work and move as a team. We could not change this. House sharing with communicable diseases like COVID is dangerous […] [ P10]

[…] To keep a person’s privacy, a separate living room is needed. This is good for disease prevention and personal comfort. In a military setting, they work and move as a team. We could not change this. House sharing with communicable diseases like COVID is dangerous […] [ P10]

Participants addressed the challenges of maintaining personal privacy and preventing diseases within the constraints of a military setting where teamwork and shared spaces are the norm. This insight can inform strategies for balancing operational requirements with health and safety considerations, especially during outbreaks of infectious disease.

This underscored the importance of flexibility and adaptability in health planning to accommodate shifting mission locations and evolving priorities. Understanding these challenges can help in developing strategies to better manage health interventions in a dynamic operational environment and ensure that health promotion interventions remain responsive and effective even when faced with unforeseen changes.

This situation highlighted the importance of targeted strategies that address the unique health risks associated with such environments. Health promotion interventions should be adapted to provide stronger support systems and tailored solutions to protect and maintain personnel health in extreme environments.

Wartime is not conducive to health promotion interventions. One participant illustrated this as:During wartime, communication may be disrupted. Going to units in a war area is not like going to a peacetime unit. It becomes difficult to work on disease prevention interventions and provide feedback to units. [P21]

Participants outlined the challenges associated with health interventions and communication during wartime, particularly the logistical barriers and breakdowns in information flow. This emphasized the need for strategies specifically designed to address communication and logistical challenges in conflict situations, ensuring health efforts remain effective despite disruptions.

When soldiers were stationed in groups at campsites, they were easier to reach for health promotion interventions. However, dispersing them across wider areas, this placement posed challenges and required the deployment of more health personnel. Moreover, age similarities among soldiers may foster peer influence, affecting their health behaviors. One informant reported:This similarity in age could have its own problems. I will get on it. This highlighted the importance of taking into account both the benefits and potential drawbacks of having personnel with similar ages and experience levels. By understanding these dynamics, strategies can be developed to better manage age-related factors to optimize health promotion interventions.

Discussion

This study identified a range of facilitators and barriers influencing the implementation of health promotion interventions in the Ethiopian military settings and developed a context-specific conceptual model based on these findings. Overall, the findings indicated that successful implementation was shaped by the interaction of organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors rather than by isolated factors. While many of the identified facilitators and barriers were consistent with those reported in previous military and civilian studies, others appeared to reflect the unique organizational and operational context of the Ethiopian National Defence Forces. The following discussion compares these findings in relation to the existing literature and examines their implications for health promotion implementation in military settings.

The present study identified organizational support, leadership, communication, collaboration, and military structures as key facilitators for implementing health promotion interventions. Overall, these findings were consistent with previous studies conducted in both military and civilian settings (Table 3). Leadership support, military command, organizational structures, communication, and access to information have consistently been reported as important facilitators across studies conducted in the USA, UK, Italy, Iran, and the Netherlands [8, 11, 23–26]. This consistency suggests that organizational commitment and effective communication are fundamental requirements for successful health promotion implementation regardless of setting.

However, several facilitators identified in the present study—including defense media, lower-level health committees, and living in mass—have not been reported previously. These findings likely reflect the unique organizational structure and operational characteristics of the Ethiopian military and the limited availability of comparable studies from military settings in resource-constrained countries. Conversely, facilitators such as rewards, health ambassadors, and evidence strength, identified in previous studies [25–28], did not emerge in the present study.

Resource constraints emerged as the most important barrier to implementing health promotion interventions. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in military and civilian settings, which have similarly identified financial limitations, inadequate organizational resources, insufficient funding, and shortages of health personnel as major implementation barriers [11, 23, 26–30].

Professional barriers identified in the present study, including staff turnover, competing priorities, and inadequate leadership support, also aligned with findings reported in previous studies [11, 23, 24, 26, 29]. These similarities indicate that workforce and organizational challenges commonly affect health promotion implementation across different settings.

Several barriers identified in the present study appear to be context-specific, including lengthy procurement processes, delayed decision-making, deployment in remote areas and desert environments, dispersed campsites, and living in mass. These barriers reflect the operational realities of the Ethiopian military and have received little attention in previous studies. In contrast, barriers such as unclear ownership of interventions and absence of formal mandates, reported in the USA military study [23], were not identified in the present study.

As shown in Table 3, several facilitators and barriers identified in the present study were consistent with findings from previous military and civilian settings. However, concepts such as defense media, lower-level health committees, living in mass, and operational deployment in remote and desert environments appear to reflect the unique organizational and operational context of the Ethiopian military. These context-specific findings informed the development of the conceptual model presented in this study.

This study has some limitations. Although demographic information was collected, interviews were not linked to participants’ demographic characteristics to protect confidentiality. In addition, the study was conducted within the Ethiopian National Defence Forces, and the findings should therefore be interpreted within this specific organizational and cultural context. A further limitation was the lack of comparable studies conducted in similar military settings using comparable qualitative approaches, which limited our ability to compare the identified conceptual categories and the resulting conceptual model with similar evidence. Furthermore, the experiences captured in this study were primarily those of health professionals involved in health promotion interventions. Future qualitative research should therefore explore and refine the identified facilitators and barriers by incorporating the experiences of soldiers and the military line of command, in addition to health personnel. Such research would strengthen the transferability and further refinement of the conceptual model while providing a more comprehensive understanding of health promotion interventions’ implementation. Further research is also needed to identify effective strategies for overcoming the key barriers identified in this study.

Conclusions

This study identified key facilitators and barriers to influencing the implementation of health promotion interventions in Ethiopian military settings. While the chain of command emerged as the most influential facilitator, resource constraints were identified as the most important barrier. Overall, the findings demonstrate that effective implementation depends on the interaction of organizational, structural, professional, collaborative, and military factors. These findings were integrated into a context-specific conceptual model that can guide the implementation of health promotion interventions and support evidence-informed decision-making within the Ethiopian military. However, as the conceptual model was primarily developed based on the experiences of experts, it should be considered an evolving framework that may require further refinement and validation as additional evidence becomes available.
