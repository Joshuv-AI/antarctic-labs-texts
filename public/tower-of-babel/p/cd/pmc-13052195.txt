Trajectories of cognitive decline before and after new‐onset dual sensory impairment: findings from two longitudinal studies
Authors: Miwen Zou, Huping Gong, Binyan Chen, Shengzhang Chen, Jiayi Teng, Jinghao Zhou, Jiaxin Shao, Yan Zhang, Fuman Cai, Pan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe trajectories of cognitive decline before and after new‐onset dual sensory impairment (DSI) remain largely unknown.METHODSParticipants were included without DSI at baseline and with at least two recorded cognitive measures. BACKGROUND

With global population aging, cognitive impairment and dementia have become major public health problems for the elderly. Cognitive decline unfolds as a dynamic, multiyear process. Thus, identifying and understanding the key risk factors and dynamic trajectories of cognitive decline is essential to reduce the burden of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. Among numerous risk factors, sensory impairment is increasingly recognized as a significant potentially modifiable factor in cognitive decline. 1 , 2 Vision and hearing, as the primary channels for the brain to interact with the external environment, are fundamental sensory functions. 3 Substantial evidence indicates that visual impairment (VI), hearing impairment (HI), and dual sensory impairment (DSI) are each independent risk factors for cognitive decline and dementia. 4 , 5 , 6 DSI refers to the simultaneous impairment of both vision and hearing. 7 The negative effects of DSI may not be a simple summation but rather a synergistic effect, posing a more severe threat to cognitive function. 8 , 9 , 10 Although existing research has preliminarily confirmed the association between DSI and adverse cognitive outcomes, 11 , 12 , 13 the evidence remains markedly insufficient, limiting our understanding of the dynamic relationship between DSI and cognitive decline.

It remains unclear whether cognitive decline accelerates only after DSI occurs or whether an accelerating trend was already present prior to its onset. If the acceleration of cognitive decline occurred after DSI, it might indicate the direct damage of sensory deprivation. 14 If it occurs before DSI, it is more likely to reflect common pathology or risk factors. 1 This difference will directly determine whether an intervention should focus on early sensory rehabilitation or joint monitoring of high‐risk populations. Furthermore, whether the pathways of the sequence of DSI (HI first, VI first, HI, and VI occurrence) differing in accelerating cognitive decline is unexplored. Moreover, DSI may exert differential effects across different cognitive domains relying on multisensory integration, such as memory, 15 orientation, 16 calculation, and attention. 17 It has been shown that there is no significant longitudinal association between VI and memory decline, but HI and DSI are significantly associated with episodic memory decline, 18 whereas the spatial orientation of vision and hearing are independently but not additively affected in elderly people with DSI. 19 Calculation and attention reflect executive function. 20 , 21 The executive function is associated with both hearing loss and visual loss. 22 Elderly people with HI have visual attention deficits, 23 and VI also results in poorer attention. 24 However, investigation into which cognitive domain is affected by DSI is still needed. Additionally, many existing studies on the association between DSI and cognitive decline are mainly derived from cross‐sectional studies 25 , 26 or cohort studies with a single population. 2 , 27 Such studies may lead to evidence that cannot establish causality and chronological order and would be susceptible to the influence of specific population characteristics, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to different sociopolitical and medical contexts and limit the opportunities for cross‐cohort validation.

Therefore, this study aimed to utilize data from two cohorts to trace the trajectory of cognitive function changes before and after the onset of DSI in individuals. We hypothesized that (1) individuals with DSI after onset would experience a more rapid decline in cognitive test scores than individuals with DSI before onset and individuals without DSI and that (2) in the DSI group, orientation, episodic memory, attention, and calculation would decline more rapidly than in the non‐DSI group.

METHODS

Study participants

This study utilized cohort data from two publicly available databases: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). Both CHARLS and ELSA provide comprehensive, high‐quality, and comparable measures of sensory function (hearing and vision) 28 and cognitive performance across multiple domains, 21 embedded within nationally representative longitudinal designs. Their parallel use enabled a robust cross‐cultural comparison of the sensory–cognitive association. CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal study of adults residing in China, with follow‐up surveys conducted every 2 years since 2011. 29 The present study integrated CHARLS cohort data from Wave 1 (2011) to Wave 4 (2018), with Wave 1 serving as the baseline survey. ELSA is a nationally representative longitudinal study of adults living in the United Kingdom (UK), with follow‐up surveys conducted every 2 years since 2002. 30 This study combined ELSA longitudinal data from Wave 7 (2014) to Wave 10 (2020), with Wave 7 serving as the baseline survey.

The same sample selection criteria were developed for each of the two databases. Inclusion criteria were participants without DSI (participants with VI only, HI only, or neither VI nor HI were included) at baseline and with at least two recorded cognitive measures. Because the study focused on the impact of new onset of DSI on cognitive trajectories, individuals with pre‐existing DSI at baseline were excluded. Those with missing hearing or vision data at baseline and those lacking any follow‐up cognitive assessment were excluded. Participants with dementia or stroke who had cognitive impairment at baseline were also excluded to ensure comparability for cognitive response among the study populations at baseline. At baseline, sensory assessments were conducted on 17,493 CHARLS and 9666 ELSA participants. Following the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the analytical samples comprised 5080 participants from CHARLS and 6418 from ELSA. After using propensity score matching (PSM), the sample size in CHARLS was 1714 pairs (1714 in the DSI group and 1714 in the non‐DSI group), and the sample size in ELSA was 456 pairs (456 in the DSI group and 456 in the non‐DSI group) (Figure 1).

Assessment of cognitive function

This study divided cognitive function into three main cognitive domains (orientation, attention and calculation, and episodic memory) and constructed a global cognitive score based on these domains. These domains were selected because they were related to multisensory integration and reflected domain‐specific and overall cognitive function. 18 , 19 , 31 Participants were asked to wear hearing aids or corrective lenses, if needed, before performing cognitive tests in ELSA. Similarly, interviewers presented HI participants with a list of words to minimize the effect of sensory impairments on test feasibility in CHARLS. Importantly, CHARLS and ELSA both use similar cognitive testing tools. Orientation was assessed by asking questions related to the current date, including the current year, month, date, and day of the week, with one point awarded for each correct answer. Attention and calculation were primarily assessed through a continuous subtraction test, which required respondents to subtract 7 from 100 five times in a row, with one point awarded for each correct answer, for a maximum score of five points. Episodic memory was assessed by immediate and delayed recall tests. The interviewer read aloud 10 words and then asked respondents to recall the words. One point was awarded for each correct recall of a word, for a maximum score of 20 points. The cognitive tests in both databases have been validated for reliability and validity and have been used in a number of studies. 21 , 32 , 33 , 34

Due to the differences in the original cognitive test score range between CHARLS and ELSA, the mean and standard deviation of the baseline scores of each cognitive test were calculated in each database, and the original scores of each individual in each wave were converted to the corresponding z‐scores. A global cognitive z‐score was then calculated by summing the z‐scores of the three domains and re‐standardizing this sum relative to baseline. 35

Ascertainment of new‐onset DSI

In this study, DSI was defined as simultaneous hearing and visual impairment. Hearing and vision status was determined according to self‐reported indicators. According to the occurrence sequence of sensory impairment, it was divided by three DSI pathways (VI first, HI first, or HI and VI concurrently). Non‐DSI consisted of individuals who did not develop DSI during the entire follow‐up period. This means individuals had no sensory impairment or had a single sensory impairment (only HI or only VI) at follow‐up.

A similar sensory testing question was used to identify sensory impairment by self‐report in both CHARLS and ELSA. Participants rated the quality of their hearing (with hearing aids if they used them) and vision (with glasses if they used them) on a five‐point scale (excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor) in CHARLS and ELSA. HI was defined as fair or poor, and VI was defined as fair/poor in either hyperopia or myopia. 36 , 37 Therefore, the same questions and cut‐off values (fair or poor) were used in the CHARLS and ELSA in this study to ensure internal consistency and comparability across cohorts (Figure S1). Although self‐reported measures may not fully capture objective sensory impairments, and HI mode may influence self‐report of vision. 38 In ELSA, previous studies showed that self‐reported hearing status is comparable to objective hearing assessments. Specifically, pure tone audiometry and the hearing screening test used in ELSA demonstrated similar sensitivities (89% and 94%) and specificities (87% and 82%). 39 For CHARLS, this is consistent with previous studies, and although objective hearing or vision tests are not routinely available across waves, self‐reported HI and VI have been widely used as key exposure variables in multiple population‐based studies. 40 , 41

In each data wave, a binary variable was constructed to distinguish individuals with and without DSI. In CHARLS, at baseline (Wave 1), all individuals were included without DSI. Individuals were then classified as new‐onset DSI if they experienced both HI and VI for the first time at any subsequent wave (Waves 2 to 4). The individual DSI onset time was defined as the wave at which both hearing and vision impairment first appeared in each individual and converted to the number of years from baseline (Wave 2 → 2 years; Wave 3 → 4 years; Wave 4 → 6 years). In ELSA, at baseline (Wave 7), all individuals were included without DSI. Individuals were then classified as new‐onset DSI if they experienced both HI and VI at any subsequent wave (Waves 8 to 10). The individual DSI onset time was defined as the wave at which both hearing and vision impairment first appeared in each individual and converted to the number of years from baseline (Wave 8 → 2 years; Wave 9 → 4 years; Wave 10 → 6 years). The non‐DSI group was composed of individuals who did not develop DSI during the entire follow‐up period.

Ascertainment of single sensory impairment on cognitive decline

To scientifically assess the independent effects of a single sensory impairment on cognitive function, we conducted two completely separate cohort studies from the original data, one in CHARLS (17,493 participants) and the other in ELSA (9666 participants): HI‐only and VI‐only analyses were performed independently. Specifically, participants in the HI‐only analysis had to meet the following criteria: (1) neither HI nor VI at baseline (“neither” status); (2) new‐onset HI (Waves 2 to 4) during the follow‐up period; (3) no VI developed during the entire follow‐up period. Any participant who developed VI during the follow‐up period, including cases of DSI transition with “HI before VI,” was strictly excluded. Symmetrical inclusion criteria were used for VI‐only analysis. This rigorous design ensured that the HI and VI groups maintained a single sensory impairment state throughout the study period, avoiding confounding effects during the transition from single to double impairment.

Covariates

We included covariates associated with DSI or cognitive function, 3 , 8 , 9 all of which were assessed at baseline. These included age, sex, education level, marital status, and body mass index (BMI). Additional variables included current smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and depressive symptom. We also added comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, lung disease, and cancer. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Depressive symptom was measured using a validated scale. Comorbidities were defined by self‐report.

Statistical analysis

Due to possible differences in demographic characteristics and health status between the DSI and non‐DSI groups, we used PSM to match individuals in the two groups 1:1 at baseline to improve group comparability in Table 1. First, we constructed a propensity score model. We used logistic regression models to estimate each participant's propensity score for developing DSI. A total of 17 covariates were included in the CHARLS and ELSA databases, covering three major categories of variables (Figure S2). Then we used 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement, with the caliper width set to 0.05 times the standard deviation of the logit value of the propensity score. Detailed statistics of matching, including matching log, matching rate, caliper width, and matching method, are presented in Table S1. The distribution map reveals that there was a significant deviation in the distribution of propensity scores between the two groups before matching, but the distribution curves of the two groups were highly overlapping after matching, which confirmed the effectiveness of matching. A distribution comparison of propensity scores before and after matching is detailed in Figure S3. The matching process was done independently in the CHARLS and ELSA datasets. The sample size included in CHARLS was 1714 pairs (1714 in DSI group and 1714 in non‐DSI group), and the sample size in ELSA was 456 pairs (456 in DSI group and 456 in non‐DSI group) (Figure 1).

The imputation method in this study was mainly used to handle missing baseline covariates and, in sensitivity analyses, missing cognitive variables during follow‐up. A hierarchical imputation strategy based on variable type was used for missing values for baseline covariates. For continuous variables, we used median imputation. For categorical variables and binary health status variables, we used mode imputation. For missing cognitive variables in sensitivity analyses, we used multiple imputation (MI) to account for uncertainty in imputed values.

A linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) was used to analyze the trend of cognitive function changes over time before and after DSI. Given this study's focus on the impact of new‐onset of DSI on the rate of cognitive decline, we introduced a piecewise time effect in the model. We used a random intercept for each participant to allow for different baseline cognitive levels. To ensure the representativeness of the study sample and avoid restricting the sample range by setting cut‐off values, no explicit cut‐off values were established for baseline cognition scores in this study. Meanwhile, to accurately adapt to the inherent characteristics of longitudinal follow‐up data, this study employed LMM with random‐effect parameter fitting for intercepts. ‐Allowing for interindividual differences in the rate of change in cognition over time. Parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method, and the significance of fixed effects was assessed using a t‐test based on the Kenward‐Roger approximation. Using the mixed models described earlier, we extracted key slope parameters from the fixed‐effects estimates to account for the relationship between DSI and cognitive ability change. The aforementioned parameters were estimated by modeling global cognition and three cognitive domains separately. Additionally, to scientifically assess the independent effects of a single sensory impairment on cognitive function, we conducted two completely separate cohort studies from CHARLS and ELSA using LMM. HI‐only analysis and VI‐only analysis were performed independently. The longitudinal effects of HI‐only, VI‐only, and DSI on cognitive domains were examined in CHARLS and ELSA (Table S2).

Subgroup analyses by age, sex, education, and pre‐defined DSI were performed to determine the association between the occurrence of DSI and cognitive decline. Within each subgroup, LMMs were constructed to assess the association between the occurrence of DSI and the longitudinal trajectories of changes in each cognitive domain. The model settings were consistent with the primary analysis, using a random intercept to fully account for within‐ and between‐individual variation. To formally test whether each stratification variable had an effect modification influence on the association between DSI and cognitive decline, we included the interaction term of the product of DSI occurrence status and each stratification variable (e.g., DSI × age group, DSI × sex) in the full‐sample model. The likelihood ratio test was used to compare the goodness of fit of models with and without interaction terms, and a p value for interaction of 0.05 was considered to indicate statistically significant effect modification.

To test the robustness of the primary results, sensitivity analyses were performed. First, baseline characteristic comparison was conducted between inclusion and exclusion after PSM in the analysis (Table S3), and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to achieve covariate balance by constructing weighted pseudo‐populations without losing samples (Table S4). Second, to assess the robustness of our findings to different approaches to handling missing data, we conducted MI and complete case analysis (Tables S5 and S6). Third, to examine whether the differences in the effect of DSI on cognitive decline could be attributed to participants seeking help with sensory function, we added sensitivity analyses for cataract surgery and education in Table S7. Finally, to strengthen the validity of DSI, we included two additional objective measures: da032 (presence or absence of glasses or corrective lenses) and da038 (presence or absence of hearing aids) in CHARLS (Table S8).

All statistical analyses were performed using the Python programming language (version 3.12.9). Statistical libraries such as stats models were used for linear mixed model fitting, and tools such as pandas and scikit‐learn were used for data management, propensity score calculation, and matching. Results are presented as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the significance level for two‐sided tests is set at 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

In CHARLS, before PSM, the scores of all cognitive domains were lower in the DSI compared to the non‐DSI group (orientation 3.3 ± 1.0 vs 3.5 ± 0.8, attention and calculation 3.8 ± 1.5 vs 4.0 ± 1.4, and episodic memory 7.8 ± 3.3 vs 8.5 ± 3.4, all p < 0.001). After PSM, good balance was achieved across all domains, with SMDs ≤ |0.1|. In ELSA, significant baseline differences were observed before PSM in attention and calculation (4.3 ± 1.0 vs 4.4 ± 0.9, p = 0.035) and episodic memory scores (10.3 ± 3.3 vs 11.4 ± 3.3, p < 0.001). These differences were substantially reduced after PSM, with all post‐match SMDs ≤ |0.1|, indicating adequate balance (Table 1).

Table 1 and Figure S1 present the baseline characteristics and cognitive assessments of participants with and without DSI in CHARLS and ELSA, before and after PSM. In the unmatched samples, individuals with DSI were more likely to be older (CHARLS: 56.8 vs 55.7 years, p < 0.001; ELSA: 70.0 vs 65.8 years, p < 0.001), lower educated (middle school or below in CHARLS was 85.9% vs 72.5%, p < 0.001; in ELSA was 35.5% vs 22.3%, p < 0.001), higher depression score (CHARLS: 7.0 vs 5.4, p < 0.001; ELSA: 1.8 vs 1.1, p < 0.001), lower orientation (CHARLS: 3.3 vs 3.5, p < 0.001; ELSA: 3.8 vs 3.9, p = 0.283), lower attention and calculation (CHARLS: 3.8 vs 4.0, p < 0.001; ELSA: 4.3 vs 4.4, p = 0.035), and episodic memory (CHARLS: 7.8 vs 8.5, p < 0.001; ELSA: 10.3 vs 11.4, p < 0.001) than those in the non‐DSI group. After PSM, these sociodemographic and health‐related differences were well balanced, and there were no significant differences in baseline cognition scores between the DSI group and the non‐DSI group (p > 0.05).

Primary analysis

Cognitive function changes before and after DSI onset showed significant differences. The annual rate of cognitive decline was similar at baseline between individuals who experienced DSI onset and individuals without DSI. Before DSI onset, the slope of global cognitive decline (in ELSA, β = 0.180, 95% CI: 0.015 to 0.346) and attention and calculation decline (in ELSA, β = 0.126, 95% CI: 0.077 to 0.175) in the DSI group were statistically flatter than that in the non‐DSI group. After DSI onset, the slopes of global cognitive decline (in ELSA, β = −0.085, 95% CI: −0.165 to −0.006; in CHARLS, β = −0.098, 95% CI: −0.145 to −0.050), episodic memory (in ELSA, β = −0.082, 95% CI: −0.152 to −0.012; in CHARLS, β = −0.071, 95% CI: −0.111 to −0.030), and orientation (in CHARLS, β = −0.016, 95% CI: −0.027 to −0.005) in the DSI group were statistically steeper than that in the non‐DSI group (Table 2 and Figure 2). Additionally, among the three DSI pathways, the rate of cognitive decline for the VI‐first group was the steepest (β = −0.141, 95% CI: −0.182 to −0.099, p < 0.001) in CHARLS. A similar finding was obtained for VI‐first (β = −0.346, 95% CI: −0.463 to −0.230, p < 0.001) in ELSA (Table S9).

Effects of single sensory impairment on cognitive decline

In CHARLS, the single sensory impairment analysis after PSM showed that the HI‐only group and the VI‐only group did not show statistically significant accelerating effects on cognitive decline in global cognition and all three cognitive domains (orientation, attention and calculation, episodic memory). In contrast to the CHARLS results, in ELSA, clear evidence was revealed that a single sensory impairment independently affected cognitive decline. In the larger and more complete ELSA cohort, we observed a significant effect of accelerated cognitive decline. The slope change in global cognition in the HI group was β = −0.351 (95% CI: −0.550 to −0.152), and in the VI group it was β = −0.239 (95% CI: −0.479 to 0.001). Both HI‐only and VI‐only groups significantly affected attention and calculation function (HI: β = −0.153; VI: β = −0.165) (Table S2).

Risk factors for cognitive decline after DSI

Subgroup analysis was performed on age, sex, education, and pre‐defined DSI to further explore the risk factors for cognitive decline after DSI. In CHARLS, individuals aged <60 years old showed significant cognitive decline after DSI (global cognition: β = −0.712, 95% CI: −1.006, −0.419; orientation: β = −0.066, 95% CI: −0.119, −0.014; attention and calculation: β = −0.147, 95% CI: −0.239, −0.055; episodic memory: β = −0.495, 95% CI: −0.709, −0.280; all p < 0.05), and the p values for interaction of all cognitive domains were <0.05 (Table S10). The occurrence of DSI was significantly associated with subsequent cognitive decline in sex subgroups in both CHARLS and ELSA (Table S11). The effect with respect to the middle school or below group was significant on global cognitive decline only in CHARLS (β = −0.273, 95% CI: −0.456 to −0.089), while the high school or above group showed a highly significant negative effect both in CHARLS (β = −0.371, 95% CI: −0.695 to −0.047) and ELSA (β = −0.537, 95% CI: −0.876 to −0.199) (Table S12). The effects of pre‐defined DSI on specific cognitive domains were different (Table S13). Compared with the no impairment (NI) group, there was no difference in the impact of cognitive decline among the HI, VI, or DSI group in ELSA. But in CHARLS, almost cognitive domains had more changes in slope after DSI onset in the DSI group compared with the NI group.

Sensitivity analysis

First, participants who were excluded after PSM tended to be older, female, and of lower education and have higher depression scores and lower baseline cognition scores in CHARLS. In ELSA, participants who were excluded after PSM tended to be younger, have higher education, be married, have lower depression scores, and have higher baseline cognitive scores (Table S3). The IPTW was performed on the full sample population, retaining all participants who met inclusion criteria (CHARLS: 5080; ELSA: 6418). In CHARLS, the accelerated effects on global cognition, episodic memory, and orientation decline remained significant after DSI. In ELSA, a significant acceleration of orientation decline was observed after the onset of DSI (Table S4). This was consistent in PSM and IPTW in the analysis of the effects of DSI onset on cognitive decline. Second, both MI and complete case analysis confirmed with high consistency that the occurrence of DSI was significantly associated with accelerated cognitive decline. The detailed information was shown in Tables S5 and S6. Third, there is no difference in the effect of DSI on cognitive decline regardless of whether participants sought help with sensory function. In CHARLS, for global cognition, the basic model (with demographic and health variables controlled) showed that the DSI × time interaction effect was −0.099 (SE = 0.024, p < 0.001), indicating that the cognitive decline in the DSI group was significantly faster than that in the control group. After controlling for cataract surgery, the effect was almost unchanged (β = −0.100, p < 0.001). After adjustment for education, the effect was −0.097 (p < 0.001). The effect remained stable at −0.097 (p < 0.001) in the full model. For global cognition in ELSA, the DSI × time interaction effect was negative in all models (basic model β = −0.041, full model β = −0.039), although it did not reach the traditional 0.05 significance level, but the direction of effect was completely consistent with CHARLS, and the effect size remained stable after controlling for cataract surgery and education (Table S7). Lastly, in CHARLS, comparing the results of DSI self‐report definition with the enhanced definition that included device use, the rate of global cognitive decline was associated with β = −0.098 (95% CI: −0.145, −0.050, p < 0.001) in the DSI self‐reported definition and β = −0.099 (95% CI: −0.147, −0.051, p < 0.001) in the enhanced definition. There was significant stability in the effect estimates across DSI manipulations (Table S8).

DISCUSSION

This study systematically examined the trajectories of cognitive function changes before and after new‐onset DSI based on two longitudinal cohorts (CHARLS and ELSA). Results indicate that participants experienced accelerated cognitive decline upon developing new‐onset DSI. Incident DSIs are associated with accelerated cognitive decline after, but not before, the event, and its adverse effects exhibit heterogeneity across different demographic characteristics. The annual rate of cognitive decline before DSI onset in the DSI group was similar to or lower than that in the non‐DSI group. The rate of decline in global cognition, orientation, and episodic memory significantly accelerated over the years after DSI onset. In addition, the occurrence of DSI was significantly associated with cognitive decline in age, sex, education, and pre‐defined DSI subgroups.

The rate of global cognitive decline in the DSI group was significantly faster than that in the non‐DSI group in both CHARLS and ELSA. The turning point in cognitive trajectory occurred after the DSI, indicating the direct damage of sensory deprivation. 7 What is more noteworthy is that the impact of DSI on cognition is not uniformly distributed across all cognitive functions but seems to particularly affect processes related to memory. 42 It may be related to the reduction in hearing and visual input associated with hippocampal atrophy, while episodic memory mainly relies on the function of the hippocampus. 43 Moreover, both independent cohorts showed that the rate of cognitive decline in the VI‐first group was relatively faster among the three DSI pathways, suggesting that VI may provide a more vulnerable foundation for subsequent cognitive decline. 41 Therefore, early identification and handling of sensory impairments, especially in VI, may become a key opportunity to prevent or slow down subsequent cognitive decline.

DSI may accelerate cognitive decline through multiple pathways. First, restricted social interactions and reduced activity due to sensory impairment may indirectly accelerate cognitive decline. 43 , 44 Second, the loss of visual and auditory input directly burdens the brain and may lead to neurological loss in the long run. 45 A variety of mechanisms, including direct neuropathological effects, competition for cognitive resources, and reduced brain resilience, increase an individual's risk of dementia. 46 In addition, DSI is highly associated with depression, loneliness, and physical inactivity, which are themselves important risk factors for cognitive decline. 47 , 48

Our findings are largely consistent with those of previous studies. Substantial evidence indicates that VI and HI are each closely associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline, while the negative effects of DSI are even more pronounced. 6 , 8 , 43 An analysis of data from 5.4 million people aged 65 years and older in the United States revealed that HI and VI are each independently associated with a significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment. Among them, DSI has the most severe impact, with a risk of cognitive impairment that is more than eight times higher than that of sensorily healthy older adults and much higher than that of a single injury. The risk of cognitive impairment caused by DSI was more than three times that caused by HI alone and more than two times that caused by VI alone. 8 Our study further emphasizes DSI as a significant predictor of cognitive decline through dual cohort validation.

The differential results between CHARLS and ELSA on the effect of single sensory impairment merit in‐depth discussion. First, there exists the difference in sample size and statistical test power. Rather than a true absence of effect, the CHARLS results may have been subject to statistical underpower. Second, there are differences in sociocultural and medical settings. CHARLS participants were predominantly from the Chinese community, including a large number of rural areas, 29 whereas ELSA participants were from the UK. 30 The third is the difference in the baseline characteristics of the population. The two cohorts differ in age distribution, education level, and comorbidity patterns, which may influence the strength and pattern of sensory disorder–cognition associations. These results provide preliminary support for the mechanism of single sensory impairment and DSI. Both HI and VI significantly affect attention and calculation. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen the protection of attention and calculation.

In addition, subgroup analysis found that age had a differential effect on the association between DSI and cognitive decline in CHARLS and ELSA. In the CHARLS, participants under 60 years old had a particularly significant cognitive decline after DSI, which is consistent with previous findings that sensory impairment in midlife has a prior effect on cognitive impairment in later life, 49 , 50 whereas in ELSA, participants at least 60 years old had a particularly significant global cognition and episodic memory decline after DSI. This might be related to the fact that the included participants after PSM in ELSA were older than in CHARLS. In addition, sensory impairment usually occurs at a later age. 51 The occurrence of DSI in the elderly is more likely to mark a crucial turning point in the process of healthy aging. New‐onset DSI was significantly associated with global cognition and episodic memory decline in both male and female. This is in contrast to some works in the literature that reported that the negative effects of DSI on cognitive function were more severe in females than in males, 2 suggesting that the potential social and biological mechanisms need to be further explored. In the CHARLS, the accelerating effect of DSI on cognitive decline is common across education levels. In ELSA, on global cognitive decline, only the group with a high level of education instead showed a stronger accelerating effect of DSI on cognitive decline, which is in interesting contrast to findings that a high level of education protects cognition. 52 More educated individuals with higher cognitive reserve (CR) can better compensate for accumulating neuropathology and can resist cognitive decline through compensatory mechanisms in its early stages, 52 , 53 but when sensory impairment occurs, their CR is rapidly depleted, showing a more significant decline. 54 This finding suggests that higher education is not protective in all situations but may be associated with a higher risk of exposure under sensory impairment conditions. It highlights higher education's complex and time‐dependent association with cognitive trajectories.

The strengths of this study are it use of two large cohorts with long‐term follow‐up, which increased the robustness of the results. The cross‐country design allowed for direct comparison and analysis of cognitive trajectories before and after the onset of DSI in different sociocultural, educational, and medical contexts and strengthened the interpretability and generalizations of the results. However, limitations should also be pointed out. First, self‐report of VI and HI may be subject to error, although using the longitudinal rate of decline (slope) helped control for static measurement difficulties. Second, the presence of vision loss might have influenced self‐reported hearing and vice versa. 38 Future studies incorporating objective hearing and vision measures are needed to characterize DSI more precisely and to further verify the robustness of the observed associations. Finally, limited by the score of the evaluation index of DSI, this study did not further quantify the severity of DSI and only analyzed it as a state of presence or absence. More research is needed on the dose–response relationship between different degrees of DSI and cognitive outcomes, so as to further explain the refined mechanism of the association between sensory impairment and cognitive decline.

In conclusion, participants experienced accelerated cognitive decline upon developing new‐onset DSI. In ELSA, DSI significantly accelerated declines in episodic memory and global cognition, while CHARLS data further confirmed the adverse effects of DSI on global cognition, orientation, and episodic memory. Its adverse effects exhibit heterogeneity across different demographic characteristics. It is necessary to strengthen the detection and intervention of sensory function, which may delay the process of cognitive decline.
