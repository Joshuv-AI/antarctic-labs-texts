Predictors and differences in nursing students’ perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings in Jordan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Majd T Mrayyan, Ahmad K Al-Omari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives To examine the predictors and differences of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings, as perceived by nursing students.DesignA quantitative cross-sectional design was carried out using the consensus-based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies checklist.SettingsA governmental and a private university.ParticipantsA non-random convenience sample of 264 nursing students from universities in Jordan was recruited.Main outcome measuresThe nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings.MethodsAfter a pilot study to validate the adopted instrument for use in a student sample, an online survey using Google Forms was posted in August 2023. The acquired data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics from the Statistical Package for Social Sciences V.26.ResultsOn a 1–5 Likert Scale, a score of less than 4 indicated low nurses’ perceived cultural humility; thus, it was considered low (mean=3.51/5, SE=0.036). This cut-off was based on the scoring method employed by Sexton et al, which defined scores below 4 on a 5-point scale as low. The 95% CI for the overall mean score was 3.44 to 3.58. The highest means of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings were that nurses are considerate (mean=3.79, SE=0.060) and they already know a lot (mean=3.73, SE=0.052). The lowest mean of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was that nurses are genuinely interested in learning more (mean=3.14, SE=0.073). Being trained in governmental hospitals, an average and junior nursing student predicted the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings (t-test=13.55, p=0.001, R2=0.082, adjusted R2=0.071). At 0.05, the 95% CIs for the predictors were as follows: governmental hospital (−7.35 to –1.69), graduate point average ≤2.5 (−9.12 to –2.96) and junior level (0.09 to 4.37).ConclusionsThis study contributed valuable insights into nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings; further research is still needed. Low-reported nurses’ perceived cultural humility calls for immediate teaching strategies to integrate cultural humility within clinical settings and create more conducive learning environments. When examining the predictors and differences of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings, it became evident that hospitals had the most significant factor. It is essential to integrate cultural humility at an early stage of students’ academic level; they will treat their patients with a culturally oriented approach and will have positive perceptions of the nurses who mentor them. Hospitals had the most significant factor, specifically the type of hospital where students were trained (governmental vs private), which strongly influenced their perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility.

Introduction

The healthcare sector is experiencing a constant increase in variety in every area it encompasses.13 Among healthcare professionals, nurses have the most extensive interaction with patients from various backgrounds.4 5 Consequently, the concept of cultural humility becomes crucial when it comes to addressing patient care.136 Currently, there is a lack of clinical training on cultural humility in clinical settings.79 By fostering cultural humility, the gaps between different cultures are bridged,10 leading to enhanced understanding and a reduction in conflicts that may hinder effective relationships6 10 and the delivery of high-quality care.2 3

Cultural humility, as defined by Nolan et al,1 involves continuously evaluating and critiquing oneself. It goes beyond the concept of cultural competence, which is often taught in healthcare education and training. While cultural competency focuses on acquiring knowledge and skills to interact effectively with diverse populations, cultural humility emphasises a lifelong commitment to self-reflection, learning and recognising power imbalances in relationships.1 8 9 Instead of being interchangeable, these concepts complement each other in promoting equitable and respectful care.

Cultural humility focuses on taking action to build rapport and provide excellent patient care.3 Cultural humility is a vital aspect of nursing that helps to advance health equity. It achieves this by implementing cultural competence and sensitivity while also acknowledging the abilities of others regarding their health.1 6 By embracing cultural humility, nurses can fulfil their ethical duty to address both implicit and explicit biases and prioritise the well-being of those under their care.1 Nursing education and practice in Jordan, a developing country with diverse cultural dynamics.

Given the critical nature of health outcomes, such as patient safety, the entire nursing profession must take responsibility for promoting cultural humility within the nursing pipeline and workforce.1 By continuously evolving and enhancing their skills, nurses can effectively care for all patients and use their full scope of practice.1 Implementing a combination of top-down and bottom-up approaches can proactively address biased and discriminatory nursing practices rather than simply reacting to them.1 As part of the top-down approach, nursing faculty and clinical leaders traditionally require annual training on cultural humility.1 8 9

By embracing cultural humility, nurses in the healthcare industry can enhance their ability to comprehend and avoid conflicts that can impede the attainment of their goals, especially as the industry becomes more inclusive.2 3 10 However, it is important to note that nursing education and practice in many developing countries, like Jordan, have not yet integrated diversity fully and effectively. To address this gap, this study was conducted in Jordan to explore the predictors and differences in the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings as perceived by nursing students. The findings of this study would enhance healthcare professionals’ comprehension of the application of cultural humility within Jordanian hospitals.

Theoretical framework

The present investigation is based on the Theory of Cultural Humility,10 which acts as the underlying framework. This theory seeks to guide individuals and leaders to flourish in a world characterised by diversity. By recognising and embracing the diversity and power imbalances among various stakeholders,10 conflicts can be approached constructively. The adoption of cultural humility practices results in mutual advantages.

Cultural humility is characterised by its emphasis on flexibility, as stated by Foronda.10 The author highlighted that individuals and groups possess distinct mindsets, life experiences, behaviours and value systems. In situations where cultures clash, the potential for establishing a partnership arises when at least one side is willing to embrace open-mindedness and adaptability.10 Demonstrating flexibility often catalyses the other party to make necessary adjustments.10

The Theory of Cultural Humility is built on several assumptions.10 First, it recognises the inherent diversity among all human beings while acknowledging their membership in a global community. Second, it acknowledges the innate altruism present within humans. Third, it asserts that every individual holds equal value. Moreover, the theory acknowledges that cultural conflict is an anticipated aspect of life. Lastly, it places great emphasis on the notion that all individuals are lifelong learners.

In the context of this study, the theory helps in looking at how nursing students view cultural humility among nurses in clinical environments. Their perceptions are influenced by various factors, including the type of hospital where they receive training, their academic standing and graduate point average (GPA). These elements act as indicators and sources of variation in how cultural humility is understood and experienced. Hughes et al5 argue that cultural humility should be woven into nursing education to equip future professionals for providing inclusive and equitable care. Their research backs up the theory’s focus on transformation through reflection and learning. Michalec et al8 add to this theoretical groundwork by examining how healthcare professionals interpret humility, highlighting its importance in ethical and effective practice. Experiential learning methods, like those discussed by Boekhout7 and Piontek,9 show how cultural humility can be nurtured through simulation and virtual reality. These approaches resonate with the theory’s focus on self-awareness and reflective practice, paving the way for innovative ways to foster humility among nurses and students. By anchoring this study in the Theory of Cultural Humility,10 we aim to investigate how contextual factors shape nursing students’ views on nurses’ humility in Jordanian clinical settings. This theory provides a solid framework for exploring both predictors and differences, making it easier to interpret our findings.

Rationale for measuring cultural humility from nursing students’ perspectives

The Theory of Cultural Humility promotes a client-centred and other-oriented mindset.10 Still, this study takes a different route by examining cultural humility through the eyes of nursing students as they observe practising nurses in clinical settings. This choice is rooted in the educational significance and contextual importance of student experience.

Nursing students are in a unique position to assess how practising nurses embody cultural humility during their training. Their insights capture how humility shows up in real-life scenarios and how it shapes their growth as future professionals. This method fits perfectly with the theory’s focus on lifelong learning, self-reflection and relationship building,136 10 as students benefit from mentorship and hands-on care practices in clinical environments.5 10

Although the used scale was initially created for clients, it has been tweaked and validated in educational scenarios where students look at the humility displayed by professionals.5 7 9 In this research, nursing students act as informed observers of nurses’ actions, shedding light on how cultural humility is perceived and absorbed during critical clinical experiences.

This viewpoint is especially pertinent in Jordan, where the concept of cultural humility is still gaining traction within nursing education and practice. Gathering students’ perceptions offers a culturally relevant and sound way to evaluate how nurses express humility in various clinical environments. Additionally, it helps pave the way for developing targeted educational strategies aimed at fostering culturally humble care.

Purpose and significance of the study

The global landscape is undergoing rapid changes due to globalisation, leading to significant transformations in the overall composition of the world population.13 In the USA, for instance, apart from racial and ethnic diversity, there were notable shifts in sociodemographic aspects, including an ageing population, diverse sex and gender identities, and varying spiritual and religious belief systems.1 The increasing diversity in clinical settings necessitates the adoption of cultural humility by nurses. However, clinical training on cultural humility in clinical settings was lacking.79 Also, there are limited quantitative studies on the topic of cultural humility.

Cultural humility plays a crucial role in Jordan, especially given its distinctive sociocultural and healthcare environment. Nestled in the heart of the Middle East, Jordan is home to a richly diverse population that includes refugees, immigrants and various ethnic minorities, alongside its local citizens.1 2 4 This rich tapestry of diversity brings about unique challenges in delivering healthcare, particularly in providing care that is equitable, respectful and sensitive to cultural nuances. Furthermore, the healthcare system in Jordan is an intricate blend of public, private and military institutions, each interacting with different levels of cultural diversity.1 2 4 Despite these complexities, cultural humility is still not widely emphasised in nursing education and clinical practices across Jordan.3 5 6 8 Nurses can enhance patient care and organisational results by practising cultural humility. This approach allows them to overcome gaps, increase comprehension and prevent conflicts.136 10 These outcomes also included nurses’ process of lifelong learning, optimal care, patient satisfaction, health, wellness, respect, improved communication, empowerment, partnerships and organisational stay in the market.10

However, it is important to note that nursing education and practice in many developing countries like Jordan have not yet integrated diversity fully and effectively. To address this gap, this study explored the predictors and differences in perceived cultural humility among nurses in clinical settings based on the characteristics of the sample. This research was one of the limited recent international clinical nursing studies focusing on the nurses’ perceived cultural humility. It encompassed various aspects, such as educational settings, and is the first study of its kind conducted in Jordan. This study answered the following research questions: (1) Is there evidence of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, as perceived by nursing students? (2) To what extent do nursing students perceive nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings? (3) Which characteristics of the sample predict the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings? (4) How do the sample’s characteristics influence the differences in the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings?

As there are limited recent clinical nursing studies conducted in Jordan about the studied concept, answering these research questions would provide in-depth insights into the functioning of nurses’ cultural humility within Jordanian healthcare settings. These insights could be used to develop evidence-based protocols for healthcare and educational settings. Additionally, by simulating cultural humility, nurses and learners could engage in open and potentially uncomfortable dialogues, thereby promoting learning and fostering an appreciation for cultural humility.

Background

Although the Theory of Cultural Humility10 highlights the importance of a client-centred approach, this study delved into how nursing students perceive the cultural humility of nurses in clinical settings. This focus is important because these students, as aspiring healthcare professionals, observe and absorb the behaviours demonstrated by established nurses during their clinical training. Their insights provide valuable insights into how cultural humility is put into practice and taught in real-life situations.1 5 8 Additionally, nursing students are engaged with the latest educational materials. They are at a crucial stage in their professional growth, making their views essential for assessing how cultural humility is woven into clinical practice. By gathering student perspectives, this study sheds light on how the next generation of nurses perceives and might embrace cultural humility in their future careers.5 10

Tervalon and Murray-Garcia11 were the pioneers in implementing practices related to the provider-patient relationship. Foronda10 expanded the scope of the target population to include all individuals in positions of power, going beyond just providers and researchers. However, it is important to acknowledge that nurses play a crucial role in the front line of patient care.2

Nurses operate in dynamic environments where they constantly interact with patients, staff and providers from diverse cultural backgrounds.4 5 As a result, they must acquire a range of cultural humility practices, encompassing self-reflection and critique, self-awareness, openness, egolessness and supportive interactions.210 1216

Self-reflection and critique

Individuals must analyse and evaluate their internal and implicit biases concerning various aspects of cultural identity through self-reflection.11 12 Engaging in self-reflection and critique is an ongoing process of learning, introspection and transformation.101216 Without internally examining and critiquing themselves, individuals would be unable to recognise their implicit biases, which could have a detrimental effect on their communication with individuals from different cultures.2

Self-awareness

Cultural humility necessitates self-awareness due to the presence of implicit biases within individuals.2 Self-awareness entails recognising one’s strengths and limitations, as well as identifying personal values, beliefs and attitudes towards others.1011 1316 By cultivating self-awareness, individuals could acknowledge, manage and respond to their implicit biases, thereby influencing their attitudes, judgements and decisions.2

Openness

It plays a crucial role in cultural humility.2 Individuals must embrace interactions with people from diverse cultural backgrounds.4 5 10 11 Without the willingness to explore innovative ideas, cultural humility could not be achieved.2 Cultural diversity encompasses not only differences in ethnicity and race but also variations in beliefs, values, language, actions, customs, social groups and religion.2 11 Moreover, individuals must possess a mindset of continuous learning and engage in self-reflection.2 12

Egolessness

Egolessness refers to the absence of an ego, as stated by Scharfenberg.2 Individuals who possess egolessness exhibit humility and perceive the value of every individual on an equal level without disregarding their worth.1011 1316 By embodying egolessness through cultural humility, individuals could reduce power imbalances and promote equality in terms of human rights.2

Supportive interactions

There are various ways in which individuals could demonstrate supportive interactions.2 These interactions are particularly important in the healthcare provider-patient relationship.2 11 Supportive interactions encompass exchanges between all individuals that would lead to positive human experiences.101316 The authors emphasised that individuals who engage in supportive interactions demonstrate empathy and humility while facilitating communication. Those who embodied the practices and principles of cultural humility enhanced satisfaction, respect, communication, optimal care and overall health.2 To achieve these outcomes, it is crucial to have humble leaders who integrate cultural humility practices to address workplace inequities.2 By radiating cultural humility practices, these humble leaders would foster inclusive workplace cultures.2 Leaders who embraced cultural humility had the power to transform rigid workplace cultures into ones that align with the needs and expectations of a constantly evolving society.2

The literature highlighted cultural humility as a dynamic and evolving concept. Nurses played a vital role in recognising the significance of diversity and implementing cultural humility for successful outcomes.

Methods

Research design

Using the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies17 (online supplemental table 1), this research employed a quantitative cross-sectional design to investigate the predictors and variances in perceived cultural humility among nurses in clinical settings based on the characteristics of nursing students. The cross-sectional design allowed for the simultaneous examination of multiple characteristics and provided a snapshot of individuals at a specific point in time.18

Patient and public involvement

None.

Sample and settings

This research focused on Jordanian nursing students enrolled in different universities. The data collection process resulted in a sample of 264 Jordanian nursing students from both a government university and a private university. The students were invited to participate in an online survey through Facebook and WhatsApp, as these were the common platforms among nursing students in the selected universities in Jordan.

To determine the appropriate sample size for the linear regression analysis, the rule of thumb was applied. According to this rule, a sample size of 50+8K was required, where K represented the number of predictors.18 In this study, the required sample size was 98, considering one major variable and six sample characteristics as predictors. The major variable was the nurses’ perceived cultural humility score. The six sample characteristics included gender, age, GPA, level of study, type of training hospital and type of university. However, a larger sample of 264 nursing students was recruited from a pool of 410 potential participants, resulting in a response rate of 64.39%. The inclusion criteria for the sample were being a nursing student studying at a university and having the ability to use electronic platforms. Non-nursing students in community colleges were excluded from the study.

The instrument

According to Hughes et al,5 cultural humility is defined as a mindset and process that enables individuals to be receptive to the preferences of others through respectful inquiry and empathy. To assess nursing students’ perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility, the Cultural Humility Scale (CHS)19 was used (online supplemental file 1). The CHS, which consisted of 12 items, was publicly available and was tested for its applicability to the Jordanian context through a pilot study to validate the adopted tool. Participants rated the items on a 5-point Likert Scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total scores on the CHS ranged from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater cultural humility (20).

The original CHS was initially used to evaluate the cultural humility of counsellors in their interactions with clients.19 It was unique because the clients themselves completed it and measured how they perceived their counsellors’ abilities to continuously acquire knowledge about them and develop a working alliance.19 The CHS demonstrated strong support for internal consistency, reliability and convergent validity with multicultural competencies.19 It was a reliable instrument, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92 in the study by Ertl et al20 and 0.80 in the current study. The pilot study, as well as the whole study, showed that the adopted instrument was valid and reliable for use in a student sample. Three experts in nursing leadership and management provided input that supported the content validity of the instrument. The Cronbach’s alpha for the first 30 student responses on the CHS was 0.81, indicating good internal consistency. The pilot study helped confirm that students understood the items and found them relevant to their clinical experiences.

Students’ sample characteristics collected were gender, age, GPA, level of study (junior (first and second year) vs senior (third and fourth year)), type of organisation where students were trained (governmental vs private) and type of the university where the students studied (governmental vs private).

Ethics

Prior to data collection, all necessary approvals were acquired. The invitation letter explicitly stated to nursing students that their involvement in the study indicated their consent to participate. It was emphasised that participation was voluntary, allowing nursing students the freedom to withdraw at any point. Confidentiality was maintained by collecting anonymous responses using unique codes and securely storing passwords for coded responses.

Data collection

Data were gathered in August 2023. The study used English as the official language of nursing education in Jordan. To assess the effectiveness and comprehensibility of the tools, a pilot study involving 30 nursing students was conducted, and no modifications were deemed necessary. Recruitment was conducted via open invitation using Facebook and WhatsApp, which were common platforms among nursing students. The survey remained accessible for 3 weeks. Following the initial week, students were reminded to complete the survey only once, and the Google Form was designed to permit a single response.

Data analyses

A statistical significance level of 0.05 was chosen, and the data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences V.26.21 Descriptive statistics such as means, SEM or frequencies, percentages, 95% CIs of the mean and range were reported to address the first and second research questions, depending on the types of variables. No intervention was carried out for the few missing data points.

Before performing the stepwise multiple regression analysis, the necessary statistical assumptions were thoroughly evaluated to ensure the validity of the model. Linearity was assessed through residual plots to confirm a linear relationship between predictors and the outcome variable. Independence of errors was assumed based on the study design. Homoscedasticity was tested using the Breusch-Pagan test (value of p=0.001), which indicated some heteroscedasticity. Normality of residuals was examined via Q-Q plots, showing an approximately normal distribution. Multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor, with all values below 2 (such as that for GPA=1.45, level of education=1.47 and the type of hospitals where the students were trained=1.01), indicating no multicollinearity concerns. Additionally, Cook’s distance was calculated to identify influential outliers (p=0.0652), and all values were within acceptable limits. These diagnostic checks confirmed that the data were suitable for regression analysis.

According to Sexton et al,22 a score of 4 or higher on a 5-point scale indicated high nurses’ perceived cultural humility, while a score of less than 4 indicated low nurses’ perceived cultural humility. In this study, the dependent interval variable was the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings, and the independent categorical variables were the characteristics of the sample.18

To answer the third research question, the researchers used stepwise regression analysis.18 Stepwise regression analysis, a form of multiple linear regression, was chosen because the authors were unsure which characteristics of the sample would have a greater impact on the prediction model. For the fourth research question, a t-test was employed to examine the differences between the total score and individual items of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings based on the sample’s characteristics.18 The t-test was conducted with two categorical independent variables. The group’s SD and items’ SD did not show extreme differences, which facilitated the comparisons. Initially, the researcher compared the total mean score of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings across all demographics. If significant differences were found, further comparisons were made at the item level of the scale.

The nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings

Similar to the scoring method employed by Sexton et al,22 a high level of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was indicated by a score of 4 or higher on the 5-point scale, while a score of 3 or below indicated a poor level of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings. On a Likert Scale of 1–5, nursing students ‘agreed’ on the presence of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, but it was low (mean=3.51/5, SE=0.036). This cut-off was based on Sexton et al,22 who defined scores below 4 on a 5-point scale as low. The 95% CI for the overall mean score was 3.44 to 3.58. The highest means of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings were that nurses were considerate (mean=3.79, SE=0.060, 95% CI 3.68 to 3.91) and they already knew a lot (mean=3.73, SE=0.052, 95% CI 3.63 to 3.84). On the other hand, the lowest mean of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was that nurses were genuinely interested in learning more (mean=3.14, SE=0.073, 95% CI 3.00 to 3.29) (table 2).

Predictors of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings

Stepwise regression analysis was used to assess whether the samples’ characteristics, the independent variables, predict the overall nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings, the dependent variable. The type of hospitals where the students were trained, GPA and nursing students’ level of education predicted the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings (t-test=13.55, p=0.001, R2=0.082, adjusted R2=0.071); around 7% of the variance in the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was attributed to the three predictors together (table 3).

The final prediction model indicates that the nurses’ perceived cultural humility score is influenced by three key factors: the type of hospital where students were trained, their GPA and their level of education. Specifically, students trained in governmental hospitals reported lower scores (−4.52), suggesting that institutional context may affect perceptions of cultural humility. Similarly, students with a GPA ≤2.5 had lower scores (−6.04), which may reflect differences in academic engagement or expectations. Conversely, junior-level students reported higher scores (+2.23), possibly due to their recent exposure to culturally sensitive training or greater receptiveness to mentorship.5 10 The intercept value of 58.26 represents the baseline score when all predictors are zero.

Differences in the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings

The t-test was used to assess the differences in the perceived nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, the interval dependent variable, and the samples’ characteristics, the categorical independent variable. There were significant differences in the overall mean of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings based on students’ GPA (t-test=2.40, p=0.022) (table 4) and the hospitals where the students were trained (t-test=4.08, p=0.001) (table 5). Based on their GPA, average students with a GPA equal to or less than 2.5 were higher in their perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings concerning that nurses were open to exploring (t-test=2.23, p=0.032), acted superiorly (t-test=3.23, p=0.003), were open to seeing things from students’ perspectives (t-test=4.74, p=0.001), and nurses knew-it-alls (t-test=3.21, p=0.003) (table 4). Based on hospitals where the students were trained, 7 of the 12 means were different. All differences came from students who were trained in government organisations (table 5). These differences were that nurses were perceived as more respectful, open to exploring, genuinely interested in learning more, open to seeing things from students’ perspectives, open-minded and not know-it-alls. Still, they thought they understood more than they actually did.

Discussion

This study examined the predictors of and differences in nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, as perceived by nursing students.

Nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings

Nursing students ‘agreed’ on the presence of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, but it was low. The current finding may be attributed to the novelty of the concept within the healthcare system of Jordan. This finding aligns with the limited integration of cultural humility in Jordanian and Middle Eastern nursing literature, where the concept remains underexplored and relatively new in both academic and clinical contexts.2 5 It is worth noting that nurses in Jordan provide equitable treatments to all patients, regardless of their cultural, religious and socioeconomic backgrounds, similar to other nurses around the world.4 5 Equity serves as a fundamental principle in numerous cultures, including the Arab Islamic culture.23 Nevertheless, it is imperative to address and overcome any perceived deficiency in cultural humility within nursing clinical settings. Such deficiencies may indicate the presence of conscious or unconscious biases towards patients, resulting in subpar quality of care, ineffective communication and diminished patient satisfaction.24

In the realm of healthcare in Jordan, cultural humility manifests through the dedication of nurses who strive to offer fair treatment to every patient, including refugees, immigrants and those from marginalised communities.1 2 4 However, despite their hard work, health disparities continue to exist in Jordan, especially among low-income groups and displaced individuals. While these disparities might not be identical to those seen in the USA, they highlight systemic issues like limited access to specialised care, overcrowded public hospitals and a lack of resources.3 5 Embracing cultural humility is crucial for addressing these inequalities, as it prompts nurses to acknowledge and tackle implicit biases, power dynamics and the distinct needs of various patient populations.1 6 10 On a global scale, health inequities are a significant concern, and Jordan’s healthcare system faces its own set of challenges. By promoting cultural humility in nursing education and practice, nurses in Jordan can more effectively tackle the complexities of global health disparities, ensuring that care is respectful, inclusive and centred around the patient.1 5 7 8

The highest means of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was that nurses were considerate and already knew a lot. The students had positive perceptions of nurses who provided them with first-hand experiences in clinical settings. This finding highlights a strength of the study, as students’ recognition of nurses as ‘considerate and knowledgeable’ underscores the positive influence of clinical mentors on learners’ perceptions of cultural humility.5 10 Culturally oriented nurses demonstrate consideration and sensitivity towards their patients, and they extend the same courtesy to their nursing students. It is expected that culturally oriented nurses, who possess both creativity and knowledge, have a profound impact on nursing students, inspiring them to become creative and knowledgeable individuals themselves.25 These nurses establish an environment of respect, therapeutic communication and openness with the students. These nurses are receptive to learning, which could also be applied to the process of teaching nursing students. The development of cultural humility among nursing students is crucial in order to deliver compassionate and effective care to diverse patients.26

Contrarily, nursing students today think that nurses working in clinical environments do not have a real desire to expand their knowledge, which contradicts what Peters et al27 found. This discovery could mean that nursing students are not happy with their clinical internships and are unhappy with the high level of supervision from both practising nurses and their academic instructors,27 as anticipated.

Predictors of and differences in the nurses’ perceived cultural humility

The perceived cultural humility of nurses in clinical settings was influenced by nursing students’ training in governmental hospitals and their status as average and junior nursing students. The first predictor, which was a unique finding, could be attributed to the fact that patients admitted to government hospitals in Jordan were often individuals with government insurance, low-income individuals or immigrants. The presence of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings was more pronounced and prevalent in government hospitals, leading to better perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility by nursing students. However, it is important to interpret this result cautiously, considering that the majority of students received their training in government hospitals. This overrepresentation may introduce bias, as students’ perceptions could be disproportionately shaped by experiences in government settings, limiting the generalisability of findings across other healthcare sectors.

Being junior nursing students predicted the perceived cultural humility of nurses in clinical settings. However, caution should be exercised when interpreting this result, as the majority of the students were in their junior years. This finding could be explained by the fact that most nursing schools in Jordan have recently incorporated diversity and cultural humility into their curricula. Although this explanation is plausible, it remains speculative without direct curricular analysis. Literature suggests that curricular exposure to cultural humility can positively influence students’ attitudes and perceptions.5 10 Junior students were just exposed to cultural humility, and thus, they could evaluate whether the nurses who mentor them practise cultural humility in clinical settings.5 10 Therefore, it is recommended that cultural humility be integrated into all courses at the early stages of students’ academic journey to enhance their transformational learning in nursing. It is worth noting that traditional teaching methods should not be used to teach cultural humility in the classrooms.13 Instead, cultural humility simulation education9 13 and clinical virtual reality7 are the most effective interventions to introduce to nursing students and, later, to nurses.

There were significant differences in the overall mean of nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings, depending on the GPAs of the students and the hospitals where they received training. The current ‘average’ nursing students were higher in their perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, considering that nurses were open to exploring, acting superiorly, open to seeing things from students’ perspectives, and that nurses were know-it-alls. This finding may reflect that average-performing students are more attuned to interpersonal dynamics and may value relational aspects of care more than high-achieving peers. In the Jordanian context, where hierarchical structures are common, these students may perceive humility as a counterbalance to authority, enhancing their appreciation of culturally humble behaviours.1 2 These results contradicted those of Pierre et al;28 the students with higher GPA scores increased their scores of ‘cultural awareness’ from 5.09 to 5.48, while those with lower GPAs increased their scores from 4.18 to 4.30. The present study suggested a potential lack of cultural tolerance among academically underperforming students, which is similar to the findings of Verkuyten et al.29 28

Nursing students who received their training in government hospitals exhibited more favourable perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings. This new finding aligns with the above finding on the primary predictor of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings. The most significant factor in predicting and comparing the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings was the training received in government hospitals. However, it is important to interpret this result cautiously due to the majority of students being trained in government hospitals, where individuals with low incomes or immigrants were typically admitted in Jordan. The nurses’ perceived cultural humility in government hospitals should not be overlooked, as it could be more prominently observed and used. Consequently, nursing students trained in government hospitals perceived nurses’ cultural humility more positively. Nurses in government hospitals were perceived to be more respectful, open to exploring, genuinely interested in learning more, open to seeing things from students’ perspectives, open-minded and not know-it-alls.

However, the nursing students reported that nurses in government hospitals thought they understood more than they actually did. This observation could be attributed to the high level of job security that existed in these hospitals, which may lead to complacency and outdated knowledge among the nurses. This perception aligned with the belief held by the current nursing students that nurses in clinical settings lacked a genuine interest in expanding their knowledge, which is in contrast to the findings of Peters et al.27 Additionally, the majority of the current sample consisted of junior students who often find themselves under excessive supervision from the nurses who mentor them.5 10 This dynamic could lead to dissatisfaction among these juniors.

Furthermore, most of the current nursing students were juniors and were trained in outdated infrastructure in government hospitals in Jordan, which are often overcrowded with other health-related students. These factors may contribute to the students’ dissatisfaction with their clinical placements and subsequently influence their perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, similar to Peters et al.27 Cultural humility comprises practising self-reflection to understand cultural differences and improve care for underrepresented populations.

In conclusion, hospitals were the most significant factor, specifically the type of hospital (government vs private) where students were trained; these hospitals influenced students’ perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility. These findings of the final prediction model highlight the importance of educational and institutional environments in shaping students’ perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility.

Strengths and limitations

As the first international study on the subject, it added to our understanding of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings in Jordan. Cultural humility comprises practising self-reflection to gain a better knowledge of cultural differences, with the ultimate goal of improving treatment for underrepresented people in clinical settings.30 Cultural humility recognises that as individuals are exposed to a range of cultures, they become increasingly mindful of their limited understanding of others.30

On the other hand, this study has some methodological issues, such as nursing students’ self-reports of cultural humility in clinical settings that may be biased. Also, the data were acquired using non-random sampling and a cross-sectional research approach, which reduced the generalisability of the findings. Using WhatsApp and Facebook for data collection limited the possible participation of nursing students who use other electronic platforms, and in turn, limited the generalisability of the results to the target population of nursing students. Collecting data in Jordan has limited applicability to other countries. Finally, the two variables ‘GPA’ and ‘type of hospital’ were considered as two important predictor variables. Variable GPA has only 28 samples in the ‘GPA<2.5’ group, and variable ‘type of hospital’ has only 26 samples in the private group; this number of samples may not be enough to conclude. Given the small number of samples, an independent t-test may not be the most appropriate test. The values of R in table 3 were very low, and it seems that these variables were not the only predictors. Because of all these limitations, findings must be interpreted with caution.

Implications for clinical settings

Nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings is important because it influences patient satisfaction, optimal care, respect, nurses’ lifelong learning process, organisational market survival, improved communication, empowerment and partnerships.136 10 Thus, cultural humility should be fostered and maintained, necessitating training programmes for all professionals, including nurses.810 This study has important consequences for nurses and nursing leaders.

The current study’s lowest perceived items of the nurses’ perceived cultural humility in clinical settings indicated that nurses were not sincerely interested in learning more; this was a worrying finding. Nurses serve as change agents for our students, performing a variety of tasks such as teaching and mentoring.5 10 If the mentors are not eager to learn new things, how will the students be? These nurses may not have been exposed to cultural humility in their curricula, or they were taught a competency-based curriculum while new ones evolved; nursing has shifted towards caring more about people.5 So, it is time to improve nurses’ cultural humility orientation and skills through in-service education.5 Cultural humility education programmes,5 8 simulation education9 13 and clinical virtual reality7 are the most effective interventions for those nurses. In the latter training, a nurse played herself in a virtual reality simulation, reliving a real-life situation in which she was a victim of cultural abuse by a patient. Two other healthcare staff members who witnessed the incident refused to assist her. Participants watched the culturally insensitive encounter and had a meaningful discussion about suitable reactions.7 9

It is important to challenge nurses to expand their knowledge early on. Nurses must understand that ongoing learning strengthens their professional reputation. Embracing cultural humility means consistently learning and reflecting on oneself.12

Implications for future research

Future data must be collected using random cluster sampling and a longitudinal research approach using different platforms other than WhatsApp and Facebook only. Nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings is linked to unique cultural contexts; hence, further studies will be undertaken in various nations. Also, using interval psychology scales, which rarely have normal residuals, results in the removal of any potential link with any confounding variables, such as the type of organisations or hospitals; these would be controlled for in future studies.

Implications for nursing management

Nursing leaders must implement early initiatives to counter nurses’ disinterest in genuine learning. To promote cultural humility in clinical settings, nursing leaders create inclusive learning environments for all workers, including nurses,7 15 especially since these nurses take the lead when it comes to patients and nursing students. Finally, cultural humility practices could be one of the nurses’ performance evaluation criteria.

Conclusions

This unique international study was conducted in Jordan and looked at the predictors and differences in cultural humility among nurses in clinical settings, as reported by nursing students. An average and junior nursing student predicted perceived cultural humility among nurses in clinical settings. Average nursing students had positive perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings.

Cultural humility in nursing practice would benefit patients, nurses and organisations. Although this study provided useful insights regarding nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, more research is needed. To address the perceived low cultural humility in nursing practice, nursing leaders should implement instructional methodologies that integrate cultural humility into clinical settings and establish conducive learning environments. When investigating the predictions and differences in students’ perceptions of nurses’ cultural humility in clinical settings, it became clear that the type of hospital was the most important factor.
