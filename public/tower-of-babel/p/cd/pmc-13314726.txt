Talking surveys: How photorealistic embodied conversational agents shape response quality, engagement, and satisfaction
Authors: Matus Krajcovic, Peter Demcak, Eduard Kuric
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Embodied conversational agents (ECAs) are increasingly more realistic and capable of dynamic conversations. In online surveys, anthropomorphic agents could help address issues like careless responding and satisficing, which originate from the lack of personal engagement and perceived accountability. However, there is a lack of understanding of how ECAs in user experience research may affect participant engagement, satisfaction, and the quality of responses. We introduce a method, Virtual Agent Interviewer, and validate it in a randomized study. Our proof-of-concept method enables the incorporation of conversations with a virtual avatar into surveys using AI-driven video generation, speech recognition, and Large Language Models. In our between-subjects study, 80 participants (UK, stratified random sample of the general population) either talked to a voice-based agent with an animated video avatar, or interacted with a chatbot. Our evaluation entails 2265 conversation responses obtained across surveys based on two self-reported psychometric tests. Statistical comparison of the results indicates that embodied agents can contribute significantly to more informative, detailed responses, as well as higher yet more time-efficient engagement. Furthermore, qualitative analysis provides valuable insights about the causes of no significant change to satisfaction, linked to personal preferences, turn-taking delays, and Uncanny Valley reactions. These findings support and inform the development of new AI-driven embodiment-based methods for the transformation of online surveys into more natural interactions resembling in-person interviews.

Introduction

Unmoderated user research is on the rise, offering quick insights that empower rapid decisions and fine-tuning of user experience and design (He, 2021). Without a flexible and well-trained moderator overseeing the study’s administration, the responsibility falls to an automated system—the user interface of the tool, research protocol, prototype design and so forth—to ensure proper participant engagement. This is essential to collect useful data and avoid wasted research resources (Schirra, Volkov, Narasimha, & Bentley, 2023). Until now, the benefits typically gained from human interviewing—rapport-building, personal and personalized touch in the collection of feedback, clearing up misunderstandings—were accepted as an inherent and inevitable tradeoff, with methods designed to partially mitigate its impact (Khayyatkhoshnevis et al., 2022; Kim, Lee, & Gweon, 2019; Ward & Meade, 2018). However, more comprehensive solutions might be within reach.

Embodied conversational agents (ECAs) are intelligent software entities, designed for interactive verbal and nonverbal communication, visually represented as anthropomorphic characters (Provoost, Lau, Ruwaard, & Riper, 2017). Driven by AI, they can act as mediators for digital communication (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020). Recent advancements in technologies like natural language processing (NLP) and generative artificial intelligence (GAI) have enhanced their conversational ability by dynamically predicting appropriate responses (Lim, Schmälzle, & Bente, 2025; Yang, Acevedo, Guo, Choi, & Mousas, 2025) and enabled creation of photorealistic embodiments (Tu et al., 2022). However, there is currently a lack of validated instruments incorporating ECAs in this context. The readiness of ECAs to take on the role of a user research moderator remains an open question. The most closely related studies were in the domain of public opinion research, investigating the effect of ECAs on the collection of sensitive information (e.g., tobacco use, sexual behaviors, emotional state) rather than explorative information-gathering within online user research environments, or otherwise limited to Wizard of Oz methods and rule-based systems without conversational AI (Conrad et al., 2015; Laban & Cross, 2024; Zhu & Broadbent, 2025).

Aiming to address this research gap, the chief contributions of our research are twofold:Methodological. We propose and implement Virtual Agent Interviewer, a prototype instrument for conducting surveys mediated by an embodied conversational agent (ECA), utilizing current AI-driven technologies like large language models (LLMs).Empirical validation. We conduct a between-subjects experiment. In the context of follow-up questions in a survey, we evaluate the effect of an embodied AI-driven agent compared to an AI chatbot baseline. Two questionnaire surveys yielded 2265 responses from follow-up conversations by 80 participants sampled from the general population in the UK. We perform a comprehensive mixed-methods evaluation of response quality, user engagement, and satisfaction, supporting the positive impact of ECAs for surveys and providing insights for tuning future solutions.The following sections contain Section “Background” the background, analyzing the current knowledge and the state of the issue, Section “Method” our solution and the proposal of our instrument, Section “Study” the experimental method for the instrument’s validation, Section “Results” the result data and findings, Section “Discussion” the discussion of implications, limitations and subjects of interest for future study, and finally Section “Conclusion” the conclusion.

Background

Prior research explored the potential of AI and machine learning to enhance the collection of data in user research, such as by incorporating LLM chatbots to improve survey interactivity (Kim et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2020) or using deep-learning-based eye tracking to extract additional information from webcam footage (Kuric, Demcak, Majzel, & Nguyen, 2025a). However, applications of AI-driven anthropomorphic agents that leverage multiple types of AI models at once to tackle complex problems of unmoderated research remain scant. This section presents the methodological thread that situates our instrument in the context of status quo methods and existing work, as well as an empirical thread to explore the capacity of ECAs to support social interaction and lay the theoretical groundwork for our instrument’s evaluation.

Conversation in user research methodology

The proposed instrument aims to incorporate embodied conversational agents in user research. We situate our instrument methodologically, through a comparison to traditional methods and problems they solved through conversations and human moderator presence, text-based conversational agents, as well as current knowledge on ECAs in user research methodology. AI-driven face-and-voice agents in surveys comprise a mode shift and could therefore affect result accuracy (Abbasi, Laban, Ford, Jones, & Gunes, 2024).

Moderation and interviewer presence

In participant-based research, moderation is a means for researchers to flexibly facilitate the collection of data (Bordegoni, Carulli, & Spadoni, 2023). Trained moderators can recognize appropriate situations to interject (De Bleecker & Okoroji, 2018), enabling them to provide additional instructions or ask ad-hoc questions. Conversations between participants and moderators are dynamic, with rotating roles of the speaker and the listener. Organic conversations can be supported by various verbalizations, including those of seemingly minimal semantic value like affirmations (e.g., “okay”, “mm hm”) (Hertzum & Kristoffersen, 2018). The flexibility of the moderator and natural conversations allows for adaptive solving of issues, such as confusing directions, or a prototype not displaying as intended on the participant’s device (Schirra et al., 2023). The accessibility of research can thus be improved for persons who may otherwise find it challenging, like people with cognitive impairments (Kleban, 2024). As a potential tradeoff, in research about sensitive topics (e.g., number of sex partners), participants are inclined to feel more discomfort in the presence of an interviewer, as Okon, Dolgin, and Schober (2025) demonstrated through preferences between hypothetical live online interviews versus unmoderated survey alternatives.

Interviewing and conversational techniques also find potential applications outside of typical UX research, such as in psychometrics. For example, in personality measurement, structured interviews are used to capture more nuanced aspects of personality and its manifestation through behavioral and lexical indicators (Heimann, Ingold, Debus, & Kleinmann, 2021; Hörz-Sagstetter et al., 2018; G. Jiang et al., 2023; H. Jiang et al., 2024). Personality information is embedded in language (Saucier & Goldberg, 2001), as evidenced by the origin of the Big Five model in lexical analysis (Piedmont & Aycock, 2007). Personality assessment can also enrich insights when incorporated into user research (Kuric, Demcak, & Krajcovic, 2026).

Despite the many advantages of moderated user research, unmoderated research has grown progressively more popular over the period of the last two decades, with crowdsourcing of unmoderated user studies now being an established industry (Kittur, Chi, & Suh, 2008; Liu, Bias, Lease, & Kuipers, 2012; Schirra et al., 2023). Its advantages—namely remote administration, scalability through crowdsourcing from large pools, and cost-effectiveness—can be traced back to the increased prominence of web-based research as a de facto go-to approach substituting previous paper-based methods (Hohwü et al., 2013), further amplified by the rising number of internet users (Huang, 2006). The ecological validity of studies can also be kept intact in the realistic conditions of the participant’s home. The absence of moderation can impact the feedback given by participants, such as by increasing their ratio of relevant verbalizations, since fewer verbalizations are process-related (Hertzum, Borlund, & Kristoffersen, 2015).

While moderated and unmoderated research are both subject to the evaluator effect to a similar degree (Hertzum, Molich, & Jacobsen, 2014), unmoderated usability testing can yield less reliable data that needs to be filtered for quality (Khayyatkhoshnevis et al., 2022). The lack of user engagement is linked to careless responding (Ward & Meade, 2018) and satisficing (responding in a passable but superficial, cognitively passive manner) (Kim et al., 2019) as sources of low-quality responses. Limited modality of communication can increase attrition, reducing the statistical power of surveys, as well as their internal validity, given that drop-off can correlate with variables such as personality traits (Ward, Meade, Allred, Pappalardo, & Stoughton, 2017). These issues underscore the potential to augment unmoderated research by developing methods that enhance its interactivity and feel more human.

Conversational agents

Colloquially referred to as chatbots, conversational agents are technologies capable of communicating with users in natural language. Whether rule-based or AI-driven, their central challenge resides in natural language processing aimed at generating appropriate responses to user inputs (Khan & Das, 2018; Ren, Zapata, Castro, Dieste, & Acuña, 2022). Large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT brought significant advancements in the field, as corroborated by their good performance in language tasks (Iizuka et al., 2024). Their advent contributed to the significant expansion of chatbot usage in various fields, including e-commerce, finance, travel, and gaming (Bilquise, Ibrahim, & Shaalan, 2022; Yenduri et al., 2024).

Conversational agents can augment user research, like traditional surveys, by transforming the process into social interaction (Celino & Re Calegari, 2020; Liu & Martens, 2024). In choice-based surveys, Kim et al. (2019) demonstrated the concept by incorporating LLMs and comparing four conditions with two independent variables: standard survey vs. chatbot and formal vs. casual communication style. The chatbot with a casual style reduced satisficing and contributed to higher enjoyment. Xiao et al. (2020) expanded the concept to chatbots asking open-ended questions. Analysis of textual responses based on Gricean maxims as indicators of communication quality corroborates that chatbot surveys are preferable for collecting high-quality information. Nonetheless, the introduction of AI chatbots into research interactions can pose new challenges. Kuric, Demcak, and Krajcovic (2024) assessed the capability of LLMs to ask follow-up questions in usability testing. While providing more detailed information, repetition triggered frustration and led to less relevant responses. To enable further systematic exploration of social interaction with LLM agents, Laban, Laban, and Gunes (2024b) developed an open-source experimentation platform.

In spite of fostering engagement, text-based conversational agents are still limited to a single channel of communication. Anthropomorphized agents that add layers of speech and embodiment could tackle the risks of detachment and disengagement common for unmoderated online research by evoking in participants a perception of proximity to the researchers and raising their sense of accountability (Ward et al., 2017).

Embodied conversational agents and virtual avatars

By combining multiple modalities of humanlike appearance and behavior (speech, animated face with lip syncing, gestures, facial expressions), embodied conversational agents (ECAs) can create the impression of socially interacting with a person in possession of a physical body (Kipp, Heloir, Schröder, & Gebhard, 2010; Lugrin, Pelachaud, & Traum, 2022). The term virtual avatar denotes embodied representations of agents in virtual environments, but also of users, who may hold a sense of embodiment towards their avatar (Genay, Lécuyer, & Hachet, 2022; Kim & Jang, 2025). Since the simulation of a realistic person is highly complex, most research on ECAs is task-oriented or specialized, originating from domains where human contact is critical for building rapport and engagement. Prevailing domains include healthcare, psychology, and education (Provoost et al., 2017; ter Stal, Kramer, Tabak, op den Akker, & Hermens, 2020; Yang et al., 2025). Other use cases include marketing, e-commerce, customer support, and gaming (Qu, Lo, & Baek, 2025; Yang et al., 2025).

Realism and anthropomorphism are central concepts for ECAs. Increasing realism is a common goal that can improve the quality of user-agent interactions (Groom et al., 2009; Qu et al., 2025). Human response to ECAs can approach their treatment of humans in the presence of highly realistic social cues (Nass & Moon, 2000; von der Pütten, Krämer, Gratch, & Kang, 2010). Depending on its implementation, however, high realism can sometimes be to a detriment. Evidence supports the Uncanny Valley theory, a concept originally from robotics, which states that small imperfections of entities bearing strong human likeness (e.g., virtual agents, humanoid robots) can evoke more disturbing and eerie experiences in humans than entities designed to resemble humans only moderately (Groom et al., 2009; Reinhardt, Hillen, & Wolf, 2020; Seyama & Nagayama, 2007). Preferences toward the degree of realism can vary between individuals (Tan, Atmosukarto, Tandianus, Shen, & Wong, 2025). User behavior can also be affected by the agent’s appearance and familiarity, as Tan et al. (2025) demonstrated with students in an educational system.

Inclusion of embodied agents with human faces in user interfaces tends to result in more positive interactions and attitudes (Yee, Bailenson, & Rickertsen, 2007). A visible agent has the advantage of additional communication cues, such as eye contact, which can contribute to a more favorable perception of pragmatic aspects such as ease of use (Reinhardt et al., 2020). Effective nonverbal communication by ECAs is an ongoing subject of study, with gesture generation being a prominent aspect alongside the likes of facial expressions and posture, which can impact the understanding of communicated information (Wolfert, Robinson, & Belpaeme, 2022).

Currently, ECAs are undergoing an AI wave (Schöbel et al., 2024). Fueled by advances in generative artificial intelligence (GAI) and natural language processing (NLP), they aim to overcome some of their past limitations. Aside from being able to lead speech-based conversations, AI-based tools such as HeyGen1, Synthesia2 or Azure AI text to speech3 can generate videos driven by data, resulting in more realistic appearances than 3D models rendered by standard computer graphics (such as those investigated by Zhu & Broadbent 2025). The need to manually model, rig, and animate 3D assets is eliminated. Although proprietary, these solutions feature pre-built customizable models, facilitating their adaptation for broad use cases. Examples of their application include generation of educational videos for students (Contreras, Hossfeld, de Boer, Wiedler, & Ghidinelli, 2024) or personalized delivery of information to medicinal patients (Badawy, Khamwan, & Carrion, 2025).

The advent of aforementioned AI-based video technologies sets the stage for innovation in user research. As this article aims to demonstrate, virtual agents simulating aspects of a human moderator could support interaction with participants through added modalities and positive engagement. This may allow unmoderated user experiments to reap some of the benefits traditionally associated with moderation, such as higher-quality data through reduced satisficing and careless responding.

Validation of embodiment

After methodologically situating AI-driven ECAs facilitation in user research, it is important to understand the current empirical evidence about the effects that embodiment may have within the field. This enables informed evaluation of our solution aimed at expanding current empirical knowledge, as well as theory-based interpretation of our findings.

The capacity of embodied agents to encourage participants to self-disclose was empirically demonstrated in human interaction with social robots and virtual humans (Laban & Cross, 2024). Embodiment—whether physical or virtual—can help humans overcome social-emotional barriers, shame, or stigma. The primary studied applications comprise therapy and companionship, such as in health interventions (Laban, George, Morrison, & Cross, 2021), psychosocial assessment interviews (Lucas, Gratch, King, & Morency, 2014; Zhu & Broadbent, 2025), emotion regulation interventions (Laban, Wang, & Gunes, 2025b), counseling to caregivers (Laban, Morrison, Kappas, & Cross, 2025a), and diagnosis of tobacco, alcohol and drug use disorders (Auriacombe et al., 2018; Bickmore, Rubin, & Simon, 2020). They can simulate reciprocity, provide value such as rich emotional expression and support, and even become the object of social bonds. Over extended periods of time, a growing social bond with an agent can further increase the volume of disclosures (Laban, Kappas, Morrison, & Cross, 2024a).

Driving factors of self-disclosure supported by embodied agents include rapport, confidentiality, emotional support, and individual catalysts (Laban & Cross, 2024). These might be applied to user research, albeit adapted in context and priorities reflecting the different social setting. Self-disclosure can be gauged through subjective and objective measures (Laban et al., 2021). The Wizard of Oz approach was used as a common explorative method to investigate the potential effectiveness of embodied agent interventions (Laban et al., 2021, 2024a), although functioning autonomous AI- and LLM-driven systems were also demonstrated (Laban et al., 2025a; Laban, Wang, & Gunes, 2025b; Lucas et al., 2014).

In the context of user research, the concept of ECA facilitation was discussed by Cassell and Miller (2007), who highlighted the risk of social desirability bias similar to the effect of a human interviewer. Comparing a scripted prerecorded motion-capture animated avatar to a disembodied voice (both of which were responded to via text), Lind, Schober, Conrad, and Reichert (2013) showed an inhibition in self-disclosure to social survey items sensitive to social desirability bias. The effect of reduced self-disclosure in sensitive questions when interacting with faces (human or agent’s) was replicated by Pickard and Roster (2020). In pre-scripted interviews, voice mode can yield reduced self-disclosures and diversity of data over asynchronous text (SMS) mode (Schober et al., 2015) . Nonetheless, in the context of web surveys and open questions, voice answers provide richer content (more topics covered by open-ended answers) while having lower lexical diversity than more polished text (Höhne, Gavras, & Claassen, 2024). The naturalness of speech-to-speech agents can be challenged by the latency of automated speech recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS) systems (Elfleet & Chollet, 2024). Higher behavioral realism (irrespective of perception as a human or an agent) can result in the sharing of more information (von der Pütten et al., 2010). Correspondingly, a Wizard of Oz study by Conrad et al. (2015) showed that agents with better dialogue capacity are treated more socially and rated as more personal. A higher degree of facial animations in the 3D head model resulted in more social engagement (acknowledging utterances, smiles)—but also in more uncanny reactions (user smiles could have been caused by discomfort, as the authors acknowledged).

While current works provide a robust theoretical groundwork, there is a lack of empirical investigations that fully combine all the following aspects:assessing fully automated LLM-driven agentsenriched by AI-driven photorealistic speech-to-speech animated embodimentapplied in the context of online user research, withhigh ecological validity of a study conducted in the real remote online user research environment (e.g., participant’s home).Rather than focus specifically on self-disclosure (Laban & Cross, 2024), we investigate how the addition of a highly realistic virtual human affects the results of surveys in user research. Instead of focusing on desirability bias—an established tradeoff, comparable to human interviewers (Pickard & Roster, 2020)—we assess how high behavioral realism and the social effects it evokes (according to the Ethopoeia concept) (von der Pütten et al., 2010) can enhance engagement and social interactivity of user research without the social presence of a moderator. Advancements in AI make such evaluation possible for virtual avatars with more realistic appearances than previous works (Bickmore et al., 2020; Conrad et al., 2015; Lind et al., 2013; von der Pütten et al., 2010; Zhu & Broadbent, 2025).

Method

To create more engaging and natural-feeling interactions in user research, we propose simulated moderation that leverages the strengths of human-like ECAs integrated with AI-driven conversational models. As a paradigm commonly embraced for its convenience and scalability, unmoderated research holds great potential for an enhancement that could mitigate its inherent challenges: the lack of human contact and spontaneous interactivity. This pursuit aligns with goals of human-centered artificial intelligence, promoting augmentation over automation to extend the abilities of researchers (Esposito, Desolda, & Lanzilotti, 2024).

With that overarching ambition, the methodological contribution of this research represents a proof of concept, balancing its vision with a manageable scope. Moderation in user research, when performed by humans, is a skill with many facets, requiring the ability to flexibly react to unique emergent problems, to know when and how to intercept, how to ask questions, and spot contradictions between expectations, participant statements, and behavior (Kuric et al., 2024). These are complex capabilities that would merit individual research. Therefore, the prototype introduced in this section is designed to isolate and assess the effects of ECAs on user research in their base form.

We introduce the Virtual Agent Interviewer (VAI), a prototype of an online survey tool that incorporates an AI-driven ECA into its survey flow. This agent can perform certain functions of a moderator by interacting with the participants, asking them follow-up questions to obtain deeper insights about their answers. Additionally, it can facilitate the delivery of instructions and debrief sessions by interactively answering participants’ questions. Because of this diversity, surveys were chosen as an appropriate baseline technique for the adoption of the Virtual Agent Interviewer. Study

An experiment was conducted to validate the proposed facilitation of surveys by AI-driven embodied conversational agents (ECAs) with face and speech. A disembodied text-driven agent (chatbot) was used as the baseline. The rationale for this is that chatbots represent the state-of-the-art AI-driven facilitation method in surveys that was empirically determined to increase participant enjoyment and increase the depth and quality of obtained data (Kim et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2020) in comparison to traditional surveys.

Research questions

The primary objective of this research is to evaluate the contribution of the Virtual Agent Interviewer toward surveys that provide a better experience to participants, and thus also better responses to conductors. An ablation study could be valuable to assess the effect of independent factors of VAI’s embodiment — avatar appearance and behavior, speech-to-speech input and output. However, this is prevented by the technical limitations (non-modularity) of the underlying Heygen integration. Therefore, VAI is evaluated as a potential gestalt, where the simultaneous presence of each factor at once could evoke a greater anthropomorphic perception. To obtain additional insights, we also direct attention at the differences between facilitated surveys and their impact on agent-participant interactions. We ask the following research questions:RQ1: Does facilitation of surveys with an embodied conversational agent (ECA) yield a different quality of responses to a text-based agent? The quality of responses is critical for obtaining reliable and valid findings from surveys, as well as other user research methods. Text-based survey interactions yield high-quality results across a multitude of perspectives, including informativeness, relevance, specificity, and clarity. By inducing even more natural conversations, we extrapolate that even better results might be obtained by incorporating an ECA. We expect that the incorporation of an ECA will improve the quality of responses.RQ2: Does facilitation of surveys with an ECA affect user engagement? Engagement in surveys can serve as a signal of participant understanding and of more thorough, natural responses (Hess & Stathopoulos, 2013). Nonetheless, there is merit to assessing it independently from response quality to capture potential dissociations. Text-based chatbot surveys raise engagement through custom interactions in comparison to traditional surveys (Xiao et al., 2020). Therefore, conversations with anthropomorphic agents can be expected to boost engagement even further through more natural interaction involving natural speech and a human face (Yee et al., 2007).RQ3: Does facilitation of surveys with an ECA affect user satisfaction? Satisfaction from a pleasant interaction can support the intrinsic motivation of participants taking part in a survey (Hossain, 2012). Therefore, we aim to evaluate scales concerning participants’ attitudes toward the interaction with virtual agents. We also analyze qualitative feedback to identify factors that influence user satisfaction.RQ4: Do the latent characteristics of different surveys lead to differences in the interaction with—and results obtained through—an ECA? Due to the heterogeneity of user surveys, with different questionnaire logic and structure applied for varied purposes, behavioral patterns linked to interactions with virtual agents may vary. The resulting usefulness of virtual agents (e.g., for eliciting higher engagement or better results) may depend on the idiosyncrasies of questionnaires. Cognitively demanding questions might make it more difficult for participants to think through and express complex answers. Therefore, to obtain theoretical findings with higher generalizability and explanatory ability, we aim to compare the effects of ECAs on different questionnaires.

Engagement in surveys can serve as a signal of participant understanding and of more thorough, natural responses (Hess & Stathopoulos, 2013). Nonetheless, there is merit to assessing it independently from response quality to capture potential dissociations. Text-based chatbot surveys raise engagement through custom interactions in comparison to traditional surveys (Xiao et al., 2020). Therefore, conversations with anthropomorphic agents can be expected to boost engagement even further through more natural interaction involving natural speech and a human face (Yee et al., 2007).

RQ4: Do the latent characteristics of different surveys lead to differences in the interaction with—and results obtained through—an ECA? Due to the heterogeneity of user surveys, with different questionnaire logic and structure applied for varied purposes, behavioral patterns linked to interactions with virtual agents may vary. The resulting usefulness of virtual agents (e.g., for eliciting higher engagement or better results) may depend on the idiosyncrasies of questionnaires. Cognitively demanding questions might make it more difficult for participants to think through and express complex answers. Therefore, to obtain theoretical findings with higher generalizability and explanatory ability, we aim to compare the effects of ECAs on different questionnaires.

Participants

Eighty participants were recruited for a between-subject experiment aimed at mitigating carryover bias that could otherwise affect the observations in within-subject design (Zhu & Broadbent, 2025). The sample size was determined using an a priori power analysis in G*Power5. The analysis was based on a two-tailed Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, with 80% power, an alpha level of .05, and a Cohen’s d of 0.65 (approximately equivalent to a medium effect size of r = 0.3), indicating that a total of 80 participants would be sufficient. The experimental conditions were defined by two surveys (see Section “Materials”) completed with one of the two conversational agent variants (text-based and embodied). To address the risks to internal validity inherited from between-subject design, participants were sampled from the general population of internet users through a random stratified process aimed at obtaining equal gender distributions and age-wise representation of the population according to statistical data6.

Participants were recruited in the UK through the online panel service of the UXtweak Research tool7. Incentives were awarded based on the standard market rate of the panel service at the time of recruiting, the estimated length of the study (approximately 30 minutes), and its complexity. Participants were using desktop devices during the experiment (verified based on screen size). Materials

Even small differences between surveys—such as the subject of the questions, the complexity of understanding them, and the difficulty of answering them—may influence the manner in which users interact with the embodied conversational agent. Therefore, we implemented two different surveys in VAI to enhance the external validity of its evaluation. By choosing standardized and well-recognized psychometric questionnaires that are self-contained and have potential relevance for UX research, with conversations that might expand the understanding of participants, we aim to support the relevance as well as the reproducibility of our experiment. The following questionnaires were adopted into surveys:Short Big Five Inventory 2 (BFI-2-S). The BFI-2-S is a short version of the personality assessment questionnaire Big Five Inventory (Soto & John, 2017), selected as a general questionnaire that most participants would be able to respond to without issue. It consists of 30 items for gauging personality traits on a five-point scale that ranges from Strongly agree to Strongly disagree. In spite of its short format that reduces completion time, it also preserves much of the reliability of the full-length questionnaire. Each item corresponds to one of five personality trait scales: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, negative emotionality, and open-mindedness. The scales can be further divided into three facets.Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ). The CFQ measures the likelihood of a person making cognitive mistakes and slips during everyday activities (Rast, Zimprich, Boxtel, & Jolles, 2009). The reason for its selection is the higher sensitivity of its contents in comparison to BFI-2-S. Its inclusion aims to assess whether ECAs could build participant trust and aid in sharing such information. CFQ comprises 25 items (cognitive failures). Participants rate the frequency at which they undergo cognitive failures on a scale from Very often to Never. Higher scores suggest a greater tendency toward cognitive lapses. Three subdimensions of failures are present: forgetfulness, distractibility, and false triggering (disruption when carrying out a series of mental or physical tasks).The frameworks of the two questionnaires integrated the conversational agents to ask a series of open-ended follow-up questions. Questionnaire rating scales maintained their original text-mode form. Since elaborating the answers to every question would be exhaustive for the participants, we consolidated the added interactivity with preservation of survey conciseness. The follow-up was restricted to selected items in both questionnaires (ten from BFI-2-S and nine from CFQ). The rest of the questions remained without follow-up—a parallel to moderated interviews where selecting the topic for follow-up to focus on is commonly the prerogative of the moderator. The selection criteria for questions to receive a follow-up prioritized the representation of diverse scales and facets (personality traits/cognitive failures), questions with potential for meaningful in-depth conversations, and even temporal distribution throughout the questionnaire. Additionally, the participants were asked whether they preferred the conversational agent that they interacted with or the method of interacting with the alternative (text-based/embodied) agent, which was described to them. At the end, an optional open-ended question was used to give participants the opportunity to provide auxiliary feedback.

Attention check questions were included to monitor whether participants were putting genuine effort into reading and following instructions (Krajcovic, Demcak, & Kuric, 2025; Thomas & Clifford, 2017). Attention checks comprised multiple-choice items in which participants were explicitly asked to choose specific, logically contradictory options.

Procedure

To simulate realistic online conditions for which the VAI tool was designed, the participants completed the survey remotely. The variability of conditions in the wild can be substantial due to an interplay of environmental and technological factors. Therefore, to alleviate concerns about ecological validity, the experiment was conducted in uncontrolled conditions that mirror the intended use of online surveys assisted by a conversational agent.

Participants, divided into four groups, each comprising 20 individuals (see Section “Participants”), were automatically assigned into corresponding experimental variants: E-BFI (embodied agent, BFI-2-S questionnaire), T-BFI (text-based agent, BFI-2-S questionnaire), E-CFQ (embodied agent, CFQ questionnaire), and T-CFQ (text-based agent, CFQ questionnaire). From the perspective of participants, the following procedure aligned with the structure of surveys in VAI as expounded in Appendix A.1 and illustrated in Fig. 7.

After participants confirmed their consent, an introductory message explained the survey’s core principles, including the concept of the conversational agent as an "interactive AI assistant." Participants assigned to the embodied agent variants completed a microphone check and selected their preferred avatar (see Fig. 8). In the initial questionnaire, they provided their descriptive information. Attention checks were incorporated throughout the experiment: in the initial questionnaire, in the middle of the main questionnaires (BFI-2-S or CFQ), and in the final questionnaire.

Survey instructions guided the participants on how to complete the Likert scales depending on their assigned questionnaire variant (BFI-2-S or CFQ). Upon completing the main agent-assisted questionnaire, participants received a result debriefing. Personality trait and cognitive failure scores were calculated automatically and accompanied by basic definitions with examples. This allowed participants to either interpret their results by reading the static explanations or through interactive discussion with the conversational agent. The optional interaction with the conversational agent in the debriefing followed the same design conventions as previous conversations. The debriefing GUI was identical between conditions, with information focused exclusively on self-reflective analysis of self-reported information to mitigate the possibility of priming the results. Summative feedback was collected through satisfaction-oriented items in the final questionnaire, after which the participants were free to close the page.

Data preprocessing and analysis

The collected data were preprocessed to ensure their accurate analysis. The follow-up conversations contain 2265 responses, after deducting 12 missing responses from the embodied agents and three from the text-based agents. The missing 0.7% of responses were attributed to connection interruptions encountered as the study was conducted remotely, and to the instability of the virtual avatar (Heygen) API. The majority of data remained unaffected and could be analyzed as intended.

The responses in the text-based variant contained typos, while the transcripts from the embodied variant contained spelling errors and incorrectly joined words. Measures

The investigated dimensions of survey results and user experience are reflected in the analyzed measures. To maintain continuity with prior work investigating text-based conversational agents in surveys by Xiao et al. (2020), we adopted theoretically consistent measures, further extending and adapting their set to enable a thorough, construct-valid comparison between agents.

Response quality. To tackle the challenge of assessing the quality of open-ended survey answers, we implement measures developed by Xiao et al. (2020) based on Grice’s maxims, four principles of effective communication: informativeness, specificity, relevance, and clarity. Lexical diversity and readability (Höhne et al., 2024) were also adopted as metrics for comparison of text and voice mode in surveys. Calculated from the text of the participant’s responses in conversation with the agent, these measures include:Informativeness. Determined as a sum of the surprisal of words, which is the inverted frequency of a word in the English language. To determine frequency, we used the library wordfreq (Speer, 2022), which collates text from various sources, including books, Wikipedia articles, news, social media posts, and comments.Specificity. Defines the level of specific detail in the response that can help researchers obtain in-depth qualitative insights. Coded as integers on a range from 0 (general responses), through 1 (mentions of specific concepts), to 2 (concepts are further elaborated on through examples or other justifications).Relevance. Conceptual match between the questions and the response, which can be warped due to a variety of factors, such as misunderstanding or self-presentation biases, and coded as integers on a range from 0 (off-topic and nonsensical responses), through 1 (responses with indirect implications, but without providing a straight answer), to 2 (completely valid answers).Clarity. The transparency of the meaning of the response allows for it to be interpreted and understood without ambiguity and coded as integers on a range from 0 (illegible), through 1 (incomplete or partially legible, such as without proper sentences), to 2 (clear and well-articulated).Lexical diversity. Measured as Yule’s K, which analyzes the frequencies of unique words to reflect their repetition. Robust to text length in comparison to other lexical diversity measures (Tweedie & Baayen, 1998). Values range from 0 to ∞ ∞, where lower values indicate higher richness.Readability. Measured as the Flesch Reading Ease Score. It incorporates the average number of words per sentence and the average number of syllables per word as factors that decrease the score.Engagement. The depth and form of the interaction can be explored through a number of proxies of user engagement with the survey:Time taken. In the context of measuring engagement, time is a fundamental metric indicating the extent of effort spent. However, specific time-based metrics must be selected with nuance, given the fact that the differences in modalities have secondary temporal effects that are independent from the user’s deliberate actions. For example, the time to complete the survey can be inflated by longer response times of the embodied agent. We thus focus on select temporal measures:Responding time. How much time was spent typing/speaking an answer.Transition time. The length of the transition space between the end of the agent’s question output and the start of the participant’s response. To define the end of the agent’s question, we consider the moment when the embodied agent stops speaking or when the text-based agent concludes its typing animation.Response length. A dedication to provide longer responses is tied to higher engagement.Word count, as a standard representative of the number of units that convey meaning.Character count, as a more granular complementary metric that could signal effects on word choice and complexity.Self-disclosure. The number of distinct topics, ideas, and concepts introduced in the response that relate to personal matters, such as the individual’s personal characteristics, hobbies, or previous experiences.Sentiment. Valence of the attitudes that participants expressed in their responses. Notably, this represents sentiment as part of the engagement with the questionnaire rather than attitudes explicitly toward the agent, which is covered by the satisfaction facet. Ordinal values are coded as integers on a range from -1 (negative), through 0 (neutral), to 1 (positive).Participant satisfaction was assessed traditionally, through explicit feedback in close-ended questions. For the capture of varied perspectives, the questions cover the following facets:Naturalness of interaction with the agent.User experience with the agent.User experience with the survey.Preference for agent variant.The coding, having involved simple heuristics and large volumes of captured conversations, was performed through a hybrid method of expert review augmented by an LLM (GPT-4.1). The development of the data labeling prompt was an iterative process, with its initial phase informed by qualitative review of responses. Over several iterations, GPT generated labeling for the sample; researchers collaboratively reviewed the results, and new prompts and strategies were designed to address issues. Explanations of labeling rules and values were made more explicit, a data description was added, temperature was lowered to 0.2 to reduce variability, and a randomized batch-processing strategy was implemented to mitigate the reference-group effect. Results

The study took the participants a similar amount of time to complete regardless of the experimental variant, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U(80) = 941,\ z = 1.36,\ p =.18$$\end{document}U(80)=941,z=1.36,p=.18 for the embodied agent M = 36.59 min, SD = 11.16, for the text-based agent M = 33.50 min, SD = 12.69. The 3-min (9.22%) time overhead between the means can be attributed to the configuration steps and slower response of the ECA. The summarized results of statistical tests and corresponding effect sizes and confidence intervals are presented in the Appendix C.

In the ECA conversations, Heygen determined the end of the participants’ responses correctly in most cases. Only two responses out of 380 were completed through the pop-up window after the conversation. A single participant mentioned this: “No, but I notice that sometimes Judy has cut me off when I haven’t finished speaking. Maybe my vocalizing (mm or hmm) is too quiet and she thinks I’ve finished?”. Thirty-one other meaningful responses (excluding “no”, “nothing”, etc.) occurred in the post-conversation popup window only because GPT-4o-mini sometimes ignored the instruction of the Wrapping module and asked another question when the participant could no longer reply using voice.

The most popular ECA models among those shown in Fig. 8 were June (14), Ann (9), and Silas (8). Preferences for models could be attributed to the influence of similarity-attractiveness and social similarity theories within the recruited sample (Qiu & Benbasat, 2010), or to other ambiguous factors and stereotypes that can influence the traits attributed to the models (Aumüller, Winklbauer, Schreibmaier, Batinic, & Mara, 2024). Only 22.5% of the participants (seven embodied, 11 text-based) interacted with the optional debriefing section of the survey for M = 1.03 min (SD = 1.26).

In comparison to methods aiming at full robotic administration of psychological questionnaires (Abbasi et al., 2024), our use of these questionnaires was only as context for embedding our main focus—open-ended follow-up questions facilitated by conversational agents. Nonetheless, in both the chatbot and ECA variants, the addition of AI follow-up questions between the items could potentially affect the psychometric validity. We performed Cronbach’s α α tests of reliability to assess the impact of our method on the questionnaires. The minimum sample size with six items per factor is 13 (Bonett, 2002); therefore, our sample (20 per variant) is sufficient for a preliminary evaluation.

Discussion

Implications

Based on our findings, AI-driven virtual agents with human likeness that participants in surveys can talk to present an effective design concept for improving the response quality and the intensity of participant engagement. This aligns with the previous studies where voice mode and higher behavioral realism of embodiment positively affected verbal behavior (Höhne et al., 2024; von der Pütten et al., 2010). By introducing ECAs that simulate social interaction more faithfully than a chatbot, researchers may seek to mitigate hindrances to the quality of responses in unmoderated and remote online research, such as satisficing, cognitive load, lacking motivation, or attention (Heerwegh & Loosveldt, 2008). For user research, this could present opportunities for obtaining detailed qualitative data at high volume, frequency, and efficiency.

The similar average number of self-disclosures between the chatbot and the ECA, despite the ECA yielding more interactions with a higher number of self-disclosures, could be plausibly attributed to two phenomena revealed by prior studies. The higher expressiveness in conversations treated as social behavior, involving perceived rapport, can increase the amount of shared topics (Höhne et al., 2024; Laban & Cross, 2024), which could affect some participants more than others. At the same time, the humanlike appearance could trigger social desirability bias (Lind et al., 2013; Pickard & Roster, 2020), similar to how fears and cognitive barriers can cause information to be withheld in human-to-human interactions (Laban, Wang, & Gunes, 2025b). Whether such desirability bias occurred would be speculation—desirability scores of BFI-2-S factors and CFQ (Kuric, Demcak, Smrecek, & Spilakova,2025c) were not different (p >.05p>.05). Nonetheless, in self-report methods of user research where social desirability bias is a salient concern yet realistic social interaction drives engagement and response quality (Höhne et al., 2024; von der Pütten et al., 2010), it might be appropriate to mitigate it through framing (Lucas et al., 2014) or alternatives (e.g., less anthropomorphic appearance).

The higher spontaneity of participants’ spoken responses in contrast to their written answers was reflected by occasional reduced clarity and indirect relevance, accompanied by consistently quick transition times. This could result in qualitative data that is more challenging to process and extract insights from. However, the natural quality of the spoken responses could be seen as preferable and more genuine.

To address the slightly lower relevance of responses to the embodied agent, the agent’s questions could also be displayed on the screen after they are verbalized. This would allow the participant to mentally revisit the question and course-correct at any time during their response.

With further technological advancements, ECA interviews could potentially be more time-efficient for participants than chatbot surveys. This is supported by spoken responses that were quicker than typing while maintaining higher or similar information value. As a consequence of the ECA’s longer response time, however, the ECA survey still took longer to complete as a whole.

By comparing the conditions of the BFI-2-S and CFQ questionnaires, it can be surmised that question characteristics such as topic, phrasing, personal relevance, and cognitive demand are moderator variables. Methodologically, the context and design of open-ended questions form the prerequisites for positive effects on participant responses in ECA follow-up. While follow-up questions in both variants were open-ended, those in BFI-2-S were broader, pertaining to personality traits that can impact the participant’s life in diverse ways (e.g., experiencing art, music, and literature), while CFQ questions were centered more narrowly around specific types of cognitive failures (e.g., distractions and coping with them). Additionally, cognitive failures as part of mental health can be viewed as more sensitive than questions about personality, which could have resulted in the slightly more cautious replies, supporting the findings by Zhu and Broadbent (2025) and Lind et al. (2013). Therefore, practitioners may expect the types of questions focused on broader and less sensitive topics to benefit from ECA conversational facilitation more noticeably.

Although satisfaction ratings were similar between embodiment and chatbot surveys, this is still a positive indicator of the higher human likeness of video-based Heygen avatars. For comparison, prior work by Zhu and Broadbent (2025) indicated 3D-modeled avatars (Soul Machines Digital People) as less anthropomorphic than a chatbot. Among facial animations that Conrad et al. (2015) determined as making participants uncomfortable in a 3D model embodiment—head, face, and eye movements, blinking, smiling, and lips synchronized with speech—only idle nodding and head movements were identified by some participants as unnatural in the AI-generated video, highlighting these as key areas for improvement.

We can draw conclusions about pragmatic and hedonic aspects of user experiences based on the thematic analysis of open-ended feedback. Across both variants, criticism linked to conversation contents indicates the critical relevance of verbal communication semantics and pragmatics. It can be argued that without strengthening the language modules as a pillar of the conversational agent, the potential of added modalities to create a sense of natural conversation can be limited. As an added benefit, more relevant questions could contribute to more relevant responses.

The positive response of the participants who interacted with the ECA is notable. Given that satisfaction was consistent between groups, and the chatbot group overwhelmingly preferred the chatbot, this increase can be attributed to a shift in perception triggered by a positive experience. The chatbot uses text mode, so preferences could be skewed in its favor due to less imposed pressure and higher convenience (Schober et al., 2015). The positive reception of the ECA is strengthened by participants attributing human traits to their description of the agent, such as kind, friendly, and sympathetic, or calling the interaction with it pleasant, smooth, and coherent. Although other factors (e.g., worldviews, personality, task alignment, taste) make it unlikely for preferences to shift toward ECAs completely, the identified issues with the embodiment (response delays, glitches, or repetitive movements evoking the Uncanny Valley effect) can be critical for its user experience (Doherty & Sorenson, 2015). Free-text feedback of the ECA group participants who preferred the chatbot referred to these issues alongside personal factors (e.g., stress, time to think, affinity for writing) and factors that ECAs and chatbots have in common (e.g., pragmatic and semantic quality of responses). Addressing them should be a priority to mitigate the biases they might introduce. Measures of satisfaction and self-disclosures might increase for ECAs freed from similar distractions.

Limitations and threats to validity

Our application of a between-subject design could present internal validity risks, which were addressed through random stratified sampling. Nevertheless, such prevention of carryover bias could be supplanted by other biases (e.g., positivity bias) tied to participants’ lack of interaction with both conversational agent variants. Namely, the self-reported ratings of naturalness and user experience are obtained as independent assessments rather than as direct comparisons.

The generalizability of this study is limited by the context of two psychometric questionnaires (BFI-2-S and CFQ) and questions within them selected for conversational agent follow-up. Different types of questions and surveys (e.g., customer experience, competitor analysis surveys) may evoke different attitudes and behaviors when facilitated by conversational agents. For example, AI-driven ECA follow-up in usability evaluation questionnaires such as SUS or UMUX-LITE could promote a higher number of shared topics if they do not demand self-disclosure. The sample may introduce demographic biases, with findings linked to the general population in the UK. High ecological validity was achieved through an experiment conducted in naturalistic conditions typical for online surveys.

Recruiting participants from an online panel and offering financial incentives could have introduced selection bias. Participants were highly motivated, with 0% attrition. In surveys without the external motivation of financial compensation, internal motivation supported by social interaction could play a more critical role in shaping the effect on response quality and engagement. This includes measures that signaled no significant differences during our experiment due to achieving consistently high values, which include specificity, relevance, clarity, and completion rate. No exclusions were performed with the data that was collected.

The avatar’s appearance and voice may moderate ECA interview results. We accounted for the subjective relatability of different embodiments (Zhao et al., 2024) by keeping the choice of the preferred avatar (see Fig. 8) at the participant’s discretion. As a tradeoff, uneven distribution of avatar appearances could contribute to bias in our findings. Given the use of identical AI models and configurations, we did not identify systematic differences in appearances and voices aside from aesthetics and characteristics attributed internally by participants. Nonetheless, our findings might be representatively biased toward the popular avatars—June, Ann, and Silas. Limited to six personas based on gender and ethnicity, our avatars also may not have been diverse enough to provide optimally relatable options to all participants.

Psychological questionnaires BFI-2-S and CFQ provided the context in which VAI follow-up conversations were evaluated. While our method was not used to administer the questionnaire items directly, it is possible that psychometric validity was altered somewhat by open-ended intermissions with the agents. Preliminary Cronbach’s α α tests indicated high reliability for the embodied variant in BFI-2-S and for both variants in CFQ, yet mixed for the text-based variant in BFI-2-S. In case these questionnaires were used with VAI follow-ups for psychometric purposes, more robust psychometric validation should be performed first (N > 1000N>1000 to be comparable to Rammstedt et al., 2018; Rast et al., 2009; Soto & John, 2017) to analyze reliability, as well as construct validity through measurement invariance (Abbasi et al., 2024).

Although the a priori power analysis was based on detecting an effect of r = .30 with 80% power, significant results for smaller effects (r = .25) were obtained in some cases. Post hoc analysis shows that these results hold lower statistical power than 80%, so the observed effect, while statistically detectable in our sample, should be interpreted with caution. Replication with a larger sample size would help confirm the stability of this finding.

Future work

The future evolutions of embodied agents in surveys will be contingent on strategies that address delays between conversation turns. During human dialogue, turn-taking offsets are below 0.62s in 75% of turns and below 1.69s in 95% (Lunsford, Heeman, & Rennie, 2016). For agents incapable of properly responding within these thresholds and holding an uninterrupted real-time conversation, alternative approaches can maintain engagement by obscuring pauses behind conversational conventions (Elfleet & Chollet, 2024). As a parallel to humans conversing under cognitive load, natural fillers could be woven into conversations, or turn-taking could be accompanied by gestures such as nodding or touching the hair or the chin (Mukawa, Sasaki, & Kimura, 2014). These could present more seamless alternatives to the loops of idle head movements and periodic nodding in Heygen, which some participants viewed as robotic. Other behavioral cues, such as postural mimicry, could also contribute to higher rapport (Gratch et al., 2007).

By validating the core principle of AI- and ECA-enhanced surveys, this study lays the groundwork for future research that could expand the concept’s methodological boundaries. Proceeding methods should aim to obtain better data, improve survey experience, and mitigate bias. Approaches could be general or customized to specific survey content, such as by managing and directing the conversation. Ablation studies focused on the face, speech input, and output could provide further insights, since the primary driver of the differences could be the transition from text to speech mode. The attributes of virtual agent interviewer’s voice (e.g., dialect, accent, prosody), appearance (e.g., facial features, clothing), and perceived social identity (e.g., race, education) should be further studied for their effects (Conrad et al., 2015). Empirical findings could inform the design of methods for tuning virtual avatar and speech modalities for specific purposes. Research adopting ECAs for various research methods could contribute to the broader vision of developing ECAs for user research, capable of simulating complex moderator conduct, such as monitoring user behavior, speech, and expressions, allowing for relevant and timely interceptions.

Certain individuals, including introverts and anxious or lonely people, are more inclined to be more open to embodied agents and enjoy their companionship (Laban & Cross, 2024). This can make them harmful if misapplied as a substitute for genuine human contact, reciprocity, and the ability to understand the nuanced needs of these people — especially in the context of therapeutic or long-term use. However, in user research that is typically limited to short interactions, approachable virtual facilitators could provide equitable conditions for socially withdrawn individuals and help them overcome personal barriers and share their perspectives. The individual differences in our results could be moderated by such individual factors. This relationship is well-suited for further exploration.

The methodological limitations of the present study could be addressed by studies with complementary strengths. Alternative experiment designs (e.g., within-subject) and studies could contribute to the establishment of robust theory by focusing on different questionnaires, items (assessing effects of cognitive complexities, topics, sensitivity, open-endedness, communication style, etc.), methodological context, populations, and measures. For example, in situations involving sensitive questions, socially desirable responses may be amplified by human likeness (Zhu & Broadbent, 2025). However, convincing faking could also become more challenging for less sensitive topics due to higher spontaneity, supporting speech-based truthfulness validation for self-reported data (Kuric, Demcak, Smrecek, & Benus, 2025b).

Conclusion

Unmoderated user research makes it easy to collect large amounts of data for understanding users. Yet its lack of genuine social interaction and co-presence can compromise the quality of its data due to factors like satisficing and perceived lack of immediate accountability. By comparing our novel instrument to the current standard for chatbot-driven surveys, we demonstrate the potential effectiveness of embodied conversational agents (ECAs) for enhancing participant engagement and response quality. By talking to an avatar with a human appearance instead of typing, participants are encouraged to share more in-depth information in a time-efficient manner. Perceptions of agents are divided between agents as personal, intuitive and authentic, and those eliciting Uncanny Valley reactions. For more natural interactions, improvements to ECAs (management of turns, facial animations, and gestures, conversation logic) should continue to be explored. While further development and research is required, we conclude that ECAs can represent a positive step for closing the gap between unmoderated and moderated user research aimed at facilitating qualitative insights.
