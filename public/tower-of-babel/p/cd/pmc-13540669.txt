Molecular effects of areca nut–derived compounds on human oral cancer cells: a systematic review of in vitro evidence
Authors: Visilmi Kaffah Putri Ayna, Irene Anastasia Tampubolon, Kharinna Widowati, Andari Sarasati, Nurina Febriyanti Ayuningtyas, Meircurius Dwi Condro Surboyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAreca nut chewing is a major risk factor for oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMD) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), yet the molecular mechanisms underlying its carcinogenic effects remain incompletely integrated. This systematic review aimed to synthesize in vitro evidence on the molecular effects of areca nut extract and areca nut–derived alkaloids in human oral cancer cells.MethodsThis systematic review followed PRISMA 2020. PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Scopus were searched from inception to the final search date. Eligible studies used human oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines or primary oral cancer cells exposed to areca nut extract or related compounds. Outcomes involving inflammation, proliferation, oxidative stress, oncogenic signaling, and tumor-suppressor regulation were qualitatively synthesized because of methodological heterogeneity.ResultsTwenty studies met the inclusion criteria. Areca nut–derived compounds were associated with increased pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, COX-2, and PGE2. They also altered cell proliferation and cell-cycle control, with increased proliferative markers and suppression of key checkpoint regulators. Oxidative stress was characterized by elevated intracellular and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, oxidative DNA damage, lipid peroxidation, and impaired antioxidant defenses. Oncogenic and stress-responsive pathways, including NOTCH1, Akt, and Nrf2/HO-1, were activated, whereas p53, p27, SIRT1, and AP-1 transcription factors were downregulated. Findings for p21 were contradictory across studies.ConclusionsIn vitro evidence indicates that areca nut constituents induce coordinated inflammatory, proliferative, oxidative, and signaling alterations that support their carcinogenic role in human oral cancer cells.

Introduction

Oral cancer remains a major global health burden, with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounting for the majority of cases.1 Among established risk factors, areca nut chewing is one of the most prevalent, particularly in South and Southeast Asia.2,3 The World Health Organization classifies areca nut as a Group I carcinogen, and epidemiologic studies consistently associate its use with an increased incidence of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) and OSCC.4 Building on these epidemiological observations, early mechanistic studies have demonstrated that areca nut constituents induce DNA damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and inflammatory responses in oral epithelial cells, thereby providing foundational evidence for its carcinogenic potential.3,5,6 Despite these strong associations, the molecular mechanisms through which areca nut constituents promote malignant transformation remain insufficiently consolidated and are often described in isolated experimental contexts.

Areca nut use is deeply embedded in cultural, social, and ritual practices across South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Pacific Islands, and parts of East Africa.7,8 More than 600 million people worldwide regularly chew areca nut, making it the fourth most consumed psychoactive substance after caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine.9 Prevalence is particularly high in India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, Papua New Guinea, and Pacific Island nations, where chewing is often initiated during adolescence and sustained into adulthood.10, 11, 12 The widespread availability of commercial preparations such as betel quid, gutka, and paan masala has further increased consumption among adolescents and urban populations.13,14 Given this scale of exposure, clarifying the biological mechanisms underlying its carcinogenic effects is of significant global importance.

Areca nut contains several biologically active constituents, including alkaloids such as arecoline, arecaidine, and guvacoline, as well as tannins, polyphenols, and nitrosamine precursors.15 These compounds have been shown to exert cytotoxic, genotoxic, pro-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects. However, the extent to which they collectively drive carcinogenic signaling pathways in human oral cancer cells has not been comprehensively synthesized.16 In particular, interactions among inflammation, oxidative stress, oncogene activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and loss of tumor-suppressor regulation remain poorly integrated across studies.

In vitro models provide a controlled experimental platform for dissecting these molecular events. Human oral cancer cell lines and primary tumor–derived cultures enable detailed precise evaluation of how areca nut–derived compounds influence cellular signaling, gene expression, oxidative injury, and proliferative behavior. Such systems enable detailed assessment of cytokine production, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, DNA damage responses, mitochondrial regulation, and transcriptional changes—mechanistic insights that cannot be fully captured through epidemiologic or in vivo studies.

Although numerous in vitro investigations have examined these pathways independently, their findings have not been systematically integrated. Individual studies have reported increased cytokine expression,17 heightened ROS production,18 activation of oncogenic pathways such as NOTCH1 and Akt, and suppression of tumor-suppressor regulators including p53, p21, p27, and SIRT1.19 However, the consistency and interplay of these pathways across human oral cancer models remain unclear, limiting their interpretation within a unified carcinogenic framework.

Although previous narrative and systematic reviews have examined the association between areca nut exposure and oral carcinogenesis, most have focused on epidemiological risk, clinical outcomes, or histopathological progression, with limited emphasis on mechanistic integration at the molecular level. Existing reviews that discuss molecular effects often present findings descriptively, without systematically synthesizing how key pathways—such as inflammation, oxidative stress, oncogenic signaling, and tumor-suppressor regulation—interact within human oral cancer cell models. In addition, few studies have specifically focused in vitro human oral cancer systems, which provide critical mechanistic insight into direct cellular response.

Recent advances in in vitro research have expanded understanding of signaling pathways, redox imbalance, and transcriptional regulation induced by areca nut–derived compounds. However, these findings remain dispersed, highlighting the need for a structured and timely synthesis to clarify their convergence toward pro-tumorigenic processes. Such mechanistic integration also has translational relevance, as understanding how areca nut constituents modulate interconnected signaling networks may inform biomarker development, risk assessment, and potential therapeutic strategies in oral cancer.

The aim of this systematic review was to synthesize in vitro evidence on the molecular effects of areca nut extract and areca nut–derived alkaloids on human oral cancer cells. Specifically, this review evaluated alterations in inflammatory signaling, cell proliferation and cell-cycle regulation, oxidative stress and mitochondrial function, oncogenic pathway activation, and tumor-suppressor gene expression to provide an integrated mechanistic framework linking areca nut exposure to processes relevant to oral cancer progression.

Materials and methods

Study designs and research question

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The objective of this review was to synthesize in vitro evidence on the molecular effects of areca nut–derived compounds on human oral cancer cells, with a particular focus on signaling pathways and molecular markers relevant to oral carcinogenesis.

The review was deliberately restricted to human OSCC cell lines and primary oral cancer cells. This approach was adopted to ensure that the synthesized evidence reflects tumor-specific biological responses, including inflammatory amplification, oxidative stress adaptation, proliferative signaling, and survival mechanisms. While studies employing normal oral keratinocytes or other non-malignant oral cells provide important insights into early cytotoxic and genotoxic events, such models primarily reflect initiation-related phenomena and do not fully capture molecular processes associated with tumor promotion and progression. Accordingly, cancer cell models were considered more appropriate for addressing the mechanistic aims of the present review.

The research question was structured using the Population–Intervention–Outcome (PIO) framework as follows:1.Population (P): Human OSCC cell lines or primary oral cancer cells2.Intervention (I): Areca nut extract, arecoline, or arecoline-related alkaloids (e.g., arecaidine, arecoline N-oxide)3.Outcomes (O): Molecular markers related to inflammation, cell proliferation, oxidative stress, oncogene activation, and tumor-suppressor gene expression

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive electronic literature search was conducted in PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Scopus from database inception to the final search date. The search strategy was designed to identify in vitro studies evaluating the molecular effects of areca nut–derived compounds on human oral cancer cells. Controlled vocabulary terms, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) where applicable, were combined with free-text keywords related to areca nut exposure, oral carcinogenesis, and molecular outcomes.

Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to enhance search sensitivity and ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant studies. No language restrictions were applied during the initial search. The core search terms included combinations of the following keywords: (“oral cancer” OR “oral squamous cell carcinoma” OR “oral carcinogenesis”) AND (“areca nut” OR “betel nut” OR “arecoline” OR “arecaidine” OR “arecoline N-oxide”).

The search strategy was adapted as necessary for each database to account for differences in indexing and search functionality. Reference lists of all included articles and relevant review papers were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies that may not have been captured through the electronic database search. The full search strategies for each database, including exact search strings, Boolean operators, and field tags, as well as the final search dates, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection and data extraction

All records identified through the electronic database search were imported into reference management software (Mendeley and EndNote) for organization and duplicate removal. No dedicated automation or screening platforms were used, and all screening and data extraction were performed manually by the reviewers. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were subsequently retrieved and assessed for inclusion. Any discrepancies between reviewers were initially resolved through discussion. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

Data from included studies were extracted using standardized data extraction tables. Extracted information included cell type and origin, experimental intervention and compound source, exposure concentration and duration, assay methods, evaluated molecular markers, main findings, and reported statistical significance.

Data synthesis

Due to substantial heterogeneity in experimental design, a formal meta-analysis was not feasible. This heterogeneity included differences in outcome metrics (e.g., gene and protein expression levels, cytokine assays, and reactive oxygen species measurements), variations in exposure conditions (compound type, concentration, and duration), diversity in assay methodologies, and inconsistent reporting formats across studies. Although the feasibility of quantitative pooling was considered, consistent summary statistics (such as means and standard deviations) and sufficiently comparable outcome measures were not consistently available, precluding valid meta-analysis even within potential subgroups.

Heterogeneity was assessed descriptively based on differences in cell types, compound characteristics (e.g., extract vs. purified alkaloids), exposure concentrations and durations, assay methods, and outcome definitions across studies.

Statistical significance was assessed based on reported p-values comparing areca nut–treated cells with untreated control groups, as provided in the original studies. In the absence of consistently reported effect sizes, confidence intervals, or variance estimates, interpretation focused on the direction of change (e.g., upregulation, downregulation, or no significant change) in conjunction with reported statistical significance. Studies that did not report p-values or formal statistical testing were included in the qualitative synthesis but interpreted with caution and not considered definitive without supporting statistical evidence. No independent recalculation of statistical measures was performed.

Molecular outcomes were summarized according to predefined categories, including inflammatory signaling, cell proliferation, oxidative stress, oncogene activation, and tumor-suppressor gene expression. Directionality of effects was determined based on reported changes relative to controls, and consistency was evaluated by identifying recurrent patterns across multiple independent studies. Statistically significant findings were considered definitive, whereas directionally consistent but non-significant or untested findings were interpreted cautiously.

No formal weighting of studies based on sample size, replication, or statistical robustness was applied, and all studies were considered equally within the qualitative synthesis.

Risk bias assessments

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using a structured appraisal informed by ToxRTool. Because ToxRTool was originally developed to assess the reliability of toxicological studies and has not been formally validated as a risk-of-bias instrument for molecular and signaling studies, its criteria were used as a methodological framework rather than applied as an unmodified validated tool.

The appraisal was based on eight prespecified domains: (1) identification and characterization of the test substance; (2) description of the cell model; (3) reporting of experimental conditions; (4) clarity of the exposure design; (5) appropriateness of control groups; (6) adequacy of replication; (7) validity and appropriateness of the reported outcome-measurement method; and (8) completeness of outcome reporting. For molecular and signaling outcomes, the outcome-validity domain considered whether the reported method was appropriate for the measured endpoint and was described sufficiently to allow interpretation. No additional molecular-endpoint-specific scoring system was developed or applied.

Each domain was categorized as low concern, unclear concern, or moderate concern based on the information reported in the original article. The appraisal was performed independently by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Given that this was an adapted, nonvalidated appraisal, the results were used to support cautious interpretation of study quality rather than to provide a definitive risk-of-bias classification.

To ensure consistency, the assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Results

Search results

The electronic database search identified a total of 1470 records. After removal of 863 duplicate articles, 607 unique records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 109 articles were selected for full-text assessment. Of these, 42 articles were retrieved and evaluated in full. Interventions consisted of areca nut extract, purified areca nut–derived compounds, or arecoline-related alkaloids. Arecoline was the most frequently investigated isolated compound, followed by areca nut extracts and related alkaloids such as arecaidine, guvacine, guvacoline, and arecoline N-oxide. Several studies evaluated whole areca nut extracts or specific areca nut fractions, whereas others focused on purified compounds to delineate compound-specific molecular effects.

Exposure concentrations varied widely across studies, ranging from submicromolar levels to high micromolar or microgram-per-milliliter concentrations, reflecting differences in experimental objectives and exposure models. Treatment durations and dosing strategies were heterogeneous, precluding direct quantitative comparison across studies. All included studies reported molecular outcomes relevant to oral carcinogenesis, including inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress responses, cell proliferation, apoptosis-related pathways, oncogene activation, and tumor-suppressor gene modulation.

Overall, the included studies encompassed both immortalized cancer cell lines and primary patient-derived oral cancer cells, with intervention doses spanning from biologically relevant micromolar concentrations to supraphysiological exposure levels. This variability should be considered when interpreting the consistency and translational relevance of the reported molecular effects. Several molecular findings were derived from experiments using high or potentially supraphysiological exposure concentrations. For example, increased PGE2, COX-2, and IL-6 expression was reported following exposure to 200–1200 μM arecoline, while decreased p21 and p27 expression was observed at 500 μM arecoline. Increased SAA1, IL6, and IL36G expression was reported at 80–160 μg/mL arecoline. Alterations in Nrf2/HO-1, intracellular ROS, p21, and p53 were reported across an exposure range of 12.5–400 μg/mL arecoline, whereas changes in BrdU incorporation, PCNA, γH2AX, and ATM were observed at 50–200 μg/mL arecoline. Findings obtained using 400 μg/mL arecoline N-oxide, 100–800 μg/mL crude areca nut extract, or 0.1–1.0% crude extract should likewise be interpreted cautiously. Because the included studies used different purified compounds, crude extracts, units, and preparation methods, their exposure concentrations could not be converted into a single standardized physiological threshold.

Effects of arecoline on inflammation and cell proliferation

Arecoline exposure was associated with modulation inflammatory and proliferative responses in human oral cancer cells (Table 2). With respect to cell viability and proliferation, several studies demonstrated a significant reduction in cell viability following arecoline treatment, as assessed by MTT assays. In contrast, other studies reported increased proliferative activity, enhanced DNA synthesis, and progression through the cell cycle, including accumulation in the G2/M phase. Increased expression of proliferation-associated markers such as BrdU incorporation, PCNA, and c-Myc was also observed. At the inflammatory level, arecoline exposure was frequently associated with upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators. Increased expression of cytokines and inflammatory markers, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17A, COX-2, PGE2, RANTES, SAA1, and IL36G, was reported across multiple studies using RT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, and biochemical assays. While most studies reported statistically significant increases relative to untreated controls, several investigations described elevated inflammatory marker expression without reporting p-values.

Arecoline also modulated regulators of cell survival, apoptosis, and cell-cycle control. Increased Bax expression and decreased BCL2 expression were reported, alongside downregulation of cell-cycle–related genes and tumor-associated markers, including cyclin D1, cyclin E1, CDK4, Rb, E2F, and p53. Conversely, increased expression of the cell-cycle inhibitors p16 and p21 was observed. Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential and activation of oxidative stress–responsive pathways, including increased Nrf2 expression, were also described, although statistical significance was not consistently reported across studies. These apparently divergent findings likely reflect dose- and context-dependent effects, where lower concentrations may promote proliferative signaling, whereas higher concentrations induce cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic responses.

Overall, despite heterogeneity in experimental design, exposure concentrations, and statistical reporting, the included studies indicate that arecoline exposure is associated with both reduced cell viability and enhanced inflammatory and proliferative signaling in human oral cancer cells. Studies lacking p-value reporting were included in the qualitative synthesis but were interpreted with caution.

Effects of areca nut–derived compounds on oxidative stress and redox imbalance

Exposure to arecoline and areca nut–derived compounds consistently induced oxidative stress in human oral cancer cells (Table 3). Increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was consistently observed using a variety of detection methods, including fluorescence-based assays, flow cytometry, and mitochondrial-specific probes. Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes in vitro evidence demonstrating a consistent and biologically coherent pattern of molecular effects induced by areca nut extract and its alkaloid components in human oral cancer models. Across diverse experimental systems, exposure to areca nut–derived compounds was associated with coordinated alterations in inflammatory signaling, cell proliferation, oxidative stress responses, oncogenic pathway activation, and tumor-suppressor regulation. These convergent molecular responses align with epidemiologic and clinical observations linking areca nut chewing to a high prevalence of OPMDs and OSCC, and provide mechanistic support for the role of areca nut as a molecular promoter of oral carcinogenesis.40

A central finding of this review is the robust pro-inflammatory response elicited by areca nut and arecoline exposure. The consistent upregulation of key inflammatory mediator indicates activation of a sustained inflammatory signaling network known to promote DNA damage, epithelial proliferation, and immune dysregulation. These cytokines and mediators also play established roles in oral submucous fibrosis, reinforcing the recognized biological continuum between chronic areca nut use, fibrosis, and malignant transformation.40 In line with this, one included study reported a shift toward a pro-fibrotic phenotype characterized by enhanced TGF-β signaling, fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition marked by α-SMA and γ-SMA expression, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition.41 Together, these findings support current mechanistic models of oral carcinogenesis that involve chronic inflammation, fibrotic remodeling, epithelial atrophy, and eventual neoplastic transformation.

In parallel with inflammatory activation, areca nut constituents were shown to modulate cell proliferation and cell-cycle control. This finding indicates, enhanced proliferative signaling occurring alongside suppression of critical cell-cycle checkpoint regulators, including p53 and p27, with variable regulation of p21 across studies. The included studies reported directly contradictory effects on p21 expression, with one study demonstrating increased p21 expression and two studies demonstrating decreased expression. Therefore, the current evidence does not support a consistent direction of p21 regulation following exposure to areca nut–derived compounds. This convergence suggests that areca nut–derived compounds promote cell-cycle progression under conditions that favor replication stress and genomic instability, thereby facilitating aberrant proliferation even in the presence of DNA damage.42

At the level of gene regulation and signaling networks, exposure to areca nut–derived compounds activated multiple oncogenic pathways implicated in OSCC development and progression. These changes reflect engagement of pathways that support tumor growth, survival, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis, alongside broader transcriptional reprogramming linked to stress adaptation. In contrast, transcriptional suppression of tumor-suppressor networks further shifted the intracellular balance toward malignant progression. Notably, the concurrent increase in vimentin and E-cadherin does not conform to the classical epithelial–mesenchymal transition pattern. Although a partial or context-dependent EMT response is one possible interpretation, methodological variability, assay-related limitations, or reporting inaccuracies in the original study cannot be excluded. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires confirmation using additional EMT markers and complementary methods. In contrast, transcriptional suppression of tumor-suppressor networks further shifted the intracellular balance toward malignant progression. The observed activation of ATM likely reflects accumulation of DNA damage beyond the capacity of repair mechanisms, consistent with sustained genotoxic stress.42 The directly opposing findings regarding p21 expression may reflect differences in exposure concentration, treatment duration, compound type, assay method, and cell model characteristics. However, these findings should be regarded as contradictory, and no consistent direction of p21 regulation can currently be established.

Oxidative stress emerged as a prominent and recurring molecular phenotype across the included studies. These findings indicate substantial redox imbalance and genomic insult, accompanied by impaired antioxidant defense and disruption of mitochondrial integrity. Persistent oxidative stress is a well-established driver of mutagenesis, genomic instability, and malignant progression in oral epithelium.43, 44, 45 Importantly, these findings support the concept that oxidative stress is not merely a secondary by-product of cellular injury, but a central driver of carcinogenic processes, including genomic instability, fibrotic remodeling, and tumor progression.

Taken together, the findings of this review support a mechanistic framework in which areca nut constituents contribute to oral carcinogenesis through five interconnected processes: (1) chronic inflammation, (2) enhanced proliferative signaling, (3) oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, (4) activation of oncogenic and stress-response pathways, and (5) suppression of tumor-suppressor and cell-cycle regulatory mechanisms. The consistency of these molecular alterations across multiple human oral cancer cell models strengthens the biological plausibility of areca nut as a direct molecular driver of cancer progression and complements existing epidemiologic and clinical evidence.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity in experimental design, including differences in areca nut extract preparation, alkaloid composition, solvent systems, and compound purity, limiting direct comparison across studies. Many experiments employed high or potentially supraphysiological concentrations of arecoline or areca nut extract. Importantly, several inflammatory, oxidative stress, proliferative, and cell-cycle findings were derived from these higher-dose experiments, including PGE2, COX-2, and IL-6 induction at 200–1200 μM arecoline; p21 and p27 suppression at 500 μM; SAA1, IL6, and IL36G induction at 80–160 μg/mL; and Nrf2/HO-1, ROS, p21, p53, γH2AX, ATM, BrdU, and PCNA alterations across concentrations of 12.5–400 μg/mL. These findings may reflect cytotoxic or stress-related responses at high exposure levels and should not be directly extrapolated to habitual human exposure. Moreover, because crude extracts and purified alkaloids were reported using different units and compositions, a uniform physiological concentration threshold could not be applied across studies. Furthermore, most studies assessed acute exposure, whereas chronic and repetitive exposure more closely represents real-world chewing behavior associated with fibrosis and carcinogenesis. Variability in cell models, ranging from well-differentiated to highly malignant OSCC cell lines, also contributes to differences in baseline gene-expression profiles and responsiveness to areca nut exposure. An additional limitation is the disproportionate representation of the OECM-1 cell line, which was used in six of the 20 included studies. Although these studies were conducted independently, repeated observations in the same cell line do not establish generalizability across biologically distinct oral cancer models. Some apparently recurrent molecular effects may therefore reflect OECM-1-specific biological characteristics rather than uniform responses across OSCC cells. This concentration of evidence may overstate the apparent consistency of the molecular findings. Further validation across multiple independently derived OSCC cell lines, primary oral cancer cells, and patient-derived models is required.

Additional limitations arise from the inherent constraints of in vitro systems. While such models provide valuable mechanistic insight, they cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the oral microenvironment, including stromal interactions, immune modulation, microbiome influences, and salivary chemistry. The absence of tumor–stroma crosstalk and immune components may oversimplify certain biological processes, thereby limiting direct extrapolation to in vivo conditions. In addition, inconsistent reporting of dose–response relationships, exposure durations, and control conditions, as well as limited validation using complementary assays, further restricts quantitative comparison.

Future research should prioritize standardization of areca nut extract preparation, alkaloid quantification, exposure duration, and dosing paradigms to improve comparability across studies. Although studies using three-dimensional cultures, organoids, and related advanced models did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria and were therefore not included in the present synthesis, they provide important contextual evidence regarding emerging approaches in oral carcinogenesis research. Such models may better reproduce cell–cell interactions, extracellular matrix organization, exposure gradients, and tumor–microenvironment interactions than conventional two-dimensional cultures. Future studies should therefore incorporate three-dimensional oral organoids, scaffold-based cultures, co-culture systems, cancer-on-a-chip platforms, and patient-derived organoids to validate the molecular alterations identified in the present review under more physiologically relevant conditions. Long-term and repeated-exposure experiments are needed to mimic chronic chewing patterns. Integration of omics-based approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, would facilitate comprehensive mapping of signaling networks affected by areca nut constituents. These molecular alterations may also have important translational implications, including their potential role as biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification, as well as their involvement in epigenetic regulation and therapeutic resistance in oral cancer. Finally, mechanistic studies linking molecular alterations to functional outcomes such as invasion, migration, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and therapeutic resistance will further clarify the role of areca nut in oral cancer progression.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that areca nut extract and its alkaloid components exert a broad range of carcinogenic molecular effects in human oral cancer cells. Across the included in vitro studies, exposure to areca nut–derived compounds was associated with activation of pro-inflammatory pathways, altered proliferative signaling, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and upregulation of oncogenic pathways. However, the generalizability of these findings across oral cancer models is limited by the disproportionate representation of OECM-1 cells. Concurrently, key tumor-suppressor and cell-cycle regulatory proteins, including p53, p27, and SIRT1, were suppressed, while AP-1 transcription factors were downregulated. Findings regarding p21 expression were contradictory across studies, preventing a definitive conclusion regarding the direction of its regulation. Collectively, these converging molecular alterations form a coherent mechanistic pattern that supports the substantial epidemiological evidence linking areca nut use to oral carcinogenesis.

Although in vitro studies cannot fully recapitulate the biological complexity of in vivo exposure, the consistency and directionality of molecular changes observed across independent experimental systems underscore the significant biological risk associated with areca nut chewing. These findings highlight the need for sustained public health efforts aimed at reducing areca nut consumption, particularly in regions with high prevalence of use. In addition, future research should prioritize standardized exposure protocols, physiologically relevant experimental models, and integrated multiomic approaches to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying areca nut–induced carcinogenesis. Overall, this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the molecular pathways disrupted by areca nut constituents and reinforces their important role in promoting oral cancer development and progression.
