Household income among families with autistic children and youths in Canada: a cross-sectional matched cohort study
Authors: Erin Collins, Ahmed Al-Jaishi, Amy Farrow, Nana Amankwah, Stelios Georgiades, Mackenzie Salt, Kieran Holmes, Rojiemiahd Edjoc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveWithin the Canadian context, we sought to examine the relationship between households with autistic children/youths and household income.DesignWe used data from the 2019 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth (CHSCY) to analyse households with a child/youth aged 1–17. Propensity-score matching was used to pair records for children/youths with a reported autism diagnosis to those without. We used linear regression for continuous outcomes (eg, total household income) and Poisson regression for binary outcomes (eg, low household income). All analyses were adjusted for the correlation between matched pairs.Primary outcome measureTotal annual income of all household members.Secondary outcome measuresLow household income; single-parent or single-income status; and whether at least one parent was not working or absent from work during the past week.ResultsAmong a total of 39 951 CHSCY records, we identified a cohort of 815 autistic children/youths. The characteristics of the matched cohort were well-balanced. Households with an autistic child/youth had a mean annual household income that was lower (mean difference: $C16 489; 95% CI $C6384 to $C27 149) compared with matched households without an autistic child/youth. Households with an autistic child/youth were also 26% more likely to be classified as having a low household income (Relative risk (RR) 1.26; 95% CI 1.17 to 1.35) and 20% more likely to rely on a single income (RR 1.20; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.33) compared with households without an autistic child/youth.ConclusionsCompared with households without an autistic child/youth, those with an autistic child/youth often face more economic challenges, including lower household income and greater risk of food insecurity. Households with an autistic child/youth are more likely to rely on a single income.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Community child health, Cross-Sectional Studies, EPIDEMIOLOGIC STUDIES

Introduction

Autism, also known as autism spectrum disorder, is a lifelong neuro-developmental condition. Autistic people may communicate and connect with other people differently, have sensory processing differences or focus intensely on certain interests or activities. They may also have other disabilities or conditions that affect learning, cognitive functioning and physical or mental health, which can introduce complexities and challenges.1,4 The financial implications of raising an autistic child can be substantial, often requiring specialised treatments, interventions and caregiving services that may not be covered by government support or insurance programmes.5,11 Families with autistic children/youths may also experience a loss of income as parents may need to adjust their employment to provide necessary caregiving.512,15 The age and functional ability of the child/youth influence the degree of support required, both at home and through formal services.5 7 9 11 16 The necessity for various health services can increase family expenses and require parents to miss work to ensure their child/youth’s attendance at appointments.1416,18 Consequently, parents may reduce their work hours or seek more flexible employment options to manage these responsibilities. This reduction in income can affect the family’s ability to afford necessities, such as food, putting households with autistic children/youths at risk of food insecurity.19 20 Low household income and food insecurity are associated with negative health outcomes throughout the lifespan and affect all members of a household.20,24

The 2019 Canadian Health Survey on Children and Youth (CHSCY) estimated that 1 in 50 Canadian children/youths aged 1–17 have been diagnosed with autism,25 26 underscoring the relevance of assessing their families’ socioeconomic conditions. While prior studies have examined the financial and food-related challenges faced by families with autistic children/youths,10 14 20 26 it remains unclear how household income compares between families with autistic children/youths and those without. Here, we have leveraged a nationally representative Canadian cohort to quantify these disparities using matched comparisons.

Our primary objective was to compare income levels between households with autistic children/youths and a matched cohort of households without. We also examined several predefined factors that may influence caregiving needs and household income, including the age and functional ability of the child/youth; single-parent or single-income household status; and the employment status of the person most knowledgeable (PMK, usually a parent) about the child/youth and their spouse. Lastly, we descriptively assessed potential disparities in household food security.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a cross-sectional matched cohort study using data from the 2019 CHSCY25 to analyse households with children/youths in Canada. The CHSCY is a national survey administered by Statistics Canada that collects health data on children/youths aged 1–17 years living in private dwellings across the 10 provinces and 3 territories. Data were collected through electronic questionnaires and phone interviews, spanning 11 February 2019 to 2 August 2019. Additional information about CHSCY is available through Statistics Canada.25 26 While the overall survey was completed by either the selected youth (aged 12–17) or a PMK, all records we included were completed by a PMK only. By design, each record represents a distinct household of a child/youth. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cross-sectional studies.27

Autism diagnosis status of child/youth

We categorised records based on whether the child/youth (aged 1–17 years) had an autism diagnosis. A child/youth was identified as having autism if the PMK responded affirmatively to the question: ‘Has this child been diagnosed with any of the following long-term conditions? - Autism spectrum disorder, also known as Autism, Autistic disorder, Asperger’s disorder, or pervasive developmental disorder’.25

Primary and secondary outcomes

Our primary outcome was the total annual income of all household members before taxes and deductions, as reported by the PMK. Our secondary outcomes included (1) a binary classification (yes/no) of household income based on low-income cut-offs (LICO), derived from comparing the total annual household income against established LICO thresholds from Statistics Canada.28 These thresholds are adjusted for household size and the size of the geographical area of residence; (2) single-parent or single-income households, and whether at least one parent was non-working or absent from work in the past week. The 2019 CHSCY asked whether the PMK and spouse (if applicable) ‘worked at a job or business’, including self-employment, had a job but did not work or did not have a job.25

Descriptive exploratory outcomes

Health service needs

Based on the reporting of the PMK, we counted the number of health services the child/youth required or received in the past 12 months, including those for difficulties or issues with speech/language, mobility/dexterity, focusing/controlling behaviour, mental health and learning.25 Although higher service use among autistic children is anticipated, these findings contextualise the additional time and resource demands placed on their families. Accessing such services often requires substantial parental involvement to coordinate, travel to and attend appointments.1416,18

Food security status

The 2019 CHSCY evaluated food security over the past 12 months using the Household Food Security Survey Module (HFSSM).25 29 Households were classified as food secure, marginally food insecure, moderately food insecure or severely food insecure based on the number of affirmative responses to the HFSSM.30 The 18-item HFSSM can be viewed in the (online supplemental box S1).

Food insecurity was examined descriptively in this study because this item was added post hoc in response to growing policy and research interest in the economic and nutritional challenges experienced by households with autistic children/youths. To avoid the risks associated with multiple comparisons and potential overinterpretation, we did not model food insecurity in our analyses. Instead, we report the proportion of households in each food security category to provide an exploratory view of this item, intended to inform future hypothesis-driven research.

Covariates

We used propensity-score (PS) matching to create a comparison group that closely resembled the group of autistic children/youths based on selected covariates (listed in box 1), thus reducing selection bias and controlling for confounding variables.31 We used PROC PSMACH (Propensity Score Matching) in SAS (Statistical Analysis System) with an optimal matching method and calliper of 0.1; we formed matched pairs that minimised the average within-pair difference in PSs.31,33

Statistical analysis

We conducted analyses at the matched pair level. Cohort characteristics were reported as means with SDs, medians with IQRs (Q1, Q3), or frequencies, as appropriate. We assessed the balance after matching by examining standardised differences, with a standardised difference of less than 0.1 indicating a good match on the selected variable.34 35 We also reported, without CIs, descriptive statistics for the matched cohort, including the working status of the PMK and spouse, autism prevalence by neighbourhood income quintile, health service needs and household food security status.

We performed bivariate linear regression analysis to evaluate the impact of having an autistic child/youth on the continuous variable of household income: Yi​=β0​+β1​Autismi​+εi (Base model), where Yi​ is the household income for household i and ​Autismi is a binary indicator of an autistic child. Similarly, we conducted a bivariate modified Poisson regression analysis for binary outcomes with robust SEs and an exchangeable correlation structure to assess the effect of having an autistic child/youth on binary outcome variables related to household income.36 We selected this approach to obtain direct estimates of relative risk, as opposed to ORs produced by logistic regression, following the method proposed by Zou36, which is recommended for common outcomes due to its interpretability and robustness. No additional covariates were included in these models, unless otherwise specified (ie, in the Sensitivity analysis). All statistical analyses accounted for the within-pair correlation. Parameters were weighted using the sampling weights provided by Statistics Canada, and variance estimates, including 95% CIs, were created for income-related estimates using balanced repeated replication to account for the complex survey design and bootstrap weights.37 Exploratory outcomes, such as health service needs and food security, were not modelled and were presented descriptively without variance estimates, as they were not part of our prespecified hypotheses. All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide V.7.1 (SAS, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Sensitivity analysis

Recognising that autism is a spectrum and that autistic children/youths require varying levels of care and resources, we performed an additional analysis of the primary outcome by adjusting for functional ability in the regression model. This was defined by the presence or absence of difficulties in various functional domains, such as seeing, hearing, walking, self-care, communication, learning, remembering, concentrating, accepting change, controlling behaviour, making friends and mental health. Functional difficulties were assessed by the CHSCY using the Washington Group/United Nations Children’s Fund Child Functioning Module (2016).26 For the primary outcome, the count of reported functional difficulty domains was included as a variable in our model. More specifically, we modelled Yi​=β0​+β1​Autismi​+β2​FuncDiffi​+εi, where Yi​ is the household income for household i, ​Autismi is a binary indicator of a reported autism diagnosis for child/youth i, and ​FuncDiffi is a count variable representing the number of functional difficulty domains for child/youth i. Additionally, considering that age can significantly influence autism characteristics, levels of functioning and care needs, we conducted an analysis that included age as a continuous variable in our model for the primary outcome. That is, Yi​=β0​+β1​Autismi​+β2​Agei​+εi, where Yi​ is the household income for household i, ​Autismi is a binary indicator of a reported autism diagnosis for child/youth i, and ​Agei is a continuous variable for the age (years) of child/youth i.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The 2019 CHSCY captured data on 39 951 children/youths living in Canada. We excluded 271 records (including four records reporting an autism diagnosis) with reported household income greater than $C500 000, as these values were above the 99th percentile and considered extreme outliers. This exclusion was implemented to prevent these values from disproportionately influencing estimates in the linear regression models used to assess household income. As a result, we included 815 records of children/youths with an autism diagnosis and 38 685 records of children/youths without an autism diagnosis, prior to matching (see figure 1). Of records reporting an autism diagnosis, the majority were male (79%) and experienced at least one functional difficulty (73%). The average age was 10 years (SD 0.2), and the average household size was 4.2 persons (SD 0.1). Most PMKs were female (86%), had an education level beyond high school (75%) and had a spouse (76%). On examining the prevalence of autism across neighbourhood income quintiles, we found that autism prevalence increased as income quintiles decreased (online supplemental figure S1). Characteristics and standardised differences between matched and unmatched cohorts are detailed in table 1 and online supplemental table S1, respectively. Following the 1:1 matching of 815 children/youths with an autism diagnosis to 815 children/youths without an autism diagnosis, the matched cohort showed well-balanced characteristics based on standardised differences of less than 0.10.

Secondary outcomes

We found that 30% of PMKs in households with autistic children/youths reported not having a job compared with 22% of PMKs in households without autistic children/youths (online supplemental figure S2a). The proportions of spouses (where applicable) who worked at a job or business were 64% for households with autistic children/youths compared with 65% for those without (online supplemental figure S2b). Households with an autistic child/youth had a relative risk of 1.26 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.35) for a low-income status compared with households without an autistic child/youth (table 3). Interpretation

Households with an autistic child/youth had significantly lower household incomes and faced greater food insecurity compared with a matched cohort of households without an autistic child/youth. These households were also more likely to rely on a single income. From our data, it is unclear if the higher unemployment rate resulted from PMKs choosing not to work to meet the caregiving needs of their child/youth.8 12 14 Although we did not quantify it here, families of autistic children/youths often incur substantial out-of-pocket expenses (eg, for private therapy sessions, specialised equipment) and significant time commitments (travel to appointments, coordination of care), which can further exacerbate financial strain and limit parental workforce participation.1416,18

Our findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that households with autistic children/youths are more likely to experience low income and food insecurity,1938,41 which are associated with adverse health impacts over the lifespan.21,2442 43 While some studies from the USA and Australia have reported an opposite trend, suggesting a higher prevalence of autism in households of higher socioeconomic status, this has been attributed to differential access to services and supports from which participants were recruited.3944,47 Specifically, higher-income families are more likely to access these services compared with lower-income families, leading to potential ascertainment bias.

Our findings that households with autistic children/youths are more likely to rely on a single income also align with previous research.58 12,15 The often-substantial caregiving demands placed on parents may restrict their employment opportunities, leading one parent, typically the mother, not to work, thereby reducing household income. These demands are influenced by various factors, including the age and functional ability of the child/youth, as well as access to both informal and formal supports and services.9 17 18 24 43 48 49 In our study, no significant differences in household income were observed when adjusting for age or the number of functional indicators. The absence of an effect on functional ability may be attributed to low variability within our cohort,26 49 which could have limited our capacity to detect a difference. The null finding for age is likely a result of this variable being included in the PS matching, which resulted in a good balance on this characteristic and no residual effect when adjusted. High caregiving demands, combined with barriers to necessary support and insufficient resources to meet family needs, can exacerbate stress and negatively impact parental health, quality of life and resilience.1448,54

We acknowledge the need for future research on how household income modifies the relationship between an autism diagnosis and health service utilisation, particularly for those with private versus public supports. This information would provide a better understanding of potential differential access and financial burden. There is also a need for research on vulnerable populations, where socioeconomic disparities are likely to be more pronounced. For example, immigrant families in Canada may face increased barriers in accessing needed services and supports, due to such factors as language barriers, restricted employment opportunities and transportation limitations.53 Future research would also benefit from a comprehensive examination of the experiences and insights of parents of autistic children/youths. Such an approach would provide a more nuanced understanding of the barriers these families encounter, the repercussions of low income and food insecurity on their health and well-being and the identification of potential solutions to mitigate these challenges. Furthermore, there is limited understanding of how varying socioeconomic conditions experienced by autistic children/youths affect their developmental trajectories, opportunities and challenges as they transition to adulthood.41 In our secondary analysis, we used the LICO rather than the Market Basket Measure (MBM), which classifies households as low income if their income falls below the amount needed to purchase a specific basket of goods and services representing a modest, basic standard of living.55 Since the MBM and LICO classify largely overlapping low-income populations, we anticipate similar findings under MBM; nevertheless, applying MBM thresholds in future work would provide a useful cost-of-living sensitive analysis.56 Finally, a longitudinal and more granular analysis of how food insecurity impacts families with autistic children/youths is warranted. Given that food insecurity across Canada was exacerbated by the pandemic,54 57 it is reasonable to anticipate that the food security situations for households with autistic children/youths have deteriorated since 2019.

Food insecurity may also have unique effects on these households, as autistic children/youths often experience greater nutrition-related challenges compared with their non-autistic peers. These challenges include food selectivity and heightened sensitivities to smells, tastes and textures.58,61 Additionally, caregivers may endeavour to provide gluten-free meals or adhere to other specialised diets, which, while potentially costly, have been suggested to reduce challenging behaviours in autistic people.62 63

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the CHSCY did not include data on children/youths residing on First Nation reserves, other Indigenous settlements within the provinces, or those living in foster homes and institutions.25 As a result, our findings cannot be generalised to these populations. Second, the overall response rate was 52.1%, and households with autistic children/youths may have been particularly susceptible to non-response, likely due to the demanding nature of caregiving responsibilities. Third, relying on self-reported data introduces the risk of response bias, where respondents may feel pressured to give socially acceptable answers or may provide inaccurate or incomplete information. Fourth, some children/youths may have had autism but lacked a formal diagnosis at the time of the survey, potentially leading to the misclassification of some true cases as non-autistic. This misclassification may have biased the findings toward the null. Fifth, the survey did not provide information on whether the parents or siblings of the surveyed children were diagnosed with autism. It is possible that a household had multiple autistic members. Sixth, we acknowledge the variability in support for autistic children/youths across different jurisdictions in Canada, which we are currently unable to account for.16 Seventh, given the spectrum nature of autism, autistic children/youths have varying levels of care and resource needs. Our study did not assess the severity of functional difficulties, though we did assess this in a previous study.49 Moreover, our sample may under-represent children with milder presentations, as the CHSCY cohort may disproportionately capture individuals diagnosed earlier or with more pronounced functional challenges, reflecting heightened parental recognition and service-seeking. Eighth, the cross-sectional nature of the study introduces temporal ambiguity, making it difficult to discern the direction of the relationship between the exposure and outcome. Finally, we cannot verify that the health services accessed by autistic children/youths were specifically required for autism. Additionally, while we recorded whether a service was required or received, we could not capture the frequency of service use over the previous 12 months.

Conclusion

Households with an autistic child/youth experienced lower household income and greater risk of food insecurity and were more likely to rely on a single income compared with those without an autistic child/youth. Increased caregiving demands and the need for health services for autistic children/youths may limit parents’ ability to participate in the workforce and can possibly constrain the family’s overall income.

Language statement

We endeavoured to use language to describe autism that is preferred by the autistic community. Hence, based on the recommendations of the Autism Alliance of Canada,64 this manuscript uses identity-first language when referring to autistic people.
