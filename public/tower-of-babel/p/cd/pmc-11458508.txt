SARS-CoV-2 Transmission Rate Depends on Infectivity of the Virus Strain, Closeness of Contact, and the Immune Competence of the Infected Person
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2024

Dear Editor:

We were interested to read the article by Sim et al. on a study about the risk of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection in a general population with laboratory-confirmed coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in a community setting in Korea [1]. Of 1,286 people with COVID-19 who had contact with a primarily uninfected person between January 24, 2020 and June 30, 2022, 10.3% were secondarily infected with SARS-CoV-2 [1]. With increasing age, the risk of exposed persons becoming infected with COVID-19 in index cases increased, especially in persons in their 70s [1]. The risk of becoming infected was particularly high in households and long-term care facilities [1]. The study is impressive, but some points need to be discussed.

The first point is that contact with a SARS-CoV-2-infected patient does not necessarily mean that a secondary infected person has become infected through this contact. How did the study design rule out the possibility that the secondary infected person was infected via a completely different route?

The second point is that no clear explanation was given as to why the transmission rate was particularly high in older patients and those living in long-term care facilities. Was this due to the increased risk of older patients being polymorbid or the likelihood that older patients were taking more medication than younger patients? It should be clarified whether the immune system is more likely to be damaged in older patients than in younger ones. Is the increased rate of SARS-CoV-2 infection in older patients due to the fact that their immune system is more likely to be compromised prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection?

The third point is that the type and extent of contact with a patient infected with SARS-CoV-2 can vary from patient to patient. The contact numbers only marginally reflect reality. Nobody counts minutes or meters. Therefore, the intensity and type of contact with an infected person should be categorized more realistically.

The fourth point is that, surprisingly, the majority of infected patients come from overseas [1]. How can this finding be explained? Was it only visitors who came to the country and not patients from the country who became secondarily infected? This is a discrepancy to the 25 million infected patients in the country. One would expect that preferably local people would have been secondarily infected.

The fifth point is that COVID-19 can be a multisystem disease, none of the included patients had multisystem involvement, not even those who were hospitalized. We should know why no critically ill patients who were in the intensive care unit were included in the study.

The sixth point is that animal transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was not considered. Since both domestic and wild animal transmission has been documented [2], it would have been imperative to at least include the question of whether or not the included patients had an infected pet in the analysis.

In conclusion, the transmission rate of SARS-CoV-2 depends on the infectivity of the virus strain, the intensity of contact, and the immune competence of the infected person.
