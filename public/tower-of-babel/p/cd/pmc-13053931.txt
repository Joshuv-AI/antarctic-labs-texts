Baseline characteristics of the SINgapore GERiatric Intervention Study to Reduce Cognitive Decline and Physical Frailty (SINGER) multidomain dementia prevention randomized controlled trial and insights from the recruitment process
Authors: Kwong Hsia Yap, Cynthia Chen, Eddie J. Y. Chong, Nagaendran Kandiah, Miia Kivipelto, Mitchell K. P. Lai, Andrea B. Maier, David B. Matchar, April Ka Sin Phua, Connie K. L. Teo, Xin Xu, Helen Juan Zhou, Christopher Philip Li‐Hsian Chen

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe SINgapore GERiatric Intervention Study to Reduce Cognitive Decline and Physical Frailty (SINGER) is the first large Southeast Asian randomized controlled trial of multidomain lifestyle interventions in older adults at increased dementia risk.METHODSBaseline assessments included sociodemographic, cognitive, lifestyle, and clinical risk factors. Recruitment strategies and adaptations to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) restrictions and operational challenges were documented.RESULTSA total of 3841 individuals were pre‐screened, with 1218 randomized 1:1 to a Structured Lifestyle Intervention or a Self‐Guided Intervention (606 each; three exclusions per arm). BACKGROUND

Recent reviews have estimated that almost half of dementia can be delayed or prevented by addressing modifiable risk factors, including hypertension, physical inactivity, social isolation, and poor diet. 1 , 2 This has increased interest in multidomain interventions targeting these factors to reduce the risk of cognitive decline across the lifecourse. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses support the potential of multidomain strategies—particularly in high‐risk individuals—for preserving cognitive function and slowing decline. 3 , 4

Previous trials have evaluated the multidomain approach. The PREvention of Dementia by Intensive VAscular care (preDIVA) trial in the Netherlands assessed the long‐term impact of a 6‐year, nurse‐led multidomain intervention focused on vascular risk management, and included lifestyle advice on diet, smoking cessation, and physical activity 5 ; the Multidomain Alzheimer Preventive Trial (MAPT) study in France tested omega‐3 supplementation and a multidomain intervention in frail older adults 6 ; and the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability (FINGER) demonstrated the effectiveness of a 2‐year structured program involving diet, exercise, cognitive training, and vascular monitoring in slowing cognitive decline among at‐risk older individuals. 7 , 8 More recently, the U.S. POINTER trial reported significantly greater improvement in participants randomized to a structured multidomain lifestyle intervention compared with a Self‐Guided Intervention (SGI), extending evidence for the efficacy of multidomain interventions beyond Europe. 9 , 10 Although FINGER 7 , 8 and U.S. POINTER 9 , 10 demonstrated significant cognitive benefits, findings from MAPT and preDIVA provided important lessons regarding feasibility, population targeting, intervention intensity, and adherence. 11 Baseline reports from these trials described participant characteristics, essential for interpreting outcomes and planning implementation.

Building on the FINGER trial, the World‐Wide FINGERS (WW‐FINGERS) network was established to adapt and test similar multidomain interventions across diverse cultural and health system contexts using harmonized methodologies for cross‐country comparisons and global learning. 12 , 13 The network now includes over 60 countries, with covering studies conducted in varied population and delivery settings, such as The US Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (US POINTER) in the United States 9 , Multimodal Interventions to delay dementia and disability in rural China (MIND‐CHINA), in China, 14 Prevention of cognitive decline in subjective cognitive decline APOE ε4 carriers after EGCG and a multimodal intervention (PENSA) in Spain, 15 A Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention in Dutch Older Adults to Prevent Cognitive Decline (FINGER‐NL) in the Netherlands 16 and The Japan‐multimodal intervention trial for prevention of dementia (J‐MINT) in Japan. 17 Across these studies, there are differences in baseline risk profiles, eligibility criteria, and intervention delivery, reflecting local demographic, cultural, and health system contexts. Consistent with these differences, reported cognitive outcomes have varied across settings. For example, significant benefits observed in the U.S. POINTER trial 9 , 10 contrasted with the subgroup‐specific effects reported in J‐MINT. 17 Together, these findings underscore the need to consider recruitment context when interpreting trial results across setting.

These strategies are particularly relevant in rapidly aging societies such as Singapore, where the emphasis on healthy aging is reflected in national policy frameworks such as the Action Plan for Successful Ageing, 18 which promotes preventive health, community engagement, and aging‐in‐place. These goals align with the Healthier SG initiative, which aims to shift the health care system toward proactive and preventive care. 18 , 19

In this context, implementing scalable multidomain interventions is both a public health priority and feasibility challenge. Success depends on the ability to recruit and retain a diverse, representative participant population, so that trial findings are generalizable to real‐world settings. Recruitment challenges were exacerbated during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic, which restricted outreach and in‐person activities. Insufficient recruitment can delay timelines, increase costs, and compromise validity and generalizability. 20 , 21 As a result, studies need to balance scientific goals with operational feasibility, often leading to pragmatic decisions that may have unintended consequences for participant diversity or study generalisability. Understanding who is ultimately recruited and the recruitment process is essential for interpreting outcomes, maintaining internal validity, and guiding real‐world implementation after study completion.

The SINgapore GERiatric Intervention Study to Reduce Cognitive Decline and Physical Frailty (SINGER) is a 2‐year randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a multidomain lifestyle intervention adapted from the FINGER protocol for older adults in Singapore. 22 The intervention includes structured guidance on nutrition, physical activity, cognitive training, and vascular risk management, with an additional focus on intensive blood pressure control for participants with hypertension. SINGER also contributes to the WW‐FINGERS network, representing one of the first trials in Southeast Asia to evaluate such strategies at scale. Funding has been secured to extend follow‐up 6 years post‐baseline, enabling long‐term assessment of intervention effects.

This article presents the baseline sociodemographic, clinical, and cognitive characteristics of enrolled participants. It also describes the recruitment strategies and adaptations during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic that ensured continuity of enrollment. These findings provide context for interpreting trial outcomes and lessons for future multidomain dementia prevention studies.

METHODS

The SINGER trial protocol is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05007353) and approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain‐Specific Review Board (NHG‐DSRB ID: 2020/01450). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment into the study.

Study design and intervention

The SINGER study is a 2‐year randomized controlled trial aimed at evaluating the efficacy and safety of multimodal lifestyle interventions combined with intensive blood pressure control. 22 The study protocol has been published previously. 22 Briefly, SINGER interventions are based on the successful FINGER study, 7 with local adaptations 23 including following the SPRINT protocol for intensive blood pressure management, 24 which targets a systolic blood pressure of 120 mmHg for hypertensive individuals.

Eligibility criteria followed the previously published protocol, 22 with one amendment: the upper age limit increased from 77 to 80 to enhance recruitment feasibility and to capture the target at‐risk population. This better reflected the demographic realities of older adults in Singapore who remain functionally independent and at increased risk of dementia beyond the original age threshold. In addition, recruitment venues were expanded to include community events and media outreach. Hence, participants were eligible if they were 60–80 years of age, had a Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Aging, and Incidence of Dementia (CAIDE) dementia risk score ≥6, a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score between 18 and 27, and at least one modifiable lifestyle risk factor from the LIfestyle for BRAin health (LIBRA) index (e.g., diet, cognitive activity, or physical activity). Participants had to be functionally independent and medically stable for intervention. Exclusion criteria included symptomatic cardiovascular disease, major depression, cognitive impairment outside the MoCA range, active malignancy, and other conditions that could compromise participation.

The multidomain intervention includes dietary guidance, exercise, cognitive and social stimulation and control of vascular and metabolic risk factors. Participants are randomized to either the intensive Structured Lifestyle Intervention (SLI), supported by lifestyle coaches or the Self‐Guided Intervention (SGI) involving educational materials for self‐directed changes. 22 Both groups have access to health educational materials uploaded on a digital platform. Blood pressure management for participants with hypertension in SLI followed the SPRINT protocol as described in the published SINGER protocol. 22 Management was coordinated by study physicians rather than through participants’ primary care providers. Assessments and medication titration were conducted during scheduled outcome visits and additional visits as clinically indicated, using standardized blood pressure measurements consistent with the SPRINT protocol. 24 The primary outcome is global cognitive composite derived from a modified Neuropsychological Test Battery (mNTB). 25 , 26 Secondary outcomes are domain specific cognition and function, brain and retinal imaging changes, blood biomarkers, incidence and progression of chronic diseases, quality of life, mental health, and cost‐effectiveness.

COVID‐related protocol changes and digital pivot

Due to restrictions on personal contact in the early pandemic phase, most intervention components were pivoted to digital formats for activities requiring group and individual interactions (see Table 1). Because social interaction is preserved through group sessions, be it online or face to face, each participant was provided with electronic tablets to access information and their online classes. Lesson materials, class schedule, and health education modules were hosted in a study specific digital application. The digital format was retained throughout the trial period and was not discontinued following the relaxation of COVID‐19 restrictions.

Recruitment strategy and COVID‐19 adaptations

At study initiation, recruitment was planned to rely primarily on screening existing research participant databases, digital dissemination of study information via institutional platforms and social media, and informal word‐of‐mouth referrals. This approach reflected both prevailing COVID‐19 restrictions at the time of study launch and the absence of established community recruitment partnerships at study inception.

As the study progressed and COVID‐19 restrictions eased, additional recruitment opportunities emerged through newly established connections with community and social organizations. These enabled in‐person outreach at community health events and community centers, which were not part of the original recruitment approach but were incorporated as adaptive strategies to enhance participant reach. A second recruitment site was subsequently added to support enrolment.

Recruitment sources

Recruitment was conducted through several complementary sources. Existing research databases included individuals from prior studies who had consented to be contacted for future research and were pre‐screened against eligibility criteria. Online recruitment included self‐referral through the study website, where interested individuals could register and undergo initial web‐based pre‐screening. Media outreach consisted of unpaid media coverage, including interviews on brain health featuring the study team, a brief documentary segment, and incidental publicity such as newspaper commentary by participants, which included study contact details. Community‐based face‐to‐face recruitment involved in‐person outreach at community health events and community centers, whereas community survey recruitment captured individuals expressing interest through community‐administered surveys. Referrals reflected participant‐reported word‐of‐mouth (e.g., family members or friends recommending the study). These approaches were deployed adaptively as opportunities arose over the recruitment period.

RESULTS

Formation of the study population

A total of 3841 individuals were pre‐screened. Of these, 2468 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria. Due to the nature of telephone and web‐based pre‐screening, complete data were not available for all who were excluded. The remaining 1373 individuals were invited for in‐person physical screening, during which an additional 155 were excluded.

A total of 1218 participants completed screening in‐person and were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the SLI group (n = 609) or the SGI group (n = 609). In each arm, three participants were later excluded due to enrollment errors, resulting in 606 participants per arm receiving the allocated intervention. Overall, 31.5% of those pre‐screened proceeded to randomization, following a multi‐stage eligibility assessment process. Across all stages of screening, the most common reasons for not meeting inclusion criteria included insufficient dementia risk based on CAIDE score, cognitive ineligibility identified during eligibility assessment, and medical comorbidities incompatible with trial participation. At the in‐person screening stage, cognitive exclusions were primarily driven by MoCA scores outside the prespecified eligibility range, as shown in Figure 1. Some individuals declined further participation due to concerns about study visits during the COVID‐19 period. Although exclusion reasons at in‐person screening were systematically recorded, detailed exclusion data were not consistently available for individuals screened via telephone‐ or web‐based pre‐screening.

Baseline characteristics of participants

Sociodemographic characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the participants in the SLI and SGI groups are shown in Table 2. The mean age of participants was 68.7 years (standard deviation [SD] 4.9), with 54.2% being female. Most participants were of Chinese ethnicity (96.4%), and the average years of education was 12.5 (SD 3.9). A majority were married or partnered (76.2%), and 12.4% lived alone. At baseline, over 70% of participants reported poor cognitive activity and physical inactivity, whereas 62.9% had suboptimal dietary patterns.

The overall population demonstrated a moderate vascular risk profile. Mean blood pressure and BMI values were in the pre‐hypertensive and overweight ranges, respectively, and cardiometabolic conditions were common. Over half reported hypertension, nearly 70% had hyperlipidemia, and 20% had diabetes. Lipid and glucose levels were within expected ranges.

Approximately two‐thirds of participants were socially engaged, defined as participation in at least one social activity at least once per week, with similar proportions observed across intervention groups. A parental history of dementia was reported by about one‐fourth of participants, and the distribution was comparable between groups. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier status was present in 16.4% of the cohort.

Cognitive characteristics

Baseline cognitive function was similar across the two intervention groups (Table 4). The overall mean MoCA score was 24.7 (SD 2.15), and the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) was 26.7 (SD 2.0). Geriatric Depression Scale scores averaged 1.6 (SD 1.9). Most participants were rated as Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) Global 0 (67.5%), with the remaining 32.3% rated as CDR Global 0.5. The average CDR Sum of Boxes (SOB) was 0.33 (SD 0.55), with comparable values across groups. No formal clinical diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was assigned at baseline. Memory function, based on both visual (Wechsler Memory Scale [WMS] Visual Paired Associates) and verbal tasks (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test [RAVLT], WMS Logical Memory), showed comparable mean values across immediate and delayed recall. Processing speed and attention, measured by the Digit Letter Substitution Test and Trail Making Test Part A, also showed similar means across groups. Executive function and working memory measures (Digit Span, Trail Making Test Part B, and verbal fluency tasks) were also similar across groups. Overall, the cognitive profiles were consistent with a community‐dwelling older adult population with variable but generally preserved function at baseline.

The characteristics of the enrolled cohort reflect the outcome of a multi‐stage recruitment and screening process carried out under evolving public health constraints. To better understand how this population was formed, the following section outlines the recruitment timeline, adaptations made in response to COVID‐19, and key strategies that enabled the study to reach its target enrolment.

Recruitment timeline and recruitment numbers by source

The recruitment for SINGER started as planned in the first study site (National University Health System [NUHS]) in August 2021, when COVID‐19 restrictions were still in effect in Singapore. COVID‐19 posed challenges, especially in the initial stages of the study. Recruitment remained slow during the first 6 months of the study, as potential participants from the research databases were either ineligible or too concerned to travel to the study site for screening and enrollment. As restrictions eased, the study team formed informal partnerships with community and social organizations and gained access to provide outreach at community health events and community centres.

Following a series of community referral drives and media campaigns in mid‐2022, recruitment had a notable increase. From early 2023 onward, broader engagement strategies including a new recruitment site at Nanyang Technological University (NTU) and expanded outreach efforts continued to accelerate recruitment. Overall, recruitment adaptations were critical in overcoming early barriers and ensuring enrollment of a population with the targeted risk characteristics. Cognitive status was assessed after enrollment and was not used to guide recruitment through specific sources. The proportion of participants rated as CDR Global 0.5 varied across recruitment sources, ranging from 22.0% among participants recruited via the study website to 53.6% among those recruited from existing research databases. Intermediate proportions were observed among referral‐based recruitment (32.9%), community‐based face‐to‐face outreach (33.6%), media recruitment (48.5%), and community survey recruitment (26.3%). Referral‐based and community face‐to‐face recruitment contributed the largest absolute numbers of participants (Figure 2B).

DISCUSSION

The SINGER trial illustrates the challenges and adaptive strategies required to implement a complex, lifestyle‐modification dementia prevention trial during a public health crisis such as the COVID‐19 pandemic. Recruitment was initially constrained by social distancing measures and concerns about in‐person interactions, particularly among older adults. The transition to digital delivery for several intervention components was necessary to maintain continuity, but may have limited engagement among those with lower digital literacy or access—a concern similarly reported in other contemporary aging studies. 21 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Despite these challenges, the SINGER study met its recruitment target through adaptive strategies, including expanded media outreach, community partnerships, and the addition of a second study site. These adaptations mirror the experiences of other multidomain trials conducted during the COVID‐19 period such as FINGER‐NL 16 and U.S. POINTER, 9 for which flexible recruitment strategies and hybrid delivery formats were required to sustain enrollment and intervention delivery under public health restrictions. The feasibility of flexible, multimodal recruitment is particularly relevant for dementia prevention trials, which frequently encounter slow enrollment and high screen‐fail rates. 32 In the SINGER study, such adaptive strategies were critical to achieving recruitment targets within a constrained study timeline.

SINGER successfully enrolled a cohort with a substantial burden of modifiable vascular and lifestyle risk factors—comparable to the at‐risk populations targeted in FINGER, preDIVA, and other WW‐FINGERS network studies.6,7 SINGER is among the first large‐scale, harmonized multidomain dementia prevention cohorts in Southeast Asia. It contributes baseline data that can support cross‐country comparisons within the WW‐FINGERS network.

However, replication of the original FINGER trial results is not assured across all settings and may differ depending on the targeted study population. For example, the J‐MINT trial in Japan, which targeted participants with MCI, reported no significant difference in their primary cognitive outcomes between the intervention and control groups at 18 months, although improvements were observed in physical and metabolic outcomes. 17 By contrast, Australia's Maintain Your Brain (MYB) trial—a large, fully online, multidomain lifestyle intervention—demonstrated statistically significant cognitive improvements over 3 years across multiple domains, including global cognition, learning and memory, and complex attention. 33 These benefits were most pronounced among participants with higher adherence, highlighting the importance of engagement. These findings reinforce the potential of scalable digital interventions while emphasizing the need for targeted strategies to support broader populations. Although outcome data from SINGER are pending, the baseline cohort demonstrates a high burden of modifiable vascular and lifestyle risks, suggesting substantial scope for prevention strategies.

The relatively lower mean BMI observed in SINGER compared with some Western multidomain trials 10 is consistent with the J‐MINT trial in Japan, which reported mean BMI values around 23 kg/m2 alongside a high prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors. 34 The high prevalence of poor cognitive activity of this study population at baseline likely reflects differences in lifestyle patterns and engagement behaviors among community‐dwelling older adults. Together, these baseline characteristics underscore the relevance of interventions targeting modifiable lifestyle and engagement‐related risk factors within this population.

The final SINGER cohort included a large proportion of older adults with modifiable vascular and lifestyle risk factors which is the intended target group for dementia prevention. Although the SINGER trial primarily targeted older adults at increased risk of dementia rather than individuals with established dementia, the cohort included participants spanning a range of cognitive functioning. Approximately one‐third of participants were rated as CDR Global 0.5 at baseline. As the CDR is a staging instrument rather than a diagnostic classification, a CDR Global score of 0.5 is not synonymous with a formal clinical diagnosis of MCI. 35 , 36 , 37 Participants were enrolled based on predefined risk‐enrichment criteria, including CAIDE score, MoCA range, and modifiable lifestyle risk factors, rather than on diagnostic categorization or presumed etiology. Accordingly, the enrolled cohort comprised both cognitively normal individuals and individuals whose cognitive profiles could meet commonly used research criteria for MCI, although no formal diagnostic adjudication was performed. Cognitive status was characterized using standardized assessments collected at baseline (CDR Global and CDR‐SOB, MoCA, MMSE, and mNTB domain composites), consistent with multidomain dementia prevention trials that recruit at‐risk, non‐demented populations within defined eligibility thresholds. 6 , 7 , 10 , 33

Baseline cognitive, vascular, and sociodemographic characteristics were well distributed between randomized arms, supporting internal validity. The relatively high rates of poor physical activity, poor cognitive engagement, and vascular burden suggest a meaningful scope for intervention responsiveness. Despite achieving recruitment targets, the cohort was predominantly Chinese and relatively well educated, suggesting that some ethnic minority groups and individuals with lower educational attainment may have been under‐represented, reflecting broader challenges in engaging these populations in prevention trials. Nevertheless, there was still meaningful variation in marital status and living arrangements, offering some real‐world relevance. Future efforts should aim to recruit a more ethnically and socioeconomically diverse sample that better reflects the broader older adult population in Singapore.

CONCLUSION

SINGER successfully recruited and randomized 1212 older adults over a period of 28 months, which included a period of significant public health disruption. The recruitment process benefited from operational flexibility, including protocol amendments, community‐based outreach, and the use of digital engagement tools. These adaptations enabled the trial to reach its target population—older adults at increased risk of dementia and physical decline—while preserving methodological rigor.

A clear understanding of the recruitment pathway and participant profile is essential for interpreting trial outcomes, informing subgroup analyses. The SINGER baseline findings contribute to the limited Southeast Asian evidence base on multidomain dementia prevention and support WW‐FINGERS cross‐country comparisons.
