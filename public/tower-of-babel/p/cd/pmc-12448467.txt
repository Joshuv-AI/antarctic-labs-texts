Diagnosed health conditions and health care use among Medicaid expansion enrollees, 2019 and 2022
Authors: William L Schpero, Manyao Zhang, Yasin Civelek
Published: 2025

Introduction

As of 2025, 40 states and the District of Columbia have implemented expansions of Medicaid eligibility under the Affordable Care Act (ACA).1 A large body of research has found that these expansions are associated with improved health care coverage, access to care, and health outcomes.2 The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) recently passed by Congress introduces significant changes to Medicaid expansion, including a requirement for states to condition enrollment on working or participating in other qualifying activities.3 Estimates from the Congressional Budget Office suggest that this work reporting requirement will result in coverage losses for millions of expansion enrollees, in large part due to the administrative barriers enrollees will face attempting to report they are in compliance.4

In this study, we used national Medicaid claims data to examine the demographic characteristics, diagnosed health conditions, and health care use of adults enrolled in Medicaid under the ACA expansion to provide a baseline assessment of the health care needs and utilization patterns of this population.

Methods

We used claims data from the Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (T-MSIS) Analytic Files (TAF) for 2019 and 2022. We selected 2019 as a baseline year to reflect pre-pandemic conditions and 2022 as the most recent year available with full claims run-out. We restricted our analysis to individuals residing in states that had implemented Medicaid expansion by January 1 in each year, as well as to states with sufficient quality for relevant data parameters in the TAF. Our sample comprised individuals in the expansion group enrolled in Medicaid at least 1 month in the year, ages 19–64 years, with full or comprehensive benefits, without dual enrollment in Medicare. To identify diagnosed health conditions, we used the Hierarchical Condition Categories (HCC) algorithm and required continuous enrollment across 2 years (2018–2019 and 2021–2022) to avoid bias from churn. Measures of health care use included number of emergency department (ED), inpatient, and outpatient visits, as well as counts of prescription drug fills. See the online Supplement for additional details.

We first provide national estimates on demographic characteristics, health conditions, and health care use of expansion enrollees. We then show variation in health care use measures across states. Analyses were performed using Stata, version 18.0 (StataCorp). The study was deemed exempt by the institutional review board at Weill Cornell Medical College.

Results

The sample included 15 033 449 enrollees in 26 states in 2019 and 21 431 326 enrollees in 33 states in 2022 with at least 1 month of enrollment in the year (Table 1). In 2022, 33.3% were under age 30, 25.5% were over age 50, and 49.2% were female. Among continuously enrolled individuals, 33.7% were diagnosed with at least 1 health condition; the most common conditions included mental health and substance use disorders (12.2%), pulmonary and respiratory disorders (9.2%), and endocrine and metabolic disorders (8.6%). Outpatient visits (60.4% of enrollees ever visited) and prescription drugs (60.3% of enrollees ever filled) were the most common forms of health care use, followed by ED visits (25.0%) and inpatient visits (5.4%).

Prescription drug use exhibited the largest variation across states in 2022 (47.1% of enrollees in Alaska to 71.2% of enrollees in Louisiana), followed by outpatient visits (50.1% in Alaska to 70.1% in Minnesota), ED visits (18.9% in New York to 35.6% in Louisiana), and inpatient visits (4.0% in Washington to 8.4% in Nebraska) (Appendix Figure 1B). Results were largely similar in 2019 (Table 1 and Appendix Figure 1A).

Discussion

In 2022, over one-fourth of adults enrolled in Medicaid under the ACA's expansion were over age 50 and over one-third had at least 1 diagnosed health condition. One-quarter had an ED visit, while about half had multiple outpatient visits and prescription drug fills.

Given our finding that many adult expansion enrollees are actively receiving care, the coverage losses predicted under the OBBBA's work reporting requirement could introduce discontinuities in care. Prior research has shown that continuity in care is associated with improved outcomes, particularly for mental health, which we found was the most common condition in the adult expansion population.5

This study is limited by use of claims data and the HCC algorithm to assess health conditions, which undercount the prevalence of disease and do not include assessments of functional status or severity of disease. It also excludes some expansion states due to data quality concerns.

Nonetheless, we established baseline evidence on health care needs and use among Medicaid expansion adults ahead of the implementation of work reporting requirements. Additional evidence on the effect of those requirements, and heterogeneity in those effects across different subpopulations, remain key areas for future research.
