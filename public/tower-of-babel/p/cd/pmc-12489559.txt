Efficacy and safety of endoscopic submucosal dissection versus endoscopic papillectomy for managing laterally spreading duodenal papillary tumors
Authors: Yuki Kano, Ken Ohata, Toshifumi Iida, Susumu Banjoya, Tomoya Kimura, Koichi Furuta, Shinya Nagae, Yohei Ito, Hiroshi Yamazaki, Nao Takeuchi, Shunya Takayanagi, Yoshiaki Kimoto, Yuji Koyama, Seitaro Tsujino, Takashi Sakuno, Kohei Ono, Yohei Minato, Yuji Fujita, Eiji Sakai, Hideyuki Chiba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) can be performed to treat laterally spreading duodenal papillary tumors (LSPTs). However, no studies have been conducted on the outcomes of ESD for LSPTs.MethodsWe retrospectively compared 47 patients who underwent endoscopic papillectomy (EP) for papillary tumors (PTs) between June 2007 and July 2023 (EP group) and eight patients who underwent ESD for LSPTs between February 2022 and July 2023 (ESD group). In the subgroup analysis, five patients who underwent EP for LSPTs were compared with eight patients who underwent ESD for LSPTs.ResultsProcedure times and tumor diameters were significantly greater in the ESD group than in the EP group. The positive or unclear vertical margin (VM1/X) rate was significantly higher in the ESD group. Additional therapies were administered for patients with VM1/X in the ESD group, and no local recurrence was observed. No delayed adverse events occurred in the ESD group; however, delayed bleeding and perforation occurred in the EP group. In the subgroup analysis, the en bloc resection rate was significantly higher in the ESD group than in the EP group. The VM1/X rate did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsWe suggest that ESD is both feasible and safe for LSPTs.

Graphical abstract

INTRODUCTION

The standard treatment for papillary tumors (PTs) is pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD), which is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates.1-3 Endoscopic papillectomy (EP) with a snare is an alternative to surgery for the treatment of PTs.4-7 EP is recommended for adenomas and is acceptable for papillary carcinomas because it is performed intramucosally and does not involve the sphincter of Oddi.8 EP has become a feasible resection method with a high success rate (61% to 92%).9-13 However, EP has a relatively high complication rate. Delayed bleeding, delayed perforation, and severe pancreatitis11,14,15 have been reported in 0% to 21.6%, 0% to 8.3%, and 0% to 23.6% of patients, respectively.16

Laterally spreading duodenal PTs (LSPTs) are often detected clinically and require treatment; however, they are difficult to resect with EP. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for superficial duodenal epithelial tumors (SDETs) is more challenging than that for other gastrointestinal tumors. However, with advancements in ESD techniques, duodenal ESD has achieved secure en bloc resections.17 In a recent study, a 100% en bloc resection rate was reportedly achieved with duodenal ESD, with an R0 resection rate of 92.4%, and delayed bleeding and perforation rates of only 1.5% and 0%, respectively.18 Duodenal ESD for LSPTs has recently been reported.19 Considering the high complication rates of EP for PTs, ESD may also have high complication rates. Although previous studies have evaluated the outcomes of ESD for LSPTs, no study has systematically compared ESD and EP for these lesions. Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ESD for LSPTs by comparing their outcomes with those of EP.

METHODS

Study design

This single-center retrospective study included 47 patients who underwent EP for PTs between June 2007 and July 2023 (EP group) and eight patients who underwent duodenal ESD for LSPTs between February 2022 and July 2023 (ESD group) at our hospital. We compared EP for PTs with ESD for LSPTs to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ESD. In the subgroup analysis, five patients who underwent EP for LSPTs during the aforementioned period were compared with eight patients who underwent ESD for LSPTs. The drug withdrawal periods for patients who received antithrombotic therapy were determined according to the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society guidelines.20 The administration of antithrombotic drugs was resumed within 24 h after the procedures in all patients.

Outcome

To assess the safety and feasibility of duodenal ESD compared with EP, we collected data on patient demographics; clinical outcomes of endoscopic resections; histopathological findings; incidence rates of delayed adverse events, such as postoperative perforations or bleeding and post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) pancreatitis (PEP); and the clinical course of each patient, including additional endoscopic treatments, additional surgical resections, and local recurrence. PEP was defined in accordance with a previously released consensus definition.21 Delayed adverse events were defined as events related to ESD and EP that occurred within 14 days after procedure. Delayed adverse events, including bleeding that required endoscopic hemostasis, perforation, or intra-abdominal abscess formation, were confirmed by computed tomography, had not been identified during ESD and EP, and were only detected after the procedure. PTs were defined as neoplastic lesions confined to the papillary regions, while LSPTs were defined as ampullary tumors that extended laterally beyond the ampullary mound by ≥20 mm or had an extra-papillary component on the duodenal wall, larger than the size of the neoplastic lesion.22

Endoscopic procedure

ESD was performed under general anesthesia with propofol and fentanyl, and endotracheal intubation. A VIO300D (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH) was used for ESD. Duodenal ESD for LSPTs was performed according to the following procedure (Fig. 1A, B): after submucosal injection of sodium hyaluronate solution, we incised the mucosa from the proximal side of the ampulla and dissected the submucosal layer using a high-resolution video endoscope (GIF-H290T; Olympus Medical Systems Co.) equipped with a Tech Knife (Micro-Tech Co. Ltd.). Subsequently, the papilla was resected (Fig. 1C), and a circumferential mucosal incision was made to achieve en bloc resection (Fig. 1D). A traction device was used only when necessary. Resected specimens were immediately retrieved for histopathological evaluation (Fig. 1E). Following ESD, endoscopists specializing in hepatobiliary endoscopy performed endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) and nasopancreatic duct drainage (ENPD) in all the patients. A drainage tube was inserted as soon as possible after ESD. ENBD and ENPD (ENBPD) were performed using a side-viewing endoscope (TJF-290V; Olympus Medical Systems Co.). During elective cholangiography and pancreatography, an ERCP catheter (MTW Endoskopie) was advanced into the biliary tree and main pancreatic duct using a 0.025-inch hydrophilic guidewire (VisiGlide 2; Olympus Medical Systems Co.). Subsequently, a 6-Fr ENBD tube (SilkyPass; Boston Scientific) was inserted into the biliary tree for ENBD, and a 5-Fr ENPD tube (GF drainage catheter set; GADELIUS Medical Co. Ltd.) was inserted into the main pancreatic duct for ENPD. The ENBD and ENPD catheters were advanced together and placed in suitable positions in the duodenum. Follow-up endoscopy was performed every 12 months even in the absence of recurrence (Fig. 1F).

Conventional EP was performed using a duodenoscope with a working channel of 3.2 to 4.2 mm (JF-240, JF-260V, TJF-260V, and TJF-290V; Olympus Medical Systems Co.) and a rotatable snare (Boston Scientific) without using a saline solution injection. After snare resection, we routinely attempted to place a 5-Fr 5 cm double flap pancreatic stent (GF stent; GADELIUS Medical Co. Ltd.) into the pancreatic duct and a 7-Fr 7 cm biliary stent (Flexima Biliary Stent; Boston Scientific) into the bile duct, without prior sphincterotomy.

Additional treatment

In cases where the resection margins were positive or unclear, or where there was suspicion of microscopic or macroscopic residual tumors after endoscopic resection, argon plasma coagulation (APC) ablation (forced APC mode, maximum 40 watts) was performed during the first half of the study period. In the second half of the study period, ERCP-directed intraductal radiofrequency ablation (ID-RFA) was performed using a Habib Endo HPB catheter (EMcision UK). For patients with local recurrence, APC, RFA, or repapillectomy was selectively performed at the discretion of the endoscopist, depending on the specific case.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR ver. 1.40 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University). Values are expressed as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles or absolute numbers (%). Differences in patient or lesional characteristics and clinicopathological outcomes between the ESD and EP groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were performed after written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of NTT Medical Center Tokyo (approval number 000200022021-01).

RESULTS

Background patient characteristics

The patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences in age, sex, comorbidities, or antithrombotic drug distribution were observed between the ESD and EP groups. Lesional characteristics, such as macroscopic type or periampullary diverticular distribution, were not significantly different between the two groups, while tumor diameters were significantly greater in the ESD group than in the EP group (25.0 vs. 12.0 mm, p<0.01).

Clinicopathological endoscopic resection outcomes

The clinical outcomes of the endoscopic resection are summarized in Table 2. The en bloc resection rate was not significantly different between the two groups (100% vs. 87.2%, p=1). The median procedure time was significantly longer in the ESD group than in the EP group (37.5 vs. 10.0 minutes, p<0.01). All the patients in the ESD group underwent ENBPD after duodenal ESD. In the EP group, 46 (97.9%) patients underwent a combination of endoscopic retrograde biliary stenting (ERBS) and endoscopic retrograde pancreatic stenting (ERPS), whereas one (2.1%) patient underwent ERPS only. No adverse events were associated with the insertion of a side-viewing endoscope or with catheter cannulation during ERCP.

The histopathological results are shown in Table 3. The ESD group had a significantly larger specimen diameter than the EP group (36.5 vs. 13.0 mm, p<0.01). In the ESD group, 50% of lesions were adenomas and 50% were adenocarcinomas (Tis–T1a). In the EP group, 55.3% were adenomas, 34.0% were adenocarcinomas (Tis–T1a), and 10.7% were other findings (hyperplasia, atypia, or inflamed mucosa). No T1b or lymphovascular invasion was detected in either group. The rate of positive or unclear horizontal margins was similar between groups (25.0% vs. 17.0%, p=0.63), while the ESD group had a significantly higher rate of positive or unclear vertical margins (75.0% vs. 19.1%, p<0.01). As a result, the R0 resection rate was significantly lower in the ESD group (12.5% vs. 63.8%, p<0.01).

Delayed adverse events and additional endoscopic treatments after endoscopic resection

The details of the delayed adverse events and additional endoscopic treatments are summarized in Table 4. No delayed adverse events were observed in the ESD group. Conversely, in the EP group, delayed perforations, which were treated conservatively, were observed in two patients (4.3%), while delayed bleeding occurred in ten patients (21.3%); bleeding was arrested using urgent endoscopy in all patients. Additionally, mild PEP, which was also treated conservatively, was observed in seven patients (14.9%). Additional endoscopic treatment was performed in patients with vertical margin positive or unclear (VM1/X) despite the absence of a macroscopic residual tumor. RFA was performed in four and two patients in the ESD and EP groups, respectively, and APC was performed in two patients in the EP group. Notably, two patients in the EP group with VM1 who were strongly suspected of having residual tumors underwent additional surgical resection without endoscopic treatment to prevent recurrence.

No local recurrence was observed in any patient in the ESD group. In contrast, five patients (10.6%) in the EP group experienced local recurrence; four of these patients underwent additional EP, while one patient received APC.

Comparison of outcomes between ESD and EP for LSPTs

The patients’ characteristics were not significantly different between the two groups, and tumor diameters were not significantly different between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1). The en bloc resection rate was significantly higher in the ESD group than in the EP group (100% vs. 40.0%, p=0.04) (Supplementary Table 2), and the VM1/X and R0 resection rates were not significantly different between the two groups (Supplementary Table 3). No delayed adverse events were observed in the ESD group, whereas delayed bleeding was observed in two patients (4.3%) in the EP group (Supplementary Table 4).

DISCUSSION

LSPTs are rarer than PTs but can be encountered in clinical practice. The recommended treatment for LSPTs is PD. Surgery is the standard option, given the high false-negative rate of endoscopic biopsy specimens for the detection of high-grade dysplasia and focal and invasive carcinoma.23-28 However, owing to its associated morbidity and mortality, surgery is a relatively extreme measure when the goal of intervention is essentially preventive. EP is relatively safe and reliable for the complete resection of major duodenal papilla adenomas.4,5,29 However, EP of the major duodenal papilla is likely to be performed during piecemeal resections, and tumors recur in up to 26% of these cases. With recent advances in techniques, duodenal ESD has been performed for LSPTs.19 However, the efficacy and safety of ESD for LSPTs remain unclear.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the outcomes of ESD and EP for LSPTs. Notably, the target lesions for ESD are LSPTs, whereas those for EP are simple duodenal PTs. Although duodenal ESD should be compared with piecemeal EP for LSPTs, we were unable to compare the outcomes of duodenal ESD with piecemeal EP for LSPTs because piecemeal EP for LSPTs was not performed at our institution. Hence, in this study, we compared ESD for LSPTs with EP for PTs, albeit with different treatment targets. This comparison was intended solely to present the treatment results of EP for PTs as a reference to objectively evaluate the treatment results of duodenal ESD for LSPTs. This was not meant to directly compare duodenal ESD for LSPTs with EP for PTs. The EP group underwent EP for PTs as the target lesions, while the duodenal ESD group underwent duodenal ESD for LSPTs as the target lesions. The tumor diameter was significantly larger in the duodenal ESD group than in the EP group (25.0 vs. 10.0 mm, p<0.01). The procedure time was significantly longer in the duodenal ESD group than in the EP group (37.5 vs. 10.0 minutes, p<0.01). However, as there was a significant difference in the tumor diameter between the two groups, it is reasonable to conclude that there was also a significant difference in the procedure time between the two groups. In contrast, the VM1/X rate was significantly higher in the ESD group than in the EP group (75.0% vs. 19.1%, p<0.01). However, the patients in the ESD group were free of recurrence and did not require additional surgical resections. Regarding adverse events, no cases of delayed perforation, delayed bleeding, or PEP were observed in the ESD group. In contrast, the EP group experienced delayed perforation, delayed bleeding, and PEP in two (4.3%), ten (21.3%), and seven (14.9%) patients, respectively. In the ESD group, ENBPD was performed as the drainage procedure in all patients, whereas the EP group underwent a combination of ERBS and ERPS or ERPS only. This suggests that the different drainage procedures may have influenced the incidence of adverse events in both groups. In the subgroup analysis, we compared the group in which EP was performed on lesions that met the definition of LSPTs with the ESD group. The en bloc resection rate was significantly higher in the ESD group than in the EP group (100% vs. 40.0%, p=0.04), and the VM1/X rate was not significantly different between the two groups, suggesting that ESD may be more efficient than EP when limited to LSPTs.

Several retrospective cohort studies have been conducted on EP for LSPTs. Sahar et al.22 reported that the piecemeal resection rate when using EP for LSPTs was 77%, which could be due to EP having a lower en bloc resection rate than ESD for LSPTs. In this study, no statistically significant difference was found in the recurrence rate between the LSPT and PT groups (8% vs. 4%, p=0.26); however, more residual adenomas were found during the first surveillance endoscopy in the LSPT group, and the total number of curative procedures required was higher. Delayed bleeding occurred more commonly after LSPT resection than after conventional adenoma resection (14% vs. 4%, p=0.02). The results of planned piecemeal EP for LSPTs have also been reported, in which the LSPTs were resected by piecemeal endoscopic mucosal resection of the laterally spreading component, followed by resection of the papilla.30,31 Klein et al.30 showed that endoscopic treatment had outcomes comparable to those of EP in terms of curative endoscopic resection and recurrence rates. However, piecemeal EP for LSPTs resulted in higher rates of intraprocedural (50% vs. 24.7%, p=0.003) and delayed bleeding (25% vs. 12.3%, p=0.08). In terms of delayed bleeding, similar findings were reported by Hopper et al.31 (30% vs. 7.7%). Although all of these treatments are effective and have low recurrence rates, they are associated with high rates of delayed and intraoperative bleeding. Thus, ESD for LSPTs may be safer than piecemeal EPs in terms of bleeding complications. ESD cauterizes the blood vessels during dissection, which may have resulted in a lower incidence of delayed bleeding.

Currently, there are only a few studies on duodenal ESD for LSPTs. Dohi et al.19 reported a case of duodenal ESD for an LSPT, indicating that ESD is an effective and safe treatment for LSPTs, with no adverse events during HM0 and VM0 resections. Fukuhara et al.32 reported the feasibility and effectiveness of ENBPD for the prevention and management of delayed adverse events after ESD for SDETs. In the present study, 11 cases involved the papilla of Vater. However, no study has summarized the outcomes of duodenal ESD for LSPTs. As a novel resection technique for LSPTs other than ESD, Takahara et al.33 evaluated a modified EP with hybrid ESD (hybrid ESD-EP) technique, consisting of a (sub) circumferential incision with partial submucosal dissection and subsequent snare resection, to attain a higher en bloc resection rate with a safer curative margin than the standard technique. En bloc resection was achieved with the hybrid ESD-EP technique in all eight patients (100%) with an HM0 rate of 100%, whereas the VMX rate was 38% (3/8), resulting in a complete resection rate of 63%. Delayed bleeding and pancreatitis occurred in 13% of patients.33 Compared with ESD outcomes and adverse events in the current study, hybrid ESD-EP had comparable results in terms of en bloc resection and lateral margins; however, delayed bleeding and PEP might be more common with hybrid ESD-EP than with ESD, suggesting that ESD is safer than hybrid ESD-EP. Additionally, the VMX rate for hybrid ESD-EP was relatively high, which is a serious issue for the hybrid technique, similar to that for ESD.

In the present study, the VM1/X rate was 66.7% in the ESD group. This is because the papilla resembles a muscle layer during ESD, which may result in shallow dissection (Fig. 2). During dissection, the muscle layer appears to rise as it approaches the papilla, resulting in potential shallow dissection (Fig. 1C). We hope that a new method for resecting LSPTs with VM0 will be developed in the future.

This study had several limitations owing to its single-center, retrospective design. First, the background characteristics of the patients were not matched between the ESD and EP groups because the target lesions were different. Second, the study population was too small to effectively compare the outcomes of the two methods and generalize the conclusions. Generally, ESD carries a higher risk of procedure-related complications than endoscopic snaring resection. The small sample size may explain the absence of delayed adverse events in the ESD group. Additionally, duodenal ESDs for LSPTs are technically challenging, limiting their application to skilled operators and making the results difficult to generalize. Therefore, the findings of the present study should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, ESD for LSPTs was performed safely without any adverse events. The VM1/X rate was significantly higher in the ESD group than in the EP group; however, the patients who underwent ESD remained recurrence-free. Additionally, the VM1/X rate was not significantly different between the ESD and EP groups for LSPTs. Therefore, ESD may be a treatment option for LSPTs; however, ESD for LSPTs requires highly skilled endoscopists, and the study population was too small. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings and determine the long-term outcomes of ESD for LSPTs.
