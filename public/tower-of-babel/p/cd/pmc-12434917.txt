Embracing embodied movement in eating disorder recovery: matters arising from Bongiorno and Heaner (2025)
Authors: James Downs, Hannah Hickinbotham, Eric Pothen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Matters Arising builds upon Bongiorno and Heaner’s (2025) narrative review of resistance training in eating disorder recovery. The authors expand the discussion by situating resistance training within a wider spectrum of embodied movement practices, such as yoga, dance, and group-based activity. Drawing on lived experience, current literature, and embodiment theory, this piece emphasises the therapeutic value of movement that enhances interoceptive awareness, emotional regulation, and body connection. The authors highlight alexithymia and interoceptive disruption as transdiagnostic features of eating disorders, suggesting that safe, intentional movement can address these challenges and support psychological as well as physical recovery. They also raise critical concerns about abstinence-based exercise approaches and advocate for co-produced, trauma-informed, and intersectionally inclusive interventions. Recognising the diversity of eating disorder presentations and identities, they call for participatory research methods and tailored movement strategies that prioritise safety, autonomy, and long-term wellbeing. The paper concludes by encouraging researchers and clinicians to view movement not as an optional adjunct, but as a potentially central component of recovery, particularly when embedded within equitable, person-centred care.Keywords: Eating disorders, Embodiment, Interoception, Alexithymia, Exercise, Physiotherapy, Trauma-informed care, Epistemic justice, Lived experience, Co-production

Introduction

As people with lived experience across a range of eating disorder (ED) diagnoses, we welcome Bongiorno and Heaner’s narrative review investigating the use of resistance training in ED recovery [1]. We write this Matters Arising piece to build on the authors’ important research focus, offering additional theoretical and empirical evidence alongside reflections from our own lived experiences. In doing so, we aim to broaden how exercise is conceptualised within ED treatment, and add to growing understanding of movement as a therapeutic tool in ED recovery.

Despite our differing positionalities, shaped by diverse lived experiences, diagnoses and identities, we share a commitment to the inclusion of appropriately supervised and intentional movement-based interventions within ED treatment. We advocate moving away from default abstinence-based approaches to exercise. This shift is needed not only to support clinical recovery, as defined by the remission of symptoms, but to support the development of a long-term beneficial relationship with movement. As humans, our existence is bound up with movement—though its form, extent, and meaning vary across individuals—and recovery involves learning how to be, and to move, in one’s body with a greater sense of care and safety.

While Bongiorno and Heaner rightly emphasise the promise of resistance training, we advocate for an expansion of this field of research and clinical practice to include the broader spectrum of embodied movement practices, tailored to the wide-ranging needs of those who experience EDs. Co-producing further research and movement-based interventions with people with lived experience will support the field’s progress towards creating a more inclusive and representative evidence base for more acceptable and effective forms of treatment [2, 3].

Beyond resistance training: an embodied movement spectrum

Our theoretical stance centralises the embodied nature of EDs, which we understand in line with Piran and Teall’s (2012) description of embodiment as ‘the experience of living as a body engaging with the world’ [4]. As such, recovery from EDs can be understood, at least in part, as restoring a connected, embodied sense of self. This includes dimensions such as interoception and bodily awareness, which are frequently experienced differently amongst those with EDs [5]. Similarly, individual differences in how one experiences their body can arise from a range of intersectional factors such as gender, race, neurodivergence, and co-occurring health conditions, amongst others. Recent ethnographic research also demonstrates how sensory environments can profoundly shape treatment experiences; in particular, how inpatient sensory landscapes can amplify feelings of entrapment and ambivalence toward both illness and treatment [6]. Taken together, these perspectives highlight that embodiment is not a fixed or universal experience, but a dynamic and contextual process that must be considered in both research and treatment.

Bongiorno and Heaner’s review highlights how resistance training is associated with improved bone density, muscle mass, and strength in individuals recovering from restrictive EDs and underweight status [1]. Multiple included studies showed increases in skeletal muscle mass, strength-to-bodyweight ratios, and lean body mass following resistance training interventions, particularly those involving higher loading protocols [7, 8]. Yet, in addition to the physiological improvements that exercise-based rehabilitation can bring, situating resistance training within a broader spectrum of embodied movement practices can also help attend to the psychological, sensory, and emotional dimensions of recovery. An embodiment perspective invites us to look beyond strength and bone health alone, to consider how different forms of movement may foster interoceptive awareness, emotional regulation, and a renewed sense of agency in relation to the body.

Examples that resonate with our personal experiences include;

Yoga: Cook-Cottone et al. (2025) found that a virtual embodiment-focused yoga intervention significantly improved interoceptive attunement, emotion regulation, and body appreciation within a randomised controlled trial of a yoga intervention for those with EDs [9]. These findings echo experiences published by author JD, which detail the use of yoga as a means of rebuilding a relationship with movement grounded in mindful sensation, acceptance, and presence, rather than in control or compensation [10]. This gradual re-exposure to embodied states that were once feared enabled a gentle process of greater self-regulation and connection.

Dance: Despite evidence of increased risk and prevalence of EDs among professional dancers [11], Dance Movement Therapy has shown promise as a movement-based therapeutic modality for people with EDs [12]. For instance, Bastoni et al. (2023) found that Dance Movement Therapy significantly improved interoceptive awareness and emotional regulation while reducing alexithymia in young women with EDs [13]. While further research is needed, current findings suggest that dance-based approaches, when delivered in safe, supportive, and non-judgmental settings, may foster greater embodiment and emotional reconnection. Such approaches provide space for individuals to experience their bodies as vehicles for meaning-making and self-expression, rather than objects of shame, scrutiny, or perfectibility [14]. This can be especially important for those whose sense of agency or emotional voice has been muted by illness.

For example, Dittmer et al. (2018) developed a manualised group intervention integrating cognitive-behavioural therapy, exercise therapy, and exposure with response management [16]. These results suggest that group movement therapies can effectively address both psychological and behavioural challenges in ED recovery while fostering social support and a sense of shared experience. However, larger studies show that while multi-component group treatments produce moderate improvements, their advantages over other treatments remain small [17], indicating more research and refinement are needed to optimise the benefits of group-based approaches to exercise.

Whilst resistance training can offer unique physical benefits to patients, it is clear that it represents only one part of a broader spectrum of movement-based practices demonstrating therapeutic promise in the context of ED treatment. The psychological, social, and emotional dimensions of recovery demand that clinicians and researchers investigate how a range of other approaches can foster positive embodiment, social connection, and emotion regulation across diverse presentations and groups. As author EP reflects, recovery involved redefining movement as joyful, communal, and free from hierarchy:

“One of the most important parts of my own recovery from an eating disorder was redefining what movement is and my relationship with it. I had created a movement hierarchy, where higher intensity workouts were ‘better,’ and I feared engaging in other forms because they wouldn’t have been ‘effective’ in the eyes of my eating disorder. Deeper into recovery, I began to dismantle that hierarchy and could see that no one form of movement is better than another. You can move without an aesthetic goal, simply because it brings joy, and there is something especially healing about being in a space of movement with others. I’m a firm believer that healing happens in community, not in isolation.”

Building interoceptive awareness and positive embodiment

Understanding the rewards

Regardless of modality, interoceptive awareness appears to be a central mechanism through which embodied practices may facilitate recovery. Interoception refers to the capacity to sense, interpret, and respond to internal bodily signals such as hunger, fullness, breath, pain, or heartbeat [18]. Emerging research points towards a crucial role for interoception across psychiatric conditions, implicated in the aetiology, maintenance, and treatment of illness [19].

In the context of EDs, differences in interoceptive awareness may relate to the well-documented presence of alexithymia across ED diagnoses, i.e. difficulty in identifying, describing, or distinguishing bodily feelings and emotional states [5, 20]. Alexithymia may function as a transdiagnostic risk factor, feature of illness, and barrier to recovery, interacting with disrupted interoception in a mutually reinforcing way: when individuals struggle to make sense of internal sensations, they may also find it harder to regulate emotions, engage relationally, or participate fully in therapy [21, 22]. Whilst reduced interoceptive awareness may contribute to emotional and bodily disconnection via alexithymic traits, the complex interplay between alexithymia, disconnection, and impaired interoception in EDs may also indicate an important treatment target [23, 24].

The findings of Bongiorno and Heaner’s review support this, indicating the therapeutic potential of interoceptive engagement in the context of resistance training [1]. Through proprioceptive feedback and repeated exposure to physical sensation, progressive resistance training can provide graded, structured opportunities for individuals to reconnect with their bodies in ways that feel measurable, tolerable, and empowering. The authors’ qualitative findings support this idea, with participants reporting how resistance training helped shift their focus away from weight loss and toward strength, citing the emphasis on building strength as an empowering motivator that deemphasises weight loss [1].

This reflects an important therapeutic shift for those with EDs, shifting from appearance and external measures of the body to a focus on functionality, awareness, and internal sensation. Author JD’s experiences align with this, as he describes:

“Movement practices like yoga and dance gave me accessible ways to be mindfully aware of my body without becoming overwhelmed. They offered something structured and repetitive to ‘do’, which really helped me as an autistic person, and created a space where I could observe sensations and listen to my body’s feedback. This felt like a kind of graded exposure: the movement could be distracting and soothing, while still gently inviting me to reconnect. Being in a room with others, moving in parallel or shared rhythm, also helped me feel less alone in my body. Over time, learning to move with my body rather than against it helped reduce fear and build a new sense of trust and safety in myself.”

Awareness of the risks

Despite these promising benefits, it is important to acknowledge the risks of exercise for some with EDs, including physical risks and the potential interplay with unhelpful psychological and behavioural patterns such as compulsive exercising [25, 26]. For individuals who have experienced trauma, emotional numbing, or sensory overload, the process of re-inhabiting the body can also be emotionally demanding, provoking distress, overwhelm, or avoidance. Trauma-informed movement practices must therefore proceed gradually, with sensitivity to physical, emotional, and neurobiological boundaries. This can include attending to sensory sensitivities, offering predictable and choice-based environments, and respecting bodily autonomy and consent throughout [27].

Crucially, prohibiting movement during treatment can itself be experienced as harmful, as we have found in our own experience. Author HH reflects:

“Abstinence from exercise simply applies a plaster over the ‘issue’. In my experience, exercise was never the problem, but instead used as a tool to navigate complex emotions. If we ban exercise, new, predominantly unhealthy behaviours develop to manage emotions. By working alongside patients to explore the role of exercise in their ED experience, beneficial results can be achieved in developing a “healthy” relationship with exercise. It also allows for individuals to reconnect with their body in a new way, developing gratitude for what their body helps them to achieve and engage in the development of new pieces of their identity, previously encapsulated by an eating disorder and compulsive exercise.”

As this quote illustrates, total bans on exercise may reinforce bodily mistrust, avoidance, and anxiety, contradicting principles of graded exposure and leaving individuals without the tools to engage with movement safely. In such circumstances, movement may continue in secrecy, compulsivity, or shame, rather than being transformed into something nourishing or self-supportive.

Similarly, in being unsupported in navigating their relationship with movement and exercise, patients may be less protected from dominant narratives within the commercial fitness and wellness industries, as well as on social media platforms, which frequently promote punitive, aestheticised, and ableist ideals of health [28]. Such messages can be especially challenging for those who have experienced EDs and may undermine efforts towards recovery [29].

In our collective experience, the risks of excluding movement-based interventions from standard treatment protocols are therefore twofold. Firstly, the absence of structured support around exercise can leave illness-driven behaviours unchallenged, allowing them to persist unchecked. Secondly, this approach may deny individuals access to recovery-facilitating experiences of movement that allow for greater bodily awareness, functional confidence, emotional expression, creativity, and connection. When approached safely and relationally, restoring movement can be more than just an adjunct to treatment; it can be part of what makes recovery easier to inhabit.

Recommendations for research and practice

1. Co-producing trauma-informed movement interventions.

We commend the authors’ emphasis on multidisciplinary teams as a foundation for integrating movement into eating disorder treatment. However, we argue that this must be taken further: toward genuinely co-produced, participatory interventions that are trauma-informed, inclusive, and person-centred.

Co-production goes beyond consultation or tokenistic inclusion. It reflects a commitment to epistemic justice: recognising lived experience not simply as anecdotal or supplementary, but as a valid and necessary form of knowledge in its own [2]. In the context of ED care, epistemic justice demands a rebalancing of power in how knowledge is created, interventions are designed, and care is delivered [3]. This means involving people with lived experience, especially those marginalised by treatment systems, in all stages of design, delivery, and evaluation of movement-based approaches. Rather than positioning patients as passive recipients of expertise, co-production enables them to act as epistemic agents who shape interventions according to their own needs, identities, and priorities.

It also means recognising the environmental and sensory dimensions of recovery. As shown in the ethnographic work of Chubinidze et al. (2025), the physical and sensory landscapes of inpatient settings can be sources of distress and disembodiment [6]. Movement interventions that are introduced into these settings without considering spatial, sensory, and interpersonal dynamics may replicate or even intensify these harms. Trauma-informed co-production must therefore attend not only to what the intervention is, but where and how it is experienced.

Importantly, co-produced interventions must allow for flexibility across recovery trajectories, helping to ensure that movement is not imposed in rigid ways, but introduced safely and meaningfully, on terms defined by those who will experience it. Some people may benefit from carefully adapted movement early in their recovery; others may need to focus on stabilisation before safely re-engaging with their bodies. Preferences will vary depending on sensory sensitivities, trauma histories, neurotype, gender identity, and other intersecting factors considered below.

2. Attending to intersectionality and co-occurring conditions.

A key limitation in the reviewed literature is its minimal attention to the intersecting realities of co-occurring conditions, neurodiversity, diverse identities, and structural marginalisation—a common problem across ED research [30]. Many people with EDs live with overlapping psychiatric, physical, and neurodevelopmental conditions, as well as minoritised identities, all of which can shape how movement is experienced and what constitutes safety, accessibility, and benefit. These factors include but are not limited to:

Hypermobility: Research findings suggest joint hypermobility is frequently present among individuals with EDs [31], which can increase the risk of joint injury during high-loading exercise. Movement interventions for hypermobile individuals may therefore need adaptation, and can be safer when they focus on proprioception, joint stability, and controlled eccentric loading, rather than maximal strength training.

While the structured, repetitive, and quantifiable nature of resistance training may appeal to some, others may find typical gym environments overwhelming or inaccessible. Tailored adaptations for autistic patients that have already shown benefit in ED treatment could be similarly applied in the context of exercise-based interventions. This may include providing predictable routines, sensory-friendly environments, and flexible communication styles [33].

Our view is that accommodating these overlapping and diverse factors impacting movement participation (which are not exhaustive) requires a fundamental departure from one-size-fits-all approaches. Rather than prescribing standardised movement protocols, clinicians and services must adopt adaptive, individualised strategies grounded in collaboration and cultural humility. Skilled facilitation, multidisciplinary input, and clinical supervision are essential, alongside Bongiorno and Heaner’s calls for greater research into the experiences of underrepresented groups.

3. Research recommendations.

Building on the authors’ call for further research, we propose the following priorities for future studies in this area to ensure they are inclusive, mechanistically informative, and grounded in lived experience:

Longitudinal outcomes: Most existing studies focus on short-term physiological or psychological changes. Long-term research is needed on how movement interventions influence interoceptive awareness, embodiment, quality of life, and recovery sustainability.

Conclusion

Bongiorno and Heaner’s review makes an important contribution to our understanding of resistance training’s potential role in eating disorder recovery. Their synthesis of physiological and psychological benefits, particularly from higher loading protocols, offers compelling evidence for rethinking the longstanding restrictions placed on movement in treatment settings.

In this Matters Arising response, we have aimed to build on their work by broadening the frame: situating resistance training within a wider landscape of embodied movement, and foregrounding interoception, co-production, trauma-informed practice, and intersectional inclusivity as essential principles for future development.

From our perspective, the question is no longer whether resistance training should be included in eating disorder treatment, but how we support people to be - and move - in their bodies with a greater sense of safety, trust, and vitality. This requires moving beyond one-size-fits-all models and engaging more deeply with the diversity of bodies, identities, and lived experiences that shape recovery.

Future research must prioritise participatory methods that centre lived expertise, explore long-term and embodied outcomes, and develop movement interventions that are not only effective, but equitable, affirming, and sustainable. Only through such comprehensive and inclusive approaches can we begin to realise the full potential of movement as a pathway to healing.
