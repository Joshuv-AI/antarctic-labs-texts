Improving neurological deficits secondary to transverse myelitis using epidural spinal cord stimulation: illustrative cases
Authors: Rishabh Gupta, Reid Johnson, Han Gil Jeong, David Balser, Afsar Sandozi, Samuel W Cramer, and Uzma Samadani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEpidural spinal cord stimulation (eSCS) is canonically used for the treatment of intractable, chronic pain. Recent efforts have successfully utilized eSCS to improve neurological deficits in patients with spinal cord injury. However, there is a paucity of literature on the use of eSCS for demyelinating conditions, with no prior case reports examining eSCS among patients with transverse myelitis (TM).OBSERVATIONSTwo patients with TM underwent eSCS and experienced improvements in motor function and bladder symptoms. The first patient exhibited enhanced motor strength in both lower extremities and no longer experienced leg spasms, increasing exercise capacity and decreasing their fall risk. The patient had reduced incontinence pad usage due to a regained ability to sense bladder fullness. The second patient also experienced improved motor scores, leading to enhanced motor functionality and independence. Furthermore, the patient observed reductions in urinary tract infections post-eSCS. Neither patient reported substantial improvement in bowel function following stimulation.LESSONSImprovements in motor functionality and bladder functioning are well-documented as factors that improve quality of life among paraplegic patients. Given the findings of the present case report, larger cohort studies examining the use of eSCS for demyelinating conditions, including TM, are warranted. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24152 Keywords: epidural spinal cord stimulation, paraplegia, demyelinationABBREVIATIONS: CRPS = complex regional pain syndrome, DF = dorsiflexion, eSCS = epidural spinal cord stimulation, HF = hip flexion, KE = knee extension, KF = knee flexion, LLE = left lower extremity, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging, RLE = right lower extremity, RUE = right upper extremity, SCI = spinal cord injury, TM = transverse myelitis, UTI = urinary tract infection.

Epidural spinal cord stimulation (eSCS) was first reported in 1967 as a successful treatment for chronic pain, a finding replicated in subsequent studies.1–4 Interest in using eSCS to treat both motor and nonmotor symptoms of spinal cord injury (SCI) developed following a case report demonstrating these improvements in a patient with motor-complete SCI.5 Since then, a growing body of evidence has affirmed that eSCS can result in improved motor and nonmotor outcomes in patients with SCI.6–9

The current literature predominantly examines the efficacy of eSCS in treating traumatic SCI. The efficacy of eSCS for other SCI etiologies, particularly nontraumatic conditions like transverse myelitis (TM), has received inadequate study. TM is defined by focal inflammation of the spinal cord resulting in 4 classic features: upper- and/or lower-extremity weakness, pain, sensory alterations, and bowel and bladder dysfunction.10 Complete recovery occurs in only one-third of patients, with an additional one-third experiencing residual deficits, and the final third having poor outcomes, including a nonambulatory status, incontinence, and severe sensory sequelae.11, 12 Few therapeutic options exist for the two-thirds of patients who do not experience a full recovery. The purpose of the present report is to describe 2 patients with residual TM symptoms, including motor function deficits, who experienced improvements following eSCS device placement.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

Initial Presentation

A 70-year-old woman received a diagnosis of TM following 3 months of Bordetella pertussis infection. Imaging indicated TM lesions extending between T4 and T10. The patient endorsed complete loss of voluntary lower-extremity movement, was fully insensate below T6 with significantly hampered proprioception, and was unable to sense bowel and bladder fullness or independently evacuate her bowel and bladder. To date, no additional demyelinating episodes have occurred, nor have brain lesions been identified.

Partial Recovery

The patient underwent several treatments prior to eSCS with partial success. Initial treatments included 5 plasmapheresis sessions, a steroid regimen, Botox, and neurorehabilitation. Over several months, motor scores improved and then plateaued for the years following. Motor scores acquired 1 week prior to eSCS for the right lower extremity (RLE) were hip flexion (HF) 4, knee flexion (KF) 4, knee extension (KE) 4, and dorsiflexion (DF) 4. For the left lower extremity (LLE), scores were HF 2, KF 3, KE 3−, and DF 2. Functionally, the patient regained the ability to ambulate short distances with a walker and cane but reported continued difficulty walking uphill, downhill, or at a slant without leg spasms. In terms of sensation, the patient fully regained her ability to feel touch; however, temperature, peripheral pain, and proprioception remained significantly altered or absent. She developed neuropathic pain, which followed a saddle distribution. The patient also reported continued bowel and bladder complaints. She remained unable to sense bladder fullness and developed bladder incontinence, requiring catheterization 6 times/day and the continuous wearing of incontinence pads. InterStim for bladder incontinence was trialed but worsened the neuropathic pain and could not be utilized long term. The patient also experienced delayed perception of bowel fullness, requiring urgency in evacuation. Only partial bowel emptying was possible without digital stimulation, and approximately 10 minutes 2 times/day was required to evacuate.

In the decade following TM, the patient developed several comorbidities, some directly resulting from TM-related deficits. Most notably, she developed extensive upper-extremity musculoskeletal injuries due to overuse during transitions from sitting to standing and while walking with a cane. Upper-extremity injuries also resulted from multiple falls due to leg spasms. Diagnoses included bilateral shoulder bursitis, bilateral rotator cuff tears, bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, and a left triangular fibrocartilage complex tear. Due to asymmetrical muscle atrophy from TM-induced paraplegia, the patient also developed a spinal deformity requiring lateral fusion at L2–4 and posterior fusion from T10 to S1. Ten years after the TM diagnosis, she experienced a transient ischemic attack and myocardial infarction.

Operative Details

The patient underwent eSCS device implantation with a primary indication to treat complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) resulting from TM. The patient’s magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is shown in Fig. 1. A trial of eSCS was not conducted before device implantation. She underwent a T11–12 laminectomy for eSCS device implantation, placement of an implantable pulse generator, and removal of the InterStim using standard procedures and neuromonitoring 11 years after the TM diagnosis. A T11–12 laminectomy with paddle placement around T12 was chosen, as this was the protocol conducted in the Epidural Stimulation After Neurologic Damage (E-STAND) trial and has been reported in the literature. Additionally, the surgeon optimized the position of the lead along the conus to enable stimulation of individual muscle groups of the lower extremities from the iliopsoas to the anal sphincter.8, 13, 14 Figure 2 features anteroposterior radiographic imaging of intraoperative eSCS device placement.

Postoperative Course

The patient experienced immediate improvement in motor strength upon postoperative eSCS activation. As the patient, physical therapist, and operating neurosurgeon began to discover the optimal stimulation parameters for the patient, she experienced continual improvement in motor strength. The most recent postoperative motor scores as of early spring of last year indicated that motor strength had improved to the RLE (HF, KF, KE, DF: 4+, 4+, 4, 4, respectively) and LLE (HF, KF, KE, DF: 3, 3+, 3−, 3−). The patient is continuing to see improvements as parameters are further optimized and is receiving routine follow-up appointments. Current stimulation parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Improvements in motor scores have translated to improved functionality in several ways. Critically, eSCS improved the patient’s ability to use her lower extremities when transitioning from sitting to standing and during walking. Given the patient’s extensive history of upper-extremity musculoskeletal overuse injuries, her ability to rely more on her lower extremities was necessary to decrease future morbidity and preserve her quality of life. Additionally, the patient experienced improvement in leg spasms, which had resulted from sensory stimuli, the primary reason for her multiple falls. The patient no longer experiences these spasms while walking on different inclines, allowing for greater mobility on different surfaces and environments and a decreased fall risk. eSCS also improved the patient’s exercise capacity, allowing her to walk for longer distances and at a faster rate. The benefits of exercise in preventing vascular incidents are well established.15, 16 Given the patient’s history of several vascular incidents, including a transient ischemic attack and a myocardial infarction, improved exercise capacity may assist in preventing or delaying future incidents.

In addition to motor improvements, the patient endorsed significant improvement in bladder function following eSCS. Bladder function among SCI patients has been established as a meaningful contributor to a good quality of life.17, 18 The patient reduced both the size and number of incontinence pads required by approximately 50%. The primary reason for this was her regained ability to sense bladder fullness, allowing for less urinary leakage. Given the continued bladder sphincter dysfunction, catheterization remained at 6 times/day.

It should be noted that the patient experienced minimal improvement in several domains, including sensation, although saddle region neuropathic pain improved following eSCS. Additionally, the patient did not experience improvements in bowel function.

Case 2

Initial Presentation

A 53-year-old, previously physically active female experienced the acute onset of TM. The patient did not have any diagnosed medical comorbidities nor any pertinent family history, including autoimmune or demyelinating conditions. The likely cause of the TM remains unidentified. Imaging indicated 2 independent TM lesions extending between C7 and T1 on the right and T1 and T3 on the left. Upon initial presentation, the patient endorsed a complete loss of bilateral lower-extremity motor strength, with motor scores for HF at 0, KF at 0, KE at 0, and DF at 0. The patient experienced mild right upper-extremity (RUE) weakness, with all muscle groups at 5/5 except for finger flexors and finger abductors at 4/5. In terms of sensation, the patient endorsed the loss of light touch, temperature, proprioception, and pain in the bilateral lower extremities, whereas the RUE sensation remained intact. She also developed a neurogenic bladder resulting in urinary retention and continuous catheterization as an inpatient, as well as a reflex neurogenic bowel, which required suppositories to assist in bowel evacuation once a day.

Partial Recovery

The patient underwent extensive treatments before receiving eSCS. Trialed medications included steroids, baclofen, gabapentin, oxybutynin, and duloxetine. Additional nonpharmacological treatments included neurorehabilitation, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, and transcranial magnetic stimulation. Notably, the patient did not undergo in-hospital plasmapheresis. The patient experienced full resolution of the RUE motor symptoms; however, scores for her HF, KF, KE, and DF in the RLE remained at 0, 0, 0, and 0, and her LLE only marginally improved to 0, 0, 0, and 1, respectively. Thus, functionality reliant on lower-extremity movement remained profoundly impaired. The patient also developed additional motor symptoms, including spasticity and rigidity. There was partial preservation of light touch; however, the patient developed severe neuropathic pain at the level of the nipple line descending into her RLE and LLE, later diagnosed as CRPS. The patient continued to suffer from neurogenic bladder and neurogenic bowel. Following initial neurogenic shock, the patient experienced bladder leakage requiring catheterization 8 times/day and experienced frequent urinary tract infections (UTIs). A neurogenic bowel, requiring suppositories once daily, continued for the patient following hospital discharge.

Operative Details

Thirteen years after the acute onset of TM, the patient underwent eSCS device placement with a primary indication to treat CRPS following TM. The patient’s MRI studies are shown in Fig. 3. A trial of eSCS was not conducted before implantation. She underwent a T11–12 laminectomy and placement of the eSCS device and the implantable pulse generator. The T11–12 laminectomy and paddle placement were conducted as per the rationale provided for the first patient. Postoperative Details

The patient experienced immediate activation of the quadriceps muscles upon postoperative eSCS activation. As the patient, physical therapist, and operating neurosurgeon began optimizing stimulation parameters for the patient in follow-up visits, continual improvement in motor strength was observed. The most recent postoperative motor scores as of early 2024 indicated motor strength for both the RLE and LLE (HF, KF, KE, and DF) improved to 0, 1, 5, and 3, respectively, with the stimulator turned on. Current stimulation parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Benefits in motor scores translated to improvements in motor functionality. The patient regained the ability to independently execute a stand-pivot-transfer sequence using preset stimulation parameters, which allowed transfer to and from a vehicle, chair-to-chair transfers (e.g., workstation, dining table, etc.), and transfer to and from a bed. The patient endorsed that these actions significantly improved her independence, including the ability to leave the house with minimal assistance. The patient observed additional benefits in her sitting posture.

Significant benefits in CPRS symptoms were qualitatively observed by the patient. Additionally, the patient observed a meaningful reduction in UTIs, although this was not quantified. Catheterization for a neurogenic bladder and suppository use for bowel function remained the same as preoperatively. The benefits the patient experienced allowed for a reduction in most medications, including gabapentin, ciprofloxacin, and baclofen.

Discussion

Observations

In the present paper, we report improvement in motor outcomes, neurogenic bladder symptoms, and neuropathic pain in 2 patients with longstanding TM who received eSCS. Motor scores for the first patient in her RLE (HF, KF, KE, and DF) improved from 4, 4, 4, and 4 to 4+, 4+, 4, and 4 and in her LLE from 2, 3, 3−, and 2 to 3, 3+, 3−, and 3−. The patient endorsed decreased reliance on her upper extremities, improvement in leg spasms, and an improved exercise capacity. Motor scores for the second patient in her RLE (HF, KF, KE, and DF) improved from 0, 0, 0, and 0 to 0, 1, 5, and 3 and in her LLE from 0, 0, 0, and 1 to 0, 1, 5, and 3. Functional improvements the patient experienced included regaining the ability to independently execute a stand-pivot-transfer sequence, allowing for independence in leaving the house. The patient also experienced an improved ability to maintain a sitting posture. Both patients endorsed improved neurogenic bladder symptoms. The first patient regained her ability to sense bladder fullness, allowing for a reduction in the size and number of incontinence pads, whereas the second patient experienced fewer UTIs. Additionally, both patients experienced improvements in their neuropathic pain. Neither patient observed meaningful improvement in bowel function following eSCS implantation surgery.

Prior Literature

There is limited literature examining the use of eSCS to assist in the functional recovery of patients with TM, aside from one study with low scientific rigor published in 1978.19 Two additional case reports demonstrated improvements in TM neuropathic pain following eSCS device implantation, but somatic motor or bladder function was not discussed.20, 21 The improvements demonstrated in the present case report align with those observed in patients receiving eSCS for traumatic SCI, with multiple studies demonstrating improvements in volitional motor movements following eSCS device placement.22–25 Greater variability and less clarity exist regarding the benefits eSCS has on bladder function. A prior case report has demonstrated amplification of reflex voiding, a finding in contrast to that seen in the first patient in the present report.26 An additional study has reported decreased volitional bladder control following eSCS.27 The mechanism of this recovery is even more poorly understood than motor recovery, and there may be conflicting effects on bladder control, such as changes in SCI-related autonomic dysfunction, stimulating involuntary reflex voiding that increases incontinence, and possible secondary activation of the nerve roots mimicking the beneficial effects of sacral neuromodulation on urinary control.26–29 Further research efforts in larger populations that examine the use of eSCS to improve the functionality and quality of life in TM patients are warranted.

Lessons

The 2 patients presented here provide preliminary evidence that eSCS can assist in improving neurological deficits among patients with TM who do not experience full recovery. This report provides preliminary justification to initiate larger studies examining whether TM patients experience improvements in quality of life that result from and outweigh the risks of eSCS. Larger studies would also be essential to gather more objective data points and determine specific symptoms that improve, the degree to which they improve following stimulation, the stimulation parameters that optimize patient outcomes, and how to integrate eSCS with existing therapeutic modalities.
