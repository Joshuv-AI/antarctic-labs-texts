New Machine Learning Models for Predicting the Organic Cation Transporters OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3 Uptake
Authors: Giovanni Bocci, Neann Mathai, Benjamin Suutari, Jonathan Harrison, Stefanie Speichert, Major Gooyit, Douglas E. V. Pires, John P. Overington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organic cation transporters (OCTs) are a small family of transmembrane proteins that regulate the pharmacokinetics (PK) of natural metabolites and xenobiotics by facilitating drug uptake and elimination. Measuring the modulation (either inhibition or substrate) of OCTs by small molecules requires expensive experiments. More cost-effective in silico models that accurately predict OCT-mediated uptake would enable the forecasting of potential PK liabilities of new drug candidates at an early stage. In this paper, we present new machine learning (ML) models to predict the uptake of OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3. Built using advanced decision tree ensemble algorithms and VolSurf molecular features, these models are based on the largest and most well-curated data sets available in the current literature. Several rounds of validation with different external test sets have confirmed the predictive power of these models, with Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) values above 0.45. We believe that these models will shed new light on the impact of OCTs on drug discovery and development.

Introduction

The organic cation transporters 1, 2, and 3 (OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3), also known as solute carrier family 22 member 1, 2, and 3 (SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3), are among the most relevant proteins affecting the pharmacokinetics (PK) of new chemical entities (NCEs) in the human body. All three transporters facilitate the transport of both endogenous and exogenous cationic substances across cell membranes. Each of the three transporters has its distinctive features. OCT1 and OCT2 have a sequence homology of ∼70%, suggesting that the overlap of compounds they interact with is relatively large. However, OCT1 is primarily expressed in the sinusoidal membrane of hepatocytes (liver), whereas OCT2 is mostly present in the basolateral membrane of proximal tubule cells (kidneys). Therefore, if a compound is transported by OCT1 or OCT2 in vitro, it might not necessarily be transported in vivo by the same transporters. In fact, depending on the compound’s major clearance mechanism, it might interact with either OCT1 (hepatic/biliary clearance), only with OCT2 (renal clearance), or with both (mixed clearance). In contrast to OCT1 and OCT2, OCT3 does not have a primary tissue of expression and can be found in a broad range of tissues where OCT1 and OCT2 are less present (e.g., placenta, testis, brain, lung, intestine, etc.). Moreover, OCT3 shares a sequence homology of only ∼50% with OCT1 and OCT2, which would be expected to be associated with a lower functional similarity of interacting compounds compared to the other two transporters. As OCTs mediate drug uptake and elimination, and hence, could potentially alter the PK and efficacy of NCEs, early identification of OCT substrates is a key consideration in drug development.

Compound uptake by OCTs is typically assessed in vitro using human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells transfected with the specific OCT or an empty vector. The amount of compound taken up by cells is quantified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), and an uptake ratio (uptake in OCT-transfected cells/uptake in empty vector) is calculated to determine the substrate potential of the compound. Such experiments can be quite expensive and are generally conducted in the later stages of drug development. Computational techniques using in silico machine learning (ML) models had been proposed to understand and predict the interaction between small molecules and OCT transporters in a more cost-efficient manner. However, these studies are primarily based on OCT1 and OCT2 , inhibition data, which is significantly more available than uptake data in the public domain. OCT2 in particular is one of the most clinically relevant transporters, and its inhibition can cause concerns over potential drug–drug interactions (DDI). Hence, predicting the level of inhibition of OCT2 at early stages lowers the possibility of incurring more severe toxicity at later stages.

In this paper, we use the OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3 uptake data recently made available by Christof Dücker’s lab , to develop a series of in silico ML models to predict the OCT-mediated transport of NCEs. These single-task learning (STL) classification models provide a differentiated range of tools for fully addressing the complex problem of estimating the interaction between small molecules and promiscuous proteins. In the case of OCT2 uptake data, the existence of multiple substrate binding sites has been suggested by previous studies. , Therefore, we use a series of decision tree ensemble, support vector, and neural network ML methods, which are more suitable than linear algorithms in modeling nonlinear structure–activity relationships (SAR), such as the presence of multiple binding site data in the same data set. We expect that this study will facilitate the understanding and prediction of the interactions between OCTs and potential NCEs.

Methods

All the data science operations were performed using Python v3.9.18. For the ML-specific tasks (such as data splitting and model building), unless stated otherwise, scikit-learn v1.3.2 was used. − For reproducibility purposes, the random seed used throughout the study was set to 0.

Data Collection

Data on the OCT1-, OCT2-, and OCT3-mediated uptake was collected and curated from preexisting literature. Data sets for this task were derived from two recent papers, , including a collection of 393 unique compounds screened for OCT-mediated uptake experiments. The OCT1 data set consists of 273 uptake measurements, while the OCT2 and OCT3 data sets consist of 264 uptake measurements. The compound collection mostly includes Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs. The result type returned by uptake experiments is the uptake ratio (UR), calculated by dividing the uptake of a compound in HEK293 cells overexpressing the transporter protein by the uptake of the same compound in mock-transfected HEK293 cells. The uptake data and SMILES and compound names were extracted from the Supporting Information files of the original publications and merged for further analysis.

We applied a 2-fold cutoff to UR values to identify in vitro substrates (UR ≥ 2) and nonsubstrates (UR < 2), as suggested by the FDA guidelines for the industry, which resulted in 91, 97, and 89 compounds classified as substrates of OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, respectively, and 182, 167, and 175 compounds classified as nonsubstrates of OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, respectively (see Tables and S1).

Chemical Structures Preprocessing

The OpenEye toolkit v2022.2.2 was used to standardize SMILES strings. First, salts were stripped; then, SMILES were normalized at pH 7.4 by calculating the most abundant tautomer and protomer. Finally, SMILES were returned in the OpenEye canonical atom ordering. Before calculating the most abundant protomer, we applied simple data quality checks based on molecular weight (MW), number of heavy atoms (NHA), aromatic ring count (AROM), hydrogen bond acceptor count (HBA), hydrogen bond donor count (HBD), rotatable bond count (ROTB), and topological polar surface area (TPSA) to ensure compound structures met certain drug-likeness criteria: 100 < MW (g/mol) < 1000, 7 < NHA < 100, 3 < TPSA (Å²) < 300, AROM ≤ 6, HBA ≤ 14, HBD ≤ 8, ROTB ≤ 15. Thirteen compounds were excluded for not complying with at least one of the above-mentioned criteria. The data set did not have any duplicates, which led to a final set of 176 nonsubstrates and 89 substrates (265 compounds in total) for OCT1, 162 nonsubstrates and 95 substrates (257 compounds in total) for OCT2, and 171 nonsubstrates and 86 substrates (257 compounds in total) for OCT3 (Tables and S1).

ML Training and Test Sets

Given the large overlap of measurements across OCTs, the preprocessed data set, totaling 380 unique compounds, was randomly split into a training set (308 compounds, 80% of the initial data) and a test set (77 compounds, 20% of the initial data). Three STL training and test sets were identified in the subsets of the OCT-specific fraction of the above training and test sets (Figure ). We then generated 5 validation splits within the initial training set of 308 compounds to be used for model selection and optimization (see Figure S1). To ensure that the folds were made by preserving the percentage of samples for each class, we used stratified shuffle splits as implemented in the scikit-learn StratifiedShuffleSplit method.

Model Features and Scaling

We used 2D fingerprints and physical chemistry molecular descriptors to describe the chemical structures. The OpenEye toolkit was used to calculate extended connectivity fingerprints with a diameter of 6 (ECFP6) and a vector length of 2048. Atom Pair and Topological Torsion fingerprints were calculated using RDKit v2024.3.5. The default parameters were maintained to generate these fingerprints. The OpenEye toolkit was also used to calculate the XlogP, aromatic ring count, molecular weight, hydrogen bond acceptor count, hydrogen bond donor count, rotatable bond count, and topological polar surface area. For these descriptors, the calculation settings were kept to the default setup, except for XlogP, where the “remove formal charge” setting was changed to false. The Chemaxon calculator v22.18.0 was used to compute logD, the most acidic and most basic pK a at pH 7.4. Finally, VolSurf v1.0.1 was used to calculate 123 3D molecular descriptors. −

Prior to performing the fit and predict steps of any model, the continuous molecular descriptors were scaled by removing the mean and scaling to unit variance, as implemented in the scikit-learn StandardScaler. Successively, the test set molecular descriptors were standardized using the mean and standard deviation calculated for the training molecular descriptors.

Classification Algorithms

The aforementioned representations were used along with six classifier architectures: extra trees (ET), gradient boosting (GB), k-nearest neighbors (kNN), random forest (RF), support vector machines (SVM), and eXtreme gradient boosting (XGB). All the algorithms were used as implemented in scikit-learn with the exception of XGB which was internally implemented from a GitHub repository.

Classification Metrics

Models’ predictive performances were estimated and compared with five binary classification metrics, namely Accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Precision, Recall, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC). , Precision, Recall, and ROC-AUC were calculated for each class, and then the unweighted mean was reported (macro averaging). The use of multiple metrics in ML is justified by the need for having independent measurements for assessing model performances. Moreover, each metric can highlight different flavors of a model’s predictive power. MCC measures the overall performance of the model by considering all elements in the confusion matrix. Therefore, MCC is less sensitive to imbalanced data with respect to other metrics, such as Accuracy. The Precision metric measures how many nonsubstrates were incorrectly predicted as substrates, while Recall measures the number of substrates that were not predicted correctly. ROC-AUC is the Recall as a function of the false positive rate across different probability thresholds. Like MCC, ROC-AUC provides a good way of estimating how well the model performs, especially for highly imbalanced data. ROC-AUC was included in our analysis to directly compare the performance of the OCT3 model in this work with the one described by Gebauer and colleagues in their publication, where they reported ROC-AUC values but not MCC values.

Statistical Analysis for Model Comparison and Selection

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the performance metrics values distribution. When the data was found to be not normally distributed, we employed non-parametric approaches. For comparing the same model under different settings (e.g., same algorithm, same hyperparameters, different features), we used the dependent samples Friedman test followed by post hoc analysis with the Wilcoxon test. For comparing different models (i.e., different algorithms and different hyperparameters), we used the independent samples Kruskal–Wallis test followed by post hoc analysis with the Mann–Whitney test. All statistical tests were used as implemented in SciPy v1.11.3 , with the exception of the Mann–Whitney test, which was accessed from the scikit-posthocs package v0.8.1. ,

Model Development

For each transporter, the best-performing model was selected based on its mean MCC value resulting from the 5-fold cross-validation described in the ML training and test sets section above. The list of hyperparameters used for model optimization is available in Table S2. Finally, the predictive power of each selected model was assessed with unbiased prediction of the transporter-specific test set.

Domain of Applicability

To assess the model’s confidence in prediction, we defined a distance-based domain of applicability. If a predicted compound is far (dissimilar) from the compounds used in training, then its prediction is considered less confident. , To define the domain of applicability, we used the method previously described by Tetko et al. Briefly, a compound is considered to be out of domain if the average distance to the 5 closest training set compounds is more than D(x) + 0.5std­(x), where D(x) and std­(x) are the mean and the standard deviation of the pairwise distances between all unique pairs of compounds in the training set. To train the domain of applicability, a kNN model, as implemented in scikit-learn, was built by using the Euclidean distance and 5 neighbors as parameters.

Results and Discussion

Hyperparameter Optimization

Identifying the best model for a specific prediction task is challenging given the wide range of possible feature representations and ML methods. A common approach in ML is to select the model with the highest performance in internal validation. However, this often leads to overfitting, resulting in poor generalization to external test data. Here, we present an alternative model selection strategy that applies statistical analysis to internal validation metrics to reduce the overfitting risk.

For each transporter, we performed 5-fold cross-validation to compute MCC values for every combination of algorithms and feature sets. Each algorithm was further evaluated with multiple hyperparameter configurations (see Table S2 for details), yielding nearly 21 000 algorithm–feature combinations (or about 105 000 cross-validation fit–predict events) per transporter. This large data set enabled robust statistical comparisons to identify models that were statistically superior, rather than merely having the highest single MCC value.

The procedure selects the best-performing model within a group of similarly trained models (i.e., same algorithm and features but different hyperparameters) if that group is statistically better than other groups. Thus, a model with the highest MCC was not chosen if it belonged to a statistically underperforming group. In other words, the selected model for an end point was the one with the highest MCC in internal validation and whose group of related models also demonstrated above-average performance.

Statistical Analysis and Model Selection

The overall distributions suggest that predictive performance was higher for OCT1 and OCT2 than for OCT3. For OCT1 and OCT2, the best models achieved MCC values between 0.5 and 0.6, with some exceeding 0.6. In contrast, the top OCT3 models reached only 0.4–0.5, although these values still indicate the potential for reasonably accurate models.

Among the five feature sets evaluated, MCC trends consistently indicated that VolSurf descriptors provided the most accurate extrapolation of structure–activity relationships between chemical structures and transporter activities. Across nearly all algorithms, VolSurf-based models outperformed those using 2D fingerprints or other physicochemical descriptors.

For each OCT, we statistically assessed whether VolSurf significantly outperformed the other feature sets. First, the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality of MCC distributions for each OCT–algorithm–feature set combination. As most distributions were non-normal, we used the nonparametric Friedman test to determine if at least one feature set performed significantly differently from the others. When the Friedman test was significant, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to compare median MCC values between each feature set pair (ordered from the highest to lowest median). These tests, designed for dependent samples, allowed us to examine performance differences when only the feature set varied while the algorithm and hyperparameters were fixed. The results (Figure S2 and Table S3) showed that VolSurf ranked first far more often than any other feature set, confirming its superiority across transporters and algorithms. Consequently, VolSurf features were selected for predicting the transporter test sets.

In contrast, algorithm performance patterns were less clear. Visual inspection of MCC distributions () revealed no single algorithm consistently outperforming others. However, ensemble tree-based methods and kNN appeared less sensitive to hyperparameter variation than SVM and MLP, as evidenced by the broader MCC value ranges for the latter. Although we did not analyze which hyperparameters most influenced SVM and MLP performance, we suspect kernel and solver choices were major factors.

To formally compare algorithms, we applied a statistical approach similar to that used for feature sets, with one modification: the number of trained models differed between algorithms because of varying numbers of hyperparameter configurations (e.g., RF had more hyperparameters than SVM; see Table S2). To standardize sample sizes, we randomly selected a number of models equal to that of the algorithm with the fewest models. This created independent rather than dependent samples; therefore, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test to detect significant performance differences. When significant, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for pairwise ranking. Results (Figure S4 and Table S4) revealed patterns not obvious by inspection: (i) OCT1: XGB ranked first in most comparisons. (ii) OCT2: ET and RF were tied first, each leading in an equal number of cases. (iii) OCT3: ET was the top performer in most of the cases. Based on these findings, the final selected models were: XGB with VolSurf features for OCT1, ET with VolSurf for OCT2, and ET with VolSurf for OCT3. Hyperparameter details for these models are in Table S5.

This model selection strategy and the resulting rankings may not generalize to other data sets or applications. For OCTs, the relatively small data set size means that performance metrics can shift substantially with only a few compounds predicted differently. Additionally, our sequential approach (first identifying the best feature set and then the best algorithm) could yield different results if the order were reversed. Ideally, both factors should be evaluated jointly. Finally, our statistical analysis focused solely on MCC values; rankings might differ if alternative metrics (e.g., recall and precision) were used. A more comprehensive approach would incorporate multiple metrics to strengthen the conclusions. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, this is the first study to address model selection for transporter prediction by using a rigorous statistical framework.

Model External Validation

To provide an unbiased estimation of the selected model’s true predictive power, we proceeded to predict the OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3 external test sets. The OCT1 test set consisted of 50 compounds: 16 substrates and 34 nonsubstrates. The OCT2 and OCT3 test sets both consisted of 51 compounds: 19 OCT2 and OCT3 substrates and 32 OCT2 and OCT3 nonsubstrates. As can be noted, the test sets were unbalanced with a higher number of nonsubstrates over substrates. The confusion matrix counts and the performance metrics values are reported in Table . All of the models had accuracy values of at least 0.75. The MCC values were 0.49, 0.53, and 0.45 for OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, respectively. F1, recall, precision, and ROC-AUC were all above 0.7 for all models. The class-specific metrics (see Table S6) indicated that the models were not biased in predicting the majority class (nonsubstrate) in the test sets. Hence, the unbalanced nature of the data sets was not a limiting factor for the models. With a recall of 0.75 and a precision of 0.78, the most performant model was the OCT2 model. The high precision of the OCT2 model was due to the low number of false positives (4 compounds) when compared to the OCT1 and OCT3 models. The OCT1 model had a good recall but also had a lower precision. In particular, the class 1 (substrates) precision was 0.6 due to 8 out of the 34 nonsubstrates being erroneously classified as substrates. The OCT1 model had the lowest number of false negatives (4 compounds), which resulted in a recall of 0.76. Regarding the OCT3 model, for class 1 (substrates), both recall and precision were below 0.7 as a result of the relatively high number of false positives (6 compounds) and false negatives (7 compounds). Nevertheless, the class 0 metrics indicated that the OCT3 model was good at predicting nonsubstrates.

To further analyze how each model predicted the test set compounds, we used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to visualize the disposition of both correct and incorrect predictions, with respect to the experimental categories (substrates, nonsubstrates), in the chemical space defined by VolSurf descriptors. The PCA plots for each OCT test set are inspected in Figure . For each OCT test set, most of the incorrect predictions were located in the area of the PCA scatter plot at the boundary between the two classes, where the separation of substrates and nonsubstrates was less clear. In this area of the VolSurf properties space, the models struggled to correctly predict compounds. In fact, for compounds falling in this boundary area, a slight change in the VolSurf properties, due for example to a different tautomeric or protomeric state, might result in a completely different class assignment by the model. Microspecies (tautomers, protomers, and stereoisomers) are different forms of the same compound, all present with different abundances in the experimental test media at the same time. A limitation of current ML research is that only one microspecies (usually the most abundant) can be considered in model training and prediction. An example of this is the compound practolol, a basic nontautomeric nonsubstrate of OCT3, which was incorrectly predicted by the model as an OCT3 substrate. Practolol has an unspecified chiral center, and it was tested in vitro as a racemic mixture. We predicted with the model the R and S stereoisomers of practolol and we obtained that (R)-practolol is predicted as a substrate (this corresponded to the original incorrect prediction by the model, where VolSurf generates the R isomer from the SMILES before calculating the descriptors), but (S)-practolol was correctly predicted as a nonsubstrate. We believe that further research is needed to create OCT predictive models able to consider all possible microspecies for a given compound via multi-instance machine learning, but this is outside the scope of this work. Comparison with Other Methods

Gebauer et al. proposed an XGBoost classifier for OCT3 using RDKit-based molecular descriptors. While their study shares an objective similar to ours, there are several methodological differences between the two approaches. Notably, Gebauer et al. used a UR (uptake ratio) threshold of ≥3 to define substrates, whereas we used a threshold of ≥2. Although a higher threshold might be justified to align with certain in vivo observations (potentially excluding borderline substrates), the authors did not provide supporting evidence for this choice. Our selected cutoff is consistent with FDA guidelines for transporter studies, which we consider more appropriate for predictive modeling in this context. Further differences exist in the preprocessing and machine learning pipelines. These include variations in structural standardization, feature scaling, and data-splitting strategies. Additionally, certain aspects of Gebauer and coworkers’ methodology remain unclear; for instance, molecular weight appears to have been included twice as a feature, and although the inclusion of logD was mentioned, it was not present in the final feature set. Despite these uncertainties, we made efforts to replicate their model as closely as possible. Our reproduction yielded an ROC-AUC of 0.69 and an MCC of 0.39, both lower than the performance metrics reported in their publication. More importantly, these results also fall short of the metrics achieved by our model, which had an ROC-AUC of 0.72 and an MCC of 0.45.

Baidya et al. described a set of machine learning models for predicting OCT1 and OCT2 substrates. For OCT1, the best-performing models were based on Monte Carlo optimization using combined SMILES and GAO descriptors with 0ECk connectivity as well as XGBoost models trained on a broad set of curated physicochemical and structural descriptors. For OCT2, the most reliable predictions came from XGBoost, utilizing normalized high-variance descriptors from sources like Dragon, alvaDesc, and CDK. The data set used in Baidya et al.’s study was originally sourced from the work of Hendrickx et al. There are several methodological differences between their approach and ours. For instance, Baidya and coworkers applied a UR (uptake ratio) threshold of 1.3 to define OCT substrates, whereas we used a threshold of 2, which is more commonly adopted in recent literature for identifying transporter substrates. Additionally, their preprocessing pipeline did not include the normalization of SMILES to account for tautomers and protomers, an important step that can significantly affect model consistency and accuracy. This omission, although not uncommon in machine learning studies on biological property prediction, may have been due to limited access to cheminformatics tools capable of performing such standardization. Another methodological concern is the treatment of missing values from the Hendrickx data set as nonsubstrates. Given that compounds can act as substrates for one OCT but not another, as shown in both the Hendrickx data set and the more recent Jensen and Gebauer data sets, this assumption could introduce classification noise. Moreover, as the authors did not report the random seed used in their machine learning pipeline, the results are not directly reproducible, which limits the ability to fully assess the robustness of their models. Despite these differences, we were able to extract valuable information from the Hendrickx data set, identifying compounds not included in either the Jensen or Gebauer data sets. After removing eight outliers with physicochemical properties outside our predefined boundaries (see Methods) and applying a consistent UR ≥ 2 threshold, we obtained 151 compounds (74 substrates and 77 nonsubstrates) for OCT1 and 87 compounds (16 substrates and 71 nonsubstrates) for OCT2 (see Table S7). This data set was used as an external test set to further assess our models. To evaluate the reliability of the Hendrickx data, we conducted a correlation analysis comparing its UR values with those reported in the more recent studies. We found 32 compounds with OCT1 UR values in both Hendrickx and Jensen et al., and 33 compounds with OCT2 UR values in both Hendrickx and Gebauer et al. The Pearson correlation coefficients were 0.97 and 0.94, respectively (p < 0.001), indicating strong agreement and reproducibility across data sets (see Figures S5 and S6). We therefore retrained our OCT1 and OCT2 models using the full Jensen and Gebauer data sets (training and test sets combined) and applied the resulting models to the Hendrickx compounds. The performance metrics for these predictions are listed in Table . Both models showed strong classification performance with accuracy values exceeding 0.8 and Matthews correlation coefficients above 0.45. While the OCT2 test set was notably imbalanced toward nonsubstrates, recall, precision, and F1 scores remained above 0.7, indicating acceptable performance under challenging conditions.

Shaikh et al. developed a set of classification models for predicting OCT1 transport using random forest classifiers trained on ECFP2 fingerprints. The training data was sourced from the Metabolism and Transport Database (Metrabase), which is no longer publicly available. The data set was highly imbalanced in favor of substrates, and to address this, the authors supplemented the nonsubstrate class with decoy compounds (molecules with unknown interaction with OCT1, assumed to be nonsubstrates). While the use of decoys is a common strategy in data augmentation, it introduces uncertainty regarding the actual predictive performance of the models, which may be overestimated as a result. In contrast, our study exclusively uses compounds with experimentally confirmed interactions with OCT transporters without relying on decoys.

An important distinction between our approach and those described in this section lies in model interpretability. While other models rely heavily on hashed fingerprints or large sets of RDKit descriptors, both of which are derived from 2D molecular representations, our models are built on a relatively small set of VolSurf descriptors, which summarize 3D molecular interaction fields into physicochemically meaningful properties. The limitation of 2D descriptors is that they fail to capture the spatial arrangement and conformational flexibility of molecules, which are especially relevant in processes such as transporter binding. In contrast, VolSurf descriptors allow for a direct interpretation of model outputs in terms of meaningful molecular properties, something not feasible with hashed fingerprints or many RDKit features, which often lack clear chemical analogues. The ability to link predictions to well-understood molecular behaviors enhances both the transparency and the scientific relevance of our models, making them especially valuable for guiding medicinal chemistry efforts and regulatory decision-making.

Model Additional Validation

We manually inspected the recent literature to find additional OCTs’ uptake data and found 14, 13, and 3 data points for OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, respectively (see Table S8). In most of the cases, the data was extracted from drug labels where compounds were reported to either be substrates or nonsubstrates of a particular OCT. For OCT1, we collected 12 nonsubstrates and 2 substrates. For OCT2, we collected 9 nonsubstrates and 4 substrates. For OCT3, we collected 2 nonsubstrates and 1 substrate. These compounds were predicted with the models trained with Jensen et al. (OCT1) data and with Gebauer et al. (OCT2, OCT3) data. The OCT1 model correctly predicted 9 out of 12 nonsubstrates (false positives were naltrexone, niraparib, and saxagliptin) but failed to predict nintedanib and panobinostat as OCT1 substrates. The OCT2 model correctly predicted 8 out of 9 nonsubstrates and 3 out of 4 substrates. Interestingly, the compounds that were wrongly predicted (naltrexone and panobinostat) were also wrongly predicted by the OCT1 model. However, niraparib and saxagliptin (wrongly predicted by the OCT1 model) were correctly predicted by the OCT2 model as nonsubstrates. Finally, the OCT3 model correctly predicted both of the 2 nonsubstrates (dasatinib, naltrexone) and the unique OCT3 substrate (pramipexole). Here, note that naltrexone was wrongly predicted by both the OCT1 and the OCT2 models. This final validation demonstrated that it is possible to accurately predict the most recent OCT data with our models. In other words, it is possible to utilize our OCT models to prospectively forecast future data as it becomes available.

Structure–Activity Relationships

To better understand the molecular properties differentiating substrates from nonsubstrates, we inspected the VolSurf descriptor distributions by compound classes (see Figure ). For all OCTs, logP and logD descriptors were significant discriminators of substrates and nonsubstrates, with nonsubstrates having significantly higher logP/logD values than substrates. More than 90% (for OCT1) and 80% (for OCT2 and OCT3) of compounds having a logP greater than 2 were nonsubstrates. In fact, lipophilic compounds can cross cell membranes by passive diffusion. Whereas hydrophilic compounds cross cell membranes thanks to their interaction with transporters such as OCTs. It should also be noted that in vitro permeability experiments of highly lipophilic compounds may result in false negatives due to the potential saturation of the transporter.

Descriptors of flexibility, molecular volume, and charged state (e.g., the percentage of neutral species at different pH) were significant in discriminating substrates and nonsubstrates for OCT2 and OCT3, but not for OCT1. Compounds that were big, flexible, and mostly neutral at high pH values will likely be nonsubstrates of OCT2 and OCT3. PSA was a significant descriptor for OCT1 and OCT2, but not for OCT3. For OCT1, substrates had higher PSA than the nonsubstrates of OCT1. Instead, OCT2 nonsubstrates had higher PSA than OCT2 substrates. This is in contrast to what Hendrickx et al. stated in their paper, where they claimed that uptake increases with decreasing PSA for OCT1; whereas no significant relationship was found between the uptake of OCT2 and PSA. In light of the higher amount of data used in our study, we believe that our finding is more truthful. Hydrogen bonding degree descriptors SE0 (acceptor) and SE1 (donor) were significant for OCT2 and OCT1, respectively. For OCT1, a higher H-bond donor degree was associated with substrates, but no significant relationship was found for the H-bond acceptor degree. However, for OCT2, a higher hydrogen acceptor degree was associated with nonsubstrates.

Conformational Analysis

Some of the VolSurf descriptors can slightly vary depending on the 3D conformation of the input molecule. Before calculating the 3D descriptors, VolSurf minimizes the 3D structures of molecules by default. In this way, VolSurf ensures reproducibility, as each time the 3D descriptors are calculated from the most stable conformer. However, when interacting with proteins, some conformers might be more relevant than others, and usually the most stable conformer is not the bioactive one. Therefore, we asked how much the predictivity of the models described in this study could vary depending on the input conformer. To answer this question, we conducted a conformational analysis. We generated 50 conformers for each molecule and removed those with an RMSD below 0.3. We generated VolSurf descriptors for all of the remaining conformers and retrained the models 50 times. We used RDKit for generating the conformers with a random seed of 42. For the molecules where the number of conformers (Nc) was less than 50, we duplicated their first conformer 50-Nc times, thus ensuring that all of the molecules could be used 50 times. The test set MCC values distribution for each OCT resulting from this analysis is depicted in Figure . As expected, some variability in the predictive performance of the models emerged, in particular for OCT1 and OCT3, demonstrating the potentially high impact of conformers’ choice on predictive power when using VolSurf descriptors. However, we believed that this finding could not be necessarily generalizable to other cases because of the size of the test sets. The performance metrics resulting from such test sets (around 50 molecules) can vary greatly by changes in the classification of just a few compounds. To find more conclusive results, this analysis should be conducted with larger test sets and by using more pharmacophoric descriptors. However, this is beyond the scope of our study.

Models’ Generalizability

To better understand the ability of the OCT models to predict compounds that are different from those used in training, we split the Jensen and Gebauer data sets by Tanimoto similarity. The Tanimoto splits were generated with the DeepChem package v2.8.0. SMILES were used as input, and ECFP4 (bit length 1024) was used for informing the splitter. The balanced accuracy values calculated by predicting the Tanimoto test set were 0.67, 0.74, and 0.62 for OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3, respectively. The results confirmed the good degree of generalizability of the two-dimensional (or three-dimensional) OCT models.

Conclusions

In this paper, we presented new models for the prediction of OCT1-, OCT2-, and OCT3-mediated uptake. We used statistical analysis to minimize the risk of selecting overfitting models. These models are based on state-of-the-art features and ML methods and showed good predictive power in multiple rounds of external validation. Moreover, thanks to the largest amount of data available at the time of this study, the models described here provide the greatest generalizability power available in the current literature for predicting the level of OCT uptake.

As new data emerge, we recommend that these models be updated to provide more insight into the interactions of OCT with NCEs. More research is needed to overcome the limitation of not being able to train models on multiple tautomeric, protomeric, and chiral forms of the same molecule. We suggest that multi-instance machine learning is applied to further improve the OCT models described here. Finally, the development of models incorporating transport data with inhibition data should also be attempted. We hope that this study will help advance in silico methods to better predict the uptake of NCEs by OCTs.
