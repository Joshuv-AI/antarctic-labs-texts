Type 1 diabetes and its complications affect preeclampsia: A Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Zhongting Li, Mingzhi Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious pregnancy condition. Having type 1 diabetes (T1D) during pregnancy increases the risk of adverse outcomes for both mother and baby. However, it is unclear whether T1D directly constitutes a risk factor for preeclampsia. The aim of this study was to investigate the causal relationship between T1D and PE using Mendelian randomization. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (TSMR) and multivariate Mendelian randomization (MVMR) were used to study the relationship between T1D and PE. MR Analysis showed that there was statistical significance between T1D and PE (P = .003, OR = 1.048, 95% CI = 1.016–1.080). In addition, complications associated with T1D also had a statistically significant causal relationship with PE. Multivariate Mendelian randomization analysis further confirmed that adjusting for hypertension and BMI did not change the original conclusions. This study provides evidence that genetically predicted T1D and its complications exert a distinct causal influence on the progression of PE.

1. Introduction

T1D, an endocrine systemic disease, has a pathogenesis in which islet B cells are abnormally targeted by the autoimmune system, ultimately leading to cell damage and the inability to secrete insulin.[1,2] Diabetes significantly impacts human health and is a risk factor for numerous diseases.[3–6] The global increase in diabetes incidence poses a grave challenge to public health.[7–9]

Preeclampsia (PE) is a multisystem hypertensive disorder affecting 2% to 8% of pregnancies globally, characterized by new-onset hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥ 90 mm Hg) after 20 weeks of gestation, coupled with either proteinuria (≥300 mg/24h or protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3) or evidence of end-organ dysfunction such as thrombocytopenia (<100 × 10⁹/L), elevated liver enzymes (>2 × upper limit), renal impairment (serum creatinine ≥ 1.1 mg/dL), pulmonary edema, or neurological complications (e.g., refractory headache, visual disturbances).[10–12] Severe PE is defined by systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ 160/110 mm Hg, progressive target organ injury, or uteroplacental insufficiency manifesting as fetal growth restriction or abnormal Doppler velocimetry.[11,12] Emerging classifications further stratify PE into placental- or maternal-origin subtypes based on angiogenic imbalance (e.g., elevated sFlt-1/PlGF ratio) or preexisting conditions like chronic hypertension.[10] Pathophysiologically, PE arises from defective spiral artery remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation, with genetic and immune dysregulation contributing to its heterogeneity.[10,13] Despite advances in biomarker-based risk stratification (e.g., sFlt-1, PlGF), PE remains a leading cause of maternal mortality (e.g., 4.96% in low-resource settings) and adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, stillbirth, and neonatal intensive care admissions.[14] Previous studies have shown that T1D may be related to the occurrence of PE and other diseases, but the causal relationship is not clear in clinical observation Therefore, this study adopted Mendelian randomization method to further study the causal relationship between T1D and PE.

MR represents a statistical methodology rooted in genetic principles that employs genes as instrumental variables for inferring causal associations between modifiable exposures or risk factors and clinical outcomes. By assessing how genetic variations related to specific exposures impact clinical outcomes, MR effectively mitigates confounding effects and reverse causation biases, providing robust evidence for establishing causality. Compared with traditional randomized controlled trials (RCTs), MR obviates direct patient interventions, thus circumventing potential ethical considerations while being more cost-effective in terms of both finances and time due to its avoidance of prolonged patient follow-ups or complex trial designs. These advantages position MR as a powerful tool for overcoming limitations associated with RCT research, offering new avenues for investigating disease etiology.[15–18] Recently, the use of MR to explore causal links between diseases has gained traction in academic inquiry.[3,19] Given the significant impact of T1D on human health alongside the heightened risks associated with PE development, this study seeks to examine their inherent relationships via the two-sample Mendelian randomization (TSMR) and MVMR approaches. Our aim is to provide precise insights into their causal connection, yielding valuable references for future medical research endeavors.

2. Research methods and design

MR analysis leverages SNPs strongly associated with exposure to assess the causal relationship between exposure and outcome. To ensure the robustness of MR analysis, 3 core assumptions must be satisfied: the correlation hypothesis, which posits a significant correlation between instrumental variables and exposure factors; the independence hypothesis, which asserts that instrumental variables remain uncorrelated with known confounders; and the exclusion restriction hypothesis, which stipulates that instrumental variables can affect outcomes only indirectly through exposure. In our TSMR study, we rely primarily on inverse variance weighted (IVW) results. Additionally, we employ MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods to bolster the robustness of the analysis. Furthermore, to mitigate potential confounding effects, we implemented an MVMR approach by specifically incorporating BMI and hypertension as potential confounders. This ensures minimal interference from confounding factors in our MR results. Similarly, for our research on PE, we also relied on the FinnGen database, which contains 3556 cases and 114,375 controls. To conduct the MR analysis, our initial step involved identifying suitable instrumental variables. To ensure that the selected instrumental variables had a strong correlation with exposure factors, P < 5 × 10−8 was used as the threshold in this study.[21] This threshold is a widely adopted significance criterion in genome-wide association studies and represents the strictness level after Bonferroni correction.[22] To minimize the impact of linkage disequilibrium on the results, we applied filters to the extracted SNPs, setting thresholds of r2 = 0.001 and kb = 10,000. Only SNPs with an F value >10 were included in the study. The formula for calculating the F value was F = (N − 2) × (R2/(1 − R2)). After applying rigorous screening criteria, we discovered only 3 SNPs strongly linked to T1D with peripheral circulatory complications and 5 SNPs strongly associated with T1D accompanied by neurological complications. Having a few numbers of SNPs is a limitation and affects the validity of results. Fewer SNPs means that there is less genetic variation explained by the instrument variables, which, in turn, weakens the association between the instrument variables and the exposure, increasing the chance of Type II error (false negatives).[23] This makes the MR estimate less accurate, with wider CIs. Therefore, in order to maximize the accuracy of the results, we chose P < 5 × 10−6 as the screening threshold for these 2 exposure factors. Lowering the screening threshold may introduce weak instrumental variable bias, but we strictly ensure that F > 10 to minimize the influence of weak instrumental quantities to the greatest extent.

2.2. Statistical analysis

This study employed odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) to evaluate the strength of associations between exposure factors and outcomes. The OR, a fundamental metric in epidemiology and statistics, quantifies the association strength between an exposure and an outcome, defined as the ratio of the odds of the outcome occurring in the exposed group to the odds in the non-exposed group. An OR >1 suggests that the exposure factor may increase the risk of the outcome, while an OR <1 indicates a potential protective effect. Statistical significance was assessed based on both the 95% CI and P-values: associations were considered statistically significant if the 95% CI excluded 1 and the P-value was <.05.[24]

Furthermore, we utilized the Cochran Q test to assess potential heterogeneity in the analysis and employed the MR-Egger regression method to evaluate possible horizontal pleiotropy.[25] To examine the influence of individual SNPs on the overall results, we implemented a leave-one-out approach by excluding them one by one. This facilitated the identification of whether specific SNPs had a notable impact on the analysis outcomes. By employing an MVMR analysis approach, we effectively adjusted for potential confounding risk factors. This significantly reduced interference from these confounding elements on causality inference, thereby enhancing the precision and reliability of our study. For all the statistical tests, we used packages such as TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.7),[26] forestplot, and other R software packages (version 4.3.3).

3. Results

3.1. TSMR analysis results

The results of the TSMR revealed that T1D was a risk factor for PE (P = .003, OR = 1.048, 95% CI = 1.016–1.080), and all complications were causally related to PE. T1D with ketoacidosis (IVW P = .001, OR = 1.035, 95% CI = 1.013–1.058), T1D with coma (IVW P = .002, OR = 1.036, 95% CI = 1.014–1.060), T1D with neurological complications (IVW P = .042, OR = 1.037, 95% CI = 1.001–1.073), T1D with ophthalmic complications (IVW P = .045, OR = 1.035, 95% CI = 1.001–1.070), T1D with peripheral circulatory complications (IVW P = .049, OR = 1.034, 95% CI = 1.000–1.069), T1D with renal complications (IVW P = .006, OR = 1.033, 95% CI = 1.009–1.057), and T1D without complications (IVW P = .003, OR = 1.065, 95% CI = 1.022–1.110). IVW offers high statistical power. If the other 4 methods do not reach significance but have the same effect direction as IVW, they still support the reliability of the results.[27,28] In the results of T1D and PE in this study, P of the MR-Egger method is >.05 and the 95% CI also includes 1, this is usually considered insignificant. However, the effect directions of each method are consistent with IVW, and IVW is significant, then this result can be considered significant.

3.2. Sensitivity analysis results

Heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests revealed no heterogeneity or pleiotropy between T1D and PE (heterogeneity test IVW P = .322, MR-Egger P = .273, horizontal pleiotropy test P = .240), and there was heterogeneity between T1D without complications and PE but no pleiotropy (heterogeneity detection IVW P = .041, MR-Egger P = .050, horizontal pleiotropy detection P = .279). There was no heterogeneity or pleiotropy between the complications of T1D and PE, except for the heterogeneity of ocular complications (heterogeneity test IVW P = .009, MR-Egger P = .007). Leave-one-out showed that the TSMR results were reliable, with no single SNP having a significant effect on the results (Figs. 4. Discussion

This study used MR analysis to demonstrate an independent causal effect of T1D on PE and further confirmed the causal relationship between T1D complications and PE. MVMR also showed that the results were not affected by confounding factors such as hypertension and BMI.

The association between T1D and PE involves multi-pathway interactions. The elevated PE risk in pregnant women with T1D is closely associated with AGEs-RAGE-NF-κB pathway-mediated vascular inflammatory injury. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes the binding of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) to RAGE, activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, which induces the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. This process forms a positive feedback loop through oxidative stress, further exacerbating endothelial cell apoptosis and dysfunction.[29,30] In pregnancy contexts, placental tissue studies demonstrate abnormal NF-κB pathway activation in placental villous trophoblasts of T1D patients, accompanied by significantly elevated inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α),[30,31] while placental perfusion abnormalities and dysregulated angiogenesis may directly contribute to PE pathogenesis.[32,33] Genetic studies suggest that the T1D-PE association may involve immune cell mediation, highlighting the central role of inflammatory-immune regulatory imbalance in their comorbidity.[34] In T1D patients, diabetic kidney disease progression involves a vicious cycle of podocyte apoptosis (e.g., synaptopodin reduction) and complement system overactivation (elevated C5a levels). Complement C5a exacerbates podocyte mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis through C5aR signaling, while podocyte injury further releases pro-inflammatory factors activating complement cascades, potentially increasing PE risk.[35,36] In this study, Mendelian randomization method was applied for the first time to reveal that T1D is an independent risk factor for PE from the perspective of genetics. This conclusion not only provides genetic evidence for the causal relationship between the 2, but also forms a scientific supplement to previous mechanism research results.

This study found a statistically significant but relatively small causal association (OR = 1.05) between T1D and PE through MR analysis, which is lower than the high risks reported in some previous observational studies.[37,38] This difference reflects the strengths and limitations of different research methods. Observational studies may overestimate the association due to confounding factors, such as chronic hypertension and diabetic nephropathy, which are independent risk factors for PE.[39] MR, by using genetic variation as an instrument, avoids these confounders and offers a purer causal estimation.[40] Secondly, MR Assesses the long-term impact of genetically determined lifelong susceptibility to T1D on PE,[41] while observational studies focus on the dynamic role of clinically diagnosed T1D during pregnancy. PE is a disease that occurs at a specific stage of pregnancy. MR May not be able to precisely capture the impact of a specific period, nor can it accurately reflect this time-dependent causal relationship. In conclusion, the results of MR And observational studies are not in opposition. The combination of the 2 helps to understand the causal relationship between T1D and PE.

Advantages of this study: the MR method used. Although RCTs have long been regarded as the gold standard for determining causation,[42–44] in practical applications, RCTs are not always feasible because of financial constraints, ethical considerations, and other practical factors. Moreover, the results of RCTs may sometimes be affected by confounding variables or reverse causality, thereby affecting their accuracy. In contrast, the MR method has demonstrated unique advantages, as it can largely avoid these potential problems and thus provide more robust and reliable causal inference. The successful application of this method in this study undoubtedly enhances the persuasiveness and scientific validity of the research findings.[15] Furthermore, this study employs a combination of TSMR and MVMR to further eliminate confounding factors.

Similarly, this study has several shortcomings. The first point is that all the data in this study are from individuals of European ancestry, which limits the wide applicability of the research results.[45,46] To verify whether this causal relationship is universal, future studies should aim to include more samples from different ethnic and geographic populations. In addition, there may be sample overlap between the exposed and resulting datasets, which may increase the risk of type I errors.[47,48]

5. Conclusion

This study revealed that T1D and its complications are risk factors for PE.
