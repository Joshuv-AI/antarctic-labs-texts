Precision banding technique for arterial steal syndrome in a patient with chronic kidney disease on hemodialysis: A case report
Authors: Carlos A. Arriaga-Verastegui, Carlos A. Pintado-Guevara, Joaquin Sarmiento-Falen, Georgette Vetanzo-Sánchez, Carlos Zavaleta-Corvera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dialysis access-associated steal syndrome is an uncommon but potentially limb-threatening complication of high-flow arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) in hemodialysis patients. We report a 48-year-old woman with progressive ischemic symptoms of the left hand secondary to a high-flow brachiocephalic AVF. Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed excessive access flow and severely reduced distal arterial perfusion. The patient underwent precision banding with continuous intraoperative Doppler guidance, allowing controlled flow reduction to a physiological range and immediate restoration of distal perfusion. Symptoms resolved completely, and AVF patency was preserved at the 1-year follow-up.

Hemodialysis (HD) is a life-sustaining therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease, and its global use continues to increase. According to the 2021 United States Renal Data System report, 492,096 individuals were undergoing in-center HD in the United States by the end of 2019, representing a 1.7% annual increase and a 34.5% increase since 2009.1 Regional differences exist, with HD accounting for 66% of renal replacement therapy in Latin America compared with 58% in Europe, and a faster annual growth rate of approximately 4% in Latin America.2,3

As the HD population expands, access-related complications remain a major clinical concern. Patients on dialysis are up to 8.5 times more likely to require emergency care than the general population.4 Among these complications, arteriovenous (AV) access-related conditions such as bleeding, limb swelling, and dialysis access-associated steal syndrome (DASS) are particularly relevant.5

DASS is an uncommon but clinically significant complication after AV access creation, with an estimated incidence of approximately 6%.6 It arises from altered hemodynamics that reduce or reverse arterial flow distal to the access,7 potentially leading to tissue ischemia, limb loss, and increased mortality if left untreated.7 Identified risk factors include brachial artery-based access, female sex, diabetes mellitus, and advanced age.8

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by findings such as diminished distal pulses, symptom relief with access compression, and ischemic manifestations including pallor, coolness, pain, and sensory deficits.9,10 The severity of DASS ranges from mild sensory changes to rest pain, motor dysfunction, and irreversible tissue loss and has been classified into stages reflecting progressive functional impairment.11, 12, 13

Management requires an individualized approach integrating clinical evaluation, imaging, and flow measurements to guide treatment selection based on access characteristics and patient factors.6, 7, 8 In high-flow fistulas, precision banding with intraoperative flow monitoring has emerged as a flow-modulating strategy aimed at relieving ischemia while preserving access patency. In this report, we describe a case of high-flow DASS successfully managed with Doppler-guided precision banding and briefly review current surgical options for its management.

Case report

A 48-year-old, Hispanic woman with end-stage renal disease undergoing HD attended the outpatient clinic reporting an 11-month history of progressive paresthesias and mild dull pain in the left hand during each dialysis session. Over time, her symptoms worsened, and 2 months before admission she began experiencing discomfort during routine mechanical activities. In the weeks before evaluation, the pain intensified and started to occur at rest, prompting referral to the thoracic and cardiovascular surgery service for further assessment.

Her medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus of 5 years' duration with poor adherence to treatment, arterial hypertension treated with losartan 50 mg twice daily for 3 years, and stage V chronic kidney disease on maintenance HD. Her vascular access history was notable for the use of two prior nontunneled high-flow internal jugular catheters (right and left) and the creation of a left brachiocephalic AV fistula (AVF) 1year before presentation.

On examination, she appeared in preserved general condition, with no respiratory compromise. Inspection of the left upper extremity revealed pallor of the skin and adnexa, coolness of the distal hand, and subtle color changes (Fig 1). A slight increase in volume of the left hand was observed compared to the contralateral side. Capillary refill in digits 1 to 3 was approximately 4 seconds. Chest auscultation revealed normal vesicular breath sounds bilaterally, and cardiac examination showed rhythmic and regular heart sounds. The abdomen was soft and depressible, with active bowel sounds. Peripheral vascular assessment identified a palpable thrill over the left brachiocephalic AVF. On vascular examination, radial and ulnar pulses were reduced in both hands, with a positive Allen test on the left showing dependence on the ulnar artery. Doppler ultrasound examination of the left upper limb demonstrated a high-flow AVF with the following measurements: proximal flow 2232 mL/min, mid-segment flow 1204 mL/min, and distal flow 882.07 mL/min. The left radial artery showed a single-phase waveform with a peak systolic velocity of 16.62 cm/s, consistent with significant distal hypoperfusion. Based on clinical and hemodynamic findings, arterial steal syndrome associated with a high-flow AVF was diagnosed.

In our practice, a volume flow of ≥1000 mL/min in a native AVF associated with clinical signs of distal ischemia is considered the threshold for intervention in suspected DASS. In this patient, markedly elevated proximal flow and severely reduced distal arterial velocities supported the indication for flow reduction surgery.

Surgical technique

All intraoperative duplex ultrasound measurements were performed by the surgical team itself. No dedicated vascular laboratory technologist was required. Doppler assessment was conducted by the first assistant using a portable ultrasound system while the primary surgeon adjusted the banding, allowing continuous real-time hemodynamic feedback.

The patient was indicated for precision banding of the AVF under continuous intraoperative Doppler monitoring, aiming to decrease excessive inflow while restoring distal perfusion and preserving AVF patency. Under regional anesthesia, a longitudinal incision was made over the proximal segment of the left brachiocephalic AVF. Careful dissection was carried out to expose the vascular structures, ensuring preservation of adjacent nerves and soft tissues. Once the fistula was fully mobilized, baseline Doppler flow measurements were obtained in the proximal, medial, and distal segments of the AVF, as well as in the radial and ulnar arteries. These measurements confirmed markedly elevated proximal flow and severely reduced radial artery perfusion. A 0-gauge black silk suture was selected owing to its favorable handling characteristics and knot security. The banding was performed using an initial double knot, followed by two additional reinforcing throws to stabilize the ligature and allow controlled, incremental tightening. Initial tension was applied by the primary surgeon in a progressive manner, guided by real-time Doppler measurements obtained simultaneously by the first assistant. After each incremental adjustment, volume flow measurements were repeated in the proximal, mid, and distal segments of the fistula, as well as in the radial artery. If adequate flow reduction was not achieved with a single ligature, a second proximal band was added following the same stepwise protocol (Table II). Once Doppler ultrasound examination confirmed improved radial artery systolic velocity and a reduction of AVF inflow into the desired range, the intervention was finalized (Fig 2). Hemostasis was secured, and the operative field was irrigated. Adequate banding was defined as achieving a postbanding AVF volume flow of ≥600 mL/min, consistently measured at the proximal, mid, and distal segments, while simultaneously improving distal arterial perfusion. In our case, we aimed for a physiological range of approximately 700 to 900 mL/min whenever achievable, while maintaining ≥600 mL/min across the fistula segments to reduce thrombosis risk and preserve dialysis adequacy.

Duplex ultrasound examination was performed using upper extremity vein settings, with an insonation angle of 60° and a sample volume adjusted to approximately two-thirds of the vessel diameter. Postoperative Doppler imaging demonstrated significant improvement in radial artery flow and appropriate reduction in AVF flow (Figs 3, 4, and 5). Clinically, the patient exhibited improved hand warmth and perfusion immediately after surgery. The patient tolerated the procedure without complications and remained hospitalized for 3 days. At follow-up visits at 6 months and 1 year, she showed sustained clinical improvement, absence of recurrent steal symptoms, and a patent AVF with stable dialysis function. Discussion

DASS represents a clinically challenging complication in patients undergoing HD, requiring individualized therapeutic strategies based on vascular anatomy, access flow characteristics, and patient comorbidities. The present case is consistent with prior reports of DASS occurring in the setting of high-flow AVFs, where the key objective is to restore distal perfusion while preserving functional dialysis access.

A focused PubMed search identified seven case reports14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 describing diverse approaches for DASS management, reflecting the heterogeneity of clinical presentations and the absence of a universally accepted algorithm. These publications highlight a broad range of conventional, hybrid, and endovascular solutions, and underscore that treatment selection is primarily driven by hemodynamic context particularly whether the access is high flow vs low flow.

In high-flow DASS, flow reduction strategies are frequently prioritized, and multiple banding techniques have been described, including simple banding, the MILLER procedure, and Doppler ultrasound-guided precision banding. Banding aims to create a calibrated stenosis near the AV anastomosis using nonabsorbable sutures, interposition grafts, or prosthetic cuffs such as Dacron or polytetrafluorethylene; in prosthetic circuits, conical graft interposition has also been proposed.21, 22, 23, 24, 25 When performed with objective intraoperative guidance, banding offers a relatively low complexity, access-preserving approach for symptom relief. In contrast, alternative reconstructive operations such as distal revascularization with interval ligation (DRIL), proximalization of arterial inflow), and revision using distal inflow, as well as complete access ligation, may be considered in selected scenarios, depending on anatomy, distal arterial reserve, and the mechanism of ischemia.

Importantly, banding is contraindicated in low-flow AVFs, where further reduction may precipitate immediate thrombosis or permanent access loss. Early series relying on transcutaneous partial pressure of oxygen reported high symptom resolution rates (90%-100%) but poor long-term patency (10%-40%). More contemporary approaches emphasize intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring, with commonly cited flow targets of approximately 400 mL/min for native fistulas and 600 mL/min for grafts, which have been associated with improved patency and lower reintervention rates.21, 22, 23, 24, 25

Despite being minimally invasive and access sparing, banding carries inherent risks, including excessive flow reduction, thrombosis, inadequate dialysis, and the need for further interventions. These limitations reinforce the importance of careful patient selection and intraoperative hemodynamic guidance to balance distal reperfusion with access durability.26,27 From a pragmatic standpoint, cost considerations may also favor banding over more complex reconstructive procedures, although overall expenses vary by insurance coverage, postoperative requirements, and complication-related care.27,28

Recent case-based literature also illustrates alternative or hybrid strategies. Yonkus et al14 described venoplasty with a vascular staple device assisted by endovascular ballooning, achieving immediate symptom relief and 12-month fistula patency. Fitzpatrick et al15 reported the use of an Amplatzer occlusion device as salvage therapy after a failed DRIL procedure, with meaningful symptomatic improvement, although cost and availability may limit broader adoption. Tolba et al16 achieved symptom resolution through distal radial artery ligation, which may be appropriate in carefully selected patients but risks worsening ischemia if collateral reserve is inadequate. Additional approaches include cephalic vein transposition to the contralateral arm,17 endovascular stent graft deployment across the anastomosis,18 and the use of flared stent grafts for access-induced hand ischemia,19 each with distinct advantages and technical constraints.

Collectively, these reports emphasize the need for individualized, hemodynamically informed decision-making grounded in assessment of inflow, outflow, distal vascular reserve, and fistula flow dynamics. The unique contribution of this case is the description of a reproducible intraoperative setup in which duplex-derived volume flow measurements are obtained by the surgical team in real time to titrate banding tension and achieve predefined hemodynamic targets. In this context, Doppler-guided precision banding, as performed in our patient, provides a controlled, physiologically guided method that allows real-time flow modulation, minimizes the risk of undercorrection or overcorrection, and preserves long-term AVF function. The sustained clinical improvement observed immediately and at the 1-year follow-up further supports its value as a safe and effective strategy for high-flow DASS.

Although Doppler-guided precision banding is best performed in an operating room setting to ensure optimal vascular exposure and aseptic conditions, the technique does not require specialized equipment or additional personnel. Its simplicity and reproducibility make it feasible beyond high-volume academic centers.

Although it can be implemented in nonacademic hospitals, we recommend an operating room environment rather than an outpatient access laboratory to ensure optimal exposure and strict asepsis/antisepsis.

Conclusions

Precision banding with intraoperative Doppler monitoring represents a safe, physiologically guided, and access-preserving option for high-flow DASS, as demonstrated in this case by the controlled reduction of AVF inflow, restoration of distal hand perfusion, and sustained symptom resolution with preserved access function at the 1-year follow-up. Compared with more complex reconstructive procedures such as DRIL, revision using distal inflow, or proximalization of arterial inflow, this technique offers real-time titration of flow reduction and lower operative complexity, making it an attractive strategy in carefully selected patients. In the context of an expanding therapeutic arsenal that includes stapled venoplasty, distal radial artery ligation, and endovascular stent graft approaches, our report reinforces the importance of individualized, hemodynamically informed decision-making to optimize both limb perfusion and long-term dialysis access viability.
