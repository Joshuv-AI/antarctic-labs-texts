BAR-PH tandem of ACAP4 remodels membranes to drive migration via Ezrin-dependent activation
Authors: Shan Huang, Jiasheng Chen, Xinyu Wang, Xiaoyu Song, Xiaojie Zhu, Chuanhai Fu, Xuan Zhang, Xing Liu, Chao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractADP-ribosylation factor (Arf)-specific GTPase-activating proteins (ArfGAPs) regulate cell migration through interactions with small G proteins, including Arfs. In ArfGAPs, the Bin/Amphiphysin/Rvs (BAR) domain plays a key role in membrane binding and curvature induction, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes remain unclear. Here, we investigate the function of the BAR domain and its adjacent pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of ACAP4 in cell migration. We demonstrate that the BAR-PH tandem of ACAP4 induces membrane curvature, promotes cell migration, forms condensates in vitro, and exhibits membrane-associated distribution in cells. The crystal structure of the ACAP4 BAR domain, determined at 2.8 Å resolution, reveals multiple positively charged surface patches. Structural modeling further identifies conserved positively charged residue pairs in the PH domain, which collectively mediate electrostatic interactions essential for both membrane remodeling and membrane localization. Mutagenesis experiments confirm that these regions are required for ACAP4’s subcellular localization and pro-migratory activity. Furthermore, we identify that the actin-binding protein Ezrin interacts with a specific C-terminal region of ACAP4 to regulate its function. Ezrin binding enhances condensate formation and enables full-length ACAP4 to associate with membranes and promote cell migration, particularly when co-expressed with the activated Ezrin (T567D). Together, our findings uncover the molecular basis by which ACAP4 coordinates membrane remodeling and cytoskeletal dynamics, offering new insights into the mechanisms that drive cell migration.

Introduction

Cell migration is fundamental to various physiological and pathological processes, including embryonic development, wound healing, immune response, and disease progression such as cancer metastasis and fibrosis (Friedl and Gilmour, 2009; Hetmanski et al., 2019; Lambert and Weinberg, 2021; SenGupta et al., 2021). Dysregulated cell migration can lead to developmental abnormalities (Miyoshi and Fishell, 2012; Akkermans et al., 2022), chronic wounds (Pawar et al., 2021), and immune deficiencies (Janssen and Geha, 2019), while excessive or misdirected migration contributes to invasive cancer (Lambert et al., 2017; Novikov et al., 2021) and autoimmune disorders (García-Cuesta et al., 2019). Understanding the regulatory mechanisms of cell migration is crucial for addressing these medical challenges.

Among the regulators of cell migration, ACAP4—also known as ASAP3, DDEFL1, or UPLC1—is a tumor-associated member of the ADP-ribosylation factor GTPase-activating protein (ArfGAP) family. It is minimally expressed in most normal tissues but is markedly upregulated in various cancers. ACAP4 has been implicated in maintaining gastric parietal cell polarity and promoting tumor cell motility and invasion (Okabe et al., 2004; Fang et al., 2006; Yao and Smolka, 2019). Although ACAP4 deficiency impairs epithelial organization and reduces cell migration in multiple systems (Ding et al., 2010; Luo et al., 2014; Tian et al., 2017; Song et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019), the molecular mechanisms underlying its function remain poorly understood.

A defining feature of ACAP4 is its N-terminal Bin/Amphiphysin/Rvs (BAR) domain, located adjacent to a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, forming the BAR-PH tandem (Zhao et al., 2013; Gasilina et al., 2019). BAR domains are known to bind to curved membranes and help remodel them, participating in diverse processes such as endocytosis, vesicle trafficking, and cytoskeletal organization (Pang et al., 2014; Carman and Dominguez, 2018). ACAP4 is a multidomain protein composed of a BAR domain, a PH domain, an ArfGAP domain, ankyrin repeats (ANK), and a C-terminal loop (Figure 1A). Functionally, ACAP4 facilitates the GTP/GDP cycling of Arf proteins through its ArfGAP domain in a calcium-regulated manner and promotes cytoskeletal rearrangement (Fang et al., 2006; Ismail et al., 2010). While the precise roles of the ankyrin repeats and the C-terminal loop remain poorly understood, the BAR domain has been shown to sense membrane curvature, particularly through epidermal growth factor-induced phosphorylation, enabling ACAP4 to modulate cell membrane morphology during migration (Zhao et al., 2013).

Additionally, ACAP4 mediates the crosslink between the plasma membrane and the cytoskeletal network through interacting with the actin-binding protein Ezrin, which has been implicated in apical membrane remodeling and cytoskeletal rearrangement, particularly in the context of gastric acid secretion (Zhou et al., 2003; Ding et al., 2010; Yuan et al., 2017). Despite these insights, the specific molecular mechanisms by which the BAR-PH tandem contributes to ACAP4’s regulation of membrane-cytoskeleton dynamics and membrane morphological changes remain elusive.

In this study, we define the molecular mechanisms by which the ACAP4 BAR-PH tandem mediates membrane binding, curvature generation, and cell migration through a combination of structural, biochemical, and cellular approaches, providing critical insights into the role of ACAP4 in coordinating membrane dynamics and cytoskeletal interactions.

Results

ACAP4 BAR-PH domain mediates membrane binding and curvature remodeling

Several members of the ACAP/ASAP protein family can bind to membranes and induce lipid tubulation through their BAR or BAR-PH domains (Nie et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2013; Pang et al., 2014; Gasilina et al., 2019, 2022). To investigate whether ACAP4 follows a similar mechanism, we examined its subcellular localization using various GFP-tagged ACAP4 constructs (Supplementary Figure S1). Notably, only the BAR-PH tandem domain of ACAP4 (ACAP4 BAR-PH) exhibited enrichment at the cell membrane, whereas full-length ACAP4 (ACAP4 FL) and other truncated constructs were primarily localized in the cytoplasm. These findings suggest that the ACAP4 BAR-PH domain drives membrane localization, and there is an intrinsic inhibitory mechanism regulating the subcellular distribution of the full-length protein.

Next, we assessed whether ACAP4 induces membrane curvature by performing negative-stain electron microscopy (EM) on artificial small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs). Neutral SUVs (POPC-SUVs) were prepared using 98% 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-sn-3-phosphocholine (POPC) and 2% 3,3′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine (Dil), while negatively charged SUVs (PIP2-SUVs) were prepared by supplementing POPC with 20% 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (DOPS) and 2% phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) (Wu et al., 2019). Both POPC-SUVs and PIP2-SUVs remained homogeneous in solution when incubated with ACAP4 FL, showing no tubulation (Figure 1B and C). However, ACAP4 BAR-PH induced distinct tubular structures specifically in PIP2-SUVs but not in POPC-SUVs (Figure 1B and C). These results indicate that ACAP4 BAR-PH preferentially binds to negatively charged membranes, suggesting that electrostatic interactions drive its membrane association.

We further investigated the membrane interaction of ACAP4 BAR-PH with liposomes using fluorescence microscopy. Giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) composed of either PIP2 (PIP2-GUVs) or brain lipid extract (Brain-GUVs) were prepared and fluorescently labeled with the lipophilic dye Dil. ACAP4 BAR-PH was labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (488-ACAP4 BAR-PH). Notably, ACAP4 BAR-PH formed distinct condensates directly on GUV surfaces, with the Alexa Fluor 488 signal outlining the GUV membrane uniformly (Figure 1D and E). To characterize the material properties of these condensates, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) was performed (Figure 1F). Although recovery kinetics were slow, measurable fluorescence recovery was observed within the bleached region of the condensates, indicating the internal molecular mobility and dynamic molecular exchange (Figure 1G). Furthermore, time-lapse imaging revealed that condensates on the GUV surface underwent dynamic growth over time, and adjacent condensates were observed to coalesce (fusion) (Supplementary Figure S2A), consistent with the behavior expected for biomolecular condensate formation.

In contrast, SUVs (∼40 nm in diameter) were too small to resolve individual signals (Supplementary Figure S2B). Upon incubation with Dil-PIP2-SUVs, 488-ACAP4 BAR-PH formed condensates, recruiting SUVs to their surfaces (Supplementary Figure S2B). The fluorescence signal of ACAP4 BAR-PH condensates wrapped by SUVs exhibited recovery post-bleaching, further confirming that these structures are dynamic assemblies rather than static aggregates (Supplementary Figure S2C and D).

We next assessed whether membrane interaction influences ACAP4 BAR-PH condensate formation. Addition of PIP2-SUVs significantly enhanced the formation of ACAP4 BAR-PH condensates in vitro (Supplementary Figure S2E and F), indicating that membrane binding promotes condensate assembly. We also performed the liposome co-sedimentation assay under different salt conditions. Without liposomes, ACAP4 BAR-PH showed partial precipitation at 100 mM NaCl but remained entirely in the supernatant at 300 mM NaCl. Upon addition of liposomes, ACAP4 BAR-PH was enriched in the pellet at both salt concentrations, demonstrating enhanced membrane association regardless of condensate formation status (Supplementary Figure S2G). These findings collectively demonstrate that ACAP4 BAR-PH forms condensate structures in vitro, preferentially binds to negatively charged membranes, and induces liposome tubulation.

Structural features of ACAP4 BAR-PH domain

To understand the molecular basis of liposome remodeling in vitro and membrane localization in vivo for ACAP4 BAR-PH, we determined its structural properties. We successfully solved the crystal structure of the ACAP4 BAR domain (amino acids 7–282) at 2.8 Å resolution using molecular replacement (Supplementary Table S1). The structure includes 259 residues in one monomer (amino acids 7–278, with a disordered region spanning residues 165–177) and 258 residues in the other monomer (amino acids 7–277, with the same disordered region).

The ACAP4 BAR domain forms a homodimer in an anti-parallel orientation, with an extensive dimer interface (∼5930 Å²) that includes charged and hydrophobic residues (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figure S3). This dimerization of BAR and BAR-PH tandem domains was confirmed using fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) coupled with static light scattering (SLS) (Supplementary Figure S3A and B). Mutagenesis of charged residues in the dimer interface did not significantly disrupt dimer formation (Supplementary Figure S3C and D), but mutations targeting hydrophobic residues completely destabilized the dimer (Supplementary Figure S3E and F). These results indicate that hydrophobic interactions are crucial for maintaining dimer stability.

Structurally, ACAP4 BAR exhibits hallmark features of classical BAR domains, with five α-helices (α0–α4). Helices α1–α3 form an antiparallel coiled-coil, while helix α4 interacts with the coiled-coil of the opposing monomer, resulting in a crescent-shaped homodimer. This dimer has a length of ∼147.3 Å, a width of ∼43.2 Å, and a height of ∼42.4 Å. The positive curvature (K) of the ACAP4 BAR domain is ∼1/20 nm−1, smaller than APPL1 (∼1/17 nm−1) but larger than ACAP1 (∼1/40 nm−1), N-BAR Amphiphysin (∼1/22 nm−1), and F-BAR FBP17 (∼1/60 nm−1) (Supplementary Figure S4). These differences in curvature suggest that ACAP4 BAR is optimized to induce membrane curvatures distinct from other BAR proteins, enabling a range of cellular functions.

To investigate membrane binding, we analyzed the concave surface of the ACAP4 BAR domain, which revealed three positively charged patches critical for the interaction with negatively charged membranes. These regions are designated as Patch 1 (R54, K56, K57, R60), Patch 2 (K145, K149, K152), and Patch 3 (K160, K163) (Figure 2B). We further utilized AlphaFold to predict the structure of the ACAP4 BAR-PH tandem and compared it to our experimentally determined BAR domain structure. The predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) plot for the AlphaFold-predicted BAR-PH tandem structure confirms high confidence in the modeled domains, supporting the reliability of our structural analysis (Figure 2C), and the alignment yielded a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.836 Å, demonstrating strong structural agreement (Figure 2D). Structurally, the PH domain consists of seven β-strands (β1–β7) and one α-helix (α5) (Figure 2E). Several positively charged residues are exposed on the PH domain surface, grouped into four key pairs: Pair 1 (R283 and K284 from Loop 1), Pair 2 (K311 from β1 and R323 from β2), Pair 3 (R317 and R318 from Loop 2), and Pair 4 (H336 and R341 from Loop 3) (Figure 2F). This structural analysis identifies critical charged patches and pairs in the BAR and PH domains that likely contribute to ACAP4’s ability to bind to membranes and induce membrane curvature, providing mechanistic insights into its functional roles.

Electropositive surfaces regulate ACAP4 membrane interaction

To assess the contribution of positively charged residues to the liposome binding of ACAP4, we generated a series of charge-reversing mutants by substituting key lysine, arginine, and histidine with glutamate. These variants included three patch mutants within the BAR domain (Patches 1–3), one residue pair between BAR and PH domains (Pair 1), and three residue pairs within the PH domain (Pairs 2–4), as defined based on structural analysis (Figure 2B and D). Fluorescently labeled mutant proteins were evaluated for membrane binding in vitro. Compared to wild-type (WT) BAR-PH, none of the mutants formed robust condensates on GUVs, but several mutants, including Patch 1, Patch 3, and Pair 1, retained very weak membrane binding, visible as faint fluorescent outlines (Figure 2G). In SUV assays, Patch 2, Pair 2, and Pair 3 mutants completely lost condensate formation and SUV clustering, whereas Patch 1, Patch 3, Pair 1, and Pair 4 maintained a limited ability to cluster SUVs (Supplementary Figure S5). These residual activities correlate with spin-down assays, which showed reduced sedimentation of the impaired mutants (Supplementary Figure S6).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that distinct positively charged regions contribute unequally to the electrostatic interactions driving BAR-PH membrane binding in vitro.

Electrostatic interactions underlie ACAP4 membrane localization and remodeling

To determine how electrostatic interactions influence ACAP4 membrane targeting in cells, we examined the subcellular localization of BAR-PH charge-reversing mutants in the context of endogenous ACAP4 depletion. We utilized short hairpin RNA (shRNA) to deplete ACAP4 in HeLa cells. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient knockdown by two independent shRNAs, with scrambled sequences serving as controls (Supplementary Figure S7A). Full-length mutant constructs, when reintroduced into ACAP4-knockdown cells, exhibited localization patterns largely indistinguishable from WT ACAP4 FL, showing diffuse cytoplasmic distribution (Supplementary Figure S7B–I). In contrast, GFP-tagged BAR-PH mutants displayed a range of membrane localization phenotypes. Patch 1 and Patch 3 mutants retained partial plasma membrane enrichment, whereas Patch 2, Pair 2, and Pair 3 mutants lost membrane localization and were diffusely cytoplasmic (Figures 3B–D, F, and G). Notably, the Pair 4 mutant accumulated in the nucleus, consistent with impaired membrane engagement and altered subcellular distribution (Figure 3H). These results indicate that specific positively charged residues, especially those within the PH domain, are essential for BAR-PH membrane localization in cells.

To assess whether these mutations also affect the membrane remodeling capability of BAR-PH, we performed negative-stain EM using PIP2-enriched SUVs. WT ACAP4 BAR-PH robustly induced membrane tubulation, while ACAP4 FL lacked tubulation activity (Figure 4A–C). All charge-reversing mutants exhibited a significant reduction in tubule counts compared to WT ACAP4 BAR-PH (Figure 4D–K), confirming that the electropositive interfaces in both BAR and PH domains are essential for membrane remodeling. Notably, mutants targeting the PH domain showed more severe defects in tubulation formation, underscoring the PH domain’s critical role in initial membrane engagement (Figure 4K). These data suggest that both BAR and PH domain residues contribute to curvature remodeling, and electrostatic interactions at these sites are required for ACAP4-induced membrane deformation.

ACAP4 BAR-PH tandem is essential for cell migration

We observed non-uniform enrichment of ACAP4 BAR-PH along the plasma membrane, occasionally occurring in peripheral regions with elevated signal intensity. BAR-PH also formed puncta in the cytoplasm that are likely associated with internal membrane compartments (Supplementary Figures S8A and B). These localization patterns were consistent across different tagging strategies (GFP and Flag) and correlate with BAR-PH’s ability to remodel membranes in vitro. These in-cell observations may reflect localized concentration changes or microdomain organization.

ACAP4 has been implicated in promoting cell migration and cancer metastasis, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. The positively charged regions identified within ACAP4 BAR-PH, which influence membrane localization, offer valuable insights into its role in cell motility. To validate the function of ACAP4 in cell migration, we assessed the migratory capacity of HeLa and MDA-MB-231 cells following ACAP4 knockdown using Transwell assays (Supplementary Figures S7A and S8C). We observed a significant reduction in the number of cells crossing the microporous membrane within 24 h upon ACAP4 knockdown (Figure 5A and B; Supplementary Figure S8D and E). Furthermore, wound-healing assays demonstrated that ACAP4-depleted cells exhibited significantly slower scratch area recovery compared to controls, confirming impaired cell migration (Figure 5C–F; Supplementary Figure S8F and G).

To mimic pathological overexpression of ACAP4, we transiently overexpressed either ACAP4 FL or the ACAP4 BAR-PH domain in HeLa cells and assessed their effect on cell migration by performing wound-healing assays. The average transfection efficiency across all conditions was consistently ∼50%, with no significant variation between groups (Supplementary Figure S9). Compared to controls, overexpression of ACAP4 FL enhanced cell migration, while overexpression of ACAP4 BAR-PH further accelerated wound closure rates (Figure 5G–I and Q).

To further dissect the role of positively charged regions within ACAP4 BAR-PH, we overexpressed the previously described BAR-PH mutant variants and performed wound-healing assays. Mutations that impaired membrane localization (Patch 2, Pair 2, Pair 3, and Pair 4 mutants) abolished the ability of ACAP4 BAR-PH to promote cell migration (Figures 5K, 5N–Q). Conversely, Patch 1 and Patch 3 mutants retained the ability to enhance migration (Figure 5J, L, and Q). Interestingly, the BAR-PH Pair 1 mutant, which maintained partial membrane localization, failed to promote cell migration (Figures 5M and Q), underscoring the importance of the loop connecting BAR and PH domains in regulating this process. These findings highlight that the BAR-PH tandem domain is essential for ACAP4-mediated cell migration, and its positively charged residues are functionally required for this activity through regulating membrane localization and possibly condensate formation.

Ezrin binding activates ACAP4 for membrane interaction

ACAP4 BAR-PH domain demonstrates a stronger ability to promote cell migration than the full-length protein, suggesting the presence of regulatory factors or mechanisms that inhibit the activity of BAR-PH in the full-length protein. Prior studies have demonstrated that Ezrin, a member of the ezrin/radixin/moesin (ERM) protein family, interacts with the I-BAR domain of IRSp53, inducing membrane curvature (Qian et al., 2017). Additionally, Ezrin interacts with ACAP4 in a phosphorylation-dependent manner, facilitating its apical localization in parietal cells (Zhou et al., 2003; Yuan et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2018). To explore the interaction between Ezrin and ACAP4, we purified recombinant proteins representing various truncations of Ezrin and ACAP4 and performed FPLC assays. The results revealed that Ezrin FERM specifically binds to the ACAP4 ArfGAP–ANK–Loop (AAL) region but not to the BAR-PH domain (Figure 6A–C; Supplementary Figure S10A–C). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments determined a weak binding affinity (Kd ∼25 μM) between ACAP4 AAL and Ezrin FERM (Supplementary Figure S11A–C), while a more defined region (ACAP4 residues 681–730, EBM) exhibited a stronger binding affinity (Kd ∼8 μM) (Figure 6D; Supplementary Figure S10A and D).

To test whether Ezrin can activate ACAP4 FL in a membrane context, we examined condensate formation on PIP2-enriched GUVs and SUVs. Cy5-labeled Ezrin FERM and GFP-tagged ACAP4 FL, purified from Expi293F cells, were analyzed individually and in combination. ACAP4 FL alone failed to bind to GUVs (Figure 6E), and Cy5-labeled Ezrin FERM alone showed no GUV interaction (Figure 6F). Strikingly, when ACAP4 FL-GFP was combined with Ezrin FERM, a continuous protein layer formed on the GUV surface accompanied by discrete co-condensates (Figure 6G). FRAP analysis revealed distinct molecular dynamics: ACAP4 FL exhibited slow, partial fluorescence recovery, whereas Ezrin FERM recovered rapidly (Figure 6H–J), indicating differential mobility within the complex.

Similarly, in SUV assays, ACAP4 FL alone formed only weak condensates that recruited minimal membrane signal, and Ezrin alone did not form detectable condensates (Supplementary Figure S11A and B). In contrast, the ACAP4 FL–Ezrin FERM complex produced large, SUV-coated condensates (Supplementary Figure S11C). FRAP of these SUV-coated condensates again showed the slow recovery for ACAP4 and the fast recovery for Ezrin (Supplementary Figure S11D and E). Time-lapse imaging captured spontaneous fusion of SUV-coated condensates despite their membrane wrapping, confirming liquid-like behavior in a physiologically relevant membrane context (Supplementary Figure S11F).

Furthermore, we examined whether ACAP4 FL can deform membranes in the presence of Ezrin. Consistently, neither ACAP4 FL alone nor Ezrin (FL or FERM) alone produced detectable tubulation, and when ACAP4 FL was combined with Ezrin FL, no significant tubulation was observed. Strikingly, when ACAP4 FL was combined with Ezrin FERM, we observed robust membrane tubulation, with numerous elongated tubules clearly visible across multiple fields (Figure 6K and L).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the interaction between Ezrin FERM and the ACAP4 EBM region modulates the functional state of ACAP4, facilitating the activity of the BAR-PH tandem domain and enabling liposome binding and remodeling by ACAP4 FL.

Ezrin enhances ACAP4-mediated cell migration

As demonstrated earlier, ACAP4 BAR-PH is critical for membrane remodeling and cell migration promotion, whereas ACAP4 FL does not exhibit these activities. Given that Ezrin can modulate the activity of the BAR-PH tandem domain in vitro, we next investigated whether Ezrin exerts a similar regulatory effect on ACAP4 in vivo. To explore this, we first overexpressed ACAP4 FL and full-length Ezrin (Ezrin FL) or its constitutively active mutant Ezrin T567D in HeLa cells. Co-expression of ACAP4 and the constitutively active mutant Ezrin T567D enhanced ACAP4 accumulation at the cell periphery (Supplementary Figure S12), suggesting that membrane-proximal clustering occurs in a cellular context. Then, we assessed their impact on cell migration by performing wound-healing assays. There was no significant difference in migration rates for cells expressing Ezrin FL and Ezrin FL T567D alone (Figure 7A–C and K). However, when ACAP4 FL was co-transfected with Ezrin FL T567D, a significant enhancement in wound-healing rates was observed compared to cells co-expressing ACAP4 FL and Ezrin FL (Figure 7D, E, and K). To further elucidate the role of the positively charged residues in ACAP4 BAR-PH, we introduced mutations in these regions of the full-length protein. The mutated ACAP4 FL variants failed to promote cell migration, even when co-transfected with Ezrin FL T567D (Figure 7F–K). This indicates that the positively charged residues within the BAR-PH tandem domain are crucial for Ezrin-mediated regulation of ACAP4 activity in vivo.

To assess whether ACAP4 regulates cell migration through modulation of Arf6 activity, we measured endogenous Arf6-GTP levels following ACAP4 overexpression. The amount of active Arf6 remained unchanged, suggesting that ACAP4 does not influence Arf6 activation in cells (Supplementary Figure S12B and C). Furthermore, FPLC analysis under multiple buffer conditions and with various ACAP4 truncations and a constitutively active Arf6 mutant (Q67L) failed to detect a stable interaction (Supplementary Figure S12D–L). Collectively, these results suggest that ACAP4 interacts only weakly, if not at all, with Arf6, and overexpression of ACAP4 FL does not significantly influence Arf6-GTP levels in cells. Given that our study primarily focuses on the membrane-targeting and curvature-inducing functions of the BAR-PH domain, rather than the GAP activity of ACAP4, we speculate that ACAP4 promotes membrane remodeling and cell migration via a mechanism independent of Arf6 regulation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that Ezrin facilitates ACAP4 activity by enhancing its membrane localization and condensate formation, thereby enabling the BAR-PH domain to drive cell migration. This highlights a regulatory mechanism wherein Ezrin relieves autoinhibition of ACAP4 FL and promotes its functional engagement at the membrane.

Discussion

ACAP4 is highly expressed in various cancers and plays a critical role in promoting cell migration and tumor metastasis. While its BAR-PH tandem domain is central to its role in sensing, inducing, and maintaining membrane curvature, the molecular mechanisms underlying these processes have remained unclear.

The electrostatic-driven membrane binding and curvature induction mediated by the concave electropositive surface is a fundamental property conserved across the BAR superfamily, including BAR-PH tandem proteins. For instance, the N-BAR domain of Endophilin features a dense cluster of positively charged residues (e.g. K149, K152, K159, K160, K171, K172, K173, and R174) on its concave surface. Structural and functional studies have demonstrated that these residues form an ‘electrostatic platform’ that engages the negatively charged headgroups of PIP2 (Gallop et al., 2006). ACAP1 is a close homolog of ACAP4, whose PH domain contains key positively charged residues (e.g. K274 and R286) that specifically recognize PIP2, while its BAR domain’s concave surface harbors additional positive charges that reinforce membrane binding (e.g. K37, K40, K114, R118, R124, and R125) (Pang et al., 2014). In this study, we found that the BAR-PH tandem of ACAP4 possesses intrinsic condensation capabilities and demonstrated that ACAP4 BAR-PH exhibits a strong affinity for negatively charged membranes, evidenced by liposome binding and tubulation assays with SUVs and GUVs (Figure 1). These findings highlight the dual role of the BAR-PH tandem in membrane binding and curvature generation, offering insights into ACAP4’s cytoplasmic-to-membrane localization during intracellular transport (Figure 8). Since membrane protrusions (e.g. lamellipodia and filopodia) require localized membrane curvature generation, ACAP4 likely facilitates the initial membrane deformation necessary for protrusion extension—a core cellular process underlying tumor invasion and metastasis. Our results indicate that the series of positively charged residues-enriched patches in the BAR-PH concave surface play a crucial regulatory role in the function of ACAP4, i.e. membrane remodeling, liposome binding, membrane localization, and migratory activity are often co-regulated, their relationship is not strictly linear, and specific mutations can decouple individual functional outputs (Supplementary Table S2). For example, Patch 1 and Patch 3 mutants showed impaired abilities to induce liposome tubulation and bind to membranes, displaying only partial association with GUVs or forming minimal condensates that inefficiently clustered SUVs in vitro (Figure 2; Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S2), yet still exhibited strong membrane localization and retained the ability to promote cell migration at levels comparable to WT BAR-PH (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure S5 and Table S2). These observations suggest that partial retention of membrane binding capability may be sufficient to support cell motility. By contrast, mutations within the PH domain (Pairs 2–4) resulted in the most consistent and severe phenotypes. They sharply disturbed liposome binding and membrane tubulation, completely disrupted plasma membrane localization, and failed to promote cell migration (Figures 2–5; Supplementary Table S2). These mutations demonstrate a tightly coupled loss of function and point to the PH domain as a critical hub integrating LLPS and membrane remodeling functions. The linker mutation (Pair 1) exhibited an intermediate phenotype—retaining membrane localization and weak liposome binding and tubulation but unable to enhance migration (Figures 2–4; Supplementary Table S2)—suggesting that membrane remodeling rather than LLPS per se may be a more direct contributor to migratory capacity in this case. These findings support a model in which robust cell migration requires the coordinated membrane localization and curvature remodeling, with the highest migratory activity observed when all functions are intact.

Working model of the ACAP4–Ezrin pathway. A conceptual model summarizes how the ACAP4–Ezrin interaction regulates cell migration through membrane and cytoskeletal dynamics. Under basal conditions, full-length ACAP4 remains functionally restrained, likely due to limited accessibility of its BAR-PH tandem domain. In pathological contexts, elevated ACAP4 expression and Ezrin activation promote their interaction, wherein Ezrin FERM binds to the ACAP4 EBM region. This interaction relieves the autoinhibition and facilitates BAR-PH-mediated membrane association. Once at the plasma membrane, ACAP4 induces curvature and, together with Ezrin, coordinates cytoskeletal remodeling. These activities collectively enhance membrane dynamics and cell migration.

Our findings also shed light on how Ezrin regulates the BAR-PH tandem of ACAP4. Ezrin is known to link the plasma membrane with the actin cytoskeleton and is enriched at protrusions where it regulates actin polymerization and membrane dynamics (Zhou et al., 2003; Tsai et al., 2018). Through interaction with the C-terminal region of ACAP4, Ezrin promotes the membrane remodeling and liposome binding capacities of ACAP4 FL and induces robust condensate formation on the surface of GUVs (Figure 6). This interaction likely facilitates ACAP4’s functional transition to the plasma membrane, where it coordinates membrane remodeling and cytoskeletal rearrangement. Continued interaction of Ezrin with ACAP4 may relieve autoinhibition and expose the BAR-PH domain, allowing ACAP4 to maintain or re-establish a condensation state in proximity to the membrane (Figure 8). Building on this mechanistic foundation, we reasonably hypothesize that the aberrant overexpression of ACAP4 under pathological conditions would reinforce the formation of invasive protrusions in tumor cells—further fueling their ability to invade adjacent tissues and initiate metastatic dissemination.

To assess whether ACAP4 modulates cell migration via Arf6 signaling, we examined its effects on Arf6 activity and interaction. Despite prior suggestions of ACAP4 functioning as an Arf6 GAP, our data showed no detectable interaction between ACAP4 and Arf6 and no significant changes in Arf6-GTP levels upon ACAP4 overexpression. These findings suggest that ACAP4’s role in promoting migration is unlikely to be mediated by direct regulation of Arf6. Instead, ACAP4 may influence membrane remodeling and motility through an Arf6-independent mechanism, potentially involving its BAR-PH-mediated membrane binding and remodeling. ACAP4 may also regulate Arf6 activity at the membrane, though our data suggest that the regulation is likely indirect or context-dependent.

Cell migration is a complex process requiring precise coordination of membrane dynamics, cytoskeletal remodeling, and adhesion turnover. While our findings reveal how ACAP4 orchestrates multiple membrane-associated functions through the BAR-PH tandem and Ezrin interaction, the detailed molecular mechanisms linking these properties to downstream effectors remain to be elucidated. Future studies investigating the structural basis of the Ezrin–ACAP4 interaction and its integration with polarity and signaling networks will provide deeper insight into the spatial control of cell migration.

In summary, this study defines a mechanistic framework by which ACAP4 coordinates membrane curvature remodeling and cytoskeletal regulation to drive directional migration. Our findings uncover key features of BAR-PH-driven condensate formation and membrane engagement and suggest that dysregulation of these processes may contribute to the invasive potential of cancer cells. These insights lay the foundation for targeting ACAP4’s regulatory machinery in diseases involving aberrant cell motility.

Materials and methods

Genes and constructs

Human ACAP4 (NCBI reference sequence: NM_017707.4) and Ezrin (NCBI reference sequence: NM_003379.5) constructs were used in this study. Fragments and mutations of these genes were generated by standard PCR and cloned into modified pET-32a vectors containing an N-terminal Trx-His6 tag and an HRV-3C protease cleavage site. ACAP4 FL was cloned into a modified CW-IGKA vector featuring three N-terminal Strep-tags, a C-terminal Trx-His6 tag, and a C-terminal GFP-tag. For transient transfection, ACAP4 FL, fragments, and mutants were inserted into pEGFP-C3 vectors with an N-terminal GFP-tag, while Ezrin and its derivatives were cloned into pCMV vectors with a C-terminal Flag-tag. Lentiviral vectors for shRNA constructs were prepared by inserting sequences targeting ACAP4 (shACAP4-1: 5′-CTCTTCAAGAACCTGATTCA-3′; shACAP4-2: 5′-CAACAAGA CCTATGAGACT-3′) into the PLKO.1 vector. All constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Protein expression and purification

Proteins expressed from modified pET-32a vectors were produced in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells grown in LB medium at 16°C for ∼20 h. Recombinant proteins were purified using Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography followed by Superdex 200 size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (GE Healthcare) in the buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.8), 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and 100 mM NaCl. ACAP4 BAR-PH was stored in 300 mM NaCl buffer, while Ezrin FERM was stored in 200 mM NaCl buffer. Protein tags were removed by HRV-3C protease cleavage at 4°C overnight, followed by an additional purification step if required.

ACAP4 FL protein was expressed in Expi293F cells grown at 37°C for 3 days post-transfection. Proteins were purified using Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography followed by Superdex 200 Increase SEC (GE Healthcare) using the same buffer.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination

The purified ACAP4 BAR (7–282) was set up for crystal screening at 10 mg/ml. The crystals were obtained by the hanging-drop method at 16°C. The best crystals were grown in a reservoir containing 0.2 M lithium sulfate monohydrate, 0.1 M Tris (pH 8.5), and 25% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 3350. The diffraction data were collected at BL18U at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The crystals were cryoprotected in reservoir solution with 25% ethylene glycol. The diffraction data were processed with XDS (Kabsch, 2010). The structure was solved by the molecular replacement method using the predicted ACAP4 BAR structure model as the searching model through the software suits of PHASER (McCoy et al., 2007). Further refinement was performed using PHENIX (Adams et al., 2010) and COOT (Emsley and Cowtan, 2004). The final refinement statistics of the structures are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Structural diagrams were prepared by PyMOL.

Protein prediction

In this study, the molecular replacement model was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. The structure was retrieved by searching the database with the protein name ‘ASAP3’ as the keyword. Among the search results, the predicted structure model identified as AF-Q8TDY4-F1 was selected. The corresponding structure file was downloaded in PDB format and used for subsequent molecular replacement analysis.

SUV preparation

Lipids (Avanti Polar Lipids) were dissolved in chloroform and stored at −20°C. Desired lipid mixtures (POPC-SUV: 98% POPC + 2% dye; PIP2-SUV: 76% POPC + 20% DOPS + 2% PIP2 + 2% dye; Brain-SUV: 98% brain lipid + 2% dye DiI) were prepared in a glass vial, dried under airflow, and further dried under vacuum for at least 3 h. Lipids were resuspended in the buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and 100 mM NaCl) containing 1% sodium cholate. Detergent was removed using a HiTrap desalting column (GE Healthcare), allowing SUVs to form spontaneously during the process.

GUV preparation

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 10% w/v) was coated onto glass slides and dried at 50°C for 30 min. Lipid mixtures (PIP2-GUV or Brain-GUV) were dissolved in chloroform at 1 mg/ml and spread on the dried PVA layer. After overnight vacuum drying, the lipid-coated slide was incubated with GUV preparation buffer (200 mM sucrose, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.2, and 50 mM NaCl) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. GUV solutions were stored on ice and used within 2 days.

Negative staining

For negative staining, 300-mesh carbon-coated copper grids were glow-discharged for 10 sec at 15 mA (easiGlow, PELCO). A 5-μl drop of 0.02 mg/ml SUV or protein-SUV mixture was applied to the grid, incubated for 2 min, blotted, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 2 min. Images were collected using a Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 200 kV, equipped with a 4k × 4k BM-Ceta camera.

FPLC coupled with SLS

Protein samples were analyzed using SEC coupled with SLS to determine their molecular weight and oligomeric state in solution. A total of 100 μM of purified protein (100 μl injection volume) was loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with SEC buffer consisting of 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Chromatographic separation was carried out at a constant flow rate of 0.5 ml/min using an ÄKTA FPLC system (GE Healthcare).

The system was coupled inline to a miniDAWN TREOS multi-angle SLS detector and an Optilab T-rEX refractive index detector (Wyatt Technology). Data were collected in real time and analyzed using ASTRA 6 software (Wyatt Technology), which enabled the determination of the absolute molecular weight based on light scattering intensity and refractive index changes, independent of protein standards.

All experiments were performed at room temperature (∼25°C), and the column was calibrated and maintained according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

ITC

ITC experiments were conducted at 25°C using a MicroCal VP-ITC calorimeter (Malvern Instruments) to determine the thermodynamic parameters of the ACAP4–Ezrin interaction. Prior to the experiment, all protein and ligand solutions were thoroughly dialyzed against the same purification buffer to minimize heat effects from buffer mismatch. The dialysate was also used to prepare reference and dilution buffers to ensure baseline consistency.

Typically, the titration cell (600 μl) was filled with the Ezrin protein solution (at a concentration of 20 μM), while the syringe was loaded with the ACAP4 proteins (at a concentration of 200 μM). A total of 27 injections were performed: the initial injection was 5 μl (to minimize system artifacts), followed by 26 injections of 10 μl each, spaced at 180-sec intervals to allow complete equilibration. The stirring speed was set to 300 rpm during the titration to ensure proper mixing.

Raw data were integrated and baseline-corrected using MicroCal Origin software (Malvern), and binding isotherms were fitted to a single-site (one set of sites) binding model to derive the binding affinity.

Protein labeling with fluorophores

Purified proteins were buffer-exchanged into labeling buffer containing 100 mM NaHCO3 (pH 8.3), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT using a HiTrapTM desalting column (GE Healthcare). Fluorophores (iFluor 488, Cy5; AAT Bioquest) were added at a molar ratio of 1:1.2 (protein: dye) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The reaction was quenched by adding 200 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.3), and unreacted dye was removed via size exclusion using a HiTrap desalting column. Labeling efficiency and dye-to-protein ratios were determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Imaging and FRAP assay

For in vitro imaging, labeled proteins or reconstituted liposome mixtures were loaded into flow chambers assembled from glass coverslips and microscope slides sealed with double-sided tape. Protein condensates were visualized using a Zeiss LSM 980 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective.

FRAP experiments were performed under the same imaging conditions. A defined region of interest within a condensate was photobleached using high-intensity laser illumination, and fluorescence recovery was monitored over time. Recovery curves were normalized and plotted using ZEN software (Zeiss) and GraphPad Prism.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T, HeLa, and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. For transient transfection, cells were transfected with plasmid DNA using polyethyleneimine (PEI; Polysciences) at a DNA:PEI ratio of 1:3 (w/w). Lentiviral infections were carried out using filtered viral supernatants harvested from HEK293T cells co-transfected with psPAX2, pMD2.G, and PLKO.1 vectors.

Immunostaining and confocal microscopy

Cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After washing, cells were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody against GFP (mouse monoclonal, Proteintech 66002-1) diluted in blocking buffer. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and coverslips were mounted in antifade mounting medium containing glycerol. Confocal images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion lens.

Immunoblotting

Cells were first rinsed twice with pre-cooled PBS to remove culture medium and impurities. Subsequently, the cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were mixed with 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer, and the mixtures were then boiled for 10 min to denature the proteins. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 2× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 95°C for 5 min, and separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on a 10% polyacrylamide gel. Proteins were then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against ACAP4 (rabbit, Bioss) and β-actin (mouse, Bioss) overnight at 4°C on a shaker. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit and anti-mouse IgG, Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Tanon-5200 imaging system) and quantified using ImageJ software. β-actin was used as a loading control for normalization.

Transwell assay

Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers with 8-μm pore-size polycarbonate membrane inserts. HeLa or MDA-MB-231 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber of each insert. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following 24 h of incubation at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Five random fields per insert were imaged under a light microscope, and the number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ software. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility, and statistical analysis was performed to compare the numbers of migrated cells between experimental groups.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded at an appropriate density in 6-well dishes and allowed to grow to confluence in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. Once confluent, a linear wound was created across the cell monolayer using a sterile pipette tip of a defined width, and the cells were gently washed with sterile PBS to remove debris. The wound area was immediately measured (0 h) using bright-field microscopy with a calibrated scale and ImageJ software. The cells were then cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and wound closure was monitored at 24 h post-wounding under identical imaging conditions. The percentage of wound closure was calculated by comparing the wound area at 24 h to the initial wound area using the formula: wound closure rate (%) = (initial wound area—wound area at 24 h)/24 h × 100%. The experiment was repeated four times to ensure reproducibility, and statistical analysis was performed to compare wound closure rates between experimental groups.

Arf6-GTP pull-down assay

The activation level of Arf6-GTP was evaluated using the Arf6 Pulldown Activation Assay Kit (Wuhan NewEast Biosciences, 82401) following the manufacturer’s protocol. HEK293T cells were seeded in 10-cm culture dishes and transfected with either GFP empty vector or GFP-ACAP4 FL plasmid. At ∼90% confluence, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in the provided lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12000× g for 10 min at 4°C. A small portion of the supernatant was mixed with 2× SDS loading buffer and used as the input control to assess total Arf6 expression. The remaining lysate was incubated with the anti-active Arf6 mouse monoclonal antibody conjugated to agarose beads at 4°C overnight with gentle rotation to specifically pull down the active GTP-bound form of Arf6. After incubation, the beads were washed thoroughly with wash buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× SDS loading buffer.

All samples, including input and pull-down fractions, were subjected to SDS–PAGE and immunoblotting with the anti-Arf6 rabbit polyclonal antibody.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For all experiments, at least three independent biological replicates were performed, and data from each experiment were analyzed. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Data were initially evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was subsequently assessed through the Bartlett’s test or Brown–Forsythe test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, if the data satisfied both normality (Shapiro–Wilk, P ≥ 0.05) and homogeneity (Bartlett’s or Brown–Forsythe, P ≥ 0.05) of variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc Bonferroni correction was applied. In cases where the variance homogeneity assumptions were violated (P < 0.05), the Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests were used instead, as they are more robust for handling data with heterogeneous variances.

Statistical significance is denoted as follows: ns, not significant (P > 0.05); *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.
