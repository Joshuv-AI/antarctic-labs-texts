Outcome Modification and Disclosure Practices in Sinonasal Clinical Trials: A Registry‐Based Cross‐Sectional Analysis
Authors: Kohl Kirby, Harrison Smith, Ty Lutze, Rachel Hazlitt, Ryan Sherry, Ahmed Elghzali, Tag Harris, Daniel Archer, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo characterize the frequency, nature, timing, and transparency of prespecified outcome modifications in interventional trials for inflammatory sinonasal disease registered on ClinicalTrials.gov.MethodsWe identified interventional trials for inflammatory sinonasal disease registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with published primary results. Earliest and most recent registry versions were compared to identify substantive modifications to primary, secondary, and other prespecified outcomes. Modifications were categorized by anticipated interpretive impact (high or moderate), and timing relative to trial completion and publication was recorded. Disclosure of outcome modifications was assessed in registry records, including posted protocol documents when available, and in associated peer‐reviewed publications. Descriptive analyses summarized modification patterns, and unadjusted regression analyses explored factors associated with high‐impact modifications.ResultsSeventy‐two trials met inclusion criteria. Substantive outcome modifications occurred in 68 trials (94.4%), with primary outcomes affected in 59 trials (81.9%). The median number of substantive modifications per trial was seven (interquartile range, 3–10). Common modification types included clarification or increased specification, changes in assessment timing, outcome additions, and complete redefinitions involving changes in measurement instruments or methodologies. All recorded modifications were entered after primary completion or publication. Disclosure was uncommon: 3 of 68 modified trials (4.4%) acknowledged changes in the registry, 1 (1.5%) in the publication, and 4 (5.9%) in either source. Trials prespecifying more than three outcomes and trials completed after implementation of the FDAAA Final Rule had lower odds of high‐impact modification.ConclusionsOutcome modifications are frequent in sinonasal trials but are rarely transparently documented. Given the central role of symptom‐based, quality‐of‐life, and endoscopic outcomes in rhinology, clearer documentation of outcome changes is essential to support interpretability and evidence synthesis.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: chronic rhinosinusitis, ClinicalTrials.gov, nasal polyps, outcome reporting, transparency, trial registration

Introduction

Clear and consistent definition of trial outcomes is essential for interpreting treatment effects and comparing results across studies. Selective outcome reporting is well documented across medical research, with numerous studies demonstrating discrepancies between prespecified outcomes and those ultimately reported in publications [1, 2, 3]. In addition to selective reporting, outcomes may also evolve over time due to methodological refinement, instrument validation, or regulatory reporting requirements. These inconsistencies weaken confidence in trial validity and complicate interpretation, particularly, when outcomes shift in ways that cannot be easily reconstructed from available records.

Despite extensive meta‐research in other medical specialties, outcome reporting practices within rhinology have not been systematically examined. One prior analysis has evaluated registration and reporting practices in rhinosinusitis trials, identifying inconsistencies in trial registration but not examining outcome modifications [4]. This gap is notable because trials in chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyposis rely heavily on patient‐reported symptom measures, quality‐of‐life instruments such as the SNOT‐22, and endoscopic or radiologic grading systems, outcomes that are inherently variable and have evolved considerably over the past decade [5, 6]. EPOS recommendations have further influenced the refinement and standardization of these measurement tools [7]. However, the extent to which such modifications are transparently documented in rhinologic trials remains unknown.

Federal policy reinforces the expectation of clear reporting. The FDAAA Final Rule (2017) requires accurate and timely updates to prespecified outcomes in ClinicalTrials.gov records [8, 9]. These requirements are intended to preserve an interpretable audit trail rather than to restrict legitimate outcome refinement. However, the extent to which rhinologic trials comply with these requirements or disclose outcome changes in either registry entries or publications has not been established.

To address this gap, we conducted a registry‐based cross‐sectional analysis of interventional trials for inflammatory sinonasal disease. Our objective was to characterize the frequency and nature of outcome modifications recorded in ClinicalTrials.gov and to determine whether these changes were transparently disclosed in registry records and associated peer‐reviewed publications. Accordingly, this analysis emphasizes outcome traceability and documentation practices to support interpretive clarity in sinonasal trials.

Methods

Study Design and Registration

We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov data to evaluate disclosure practices surrounding outcome modifications in interventional studies of inflammatory sinonasal diseases. For each included trial, we compared the original registry entry with the most recent version to identify changes to prespecified outcomes as reflected in registry version histories. We then assessed whether these modifications were disclosed in the registry's “History of Changes” section, posted protocol documents, and in associated peer‐reviewed publications. This approach captures cumulative changes documented across the trial lifecycle as recorded in the registry. We followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines for literature searching, screening, and data extraction [10]. This study did not constitute human subjects research under 45 CFR §46.102(d) and (f) and therefore did not require institutional review board approval [11].

Data Source and Search Strategy

We searched ClinicalTrials.gov on June 28, 2025, to identify interventional trials evaluating treatments for inflammatory sinonasal diseases. To optimize search precision, we developed a Boolean search strategy using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and relevant synonyms rather than relying on ClinicalTrials.gov's default Unified Medical Language System expansion [12]. We used common clinical terms for inflammatory sinonasal disease, including “chronic rhinosinusitis,” “nasal polyps,” “CRSwNP,” and “sinusitis,” combined using Boolean “OR” operators to capture trials using different but clinically equivalent labels. These terms were then combined with ClinicalTrials.gov filters for interventional study design and eligible trial phases using Boolean “AND” operators, and the search was restricted to trials with posted results to enable evaluation of outcome disclosure practices. The complete search string and list of included trials are publicly archived on the Open Science Framework [13].

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible trials met the following criteria: (1) interventional design; (2) evaluation of pharmacologic, biologic, or device‐based treatment for inflammatory sinonasal diseases; (3) trial phase of 2, 3, 4, or “Not Applicable”; (4) start date between September 27, 2009, and December 31, 2024; (5) at least one prespecified outcome in the most recent ClinicalTrials.gov entry; and (6) linkage to a peer‐reviewed publication reporting primary trial results.

We excluded Phase 1 trials, noninterventional studies, trials focusing exclusively on behavioral interventions or lifestyle counseling, and studies investigating secondary effects of sinonasal diseases rather than primary therapeutic interventions.

Registry‐Publication Matching

We identified corresponding publications by reviewing citations linked within each trial's ClinicalTrials.gov record. When no citation was listed, we performed targeted searches in PubMed and Google Scholar using the NCT number, official trial title, sponsor name, and principal investigator. Only peer‐reviewed journal articles reporting primary trial outcomes were eligible. When multiple publications existed for a single trial, we selected the article presenting results for the timeframe aligned with the registry's primary completion date. We excluded conference abstracts, preprints, and nonpeer‐reviewed materials.

Two reviewers (K.K., T.L.) independently performed publication matching and eligibility assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer (A.E.) when necessary.

Data Extraction and Training

Prior to data collection, reviewers completed structured training on the study protocol, outcome classification criteria, and use of a standardized Google Form for data extraction. A calibration phase using 10 pilot studies ensured consistency in coding and interpretation and was used to refine classification definitions prior to full extraction.

Two reviewers (K.K., H.S.) independently extracted data from ClinicalTrials.gov and matched publications. Extracted variables included trial characteristic's (NCT number, phase, sponsor, enrollment, intervention type), outcome modification details (type, timing, category), and disclosure status in both registry (including narrative fields and posted protocol documents when available) and publication. We used ClinicalTrials.gov's “History of Changes” tool to perform side‐by‐side comparisons of the earliest and most recent registry versions [14].

Outcome Modification Classification

To characterize changes to registered outcomes, we reviewed all modifications recorded in the ClinicalTrials.gov outcome history and mapped each change to its corresponding structured registry field (e.g., outcome added or removed, measurement instrument or methodology changed, assessment timing changed). Modifications were grouped according to their potential impact on how trial outcomes are interpreted. Although reporting guidelines such as CONSORT and SPIRIT emphasize prospective outcome specification and transparent reporting of changes, they do not distinguish modification types by interpretive consequence [15, 16].

High‐impact modifications included changes affecting the interpretation or prioritization of trial endpoints: addition or removal of a primary outcome, reclassification between primary and secondary outcomes, or complete redefinition of a primary outcome, defined as a change in the measurement instrument or assessment methodology used to operationalize the outcome (e.g., changing from a qualitative symptom description to a validated scoring system such as the Lund–Mackay CT score). These changes alter the construct used to judge trial efficacy.

Moderate‐impact modifications included changes influencing outcome specification without altering the underlying construct, such as addition or removal of secondary outcomes, changes in assessment timing, or increased specificity of outcome definitions.

Low‐impact modifications were limited to minor wording, formatting, or typographical changes unlikely to affect interpretation.

For analysis, substantive outcome modification was defined as the presence of at least one high‐ or moderate‐impact change. Trials were classified as having modified outcomes if any substantive modification occurred between the original and most recent registry versions.

The primary outcome was the presence of at least one high‐impact modification affecting prespecified primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included the presence of any substantive modification, the number and type of modifications per trial, modification timing relative to trial completion and publication, and disclosure of modifications in the registry and/or publication.

Disclosure Assessment

For each trial with substantive modifications, two reviewers independently assessed disclosure in two locations:

ClinicalTrials.gov registry: We reviewed the “History of Changes” section, posted protocol documents (when available), and any explanatory text fields to determine whether outcome modifications were acknowledged in any form beyond the automatic field‐level change log generated by the registry.

ClinicalTrials.gov registry: We reviewed the “History of Changes” section, posted protocol documents (when available), and any explanatory text fields to determine whether outcome modifications were acknowledged in any form beyond the automatic field‐level change log generated by the registry.

Published manuscript: We conducted a full‐text review of the methods, results, discussion, footnotes, and Supporting Information. Disclosure was considered present if the publication made any explicit mention, however brief, that one or more outcomes had been modified, including general statements acknowledging outcome changes without justification, explanation, or timing. We did not assess the adequacy, rationale, or timing of disclosures, only their presence or absence. Verbatim text supporting disclosure classifications was copied into the extraction form for verification.

Verbatim text supporting disclosure classifications was copied into the extraction form for verification.

Statistical Analysis

Trial characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Associations between trial characteristics and outcome modification were assessed using χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Firth penalized logistic regression was used to estimate unadjusted odds ratios for predictors of any substantive modification and high‐impact modification. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 with α = 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Open Science and Transparency

All study materials, including the protocol, search strategy, list of included trials, data extraction form, outcome classification guide, and analysis code, are publicly archived on the Open Science Framework to ensure reproducibility [13].

Results

Study Selection and General Characteristics

The search identified 375 ClinicalTrials.gov records, of which 72 interventional trials met inclusion criteria after screening and full‐text review (Figure 1). The included trials were predominantly late‐phase and industry sponsored, with Phase 3 studies most common (37.5%) and nearly all trials using randomized designs (93.1%). Most studies were conducted in the United States (79.2%), and drug interventions accounted for the majority of trials (72.2%). Median enrollment was 101 participants (IQR 51–330), reflecting moderate sample sizes typical of interventional sinonasal research.

Outcome Modifications

Substantive outcome modifications were nearly universal. Overall, 68 of 72 trials (94.4%) had at least one high‐ or moderate‐impact change, with a median of 7 substantive modifications per trial (IQR 3–10). The most frequent modification types were clarification or increased specification (81.9%), changes to outcome timing or assessment windows (69.4%), and complete outcome redefinitions (66.7%). Outcome additions occurred in just over half of trials (54.2%), while removals (27.8%) and reclassifications (2.8%) were less common. Modifications most often involved primary (81.9%) and secondary outcomes (77.8%), with other outcomes rarely affected (9.7%) (Figure 2).

High‐impact modifications affecting primary outcomes were present in 42 trials (58.3%), while 26 trials (36.1%) had only moderate‐impact changes; only 4 trials (5.6%) showed no substantive modification. Concordance between original and final outcome specifications was low, particularly for primary and secondary outcomes: primary outcomes were fully concordant in 18.1% of trials and secondary outcomes in 22.2%, compared with 90.3% concordance for other outcomes. Timing and Disclosure

Among trials with at least one substantive modification (N = 68), all outcome changes were recorded after primary completion (35/68, 51.5%) or after publication (33/68, 48.5%); no trials documented modifications during enrollment. Disclosure was rare: three trials (4.4%) reported any acknowledgment in ClinicalTrials.gov, one (1.5%) in the peer‐reviewed publication, and four (5.9%) in either source. Consequently, the vast majority of modified trials (64/68, 94.1%) disclosed outcome changes in neither the registry nor the publication (Table 3).

For any substantive modification, no trial characteristics were significantly associated with modification in unadjusted analyses; adjusted effects were nonestimable. Industry funding showed a borderline association with substantive modification (OR 9.4, 95% CI 0.7–519.6; p = 0.05) but did not meet the prespecified significance threshold (Figure 3a).

Presence and Total Number of Modifications

Across modification types, changes were concentrated in primary and secondary outcomes, whereas other outcomes were infrequently affected. Additions were most common among secondary outcomes (35/72 trials, 48.6%), compared with primary outcomes (13/72, 18.1%) and other outcomes (2/72, 2.8%). Primary outcomes exhibited the highest rates of timing changes (40/72, 55.6%), complete redefinitions (38/72, 52.8%), and clarification or specification updates (51/72, 70.8%). Removals and reclassifications were uncommon across all outcome categories.

A similar pattern was observed for the magnitude of modification. Secondary outcomes had the highest mean number of additions per trial (mean 2.7, SD 4.4), compared with primary outcomes (mean 0.3, SD 0.9) and other outcomes (mean 0.0, SD 0.2). When summed across all substantive modification types, secondary outcomes carried the greatest overall modification burden (mean 6.2, SD 8.0 per trial), followed by primary outcomes (mean 2.5, SD 2.1), with minimal modification of other outcomes (mean 0.4, SD 1.5). Discussion

Principal Findings

In this cross‐sectional evaluation of interventional trials for inflammatory sinonasal disease, we found that outcome modifications, as recorded in registry version histories, were common and affected nearly all trials. Primary outcomes, those most central to interpreting efficacy, were affected in the majority of trials. Disclosure of these changes, however, was uncommon. Although federal policy requires accurate and timely updates to registered outcomes, very few trials documented modifications in the registry or in their associated publication [8]. This lack of transparency challenges the ability of clinicians and reviewers to understand how closely published analyses reflect the trial's original intent [2, 17].

Context and Interpretation

The modification rate observed here exceeds those reported in many other specialties, where outcome discrepancies typically range from 30% to 60% depending on field and methodology [1, 2]. By examining a broad range of modification types, including timing changes, redefinitions, and additions to secondary outcomes, this study provides a detailed view of how outcome specifications evolve over time. The high frequency of primary outcome modification, however, highlights substantial instability in the outcomes most central to interpreting treatment efficacy.

This pattern likely reflects, in part, the evolving nature of rhinologic outcome measurement. As symptom indices, quality‐of‐life instruments, and endoscopic scoring systems have matured, investigators may reasonably refine endpoints, for example, transitioning from nonvalidated symptom measures to SNOT‐22, refining endoscopic grading systems, or adopting composite responder definitions aligned with EPOS recommendations [7, 18, 19, 20]. Such refinements do not necessarily imply inappropriate analytic flexibility but underscore the importance of transparent documentation so that outcome changes can be interpreted in context.

The most consequential changes were those altering what was measured (instrument or methodological changes) or when it was measured (timepoint shifts), as these modify the clinical construct used to judge benefit. Notably, recorded modifications were entered after primary completion or publication. Although registry timestamps do not reveal when decisions were made, retrospective entry without clear documentation limits interpretability of reported endpoints. Retrospective updates need not imply bias, but without explanatory text, their rationale remains unclear. In a field where symptom‐based and endoscopic outcomes strongly influence perceptions of benefit, clarity regarding when and why outcomes change is essential [18, 20].

Two findings are particularly relevant to outcome interpretability. Trials prespecifying more than three outcomes had lower odds of high‐impact modification, suggesting that more comprehensive upfront outcome planning may reduce later changes affecting interpretability. Trials completed after implementation of the Final Rule were also less likely to modify primary outcomes, indicating that regulatory expectations may be improving outcome stability. Although modest in magnitude, these associations are clinically meaningful, as high‐impact modifications alter the constructs used to judge treatment efficacy in CRS and CRSwNP.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Evidence Synthesis

For clinicians, undisclosed outcome modification complicates interpretation of treatment efficacy, especially in a field where patient‐reported measures and symptom scores play a central role. When outcomes are modified without clear explanation, the link between the prespecified hypothesis and the reported result becomes difficult to trace.

For systematic reviewers and guideline developers, this ambiguity poses challenges in assessing risk of bias and determining whether outcomes were chosen prospectively or reflect post hoc decisions. Because chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyposis management frequently depends on nuanced interpretation of heterogeneous outcomes, transparent reporting is particularly important to support evidence‐based recommendations [21, 22].

Pathways to Improved Transparency

Frameworks such as SPIRIT‐Outcomes and CONSORT‐Outcomes offer a clear foundation for improving outcome reporting by defining how prespecified endpoints should be documented, tracked, and transparently updated [15, 16]. Building on these principles, investigators can strengthen reporting by including brief statements explaining when prespecified outcomes change and why. Journals can reinforce this approach by requesting an outcome‐consistency statement at submission and by verifying registered outcomes during peer review. Registries could also prompt investigators to provide short justifications when outcome fields are updated, creating an accessible audit trail without restricting legitimate protocol evolution. Collectively, these measures would support more consistent and interpretable disclosure practices and better align rhinology research with contemporary expectations for clinical‐trial transparency.

Limitations and Strengths

This study is limited to trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, and we focused on trials with published results, leaving modification patterns in unpublished studies unknown. In addition, this analysis did not evaluate whether outcome modifications favored statistically significant results or altered the direction of reported findings. Classification of modifications required judgment, although structured criteria and dual‐reviewer assessment were used to reduce subjectivity. While our classification scheme was intentionally pragmatic and applied consistently, alternative frameworks might categorize specific modifications differently. Comparing only the earliest and most recent registry versions limited our ability to fully reconstruct intermediate changes.

Strengths include systematic review of registry version histories, detailed categorization of multiple modification types, and dual‐reviewer extraction. This approach provides a comprehensive and clinically relevant assessment of outcome reporting practices in sinonasal trials.

Conclusions

Substantive outcome modifications were widespread in sinonasal trials, yet disclosure was uncommon. Many modifications likely reflect reasonable refinements based on evolving measurement standards, but the absence of transparent reporting makes it difficult to assess their impact on interpretation. Strengthening transparency, through investigator reporting, journal policies, and modest registry improvements, would enhance the clarity, credibility, and clinical utility of evidence in inflammatory sinonasal disease research.
