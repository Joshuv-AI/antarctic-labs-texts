Kinesiophobia and knee biomechanical characteristics in patients with postoperative tibial plateau fractures across body mass index categories: a cross-sectional mediation analysis
Authors: Gangqiang Du, Zihao Sun, Bixuan Duan, Lina Zhang, Pei Ma, Li Yu, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate knee biomechanical characteristics and kinesiophobia levels across different body mass index (BMI) categories in patients following tibial plateau fracture surgery, and to examine the mediating role of kinesiophobia in the relationship between BMI and knee biomechanical outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional controlled study was conducted. Sixty postoperative tibial plateau fracture patients were stratified into Group A (obese, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2), Group B (overweight, BMI 24.0–27.9 kg/m2), and Group C (normal weight, BMI 18.5–23.9 kg/m2), with 20 patients per group, alongside 20 healthy controls (Group D). Kinesiophobia and knee function were assessed using the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia-17 (TSK-17) and the Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale, respectively. A three-dimensional motion capture system synchronized with a force platform was used to collect spatiotemporal gait parameters and knee kinematic and kinetic data. Between- and within-group bilateral limb differences were analyzed, and correlation analysis, multiple linear regression, and bootstrap mediation analysis were performed to examine the mediating effect of kinesiophobia.ResultsBetween-group comparisons revealed that Lysholm scores progressively decreased (Group A: 73.80 ± 4.74; Group B: 77.95 ± 2.86; Group C: 81.65 ± 3.63), whereas TSK-17 scores progressively increased (Group A: 57.00 ± 3.88; Group B: 51.40 ± 3.44; Group C: 46.70 ± 4.66) with increasing BMI (P < 0.01). On the affected side, cadence (Group A: 60.90 ± 12.56; Group D: 105.90 ± 4.61 steps/min), gait velocity (Group A: 0.58 ± 0.19; Group D: 1.08 ± 0.06 m/s), and sagittal plane range of motion (Group A: 47.46° ± 9.46°; Group D: 67.23° ± 2.82°) progressively decreased, whereas sagittal plane moments progressively increased (Group A: 680.35 ± 130.98; Group D: 225.13 ± 85.78 N·m) across BMI groups (P < 0.01). Within-group comparisons demonstrated that sagittal plane range of motion on the affected side was significantly smaller than that on the unaffected side, and that joint moments were significantly greater on the affected side across all three patient groups (P < 0.05); however, no significant bilateral differences were observed in the control group. Mediation analysis indicated that kinesiophobia partially mediated the relationship between BMI and gait velocity on the unaffected side (indirect effect = − 0.01; 95% CI [− 0.03, − 0.00]) as well as between BMI and sagittal plane moments on the affected side (indirect effect = 7.45; 95% CI [0.43, 18.56]), with mediation proportions of 48.4% and 27.5%, respectively.ConclusionPatients with postoperative tibial plateau fractures and higher BMI exhibited significantly worse knee function, greater kinesiophobia, and compromised biomechanical characteristics, including slower gait, reduced sagittal plane range of motion, and elevated knee joint moments. Kinesiophobia partially mediated the adverse effects of elevated BMI on gait velocity and sagittal plane moments. Clinical rehabilitation programs should incorporate BMI-stratified individualized interventions integrating weight management with targeted psychological support to reduce kinesiophobia.Trial registration: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2500101020. Registered on 18/04/2025.Keywords: Body mass index, Tibial plateau fracture, Gait analysis, Kinesiophobia, Biomechanics, Rehabilitation

Introduction

Tibial plateau fractures are common intra-articular injuries of the knee joint, accounting for approximately 1% of all fractures, and occur predominantly in individuals aged 40–50 years following high-energy trauma [1]. As a critical weight-bearing structure of the lower extremity, the anatomical integrity of the tibial plateau is essential for maintaining knee joint stability and normal gait function [2, 3]. Although open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) can effectively restore joint anatomical alignment, many patients experience persistent postoperative functional impairments, including restricted joint mobility, muscle weakness, and gait abnormalities [4–6]. Consequently, postoperative rehabilitation and outcome evaluation must extend beyond radiographic healing to encompass functional and movement-related indicators that reflect real-world performance.

Knee biomechanical characteristics during functional tasks—such as walking, stair negotiation, and sit-to-stand transitions—provide objective insights into load redistribution patterns, joint motion regulation, and moment generation [7, 8]. Following surgical treatment, patients frequently adopt protective movement strategies, including unloading of the operated limb, modification of knee flexion patterns, and increased reliance on the contralateral limb for compensatory support [9]. These biomechanical adaptations carry significant clinical implications, as abnormal joint loading and movement asymmetry may contribute to persistent pain, diminished physical function, and accelerated cartilage degeneration [10, 11].

Beyond structural and neuromuscular impairments, psychological factors substantially influence postoperative movement behavior. Kinesiophobia—defined as an excessive, irrational fear of movement due to perceived vulnerability to reinjury—is increasingly recognized in musculoskeletal and postsurgical populations [12, 13]. According to the fear-avoidance model, pain-related fear motivates patients to avoid physical activity, leading to deconditioning and progressive disability that ultimately impedes recovery [14]. Emerging evidence suggests that kinesiophobia may serve as a key determinant of rehabilitation outcomes following surgical treatment of tibial plateau fractures [15, 16].

BMI represents another critical factor affecting postoperative recovery. Patients with elevated BMI demonstrate lower knee function scores, higher complication rates, and prolonged rehabilitation periods [17–20]. However, the mechanisms underlying the association between BMI and functional outcomes remain poorly understood. Increased body weight directly elevates mechanical loading on the knee joint, thereby altering gait parameters and joint kinematic and kinetic characteristics [21–25]. Moreover, patients with elevated BMI often experience prolonged postoperative pain and heightened susceptibility to kinesiophobia, resulting in activity avoidance, muscle atrophy, and joint stiffness [14, 26, 27]. Importantly, kinesiophobia may mediate the relationship between BMI and adverse biomechanical alterations. Despite this potential mechanistic pathway, most existing studies rely exclusively on subjective functional scales and lack objective biomechanical comparisons across BMI categories, and the mediating role of kinesiophobia in this relationship remains inadequately investigated.

Therefore, this study systematically compared kinesiophobia levels and knee biomechanical characteristics among postoperative tibial plateau fracture patients across BMI categories, elucidated the mediating role of kinesiophobia in the relationship between BMI and postoperative knee biomechanics, and aimed to provide evidence for individualized BMI-stratified rehabilitation strategies.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional controlled study was conducted at the Departments of Trauma Orthopedics and Rehabilitation Medicine, Binzhou Medical University Hospital, between April and November 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University Hospital (Approval No.: KYLL-366) and prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2500101020; Registration Date: 18/04/2025). All procedures were performed in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent following a comprehensive briefing on the study objectives and procedures. A single-blind design was implemented, with data analysts blinded to participant group allocation.

Sample size calculation

Based on the study by Adouni et al. [21, 28], assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.90, the required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1, yielding a minimum of 17 participants per group. Accounting for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 15%, the final sample size was set at 20 participants per group, totaling 80 participants.

Study participants

Sixty patients who had undergone tibial plateau fracture surgery were enrolled and stratified into three groups based on Chinese adult BMI classification criteria: Group A (obese, BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2, n = 20), Group B (overweight, BMI 24.0–27.9 kg/m2, n = 20), and Group C (normal weight, BMI 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, n = 20) [29, 30]. Additionally, 20 healthy volunteers matched for age, sex, and height were recruited as Group D (healthy controls).

Inclusion criteria (1) age 18–65 years; (2) unilateral tibial plateau fracture (Schatzker Type II) treated with ORIF, followed by completion of standard rehabilitation; (3) postoperative duration ≥ 12 months with radiographically and clinically confirmed fracture union; (4) ability to ambulate independently for ≥ 10 min without assistive devices; (5) intact cognitive function sufficient to understand and comply with all study assessments; and (6) voluntary participation with provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria (1) multiple lower extremity fractures or history of ipsilateral lower-limb surgery; (2) severe cardiopulmonary, hepatorenal, or neurological disorders; (3) prior diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis or other degenerative joint diseases of the lower extremity; (4) long-term use of medications affecting neuromuscular function; (5) pregnancy or lactation; and (6) psychiatric disorders or poor compliance precluding assessment participation.

Assessment methods

Clinical assessments

Baseline demographic and anthropometric data, including age, sex, height, weight, and BMI, were collected. (2) Kinesiophobia was assessed using the TSK-17, which comprises 17 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 17 to 68; higher scores indicate greater kinesiophobia [31, 32]. (3) Knee function was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale, which includes eight domains: limping, support, locking, instability, pain, swelling, stair climbing, and squatting; total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating superior knee function [33, 34].

Lower extremity biomechanical assessment

Lower extremity gait biomechanics were assessed using an eight-camera Vicon three-dimensional motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) sampling at 200 Hz, integrated with two AMTI force plates (Watertown, MA, USA) sampling at 1000 Hz. The two systems were hardware-synchronized to ensure temporal alignment of kinematic and kinetic data. Prior to data collection, each participant changed into form-fitting shorts and underwent anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, leg length, knee width, and ankle width, which served as input parameters for individualized biomechanical modeling. Fourteen-millimeter reflective markers were then placed bilaterally on standardized pelvic and lower extremity anatomical landmarks by a single trained examiner in accordance with the Plug-in Gait lower-limb model. Following system calibration and force plate zeroing, a static standing trial was recorded to establish the anatomical reference frame for subsequent dynamic analysis (Fig. 1).

Placement of retroreflective markers

Each participant was instructed to walk barefoot along a 6-m level walkway at a self-selected speed with natural arm swing. Five trials were performed per participant, and one valid trial—exhibiting a stable gait pattern, complete unilateral foot contact on the force plate, and no marker occlusion—was selected for analysis. The spatiotemporal parameters of the selected trial were confirmed to be consistent with the participant’s mean values across all trials to ensure representativeness. Marker trajectory data were post-processed using Vicon Nexus 2.12 software, with a Woltring spline smoothing filter at a cutoff frequency of 6 Hz applied to attenuate high-frequency noise; any trial containing trajectory gaps exceeding 10 consecutive frames was excluded. Joint angles and internal moments of the knee in the sagittal, coronal, and transverse planes were subsequently calculated throughout the full gait cycle using the constructed lower extremity model.

The outcome variables comprised spatiotemporal parameters (cadence, gait velocity, stride length, step length, and step width), knee joint kinematic parameters (peak angles and angular excursions in three planes), and knee joint kinetic parameters (peak internal moments in three planes). All variables were extracted separately for the affected and unaffected limbs to facilitate inter-limb and inter-group comparisons.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Knee function and kinesiophobia scores were compared among the three patient groups, whereas gait and biomechanical parameters were compared among all four groups including the control group. For data meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance was performed followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple comparisons; for data violating these assumptions, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied, with Dunn’s test and Bonferroni correction used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Within-group comparisons between affected and unaffected limbs were performed using paired-samples t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate associations between BMI and outcome measures, as appropriate for data distribution. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with BMI as the independent variable, adjusting for age and height as potential confounders, to evaluate its independent association with biomechanical parameters. The mediating effect of kinesiophobia was examined using bias-corrected bootstrap analysis with 5000 resamples, with statistical significance defined as a 95% confidence interval (CI) not including zero. All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of general characteristics

No significant differences were observed in sex, age, height, or affected side distribution among groups (all P > 0.05). Body weight and BMI were significantly higher in Group A (30.92 ± 1.59 kg/m2) than in Groups B (26.72 ± 0.79 kg/m2), C (22.06 ± 1.84 kg/m2), and D (21.52 ± 1.69 kg/m2), and Group B was significantly higher than Groups C and D (P < 0.01). No significant difference was observed between Groups C and D (P > 0.05), consistent with the predefined grouping criteria (Table 1).

Data represent within-group comparisons between affected and unaffected limbs. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01, indicating statistically significant bilateral differences. Group D (healthy controls) showed no significant bilateral differences.

Comparison of knee joint range of motion

Between-group comparisons affected-side sagittal plane range of motion (ROM) decreased progressively with increasing BMI. Group A (47.46° ± 9.46°) was significantly smaller than Groups B (56.81° ± 3.43°), C (60.27° ± 3.62°), and D (67.23° ± 2.82°); Group B was significantly smaller than Groups C and D, and Group C was significantly smaller than Group D (all P < 0.01; Table 3).

Within-group comparisons in Groups A, B, and C, sagittal plane ROM on the affected side was significantly smaller than that on the unaffected side, whereas frontal plane ROM was significantly greater on the affected side (all P < 0.05). No significant bilateral differences were observed in any plane in Group D (P > 0.05; Table 2).

Comparison of knee joint moments

Between-group comparisons sagittal plane moments on the affected side increased progressively with increasing BMI. Group A (680.35 ± 130.98 N·m) was significantly greater than Groups B (484.80 ± 57.75 N·m), C (388.50 ± 71.62 N·m), and D (225.13 ± 85.78 N·m); Group B was significantly greater than Groups C and D, and Group C was significantly greater than Group D (all P < 0.01). Similar trends were observed for frontal and transverse plane moments (Table 3).

Within-group comparisons in Groups A, B, and C, sagittal and frontal plane moments on the affected side were significantly greater than those on the unaffected side (all P < 0.05). No significant bilateral differences were observed in any plane in Group D (P > 0.05; Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted across all participants (n = 80). BMI was significantly negatively correlated with affected-side cadence (r = − 0.80), gait velocity (r = − 0.75), and sagittal plane ROM (r = − 0.68), and significantly positively correlated with sagittal plane moments (r = 0.76) and frontal plane moments (r = 0.64) (all P < 0.01). After adjusting for age and height, multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that BMI independently predicted affected-side cadence (β = 8.27, P = 0.03) and affected-side transverse plane moments (β = 1.40, P = 0.04) (Table 4).

Correlation and mediation analysis between kinesiophobia and biomechanical parameters

Kinesiophobia was significantly negatively correlated with affected-side cadence (r = − 0.64) and gait velocity (r = − 0.59), and with unaffected-side cadence (r = − 0.63), gait velocity (r = − 0.57), and stride length (r = − 0.33) (all P < 0.05). Kinesiophobia was also significantly negatively correlated with affected-side sagittal plane ROM (r = − 0.53) and transverse plane ROM (r = − 0.33), and with unaffected-side transverse plane ROM (r = − 0.30) (all P < 0.05). Additionally, kinesiophobia was significantly positively correlated with affected-side sagittal plane moments (r = 0.62) and frontal plane moments (r = 0.39), and with unaffected-side sagittal plane moments (r = 0.49) and frontal plane moments (r = 0.34) (all P < 0.01). BMI was significantly positively correlated with kinesiophobia (path a = 1.06, P < 0.001). Mediation analysis indicated that kinesiophobia partially mediated the relationship between BMI and unaffected-side gait velocity (indirect effect = − 0.01; 95% CI [− 0.03, − 0.00]), accounting for 48.4% of the total effect, and the relationship between BMI and affected-side sagittal plane moments (indirect effect = 7.45; 95% CI [0.43, 18.56]), accounting for 27.5% of the total effect (Table 5).

Discussion

This study systematically examined kinesiophobia and knee biomechanical characteristics across BMI categories in patients with postoperative tibial plateau fractures, with a focus on the mediating role of kinesiophobia. Elevated BMI was significantly associated with poorer knee function, greater kinesiophobia, and adverse alterations in biomechanical parameters, including spatiotemporal gait characteristics, knee joint range of motion, and joint moments. Mediation analysis indicated that kinesiophobia partially mediated the relationship between BMI and specific biomechanical parameters. Manidakis et al. [35] and Dinçel et al. [19] similarly identified elevated BMI as a risk factor for poor postoperative recovery and reduced knee function; however, these studies primarily relied on subjective scales to assess functional status, with limited objective quantitative analysis of gait and joint biomechanical parameters and minimal attention to psychological factors. By integrating three-dimensional motion capture with psychological assessment, the present study corroborated these findings and revealed a potential mediating role of kinesiophobia in the influence of BMI on postoperative knee biomechanics, providing novel insight into the multidimensional mechanisms underlying impaired functional recovery in patients with elevated BMI.

Lysholm scores were significantly lower in the obese group than in the overweight and normal-weight groups, consistent with findings by Hoogeboom et al. [36] demonstrating that elevated BMI delays postoperative knee functional recovery. This disparity may be attributable to increased joint loading associated with elevated BMI. Messier et al. [37, 38] demonstrated that knee joint compressive forces during ambulation can reach two to four times body weight, exposing the joints of heavier individuals to sustained high-load conditions and disrupting cartilage nutritional metabolism. Furthermore, obesity-related chronic low-grade inflammation may accelerate cartilage degeneration via pro-inflammatory cytokines [39–41]. These pathological alterations manifest at the gait level as reduced cadence and gait velocity. In this study, the high-BMI group exhibited significantly lower cadence and gait velocity than the normal-weight group and healthy controls, with greater affected-side stride length and step length, consistent with findings by DeVita et al. [42]. Reducing gait cycle frequency decreases the number of joint loading cycles per unit time, while increasing stride length helps maintain basic locomotor capacity. Collectively, these adjustments represent a compensatory strategy that sacrifices gait efficiency for joint protection; however, such compensation alters lower extremity kinetic chain mechanics, potentially increasing loading on adjacent joints [3, 43].

These gait adaptations are closely related to alterations in knee joint kinematics [44]. Affected-side sagittal plane angular excursion was significantly smaller than that of the unaffected side in Groups A, B, and C, decreasing progressively with increasing BMI, with Group A significantly smaller than Groups B, C, and D. Harding et al. [45, 46] reported that reduced knee flexion in patients with elevated BMI is associated with pain avoidance, joint stiffness, and relative quadriceps weakness; Fändriks et al. [44]. similarly confirmed persistent knee kinematic deficits following tibial plateau fracture surgery. The observed kinematic and kinetic alterations may therefore reflect the combined influence of BMI-related mechanical loading and secondary factors, including postoperative pain, joint stiffness, and neuromuscular deficits. Increased coronal plane excursion represents a compensatory strategy for ambulatory stability when sagittal motion is restricted; however, given the limited tolerance of passive restraint structures such as collateral ligaments and menisci to lateral stress, sustained increases in coronal plane motion may accelerate osteoarthritis progression, as confirmed by long-term follow-up studies by Sharma et al. [47, 48]. Peak joint moments in all three planes on the affected side increased progressively with BMI, with Group A significantly exceeding other groups, and a similar but attenuated trend on the unaffected side. Browning et al. [49] reported that lower extremity joint moments increase with body mass in obese individuals, and Millar et al. [43] further highlighted that joint loading following tibial plateau fracture is influenced by multiple factors. Elevated joint moments increase neuromuscular demand and energy expenditure, potentially reducing exercise tolerance and rehabilitation adherence [50, 51].

Beyond the biomechanical alterations described above, psychological factors warrant equal attention. TSK-17 scores were significantly higher in the obese group than in the overweight and normal-weight groups, with kinesiophobia increasing by 1.06 points per unit rise in BMI, negatively correlated with cadence and gait velocity, and positively correlated with sagittal plane moments. These findings align closely with the fear-avoidance model, whereby pain catastrophizing triggers kinesiophobia, promotes activity avoidance, and leads to progressive functional deterioration [52, 53]. Deniz et al. [54]. similarly confirmed the negative impact of kinesiophobia on gait parameters in patients following knee surgery. Bootstrap mediation analysis further demonstrated that kinesiophobia mediated 48.4% of the BMI effect on unaffected-side gait velocity and 27.5% of the BMI effect on affected-side sagittal plane moments. Although kinesiophobia and biomechanical parameters were measured simultaneously, the significant positive prediction of kinesiophobia by BMI (path a), the independent effect of kinesiophobia on biomechanical parameters (path b), and the theoretical grounding in the fear-avoidance model collectively support a substantive mediating role of kinesiophobia rather than mere correlation. Leeuw et al. [13] further noted that kinesiophobia promotes muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and altered joint moments through reduced rehabilitation participation, increased muscle co-contraction, and modified motor control strategies [55–57], ultimately perpetuating a vicious cycle of elevated BMI, worsening kinesiophobia, and progressive biomechanical deterioration.

Multiple regression analysis further confirmed that, after adjusting for age and height, BMI independently predicted affected-side cadence, unaffected-side cadence, unaffected-side stride length, and affected-side transverse plane moments, indicating a direct effect of BMI on these parameters independent of kinesiophobia. The independent prediction of cadence likely reflects central nervous system regulation of gait rhythm in response to mechanical loading and energy expenditure demands, while the prediction of transverse plane moments suggests that patients with elevated BMI require greater neuromuscular resources to maintain knee rotational stability. Taken together with the mediation findings, these results indicate that BMI influences postoperative knee biomechanics through both a direct mechanical pathway and an indirect psychological pathway via kinesiophobia, collectively revealing the multidimensional mechanisms underlying biomechanical impairment following tibial plateau fracture surgery. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, the directionality of the relationship between biomechanical alterations and kinesiophobia cannot be definitively established, and bidirectional interactions remain plausible, warranting verification in future prospective longitudinal studies.

In summary, elevated BMI is significantly associated with decreased knee function, greater kinesiophobia, and adverse biomechanical alterations in patients following tibial plateau fracture surgery, with kinesiophobia partially mediating the relationship between BMI and specific biomechanical parameters, collectively underscoring the multidimensional mechanisms underlying postoperative functional impairment. Clinically, weight management and psychological intervention should be integrated into postoperative rehabilitation programs, with active assessment and management of kinesiophobia alongside strategies to reduce joint loading, thereby promoting functional recovery. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger samples are warranted to further investigate the long-term effects of BMI-stratified individualized rehabilitation protocols on postoperative functional outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference among BMI, kinesiophobia, and biomechanical parameters; although the mediation pathway was theoretically grounded in the fear-avoidance model, directionality cannot be confirmed and bidirectional relationships cannot be excluded. Second, pain severity, muscle strength, and neuromuscular function were not directly measured, limiting the ability to distinguish whether the observed kinematic and kinetic alterations represent direct consequences of elevated BMI or secondary adaptations to these factors. Third, the simultaneous measurement of kinesiophobia and biomechanical parameters prevents the establishment of temporal precedence; nevertheless, the bias-corrected bootstrap resampling approach and theoretical grounding in the fear-avoidance model collectively support a mediating role beyond simple correlation. Fourth, the relatively small single-center sample may limit the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, inclusion was restricted to Schatzker Type II fractures, limiting applicability to other fracture classifications. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating larger samples, direct measurement of musculoskeletal confounders, and diverse fracture classifications are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Elevated BMI is significantly associated with decreased knee function, increased kinesiophobia, and altered knee joint biomechanics after tibial plateau fracture surgery, manifesting as reduced cadence and gait velocity, decreased sagittal plane angular excursion, and elevated joint moments. Kinesiophobia partially mediates the relationship between BMI and specific biomechanical parameters, highlighting the importance of addressing both mechanical and psychological factors in postoperative rehabilitation. Clinical rehabilitation programs should therefore integrate weight management with targeted assessment and intervention for kinesiophobia to promote optimal functional recovery in this population.
