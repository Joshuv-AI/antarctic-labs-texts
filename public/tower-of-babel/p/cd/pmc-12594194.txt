Functional divergence of tandem duplicate SCPL acyltransferase genes reveals convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis
Authors: Yuting Xiang, Tingting Liang, Ruixi Yang, Hsihua Wang, Alisdair R. Fernie, Yang Zhang, Rao Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chicoric acid, a caffeic acid derivative exhibiting diverse bioactivities, has been identified in more than 60 plant species. Here we report an alternative biosynthetic pathway in lettuce (Lactuca sativa). BAHD acyltransferases catalyze the synthesis of chlorogenic acid, which serves as the acyl donor for four serine carboxypeptidase-like (SCPL) acyltransferases (LsSCPL1-4) encoded by tandemly duplicated genes. LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 catalyze the synthesis of chicoric acid using caftaric acid as the acyl acceptor. In contrast to the BAHD acyltransferases found in Echinacea species, LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 alter their substrate preferences through key amino acid substitutions and use tartaric acid as the acyl acceptor to synthesize caftaric acid. These tandemly duplicated SCPL genes are observed across several evolutionarily related species within the tribe Cichorieae. These findings illustrate the convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis in Asteraceae and highlight the complexity of acyltransferase activity in specialized metabolism in plants.

INTRODUCTION

For millennia, humans have harnessed plant-derived specialized metabolites to treat diseases and maintain health (1). Today, these compounds are pivotal resources for pharmaceutical innovations (2). Structurally diverse specialized metabolites, often derived from a limited set of primary metabolites, undergo modifications such as oxidation, acylation, glycosylation, methylation, and hydroxylation (3). These reactions are catalyzed by a diverse array of decorating enzymes that have evolved via gene duplication and subsequent mutation (4). The biosynthesis of specialized metabolites is often species specific in plants (5). However, for any given metabolite produced by multiple species, its biosynthetic pathway may be conserved, suggesting an ancestral biosynthetic pathway retained in descendant species, or the biosynthetic pathways may differ among species, reflecting instances of convergent evolution (6, 7).

Chicoric acid (dicaffeoyltartaric acid), a derivative of caffeic acid that was first identified in chicory in 1958 (8), has a range of beneficial properties. These are reported to include antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anticancer, antiviral, and immune-stimulating effects as such, suggesting that the compound can considerably contribute to protecting human health (9). Chicoric acid is a key active component of the renowned medicinal plant, purple coneflower (Echinacea purpurea), and its biosynthetic pathway and regulatory network have been recently elucidated (10, 11). Two cytosolically localized BAHD acyltransferases, namely, hydroxycinnamoyl–coenzyme A (CoA):tartaric acid hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (EpHTT) and hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA:quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (EpHQT), catalyze the formation of caftaric acid and chlorogenic acid, respectively, using caffeoyl-CoA derived from phenylpropanoid metabolism as the acyl donor. Subsequently, chicoric acid synthase (EpCAS), a member of the serine carboxypeptidase-like (SCPL) family, uses chlorogenic acid as the acyl donor and caftaric acid as the acyl acceptor in the production of chicoric acid in the vacuole (Fig. 1). This SCPL acyltransferase has evolved an acyl donor specificity distinct from the more common 1-O-β-D-glucosides typical of this class of enzymes. It had been proposed that this pathway is unique to chicoric acid–producing Echinacea species (10). However, in addition to the Echinacea genus, chicoric acid has been identified in more than 60 plant species, mostly belonging to the family Asteraceae (9), but the mechanisms of synthesis in these other species are, as yet, uncharacterized. For example, in the widely consumed fresh leafy vegetable lettuce (Lactuca sativa) (12, 13), the enzymes responsible for chicoric acid biosynthesis remain unidentified, which impedes the breeding and selection of high-quality lettuce varieties.

The convergent evolution of the chicoric acid biosynthetic pathway in the family Asteraceae.

The biosynthetic pathway of chicoric acid in E. purpurea involves two distinct types of plant acyltransferases: Two cytosolically localized BAHD acyltransferases, EpHTT and EpHQT, catalyze the biosynthesis of caftaric acid and chlorogenic acid, respectively, using caffeoyl-CoA as the acyl donor and tartaric acid and quinic acid as acyl acceptors. Subsequently, a vacuole-localized SCPL acyltransferase, EpCAS, catalyzes the synthesis of chicoric acid in the vacuole, using chlorogenic acid as the acyl donor and caftaric acid as the acyl acceptor (10). In L. sativa, however, the biosynthesis of chicoric acid involves four SCPL acyltransferases encoded by tandemly duplicated genes, which all use chlorogenic acid as their acyl donor. LsSCPL1 (LsCFS1) and LsSCPL3 (LsCFS2) preferentially use tartaric acid as their acyl acceptor and catalyze the synthesis of caftaric acid in the vacuole. In contrast, LsSCPL2 (LsCAS1) and LsSCPL4 (LsCAS2) preferentially use caftaric acid as their acyl acceptor and chlorogenic acid as their donor and synthesize chicoric acid. The phylogenetic analysis suggests that the BAHD-type acyltransferase responsible for caftaric acid synthesis, EpHTT, appears to have duplicated from EpHCT in E. purpurea. Meanwhile, the functional divergence of tandem duplicate LsSCPL genes in lettuce led to the evolution of SCPL-type acyltransferases, LsCFSs, which are involved in the synthesis of caftaric acid. The different routes of caftaric acid biosynthesis suggest the convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis in the family Asteraceae. HTT, hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA:tartaric acid hydroxycinnamoyl transferase; HQT, hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA:quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase; HCT, hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA:quinate/shikimate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase; CAS, chicoric acid synthase; CFS, caftaric acid synthase. Proteins from E. purpurea used for phylogenetic analysis are EpHCT (QRI59128), EpHQT (QRI59127), EpHTT (QRI59126), and EpCAS (QRI59129).

In the current study, we report a biosynthetic pathway for chicoric acid, distinct from that found in Echinacea, in members of the tribe Cichorieae (Fig. 1). In lettuce, genes encoding HQTs were identified, but no genes encoding BAHD-type HTTs involved in the biosynthesis of caftaric acid were detected. Instead, four tandem duplicate genes encoding SCPL acyltransferases (LsSCPL1-4) complete the biosynthesis of chicoric acid. LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 use tartaric acid as their acyl acceptor and chlorogenic acid as their acyl donor to produce caftaric acid. LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 catalyze the synthesis of chicoric acid using caftaric acid as their acyl acceptor and chlorogenic acid as their acyl donor, with activities similar to EpCAS. We identified the key amino acid responsible for determining the acyl acceptor specificities of these SCPL acyltransferases. These tandemly arranged SCPL genes are also found in several evolutionarily related species within the tribe Cichorieae of the family Asteraceae. These different pathways for caftaric acid biosynthesis suggest convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis and highlight the complexity of acyltransferase activities in specialized metabolism in plants.

RESULTS

Tandemly duplicated SCPL genes participate in chicoric acid biosynthesis

Since the biosynthetic pathway for chicoric acid in the Echinacea genus had been elucidated and found to involve two types of plant acyltransferases, BAHD and SCPL (10), we initially adopted a phylogenetic approach to identifying the biosynthetic genes responsible for chicoric acid synthesis in lettuce (14). We first identified four BAHD acyltransferases (Lsat_1_v5_gn_4_86561, Lsat_1_v5_gn_5_8960, Lsat_1_v5_gn_7_50101, and Lsat_1_v5_gn_8_63761) from the lettuce genome dataset (15), which belong to the HCT/HQT clade (fig. S1). In vitro enzyme assays using recombinant proteins from Escherichia coli confirmed that Lsat_1_v5_gn_7_50101 (LsHQT1) and Lsat_1_v5_gn_8_63761 (LsHQT2) belonging to the HQT subclade catalyze the synthesis of chlorogenic acid from caffeoyl-CoA and quinic acid (fig. S2A). However, none of these enzymes could catalyze the synthesis of caftaric acid when caffeoyl-CoA and tartaric acid were used as substrates (fig. S2B). These results suggested that caftaric acid may not be produced by the four HCT/HQT clade BAHD acyltransferases, which are close homologs to EpHTT in lettuce.

We undertook a phylogenetic analysis of SCPL acyltransferases and identified four lettuce SCPL acyltransferases (Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118400, Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118321, Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118280, and Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_119141) that are closely related to EpCAS within clade IA (Fig. 2A), suggesting their potential involvement in chicoric acid synthesis (10). Notably, these four genes are tandemly arranged on chromosome 1 of lettuce (Fig. 2B) and were sequentially named LsSCPL1 (Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118400), LsSCPL2 (Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118321), LsSCPL3 (Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_118280), and LsSCPL4 (Lsat_1_v5_gn_1_119141). All four SCPL acyltransferases contain the conserved Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad (fig. S3), which is essential for the hydrolytic activity of serine proteinase (16) and the acyltransferase activity of SCPLs (10, 17). Furthermore, these genes exhibit notable structural homology, and the proteins they encode also show a high sequence identity, with LsSCPL1 being closely related to LsSCPL3 and LsSCPL2 to LsSCPL4 (Fig. 2C and fig. S4). Similar to other reported SCPL acyltransferases (10, 18), the green fluorescent protein fusion proteins of these four LsSCPL acyltransferases were localized in the vacuole (fig. S5). The expression levels of these four LsSCPL genes were all induced significantly when methyl jasmonate (MeJA) was applied to lettuce leaves, which further increased the contents of chicoric acid, chlorogenic acid, and caftaric acid (fig. S6), suggesting that these four LsSCPL acyltransferases are likely involved in chicoric acid biosynthesis.

The hairy root system has been widely used to investigate the biosynthetic pathways of plant specialized metabolites due to its advantages of rapid growth, as well as genetic and biochemical stability (10, 19). Notably, chicoric acid and its potential precursors were highly synthesized in lettuce hairy roots (fig. S7). In addition, all four LsSCPL genes were differentially expressed in lettuce hairy roots. LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 exhibited similar moderate expression levels, LsSCPL3 was highly expressed, and LsSCPL1 showed the lowest expression level (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we engineered a CRISPR-Cas9 target sequence that was conserved across all four LsSCPL genes and used a multiplex gene editing approach to achieve the simultaneous knockout of these genes in the lettuce hairy root system (fig. S8). The total ion chromatograms from liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) analysis of Lsscpl1/2/3/4 knockout hairy root lines revealed a substantial reduction in chicoric acid levels and a notable accumulation of chlorogenic acid compared to the control (CK) line (Fig. 2E). In addition, nontargeted metabolomic analysis identified distinct chemical compositions among the different groups, with the knockout lines showing notable separation from the CK group (fig. S9). The S-plot of the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) highlighted substantial changes in ions belonging to chicoric acid, caftaric acid, and tartaric acid (Fig. 2F). Quantitative analysis confirmed a substantial reduction in chicoric acid content in the Lsscpl1/2/3/4 knockout hairy roots (Fig. 2G). A low level of chicoric acid was still detected in the edited hairy roots, likely due to mosaic editing issues (20). The knockout of LsSCPL1/2/3/4 led to a significant decrease in caftaric acid, accompanied by an accumulation of chlorogenic acid and tartaric acid (Fig. 2G), suggesting that these four LsSCPL enzymes are not only involved in chicoric acid synthesis but also play a role in the biosynthesis of caftaric acid in lettuce.

LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 function as chicoric acid synthases

To further elucidate the roles of these four SCPL acyltransferases in chicoric acid biosynthesis, we recombinantly expressed them in yeast and evaluated their crude enzyme activity in vitro. LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 demonstrated substantial catalytic activities using caftaric acid and chlorogenic acid as substrates to produce chicoric acid, whereas LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 displayed negligible activities (Fig. 3, A and B). These results indicated that LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 may function as chicoric acid synthases (CASs).

To verify their in vivo functions in chicoric acid biosynthesis, we generated both double and individual knockout hairy root lines for these two genes (fig. S10). The double knockout of LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 genes resulted in a marked decrease in the content of chicoric acid, accompanied by an increase in the contents of its precursors, caftaric acid and chlorogenic acid (Fig. 3C). By contrast, single knockout of either LsSCPL2 or LsSCPL4 exhibited minimal effects on the contents of chicoric acid and its precursors (Fig. 3D), suggesting redundant roles for these genes in chicoric acid biosynthesis in lettuce hairy roots. Collectively, these results demonstrated that both LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 are homologs of EpCAS and are involved in chicoric acid biosynthesis in lettuce; therefore, we designated them as LsCAS1 and LsCAS2, respectively. Transient overexpression of LsCAS1 or LsCAS2 in lettuce seedlings increased the content of chicoric acid (fig. S11). In addition, yeast feeding assays using caftaric acid and chlorogenic acid as substrates demonstrated that both LsCAS1 and LsCAS2 convert these compounds into chicoric acid (fig. S12). These results confirmed that LsCAS1 and LsCAS2 function as CASs.

LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 function as caftaric acid synthases

The knockout of LsSCPL1/2/3/4 resulted in a reduction in the content of caftaric acid (Fig. 2G). However, the knockout of LsSCPL2/4 increased the content of caftaric acid (Fig. 3C). This suggested that LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 might be involved in the biosynthesis of caftaric acid. Chemically, tartaric acid serves as the acyl acceptor in caftaric acid synthesis. Given that LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 also exhibit the potential to use chlorogenic acid as the acyl donor (Fig. 3A), we hypothesized that LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 could directly transfer the caffeic acid group from chlorogenic acid to tartaric acid. To test this hypothesis, we conducted in vitro crude enzyme assays using chlorogenic acid and tartaric acid as substrates. LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 produced notable levels of caftaric acid, while LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4 displayed negligible activities (Fig. 4, A and B).

To validate the in vivo roles of LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 in caftaric acid biosynthesis, we attempted to create a double knockout mutant of these genes in hairy roots. Despite our efforts to target a common site in both LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3, we were unsuccessful. However, fortunately, we identified a Lsscpl1/3 double knockout line from a population of partially knockout hairy roots of Lsscpl1/2/3/4, in which only LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 were edited (fig. S13A). The double knockout of LsSCPL1/3 resulted in a significant decrease in the content of caftaric acid, which in turn led to a reduction in the level of chicoric acid. Concurrently, the content of the precursor tartaric acid was significantly increased (Fig. 4C). Since LsSCPL3 exhibited a much higher expression level than LsSCPL1 in hairy roots, a single knockout of Lsscpl3 hairy root lines was subsequently established (fig. S13B). We found that the single knockout of LsSCPL3 resulted in a significant reduction in the contents of caftaric acid and chicoric acid (Fig. 4D), although not as pronounced as the double knockout of LsSCPL1/3. This suggested that, despite the low expression level of LsSCPL1, it also plays a role in the synthesis of caftaric acid in lettuce hairy roots. Overall, these results demonstrate that LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3 catalyze the biosynthesis of caftaric acid by using chlorogenic acid and tartaric acid as substrates in lettuce; therefore, we designated them as LsCFS1 and LsCFS2, respectively. Transient overexpression of LsCFS1 or LsCFS2 in lettuce seedlings increased the content of caftaric acid (fig. S14). In addition, the yeast feeding assay demonstrated that LsCFS1 and LsCFS2 catalyze the synthesis of caftaric acid using chlorogenic acid and tartaric acid as substrates (fig. S15). In lettuce, SCPL acyltransferases act as caftaric acid synthase (CFS), while in Echinacea, BAHD acyltransferase (EpHTT) catalyzes caftaric acid synthesis (10, 21), suggesting that the caftaric acid biosynthesis undergoes convergent evolution in lettuce and Echinacea species.

The identification of SCPL-type CFS offers an alternative pathway to produce chicoric acid. The caffeoyl-CoA used by EpHTT is quite unstable and not easy to obtain in vitro. In contrast, chlorogenic acid is relatively stable and readily accessible. In a one-pot double enzyme reaction system involving LsCFS1 and LsCAS2, tartaric acid and chlorogenic acid could be converted into chicoric acid directly (fig. S16).

The underlying mechanism of acceptor specificities of LsSCPL acyltransferases

We then selected LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 to further investigate the amino acid residues that determine their substrate specificity. Additional analysis of enzyme kinetic parameters using in vitro enzyme assays with purified proteins (fig. S17) indicated that LsCFS1 primarily functions as a CFS, while LsCAS2 acts as a CAS (table S1).

The protein structures of LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 were predicted using AlphaFold and revealed similar frameworks (fig. S18). To gain insights into their interactions with ligands, molecular docking analyses were performed with caftaric acid and chicoric acid (Fig. 5A). The serine residue in the Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad is crucial for the acyltransferase activity of plant SCPL acyltransferases (10, 17). Substitutions of Ser179 in LsCFS1 and Ser178 in LsCAS2 with alanine residues abolished their acyltransferase activities (Fig. 5B), highlighting the essential role of the serine residues.

Key amino acid residues determine the acyltransferase activity and substrate preference of LsSCPL acyltransferases.

(A) Molecular docking studies of LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 with caftaric acid and chicoric acid, respectively, reveal that the serine residue in the active site targets the ester bond. The Y299 residue of LsCFS1 is linked to E278, which reduces the size of the active pocket compared to that of LsCAS2. This structural difference may explain why LsCFS1 preferentially accepts tartaric acid as an acyl acceptor, whereas LsCAS2 favors caftaric acid. (B) In vitro enzyme assays demonstrate that S179 and S178 are critical for the acyltransferase activities of LsCFS1 and LsCAS2, respectively. In addition, the tyrosine (Y) and phenylalanine (F) residues in LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 are likely key amino acids influencing substrate specificity. Mutation of F298 in LsCAS2 to Y results in a significant decrease in CAS activity and an increase in CFS activity. Meanwhile, mutating Y299 in LsCFS1 to F significantly reduces its CFS activity but does not enhance its CAS activity. In (B), the data are mean ± SD (n = 3). * indicates a significant difference from WT (P < 0.05) analyzed by a two-sided Student’s t test.

Despite their similar three-dimensional structures, LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 exhibited notable differences in their active sites (Fig. 5A). Specifically, LsCAS2 features a larger active pocket accommodating the entrance of caftaric acid, whereas LsCFS1 has a smaller active pocket for smaller acyl acceptors like tartaric acid. Analysis of the amino acid residues surrounding the active pocket revealed that the Y299 (tyrosine) residue in LsCFS1 and its counterpart F298 (phenylalanine) in LsCAS2 might be critical for their respective functions. The hydroxyl group of Y299 in LsCFS1 could interact with the E278 (glutamic acid) residue to form an ester bond, reducing the size of the active pocket. Conversely, F298 in LsCAS2, lacking a hydroxyl group, could not form such an interaction. Mutation of F298 to Y in LsCAS2 resulted in a significant decrease in CAS activity and an increase in CFS activity. Although the LsCFS1 Y299F mutant did not exhibit an increase in CAS activity, its CFS activity was significantly impaired (Fig. 5B). These results suggested that the alteration of a key amino acid (from F to Y) narrows the active pocket and consequently changes the substrate specificities of these LsSCPL acyltransferases.

Evolution of SCPL acyltransferase genes in chicoric acid biosynthesis

The results presented above demonstrated that tandemly duplicated SCPL genes in lettuce are involved in the biosynthesis of chicoric acid and its key biosynthetic intermediate, caftaric acid (Fig. 1). These SCPL genes include two CFSs and two CASs, indicating functional divergence (Fig. 6A). In addition, two LsCFSs and two LsCASs exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns in lettuce, suggesting the subfunctionalization of the two gene copies (Fig. 6A and fig. S19). In the Echinacea genus, CASs may have evolved from chlorogenic acid condensing enzymes (CCEs), which use chlorogenic acid as a substrate to produce 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid—a more conserved metabolic pathway that prevents the self-toxicity of chlorogenic acid during germination (22). In lettuce, we identified trace amounts of 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid in the in vitro enzyme products of all four SCPL acyltransferases (fig. S20). However, the knockout of LsSCPL1/2/3/4 did not reduce the levels of 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid, and there are other CCE homologs, suggesting that they may not function as CCEs in vivo (fig. S21). The remaining CCE activity of LsSCPL acyltransferases supports the speculation that they may have originated from the CCEs.

To further investigate the conservation of this biosynthetic pathway, species capable of synthesizing both caftaric acid and chicoric acid and having reference genomes were selected for analysis. Through public AMIR databases (23), analogous groups of SCPL genes were identified in closely related species from the tribe Cichorieae: chicory (Cichorium intybus) and cultivated chicory (Cichorium endivia) from the Cichorium genus, dandelion (Taraxacum mongolicum) from the Taraxacum genus, and wild lettuce (Lactuca saligna) from the Lactuca genus (Fig. 6B). These SCPL acyltransferases were phylogenetically categorized into two clades: CFS and CAS (Fig. 6C). In terms of key amino acid residues, all members contained the conserved Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad. Notably, members of the CFS clade exclusively had a tyrosine residue, whereas those of the CAS clade contained a phenylalanine residue (fig. S22).

SCPL acyltransferases from chicory were selected for recombinant expression and in vitro crude enzyme activity assessments. Notably, CiSCPL1 (CiCFS) from the CFS clade primarily produced caftaric acid (Fig. 6, D and E), whereas CiSCPL2 (CiCAS) from the CAS clade yielded a higher amount of chicoric acid (Fig. 6, F and G), mirroring the behavior observed in the LsSCPL acyltransferases. By contrast, no such high similarity or tandemly duplicated SCPL genes were found in sunflower (Helianthus annuus) or Chrysanthemum coronarium, two species from distinct intervening plant lineages that lack the production of both caftaric acid and chicoric acid. These findings underscored the potential conservation of tandemly duplicated SCPL genes in facilitating complete chicoric acid biosynthesis within the tribe Cichorieae, as well as the convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis in the family Asteraceae.

DISCUSSION

Plants produce an array of secondary metabolites that are crucial to their adaptation to specific ecological niches (5). In nature, some key metabolites are synthesized by different plant species through distinct metabolic pathways, a phenomenon known as convergent evolution (6). Chicoric acid, a widely occurring caffeic acid derivative, has been found in many plant species. It not only exhibits a plethora of bioactivities beneficial to humans (9) but also plays important biological roles in planta. For example, chicoric acid enhances ultraviolet protection and functions as an allelochemical in purple coneflower (24, 25). In addition, the content of chicoric acid is influenced by various environmental stresses, implying its biological functions in lettuce (26). Previously, two types of plant acyltransferases, BAHD (HQT and HTT) and SCPL (CAS), have been identified through an activity-guided approach as being involved in the biosynthesis of chicoric acid in the Echinacea genus (10, 21, 27). CAS is conserved throughout the Echinacea genus, although two species do not synthesize chicoric acid due to the absence of the gene encoding HTT and consequently, its product, caftaric acid (21), highlighting the important role of caftaric acid in chicoric acid biosynthesis. Crude protein extracts from lettuce exhibit only HQT activity, but not HTT or CAS activities, suggesting an alternative pathway for chicoric acid biosynthesis (10). Identifying the biosynthetic genes responsible for chicoric acid production in lettuce, one of the most popular vegetables worldwide (12, 13), is vital for breeding and selecting high-quality varieties. Here, we investigated the biosynthetic pathway of chicoric acid in lettuce through phylogenetic analysis to identify corresponding homologous genes (14). Unexpectedly, we managed to identify LsHQTs that catalyze only the biosynthesis of chlorogenic acid but did not find LsHTTs. However, the key precursor, caftaric acid, is present in lettuce, suggesting the existence of alternative biosynthetic pathways.

When evaluating the SCPL acyltransferases, we found four members encoded by tandemly arranged genes that are phylogenetically closely related to EpCAS. To rapidly and efficiently verify the roles of these SCPL genes in chicoric acid biosynthesis, we used a hairy root system despite potential challenges such as mosaic editing and metabolic redundancy, which can result in residual target metabolites and variability among different lines (20). Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that two of these enzymes, LsSCPL2 and LsSCPL4, primarily function as CAS to synthesize chicoric acid, similar to EpCAS in E. purpurea. The identification of LsCASs contrasts with earlier false-negative results (10), which may have been caused by the use of low substrate concentrations or differences in the composition of crude protein extracts. Unexpectedly, the other two enzymes, LsSCPL1 and LsSCPL3, function as CFS using chlorogenic acid and tartaric acid as substrates to produce caftaric acid. Unlike HTTs from the BAHD family identified in the Echinacea genus, here, the SCPL-type CFSs were found. Previous studies in several plant species indicate that tartaric acid is synthesized in cytoplasm and then transported into vacuoles by aluminium-activated malate transporter and stored (28–30). Similarly, chlorogenic acid is synthesized by HQT in cytoplasm and then may be transported into vacuoles via vesicles (31, 32). These subcellular distributions of tartaric acid and chlorogenic acid allow cytosolically localized BAHD acyltransferases and vacuole-localized SCPL acyltransferases to use them for the synthesis of caftaric acid. These distinct biosynthetic routes for caftaric acid indicate the convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis across plant kingdoms and highlight the complexity of acylation processes in plant secondary metabolism. Furthermore, the identification of these SCPL acyltransferases provides a cell-free catalytic system for chicoric acid production.

Gene duplication and subsequent mutations contribute to the diversity of plant metabolic enzymes (4). In lettuce, all four SCPL acyltransferases encoding genes exhibit similar gene structures and chromosomal colocalization, indicating apparent duplication events. After duplication, these enzymes have promiscuous catalytic activities but display distinct substrate specificities. Mutation of a key amino acid altered substrate preference, resulting in functional divergence (33). The conservation of this gene duplication event suggests that it occurred before the speciation in the ancestor common to the tribe Cichorieae. The varying copy numbers of CFSs and CASs across different species indicate that they underwent further duplications and subfunctionalization, such as the tissue-specific expression differences observed in LsCFSs and LsCASs. In addition, CAS genes may have emerged before the production of chicoric acid and are proposed to have evolved from CCEs (22). The residual CCE activity observed in LsSCPL acyltransferases further support this hypothesis. Collectively, these duplication and mutation events reinforce the viewpoint that SCPL-type acyltransferases are now undergoing evolutionary change (34, 35). Regarding the convergent biosynthesis of chicoric acid, an important metabolite with notable biological and ecological roles in planta, CAS appears to have initially duplicated from CCE. Subsequently, the duplication of HTT from HCT in the Echinacea genus (10, 27) and of CFS from CAS in the Cichorieae tribe led to the production of caftaric acid. These findings highlight the significance of gene duplication and subsequent functional divergence in chicoric acid synthesis across different plant lineages. However, the potential presence of BAHD-type HTT in the Cichorieae tribe and SCPL-type CFS in the Echinacea genus, as well as the fate of duplicated genes—including deletion and functional divergence—still requires further investigation.

In summary, building on the previously activity-guided elucidation of the chicoric acid biosynthetic pathway in the Echinacea genus (10), we have demonstrated that tandemly duplicated SCPL genes complete the biosynthesis of chicoric acid and its key precursor, caftaric acid, in lettuce through a phylogenetic approach. This finding underscores the importance of integrating various pathway elucidation methods (14) and provides molecular markers for breeding high-quality lettuce varieties. In addition to the BAHD acyltransferase HTT, the identification of SCPL-type CFS indicates convergent evolution of chicoric acid biosynthesis and highlights the complexity of acylation processes in plant secondary metabolism. Furthermore, the functional divergence of tandem duplicate SCPL acyltransferase genes emphasizes the critical role of gene duplication and subsequent mutations in generating the diversity of plant secondary metabolites (4). Notably, different biosynthetic routes have evolved to synthesize chicoric acid among various plant lineages within the family Asteraceae, suggesting the biological and ecological significance of chicoric acid for plants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material and treatment

Lollo Rossa lettuce (L. sativa L.) and Nicotiana benthamiana were cultivated in a greenhouse (25°C, 16 hours of light and 8 hours of dark, light intensity of 250 μmol m−2 s−1). Four-week-old lettuce leaves were treated with 500 μM MeJA for 24 hours. The fresh leaves were then collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

The CRISPR-Cas9 vector was provided by A. Granell (IBMCP, CSIC-UPV, Valencia, Spain) (36). The target editing sites for the LsSCPL genes were designed using the CCTop tool (https://cctop.cos.uni-heidelberg.de:8043/) (37, 38). Subsequently, CRISPR vectors were constructed through homologous recombination approach. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into the Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain ArA4 and plated on TY medium until OD600 (optical density at 600 nm) reached 0.5 to 0.6. The A. rhizogenes were harvested, washed, and resuspended in infiltration buffer [1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) liquid medium with 200 μM acetosyringone] to an OD600 of 0.5. The resuspension was incubated in the dark for 2 hours before use.

Sterilized lettuce seeds were placed on MS medium for 5 to 7 days at 25°C under a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycle with 7000 lux light. Cotyledons were excised and placed on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 200 μM acetosyringone for 1 day, followed by infiltration with above Agrobacterium suspension for 30 min. The infiltrated cotyledons were then transferred back to the medium and cultured for an additional 2 days. Subsequently, they were moved to 1/2 MS selective medium containing kanamycin (50 mg/ml) and cefotaxime (200 mg/ml). The medium was replaced every 10 days. Hairy roots emerged from the wound sites ~2 weeks later. Once the roots reached a length of 2 to 3 cm, they were excised and transferred to 1/2 MS liquid medium containing kanamycin (50 mg/ml) and cefotaxime (200 mg/ml), maintained at 25°C with continuous shaking at 110 rpm. The hairy roots were harvested after 45 days. The whole induction and cultivation process was carried out in the dark.

LC-HRMS analysis

Fresh lettuce samples (300 mg) were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into powder, and subsequently extracted using 1 ml of 80% methanol (v/v) in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min for subsequent analysis. Metabolite analysis was performed using an LC-HRMS system (Nexera UHPLC LC-30A coupled with an AB SCIEX qTOF X500R). The LC conditions were set as follows: a Hypersil Gold C18 column (100 mm by 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) maintained at 40°C, with a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and 95% acetonitrile (B), and the gradient was programmed as follows: 2% B for 0 to 0.5 min, 2 to 85% B for 0.5 to 8 min, 85% B for 8 to 9 min, 85 to 2% B for 9 to 9.1 min, and then 2% B was maintained for 1.9 min. The injection volume was 3.0 μl. The mass spectrometry data acquisition was performed in an information-dependent acquisition (IDA) model with a negative polarity electrospray ionization source. The ion source gases were set at 50 psi for both gas 1 and gas 2, with a curtain gas of 35 psi and CAD gas of 8. The temperature was set at 500°C, and the spray voltage was −4500 V. For time-of-flight–mass spectrometry, the mass range was 50 to 1000 Da, with a declustering potential (DP) of −80 V, collision energy (CE) of −10 V, and an accumulation time of 0.12 s. IDA criteria were established for small molecules. For time-of-flight–tandem mass spectrometry, the mass range was 50 to 1000 Da, with a DP of −80 V, CE of −40 V, CE spread of 20 V, and an accumulation time of 0.05 s. The peak area list was processed using SCIEX OS software (version 1.7), which was then used for OPLS-DA analysis based on SIMCA (version 14.1). The metabolomic data of CK and Lsscpl1/2/3/4 knockout hairy roots are presented in dataset S1. The quantification of tartaric acid, caftaric acid, chicoric acid, chlorogenic acid, and 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid was conducted using reference standards with SCIEX OS software (version 1.7). The standard calibration curves for compound quantification were established as follows: chicoric acid, Y = 2.76580 × 105 X + 133.78995 (with X ranging from 0.061 to 7.589 mg/liter, r = 0.99999), chlorogenic acid, Y = 9.98861 × 105 X + 1.07341 × 105 (with X ranging from 0.045 to 5.669 mg/liter, r = 0.99973), caftaric acid, Y = 1.03123 × 105 X + 5688.10758 (with X ranging from 0.040 to 4.996 mg/liter, r = 0.99944), 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid, Y = 7.49020 × 105 X + 7.85902 × 104 (with X ranging from 0.066 to 8.263 mg/liter, r = 0.99990), and tartaric acid, Y = 1.09302 × 105 X + 2512.41392 (with X ranging from 0.019 to 2.403 mg/liter, r = 0.99885), where X represents the concentration of the standard compounds in milligrams per liter and Y represents the peak area.

Screening of LsBAHDs and LsSCPLs

Protein sequences of lettuce were retrieved from JGI Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/), while Arabidopsis SCPL sequences (AtSCPLs) and BAHD sequences (AtBAHDs) were obtained from TAIR (http://www.arabidopsis.org). To identify homologous SCPL proteins in lettuce, BLASTP analysis was performed using AtSCPLs as queries. Meanwhile, hmmsearch was conducted with the HMM profile (PF00450) from Pfam (http://pfam.sanger.ac.uk/) to confirm the presence of the PF00450 domain in LsSCPLs. Similarly, to identify homologous BAHD proteins in lettuce, BLASTP analysis was carried out using AtBAHDs as queries, and hmmsearch was also used with the HMM profile (PF02458) to verify the presence of the PF02458 domain.

Phylogenetic analysis

The protein sequences were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm within MEGA software (version 7.0). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the maximum likelihood model with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The BAHD and SCPL proteins used in the phylogenetic analysis were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and AMIR (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/AMIR/home/) databases (23). All accession numbers are listed in table S2.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Fresh lettuce samples (60 to 80 mg) were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and subjected to total RNA extraction using the plant RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, RC401-01). Qualified samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using 1 μg of RNA with the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, catalog no. AK4201). The cDNA was diluted 20-fold and analyzed using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction on the Bio-Rad CFX384, following the previously described programs (39). The relative gene expression levels were determined using the ΔCt method. The lettuce LsUBI gene served as the internal reference gene. All primers used are listed in table S3.

Expression of recombinant LsBAHD enzymes and their in vitro enzyme assay

The coding sequences (CDSs) of LsBAHD acyltransferase-encoding genes were cloned from lettuce cDNA and subsequently inserted into the pDest17 vector. LsBAHD proteins were expressed in the E. coli strain BL21(DE3), which was grown at 37°C in an LB liquid medium containing carbenicillin (50 μg/ml) until an OD600 of 0.7 to 0.9 was reached. Following this, 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside was added to induce protein expression for 20 hours at 16°C with shaking at 180 rpm. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 5000g for 10 min and resuspended in lysis buffer [25 mM tris-HCland 150 mM NaCl (pH 8.0)]. Cell lysis was achieved using an ultrasonic disruptor (Sonics, Vibra-Cell, Newtown, USA) at 4°C for 10 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000g at 4°C for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected as the crude protein extract. The protein concentrations of different BAHD crude extract were adjusted to 4 mg/ml using a BCA kit before enzyme assays.

For the HTT and HQT assays, a 100-μl reaction mixture contain phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.0), 20 μl of crude protein extract (80 μg of protein), 100 μM caffeoyl-CoA, and either 100 μM tartaric acid or 100 μM quinic acid. The mixture was incubated at 30°C for 30 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding 400 μl of methanol. Subsequently, the reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 20,000g at 4°C for 10 min. The supernatants were then analyzed using LC-HRMS.

Subcellular localization

The CDSs of LsSCPL acyltransferase-encoding genes were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector with a 35S promoter via homologous recombination. Recombined plasmids were isolated and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Positive colonies were cultured on LB medium to an OD600 of 1.0, then harvested, washed, and resuspended in a solution containing 10 mM MES (pH 5.6), 10 mM MgCl2, and 200 μM acetosyringone to achieve an OD600 of 0.8 to 1.0. After 2 hours of activation, the resuspension was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. The tobacco plants were kept in darkness for 4 days. The vacuoles were stained with 10 μM SNARF-1 for 2 hours before observation (40). Subcellular localization was visualized using a LEICA M205 FA fluorescence microscope (22).

Expression of recombinant LsSCPL enzymes and their in vitro enzyme assay

The CDSs of the LsSCPL acyltransferase-encoding genes were cloned from lettuce cDNA. The CDSs of the CiSCPL acyltransferase-encoding genes were chemically synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). These CDSs were constructed into the pESC-HIS vector using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China) and induced for expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BY4741. Briefly, yeast transformations were performed using the lithium acetate method (41) and incubated at 30°C. Single colonies were selected and cultivated overnight in 5 ml of SD dropout medium (Solarbio, China) containing 2% glucose but lacking histidine, at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, the cultures were inoculated into 50 ml of the same medium until reaching an OD600 of 0.4. All cells were then transferred into 50 ml of SD dropout medium supplemented with 2% galactose and incubated with shaking at 30°C for 36 hours. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1500g for 5 min at 4°C and washed twice with 10 ml of sterile water. The cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM NaH2PO4 and 300 mM NaCl (pH 8.0)] and broken using acid-washed glass beads (425 to 600 μm, Sigma-Aldrich) by shaking in a grinding machine (60 Hz, 60 s for eight times). The lysate was centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min at 4°C, followed by a second round of centrifugation at 20,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then collected as crude protein extract. The concentrations of different SCPL crude extract were adjusted to the same (40 mg/ml) using a BCA kit before enzyme assays.

To evaluate the activities of CFS and CAS within LsSCPL acyltransferases, we performed in vitro enzyme assays. Each assay involved a 100-μl reaction mixture that included PBS buffer at pH 4.0, 20 μl of crude protein extract (0.8 mg of protein), 1 mM chlorogenic acid, and either 1 mM tartaric acid or caftaric acid as substrates. The mixture was incubated at 30°C for 12 hours, and the reaction was terminated by adding 400 μl of methanol. Subsequently, the reaction mixtures were centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were then analyzed using LC-HRMS.

Transient overexpression of LsSCPLs

The CDSs of LsSCPL genes were cloned into the pEAQ-HT vector via homologous recombination. The recombinant plasmid (pEAQ-HT-LsSCPL) and the empty vector were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Positive colonies were cultured in LB to an OD600 of 0.8, harvested, and gently resuspended in infiltration buffer [10 mM MES (pH 5.6), 10 mM MgCl2, and 200 μM acetosyringone]. After induction for 2 hours at room temperature, 0.01% Silwet L-77 (Solarbio, China) was added, and the suspension was vacuum-infiltrated into lettuce seedlings twice for 2 min each at 16 kPa. The plants were maintained in darkness for 3 days, then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses. Twenty seedlings were pooled to form one biological sample.

Yeast substrate feeding of LsSCPL acyltransferases

BY4741 yeast cells carrying either the empty pESC-HIS vector or pESC-HIS-LsSCPL constructs were initially cultivated for 36 hours at 30°C in 50 ml of SD-His medium containing 2% (w/v) glucose. The cultures were then transferred to 50 ml of SD-His medium containing 2% (w/v) galactose and grown for an additional 24 hours to induce protein expression. Subsequently, CFS-expressing strains (LsSCPL1/3) were supplemented with 1 mM chlorogenic acid and 3 mM tartaric acid in the medium, while CAS-expressing strains (LsSCPL2/4) were supplemented with 1 mM chlorogenic acid and 1 mM caftaric acid. After a further 24 hours of cultivation, cultures were centrifuged at 1500g for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected. The pH of each supernatant was adjusted to 2 to 3 using HCl, and metabolites were extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate. The organic phase was evaporated to dryness under vacuum, reconstituted in 200 μl of methanol, and subjected to LC-MS analysis.

Purification of LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 and kinetic analysis

For the purification of LsCFS1, LsCAS2, and their mutants, yeast cell pellets were resuspended in an equal volume of ice-cold lysis buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 5 mM imidazole] containing glass beads. The cells were disrupted on ice using four 60-s pulses at 60 Hz, with 30-s cooling intervals between pulses. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 20,000g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then incubated with one-quarter volume of TALON metal-affinity resin (Takara) for 2 hours at 4°C with gentle rotation. After packing the resin into a column, it was washed three times with three column volumes of wash buffer [50 mM tris-HCl, (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 10 mM imidazole] and eluted four times with one column volume of elution buffer [PBS (pH 4.5) and 10% glycerol]. The eluate was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-30 kDa centrifugal filter (6500g for 8 min at 4°C). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Sangon Biotech).

For the determination of kinetic parameters, purified LsCFS1 (0.03 μg) and LsCAS2 (0.16 μg) were used. For CAS activity, 1 mM chlorogenic acid was combined with 2.5 to 200 μM caftaric acid for LsCFS1, and 1 mM caftaric acid was combined with 5 to 300 μM chlorogenic acid LsCAS2. For CFS activity, 1 mM chlorogenic acid was combined with 100 to 2000 μM tartaric acid for LsCAS2 and 10 to 500 μM tartaric acid for LsCFS1. In addition, 1 mM tartaric acid was combined with 10 to 400 μM chlorogenic acid for LsCAS2 and 25 to 600 μM chlorogenic acid for LsCFS1. Enzyme activity was calculated on the basis of product formation and expressed in picomoles per second. Km (Michaelis constant) values were determined from Michaelis-Menten plots using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). All in vitro enzyme assays were performed at least three times.

Molecular docking

The protein three-dimensional structures of LsSCPL acyltransferases were modeled using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) (42). Docking simulations were conducted using CB-Dock (43). The resulting molecular graphics were visualized using PyMOL (version 2.3.2).

Mutagenesis of LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 and in vitro enzyme assay

Overlapping gene fragments were amplified separately using LsCFS1 and LsCAS2 as templates, each with primers containing the desired single amino acid substitutions (44). The fragments were then simultaneously cloned into the pESC-HIS vector for expression, as described above. For the determination of key amino acids, purified enzymes of wild type (WT) and mutants (0.51 μg) were used. Each assay involved a 100-μl reaction mixture that included PBS buffer at pH 4.0, 1 mM chlorogenic acid, and either 1 mM tartaric acid or caftaric acid as substrates. Enzyme activity was calculated as production of products and expressed as picomoles per second and further calculated as relative activity to the WT.

Statistical analysis

Data from three biological replicates were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical evaluations included unpaired two-sided Student’s t test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparisons, followed by Tukey tests. Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
