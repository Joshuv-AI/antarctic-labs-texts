Limited culprit-vessel reperfusion before endovascular aneurysm repair for ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm with concurrent inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Naoki Yamada, Reo Izumi, Naoto Tama, Atsushi Takamori, Taketsune Kobuchi, Hiroyuki Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 54-year-old man presented with sudden abdominal and low back pain, hypotension, inferior ST-segment elevation, and recurrent ventricular fibrillation and tachycardia. Computed tomography showed a ruptured infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm. Coronary angiography in a hybrid operating room demonstrated acute thrombotic occlusion of the distal right coronary artery. As malignant arrhythmias threatened safe aneurysm repair, limited culprit-vessel reperfusion with thrombus aspiration and balloon angioplasty was performed without stenting. Endovascular aneurysm repair followed immediately in the same room. This case illustrates that limited coronary reperfusion may bridge selected patients with ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm and true ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction to endovascular aneurysm repair.

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (RAAA) requires immediate hemorrhage control by open or endovascular repair. Concurrent ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) creates a competing time-critical indication because coronary reperfusion usually requires anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, whereas aneurysm rupture requires rapid bleeding control. Misdiagnosis of RAAA is common and is associated with increased mortality.1,2 The optimal sequence and the extent of coronary reperfusion before aneurysm exclusion remain uncertain, particularly when malignant ventricular arrhythmias are present. Previous reports have described RAAA mimicking inferior STEMI, myocardial ischemia secondary to RAAA, or concomitant myocardial infarction and RAAA treated with a hybrid strategy.3, 4, 5 We report RAAA with angiographically confirmed inferior STEMI in which limited culprit-vessel reperfusion stabilized recurrent ventricular arrhythmias and enabled immediate endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in a hybrid operating room.

Case report

A 54-year-old man with a history of untreated hypertension, obesity, and heavy smoking developed sudden lower abdominal and low back pain without chest pain. Emergency medical services found diaphoresis, inability to move because of pain, hypotension, and inferior ST-segment elevation. He arrived at our emergency department 78 minutes after symptom onset with blood pressure of 86/52 mm Hg. Electrocardiography showed ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, aVF, V5, and V6, with reciprocal depression in aVR, aVL, and V1-V3 (Fig 1, A). As abdominal and back pain predominated, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed. During the CT sequence, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia occurred. CT demonstrated an approximately 7 cm infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm with surrounding retroperitoneal hematoma, consistent with rupture, without definite active extravasation (Fig 1, B). Broad hypoenhancement of the left ventricular inferior-posterior wall supported acute infarction in the right coronary artery (RCA) territory.

After CT, the patient had recurrent ventricular fibrillation (VF) and ventricular tachycardia (VT) requiring repeated defibrillation or cardioversion. After each shock, consciousness and circulation returned promptly, with blood pressure recovering to 120-130/71-100 mm Hg. Emergency physicians, cardiologists, cardiovascular surgeons, anesthesiologists, and operating room staff decided to perform coronary angiography and, if needed, limited coronary reperfusion followed immediately by EVAR in the same hybrid operating room.

At 00:00, right femoral venous access and right radial arterial access were obtained. Unfractionated heparin 2000 units was administered before coronary angiography. Coronary angiography demonstrated complete thrombotic occlusion of the distal RCA (No. 3), which was judged to be the culprit lesion for the inferior STEMI and recurrent ventricular arrhythmias (Fig 1, C). An additional 5000 units of unfractionated heparin was administered because repeated thrombotic reocclusion occurred during the procedure. A guidewire was advanced across the occlusion with the support of a microcatheter, followed by repeated thrombus aspiration using a Thrombuster PRO GR catheter (Kaneka Corporation). Because the RCA was markedly large, undersized balloon dilatation was intentionally selected to avoid coronary dissection while obtaining sufficient flow for subsequent aneurysm repair. Balloon angioplasty was performed using a SAPPHIRE NC PRO 4.5 × 10-mm balloon (OrbusNeich Medical) inflated to 18 atm. Final angiography showed thrombolysis in myocardial infarction grade 3 flow, approximately 50% residual stenosis, and no apparent wire perforation, dissection, or other procedural injury (Fig 1, D). No stent was implanted because the immediate goal was not definitive coronary revascularization but temporary culprit-vessel reperfusion as a bridge to EVAR, and stenting would have required immediate dual antiplatelet therapy before hemorrhage control. Percutaneous coronary intervention was completed at 01:25.

The team proceeded directly to EVAR in the same hybrid operating room. Although local anesthesia was considered preferable to avoid induction-related hypotension in RAAA, general anesthesia was selected because recurrent VT/VF requiring repeated defibrillation made airway control and circulatory management necessary. The rupture was considered contained within the retroperitoneal space without definite active extravasation on CT, and blood pressure was relatively maintained after resuscitation. Aortic balloon occlusion was prepared but not performed because hemodynamic stability was maintained. EVAR was started at 01:50. Bilateral femoral artery exposure was performed because it was our standard approach at that time and the patient's hemodynamics permitted femoral exposure. A Gore Excluder endograft (W. L. Gore & Associates) was deployed below the renal arteries with bilateral iliac limb extension. During EVAR, heparinized saline containing 10,000 units of unfractionated heparin in 50 mL of normal saline was infused at 2 mL/h through the sheath; no additional systemic heparin bolus was administered. Embolization of the patent inferior mesenteric artery was attempted because postoperative antiplatelet therapy was anticipated after percutaneous coronary intervention and the patient’s hemodynamic status allowed additional endovascular procedures. However, the embolization was technically difficult and prolonged the procedure. Completion angiography showed no evident endoleak or access artery injury. EVAR was completed at 06:49. The patient did not require blood transfusion.

Postoperatively, he required neither mechanical ventilation nor vasopressor support. Aspirin was started after EVAR on the day of the procedure, and prasugrel was added on postoperative day 2. Staged coronary stent implantation for residual coronary artery disease was performed later during the hospitalization, after which dual antiplatelet therapy was continued. He was discharged ambulatory on hospital day 23. Three-month follow-up CT showed no endoleak, graft-related complication, arterial injury, thromboembolism, or rerupture (Fig 2). Discussion

This case highlights a sequencing dilemma in RAAA complicated by angiographically confirmed STEMI and recurrent malignant ventricular arrhythmias. Previous reports have described RAAA mimicking inferior STEMI, myocardial ischemia secondary to RAAA without an acute culprit lesion, and concomitant inferior myocardial infarction and RAAA treated with coronary stenting followed by surgical aneurysm repair.3, 4, 5 In contrast, our patient had complete thrombotic occlusion of the distal RCA, restoration of flow after coronary intervention, and recurrent VF and VT before aneurysm repair. The coronary lesion therefore represented true type 1 myocardial infarction rather than isolated demand ischemia from hemorrhagic shock.

The goal of coronary intervention was not to complete standard STEMI treatment before aneurysm repair, but to achieve sufficient electrical stabilization to permit definitive hemorrhage control. Treating the aneurysm first would have left ongoing coronary instability and risked recurrent cardiac arrest during induction, transfer, or endograft deployment. Conversely, prolonged coronary intervention or definitive stenting would have delayed aneurysm exclusion and required immediate dual antiplatelet therapy before hemorrhage control. The treatment sequence was therefore based on the immediate arrhythmic threat, the angiographically proven culprit vessel, and the ability to proceed directly to EVAR.

The coronary strategy was deliberately limited. Restoration of thrombolysis in myocardial infarction grade 3 flow reduced the immediate arrhythmic risk, whereas acceptance of residual stenosis avoided a longer procedure before aneurysm exclusion. Deferring stent implantation also avoided immediate dual antiplatelet therapy before exclusion of the contained ruptured aneurysm. Once the aneurysm was excluded and bleeding risk was lower, staged treatment of residual coronary disease and dual antiplatelet therapy could be planned more safely.

The hybrid operating room enabled coronary angiography, limited culprit-vessel reperfusion, and EVAR without repeated transfers while maintaining readiness for defibrillation, anesthesia, and surgical exposure. This sequence may not be generalizable without immediate multidisciplinary endovascular capability. However, in selected patients with RAAA and true STEMI, limited coronary reperfusion may serve as a bridge to definitive EVAR when ongoing malignant arrhythmias make immediate aneurysm repair unsafe. Definitive hemorrhage control should remain the priority, and the extent of coronary intervention should be minimized to what is necessary for safe aneurysm exclusion.
