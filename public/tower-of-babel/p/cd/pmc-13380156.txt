Successful Endovascular Treatment of Traumatic Renal Artery Thrombosis and Dissection Associated with Liver Injury: A Case Report
Authors: Nguyen Duc Hung, Nguyen Duc Cuong, Ho Danh Tuan, Phan Tien Hong, Le Thanh Dung, Le Tuan Vu, Nguyen Tien Long, Vu Van Kham, Doan Thu Huyen, Tran Quang Loc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Combined liver trauma and renal artery thrombosis are severe and rare injuries that may lead to hemorrhagic shock and extensive renal infarction if timely intervention is not performed. We report the case of a 15-year-old male with Grade IV liver trauma (AAST 2018 classification) associated with complete thrombosis of the right renal artery. The patient underwent emergency hepatic arterial embolization for hemostasis, followed by renal artery thrombectomy and balloon angioplasty, resulting in reperfusion of most of the renal parenchyma. During follow-up, a pseudoaneurysm and dissection of the right renal artery were detected and successfully treated with covered stent placement, completely excluding the lesion and restoring arterial flow. The patient recovered well and was discharged. This case highlights the crucial role of multiphasic computed tomography (CT) in identifying combined hepatorenal injuries and confirms the effectiveness of endovascular intervention in controlling hemorrhage, restoring renal arterial patency, and managing complications, thereby maximizing organ function preservation in young patients.

Introduction

Liver trauma is one of the most common solid organ injuries in the abdomen, predominantly resulting from blunt abdominal trauma and, less frequently, penetrating injuries. In recent years, advances in diagnostic imaging—particularly contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT)—and interventional techniques have significantly transformed management strategies [1]. Renal trauma also occurs primarily due to blunt mechanisms and accounts for approximately 5% of all traumatic injuries [2]. Among these, only about 5% involve renal vascular injuries. Deceleration mechanisms may cause intimal tears or renal artery dissection, leading to thrombus formation, distal embolization, and subsequent renal parenchymal infarction [3]. In some cases, these injuries may progress to acute arterial occlusion, renal ischemia, or chronic arterial stenosis due to vascular wall remodeling, with an associated risk of pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula formation [3].

Owing to advances in diagnostic imaging and endovascular techniques, selective transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) has assumed a central role in management. CT enables rapid detection and comprehensive assessment of abdominal organ injuries in hemodynamically stable patients. Subsequently, TAE can be performed to control hemorrhage, treat pseudoaneurysms, or manage arterial thrombosis, allowing most cases to be treated conservatively without surgery. To date, only a few case reports have described the combination of high-grade liver trauma and renal artery thrombosis. Most reported cases focus on isolated renal or hepatic injuries. The novelty of our case lies in the sequential endovascular management of both injuries in a single session, the unexpected development of a pseudoaneurysm after initial revascularization, and the successful use of a covered stent in a pediatric trauma patient. This is the first report, to our knowledge, of a covered stent placement for traumatic renal artery dissection in a 15-year-old.

Case report

A previously healthy 15-year-old male was admitted following a motorcycle accident, on admission, physical examination revealed severe right upper quadrant (RUQ) tenderness and guarding, while there were no signs of generalized rebound tenderness. Explicit examination of the right costovertebral region elicited acute pain, secondary to the retroperitoneal hematoma. Initial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a Grade IV liver injury in segment VII-VIII with active contrast extravasation, a perihepatic hematoma, and a large-volume hemoperitoneum; the liver laceration extended 6.5 cm into segment VII-VIII, with a surrounding subcapsular hematoma measuring 4 × 3 cm (Fig. 1). The patient‘s vascular access was obtained via the right common femoral artery using a 6F sheath, Intra-arterial heparin (2000 IU) was administered. Underwent emergency hepatic arterial embolization using a Histoacryl-Lipiodol mixture (ratio 1:3; 0.5 mL) to achieve hemostasis via the microcatheter 2.2F (Carnelian, Tokai, 135 cm, Tip straight). Subsequently, mechanical thrombectomy and balloon angioplasty of the right renal artery were performed using Sophia Plus 6F (Terumo, 131 cm, Tip straight)–1 pass, Sterling 5.0 × 20 balloon (Boston) inflated once at 6 atm for 30 seconds resulting in the reperfusion of most of the renal parenchyma, although residual arterial dissection and distal branch thrombosis persisted (Fig. 2). During postprocedural follow-up, repeat CT imaging demonstrated the development of a right renal artery pseudoaneurysm and persistent arterial dissection. Because covered stents for the renal artery were out of stock at the facility, the stent was placed 3 days following the diagnosis of the pseudoaneurysm. The patient underwent Stent graff (Bentley InnoMed) 6 × 8mm (Fig. 3), which successfully excluded the pseudoaneurysm and restored renal arterial patency. Postintervention, the patient developed a right-sided hydropneumothorax, which was managed with chest drainage. Associated pulmonary consolidation gradually improved. Follow-up imaging showed stable hepatic and renal injuries, a patent stent without evidence of stenosis or occlusion, and no residual active bleeding (Fig. 4). The patient made a good recovery and was discharged in stable condition. Laboratory values at discharge were: RBC 3.83G/L, hemoglobin 105 g/L, and hematocrit 0.328. Serum creatinine was 95 µmol/L on admission, peaked at 124 µmol/L on postoperative day 2, and gradually normalized to 82 µmol/L at discharge, with a documented stable urine output of >1.5 mL/kg/h throughout the postprocedural course. Given the patient's young age (15 years old) and the long-term risks of restenosis, renovascular hypertension, or delayed renal atrophy, a strict long-term follow-up protocol was established. At the 3-month outpatient follow-up, the patient remained completely asymptomatic with a normal blood pressure profile (110/70 mmHg). Repeat Doppler ultrasound and serum creatinine tests confirmed normal parenchymal perfusion, no evidence of in-stent restenosis, and preserved renal function.

Discussion

Liver trauma and renal vascular injury are 2 severe entities in blunt abdominal trauma, each requiring distinct management strategies; when they occur concomitantly, the complexity of care increases substantially. The advent of CT has represented a major turning point in the grading of liver injuries, the detection of active bleeding, and the assessment of associated vascular injuries, thereby optimizing nonoperative management strategies. Numerous studies have confirmed that multiphasic CT is the gold standard for evaluating high-grade liver trauma, enabling the timely selection of patients who require emergency TAE and significantly reducing the need for open surgery compared with previous eras [1,3,4]. In this patient, the rationale for prioritizing hepatic arterial embolization over renal revascularization was dictated by hemodynamic damage control principles. Active hepatic contrast extravasation with a large-volume hemoperitoneum posed an immediate, life-threatening hemorrhagic risk. In contrast, while renal artery occlusion carries a high risk of organ loss, securing systemic hemodynamic stability by controlling the high-grade hepatic hemorrhage was deemed paramount before initiating secondary organ-salvage revascularization. Accurate identification of the active bleeding site in this case enabled an early indication for selective TAE, contributing to effective hemorrhage control while minimizing additional injury to the remaining hepatic parenchyma. Similarly, timely thrombectomy was appropriately indicated for the thrombotic lesion of the right renal artery.

Regarding the kidney, although renal trauma accounts for approximately 5% of abdominal trauma cases, severe renal vascular injuries—particularly renal artery occlusion or dissection—represent only a small proportion, estimated at around 5% of those cases. However, these injuries may result in complete renal infarction if timely revascularization is not achieved [2,3]. Deceleration mechanisms can cause intimal tearing, thrombus formation, and the creation of a dissection plane, which are typical patterns of injury, as observed in our patient. However, according to the study by Xia et al. [5], renal artery thrombosis more commonly involves the left renal artery, whereas in our case, the right renal artery was affected. Treatment options for renal artery thrombosis include anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, and surgical revascularization; however, each approach has inherent limitations, particularly regarding renal function recovery and the risk of complications [6]. Endovascular techniques, especially mechanical thrombectomy, have increasingly been favored due to their ability to rapidly restore arterial flow, improve renal perfusion, and reduce complication rates compared with surgical intervention or conventional thrombolytic therapy [6]. In this case, thrombectomy combined with arterial angioplasty successfully restored blood flow to the majority of the renal parenchyma, confirming the effectiveness of emergency endovascular intervention in the management of acute renal artery occlusion.

A particularly important feature of this case was the development of a pseudoaneurysm and progressive dissection of the right renal artery during the follow-up period. This complication has been reported in renal vascular injuries and is thought to result from the weakening of the arterial wall following trauma or subsequent revascularization [3]. According to a report by Hörer et al. [7], in cases of main renal artery dissection, the placement of a covered stent represents a useful option for restoring the arterial lumen and excluding the injured segment, complementing the expanding role of endovascular intervention in renal trauma management. Therefore, the decision to deploy a covered stent was consistent with emerging evidence, enabling timely control of the complication and maximizing renal function preservation.

The coexistence of Grade IV liver injury and renal artery occlusion–dissection is rare, and optimal management relies on close coordination among resuscitation, diagnostic imaging, and interventional radiology. Sequential management of hepatic and renal injuries within a single session helped shorten bleeding time, reduce patient transfers, and improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The combination of Grade IV liver injury with right renal artery occlusion–dissection in a young patient represents a rare and complex clinical scenario requiring a multimodal management approach. Multiphasic CT played a pivotal role in detecting injuries and facilitating timely interventional decision-making. Endovascular techniques, including TAE, thrombectomy with angioplasty, and covered stent placement, proved highly effective in controlling hemorrhage, restoring renal perfusion, and managing complications. This case highlights that endovascular intervention not only avoids open surgery but also maximizes organ function preservation, significantly improving the patient's prognosis.
