Precise correction of G6PD Viangchan mutation in iPSCs by prime editing strategy
Authors: Chaiwat Netsawang, Malinee Tongbaen, Natee Jearawiriyapaisarn, Kamonlak Leecharoenkiat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency hold a significant risk of severe hemolytic crises under oxidative stress. Currently, the definitive and curative treatment for the disorder has not been developed. Among over 200 G6PD variants, G6PD Viangchan (c.871 G > A) is the most prevalent and has been extensively studied in Southeast Asia. This study assessed the effectiveness of prime editing for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation in an established mutant HEK293T cell line and G6PD-deficient induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Using optimized modalities, prime editing achieved a high correction efficiency of over 25% in the HEK293T cells. In iPSCs, this gene editing tool yielded satisfactory correction outcomes, with approximately 5% corrected alleles. Our findings indicate that prime editing provides high precision, producing minimal by-products below baseline and showing undetectable off-target effects. Overall, prime editing has the potential to correct the G6PD Viangchan mutation, providing a valuable approach for future therapeutic strategies and the generation of isogenic cell lines to promote extensive studies in drug discovery and the pathogenesis of the G6PD variant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15463-1.Keywords: Prime editing, CRISPR-Cas9, IPSCs, G6PD, Viangchan mutation, SequencingSubject terms: Experimental models of disease, Stem-cell research, Translational research

Introduction

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is an inherited enzyme defect1, caused by mutations in the G6PD gene, which is located near the telomere of the X chromosome2, resulting in depletion of the cytosolic number and catalytic activity of the G6PD enzyme3. The enzyme protects erythrocytes from oxidative destruction by eliminating reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). G6PD-deficient erythrocytes are compromised in the elimination of ROS, causing oxidative damage and hemolysis4. While most individuals with G6PD deficiency are asymptomatic, they are highly at risk of acute hemolytic anemia (AHA) and methemoglobinemia when exposed to environmental triggers, including infection, medications, and particular foods3. Unfortunately, there has been no definitive therapeutic approach for G6PD deficiency. Supportive care, such as withdrawing triggers and providing blood transfusions, is the primary intervention during severe hemolytic episodes. However, this may bring the risks of post-transfusion hemolytic reaction and transfusion-associated infectious diseases to the patients5.

Across over 200 genetic variants described throughout the G6PD gene2, G6PD Viangchan (c.871G > A) predominates in Southeast Asian countries, with reported prevalence of 35.6–97.9% among G6PD-deficient individuals in Thailand6, Laos7, Cambodia8, Malaysia9, and Vietnam10. This variant carries a missense mutation in exon 9 of the G6PD gene, causing an amino acid change from lysine to methionine, causing loss of enzyme catalytic activity11. Individuals with G6PD Viangchan exhibit severe enzyme deficiency, holding less than 10% of normal erythrocyte enzyme activity, and are vulnerable to hemolytic crises when exposed to oxidative triggers12.

To date, only a limited number of studies have explored manipulation of the G6PD gene through viral transduction or CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing. Nonetheless, these studies have highlighted the potential of gene editing both as a curative approach for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and as a tool for generating mutant cell lines to investigate the pathogenic role of a novel G6PD variant13,14. Recent advances in gene editing tools for correcting mutations in stem cells and the development of laboratory-grown erythrocytes offer promising avenues for treating G6PD deficiency through corrected autologous blood transfusions15,16.

CRISPR-Cas9 has been highlighted as an RNA-programmable genome editing tool. Canonical CRISPR-Cas9 is based on inducing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and DNA repair via non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) and homology-directed repair (HDR)17,18. The DSBs can result in undesired random insertions and deletions (indels), which can cause deleterious effects, and HDR is active only in proliferative cells, providing ineffective edits in particular cell types19. Prime editing (PE) is a precise and versatile CRISPR-Cas9-based gene editing strategy that utilizes a fusion protein, comprising Cas9 nickase (Cas9n) and reverse transcriptase (RT), along with an engineered prime editing guide RNA (epegRNA)20. The epegRNA directs the prime editor to the target site via spacer sequence, where Cas9n creates a single-strand DNA nick. This exposes a 3’ end that allows the epegRNA primer-binding site (PBS) to bind, enabling the RT to synthesize a new DNA strand with the desired edit specified by the reverse transcription template (RTT) of the epegRNA. Cellular DNA repair mechanisms then incorporate this edit into the genome. Unlike traditional CRISPR-Cas9 methods, PE achieves high precision and efficiency with minimal indels, as no DSB is induced19. PE has been successfully applied to introduce or correct disease-associated mutations in various cell types, including hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), enabling both curative gene and cell therapy approaches and the generation of disease models for biomedical research21–23.

Recently, a patient-cell-derived iPSC, CHULAi001-A, carrying G6PD Viangchan was established to facilitate the comprehensive studies of G6PD Viangchan, including its pathophysiology, drug discovery, and gene and cell therapy24. In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of prime editing for the genetic correction of the G6PD Viangchan mutation. Our results revealed that prime editing achieves high efficiencies in both the installation and correction of the G6PD Viangchan variant in the HEK293T cell line. A satisfactory level of correction was also observed in CHULAi001-A iPSC, with a non-significant presence of by-products when employing the PE3 system. These findings highlight prime editing as a precise and feasible tool for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation, supporting its potential for developing novel therapies for G6PD deficiency.

Results

Generation of a G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T cell model for gene correction studies

The simple maintenance and transfection methods make HEK293T cells a suitable model for evaluating prime editing activities in gene correction, as shown in previous studies20,23,25. In this study, we employed the PE3 to install a single-nucleotide substitution (c.871G > A) into G6PD exon 9 to establish a G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T cell line as a model for correction of the pathogenic mutation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Following the initial transfection, cells expressing green fluorescence protein (GFP+) were selectively sorted via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and isolated as single-cell clones (Supplementary Fig. 1b). These clones were then screened for the Viangchan alleles using a tetra-primer amplification refractory mutation system-PCR (tetra-primer ARMS-PCR). Among the 30 screened clones, 40% (12 out of 30) were identified as G6PD Viangchan heterozygotes (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Sanger sequencing further revealed that 23.3% (7/30) were heterozygous mutants carrying mono-allelic Viangchan mutations, while 16.7% (5/30) harbored bi-allelic mutations distributed across the 4 copies of the X chromosome26 (Fig. 1b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 1 d). To achieve the homozygous mutants, the installation was repeated in one selected biallelic mutant clone. Following the second round of transfection, both tetra-primer ARMS-PCR and Sanger sequencing confirmed that 21.4% (3/14) of the isolated single-cell clones were tri-allelic mutants, and another 21.4% (3/14) were homozygous mutants for the G6PD Viangchan mutation (Fig. 1b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 1 d). No undesired insertions or deletions (indels) were detected in these clones (Supplementary Fig. 1e). One confirmed homozygous clone was then chosen as the G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T model for subsequent gene correction studies.

G6PD Viangchan correction in HEK293T cells

Prime editing efficacy is influenced by the design of the epegRNA, particularly the lengths of its primary spacer, primer-binding site (PBS), and reverse transcription template (RTT), which must be tailored to the specific loci. These compositions should be systematically optimized to construct an effective epegRNA20. Due to the limited available target sequence near the Viangchan mutation, the epegRNA primary spacer was designed to target 12 bp upstream of the mutation (Fig. 2a). To identify the optimal epegRNA, we first evaluated PBS lengths of 12, 13, and 14 nt, each paired with a constant RTT of 20 nt. Established homozygous G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding PEmax-GFP, epegRNA, and a nicking sgRNA (+ 45, targeting 45 bp downstream of the epegRNA nick site). Approximately 70–90% of the cells were successfully transfected, indicated by the percentage of GFP + cells measured via flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 2a-c). The target region spanning G6PD exon 9 was amplified from bulk populations and analyzed by Sanger sequencing followed by TIDER analysis to determine the correction efficiency (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 2 d). Among the three PBS variations, the 14-nt PBS yielded the highest level of intended edit up to 17.6 ± 0.5%. Although low levels of indels were detected in the experimental groups, these did not significantly differ from those observed in mock control (p = 0.836, Kruskal-Wallis Test) (Fig. 2c). To determine if the prime editing efficiency was influenced by transfection efficiency, TIDER-based correction efficiencies were normalized to GFP fluorescence intensity of individual samples. The normalized results supported the initial findings and revealed that correction efficiency increased in a manner dependent on the length of the PBS (Supplementary Fig. 3a). We next sought to further optimize the epegRNA by fixing the PBS length at 14 nt, varying the RTT length from 18 to 25 nt, and nicking sgRNA (+ 45). Among the tested designs, the 19-nt RTT demonstrated the highest efficiency, achieving an intended edit rate of 23.2 ± 2.1%. (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 3b). In addition, we attempted to incorporate a silent mutation (G > A) to the position immediately upstream (−1 bp) of the Viangchan mutation, as silent mutations have been previously reported to enhance prime editing efficiency by evading cellular mismatch repair (MMR) recognition27. Unexpectedly, the silent mutation significantly reduced editing efficiency by approximately 4-fold compared to the original design without the silent change (17.6 ± 0.5% versus 4.3 ± 0.9%; p < 0.001, one-tailed unpaired t-test) (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Moreover, the silent mutation was rarely detected in the sequencing traces (Supplementary Fig. 3e). This reduction in efficiency was similarly observed with epegRNAs containing 12- and 13-nt PBS lengths.

Introducing a secondary nick on the complementary strand can increase the chance of incorporating the desired edits into the non-edited DNA strand20. To evaluate this strategy, we tested four different nicking sgRNAs, each targeting distinct positions upstream or downstream of the epegRNA target site (Fig. 2a). Among these, the nicking sgRNA (+ 64), targeting + 64 bp downstream of the epegRNA nick site, exhibited the highest editing efficiency, providing 25.2 ± 2.5% intended edits when used in combination with an epegRNA containing a 14-nt PBS and a 20-nt RTT (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 3f).

To further explore strategies to enhance editing efficiency, we compared the PE3 and PE5 systems. PE5 incorporates an engineered dominant-negative MLH1 mutant (MLH1dn), which suppresses the MMR pathway and has been shown to enhance editing efficiencies at certain genomic loci in HEK293T19,27. However, in our system, PE5 exhibited significantly lower correction efficiency than PE3 (9.8 ± 1.7% versus 17.6 ± 0.5%; p < 0.001) despite comparable levels of undesired indels (1.3 ± 0.7% for PE5 versus 1.6 ± 0.2% for PE3; p = 0.266), when using the same epegRNA (14-nt PBS/20-nt RTT) and a nicking RNA (+ 45) (Fig. 2f). Thus, PE5 does not provide an advantage over PE3 for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation. Taken together, these results indicate that the combination of the epegRNA carrying a 14-nt PBS and a 19-nt RTT, along with the nicking sgRNA (+ 64) targeting 64 bp downstream, represents the most effective configuration for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation.

Gene correction in G6PD-deficienct iPSCs

To validate the prime editing approach for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation, we applied the PE3 system to the G6PD-deficient iPSC line, CHULAi001-A, which carries a hemizygous Viangchan mutation (c.871G > A)24. Plasmids expressing PEmax-GFP, the optimized epegRNA (14-nt PBS and 19-nt RTT), and the nicking sgRNA (+ 64) were co-delivered into the iPSCs via electroporation. Transfection efficiency, assessed by GFP expression during FACS, averaged 27.8 ± 12.2% (Supplementary Fig. 4). Correction efficiency was evaluated in both bulk-transfected and GFP+-sorted populations. The target sequences were amplified and analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) and Sanger sequencing to quantify the frequencies of prime-edited and by-product alleles (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 5a). NGS analysis demonstrated that the PE3 system achieved a correction rate of 3.0 ± 3.3% in the bulk population, which increased by 1.7-fold to 5.0 ± 2.1% in the GFP + cells (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results were consistent with correction frequencies estimated by TIDER analysis of Sanger sequencing results, which showed relatively lower efficiencies of 0.6 ± 0.7% in bulk and 1.9 ± 0.7% in GFP + populations, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Gene editing by-products, including random indels and unintended base substitutions near the target site, were detected by both NGS and TIDER analysis. However, their levels did not significantly differ from those observed in untransfected controls (p > 0.05, Student’s t-test) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 5b-c).

Off-target analysis in prime-edited iPSCs

We investigated the off-target effects of the prime editing system used for G6PD Viangchan correction by examining potential intergenic and intragenic loci with high sequence similarity to the epegRNA and the nicking sgRNA (+ 64), as predicted using CRISPOR28. The top five potential off-target sites for each guide RNA were selected for analysis (Table 1). Genomic regions corresponding to these sites were amplified from GFP+-sorted iPSCs (GFP+), untransfected parental cells (UT), and mock-transfected controls, and analyzed by NGS to detect potential mutations. The analyses revealed that none of the candidate off-target loci in GFP+-sorted iPSCs exhibited mutation frequencies above the background levels observed in untransfected parental iPSCs and mock-transfected controls (p > 0.05, Student’s t-test; Supplementary Fig. 6 and Fig. 4a-b). These results suggested that no evidence of random indels or sequence alterations attributable to either the epegRNA or the nicking sgRNA at any of the predicted off-target sites. These findings were further supported by TIDE analysis, which confirmed that indel frequencies at the predicted off-target sites did not exceed baseline levels (Supplementary Fig. 7), consistent with the NGS results. Collectively, these data indicate that the prime editing system did not induce detectable off-target effects at the predicted sites during correction of the G6PD Viangchan mutation.

Discussion

G6PD-deficient individuals, including those with G6PD Viangchan (c.871G > A), are highly at risk of hemolytic crises when exposed to oxidative triggers. In the current mainstream of G6PD studies, understanding the mechanism behind the pathology and identification of potential hemolytic drugs and molecules that restore G6PD enzyme function are of focus to promote management for G6PD-deficient individuals29. Development in genome editing technologies, along with the ability to manufacture erythrocytes that originated from human stem cells, has opened the possibility of autologous blood transfusion during hemolytic episodes15. Prime editing has emerged as a powerful tool for introducing and correcting various disease-associated mutations and for generating isogenic cell models from patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)21,23,30. This approach offers a promising strategy for correcting the G6PD Viangchan mutation to generate genetically matched, mutation-free cell lines, enabling insight into mechanistic studies and the development of novel therapeutic approaches. Our findings demonstrated that prime editing enables efficient and highly precise correction of the G6PD Viangchan mutation, one of the most common G6PD mutations in Southeast Asia, in both HEK293T and G6PD-deficient iPSCs.

Prime editing efficiency varies in a locus-dependent manner20,23,27. In this study, prime editing effectively introduced the Viangchan mutation into HEK293T cells, which carry four copies of the X chromosome. The initial transfection yielded 40% (12/30) heterozygous mutant clones, comprising 7 mono-allelic and 5 bi-allelic mutants. A second transfection of a selected biallelic clone resulted in 21.4% (3/14) tri-allelic and 21.4% (3/14) homozygous mutants. The corresponding allele installation frequencies were 14.2% and 32.1%, respectively, with the higher frequency in the second round reflecting the reduced number of target al.leles. When normalized to allele number, editing efficiencies were comparable across both transfections, supporting the reliability of prime editing at the G6PD Viangchan locus. Notably, the epegRNA used for the installation of the Viangchan mutation was designed to specifically target the wild-type sequence, as the installed Viangchan mutation itself is a PAM-disrupting mutation (AGG → AGA), thereby preventing Cas9n from nicking the edited alleles20. This PAM-disrupting design enhances editing efficiency by preventing reversion of the introduced mutation back to the wild-type allele.

Correction of the G6PD Viangchan mutation was constrained by the limited availability of suitable target sequences near the edit site, despite the versatility of prime editing and the widespread presence of NGG PAM sequences in the human genome. In this case, the only suitable protospacer is located 12 bp upstream of the mutation. Through this restriction, we attempted to maximize the correction efficiency by optimizing the characteristics of the epegRNA and secondary nicking sgRNA. Using the most effective PE modalities, we achieved a correction efficiency exceeding 25% in our mutant HEK293T line, which is consistent with previously reported efficiencies in other loci20. We also attempted to enhance the efficiency by incorporating a silent mutation near the Viangchan mutation to evade the cellular MMR mechanism27. We observed that a G-to-A substitution at the − 1 position (immediately upstream of the Viangchan mutation) drastically lowered the correction efficiency by approximately 4-fold rather than enhancing it. This outcome suggests that a single neighboring mismatch may be insufficient to bypass MMR, as previous studies indicate that at least three adjacent mismatches are typically required25,27and that the nature of the substituted bases also influences the outcome. However, because the Viangchan mutation lies near the intron-exon junction of the G6PD gene, the introduction of a silent mutation required careful design to avoid disrupting splicing signals. There remains potential to improve the prime editing efficiency at this locus. For instance, small sequence modifications in the PBS could resolve epegRNA misfolding caused by unintended interactions between the spacer and PBS sequences, which can hinder epegRNA-Cas9n complex formation and target binding31. Additionally, transient expression of MMR inhibitor, MLH1dn (PE5 system), did not enhance the editing efficiency over the PE3 system in the correction of the Viangchan mutation. This observation has been previously observed in other loci25,32, and may be attributed to the partial MMR deficiency in HEK293T33.

Our results from TIDER analysis revealed low levels of indels in both HEK293T cells and iPSCs following transfection with prime editing components. However, these undesired outcomes were not noticeably different from those observed in mock-transfected controls. This suggests that the detected indels may be attributed to background noise in Sanger sequencing chromatograms, and the inherent sensitivity of TIDER, which can sometimes overestimate indel frequencies by misinterpreting sequencing artifacts as genuine edits34.

Prime editing efficiency in iPSCs is influenced by the delivery method and the form in which editing components are delivered. In this study, we co-transfected PE3 components as expression plasmids via electroporation, achieving a transfection efficiency of approximately 30% with an average gene correction of 5%. Similar efficiencies have been observed in other loci when using comparable plasmid-based delivery approaches23. However, higher editing efficiencies could be achieved by alternative delivery methods, such as in vitro transcribed mRNA and synthetic epegRNA, or ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, which have been demonstrated to outperform plasmid-based systems19. Additionally, correction efficiency in iPSCs could be further improved by regulating certain cellular factors. Transient inhibition of MMR by MLH1dn expression, as utilized in the PE5 system, has been shown to enhance editing efficiency in MMR-proficient iPSCs35. Similarly, inhibition of p53 by its dominant negative variant (p53DD) has been demonstrated to enhance PE efficiency36. Hence, co-delivery of these modulatory elements along with basal PE components, as in vitro transcribed mRNA or RNP formats, may offer a promising strategy to maximize gene correction efficiency in iPSCs.

Successful prime editing requires two layers of sequence complementarity: between the target DNA and the epegRNA spacer, and between the target site and the PBS. These specific interactions contribute to the high precision of prime editing, distinguishing it from other CRISPR-Cas9-based genome editing approaches. To assess off-target effects in our iPSCs, we performed targeted sequencing of predicted off-target loci using NGS and found no significant off-target mutations. This indicates that the prime editing strategy can precisely correct the G6PD Viangchan mutation in iPSCs. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, which reported minimal or undetectable off-target activity when prime editing was used to correct pathogenic nucleotides, as confirmed by targeted deep sequencing37 and whole genome sequencing25. Collectively, these findings support the high specificity of prime editing in gene correction applications.

Inclusion of additional iPSC lines with diverse biological and genetic backgrounds would further strengthen the validation of the prime editing system for G6PD Viangchan correction. However, our experiment was confined to the CHULAi001-A iPSC line24, currently the only established iPSC line carrying the G6PD Viangchan variant. Despite this limitation, it is unlikely that genetic variability would compromise the efficacy of our prime editing approach. Although the G6PD gene harbors hundreds of characterized polymorphisms2, none of the reported mutations overlap with the target sequences of the epegRNA and nicking sgRNA used to correct the G6PD Viangchan in this study. This suggests that genetic polymorphisms might not affect the outcomes of prime editing-mediated correction of the G6PD Viangchan mutation.

The recovery of G6PD enzyme function following prime editing remains to be further validated. Since the clinical pathology of G6PD deficiency primarily affects erythrocytes2,4, isolating gene-corrected iPSC clones and differentiating them into the erythroid lineage would enable comprehensive functional assessment. However, this evaluation was constrained by the limited efficiency of current protocols for differentiating iPSCs into mature erythroid cells. Existing iPSC-derived erythroid differentiation methods typically yield only ~ 33% mature enucleated erythrocytes38–40, a proportion that may be insufficient for robust evaluation of G6PD enzymatic activity. Moreover, in nucleated cells, the deficient G6PD phenotype could be masked due to the synthesis of partially functional enzyme and compensatory NAD(P)H-related metabolic pathways, despite the presence of G6PD mutations41,42. As a result, the heterogeneous erythroid populations with a high proportion of nucleated precursors, produced by current protocols, may obscure the differences in G6PD phenotype of pre- and post-gene correction. These findings underscore the need for optimized iPSC culture systems and improved erythroid differentiation protocols to enable accurate validation of G6PD functional rescue.

In conclusion, these findings highlight prime editing as a promising platform for the precise correction of G6PD Viangchan, paving the way for future therapeutic development and disease modeling in G6PD deficiency.

Methods

Prime editing guide RNA design and plasmid construction

PrimeDesign (https://drugthatgene.pinellolab.partners.org/)43 and pegFinder (http://pegfinder.sidichenlab.org/)44 were used to design epegRNA and nicking sgRNA candidates. The designed sequences are depicted in Supplementary Table 1. Oligonucleotide duplexes for epegRNA components, including primary spacer, epegRNA scaffold, and 3’-extension, were assembled and inserted into the pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor (Addgene #174038; a gift from David Liu)45 using Golden Gate Assembly (NEB) as previously described19,20. Oligonucleotide duplexes corresponding to the spacer sequences of the nicking sgRNAs were ligated into the BPK1520 vector plasmid (Addgene #65777; a gift from Keith Joung)46 by T4 ligase (NEB)20.

HEK293T and iPSC culture

Human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK293T) cells were purchased from ATCC and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, Missouri, USA), 2mM L-glutamine (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). CHULAi001-A iPSC line (hPSCreg, RRID:CVCL_C6P1), previously established and characterized24, were maintained in Essential 8 (E8) medium (Gibco) in cell culture plates coated with iMatix-511 silk (Matrixome, Inc., Osaka, Japan) at 37℃, 5% CO2 under normoxic conditions. When passaged, the iPSCs were dissociated into single cells using 0.5X TrypLE Select™ (Gibco) in 0.5 mM EDTA as described47 and sub-cultured in E8 medium supplemented with 10 µM Y-27,632 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) for 24 h before routine medium change according to the manufacturer.

HEK293T cell transfection

For the establishment of the G6PD Viangchan mutant cell line, 1 × 104 WT HEK293T cells (for the first installation) or biallelic G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T cells (for the second installation) were seeded into a 24-well plate 16–24 h prior to transfection. The cells were transfected at approximately 70% confluency with 800 ng pCMV-PEmax-P2A-GFP (Addgene #180020; a gift from David Liu)27264 ng epegRNA plasmid, and 88 ng nicking sgRNA plasmid resuspended in Opti-MEM™ (Gibco) using Polyethyleneimine Max (PEI MAX; Polysciences, Warrington, PA) in a total volume of 25 µL. For the correction of G6PD Viangchan mutation, 5 × 104 G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T cells were seeded into a 12-well plate 16–24 h before transfection. Cells were transfected using PEI MAX reagent in Opti-MEM™ with a total volume of 25 µL, containing 1,200 ng pCMV-PEmax-P2A-GFP, 396 ng epegRNA plasmid, and 132 ng nicking sgRNA plasmid for the PE3 system. For the PE5 system, an additional 600 ng of pEF1a-hMLH1dn (Addgene #174824; a gift from David Liu)27 was included. At 24 h post-transfection, the plasmid-containing medium was replaced with the complete medium. GFP + cells were analyzed 72 h post-transfection using BD Accuri™ C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA).

G6PD Viangchan mutant HEK293T clone isolation

Transfected cells were harvested at 24 h post-transfection. GFP + cells were analyzed and sorted via FACS using a BD FACS Melody™ cell sorter (BD Biosciences). The sorted GFP + populations were expanded in 24-well plates until they reached 70–80% confluency, after which they were subcloned by limiting dilution at a density of 1 cell/well in 96-well plates. Clonal genomic DNA samples were screened for the G6PD Viangchan allele using tetra-primer ARMS-PCR and confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing.

Gene correction in iPSCs

The iPSCs were pre-treated with 10 µM Y-27,632-supplemented E8 medium for 4 h prior to dissociation into single cells using 0.5X TrypLE™ Select (Gibco) in 0.5 mM EDTA. A total of 1.7 × 106 cells were mixed with a plasmid cocktail comprising 2.8 µg pCMV-PEmax-P2A-GFP, 900 ng epegRNA plasmid, and 310 ng nicking sgRNA plasmid, resuspended in either MaxCyte Electroporation Buffer (MaxCyte, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) or Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco). The cell mixture was electroporated using either the MaxCyte ExPERT ATx™ electroporator (program iPSC-XL2; Maxcyte, Inc.), or the Etta Electroporator X-Porator H1 (EBXP-H1; Etta Biotech, China) with the following pulse parameters: 200 V, 800 µs pulse duration, 5 pulses, and 507 µs intervals. Immediately after electroporation, cells were transferred to a 6-well plate coated with iMatix-511 silk and containing E8 medium supplemented with 10 µM Y-27,632, and incubated at 37℃, 5% CO2 under normoxic conditions. At 48 h post-electroporation, a portion of cells were subjected to GFP + cell sorting using BD FACS Melody™ cell sorter (BD Biosciences), while the remaining cells were maintained as a bulk population. Cells were then further expanded for approximately 7 days prior to genomic DNA extraction for analysis of correction efficiency.

G6PD Viangchan mutation detection by tetra-primer ARMS-PCR

We developed a tetra-primer ARMS-PCR for the detection of the G6PD Viangchan mutation. The outer forward primer (OF-primer) and outer reverse primer (OR-primer) produce a 396-bp PCR product serving as an internal control for the PCR reaction. G6PD Viangchan and WT alleles are discriminated by allele-specific inner primers. The Viangchan-specific inner forward primer (IR-primer) and the OR-primer amplify the G6PD Viangchan-specific fragment of 292 bp, while the WT-specific inner reverse primer (IR-primer), together with the OF-primer, provide a 151-bp PCR product specific to the WT allele. Sequences of the primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The PCR reaction mixture was prepared containing 0.4 µM of each OF- and OR-primer, 0.3 µM IF-primer, 0.5 µM IR-primer, 1X PCRBIO Reaction Buffer, 0.625 units PCRBIO HS Taq DNA polymerase (PCR Biosystems, London, UK), and 100 ng genomic DNA. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 2 min; 35 cycles of 95℃ for 15 s, 67℃ for 15 s, and 72℃ for 20 s; and a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. The PCR products were separated in 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV illumination. The specificity of the tetra-primer ARMS-PCR was validated by Sanger DNA sequencing of G6PD exon9.

PCR amplification of G6PD exon9 for Sanger sequencing

The exon 9 of the G6PD gene was amplified using G6PD exon 9 forward and reverse primers (Exon9-F and -R; Supplementary Table 3), yielding a 521-bp product. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 0.5 µM of each primer, 200 μm dNTPs, 1X HF Buffer (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), 1 unit of Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB), and 250 ng of genomic DNA. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98℃ for 30 s; 35 cycles of 98℃ for 10 s, 68℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 15 s; followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. PCR products were resolved on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV light before submission for Sanger DNA sequencing.

Quantification of correction efficiency by TIDER analysis

Correction efficiency was assessed using TIDER analysis (http://shinyapps.datacurators.nl/tider/)34 based on Sanger sequencing results of the 521-bp G6PD exon 9 amplicons. Sequence chromatograms from prime-edited and mock-transfected samples were compared with those from untreated and WT G6PD controls, serving as the TIDER control and reference traces, respectively. Analyses were performed using default parameters, and only correction frequencies with an R2 > 0.9 were considered reliable.

On-target and off-target analyses by next-generation sequencing

For on-target analysis, target sequences spanning G6PD exon 9 were amplified from genomic DNA derived from bulk and GFP + iPSC populations using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) with OF-primer and Exon9-R primers, producing a 396-bp amplicon. The amplicon libraries containing Nextera adapter sequences were generated and subjected to 150 bp paired-end sequencing using the Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequence reads were analyzed using CRISPResso2 in Prime editing mode (https://github.com/pinellolab/CRISPResso2)48 to quantify correction efficiencies and by-product frequencies. Editing outcomes in bulk and GFP + populations were compared and corrected for background events detected in untransfected parental cells. Statistical significance was determined by a one-tailed unpaired t-test.

For off-target analysis, potential off-target sites of epegRNA and nicking sgRNA were predicted using the CRISPOR web tool (http://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/crispor.py/)28. Genomic DNA from GFP + iPSC populations was used to amplify candidate off-target regions with Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) and primers listed in Supplementary Table 4. The amplicon libraries were constructed and sequenced as described above. Sequencing data were analyzed using CRISPResso2 in standard mode to detect and quantify mutation events at predicted off-target loci. Frequencies of random mutations at these sites in the GFP + iPSCs were compared to those in untransfected parental cells and mock-transfected controls.
