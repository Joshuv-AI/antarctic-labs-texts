Social support intervention for enhancing smoking treatment utilization and cessation among veterans in the United States: A pilot study
Authors: Mariah Branson, Christi Patten, Patrick Hammett, Tabetha Brockman, David Nelson, Erin Rogers, Steven S. Fu, the VAntage Support Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSocial support interventions may enhance access and use of evidence-based tobacco cessation treatments (EBCTs). We assessed the feasibility of a proactive social support intervention for Veterans who smoke.MethodsWe used national Veterans Health Administration (VHA) data to identify Veterans who smoke cigarettes in the United States. We mailed invitation letters to a random national sample, followed by telephone outreach. Veteran-support person dyads were randomly assigned to intervention or control conditions. Support persons in the intervention group additionally received a 1-call coaching session. Assessments were conducted at baseline, 1-month, and 4-months post-randomization. Data collection occurred from March 2021 – August 2022.ResultsA total of 27 dyads were randomized. Two-thirds of Veteran participants were women. Veteran follow-up survey response rates were 81.5 %. Intervention participants reported a higher rate of cessation medication use (57.1 % vs. 30.8 %), use of any EBCT (64.3 % vs. 38.5 %), and biochemically confirmed seven-day point-prevalence abstinence (14.3 % vs 7.7 %) than control participants. The proactive recruitment rate was 6 % for Veterans, 74 % for support persons, and a 93 % support person completion rate for the coaching call intervention.ConclusionsRecruitment methods were feasible, especially for women Veterans. A proactive social support intervention shows promise for increasing Veteran utilization of EBCTs.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is the leading cause of preventable disease and death in the United States and worldwide (Health Effects of Smoking | American Lung Association. Accessed February 18, 2025; Giaquinto et al., 2022). Women who smoke cigarettes experience additional health consequences and mortality risk compared to men. (Yang et al., 2024).

In 2023, the rate of smoking among Veterans was 13.2 % compared to 10.6 % among non-Veterans (Percentage of Current Cigarette Smoking for Adults Aged 18 and over, United States, 2023. National Health Interview Survey. National Center for Health Statistics, 2023). While men and women Veterans smoke at similar rates (2024 Survey of Veteran Enrollees Health and Use of Health Care: Findings Report 2024, 2024), women Veterans may experience unique barriers to quitting including mental health issues, social support disparities, and access to resources (Danan et al., 2019). Thus, new methods are needed to enhance uptake of cessation treatment in Veterans, particularly women Veterans.

The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) offers an array of evidence-based tobacco cessation treatments (EBCTs), including Veteran-tailored resources. Clinical guidelines recommend combining tobacco cessation counseling and medication, but only 5 % of Veterans engage in combined treatment (Fu et al., 2014). The low uptake of EBCT among Veterans may be related to low awareness of VHA cessation resources (Cunningham et al., 2011). Connecting Veterans who smoke with smoking cessation resources, and encouraging their utilization, are high priorities for the VHA.

An innovative approach to connecting Veterans to EBCTs involves training a familiar support person to guide the cessation process. Regardless of readiness to quit, support persons may enable preparation, initiation, and successful completion of the cessation process (Burris et al., 2013). Prior studies have found that having a support person who received coaching calls increased use of quitlines (Patten et al., 2017). We conducted a pilot study to assess the feasibility of a proactively delivered support person intervention for Veterans who smoke within VHA.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Minneapolis VHA Health Care System (MVAHCS) Institutional Review Board (IRB). Study materials were adapted for Veterans in previous work (Patten et al., 2017). The study met the VHA's guidelines for protection of human subjects concerning their safety and privacy. Two Veteran engagement panels reviewed study materials, recruitment, implementation and dissemination strategies and provided feedback based on their lived experiences utilizing healthcare within and outside the VHA. One panel comprised 11 women ages 35–75, and the second panel comprised 10 men and women members ages 30–70. Panel members were racially diverse and service history ranged from post-Vietnam, Gulf War, to post-9/11. Study enrollment occurred from March 2021 – August 2022.

Design

The overall goal of this population-based pilot was to assess the effects of a social support intervention on increasing Veteran treatment engagement even among those not ready to quit. We utilized a two-arm RCT design recruiting VHA-enrolled Veterans who smoke. The study aims were to: 1) assess the feasibility of recruiting dyads comprised of Veterans who smoke and their support person, and 2) assess the feasibility of biochemical verification of smoking abstinence using a remote collection method. Baseline surveys had to be completed by both the Veteran and support person for a dyad to be randomized. We aimed to recruit a total of 30 dyads, based on sample size recommendations for behavioral addictions treatment development (Rounsaville et al., 2001). Eligible dyads were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive EBCT resources plus written support tips for the support person (control) or the same EBCT resources and social support tips plus the social support intervention (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram of a randomized controlled pilot of a support person intervention for smoking cessation among United States Veterans (March 2021–August 2022): recruitment, randomization, and follow-up.Fig. 1

Participants

Veterans

Data from the VHA Corporate Data Warehouse were used to identify Veterans with tobacco use during the past year. Potentially eligible Veterans were mailed a recruitment letter and then received a telephone call. Veterans who expressed interest completed a short phone screener to assess eligibility. The inclusion/exclusion criteria were: 18 years of age or older, smoked ≥1 cigarette in the past 30 days (even a puff) [to capture daily, nondaily and intermittent smoking], cigarettes were the main or only tobacco product used, access to a smartphone, not enrolled in a stop smoking program and did not use cessation pharmacotherapy within the past 3 months (excluded because treatment engagement is an outcome), and ability to identify a friend or family member (support person) who would enroll in the study. Veteran participants were not required to be ready to quit smoking at the time of enrollment.

Support persons

Study staff contacted support persons and if they were willing to participate, an eligibility screener was completed. Support person inclusion criteria were: aged 18 years or older and has contact with the Veteran at least 3 times a week (in-person or virtual). The support person could be a Veteran or a non-Veteran, and their smoking status did not affect eligibility.

Interventions

Dyads in both arms received EBCT resources: (1) a VHA Veteran smoking cessation workbook (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. My tobacco cessation workbook: a resource for veterans. Published online, 2017; U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. My Smoking Cessation Workbook: A Resource for Women. Published online, 2017); and (2) Veteran Resources for Quitting Smoking handout with information for quitlines, websites, coaching, health benefits and tips on quitting, and a list of cessation medications. Dyads also received contact information for Quit VET tobacco cessation quitline (1855QUITVET), and Smokefree Vet website (smokefree.gov).

Social support intervention

All support persons received a brochure containing evidence-based tips on how to help a Veteran quit smoking. Support persons randomized to the control condition received no additional intervention.

Support persons randomized to the social support intervention received the Contemplation ladder (Zhu et al., 2006), a visual aid to facilitate understanding of readiness to quit, and a 15–25 min coaching call with a health coach. Coaches used a manual for quality assurance. Behavior change strategies were delivered using a motivational interviewing approach (Cohen, 2004). Based on Cohen's social support theory, the intervention emphasized supportive actions associated with smoking cessation success, including instrumental (e.g., providing material aid), informational (e.g., quitline resources), and emotional support (e.g., empathy, reassurance) (Cohen, 2004; Cohen and Lichtenstein, 1990). Session topics included intervention rationale, the role of the support person, details about QUIT VET Helpline and other EBCTs, and education on nicotine dependence. The session also addressed readiness to quit, supportive behaviors, techniques for reinforcing the Veteran's progress (e.g., shaping behaviors), and strategies for after the program concludes.

Data collection and outcomes

Feasibility indicators

Indicators of dyad recruitment feasibility included rates of eligibility among those approached, reasons for non-eligibility, and the enrollment/randomization rate among those eligible. Benchmarks included a ≥ 6 % Veteran recruitment rate, enrolling 30 dyads, and a ≥ 65 % rate for follow-up survey responses. Prior proactive outreach trials achieved a recruitment rate of 13 % but as dyads were being recruited, a lower recruitment rate was anticipated. Staff also tracked retention of dyads, completion of biochemical verification among Veterans self-reporting smoking abstinence, and completion of coaching calls.

Survey measures

Surveys were mailed to participants at baseline, one-month, and four-months post-enrollment.

Veterans

Veteran baseline surveys collected information on smoking behaviors, demographics, and social support for smoking behavior change assessed using the 20-item Partner Interaction Questionnaire (PIQ) (Cohen and Lichtenstein, 1990). One-month and four-month surveys had the same questions and collected information on changes in smoking behavior and support received. Measures included the Follow-up surveys assessed 30-day and seven-day point prevalence smoking abstinence.

Veterans who reported abstinence in the follow-up surveys were asked to complete biochemical verification using an iCO Smokerlyzer to test for exhaled carbon monoxide which was mailed to the participant (Meredith et al., 2014). The device measures the amount of breath carbon monoxide in parts per million (ppm); 0–3 ppm confirmed abstinence. Using a smartphone app, participants emailed their results to the study team VHA. Participants were paid for completing the test.

Support persons

Support persons answered questions on the baseline survey about current smoking behavior, demographics, and their support of the Veteran quitting smoking. The one-month and four-month follow-up surveys had the same questions and assessed changes in smoking behavior and support of the Veteran. Measures included a 22-item support provided measure (Thomas et al., 2005). and past 30-day smoking abstinence.

Statistical analysis

Participants in the intervention and control arms were compared descriptively at each survey timepoint using counts, proportions, and means. As the primary aim of this pilot was to establish feasibility of recruitment and retention, between-group tests of statistical significance were not conducted for treatment utilization or abstinence outcomes. All analyses were conducted with the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4.

Results

Dyad participant baseline characteristics

Most Veterans and support persons were White (see supplemental Table) and nearly half the Veteran participants reported an income of ≤$40,000 annually. Half of the Veteran participants chose a spouse/partner as their designated support person. Twelve of the 27 support persons (44.4 %) reported current smoking. The Veteran participant population consisted of 67 % female Veterans. Ages ranged from 30's to 70's for all participants.

Feasibility of dyad recruitment and social support intervention outcomes

A total of 605 Veterans received the invitation letter followed by a phone call. Of those approached, 61 Veterans and 46 support persons were eligible (see Fig. 1). One quarter of Veterans (n = 15) were unable to identify a support person and 38 (6 %) Veterans completed the baseline survey. Twenty-seven dyads provided informed consent, completed baseline surveys and were enrolled (18 female and nine male Veterans). Thirteen dyads were randomized to control, and 14 dyads to the intervention. Our target recruitment goal was 30 dyads and we successfully enrolled 27 dyads. We met our recruitment feasibility goal with a 6 % Veteran recruitment rate. In addition, we achieved a 74 % recruitment rate for support persons. Of intervention participants, 93 % of support persons completed their one-time coaching call.

Feasibility of data collection

The response rates for the one and four-month survey for Veteran participants was 81.5 %. The support person response rates were 77.8 %, and 81.5 %, respectively. These survey response rates were higher than the retention goal of a ≥ 65 % rate for follow-up survey responses.

Veteran participants reported seven-day point prevalence smoking abstinence at the one and four-month surveys. At one month and at four months, one Veteran in the control group and two Veterans in the intervention self-reported abstinence (Table 1) (Meredith et al., 2014). All four iCO readings were 0–3 ppm, confirming abstinence. Veteran use of cessation treatment and smoking abstinence

Veterans randomized to the social support intervention arm were more likely to report medication use (57.1 % vs. 30.8 %), use of any of EBCT (64.3 % vs. 38.5 %), and use of any type of assistance (71.4 % vs 53.8 %) compared to Veterans in the control arm. Rates of combined use of counseling and medication were 23.1 % for control and 21.4 % for intervention. Veterans in the intervention had a higher rate of biochemically-confirmed seven-day point prevalence abstinence than Veterans in the control arm at four months (15.4 % vs 7.7 %) (See Table 1).

Discussion

This pilot study provides promising data on the feasibility of a proactive social support person intervention for Veterans who smoke cigarettes. We successfully recruited dyads of Veterans and support persons, and enrolled a high proportion of women Veterans. In general, there were higher rates of treatment utilization and smoking abstinence in the intervention group.

Due to the small sample size of this pilot, the observed differences between the intervention and control groups, particularly with respect to smoking abstinence, should be interpreted with caution. Another limitation is that iCO breath test results were received by email. Therefore, someone other than the Veteran could have completed the test to have a non-smoking test result. However, strengths of the study include the pragmatic design with minimal inclusion/exclusion criteria that supported study feasibility by expanding the population of Veterans eligible to enroll, as well as a manualized intervention and high intervention completion rate among support persons, and high study retention rates.

Our findings are consistent with prior research and contributed to the development of the current VAntage Support RCT, which aims to recruit 1188 dyads (ClinicalTrials.Gov ID: NCT05868421). A meta-analysis of 15 social support intervention trials found an overall positive effect on smoking abstinence outcomes, but the quality of evidence was deemed very low and recommended additional large, high quality trials (Yuan et al., 2023). The review also examined potential moderators including the support persons' smoking status. Former smoking status was found to have a positive effect, but current smoking status was inconclusive. Of note, 44.4 % of support persons enrolled in the current pilot study reported current smoking at baseline. The ongoing VAntage Support trial addresses gaps noted in prior research and will explore potential moderators (e.g. support person tobacco use status and sex) of intervention effects on study outcomes.
