Pyridium-π interaction preserves N,S-benzylidene thioacetals in acidolysis enabling efficient protein chemical synthesis
Authors: Zhenquan Sun, Yaoyue Zhang, Xueqian Zhao, Yisa Xiao, Zhixiang Zhong, Hongxiang Wu, Xuechen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical protein synthesis provides the atomic-level precision to access peptides and proteins, yet the synthesis of aggregation-prone one remains a major challenge. Here, we report the picolinoylated N,S-benzylidene thioacetal dipeptides (NTDs) strategy to facilitate the chemical synthesis of difficult peptides and proteins. NTDs can serve as an effective aggregation disruptor and be robustly prepared in high yield, which can be rapidly introduced through standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS). Furthermore, we discover a picolinoylation-based strategy to address the acid lability of NTDs, which preserves the scaffold during global deprotection through pyridium-π interaction and can be selectively removed on demand. This approach effectively suppresses aggregation both on-resin and in-solution, facilitates macrocyclization, and permits selective manipulation of cysteine residues during multi-fragment assembly. As a demonstration, we achieve the chemical synthesis of the hydrophobic protein human erythropoietin (hEPO) via a convergent route with native chemical ligation and serine/threonine ligation incorporating the NTD chemistry.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Peptides

Introduction

The development of solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) and chemical ligation has enabled researchers to readily access peptide or protein molecules with atomic-level precision1–3. Especially the latter ligation strategies that chemoselectively joint two unprotected peptide segments together broke the length limitation of SPPS and facilitated the assembly of proteins with several-hundred amino acid residues4–7. These advancements provided a straightforward platform for generating homogeneous, chemically synthesized peptides and proteins, which are invaluable for uncovering the roles of site-specific post-translational modifications (PTMs) in chemical biology and for developing next-generation peptide- and protein-based therapeutics8–11. However, despite significant progress, many peptides and proteins such as membrane proteins12, immune checkpoint proteins13 and other extremely hydrophobic proteins14, remain challenging to be synthesized using SPPS and conventional peptide ligation methods alone15. Although many auxiliary strategies have been developed to improve synthetic performance of above difficult proteins16–18, simpler and more generalized approaches are still required to overcome these obstacles.

Serine/threonine ligation (STL) and cysteine/penicillamine ligation (CPL) were established chemical ligation strategies utilizing the C-terminal salicylaldehyde (SAL) esters of an unprotected peptide fragment to ligate specifically with the N-terminal Ser/Thr/Cys/Pen residue of the other peptide fragment4,5,19. The resulting ligation intermediates, N,O-benzylidene acetal for STL and N,S-benzylidene thioacetal for CPL, can undergo acidolysis by TFA treatment to restore the native amide backbone at the ligation site. More importantly, both STL and CPL intermediate structures can serve as effective aggregation disruptors demonstrated by the successful syntheses of various difficult peptides and proteins, including the immune checkpoint proteins programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)13, and interleukin-220. However, the introduction of such motif heavily relied on the in-solution peptide ligation, increasing extra steps and decreasing efficiency in the synthesis especially when multiple insertion sites are required. The N,O-benzylidene acetal dipeptides (NBDs) strategy greatly simplified the installation of N,O-benzylidene acetal and achieved the direct SPPS of many difficult peptides and proteins21. But the introduced acid-labile N,O-benzylidene thioacetal could not be preserved off resin after the global deprotection, limiting its power on suppressing aggregation in later stage of chemical protein synthesis (i.e. chemical ligations). On the other hand, more acid-stable N,S-benzylidene thioacetal may be a better candidate to solve the on-resin and off-resin aggregation at the same time22. However, an appropriate masking group must be developed for preserving the integrity of N,S-benzylidene thioacetal during global deprotection, otherwise the p-MeO-substituted acetals would undergo rapid acidolysis within 120 s22. Although the temporal acetylation capping at the phenol group of N,S-benzylidene thioacetal has been adopted to increase the acid tolerance in the tunable backbone modification strategy20, sophisticated optimizations such as acidolysis time (typically in 1 ~ 2 hr) and limited types of scavengers were necessary to avoid unmature cleavage as well as over reduction. Therefore, an efficient and robust capping strategy needs to be developed for preserving the N,S-benzylidene thioacetal and, in the best scenario, the N,O-benzylidene thioacetal. Meanwhile, the introduction of N,S-benzylidene thioacetal was also restricted by the on-resin CPL due to its lengthy construction steps. Furthermore, the installation buffer (i.e. pyridine-acetic acid) was incompatible with acid-labile resin such as 2-chlorotrityl resin, limiting its applications on preparing for activated esters of difficult peptides such as SAL esters. In general, a better way to introduce and preserve the N,S-benzylidene thioacetal is in need to unlock the potential of such thioacetal moiety in chemical synthesis of peptide and protein.

In this study, we comprehensively investigate the effect of various capping reagents on enhancing the acidolysis stability of N,S-benzylidene thioacetals, and discover that picolinoylation gave extraordinary capping efficiency as well as acid tolerance probably facilitated by the pyridium-π coordination. For the introduction, we develop a simple and robust one-pot synthetic method to pre-construct the N,S-benzylidene thioacetal scaffold into amino acid building blocks, N,S-benzylidene thioacetal dipeptides (Fig. 1). NTDs are prepared in acid form which can be directly incorporated into the conventional SPPS to install the aggregation-disrupting motif in a much more efficient way. Combining the NTD-incorporated SPPS and the picolinoylation capping method together offer a practical solution to tackle limitations above and facilitate the synthesis of hydrophobic peptide/protein both on resin and in solution. Finally, we successfully achieve the chemical synthesis of a hydrophobic hormone protein, the human erythropoietin (hEPO) using the NTDs strategy, further demonstrating the utility of it in the chemical protein synthesis.

Results

Picolinoylation enhances NTD stability in acidolysis

To develop a robust strategy to construct and preserve N,S-benzylidene thioacetals, we firstly investigated various acylation reagents in maintaining the integrity of thioacetal (Fig. 2a). In the model study, peptide 2 with a Val-Cys CPL intermediate at the N-terminus was prepared by coupling of NTD 1a (Supplementary Fig. 1). When the phenol group of NTD was left uncapped (NTDon) after completion of the sequence, the NTD moiety would be acid-labile and be cleanly cleaved to release the native Xaa-Cys/Pen amide bond linkage by the global deprotection cocktail22. Here we used the trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/ethane-1,2-dithiol (EDT)/water for the deprotection of peptide 2 to generate acidolysis product 3 as the demonstration (Fig. 2a, c). While the phenol group was capped by acylation reagents, the acid lability of NTD would decrease and ideally could be switched off (NTDoff) due to the less electron density at NTD benzene ring. To our surprise, the NTD of peptide 4a with the commonly used acetyl (Ac) group was not completely compatible under such acidolysis conditions. Only around 45% of 4a was observed in one hour deprotection and less than 5 % remained after 3-hr acidolysis (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 5). The complete Ac capping prior to acidolysis was confirmed based on the analysis of fully protected peptide yielded from the soft cleavage of peptide on the acid-labile 2-chlorotrityl chloride (CTC) resin by dichloromethane (DCM)/trifluoroethanol(TFE)/acetic acid (HAc) (Fig. 2c). Such a contradictory result might be due to the following two reasons: 1) the N,S-benzylidene thioacetal moiety was not located in the hydrophobic region but more solvent-exposed region; 2) a TFA cocktail recipe with stronger scavenger EDT was adopted in this model study20,22.

We proposed that a more electron-withdrawing acyl group may further increase the acid tolerance of CPL intermediate. Therefore, various acyl groups including substituted benzoyl (4c-4g) or heterocyclic acyl (4h-4k), sulfonyl (4l-4m) and phosphoryl (4n) were examined (Fig. 2a). Both of their introduction efficacy to SPPS and compatibility to TFA global deprotection were evaluated (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the carbamate 4b with self-cleavable tag was examined as well (Supplementary Fig. 3)23. While many selected acyl groups could not be efficiently integrated to the benzylidene thioacetal on resin from their acyl chloride or anhydride, substituted benzoyl (4c-4f), picolinoyl (4 h) and p-toluenesulfonyl (4 l) were found to have >95% capping conversion to the model peptide, probably due to the combined reasons of reagents stability and the weak nucleophilicity of phenol group at the CPL intermediate. In the following acidolysis experiment, unexpectedly complete deprotection of NTD was observed for the case of 4c, 4 f and 4 l. Meanwhile, a similar acidolysis result as 4a was obtained in the carbamate-protected one 4c and nitrobenzoyl 4d-4e.

To our delight, picolinoyl capped peptide 4 h showed superior stability under acidolysis conditions and it remained nearly intact even after 3-h treatment (Figs. 2b-2d and Supplementary Fig. 6). Picolinoylated NTD exhibited broader compatibility of TFA cocktails including Reagent B (TFA/phenol/H2O/TIPS) and K (TFA/phenol/thioanisole/H2O/EDT) compared to 4a and 4 d, although it could not tolerate harsher conditions such as TMSOTf or TFMSA cocktails (Fig. 2d). The ortho pyridine nitrogen was crucial for the installation of picolinoyl group to the NTD phenol as changing the N position to meta (nicotinoyl, 4i) or para (isonicotinoyl, 4j) led to poor or even no capping, which may be due to the proximity-induced self-catalyzed acylation (Fig. 2e). Moreover, the pyridine nitrogen of 4 h played a key role in the stabilization of benzylidene thioacetal under acid conditions since its tolerance to TFA was completely abolished by the replacement of nitrogen atom to carbon (benzoyl, 4e). Presumably, the remarkable stability of 4 h against TFA/EDT/H2O came from the protonation of pyridine (pKa ~5.5) under such conditions into pyridinium, which acted as a stronger electron-withdrawing group. In addition to that electron withdrawing effect, the pyridinium was also pivotal to suppress the first step of acidolysis, NTD sulfur atom protonation, through cation charge repulsion (Fig. 2e), which was indicated by the loss of acid stability when replacing picolinoyl to similar electron withdrawing groups nitrobenzoyl 4d-4f. Another mechanistic evidence of picolinoylation for preventing NTD acidolysis was that none of the over-reduced NTD was observed for 4 h under reagent B conditions while for other capping groups 4a and 4 d, majority part of materials was reduced with 2.0 Da molecular weight increment, suggesting the ring opening of benzylidene thioacetal followed by silane reduction of the benzyl cation (Fig. 2d, and Supplementary Fig. 7–9).

A pyridinium-π coordination was therefore proposed to stabilize the cation-repulsion conformation24,25. Further DFT calculations on model compounds Pic-NTDoff-H revealed that the pyridinium N+ sits 3.04 Å above the proximal benzene ring (37.9° offset from normal), establishing an offset face-to-face cation-π interaction that depletes electron density at sulfur (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Fig. 10–12) and enhances the negative potential across the pyridinium (dashed circles, red region)26–29. Strikingly, this interaction is absent in the analog Iso-Nic-NTDoff-H (RDG analysis, Supplementary Fig. 11), rationalizing the divergent NTD preservation performance between these positional isomers. Under the d4-TFA titration in d3-acetonitrile, shifts of both pyridine and NTD aromatic protons to downfield were observed, together with a weak NOE interaction between thioacetal proton and pyridine proton (Supplementary Fig. 13-16). Due to the proton exchange of pyridinium proton, the NOE interaction between it and the benzene protons in NTD was not included in this study. It should be also noted that under high content of TFA denaturing environment, the cation-π interaction would be decreased correspondingly but the cation repulsion in proximal still dominated the stability of NTD. Given that cation shield effect from picolinoylation enhancing acid tolerance of NTD, we were curious whether it could also preserve the N,O-benzylidene acetal dipeptide (NBD), which is far more acid labile than NTD (Fig. 2g). To our surprise, in several peptide cases, over 70% of different NBDs could be indeed preserved by picolinoylation during the global deprotection by TFA/TIPS/H2O conditions. However, the integrity of NBDoff was found to be dependent on the steric hindrance at NBD site and the peptide sequence as well. Thr-NBDoff was more prone to undergo acidolysis (71% in Tyr-Thr vs 89% in Ile-Ser NBDoff) and especially with Thr-Thr NBDoff one, complete acidolysis was observed instead. In addition, the picolinoylation could be mildly removed under pH 4.0–6.0 conditions (Supplementary Fig. 17–20). According to these results, a new capping strategy based on picolinoylation was successfully developed to preserve the structure of NTD and potentially NBD as well in the peptide/protein synthesis, facilitating their aggregation disruption in the following ligations or modifications.

Preparation of Xaa-Cys/Pen NTD

After solving the acidolysis challenges by the discovery of picolinoylation capping method, we are confident to continue the work on developing a simple, robust and efficient preparation strategy of NTD building blocks. After optimizations under various conditions, a feasible one-pot synthetic route using all commercially available chemicals was established as shown in Fig. 3. Briefly, the Fmoc-protected amino acid was firstly activated to form the corresponding salicylaldehyde (SAL) ester by 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide (EDCI) and 4-methoxysalicylaldehye in the presence of a catalytic amount of 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP). Then, the resultant SAL ester without purification was ligated to free cysteine by the Cys/Pen Ligation under ethanol containing 5% pyridine-acetic acid to yield NTDs 1, 6-24 with moderate to high yield in one pot manner (method A, see Supplementary text). Notably, high crude purity could be achieved in most of NTD syntheses, simplifying its purification (Supplementary Fig. 22). Alternatively, stepwise protocol can be also adopted for some amino acids SAL esters prone to hydrolyze such as Arg (Supplementary Fig.23). By utilizing this synthetic method, all kinds of Xaa-Cys NTDs covering 20 canonical amino acids were facilely synthesized with 30-96% isolated yields.

Various types of side chains in amino acids including hydrophobic, electrically charged and polar uncharged side chains with protecting groups were well tolerated. Substrates with delicate protecting groups such as His(Trt) were also compatible albeit the yield was lower (30%). Even though the proline with the secondary bulky amine could be feasibly converted into Pro-Cys NTD 14 in 89% yield. Moreover, this route provides a simple way to access d-configuration NTD as well, where d-Ala-d-Cys NTD 8 d was obtained in one pot with over 76% yield. Apart from the Cys, the other thiol-containing unnatural amino acid penicillamine (Pen) was applicable as well in this route to afford Xaa-Pen NTDs in 41-66% yield (i.e. Val-Pen NTD 1b). By combining this with desulfurization chemistry, the NTD insertion sites could be further extended to a broad range. More importantly, the R2 group at salicylaldehyde moiety can be further functionalized such as adding an orthogonally protected amine for the later attachment of solubilizing tags in SPPS (i.e. 6c, 15c, etc.). Meanwhile, the highly twisted structures of NTDs were observed by our extensive NMR studies (Supplementary Fig. 50–51). Accompanying with a pair of trans- (major) and cis- (minor) conformational isomers, the S-configuration stereochemistry at the position of N,S-benzylidene thioacetal carbon was confirmed by nuclear overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY), which was in agreement with our previous observations on N,O-benzylidene acetal intermediates5,21. Such structures of NTDs could help prevent the peptide aggregation by blocking the amide hydrogen bonding and altering the peptide backbone conformation22.

Applications of Xaa-Cys/Pen NTD on peptide synthesis

With above practical NTD preparation method and picolinoylation capping (NTDoff) strategy in hand, we are delighted to investigate the application of NTD-SPPS in chemical synthesis of peptides and proteins (Fig. 4). The free phenol group in the NTD will not affect the coupling and is fully compatible with Fmoc-SPPS. Even though O-acylation may occur by the long-time treatment of excess amount of activated ester from amino acids in some cases, the phenolic ester is unstable and can be completely removed under Fmoc deprotection conditions. Clearly, the rapid introduction of NTD especially multiple NTDs by conventional SPPS circle is one of the biggest advantages of NTD-SPPS. Previously established methods to introduce N,S-benzylidene thioacetal moieties relied on either the in-solution or the on-resin Cys/Pen Ligation, which cost more purification steps or longer time20,22. For example, in the on-resin installation method, two overnight processes (deprotection of Cys and CPL) were required in total to introduce one CPL ligation intermediate structure to the desired peptide sequence. Also, the utilization of pyridine-acetic acid buffer in the on-resin CPL limits its application on acid-labile resin (e.g., CTC resin). By contrast, the easily obtained NTD acids can be efficiently incorporated into Fmoc-SPPS in one hour similar as other normal amino acid building blocks, greatly simplifying its introduction. More importantly, the NTD-SPPS is fully compatible with CTC resin, giving unique access to NTDs-containing peptide with C-terminal functionalization for later chemical peptide/protein synthesis. Combining with NBDoff by picolinoylation, it is also possible to isolate peptide containing NBD scaffold as well (Fig. 4a).

a Synthesis of peptides preserving NBDoff and peptidyl activated ester bearing one or multiple NTDsoff. b NTD-assisted peptide macrocyclization. c chemoselective cleavage of CPL/STL intermediate in the presence of NTDoff. NBDs: N,O-benzylidene acetal dipeptides.

Firstly, the NTDs/NBDs was switched off by picolinoyl capping after the completion of the whole peptide sequence (Fig. 4a). Then the peptide was either globally deprotected by TFA cocktails to give peptide 25-27, or cleaved with side-chain protection by soft cleavage and coupled to amino acid bearing SAL ester or thioester through N + 1 strategy to produce 28-3030. The Ile-Ser NBDoff could be preserved in peptide 25a-b and 26 with 18-24% isolated yield, while for peptide 27 containing Tyr-Thr NBDoff, a lower yield was obtained (6%). Following by global deprotection, peptidyl salicylaldehyde ester 28 with one Val-Cys NTDoff was obtained at 42% HPLC yield. Similarly, two NTDs could be conveniently introduced as well by NTD-SPPS. Later by the N + 1 reaction and TFA deprotection the peptidyl thioester 29 with Val-Cys and Val-Pen NTDoff was produced at 24% isolated yield. It should be noted that the Val-Pen NTD, which is slightly more acid labile based on our previous kinetic study, could be well preserved as well as Cys NTD in the TFA cocktail after its capping with picolinoyl chloride22. Additionally, in total three NTDoff moieties could be maintained in the peptide 30 with 19% isolated yield, further demonstrating the efficacy and flexibility of NTD introduction by the NTD-SPPS strategy. By contrast, no product was obtained in the synthesis of 30 by on-resin protocol as the peptide has been prematurely cleaved during the on-resin ligation conditions.

In addition to the preparation of peptidyl activated (thio)esters for chemical ligations, the incorporation of NTD could be applied as a “turn” inducer for peptide macrocyclization (Fig. 4b)31,32. Following the standard NTD-SPPS, linear crude peptides 31a-c was feasibly prepared. Without capping NTD, linear peptides could directly undergo macrocyclization under 1 mM concentration and subsequently TFA treatment to give the cyclic peptide intermediate 32a-c. After the sodium tetraethylborate (NaBEt4) mediated add-and-done desulfurization33, the mutated Cys for NTD installation was smoothly restored into Ala, affording the final product 33a-c. Three cyclic peptide examples Cyclogossin B 33a34, Segatalin B 33b and G 33c35, were successfully synthesized with 11–23% isolated yield in cyclization step and 46%–67% HPLC yield in desulfurization.

Furthermore, the NTDoff can serve as a flexible protecting group, differentiating other Cys residues introduced by later ligations (Fig. 4c). For example, peptidyl SAL ester 28 underwent a CPL with peptide 34a containing N-terminal Cys to generate peptide 35a carrying one NTDoff and one uncapped N,S-benzylidene thioacetal. The unprotected CPL intermediate can be selectively deprotected under acidolysis condition in the presence of NTDoff to produce peptide 36 in 60% HPLC yield, providing convenience for latter desulfurization or other manipulation. Similarly, the generated N,O-benzylidene acetal from STL between peptide 28 and the N-terminal Thr of peptide 34b could be also chemoselectively removed by TFA cocktail to afford peptide 37 in 71% yield while its NTDoff remained intact. In brief, the NTD-SPPS and picolinoylation together could provide a simple and effective way to synthesize NTD-containing peptide with diverse C-terminal functionalities such as cyclization, salicylaldehyde esters and thioesters that could be difficult to obtain by other methods.

Chemical synthesis of hEPO using NTD chemistry

With the establishment of NTD-SPPS, we were excited to further investigate whether it could be applied for the chemical synthesis of a difficult protein, human erythropoietin (hEPO)36–39. hEPO is an important therapeutic hormone protein containing 166 amino acid residues and complicated glycosylation modification. We envisioned that the introduction of NTD-SPPS and a convergent synthetic route applying both NCL and STL, may develop an efficient chemical synthesis of hEPO and provide more opportunities in finding more potent therapeutic modalities.

To proceed the total synthesis of hEPO, we divided this 166-AA hormone into five fragments 39–43 for a convergent synthesis adopting two native chemical ligations (NCL) and two Ser/Thr Ligations (STL) (Fig. 5). Ala19 of 39, Ala60 of 41, Ala79,98 of 42 were temporarily mutated to Cys for NTD installation or NCL, which could be all restored back to native Ala by free-radical based desulfurization40. All native Cys residues in hEPO were orthogonally protected by Acm group that could be removed at the end of the synthesis. Peptidyl salicylaldehyde esters 39 and 42 were synthesized by NTD-SPPS. The incorporation of Glu-Cys NTD at 38 and Lys-Cys NTD at 42 were important for the peptide purity after SPPS. It was found that the removal of the NTD at the first fragment could decrease the co-elution of final ligation product and impurities during HPLC so that it was deprotected after the preparation of salicylaldehyde ester 39. Meanwhile, the other NTD at 42 where a poly-His tag was attached was essential to increase the fragment solubility and reactivity in later chemical ligation. As for other fragments 40, 41 and 43, they were prepared by the conventional Fmoc-SPPS.

Chemical synthesis of hEPO (1-166).

a synthetic scheme of hEPO. b UV-trace comparison of hEPO before and after refolding. c Deconvoluted MS spectra comparison of unfolded and folded hEPO potein. d CD spectrum of hEPO. e Amino acid sequence of hEPO (1–166) where residues highlighted in orange, gray, and underlined in blue represented the NTD insertion, NBD position and ligation site respectively.

With all starting materials in hand, we performed the first Ser/Thr ligation between the peptidyl salicylaldehyde ester 39 and fragment 40. Under the pyridine-acetic acid buffer conditions41, the N-terminal threonine of 40 was chemoselectively ligated to the C-terminal salicylaldehyde ester of peptide 39 without affecting the p-toluene thioester. The ligated product 44 was obtained in 18% HPLC yield. Then it was subjected to the native chemical ligation with the N-terminal Cys of peptidyl hydrazide 41 under aqueous buffer conditions, affording peptide 45 at 23% yield. Without further acidolysis, the STL intermediate was maintained in the peptide 39 for disrupting peptide aggregation in the following manipulation13.

On the other hand, the second STL was proceeded smoothly between the C-terminal salicylaldehyde ester of fragment 42 and the N-terminal serine of peptide 43. With the help of solubilizing tag on NTDoff at 42, substrate could dissolve very well in the STL buffer to afford the ligated product in high purity. Subsequently, the N-terminal Thz group of the ligated peptide was removed in one pot by the treatment of methoxyamine hydrochloride under pH 3.8 denaturing conditions. Followed by hydrazine treatment to remove the picolinoylation at NTDoff, the ligated product 46 was obtained in 20% yield over 3 steps.

Finally, the peptidyl hydrazide 45 was activated by NaNO242 at -15 °C to form the corresponding 4-mercaptophenylacetic acid (MPAA) thioester in situ. Then the last NCL was performed to assemble the generated peptidyl thioester from 45 and the fragment 46 together to yield the peptide 47 in 30% isolated yield. After the acidolysis of 47 under TFA conditions to deprotect NTD and STL intermediates with 47% HPLC yield, the resulted intermediate were smoothly desulfurized by the NaBEt4-mediated superfast desulfurization to afford the peptide 48 in 38% isolated yield. After removal of Acm groups at the native Cys residues by PdCl243,44, the linear hEPO 49 was acquired in 74% yield. At last, the unfolded protein 49 was presented to refolding conditions37 with gradient dialysis to produce the folded hEPO 50 with 24% yield. Compared to the UPLC-MS spectra before and after folding, a discernible retention time shift of in the UPLC UV trace as well as changes of MS pattern suggested the formation of folded protein structure (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 107–113). That was further supported by deconvoluted MS spectra showing 4-Da difference, indicating the formation of two pairs of disulfides (Fig. 5c). Meanwhile, the highly abundant alpha-helix of 50 was characterized by circular dichroism (CD) spectra, exhibiting similar structure as the reported literature (Fig. 5d). In summary, the chemical synthesis of hEPO (1–166) was successfully achieved via NTD-SPPS strategy to suppress peptide aggregation and increase ligation solubility.

In conclusion, the NTD-SPPS strategy adopting readily accessible NTD building blocks was developed to efficiently introduce N,S-benzylidene thioacetals as effective aggregation disruptors for both on-resin SPPS and in-solution chemical ligation. O-Picolinoylation was the key to switch off the acid lability of NTDs and even NBDs in some cases, well preserving the precious scaffold during TFA treatment. By applying this strategy, the hydrophobic protein human erythropoietin (hEPO) could be successfully synthesized through the combination of native chemical ligation and serine/threonine ligation. With other unnatural beta-thiolated amino acids45,46 and desulfurization chemistry together, it is believed that the NTD-SPPS method could provide more flexibility and functionality in the chemical synthesis of broad range of difficult or hydrophobic peptide and protein in the future.

Methods

Syntheses of NTDs

Method A (one-pot synthesis)

To a 100 mL flask containing Fmoc-AA-OH (10 mmol) in DCM (40 mL), 2-Hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde (1.52 g, 1.0 equiv., 10 mmol), EDCI (1.0 equiv, 10 mmol), DMAP (1 mol%) were added. The reaction mixture was stirred under room temperature for 1–3 hr. After that, the mixture was washed by 0.1 M HCl*3, water and brine*1 (10 ~ 20 mL) sequentially. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and then the volatiles were removed by rotary evaporator. The residual crude SAL ester was dissolved in 100 mL EtOH containing 5-10% pyridine-acetic acid together with L-cysteine hydrochloride (2.36 g, 1.5 equiv, 15 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. After the complete of reaction, the mixture was condensed and extracted by EA (100 mL), 0.1 M HCl*3, water and brine*1 (50 ~ 100 mL). Subsequently, the organic solvent would be removed in vacuo and the crude product was further purified by column chromatography (Hexene: EA 2:1 with 1% HAc), affording the desired NTD as white solid in 60–80% yield.

Method B (stepwise method)

To a 100 mL flask containing Fmoc-AA-OH (10 mmol) in DCM (40 mL), 2-Hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde (1.52 g, 1.0 equiv, 10 mmol), EDCI (1.0 equiv, 10 mmol), DMAP (1 mol%) were added. The reaction mixture was stirred under room temperature for 1–3 hr. After that, the mixture was washed by 0.1 M HCl*3, water and brine*1 (10 ~ 20 mL) sequentially. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and then the volatiles were removed by rotary evaporator. The residual oil was purified by column chromatography (Hexene: EA 4:1), generating the desired SAL ester in 60-95% yield. The purified SAL ester was dissolved in 100 mL EtOH containing 5-10% pyridine-acetic acid together with L-cysteine hydrochloride (2.36 g, 1.5 equiv, 15 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. After the complete of reaction, the mixture was condensed and extracted by EA (100 mL), 0.1 M HCl*3, water and brine*1 (50 ~ 100 mL). Subsequently, the organic solvent would be removed in vacuo and the crude product was further purified by column chromatography (Hexene: EA 2:1 with 1% HAc), affording the desired NTD as white solid in 80-90% yield.

Installation of N, S-benzylidene thioacetal dipeptides (NTDs)

The N, S-benzylidene thioacetal dipeptides (4.0 equiv), HATU (3.9 equiv) and DIPEA (8.0 equiv) were added, and the solution was stirred for 1 min. The above solution was added to peptide bound resin containing free N-terminal amine and coupled for 1 h to enable the installation of NTD (for 2.0 eq. NTDs, extension of coupling time to 2 h was suggested). Next, the resin was washed with dimethylformamide and dichloromethane. The resin was subjected to the standard Fmoc-SPPS to give the desired peptide sequence (for more details about Fmoc-SPPS, see supplementary text).

Capping of NTDs by 2-picolinoyl chloride

After the last coupling and washing step of the Fmoc-SPPS, picolinoyl chloride (25.0 equiv) and DIPEA (50.0 equiv) in DMF were added to the peptide-bound resin for 3 hr to overnight. Then the peptide was cleaved from the resin either by soft cleavage (HAc/TFE/DCM, 1/1/8) or global deprotection (TFA/DODT/TIPS/H2O = 94/2/2/2, vol.).
