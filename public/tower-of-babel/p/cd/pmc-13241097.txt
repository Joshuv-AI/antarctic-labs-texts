Uncovering hidden risk: The role of social history in identifying possible latent tuberculosis infection in primary care
Authors: Mayisah Rahman, Farah Leclercq Ferreiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculosis (TB) remains a significant public health concern worldwide, with up to 13 million individuals in the United States estimated to have latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI). Although active TB is rare in the general population, latent infection persists among high-risk groups such as those with a history of incarceration, homelessness, or immigration from endemic areas. This case illustrates how thorough social history-taking in a primary care setting led to the identification of possible LTBI in an asymptomatic patient presenting with fatigue. This case emphasizes the critical role of comprehensive social history in uncovering latent disease and preventing missed opportunities for intervention.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world’s most significant infectious diseases, despite ongoing global prevention and treatment efforts. According to the World Health Organization’s Global Tuberculosis Report 2023, an estimated 10.6 million people developed TB and 1.3 million died from the disease in 2022, making it the second leading cause of death from a single infectious pathogen worldwide.[1]

While the global burden remains substantial, the United States continues to face challenges related to latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), which affects an estimated 13 million These persons constitute a hidden reservoir for potential reactivation and transmission, especially among high-risk populations such as those with a history of incarceration, homelessness, or immigration from endemic regions.[2]

Recent public health recommendations have renewed focus on expanding screening efforts in primary care. The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) 2023 statement, as summarized in JAMA Network Open by Menzies, emphasizes the need for broader LTBI screening in adults at increased risk and highlights primary care as a critical, yet underutilized, setting for implementation.[3]

Primary care clinicians are uniquely positioned to identify such risks through detailed social history-taking, enabling early diagnosis and referral for preventive therapy. This case demonstrates how comprehensive social history in a routine family medicine visit led to appropriate screening and identification of possible latent TB infection in an asymptomatic patient, underscoring the vital role of primary care in bridging individual and public health priorities.

Case Report

A 56-year-old male presented to a family medicine clinic as a new patient, reporting several months of mild fatigue. He denied fever, chills, night sweats, cough, hemoptysis, or weight loss. His medical history was unremarkable, and he was not taking any medications.

During review of his social history, the patient disclosed a history of incarceration several years earlier and recalled receiving medication for an unspecified illness at that time. He denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use, recent travel, or known TB exposure.

Examination and investigations

Physical examination was unremarkable, and vital signs were within normal limits. Lungs were clear to auscultation bilaterally. Routine laboratory evaluation included a complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and sexually transmitted infection screening (HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea, and chlamydia).

Given the history of incarceration, a QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus test was obtained and returned positive (TB1–Nil = 0.72 IU/mL; TB2–Nil = 0.83 IU/mL; positive cutoff ≥ 0.35 IU/mL). The patient was advised to obtain a chest X-ray to rule out active pulmonary disease and was referred to an infectious disease for evaluation and management of presumed LTBI.

A chest X-ray was obtained and showed no abnormalities. In accordance with centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) reporting requirements, the local health department was notified, and outreach efforts were initiated to facilitate follow-up.

Discussion

This case underscores the critical role of comprehensive social history-taking in identifying individuals at risk for LTBI, even when presenting with nonspecific symptoms. Although TB incidence in the United States remains relatively low, latent infection remains prevalent among high-risk groups, including individuals with prior incarceration, homelessness, or limited access to health care.[145]

The USPSTF recommends screening adults at increased risk for LTBI using either tuberculin skin testing or interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) such as the QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus.[6] A positive IGRA indicates prior sensitization to Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens but does not distinguish between active and latent infection, making further evaluation with chest imaging and symptom assessment essential before initiating In this case, appropriate screening and imaging confirmed latent TB, allowing timely referral and public health notification.

Recent evidence from JAMA Network Open emphasizes expanding LTBI screening to primary care settings to enhance accessibility and continuity of care, particularly for individuals who might otherwise remain undiagnosed.[3] Addressing barriers such as unstable housing, limited communication access, or competing life priorities requires collaboration between primary care, infectious disease specialists, and public health departments to ensure treatment initiation, adherence, and follow-up.

Current treatment regimens for LTBI include isoniazid for 6–9 months, rifampin for 4 months, or once-weekly isoniazid–rifapentine for 12 weeks (3HP).[7] Successful outcomes depend on coordinated care between primary and specialty services and ongoing patient education about treatment adherence and potential side effects.

From an educational standpoint, this case reinforces the family physician’s dual role as both clinician and public health advocate. Through intentional and comprehensive social history-taking, family physicians can uncover hidden risk factors that may otherwise remain undetected. This underscores how primary care serves as a vital bridge between individual health management and community-level disease prevention.

Conclusion

Incorporating social history as a routine, deliberate part of every patient encounter allows clinicians to identify latent or hidden health risks that standardized screening may overlook. Thoughtful inquiry in primary care not only improves individual outcomes but also strengthens the broader public health response to TB and other latent infections. By integrating social and clinical perspectives, family physicians play a central role in advancing TB prevention and strengthening community health resilience.

Limitations and future considerations

This report reflects a single case, and findings may not be generalizable across populations. Future studies should evaluate the prevalence and outcomes of LTBI detection through enhanced social history screening in primary care settings. Further research is also warranted to identify strategies that improve linkage to care and treatment completion among high-risk individuals.
