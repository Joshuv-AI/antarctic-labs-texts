Can PIM3 predict mortality adequately for patients admitted to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit in a resource limited setting? A single-center study
Authors: Desy Rusmawatiningtyas, Renova Astriani Hutapea, Antonius H Pudjiadi, Firdian Makrufardi, Hennie Knoester, J B M van Woensel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Indonesia the number of critically ill children by far exceeds Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) capacity. A quantitative scoring system that can be used by clinicians from the initial encounter might be helpful as an objective assessment method to predict patient outcomes, thus aiding clinical decision-making, patient stratification and prioritization. The aim of this study was to assess the performance of the Paediatric Index of Mortality-3 (PIM3) score to predict mortality of critically ill pediatric referral patients that had been accepted and admitted to the PICU of Dr Sardjito General Hospital Yogyakarta.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included patients from August 1st, 2022 -January 31st, 2023 who were referred and admitted to the PICU of Dr. Sardjito General hospital, Yogyakarta, Indonesia. The discriminative ability (to differentiate between survivors and non survivors) of PIM3 scoring system was analyzed with the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsA total of 220 cases (median age 64 (1-215) months) were included. The overall mortality rate was 22.1% (49/220). Subjects’ PIM3 score ranged from 0.11 to 98.03. The Standardized Mortality Ratio (SMR) of PIM3 with 95% confidence interval (CI) was 2.23 (1.65–2.90). The AUC with 95%-CI was 0.84 (0.77–0.91, p < 0.001). The difference between observed and predicted mortality by PIM3 was statistically significant, indicating that actual mortality was higher than PIM3 predicted.ConclusionAmong critically ill children who were referred form peripheral hospitals and admitted to PICU at Dr Sardjito General Hospital, PIM3’s discrimination was good, but the calibration was poor, indicating that PIM3 was unfitted as mortality predictor in this study population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06114-5.Keywords: PIM3 score, Pediatric, Mortality, Pediatric Intensive Care Unit

Background

In terms of limited resource setting as well as Indonesia’s unique characteristics with vast territory, archipelagic nature and variable high population density, the availability of PICU beds is scarce and unevenly distributed across the country, posing a challenge in providing care to critically ill pediatric patients. Not only is there a shortage of PICU facilities in Indonesia, but there is also a lack of pediatric intensivists. In addition, there is no well-organized and dedicated transport team to assist in transferring critically ill pediatric patients between healthcare facilities [1, 2].

Due to the limited PICU capacity, the Indonesian Pediatric Association (IPA) has issued recommendation for patient admission to the PICU based on a priority system, which divides patients into four groups. Within this priority system, patients who have the highest chances of surviving the critical illness (i.e. those who were previously healthy) are prioritized for PICU admission. The priority system we use is as follows: Priority 1: very ill children who, with intensive therapy, can fully recover and grow and develop according to their genetic potential. Priority 2: critically ill children with underlying diseases that are currently untreatable medically, but can be fully managed with intensive therapy, until the child returns to their pre-PICU condition. Priority 3: critically ill children with underlying diseases that result in a permanent lack of contact with their environment and hinder their growth and development. Priority 4: critically ill children with a very poor prognosis, to the extent that even intensive therapy cannot prevent the process of death (not an indication for PICU admission) [3]. Despite the presence of this recommendation, in daily practice frequently difficulties in determining which patient should be prioritized for PICU admission are encountered.

An objective quantitative scoring system might be helpful as an assessment method to predict patient outcomes, ideally providing objective information that may aid clinical decision-making, patient stratification and prioritization [4–7]. Several scoring systems have been developed to assess the severity of disease in children, including pediatric logistic organ dysfunction (PELOD) score, pediatric sequential organ failure assessment score (pSOFA) and the pediatric risk of mortality (PRISM) score. Limitations of these scoring systems include licensing cost, the need for measurement of vital signs and extensive blood sampling, that may not be readily available in referring hospitals in limited resources setting such as Indonesia [4, 8].

Compared to other scoring systems, the PIM3 score requires less data and does not require a licensing fee. It is available as an online application and therefore the PIM3 score could be a simple score that may be feasible to apply in the pre-referral setting. The PIM3 score is the latest revision of the initial PIM score that was developed in 1997 and consists of 10 clinical variables [8–11]. The necessary clinical variables are routine data obtained from anamnesis and physical examination at the first hour within PICU admission, while the laboratory variable is an arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis. It has been reported that the PIM3 score is an affordable and highly useful option, particularly in developing countries. The PIM score is developed for benchmarking, to compare groups of patients in different PICU’s [12–17]. A study from South-Korea reported that for patients aged < 18 years, the discrimination was acceptable (c-index = 0.76) and the calibration was good, with a χ2 of 9.4 in the Goodness of Fit (GOF) test (P = 0.313) [18]. However, the risk stratification in the PIM3 score is based on diagnoses according to the Australian and New Zealand Paediatric Intensive Care (ANZPIC) register, which includes more commonly encountered diseases in developed countries which may affect the validity in countries with different diagnostic trends [19–21].

Considering the use of PIM3 as a single-point-in-time objective predictor of mortality in PICU, our objective is to evaluate its potential integration into the triage system for patients that are referred from peripheral hospitals and admitted to a tertiary-level PICU in Indonesia. We first included the critically ill children who have been hospitalized to our PICU in order to assess the feasibility of applying this score in the pre-referral setting and thus aiding clinical decision making and prioritization for critically ill children who need PICU admission in the Indonesian setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective observational cohort study conducted in the PICU at DR Sardjito General Hospital, Yogyakarta, Indonesia. Our hospital is a tertiary public university-based hospital in Yogyakarta Province, Indonesia which serves approximately 4000 pediatric inpatients and provides Emergency Department (ED) services to an average of 2000 pediatric patients annually. Our PICU is equipped with 16 medical and surgical intensive care unit beds and has approximately 900 admissions annually. More than half of our patients are referral patients from peripheral hospitals.

Patients and operational definition

We enrolled all paediatric patients from 1 month to 18-year-old who were transferred from other lower-level hospitals as emergency referrals requiring tertiary PICU and admitted to our PICU from August 1 st, 2022 to January 31 st, 2023. We defined PICU mortality as the occurrence of patient deaths during PICU admission. We defined hospital mortality as the occurrence of patient deaths during the course of care within hospital.

Data collection

Data were collected from patient’s Electronic Medical record (EMR) using a structured case record form, done by the data collector (RAH). We recorded patient demographic data and PICU length of stay (LOS). PIM3 data were taken from 1-hour PIM3 score calculations after PICU admission. The PIM3 data includes 10 variables: systolic blood pressure (mmHg), pupillary reaction to light, PaO2 to FiO2 ratio, base excess (mmol/L), mechanical ventilation, elective admission to the Emergency Department (ED), recovery after surgical procedure, and diagnosis. The first recorded values in EMR of FiO2, BE, PaO2, blood pressure, pupillary reaction to light and the need for mechanical ventilation within the first hour after admission to the PICU were used.

The software SPSS 28 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 28) was used for all the statistical analyses. Continuous quantitative variables are expressed as median and minimum and maximum according to data distribution. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. We used chi-square or fisher exact test (if there was an expected data < 5 more than 20%) for categorical variable. We used the Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed continuous variables to determine differences between two populations.

We assessed the overall performance analysis of the score with the following analysis: Discrimination: The discriminative ability (to differentiate between survivors and non survivors) of PIM3 scoring system was analyzed with the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (AUC − ROC) curve and its 95% confidence interval (CI), which provides the discriminatory performance of the score. An area under the ROC curve above 0.70 was considered to be acceptable discriminatory power, whereas a value of > 0.8 was regarded as good discrimination and > 0.9 as excellent [17].

Calibration: The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit (HL-GOF) test was used to assess the calibration of the score, which reflects the compatibility of expected and observed outcomes in different groups. This HL-GOF was assessed by dividing the patients into 10 equal groups (in patient numbers) according to their predicted mortality risk. Decile cutoffs were chosen based on the distribution of each of the scores in the cohort. The chi-square statistic (∑ [O-E]2/E, where O is the observed and E the expected number of deaths in each group of risk) was calculated as a summary indicator of calibration in each interval of risk and then a p value was calculated. A higher chi-square value means greater discrepancy between the observed and predicted risks of death. A p value greater than 0.05 was accepted to indicate good calibration [18].

Standardized mortality ratio (SMR): SMR is a parameter that compares the observed number of deaths (O) with the expected number of deaths (E) derived from a specific scoring system in a specific period and it is typically employed to assess the overall quality of PICUs. It is calculated by dividing the observed number of deaths to the number of expected deaths in a study population (SMR: O/E). An SMR greater than 1.0 indicates that the scoring system underestimates the mortality rate whereas an SMR less than 1.0 is suggestive of an overprediction of mortality. An SMR of 1.0 indicates a perfect agreement between observed and estimated mortality. If the upper 95% CI of the SMR was < 1.0 then mortality was regarded as being lower than predicted, and conversely, confidence intervals greater than 1.0 signify higher mortality [19, 20].

Ethical consideration

Ethic approval was obtained from the Medical and Health Research Ethic Committee (MHREC) with the Ref No: KE/FK/1242/EC/2023. Confidentiality and privacy were strictly maintained. Only the principal investigator and the data collector had access to the data. All methods throughout the study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Patients characteristics

In total 222 critically ill children were referred from peripheral hospitals and admitted to the PICU at DR. Sardjito General Hospital during the study period. Two cases were excluded from the analysis, one case because the patient was transferred to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) during treatment, and the other because the patient was older than 18 years on admission. Of the remaining 220 patients 49 (22.1%) died during PICU admission, of whom 17 within the first 24 h after PICU admission. In addition, 6 patients died in the ward after being discharged from PICU. The flowchart of the study’s subject is shown in Fig. 1. The overall PICU mortality within the study period was 103/428 (23.5%) of PICU admissions and the referral patients account for 51.4% of all our PICU patients. The most common pre-referral emergency diagnosis was respiratory distress/failure (85/220 (38.6%)) and respiratory system disease (50/220 (22.8%)) was the most common diagnosis group (Table 1).

Discrimination of scores for the predictive outcome of patient mortality

The discrimination of PIM3 based on the ROC curve (Fig. 2) is considered good with an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI 0.77–0.91; p < 0.001). The ideal Youden index score for PIM3 is obtained as 3.329. With this cut-off the sensitivity and specificity of the PIM3 score are 81.6% and 76.6% respectively, with a relative risk (RR) of 7.8 (95% CI 3.984–8.316; p < 0.001) (Table 3).This means that subjects with a PIM3 score higher than 3.3 have a 7.8 times greater risk of mortality compared to subjects with a score lower than 3.3.

Calibration performance of PIM-3 scoring systems

Based on the HL-GOF results by PIM3 the predicted mortality rate was 22 patients, but the observed death rate was 49 patients, resulting in an overall SMR score of 2.2. Fig 3 illustrates the calibration of observed death and expected death based on PIM3 decile scores. In all (except the 1 st and 5th deciles) the actual mortality was higher than expected based on the PIM3 score. The SMR values in the decile increase indicate an underprediction of the PIM3 scoring system, with the highest SMR of 16.3 in the 4th decile. The HL-GOF test yielded a chi-square statistic of 154.31 (p < 0.001), indicating significant discrepancy between the predicted scores of the PIM3 model and the actual observed death outcomes in this study.

Discussion

We found that PIM3 was unfitted as mortality predictor in critically ill children who were referred form lower level hospital and admitted to our PICU. Although the PIM3 scoring system had a good discriminative ability (to differentiate between survivors and non survivors), the calibration indicates that it tends to underestimate the risk of mortality. The PIM score was developed for benchmarking and not prediction of mortality in individual patients [12–17]. However, as there is an urgent need for a scoring system that can be done in referring hospitals in LMIC, we hypothesized that PIM3 with its advantages could help in triaging patients to the scarce available PICU beds in Indonesia.

The reason for this underestimating mortality might be caused by several factors such as differences in patient characteristics and variations in healthcare system standards, including different resources, demographic and geographic conditions, disease patterns, and prevalent pathogens during a specific period. Our population may have a higher risk for worse mortality outcomes. The majority of disease diagnoses in our study did not fit into the diagnostic categories in the PIM3 score. There is no classification of the diagnoses we frequently encounter and are clinically severe such as severe pneumonia, dengue shock syndrome, gastroenteritis with hypovolemic shock, meningo-encephalitis tuberculosis, multiple trauma and acute kidney injury.

We experience many difficulties in daily practice if we only rely on the use of a priority system. The way we communicate with referring hospitals is by using an Information and Technology (IT)-based integrated health service delivery information system [2], which does not yet accommodate direct communication. Much of the data we need to classify patients into groups based on the priority system is not available in the referral statements. Mortality risk prediction models in intensive care which have been developed in many high-income countries are not widely used in our setting, particularly in peripheral health services. Apart from this, although simple, one of the components of PIM3 is the blood gas analysis examination, which is one of the examinations that is quite expensive and not widely available in referring hospitals in Indonesia.

We found that the PIM3 score system underestimates its role as a predictor of mortality in this study population. Comparable results were obtained in a multicenter study in South Africa that found an SMR of 1.28 [21]. In contrast in a study conducted in Dubai, with an SMR of 0.53 (p-value < 0.001), indicated that the PIM3 scoring model overestimated mortality [22]. Yet the studies may not be comparable as population characteristics differ between developed and developing countries [20, 23].

The data from this study revealed that pupillary response, PaO2/FiO2, and the need of mechanical ventilator support in the first hour of PICU admission showed significant differences between the surviving and non-surviving groups. In the paediatric population, cardiac arrest is most frequently preceded by respiratory issues and airway obstruction, unlike adults, where heart-related problems are the primary cause [24]. Pupillary conditions can be affected by prolonged hypoxia, an oxygen deficiency condition [25]. These findings emphasise the importance of early detection of respiratory emergencies, initial management, and monitoring of every child with respiratory system problems in pre-referral settings to prevent the patients from deteriorating. Simple respiratory variables such as SpO2/FiO2 should be the an important parameter in developing triage scores in future studies.

Given the limited capacity of the PICU beds, it is necessary to establish objective criteria that can serve as recommendations for prioritizing the admission of certain patients to the PICU and to be conducted at the referring hospital. There is a need to develop a specific scoring system tailored to the population in Indonesia. An established prioritization system and scores derived from anamnesis, physical examination, and basic laboratory tests can be utilized in a pre-referral setting.

This study is the first to analyse the performance of the PIM3 score on the population of critically ill children referred to the PICU in a LMIC setting. There are limitations in this study. The findings may not be generalizable to all PICUs in Indonesia, as the study was conducted at a single center over a relatively short period with a limited sample size. We did not involve patients from other intensive care units such as Surgical ICU or ICU for cardiac surgery in the study.

Conclusion

The discriminative ability of the PIM3 scoring system in predicting mortality outcomes in a group of critically ill children who were referred and admitted to the tertiary PICU at DR. Sardjito General Hospital is good. However, the calibration is poor. PIM3 was unfitted as a mortality predictor in this study population.
