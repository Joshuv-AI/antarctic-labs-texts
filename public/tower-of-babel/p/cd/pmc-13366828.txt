Ultrasound vessel sizing before cannulation: a measurement-first approach for anesthesiologists
Authors: Luis Alberto Rodriguez Linares, Sarah Cerillo Lopes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The catheter gauge and length are often chosen based on visual estimation. Ultrasound allows for the direct measurement of vessel diameter and depth, which may reduce failed attempts, particularly in patients with difficult access and pediatric patients. This narrative review evaluated trials, meta-analyses, and guidelines on ultrasound-guided vascular access in adults and children to derive practical sizing rules for venous and arterial cannulation in anesthesiology. Key recommendations include targeting a catheter-to-vein ratio (CVR) ≤ 0.45 for peripheral IVs and using long catheters for deeper veins (≥ 8–10 mm); limiting catheter size to ≤ 1/3 of the vessel’s luminal area (outer diameter/inner diameter ratio ≤ 0.577) for arterial lines to prevent spasm. A pediatric reference table links typical radial artery diameters to appropriate 22–24G catheter choices. Selecting central venous catheters that occupy approximately ≤ 33% of the vein’s diameter may be a reasonable strategy to minimize complications. Evidence from clinical trials supports US-guided peripheral IV and radial artery cannulation over landmark-based techniques. We propose a five-step workflow (scan, measure, select gauge/length, US-guided cannulation, and confirmation) to implement this measurement-first approach. In conclusion, an ultrasound measurement-first strategy aligns catheter selection with vessel dimensions, improving first-pass success and reducing complications in both adult and pediatric practices. The proposed sizing tables and algorithm are intended to serve as practical decision-support tools for bedside applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44158-026-00399-3.

Introduction

Vascular access is central to perioperative and critical care, yet decisions on catheter gauge and length are often based on visual inspection or palpation of vessels despite wide anatomical variability. Bedside ultrasonography (US) enables direct inner-to-inner lumen diameter measurement, depth quantification, and dynamic assessment of vessel patency and collapsibility before puncture. Vein depth and diameter predict the likelihood a vein is palpable or visible and correlate with cannulation difficulty [1]. Furthermore, US guidance improves outcomes for peripheral IV (PIV) placement and radial arterial cannulation – including in pediatric patients – compared to landmark techniques [2–5]. Contemporary multidisciplinary guidance recommends routine US vessel sizing and real-time US guidance for venous and arterial access in both adults and children [6, 7].

However, translation of ultrasound measurements into specific device selection remains poorly standardized. We therefore propose a “measurement-first” strategy based on pre-puncture ultrasound measurement of vessel diameter and depth before venous or arterial cannulation and applying practical size thresholds (e.g. PIV target CVR ≤ 0.45 [8, 9]; arterial line occupying ≤ 1/3 of arterial cross-sectional area [3, 6, 7]) alongside a stepwise implementation algorithm. This approach is especially critical in high-risk and difficult IV access (DIVA) scenarios, where oversized catheters are associated with higher failure and thrombosis rates [8, 9]. In this narrative review, we compiled recent evidence to support the measurement-first approach. We also provide practical bedside sizing tables intended as decision-support aids for PIVs, including a pediatric sizing reference, to encourage adoption in clinical practice.

Methods

This practice-focused narrative review was prepared in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) guidelines for narrative reviews and the PRISMA-S extension for search reporting. No review protocol was registered. No patient-level data were used, and ethics committee approval was not required.

This work is a structured narrative review with a practice-oriented focus. Although a systematic-style literature search was performed across multiple databases with predefined eligibility criteria and transparent reporting of search methods, the objective of this study was not to conduct a formal systematic review. Instead, the identified literature was qualitatively synthesized to derive clinically practical recommendations for ultrasound-based vessel measurement and catheter selection. The present work should be interpreted as a structured narrative review that integrates available evidence to support a pragmatic, measurement-first approach to vascular access.

Eligibility criteria

Population: Adults and children undergoing peripheral IV (PIV) placement, central venous catheter (CVC) placement, or arterial cannulation. Intervention/Exposure: Pre-puncture ultrasound assessment of vessel inner diameter and depth, combined with real-time ultrasound-guided cannulation. Comparators: Traditional landmark/palpation techniques or alternative guidance methods (e.g. Doppler), as well as comparisons of different catheter gauges/lengths. Outcomes: First-attempt success, number of attempts, time to successful cannulation, and complications (e.g. hematoma, inadvertent arterial puncture, vasospasm or occlusion, catheter failure). We also noted any reported associations between CVR and failure rates and the diagnostic utility of ultrasound measurements (depth/diameter) in predicting success. Design: Included sources were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews/meta-analyses, observational studies, clinical guidelines/consensus statements, and relevant technical standards. Exclusion: We excluded animal or ex vivo studies, case reports/series lacking generalizable methods, studies on non-US guidance (e.g. near-infrared or landmark only techniques) for vascular access, surgical graft literature, and studies that did not report outcomes relevant to our objective.

Information sources and search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of multiple databases and platforms: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase (Ovid interface), PubMed Central (PMC), and select publisher websites (including British Journal of Anaesthesia, Anaesthesia, Journal of the American Society of Echocardiography, PLOS One, and Critical Care). Searches were limited to publications from January 1, 2013 through October 8, 2025 (final search update on October 8, 2025) and to English, Spanish, or Portuguese language results. The search strategies combined MeSH/Emtree terms and text keywords related to ultrasonography, venipuncture/peripheral catheterization, central venous catheters, arterial (radial) lines, catheter-to-vein ratio, difficult intravenous access (DIVA), pediatrics. Full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Manufacturer documentation was consulted only to obtain standard catheter outer-diameter (OD) specifications. Use of AI assistance: Claude (Anthropic) was used only to assist with language polishing and formatting; all content, data interpretations, and conclusions were formulated and verified by the author, who takes full responsibility for the manuscript.

Study selection and data handling

One reviewer performed the primary screening of search results, including de-duplication of records and initial screening of titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant studies then underwent full-text assessment. A second reviewer independently verified inclusion of relevant full-text articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. We also hand-searched the reference lists of included studies and pertinent guidelines to identify any additional relevant sources. The study selection process is depicted in a PRISMA-style flow diagram (Supplementary Material 1).

The literature search was designed to be comprehensive across major databases in order to capture studies addressing ultrasound-based vessel measurement and catheter selection for vascular access. Despite this broad strategy, the number of eligible studies was relatively limited. This reflects the still-developing evidence base specifically examining quantitative relationships between ultrasound-measured vessel size and catheter sizing rather than a restrictive search approach. All identified studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were included in the qualitative synthesis to ensure transparency and completeness.

Data extraction and synthesis

Key quantitative relationships between vessel measurements and outcomes or recommended thresholds were extracted. For example, the recommended PIV CVR ≤ 0.45 was derived from clinical studies linking higher CVR to increased failure rates [8, 9]. The arterial “ ≤ 1/3 area” rule (outer-diameter to inner-diameter ratio ≤ √1/3 ≈ 0.577) was drawn from pediatric and adult literature and guidelines [3, 6, 7], and we used this principle to calculate minimum arterial diameters suitable for each catheter gauge. Typical pediatric radial artery diameters were obtained from ultrasound studies [10, 11], and standard catheter ODs from manufacturer specs [12]. We constructed tables summarizing the pediatric radial artery sizing recommendations (Table 1), adult CVC sizing guidance (Table 2), and a summary of evidence including key effect sizes favoring US guidance (Table 3). We also developed a five-step clinical algorithm for implementation, which was iteratively refined against the gathered evidence and expert guidelines. Synthesis and appraisal

Findings were synthesized qualitatively (no meta-analysis or quantitative pooling was performed). We did not formally assess risk of bias of individual studies due to the narrative scope, but we note limitations where relevant and base our recommendations on the most robust available evidence (e.g. RCTs and high-quality cohort studies) and guideline consensus. We paid special attention to differences by patient age (pediatric vs adult populations) and clinical context (e.g. anticipated DIVA or greater vein depth). All data supporting the statements in this review are drawn from previously published studies and are cited accordingly. No new datasets were generated for this review. (The detailed search strategies can be made available on request and will be archived on an open repository such as searchRxiv upon publication acceptance.)

Results

A total of 15 sources met inclusion criteria, comprising clinical guidelines/consensus statements (n = 2), systematic reviews or meta-analyses (n = 3), RCTs (n = 2), observational studies (n = 7), and one manufacturer specification document.

Peripheral IV (PIV) access

Ultrasound guidance vs landmark technique

Ultrasound-guided PIV placement significantly increases first-pass success and reduces the number of attempts and time to cannulation compared to landmark-based cannulation, particularly in patients with DIVA [2]. Current evidence strongly supports using US guidance as the default for challenging PIV placements.

Measurement-driven catheter selection

Available evidence suggests that targeting a catheter-to-vein ratio ≤ 0.45 may reduce catheter failure. Higher CVR values (i.e. using a catheter nearly as large as the vein) have been associated with increased likelihood of insertion failure and early catheter failure [8, 9]. Additionally, the use of long peripheral catheters for deeper veins (≥ 8–10 mm below the skin) is advised, as greater catheter length within the vessel lumen reduces the risk of dislodgement and early infiltration/extravasation [8, 9].

For example, a vein of approximately 2.0 mm inner diameter can comfortably accommodate up to a 22G catheter (which has an OD of ~ 0.9 mm). A ~ 2.5 mm vein can accept up to a 20G catheter (~ 1.1 mm OD). Very large veins (≥ 4.7 mm) could theoretically allow a 14G, though such large-bore PIVs should be used only when absolutely necessary. Importantly, for any vein that lies ≥ 8–10 mm deep beneath the skin, a longer catheter (such as a long peripheral IV or midline catheter) should be chosen to ensure adequate intraluminal length.

Central venous catheters – adults

Optimal sizing relative to vein diameter

For central lines, targeting a catheter occupancy of approximately one-third of the vein’s diameter may represent a reasonable approach, with occupancy above ~ 45% best avoided. In practice, this corresponds to selecting a French size such that the vein’s internal diameter is at least 3 times the catheter’s outer diameter (see Table 2). For example, a 7 French triple-lumen CVC (approximately 2.3 mm OD) is ideally placed in a vein ~ 7.0 mm or greater in diameter; 7.0 mm diameter corresponds to ~ 33% occupancy by the catheter. The upper limit would be a vein around 5.1 mm (45% occupancy), below which a smaller catheter would generally be preferable. Thus, if the internal jugular vein (IJV) measures only ~ 5 mm, a 7 Fr line would be too large (> 45%); one might opt for a 5 Fr or 6 Fr line in such a case. Ultrasound measurement of the IJV or subclavian/axillary vein prior to line placement can guide appropriate French size selection.

Impact of US guidance and technique

Real-time ultrasound guidance is the standard of care for IJV cannulation and has been shown to reduce failed attempts and mechanical complications compared to landmark techniques [13]. For subclavian vein access, many operators now visualize the vein via the infraclavicular axillary vein approach under US, rather than a true blind subclavian stick. Pre-measuring the vein’s diameter via US at the intended puncture site can help in choosing between catheter sizes (e.g. deciding between a 7 Fr vs 8.5 Fr introducer for a large-bore central line) [7, 13].

Arterial lines (Adults & Pediatrics)

Efficacy of ultrasound guidance

Dynamic US guidance improves arterial cannulation success as well. In adults, ultrasound guidance (especially using real-time needle tip tracking techniques) significantly improves first-pass success for radial artery lines, particularly in older patients with difficult-to-palpate pulses [4]. In pediatrics, both meta-analyses and RCTs have shown higher success rates and fewer complications for US-guided arterial cannulation (typically radial artery) compared to palpation or Doppler guidance [3, 5]. Accordingly, recent guidelines endorse using ultrasound both to assess small pediatric arteries and to guide the needle for arterial line placement in children [6, 7].

Catheter size and vasospasm risk

To minimize the risk of arterial spasm or occlusion, we apply the “one-third rule” for catheter size in arteries: limiting the catheter’s outer diameter to approximately one-third of the arterial inner diameter may help reduce the risk of vasospasm and occlusion (equivalently, catheter cross-sectional area ≤ 1/3 of the lumen area, which is OD/ID ≤ 0.577) [3, 6, 7]. In adults, a typical radial artery 2–3 mm in diameter can accommodate a 20G catheter (~ 1.1 mm OD) without issue; larger catheters (18G) may cause spasm if the artery is small. In infants with tiny arteries ~ 1–1.5 mm, a 24G catheter is often required; even a 22G may be too large. If the radial artery is under the size threshold for a 22G, one should either use a 24G or consider an alternative site (e.g. the ulnar artery, which can sometimes be larger and more amenable to cannulation) [14].

Pediatric radial artery sizing guidelines (Table 1)

Based on available ultrasound data on radial artery diameters by age [10, 11], we can recommend the following: For neonates and young infants, use a 24G arterial catheter if the artery is ≥ 1.23 mm (diameter), since a 22G is usually too large for this age group. In preschool-aged children, a 22G catheter is typical; a 20G should only be attempted if the artery is at least ~ 1.9 mm. By adolescence, radial artery size approaches adult dimensions – a 22G remains the standard in most cases, but a 20G can be used in larger adolescents if the artery’s size allows and it is clinically necessary (e.g. high-demand situations). These guidelines emphasize measuring the child’s artery with ultrasound to inform the catheter choice, rather than relying purely on age or weight.

Measurement principles at the bedside

When implementing a measurement-first approach, clinicians should follow standardized ultrasound measurement techniques:Vessel diameter (planes and calipers): Measure the internal diameter of the target vessel in a short-axis (transverse) B-mode view. If the vessel appears elliptical rather than perfectly round, measure two perpendicular diameters (D1 and D2) and calculate the average to estimate the true lumen size. Confirm vessel patency and identify a straight segment with no proximal occlusion or valve that would impede cannulation.Measure vessel size: Measure the vessel’s inner diameter (and depth from skin). If needed, measure two axes for elliptical veins to estimate the true size. Note the depth to decide on catheter length.Select an appropriate catheter: Choose the catheter gauge and length based on the measurement rules – for peripheral veins, ensure CVR ≤ 0.45; for arteries, ensure OD/ID ≤ 0.577 (catheter no more than ~ 1/3 of artery diameter); for CVCs, target ~ 33% vein occupancy (vein ≥ 3 × catheter OD) and avoid > 45%. If a vein is deeper than ~ 8–10 mm, opt for a long catheter or a midline. Discussion

Adopting a measurement-first, ultrasound-guided workflow has the potential to significantly improve vascular access outcomes, particularly in patients with difficult IV access and those requiring routine radial arterial lines. The evidence compiled in this review indicates higher first-pass success rates, fewer attempts, faster placement times, and reduced complication rates when ultrasound pre-measurement and real-time guidance are used compared to traditional landmark techniques [2–5, 13]. Importantly, tailoring the catheter selection (gauge and length) to the vessel’s actual size before attempting cannulation addresses one of the major avoidable causes of failure: using an inadequately sized device.

It is important to emphasize that the numerical thresholds presented throughout this review are evidence-informed practical approximations derived from heterogeneous data sources, including observational studies, randomized trials, and expert consensus, and should not be interpreted as fixed or universally applicable rules. They are intended to support clinical decision-making and should always be interpreted in light of individual patient factors and clinical judgement. The specific thresholds highlighted in this review (PIV CVR ≤ 0.45; arterial catheter OD ≤ ~ 1/3 of the lumen) are grounded in the best available evidence to date, but they should be applied in context. These guidelines likely represent general optimal practices that may require adjustment for specific clinical contexts. For instance, the ideal CVR for a given situation might vary; a slightly higher CVR might be tolerated in a temporary emergency access versus an elective situation, or in an adult versus a small child. Similarly, the pediatric arterial size cut-offs we suggest are based on limited data from small studies; clinical judgment should prevail if, for example, a slightly larger catheter is needed for an urgent line in a child; careful technique and vigilant monitoring for spasm are key [10, 11, 14]. We also note that vessel size can vary with patient physiology (e.g., vasodilation under general anesthesia or vasoconstriction when hypothermic). Repeated measurements can show biological variation that exceeds inter-observer measurement error [15], underscoring that real-time measurement is more useful than relying on age/size charts alone. Standardizing the technique (ensuring minimal probe pressure and consistent patient positioning) will improve the reliability of the measurements.

The widespread implementation of ultrasound vessel measurements will require training and practice. Clinicians must be adept not only at performing ultrasound-guided cannulation but also at obtaining accurate measurements and interpreting them to make decisions. Training programs and institutional protocols should incorporate these principles. While there may be an initial learning curve and slight time overhead to scan and measure, studies suggest that improved first-pass success and avoidance of multiple attempts ultimately save time and reduce resource utilization (as well as patient discomfort).

The numerical thresholds presented in this review should be interpreted as pragmatic suggestions derived from the available literature, rather than as definitive clinical guidelines. These thresholds are based on observational studies, randomized trials, and expert consensus, which consistently suggest improved outcomes when the catheter size is proportionate to the vessel diameter. However, given the narrative nature of this review and the heterogeneity of the underlying evidence, these values are intended to provide practical guidance to support clinical decision-making and ultrasound-based assessment rather than rigid rules applicable to all clinical situations.

Future research should validate and refine these size thresholds and workflows. Prospective trials comparing a measurement-first algorithm with standard care could quantify improvements in first-pass success, total attempts, insertion time, dwell time of catheters, and complication rates across various patient populations (especially DIVA patients and pediatric cases). Cost-effectiveness analyses would also be valuable; for instance, does the upfront time investment in ultrasound translate to enough reduction in escalations (such as central line placements due to failed IVs) to justify routine use? Additionally, some specific questions remain, such as the optimal intraluminal length for peripheral catheters in deep veins (to minimize failure) and how CVR thresholds might be adjusted in different clinical scenarios (e.g., trauma resuscitation vs. elective IV). The ongoing refinement of pediatric arterial guidelines by age group would also be beneficial as more data emerge.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, it does not apply formal systematic review methodology or risk-of-bias assessment to individual studies; the synthesis is inherently subject to selection bias. Second, the total number of eligible studies specifically addressing quantitative vessel-to-catheter sizing relationships was limited (n = 15), reflecting an early-stage and heterogeneous evidence base. Third, the sizing thresholds presented (CVR ≤ 0.45, OD/ID ≤ 0.577, CVC occupancy ≤ 33%) are derived from a mixture of observational, randomized, and consensus-based sources with varying populations, endpoints, and follow-up periods, and should therefore be interpreted as pragmatic approximations rather than definitive clinical cut-offs. Fourth, most evidence on pediatric radial artery sizing comes from small studies with limited age strata, introducing uncertainty around recommendations for neonates and infants in particular. Finally, vessel dimensions vary with physiological state (hydration, vasomotor tone, anesthetic depth), limiting the generalizability of any single pre-procedure measurement; real-time assessment at the time of cannulation is therefore essential.

Conclusions

Pre-puncture ultrasound assessment of vessel diameter and depth enables more precise selection of catheter gauge and length, fundamentally aligning the device choice with the patient’s unique anatomy. Embracing this measurement-first strategy may support improved first-attempt success and minimize complications by avoiding the common mismatch between catheter and vessel. This review highlights the potential value of incorporating routine ultrasound vessel sizing into anesthesiology practice, a field in which formal device sizing guidance remains underdeveloped.

In summary, the combination of ultrasound vessel measurement and real-time image-guided cannulation may offer a safer and more evidence-based approach to vascular access. By reducing failed attempts and catheter-related complications, a measurement-first strategy may promote more consistent and individualized practice for both adult and pediatric patients, aligned with evolving standards of care.
