The trifecta approach: a staged alternative to the extended iliofemoral approach for complex acetabular fractures—technique and case report
Authors: Navid Ziran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ilioinguinal, anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa), and Kocher-Langenbeck approaches are mainstays of acetabular fracture surgery. For select patterns involving the sciatic buttress or inferior sacroiliac joint, an extended iliofemoral approach is sometimes required but carries significant morbidity. This case report introduces a novel, staged combination of exposures—the “Trifecta Approach”—that is designed to provide staged circumferential access while potentially reducing soft-tissue morbidity. Clinical and radiographic outcomes suggest that this technique may serve as a viable alternative to the extended iliofemoral in appropriately selected patients.

1. Introduction

Acetabular fracture management has evolved considerably during the 20th century. Early and mid-century treatments were predominantly nonoperative with a shift to operative treatment in the latter half of the 20th century. Modern understanding of acetabular fractures was shaped most significantly by Judet,1 Letournel,2,3 and later Matta.4 Letournel's classification system and the ilioinguinal (II) and Kocher-Langenbeck (KL) approaches remain foundational to the treatment of these injuries.

Although the KL, II, and the anterior intrapelvic (AIP/modified Stoppa) approach introduced by Hirvensalo5 (later detailed by Cole and Bolhofner6) provide reliable access for most patterns, certain fracture configurations involving the sciatic buttress, inferior sacroiliac (SI) joint, or extensive displacement require broader visualization. For these cases, Letournel developed the extended iliofemoral (EIF) approach, which offers comprehensive exposure of both columns and the internal and external ilium.7 However, the EIF carries substantial morbidity, including wound complications, abductor weakness, infection, heterotopic ossification, devascularization, and reported rates of osteonecrosis and loss of reduction in published series such as Stöckle et al.8

In rare cases where visualization requirements exceed the capabilities of KL, II, or AIP exposures, but where the morbidity of an EIF is undesirable—particularly in the presence of extensive soft-tissue injury—the surgeon must consider alternative strategies.

This report describes a staged combination of familiar surgical exposures—the Trifecta Approach—designed to provide circumferential access while potentially reducing soft-tissue morbidity in select complex acetabular fractures.

2. Case report

The patient was a 41-year-old man who sustained a pelvic crush injury after being pinned by a forklift at work. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus and active methamphetamine use. Examination revealed a large Morel-Lavallée (ML) lesion extending from the right pelvic flank to the mid-thigh (Fig. 1) and a complete motor and sensory sciatic nerve palsy. Imaging demonstrated a right associated both column acetabular fracture with posterior wall marginal impaction (Figs. 2–4). The posterior column, including the sciatic buttress and inferior SI joint, was displaced medially into the true pelvis.

2.1. Stage 1: posterior exposure and reduction of sciatic buttress

With the patient prone on a Jackson flat-top table, skeletal fixation was applied to the contralateral left hemipelvis and subtrochanteric femur to assist with reduction (Fig. 5). Two incisions were marked for a posterior sacral approach and a KL approach (Fig. 6).

Photograph of the patient's prone position for the first surgical stage (stage 1) demonstrating table skeletal fixation (TSF) of the patient's contralateral normal left side to the table to facilitate reduction. A 5.0-mm Schanz screw is inserted in the left subtrochanteric femur before sterile prep and affixed to the table.

A‒C, Photograph of the patient's prone position demonstrating the KL incision and posterior approach to the sacral incision (A). B, The 5.0-mm Schanz screw inserted into the contralateral left sciatic buttress as adjunctive table skeletal fixation. C, An illustration to further illustrate the surgical incisions relative to the deep anatomy.

The posterior sacral approach exposed the inferior SI joint and the superior portion of the displaced sciatic buttress fragment. Through the KL approach, the inferior buttress, posterior column, and posterior wall were visualized, creating a dual-window construct: a supra-gluteal window (sacral exposure) and a trans-gluteal window (KL exposure). Intraoperatively, the gluteus maximus origin was traumatically avulsed from the external iliac fossa, and the subcutaneous tissues were extensively sheared—consistent with a large ML lesion.

The sciatic buttress fragment was reduced using Schanz screws as joysticks, placed superiorly through the sacral approach and inferiorly via the KL approach (Fig. 7). This dual-interval strategy allowed manipulation and fixation of the fragment without further gluteal stripping. The fragment, which articulated with the inferior SI joint, was reduced and fixed to the distal posterior column and proximal posterior iliac crest through the respective windows. Fixation screws were kept intentionally short to avoid conflict with subsequent anterior reduction and fixation.

A, B, A The joystick of the sciatic buttress fracture fragment via the posterior approach to the sacrum (“supra-gluteal” window). B, The joystick of the sciatic buttress fracture fragment via both the KL approach and the posterior sacral approach.

Through the KL approach, the joint surface was inspected and marginal impaction identified (Fig. 8). The impaction was dis-impacted, bone graft was placed, and the posterior wall fragment was stabilized with a spring plate. Fluoroscopic images confirming reduction and fixation of the sciatic buttress and posterior wall are shown in Figure 9. The wounds were closed, and a large vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) device was applied over the ML lesion.

Intraoperative photograph of the KL approach demonstrating the cartilage impaction of the right posterior wall.

A‒D, Intraoperative fluoroscopy images of the right acetabulum which demonstrate reduction and fixation of the right sciatic buttress, posterior column, and posterior wall acetabular fracture. Note the screws were kept short to prevent anterior column reduction and fixation for the subsequent surgery (stage 2).

2.2. Stage 2: anterior and posterior column fixation

Forty-eight hours later, the patient underwent anterior reduction and fixation through a combined ilioinguinal and AIP approach (Fig. 10). This exposure provided access to the internal iliac fossa, anterior column, pubis, and quadrilateral surface extending to the SI joint. The iliac wing was first reduced and fixed, followed by reduction of the anterior column to the stabilized posterior column.

Photograph of the right pelvis demonstrating the ilioinguinal approach with reduction forceps.

Fixation of the large iliac fracture was achieved with a contoured tension-band “snake” plate placed along the internal iliac fossa, curving over the iliac crest and ending lateral to the pelvic brim (Fig. 11). This construct, originally introduced to the author by Dr Joel Matta, was used to counteract deforming forces from the abductors and iliotibial band. These deforming forces can potentially compromise fixation of large iliac fragments with low iliac fossa fracture lines. This tension band plate can be used in isolation or in conjunction with screws between the inner and outer iliac tables.

A‒D. Illustration of an outlet view of the right hemipelvis which demonstrates the utilization of the tension band plate (“snake” plate) for large iliac wing fractures. Note the plate is placed on the tension-side of the fracture, in the internal iliac fossa, to prevent fracture displacement from the abductors. Depending on fracture line orientation, the top screw along the iliac crest may/may not traverse the fracture.

The medially displaced anterior and posterior columns were further stabilized with a quadrilateral surface plate and posterior column screws. A lag screw placed from the anterior inferior iliac spine toward the posterior inferior iliac spine further stabilized the sciatic buttress fragment to the anterior column, providing posterior column compression and rotational stability. A second wound VAC was applied over the ML region. Intraoperative fluoroscopic views from Stage 2 and postoperative radiographs are shown in Figures 12 and 13. The “snake” plate contoured in the iliac fossa is shown in Figure 13B (red line).

2.3. Postoperative recovery

From initial resuscitation through discharge, the patient received 15 units PRBCs, 7 units FFP, and 3 units platelets. After the second-stage procedure, his inpatient recovery was uneventful, with no return trips to the operating room. He remained non–weight-bearing for ∼4 months, and his wounds healed without complication; VAC dressings were discontinued at 10 to 12 weeks. He participated in neuromuscular electrical stimulation and physical therapy for the sciatic nerve palsy.

At 4 months postoperative, an AP pelvic radiograph demonstrated stable implant position and fracture alignment (Fig. 14).

Postoperative AP pelvis radiograph at 4 months.

At 6 months postoperative, he ambulated with a walker. Radiographs showed progressive consolidation and no post-traumatic arthritis. Neurologically, he had 0/5 peroneal motor function, 4/5 gastrocnemius-soleus strength, complete peroneal sensory loss, and partial tibial sensory return. The Harris Hip Score was 44, and the Merle d'Aubigné score was 11.

At 1 year postoperative, he remained walker-dependent but reported no pain and improved function. Radiographs again showed consolidation and stable implants (Fig. 15). He had persistent 0/5 peroneal nerve motor function, improving peroneal sensation, and full tibial nerve recovery (5/5 gastrocnemius-soleus, normal tibial sensation). The Harris Hip Score was 51, and the Merle d'Aubigné score was 14 (Fig. 16).

A–C, Postoperative AP pelvis and Judet views at 1 year postoperatively.

A–C, Photographs of the patient's right hip and pelvic incisions at 1 year postoperatively.

At 1.5 years postoperative, he was ambulating with a walker without pain. Radiographs confirmed a healed fracture with no change in implant position and no hip arthritis (Fig. 17). Neurologically, lesser toe extension strength improved to 4/5, tibialis anterior remained 0/5, superficial peroneal sensation was nearly normal, deep peroneal sensation reduced, and tibial nerve motor/sensory function remained fully recovered. The Harris Hip Score was 83, and the Merle d'Aubigné score was 16.

A–C. Postoperative AP pelvis and Judet views at 1.5 years postoperatively.

At 2 years postoperative, the patient was ambulating without an assistive device except for long distances for which he used a cane. An AP pelvis confirmed no change in superior joint space from previous radiographs (Fig. 18). Neurologically, lesser toe extension motor strength was 4/5, and tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus improved to 1/5. Sensation was unchanged from the previous exam. The Harris Hip Score was 89, and the Merle d'Aubigné was 17.

3. Discussion

This patient sustained a complex acetabular fracture involving the sciatic buttress and inferior SI joint—an injury pattern typically managed with the Extended Iliofemoral (EIF) approach. However, the presence of a significant Morel–Lavallée (ML) lesion and extensive soft-tissue trauma made the morbidity of an EIF—including additional muscular devascularization, infection, and heterotopic ossification—unacceptable. A staged combination of surgical intervals was therefore used to obtain the necessary osseous access while minimizing soft tissue stripping.

A central goal was anatomic reduction and stable fixation of the sciatic buttress fragment, which was medially displaced into the pelvis and incorporated the inferior SI joint. Restoring this fragment is essential for posterior column alignment and pelvic stability. Two complementary exposures provided access to this fragment: a supra-gluteal interval through a posterior sacral approach for the superior portion, and a trans-gluteal interval through the KL approach for its inferior aspect. Using Schanz screws as joysticks through both windows allowed controlled manipulation and fixation without the circumferential dissection required in an EIF.

The severity of soft-tissue injury also influenced the strategy. The gluteus maximus origin was traumatically avulsed, and the ML lesion produced extensive subcutaneous and fascial shearing. Because this region is highly dependent on gluteal and iliac vascular perforators, soft-tissue preservation was prioritized to minimize postoperative complications.

A focused review of pelvic vascular anatomy helps explain the rationale for the Trifecta Approach. The internal iliac artery divides into anterior and posterior trunks; the posterior division gives rise to the superior and inferior gluteal arteries supplying the gluteus maximus, medius, and posterior column. The superior gluteal artery (SGA) and nerve exit the greater sciatic notch above the piriformis and are particularly vulnerable during extended posterior dissection. If injured and retracted into the pelvis, hemostasis typically requires packing; the author has anecdotally found allogenic platelet-soaked laparotomy sponges effective as biologic and mechanical tamponade.

The external ilium receives blood from several sources: the SGA to the posterior iliac crest and gluteal fossa; the inferior gluteal artery to the inferolateral ilium; the ascending branch of the lateral femoral circumflex artery (a-LFCA) to the anterior crest and gluteus medius/TFL; and the deep circumflex iliac artery to the anterolateral ilium. The internal iliac fossa is supplied by the iliolumbar, obturator, and deep circumflex iliac artery branches. The acetabulum is perfused by the obturator, SGA, inferior gluteal artery, and medial femoral circumflex arteries. Devascularization of these territories—particularly during an EIF—can lead to osteonecrosis, gluteal muscle necrosis, and heterotopic ossification.

In this case, soft tissue preservation was particularly critical. The origins of the gluteus maximus and medius on the outer ilium and posterior column are supplied by SGA branches and associated periosteal perforators. Dissecting these muscles during an EIF—or disrupting them in the setting of traumatic avulsion—can lead to devascularization of the outer iliac table and compromise of gluteal viability. In addition, the a-LFCA, which supplies the anterior gluteus medius, tensor fascia lata, and anterior crest, is at risk during subperiosteal stripping of the lateral ilium. These vessels are frequently disrupted during circumferential exposure through EIF.

By contrast, the Trifecta Approach preserves these critical vascular structures through staged, interval-specific exposure. In this case, only limited elevation of the gluteus maximus origin from the multifidus and posterior iliac crest was performed, avoiding full detachment from the external ilium. This surgical strategy maintained perfusion to the gluteal musculature and lateral ilium, while still permitting access to the superior sciatic buttress and posterior column.

For access to the sciatic buttress, dissection was confined to the outer aspect of the greater sciatic notch via a posterior sacral window. The inferior portion of the same fragment was addressed separately through the KL approach, eliminating the need for posterior circumferential exposure. If present, iliac fractures can be treated through the lateral ilioinguinal (II) window, minimizing posterior muscle disruption and preserving vascular input from the internal iliac system.

Although table skeletal fixation9 was used in this case, in hindsight, it was not necessary. The author typically reserves this technique for cases involving cranially displaced pelvic fractures or pelvic malunions, where mechanical traction with the patient fixed to the table significantly facilitates reduction and alignment.

Although the described approach was termed “Trifecta,” it could be considered a “Quadrifecta,” as some may view the AIP approach as distinct from the lateral and middle windows of the ilioinguinal (II) approach. The full II uses the lateral, middle, and medial windows. The medial window and the AIP both offer access to the space of Retzius but have different utilizations. In practice, the author routinely uses both: the medial window of the II—by incising the conjoint tendon to access the space of Retzius—and the AIP via a vertical rectus abdominis split. Each offers unique advantages depending on the surgical objective.

For instance, when placing a pectineal brim plate and only superior pubis exposure is needed for screw placement into the pubis, the medial II window is ideal due to its simplicity, minimal soft tissue dissection, and direct visualization of the pubis and plate. Conversely, when exposure of the quadrilateral surface (QS) and inner posterior column (PC) is necessary to facilitate reduction and implant placement—as in the current case—the AIP is more appropriate. For some fracture patterns and/or patients with poor bone stock, the AIP with subsequent utilization of a buttress plate on the quadrilateral surface and inner posterior column can be beneficial. For other fracture patterns of the QS/PC, an II approach with PC screws and/or an “outside-in” supra-acetabular screw skewering the QS/PC is beneficial. The author uses both techniques depending on the fracture lines and the patient's bone stock. When performing the AIP, care must be taken to avoid injury to the femoral vein during excessive upward retraction. In this case, despite involving both the AIP and the II approaches, the author retains the term “Trifecta,” as the AIP was performed as part of the broader II exposure.

The Trifecta Approach offers a practical, anatomy-based alternative to the extended iliofemoral (EIF) for rare fracture patterns involving the sciatic buttress and inferior sacroiliac (SI) joint. In this case, it allowed for staged, targeted exposure of the posterior column, quadrilateral surface, and internal iliac fossa without the need for circumferential dissection. By separating posterior and anterior work into 2 distinct stages and minimizing muscular stripping, this technique reduced the overall soft tissue burden while still achieving anatomic reduction and implant fixation.

A key strength of this approach is its modular design: exposure can be expanded or limited based on fracture morphology, soft tissue viability, and surgical goals. Each interval—posterior sacral, KL, ilioinguinal, or AIP—can be used independently or in combination. In addition, the technique avoids the need to detach the abductors or perform a trochanteric osteotomy, thus preserving muscle function and blood supply to the acetabulum and hip.

However, the approach does have limitations. First, it requires repositioning and multiple incisions, which may prolong operative time. Second, it demands surgeon familiarity with the 3 pelvic/acetabular windows described as well as careful coordination between operative stages. If the surgeon is not comfortable with these approaches, then consideration should be given to alternatives the surgeon is more comfortable with (i.e., EIF). Third, while the exposures potentially avoid many complications associated with the EIF, they still carry risks of neurovascular injury, especially in the setting of prior trauma or distorted anatomy.

Combined and staged approaches for complex acetabular and pelvic injuries are well described in the literature. Surgeons have long used sequential or hybrid exposures to balance visualization with soft tissue preservation. The Trifecta Approach builds on this principle by combining 3 established windows in a staged manner tailored to fracture morphology. Rather than replacing extensile approaches, this strategy represents an additional tool that may be considered in selected cases when surgeon experience and fracture characteristics permit. Further study is needed to define the indications, potential complications, reproducibility, and long-term outcomes of acetabular fractures involving the sciatic buttress repaired using the Trifecta Approach compared with traditional extensile approaches.
