Breast cancer awareness and its predictors among university students and employees in Asir region, Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Umme Hani, Mutha Manzoor, Wajan Khalid Alqathanin, Reema Saeed Alghaseb, Saja Mohammed Al Asmari, Raghad Mushabab Al ahmari, Jonaid Ahmad Malik, Nazima Haider, Bilal Ahmad Para, Ali H Alamri, Adel Al Fatease
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) is the most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in Saudi Arabia. However, this malignancy can be tackled effectively with early detection, resulting in better patient outcomes and reduced mortality rates. Breast screening, whether self-screening or clinical screening, plays a pivotal role in early BC detection. Hence, understanding, knowledge, and perception of BC and its risk factors are very important in early BC diagnosis. The current study has been conducted to assess the understanding, knowledge, perception, and attitude of educated population of Asir region towards BC and its risk factors. The study was conducted among the participants aged 18 or above in various institutions of the Asir region in KSA using an online pre-validated questionnaire to assess the understanding, knowledge, and perception of BC and its risk factors. The study was completed by a total of 979 participants. The proportion of participants who demonstrated adequate knowledge and positive perception regarding the role of breast self-examination and early detection was greater than 50%, indicating an overall satisfactory level of breast cancer awareness among the study population. However, in identifying the risk factors of BC, < 50% of participants correctly identified the 8 correct risk factors out of 12. When analyzing the association of BC awareness with demographic characteristics, the results revealed that females had 2.012 times higher odds of BC awareness than males. Additionally, participants with higher education qualifications had 3.867 times higher odds of BC awareness than those with bachelor’s degrees. Also, the results show that demographic factors like female sex, age, marital status, urban residence, occupation (administrative staff), and higher education level were associated with the level of knowledge about breast cancer risk factors. Participants demonstrated generally good knowledge and perception of breast cancer, particularly regarding early detection and available screening tools. However, awareness of breast cancer risk factors was notably limited. Therefore, there is a clear need for targeted and region-specific awareness programs to enhance understanding and promote early intervention.Keywords: Breast cancer, Risk factors, Breast self examination (BSE), Clinical breast examination (CBE), MammographySubject terms: Neuroscience, Chemistry

Introduction

In 2020, breast cancer overtook lung cancer as the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide. It ranks as the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with approximately 2.3 million new cases accounting for 11.7 percent of all cancers and around 685,000 deaths globally1. Breast cancer is responsible for one in six cancer deaths and one in four cancer diagnoses among women, making it the most prevalent cancer in 159 out of 185 countries and the leading cause of female cancer mortality in 110 countries. Its incidence is significantly higher, by 88 percent, in high-income nations (55.9 per 100,000) compared to countries still undergoing development (29.7 per 100,000), with the highest rates observed in Australia and New Zealand, Western and Northern Europe, and North America. Belgium reports the highest incidence globally. Conversely, the lowest incidence rates occur in regions like Central America, Eastern and Middle Africa, and South Central Asia. Despite lower incidence, mortality is 17 percent higher in developing nations (15.0 vs. 12.8 per 100,000), with the highest death rates reported in Melanesia, Western Africa, Micronesia and Polynesia, and the Caribbean, with Barbados topping the list. In Saudi Arabia, breast cancer is the leading cancer among women, both in terms of incidence and mortality1.

Risk factors like female sex, age, late age of menopause, hormonal contraceptive methods, postmenopausal hormone therapy, positive family history, genetics, breast implant, benign breast disorders, nipple discharge, nipple retraction, dimpling, obesity, overweight, alcohol consumption, active smoking especially among postmenopausal and prenatal women, high-fat diet, less physical activity, air pollution, night work, diabetes, and exposure to radiations are the most significant and easily self-identifiable risk factors2. Breast awareness, or the familiarity of women with their breasts, abetted with sufficient knowledge and understanding of BC risk factors, is considered an effective tool for early detection of BC3. Early diagnosis of breast cancer leads to better treatment before the onset of metastasis, thus resulting in significant patient management outcomes. Seeking medical care initially during the onset of this cancer, subjects need to have the right knowledge about BC risk factors and perform regular Breast self-examinations (BSE). A positive attitude towards BC screening is also necessary to ensure participation in future population-based screening programs. Thus, imparting education about BC, associated risk factors, and BSE practices among the masses, especially females, and addressing the barriers to diagnosis and treatment of BC can play a pivotal role in tackling this fatal cancer.

In clinical settings, clinical breast examination (CBE), mammography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are significantly reliable screening methods for BC detection, with mammography being the most effective diagnostic tool associated with reduced mortality due to BC. However, due to its costly nature and the requirement of specialized technical expertise, mammography is not a suitable option for low-income countries. BSE in such settings holds a great promise in early detection. BSE as a BC screening tool is currently under many research trials and it has been shown that 90% of the time, the affected individual detects BC. BSE performed regularly once a month is recommended in women over the age of 20, since it is a simple, affordable, safe, and non-invasive procedure and does not require any special materials or tools4. Early detection is crucial in reducing BC related deaths as delayed diagnosis and treatment leads to incurable advanced disease or distant metastasis , resulting in increased morbidity and mortality5. Many women omit early detection and treatment opportunities due to inadequate knowledge about BC, BC risk factors, and BSE6. Previous studies on Saudis have shown low levels of BC awareness and knowledge, as well as a lack of participation in breast screening programs7–10.

Although the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia offers free healthcare, including no-cost breast cancer screening, the participation rate in these screening programs remains low11. The Saudi Arabian Ministry of Health recommends that women aged 40–49 undergo mammography screening every two years, while women between 50 and 69 years of age should be screened annually12,13. A 2013 study revealed that 89 percent of women in Saudi Arabia had never undergone a clinical breast examination, and 92 percent had never received a mammogram11, this highlights the considerable reluctance among Saudi women to seek breast cancer screening and care.Various cultural, practical, and personal factors have been identified as key challenges to the successful implementation of breast cancer screening programs. In Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, low participation rates may be attributed to fears related to treatment, discomfort with breast examinations, hesitation to consult male or non-Arabic-speaking physicians, and limited awareness or understanding of available screening services. On the other hand, a sense of susceptibility to the disease and a supportive social network can positively influence women’s willingness to undergo breast cancer screening14. To tackle the rising incidence of breast cancer in the region, it is essential to raise public awareness and provide education about the disease, along with implementing culturally appropriate screening initiatives. In order to do so effectively, assessing the level of knowledge among high-risk populations is vital.

This study aimed to evaluate the awareness, perceptions, and understanding of breast cancer and its risk factors among individuals in the educational institutions of the Asir region of Saudi Arabia. Although multiple cross-sectional studies have assessed breast cancer awareness across Saudi Arabia, including regional surveys from Asir10,15,16 and other provinces, existing work varies widely in target population, sample size, and outcome measures. This study specifically assesses knowledge, awareness, and perceptions among individuals working in educational institutions in Asir, as this group often plays a key role in health promotion and dissemination of knowledge within society thereby providing region-specific insights needed to design effective awareness strategies.

Methodology

Study setting and design

The present study was a cross-sectional, survey-based study conducted in the Asir region of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) for 4 weeks from March 2023 to April 2023.

Study population

The target population for this study included individuals aged 18 and above selected from various institutions in the Asir region, reflecting varied academic levels and demographic backgrounds, ensuring representation across the region. Initially, participants were recruited through snowball sampling with sample size of 500 as determined by a sample size calculator, which grew to 979 towards the end of the study period. As the study progressed, new participants were contacted based on referrals, ensuring maximum participation from subjects across all institutions. Snowball sampling proved useful in encouraging participation for the current study and facilitated efficient recruitment by leveraging social networks, however, this technique may be less appropriate for studies that demand random sampling or more generalizable findings17.

Data collection tool and approach

An online questionnaire designed after a thorough literature search18–21 was employed as a tool for data collection. The questionnaire was designed to align with the cultural and educational background of the target population. A team of three experts in public health, oncology, and survey design reviewed the questionnaire to ensure its content validity, evaluating each item for relevance, clarity, and suitability.After a minor revision, based on their feedback, a pilot study with 20 students and staff (excluded from the final sample) was conducted to assess face validity; also, internal consistency was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value of ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. The questionnaire was written in English, with Arabic translations in brackets for better comprehensibility. To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural relevance, the Arabic version was back-translated into English by a bilingual expert not involved in the initial translation. Both versions were then reviewed by subject-matter experts to confirm equivalence in meaning and appropriateness for further data analysis. The questionnaire comprised 3 sections: Section A sought information about demographics, section B contained 11 questions about the perception and knowledge of BC, and Section C comprised 13 questions about understanding BC risk factors. All the questions in sections B and C must be answered by marking either of the three choices: correct, incorrect, and do not know.

The data was collected by distributing the online questionnaire via social media platforms, mainly WhatsApp and Gmail, through the department heads of the study site institutions. All individuals of either sex were invited to participate in this study. The participants were provided with an informed consent form enumerating the purpose of the study, their right to withdraw, assurances of confidentiality, and voluntary participation, and henceforth, their agreement to participate was obtained.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was done using Statistical Package R version 3.5.3. Descriptive statistics was employed for the analysis of demographic variables. The data about demographics, knowledge of risk factors, and breast cancer awareness were expressed in frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis assessed the overall knowledge about breast cancer among different categorical variates. Odds ratios for each variable category and 95% confidence intervals were reported. The results were considered statistically significant when the p value was < 0.05. All demographic variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariate logistic regression model to calculate adjusted odds ratios.

Ethical considerations

The ethical approval for the study conduct was ratified by the research ethics committee at King Khalid University (HAPO-06-B-001) with approval number ECM#2023–901. The study adhered to ethical guidelines, ensuring participants’ anonymity and privacy. During the research, no personally identifiable information was sought at any point.

Results

Socio-demographics

Of the 979 participants who completed the survey, 789 (80.6%) were females and 190 (19.4%) were males. Nearly half of the participants (45.0%) were aged 20–29 years, and the majority (80.9%) resided in rural areas. Most respondents were single (57.3%), students (62.3%), and held a bachelor’s degree (63.6%). In addition, only 24.3% reported knowing someone with breast cancer (Table 1).

Knowledge and perception of BC

BC is the most common cancer among women in KSA, and more than half of the participants (56.1%) did not know about it. Nearly half of the study participants correctly reported that a color or shape change of the nipple (50.5%) is an early warning sign of BC and that men can also be affected by BC (47.5%). More than half (76.3%) of the respondents had a good knowledge about the role of Breast self-examination (BSE) in the early detection of BC, while an equal number of participants (45.5%) either had an idea or did not know about the appropriate timing to do BSE. Out of 6 incorrect questions provided in the questionnaire, only three questions__ poor survival of BC patients even if detected early, women younger than 30 not getting BC, and women with large breast mass being prone to get BC were correctively marked by > 50% of participants. Understanding BC risk factors

Knowledge about the 12 most commonly discussed breast cancer (BC) risk factors was assessed among participants, revealing variable levels of understanding. While some correct risk factors were identified by a moderate proportion of participants—such as lack of physical activity (48.1%) and overweight (41.0%)—knowledge regarding other important risk factors was limited. For instance, the majority of participants were unaware of the risks associated with hormone replacement therapy (66.0%), breast implants (61.3%), oral contraceptive use (50.6%), and high-fat diets (45.4%).

Misconceptions were also evident. A significant portion of participants incorrectly identified hard blows to the breast (51.3%) and mammograms (50.5%) as risk factors. Although awareness of family history and stress as risk factors was relatively higher, responses were nearly equally divided between correct identification and uncertainty. For incorrect risk factors, over half of the participants correctly identified breastfeeding (53.2%) and having children before age 30 (44.3%) as not increasing BC risk.

These findings suggest that while some understanding of BC risk factors exists, especially regarding lifestyle-related and myth-based factors, overall knowledge remains incomplete and inconsistent, highlighting the need for targeted educational efforts (Table 3).

Predictors of BC knowledge

When analyzing the association of BC awareness with demographic characteristics, the multivariate logistic regression analysis results revealed that females had 2.012 times higher odds of breast cancer awareness compared to males (OR = 2.012, 95% CI 1.289–3.141, p = 0.002). Since the p-value was less than 0.05, it was concluded that there was a statistically significant association between sex and engagement at the 5% level. Compared to those aged < 20 years, those aged 40–49 years had 3.616 times higher odds of breast cancer awareness (OR = 3.616, 95% CI 1.604–8.150, p = 0.002), while those aged > 50 years had 5.411 times higher odds (OR = 5.411, 95% CI 1.986–14.738, p = 0.001). For both age groups, the p-values were less than 0.05, indicating statistically significant associations at the 5% level. No statistically significant associations between marital status (single, married, divorced) and engagement were found with breast cancer awareness at the 5% level since all p values were > 0.05. No significant association was found between rural and urban residences.

Similarly, no statistically significant associations were found between employment category (admin staff, teaching staff, students) and engagement at the 5% level since all p values were > 0.05. Those with a PhD had 3.867 times higher odds of breast cancer awareness compared to bachelor’s degree holders (OR = 3.867, 95% CI 1.884–7.937, p < 0.001). Those with a master’s degree had 2.046 times higher odds (OR = 2.046, 95% CI 1.090–3.842, p = 0.026). Both associations were statistically significant at the 5% level. However, those with “other” education had significantly lower odds of breast cancer awareness (OR = 0.454, p < 0.001) compared to bachelor’s degree holders. In summary, female sex, older age (40 + years), and higher education (masters/PhD) had statistically significant positive associations with breast cancer awareness at the 5% significance level (Table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the level of knowledge and awareness about BC, including recognition of its risk factors, among individuals from various educational institutions in the Asir region of Saudi Arabia. The findings indicate that while participants showed a fair understanding of general aspects of BC, such as the value of early detection and identification of certain symptoms, their awareness of specific risk factors was comparatively low. These results align with similar studies conducted in other regions of Saudi Arabia8,10,22–24 where general awareness of BC was relatively high, particularly among educated and employed individuals. Since the study participants had acquired a basic minimum education owing to their recruitment from all educational institutions of the Asir region, their noteworthy knowledge and awareness about BC may be attributed to their education and stable employment status, which had privileged them in seeking knowledge about BC. This statement unanimously agrees with two previous studies conducted in the Jeddah and Riyadh regions of KSA, where the educated and employed participants had a fair knowledge of BC25,26.

While many had heard of breast cancer and were aware of early detection tools like breast self-examination (BSE), their ability to correctly identify established risk factors was poor. For example, the majority of participants were unaware of risk factors such as hormone replacement therapy (66.0%), oral contraceptive use (50.6%), and being overweight (41.0%). Additionally, many held misconceptions, incorrectly identifying factors like mammograms and physical trauma as causes of BC. These results align with previous studies from the Kingdom8,24,27,28 reinforcing the notion that while arbitrary awareness can be achieved through media and general education, in-depth knowledge of causative factors often requires structured education delivered by trained healthcare professionals.

It’s noteworthy that more than half (56.1%) of the participants were unaware that BC is the most common cancer affecting women in Saudi Arabia, which points to a lack of awareness regarding BC, as confirmed in other studies from different regions of KSA23,27. Although a fair proportion (76.3%) of the respondents had a good knowledge about the role of Breast self-examination (BSE) in the early detection of BC, while 45.5% of participants were either unsure or unaware of proper BSE techniques. This points to a notable gap between theoretical awareness and practical application. Bassam AlRajhi et al., in a systematic review, also reported a lack of sufficient knowledge and practice of BSE among Saudi women29. Evidence across the world continuously highlights the large discrepancy between screening behavior and awareness of breast cancer. As an example, a multi-country study that included more than 10,000 female university students from Asia, Africa, and the Americas discovered that while 50.4% of the participants said they knew how to perform breast self-examination (BSE), 59.3% had not done so in the previous year, and only a small percentage did so on a regular basis30. Similar discrepancies were noted in Cameroon, where 73.5% of people had heard of BSE but just 9% were proficient in its use and only 3% consistently practiced it31. These results are indicative of a worldwide phenomenon: awareness by itself does not always translate into accurate or reliable screening practices. This discrepancy between awareness and action, where a majority of participants knew about breast self-examination but nearly half were unsure of its correct timing and technique, underscores the critical knowledge–behavior gap that hinders effective early detection practices. In line with these patterns, our study also highlights the need for interventions that not only raise awareness but also build practical skills, self-efficacy, and culturally sensitive education.

When it came to understanding and perceiving risk factors, participants lacked knowledge. Out of the given 8 correct risk factors, only 3 were identified positively by less than 50% of participants, while from the given 4 incorrect risk factors, only 2 were identified correctly. Lifestyle-related risk factors like being overweight and having a fatty diet were not known by more than one-third of the participants, and more than 50% of participants did not know that breast implants are a risk factor. Additionally, more than half of the participants did not know common gynaecological risk factors, such as HRT and the use of OCPs. The low awareness of important medical risk factors such hormone replacement therapy, the usage of oral contraceptives, and being overweight is probably due to a lack of focused health education, cultural sensitivity when it comes to talking about reproductive health, and a lack of focus on these issues in awareness programs. These results undeniably indicate a lack of practical and impactful knowledge of BC risk factors among participants, as is evident from other studies23,24,27–29.

In our demographic analysis, we found that female sex, older age, and higher education were statistically significant with BC awareness. Interestingly, while women were more aware of BC, it is important to note that men are 75% more likely to die from BC due to late diagnosis32. This underlines the need for inclusive education targeting all genders. Knowledge of risk factors was significantly associated with being female, married, and residing in urban areas, suggesting that demographic and social factors influence access to and comprehension of BC-related information.

Although the Ministry of Health in Saudi Arabia provides free mammography services, awareness and knowledge about breast cancer (BC) remain notably low, and the number of cases continues to increase. While several awareness initiatives are underway, their overall impact appears limited. This highlights the need for more targeted and effective strategies. To enhance outreach and awareness about BC, we suggest to develop culturally appropriate social media campaigns based on scientific evidence, in collaboration with public health experts and behavioural scientists. These campaigns should address common myths and facts about BC using engaging formats like short videos, quizzes, and survivor testimonials. Popular platforms among young Saudis, such as Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), and Snapchat, should be used for dissemination. Local influencers and well-known figures, including female celebrities and healthcare professionals, should be involved to act as ambassadors for breast cancer education. Compulsory breast cancer education in university curricula, especially for students outside the health sciences should be considered. Training female health educators from local communities to conduct breast health awareness sessions and teach self-examination techniques in schools and universities will support cultural sensitivity and encourages open participation among women. Implementing these practical, community-based interventions can improve the effectiveness and reach of breast cancer awareness efforts in Saudi Arabia, leading to earlier diagnosis and better health outcomes.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. The study sample involved participants from educational institutions only, which may affect the inferences of findings to the wider population in the Asir region. Individuals from other socioeconomic backgrounds, professions, or those not affiliated with academic settings may have different levels of awareness or perceptions regarding breast cancer. Future research should aim to include participants from diverse demographic and occupational groups to enhance representativeness. Additionally, the use of snowball sampling may have led to overrepresentation of students and highly educated individuals, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to the wider community.

Second, the use of a self-administered questionnaire introduces the possibility of self-reporting bias. Participants may have overestimated their knowledge or provided socially desirable responses, which could affect the accuracy of the results. Additionally, recall bias may have influenced how participants responded to questions regarding past health behaviors or awareness campaigns.

Despite these constraints, the study offers meaningful insights into breast cancer awareness within an important demographic and identifies key areas for focused intervention.
