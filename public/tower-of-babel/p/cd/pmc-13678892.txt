Bezoar ileus in patients with previous abdominal surgery: an observational comparison of conservative and surgical management
Authors: Ali Muhtaroğlu, Kayhan Özdemi̇r, Emine Sena Cünük, Merve Yeşi̇lsancak, Alp Ömer Cantürk, Emrah Akin, Fatih Altintoprak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on the management of bezoar ileus in patients who have already undergone abdominal surgery is limited. We reviewed conservative and surgical pathways in clinically selected patients, with particular attention to patient selection criteria and how each pathway was defined.MethodsWe reviewed 80 patients with CT-confirmed bezoar ileus and previous abdominal surgery who were treated between January 2010 and December 2021. Twenty-six completed conservative treatment, and 54 underwent surgery. In practice, conservative treatment was used for clinically stable patients without peritonitis or CT signs suggesting ischaemia, perforation or closed-loop obstruction, provided that close clinical monitoring was possible. Conservative treatment included bowel rest, intravenous fluids, electrolyte correction, symptom-directed medication and nasogastric decompression when needed. Failure was defined by clinical deterioration, persistent or worsening obstruction, inability to tolerate oral intake, or new radiological concern. The primary outcome was clinical resolution during the index admission. Secondary outcomes were hospitalisation, in-hospital mortality, postoperative complications and readmission after discharge.ResultsThe treatment groups were not equivalent at baseline. Patients treated surgically more often had gastric bezoars (18/54, 33.3% vs. 0/26, p = 0.002), multiple bezoars (18/54, 33.3% vs. 1/26, 3.8%, p = 0.009) and greater small-bowel dilatation (median 4.0 cm vs. 3.4 cm, p = 0.014). All 26 patients in the conservative pathway completed the index admission without operation, although routine radiological confirmation of resolution was not performed. Median hospitalisation was 4 days (IQR 4–5) in the surgical group and 7 days (6–8) in the conservative group (p < 0.001); this difference should be interpreted in the context of clinical selection and local practice. Postoperative complications occurred in 11/54 surgical patients (20.4%), including two deaths from pulmonary embolism. Among discharged patients, readmission did not differ significantly between groups (13/52, 25.0% after surgery vs. 5/26, 19.2% after conservative treatment; p = 0.776). Exploratory regression findings were treated as hypothesis-generating only.ConclusionsConservative treatment may be feasible in selected stable patients with bezoar ileus after previous abdominal surgery. Surgery remains appropriate for patients with peritonitis, suspected ischaemia, perforation or closed-loop obstruction, clinical deterioration, or failure of conservative management. Because treatment was not protocolised and the groups differed at baseline, the findings should be read as observational and hypothesis-generating, not as evidence that either strategy is superior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13017-026-00719-6.

Introduction

Bezoar ileus is an uncommon cause of mechanical bowel obstruction. It occurs when indigestible material accumulates within the gastrointestinal tract and obstructs the lumen, producing abdominal pain, distension, nausea, vomiting and, in severe cases, dehydration, sepsis or bowel compromise [1–3].

The decision is particularly delicate in patients with previous abdominal surgery. Prior operations may alter anatomy and motility, and adhesions can complicate both the disease process and the operation. In practice, some patients are taken early to surgery because of gastric bezoars, multiple bezoars, increasing bowel dilatation, clinical deterioration or concern for ischaemia. Others can be managed safely with decompression, fluid replacement and close observation. The literature remains thin, and many reports mix bezoar-related obstruction with other forms of small-bowel obstruction, making bedside decision-making more difficult [4–10].

We therefore reviewed our experience with bezoar ileus in patients with a history of abdominal surgery. Rather than treating the two groups as interchangeable, we present this as an observational comparison of clinically selected conservative and surgical pathways. Our aim was to clarify what was done in practice, describe the outcomes, and offer a cautious basis for future prospective work.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-centre retrospective cohort study. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine (01 December 2025; decision no. 01.12.2025/73). The need for informed consent was waived because the study used de-identified retrospective data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Patients

We reviewed patients with previous abdominal surgery who presented to the emergency department between January 2010 and December 2021 with symptoms suggestive of bowel obstruction, including abdominal pain, distension, nausea or vomiting, early satiety, weight loss, loss of appetite, gastrointestinal bleeding or constipation. Patients were included when abdominal CT confirmed bezoar ileus. Patients without a history of abdominal surgery or without CT evidence that the obstruction was related to a bezoar were not included.

The final cohort contained 80 patients. Twenty-six completed conservative treatment, and 54 underwent surgery during the index admission. The groups therefore reflect the treatment pathway completed during that admission. This is important: the study does not compare protocol-assigned or randomised strategies, and it cannot be read as an intention-to-treat comparison of the first decision made in the emergency department.

Treatment pathways and operational definitions

Treatment was chosen by the attending surgical team according to clinical presentation, CT findings and the patient’s overall condition. There was no fixed institutional protocol. In practical terms, patients were considered for conservative treatment when they were clinically stable, had no peritonitis, had no CT signs suggesting ischaemia, perforation or closed-loop obstruction, showed no immediate clinical deterioration, and could be followed with close serial examination. These criteria describe how patients were selected in practice; they should not be read as a prospective treatment algorithm.

Conservative treatment generally consisted of nil by mouth, intravenous fluids, correction of electrolyte disturbance, symptom-directed medication and nasogastric decompression when vomiting, distension or gastric stasis was present. The approach was continued only while the patient remained stable and showed clinical improvement. Failure of conservative treatment was defined for this retrospective analysis as persistent or worsening abdominal pain or distension, ongoing vomiting or high nasogastric output, inability to tolerate oral intake, failure of bowel function to return, clinical deterioration, or CT findings raising concern for ischaemia, perforation, or closed-loop obstruction. Patients with peritonitis, suspected bowel ischaemia, closed-loop obstruction, clinical deterioration, or failure of conservative treatment were treated surgically. Endoscopic treatment of gastric bezoars was not part of routine practice during the study period; patients with gastric bezoars were therefore usually managed operatively at the discretion of the surgeon.

Delay to surgery was recorded as the number of days from hospital admission to operation. All operations were performed through a midline laparotomy. The surgical procedures included enterotomy, small-bowel resection, gastrotomy, gastrotomy with milking, and milking alone.

Confirmation of resolution

Resolution after conservative treatment was judged clinically: improvement in obstructive symptoms, return of bowel function, tolerance of oral intake and discharge without an operation during the index admission. Repeat CT was not performed routinely after conservative treatment; it was used when the clinical course was uncertain or when deterioration was suspected. Surgical resolution was confirmed intra-operatively and by the postoperative clinical course.

Imaging and variables

All patients underwent abdominal CT. Bezoar location and size were recorded from CT images, and actual size was measured postoperatively during surgery. On CT, bezoar length was defined as the maximum diameter in the relevant cross-section and width as the minimum diameter in the same section. We also recorded the number of bezoars, maximum small-bowel diameter, presence of free intraperitoneal fluid, previous incision type, comorbidities, hospitalisation, postoperative complications, mortality, and readmission after discharge.

The records classified bezoars reliably by location and size, but not by composition. Distinctions such as phytobezoar, trichobezoar, pharmacobezoar or mixed material were not documented consistently enough for analysis. Similarly, the database did not contain a reliable baseline field for psychiatric disease, cognitive impairment, trichotillomania, pica or eating disorders. These omissions are addressed in the limitations.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was clinical resolution during the index admission. Secondary outcomes were length of hospitalisation, in-hospital mortality, postoperative complications classified by the Clavien-Dindo system, and readmission after discharge. Readmission analyses excluded the two patients who died during the index admission.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]), according to distribution. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and the continuity-corrected chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for continuous associations.

Logistic regression was used only as an exploratory analysis. Because the number of events was small and confidence intervals were wide, these models were treated as hypothesis-generating signals rather than confirmatory findings. To reduce emphasis in the main text, the full regression models are provided in the supplementary material. Candidate covariates were chosen from clinically relevant variables and variables showing a signal in univariable analysis. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Wald statistics are reported. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Treatment groups

Eighty patients were included: 26 completed conservative treatment and 54 underwent surgery. The groups were clinically selected and differed in several baseline features. Age, sex distribution and overall comorbidity were similar. In contrast, gastric bezoars, multiple bezoars and greater maximum small-bowel diameter were more common in the surgical group. Free fluid on CT and a history of malignancy were more common in the conservative group. The conservative group contained no patients with gastric bezoars, reflecting the local practice of treating gastric bezoars operatively rather than endoscopically during the study period. Patients in the conservative pathway were stable enough for close observation and had no immediate clinical or CT findings that mandated operation (Table 1).

All 26 patients in the conservative group completed the index admission without an operation. Among surgical patients, the median delay from admission to operation was 1 day (IQR 1–2).

Readmission after discharge

Readmission was analysed among the 78 patients discharged alive. Eighteen patients were readmitted after discharge. Age, sex, comorbidity, bezoar location, number of bezoars, maximum small-bowel diameter, free fluid on CT, delay to operation, postoperative complication and treatment group were not significantly different between those readmitted and those not readmitted. Bezoar width and length were greater in the readmission group, and upper midline incision was more frequent (Table 2).

Hospitalisation and complications

Median hospitalisation was 4 days (IQR 4–5) in the surgical group and 7 days [6–8] in the conservative group (p < 0.001). This difference is reported as an observed difference between clinically selected pathways, not as proof that either approach is superior. No significant correlation was found between hospitalisation and age, bezoar number, bezoar width, bezoar length or maximum small-bowel diameter. Hospitalisation was also similar by sex, number of bezoars, comorbidity, bezoar location, free fluid on CT and previous incision type.

Postoperative complications occurred in 11 of 54 surgical patients (20.4%). According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, one patient had a grade II complication, five had grade IIIa complications, three had grade IIIb complications, and two had grade V complications. The two deaths were due to postoperative pulmonary embolism. No in-hospital complications were recorded in the conservative group.

Among surgical patients, female sex and gastric location were more frequent among those with postoperative complications. Age, comorbidity, bezoar number, bezoar size, maximum small-bowel diameter, free fluid on CT, delay to operation, and previous incision type did not differ significantly (Table 3).

Female sex showed a signal for association with complications, but the confidence interval was very wide. Discussion

This series offers a practical view of a rare problem. About one-third of patients with bezoar ileus after previous abdominal surgery completed treatment without an operation. Surgery was associated with a shorter hospital stay in this cohort, but it also carried postoperative complications and two deaths. The important point is not that one pathway should be favoured over the other. Rather, the study shows what happened when experienced surgeons selected patients for either close conservative observation or operation in everyday practice.

The differences between groups are central to interpretation. Surgeons tended to operate on patients with gastric bezoars, multiple bezoars and greater bowel dilatation. This is understandable. Gastric bezoars in our centre were not routinely treated endoscopically during the study period, and multiple bezoars or more pronounced dilatation often led to an earlier decision for operation. The shorter stay in the surgical group may therefore reflect not only the treatment itself but also local decision-making, discharge practice and the way patients were selected.

The conservative pathway deserves attention because it is the most clinically useful observation from this study. Patients selected for conservative treatment were stable, had no peritonitis, had no CT signs suggesting ischaemia, perforation or closed-loop obstruction, showed no immediate deterioration, and could be monitored closely. In that setting, bowel rest, hydration, electrolyte correction, symptom control, and decompression, when needed, were feasible. This does not mean that conservative treatment should be used broadly. It supports a carefully watched trial only in selected stable patients.

The regression findings should be kept firmly in perspective. Upper midline incision showed a signal for readmission, and female sex showed a signal for postoperative complications, but both estimates had wide confidence intervals and came from few events. These results are therefore best treated as prompts for future studies, not as clinical predictors or decision rules.

Our findings sit within a literature that often recommends surgery for bezoar-related small-bowel obstruction, especially when obstruction is complete, or complications are suspected [11–21]. At the same time, more recent work has explored CT features and clinical conditions that may allow conservative passage in selected patients [7]. Our data add to this discussion by focusing on patients with previous abdominal surgery, a group in which clinicians are often especially cautious.

Limitations

Several limitations matter. First, this was a retrospective single-centre study, and treatment allocation was not protocolised. Selection bias is therefore unavoidable. The surgical and conservative groups differed at baseline, particularly in gastric location, number of bezoars, bowel dilatation, free fluid on CT, and history of malignancy. These differences limit any claim about the superiority of one treatment over the other.

Second, the study compares the treatment pathway completed during the admission, not the initial management intention. The records did not allow a reliable reconstruction of every early bedside decision or every possible crossover before the definitive treatment pathway. Third, conservative resolution was primarily clinical. Repeat CT was not routinely performed after successful conservative treatment, so radiological resolution cannot be confirmed in every conservatively treated patient.

Fourth, the composition of bezoars was not consistently documented. We could not reliably distinguish phytobezoars, trichobezoars, pharmacobezoars or mixed bezoars. This is important because composition may influence both pathogenesis and response to treatment. Fifth, psychiatric and mental health variables, including pica, trichotillomania, eating disorders and cognitive impairment, were not captured in a consistent baseline field. These factors may be relevant to bezoar formation and recurrence, and future studies should prospectively record them.

The limited use of endoscopic treatment also affects generalisability. During the study period, gastric bezoars in our centre were usually managed surgically rather than by routine endoscopic fragmentation or dissolution. Centres that use endoscopy more readily for gastric bezoars may see a different balance between conservative, endoscopic and operative pathways.

Finally, the number of readmissions and postoperative complications was small. The logistic regression analyses were therefore exploratory, and the wide confidence intervals show that the estimates are unstable. Longer follow-up was also unavailable, so recurrence and late mortality could not be assessed.

Conclusion

In patients with previous abdominal surgery, conservative treatment may be feasible when bezoar ileus occurs in a clinically stable patient with no peritonitis, no CT features suggesting ischaemia, perforation, or closed-loop obstruction, and no clinical deterioration on close observation. Surgery remains appropriate for high-risk presentations and for patients who fail conservative management. The present data are best read as a cautious, hypothesis-generating description of selected pathways rather than as evidence favouring one strategy over the other. Prospective studies with clear treatment criteria, standardised follow-up and systematic recording of bezoar composition and psychiatric history are needed.
