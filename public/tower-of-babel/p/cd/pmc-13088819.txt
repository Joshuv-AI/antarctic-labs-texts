The effect of high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy on preventing hypoxemia during deep sedation extubation after laparoscopic surgery: a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yi-Kai Wang, Xiang Li, Shi-You Wei, Hui Yang, Zhou-Peng Tan, Sheng-Ming Yan, Wei Liu, Xin Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy has shown potential benefits in improving oxygenation in perioperative settings. However, its role in preventing early postoperative hypoxemia after extubation under deep sedation during laparoscopic surgery remains unclear.MethodsThis single-center randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 patients undergoing elective laparoscopic surgery. After extubation under deep sedation, patients were randomized to receive either HFNC or NC until discharge from the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Extubation under deep sedation was performed during emergence after discontinuation of anesthetic agents, when patients had regained spontaneous respiration but had not yet fully recovered consciousness, according to predefined clinical and bispectral index criteria.The primary outcome was hypoxemia, defined as peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) < 90% for > 60 s between extubation and PACU discharge. Secondary outcomes included arterial partial pressure of oxygen/inspired oxygen fraction (PaO₂/FiO₂), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂), heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and perioperative complications. Parameters were measured at six time points: before anesthesia (T0), immediately after extubation (T1), and at 5 (T2), 15 (T3), 30 (T4) minutes post-extubation, and at PACU discharge (T5).ResultsHypoxemia occurred in 24/60 (40%) NC patients and 12/60 (20%) HFNC patients (P = 0.028). Of those, mask-assisted positive pressure ventilation was needed in 11/24 NC and 2/12 HFNC patients (P = 0.026); jaw thrust was performed in 13/24 and 10/12, respectively. At T2 and T3, PaO₂/FiO₂ was higher (T2: 333.0 ± 34.6 vs. 286.3 ± 41.7; T3: 345.9 ± 34.5 vs. 303.2 ± 40.3 mmHg; both P < 0.001) and PaCO₂ lower (T2: 42.2 ± 4.2 vs. 48.8 ± 3.8; T3: 39.6 ± 3.7 vs. 45.3 ± 3.6 mmHg; both P < 0.001) in the HFNC group. No differences were found in PACU stay or other complications.ConclusionsHFNC oxygen therapy after extubation under deep sedation significantly reduces hypoxemia and improves early respiratory function versus NC, with comparable safety.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500107249, August 7, 2025).

Introduction

Extubation under deep sedation is increasingly adopted in clinical practice to attenuate airway irritation, coughing, agitation, and hemodynamic fluctuations during emergence from general anesthesia. Compared with awake extubation, this strategy may improve patient comfort and reduce peri-extubation sympathetic responses. However, accumulating evidence suggests that extubation under deep sedation is associated with a relatively higher risk of early postoperative hypoxemia, with reported incidence rates of approximately 30–40% in surgical patients [1, 2].

A key mechanism underlying this increased risk is that, during deep sedation, spontaneous respiratory drive and airway protective reflexes may not be fully restored at the time of extubation. In this setting, routine low-flow oxygen therapy delivered via nasal cannula or simple face mask may be insufficient to meet the elevated oxygen demand and to counteract transient hypoventilation, airway collapse, or ventilation–perfusion mismatch [3]. Notably, current perioperative guidelines generally recommend low-flow oxygen therapy after extubation as routine practice, but provide limited guidance regarding oxygen delivery strategies tailored to different depths of sedation at extubation [2]. As a result, optimal respiratory support for patients extubated under deep sedation remains incompletely defined.

This issue is particularly relevant in laparoscopic surgery. Pneumoperitoneum [4], patient positioning, residual anesthetic effects [5], and postoperative atelectasis [6] may collectively impair respiratory mechanics and gas exchange during the early post-extubation period, thereby increasing susceptibility to hypoxemia. These physiological challenges highlight the need for more effective oxygenation strategies during recovery from anesthesia in patients undergoing laparoscopic procedures.

High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy has emerged as an alternative respiratory support modality in perioperative and critical care settings [7, 8]. By delivering heated and humidified oxygen at high flow rates, HFNC can improve airway patency, reduce work of breathing, provide a low level of positive end-expiratory pressure [9], and enhance oxygenation efficiency. Previous studies have suggested that HFNC may reduce postoperative hypoxemia and the need for escalation of respiratory support after extubation [8–10]. However, most available evidence has focused on patients who are awake or only lightly sedated at the time of extubation, and data specifically addressing its use following extubation under deep sedation are limited.

Given these considerations, we designed a prospective randomized controlled trial to test the hypothesis that HFNC oxygen therapy, compared with conventional low-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy, could more effectively reduce early post-extubation hypoxemia and improve respiratory function in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery with extubation under deep sedation.

Methods

This single-center, randomized controlled trial included patients undergoing elective laparoscopic surgery at our institution between August 2025 and September 2025. The study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500107249). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bozhou People’s Hospital (approval number: 2025–157, Bozhou People’s Hospital Ethics Committee). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and trial reporting adhered to the CONSORT guidelines.

Patients

Inclusion criteria were adult patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic surgery under general anesthesia with ASA physical status I–III. Eligible patients were screened and recruited by the site investigator on the day of hospital admission. Subjects with one of more of the following conditions were excluded: (1) body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2; (2) severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; (3) known or anticipated difficult airway, prior airway or peri-airway surgery (including thyroid surgery), or patients at high risk who could not communicate; (4) history of gastrointestinal surgery or intestinal obstruction; (5) use of deep sedation or neuromuscular blocking agents within 48 h before surgery; (6) use of medications affecting gastrointestinal motility within one month prior to enrollment; (7) severe fluid or sodium retention, or active infection; (8) coagulation disorders; and (9) transfer to the ICU with a tracheal tube postoperatively.

Randomization, concealment, and blinding

Randomization was performed using a simple randomization method. An independent researcher generated the allocation sequence using a random number table, assigning patients to either the nasal cannula (NC) group or the HFNC group in a 1:1 ratio. To ensure allocation concealment and randomness, the randomization list was placed in sealed, opaque envelopes without any external markings. During patient enrollment, a researcher would randomly select an envelope, and the group assignment was determined according to the number inside. This process continued until all patients were enrolled.

This study employed a single-blind design. Due to the distinctive appearance and operational characteristics of HFNC devices compared to conventional nasal cannula, blinding of the clinicians administering oxygen therapy was not feasible. However, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation throughout the trial to minimize bias. Group assignments were concealed from these personnel during data collection and analysis.

Standard procedure

All patients fasted preoperatively according to standard protocols. Upon entering the operating room, an intravenous line was established, and monitoring included heart rate (HR), electrocardiogram (ECG), invasive mean arterial pressure (MAP), pulse oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and bispectral index (BIS). Anesthesia was induced with intravenous agents: penehyclidine hydrochloride 0.5 mg, midazolam 0.05 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.5 µg/kg, etomidate 0.3 mg/kg, and rocuronium 0.9 mg/kg. Tracheal intubation was performed under video laryngoscope guidance. Mechanical ventilation was initiated in volume-controlled mode with a tidal volume of 8 mL/kg, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2, and the respiratory rate adjusted to maintain end-tidal CO₂ at 35–45 mmHg. Neuromuscular blockade was managed according to routine clinical practice at our institution. Quantitative neuromuscular monitoring was not routinely applied. Extubation was performed only after discontinuation of anesthetic agents and confirmation of adequate spontaneous ventilation, including a regular respiratory pattern, sufficient tidal volume, and stable oxygen saturation, together with clinical signs of recovery from neuromuscular blockade. No patient underwent extubation in the presence of obvious residual neuromuscular weakness.

Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of propofol (4–6 mg·kg⁻1·h⁻1) and remifentanil (0.1–0.3 µg·kg⁻1·h⁻1), with pump rates adjusted to keep blood pressure and heart rate within ± 20% of baseline. Rocuronium was administered intermittently to maintain neuromuscular blockade. At the end of surgery, all anesthetic agents were discontinued. Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) was initiated, consisting of sufentanil (2 µg/kg) diluted in normal saline to a total volume of 150 ml. The PCA settings included a 4 ml loading dose, a 3 ml/h background infusion, a 3 ml bolus dose, a 20-min lockout interval, and a maximum dose of 10 ml/h. After surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for observation.

Extubation under deep sedation

Upon arrival in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), extubation under deep sedation was performed during the emergence phase after discontinuation of all anesthetic agents. At this stage, patients had regained spontaneous respiration but had not yet fully recovered consciousness.

Extubation under deep sedation was conducted according to predefined criteria, including confirmation of adequate spontaneous ventilation and clinical recovery from neuromuscular blockade. Specifically, extubation was performed when the bispectral index (BIS) value was between 68 and 75, with a regular spontaneous respiratory waveform showing a stable plateau phase, tidal volume greater than 5–6 mL·kg⁻1, respiratory rate between 10 and 15 breaths per minute, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) ≥ 95% or no decline after three minutes of oxygen withdrawal. After endotracheal and oral suctioning, the tracheal tube was removed.

Intervention and control

In the NC group, oxygen was delivered via a standard nasal cannula (JH906; Yuyao Jiahua Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Yuyao, China) at a flow rate of 3–4 L·min⁻1, which corresponds to an estimated inspired oxygen fraction (FiO₂) of approximately 0.32–0.36, consistent with routine clinical practice.

In the HFNC group, oxygen therapy was delivered using a heated and humidified high-flow nasal cannula system (HUMID-BH; Shenyang Maisi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China). HFNC was initiated with a flow rate of 30–40 L·min⁻1, an FiO₂ of 0.60, gas temperature set at 31 °C, and 100% relative humidity.

The selection of flow rates and FiO₂ settings was based on previous perioperative studies and clinical experience, aiming to provide adequate oxygenation while minimizing discomfort and excessive airway pressure during the early recovery period.

Following extubation, if SpO₂ dropped below 90% for more than 60 s, the anesthesiologist first performed a jaw thrust maneuver to open the airway. If SpO₂ did not improve, mask-assisted positive pressure ventilation was initiated. If hypoxemia persisted, a new airway (laryngeal mask or endotracheal tube) was established.

Measurements and outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of hypoxemia during the recovery period after extubation, defined as an SpO₂ < 90% lasting for more than 60 s between extubation and discharge from the PACU. This was analyzed as a proportion of patients experiencing the event in each group.

Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic parameters (heart rate, MAP, and SpO₂), recorded at six predefined time points—before anesthesia (T0), immediately after extubation (T1), 5 min (T2), 15 min (T3), and 30 min (T4) after extubation, and upon discharge from the PACU (T5)—to monitor cardiovascular and respiratory stability during the post-extubation period. Arterial blood gas measurements (PaO₂/FiO₂ and PaCO₂) were assessed at five time points (T0, T2, T3, T4, and T5) to evaluate perioperative changes in oxygenation and ventilation. In addition, the incidence of post-extubation complications such as hypotension (defined as MAP < 65 mmHg lasting more than 60 s), hypercapnia (PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg, categorized as mild [46–60 mmHg], moderate [61–75 mmHg], or severe [> 75 mmHg]), nausea and vomiting, agitation, choking, and airway spasm was documented during the recovery period.

Additional outcomes included the management of hypoxemia (e.g., requirement for airway maneuvers, mask positive pressure ventilation, or reintubation), as well as clinically relevant time-based endpoints such as time from cessation of anesthetic agents to recovery of spontaneous breathing, time to extubation, duration of anesthesia and surgery, PACU length of stay, time to first postoperative flatus, and overall length of hospital stay. These measures were used to comprehensively assess perioperative safety, respiratory support effectiveness, and postoperative recovery.

All outcomes were assessed according to predefined time points and analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis.

Sample size calculations

Sample size estimation was performed using PASS version 15. The calculation was informed by both published evidence and a pilot study conducted at our institution. In a randomized controlled trial by Hernández et al. [10], which included relatively low-risk patients undergoing planned, stable extubation, oxygenation-related adverse outcomes (including reintubation, post-extubation respiratory failure, and respiratory infection) occurred in approximately 31.5% of patients receiving conventional oxygen therapy and 15.5% of those receiving HFNC.

Compared with the population in that study, patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery in the immediate postoperative period are exposed to additional risk factors for hypoxemia, including residual anesthetic effects, pneumoperitoneum-related diaphragmatic dysfunction, and postoperative atelectasis. Therefore, we considered that a higher incidence of post-extubation hypoxemia would be clinically plausible in our study population.

This assumption was supported by a pilot study involving 16 patients (8 per group), which showed a hypoxemia incidence of 37.5% in the conventional nasal cannula group and 12.5% in the HFNC group during the recovery period after extubation. Based on these estimates, assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated to be 60 patients per group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test; those not normally distributed were expressed as median and interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Repeated measures continuous variables (such as PaO₂/FiO2, heart rate, SpO₂, and arterial blood gas parameters) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with patients as random effects and time, group, and their interaction as fixed effects, to assess the influence of time and intervention on outcome variables.

If the interaction term was statistically significant, pairwise comparisons between the HFNC and NC groups at each timepoint were conducted using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. For within-group comparisons, changes from baseline (T0) at each timepoint were assessed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, also with Bonferroni correction. The method for handling missing values in this study is mean difference imputation. All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For multiple comparisons, only Bonferroni-adjusted P values are reported.

Heart rate did not differ significantly between groups at T0 or T1 (P = 1.000 for both). At T2, T3, and T4, heart rate was significantly higher in the NC group (P < 0.01 for all comparisons), returning to similar levels by T5 (P = 0.902). Within-group comparisons, using T0 as baseline, showed that in the NC group, heart rate increased significantly at extubation and at all subsequent timepoints, peaking at T2 (P < 0.001), and then gradually declined. In contrast, in the HFNC group, heart rate was significantly elevated only at T1 and T2 (both P < 0.001), returning to baseline levels from T3 onward.

Similarly, MAP showed no significant group differences at T0 or T1 (P = 1.000 for both). MAP was higher in the NC group at T2 and T3 (both P < 0.001), with no significant differences at later timepoints. Within-group comparisons, using T0 as baseline, demonstrated that in the NC group, MAP increased significantly at T0 and at all subsequent timepoints except at T5 (all P < 0.001, except P = 0.125 at T5), with the highest value observed at T2. In the HFNC group, a significant increase in MAP was observed only at T2 (P < 0.001), and T3 (P = 0.005).

Postoperative outcomes

There were no significant differences between groups in key postoperative recovery metrics. The median length of PACU stay was 49.00 min (IQR, 29.00–74.00 min) in the NC group and 44.00 min (IQR, 29.00–53.00 min) in the HFNC group (median difference, 5 min; P = 0.071). Time to first flatus was identical in both groups, with a median of 3.00 days (IQR, 2.00–4.00 days; P = 0.900). Median hospital stay was 11.00 days (IQR, 8.00–13.00 days) in the NC group and 10.00 days (IQR, 9.00–12.00 days) in the HFNC group (median difference, 1 day; P = 0.617).

Regarding perioperative complications during the recovery period, the incidence of hypotension was 55.0% (33/60) in the NC group and 46.6% (28/60) in the HFNC group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.401). In the NC group, there was one case of nausea and vomiting, six cases of agitation (8.2%), four cases of choking (5.5%), and one case of airway spasm (1.4%). None of these complications occurred in the HFNC group. No major postoperative complications, such as reintubation, pneumonia, unplanned ICU admission, or reoperation, were observed in either group during the hospital stay.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that, in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery with general anesthesia and extubation under deep sedation during the recovery phase after discontinuation of anesthetic agents, postoperative application of high-flow nasal cannula significantly reduces the incidence of post-extubation hypoxemia. Furthermore, HFNC improves early post-extubation arterial blood gas parameters (PaO₂/FiO₂ and PaCO₂), and attenuates fluctuations in heart rate, blood pressure, and SpO₂ following extubation.

First, HFNC demonstrated a clear advantage in reducing the incidence of post-extubation hypoxemia. In this study, the rate of hypoxemia in the HFNC group was 20% (12/60), which was significantly lower than that in the NC group 40%, 24/60). Hypoxemia is one of the most common respiratory complications after extubation and can lead to a range of adverse outcomes, including prolonged PACU stay, increased risk of reintubation, and even greater incidence of postoperative cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events [11–13]. By delivering high flow rates (up to 60 L/min), precisely controlled oxygen concentrations, and continuous warmed and humidified gas [14], HFNC reduces upper airway resistance, mitigates airway edema and collapse, and generates a mild continuous positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) effect [15], thereby improving functional residual capacity and promoting alveolar recruitment [16]. These mechanisms help alleviate post-extubation atelectasis and ventilation-perfusion mismatch, thus reducing the risk of hypoxemia. Notably, in the present study, the median length of PACU stay in the NC group was longer than that in the HFNC group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.071). Moreover, only 2 patients in the HFNC group required escalation to mask-assisted positive pressure ventilation, compared to 11 patients in the conventional group, indicating that HFNC not only reduces the incidence of hypoxemia but also lessens the need for higher-level respiratory interventions. This is clinically meaningful for reducing medical resource utilization, improving patient comfort, and increasing the efficiency of postoperative care. In line with ICU-based studies evaluating prophylactic HFNC use after planned extubation [10, 17], our findings extend the potential benefits of HFNC to the immediate postoperative period under deep sedation. These advantages are consistent with recent studies [18–20], further supporting the superiority and clinical promise of HFNC for perioperative oxygen therapy.

Second, HFNC had a positive impact on early post-extubation arterial blood gas parameters. In this study, PaO₂/FiO₂ at 5 and 15 min after extubation was significantly higher in the HFNC group than in the NC group. Within-group comparisons showed that the decline in PaO₂/FiO₂ after extubation was substantially less in the HFNC group compared to NC group, indicating that HFNC provides sustained improvement in pulmonary oxygenation efficiency during the early post-extubation period. The mechanism underlying this benefit likely involves the low-level PEEP effect generated by high flow rates, which helps maintain alveolar recruitment and reduces the risk of atelectasis and alveolar collapse in the immediate post-extubation phase, as well as decreasing the likelihood of anatomical airway obstruction [21]. Additionally, the use of warmed and humidified oxygen can effectively decrease airway mucosal injury and secretion retention [22], further promoting homogeneous ventilation and improving overall oxygenation. Furthermore, our study found that PaCO₂ at 5 and 15 min after extubation was significantly lower in the HFNC group compared to the NC group. This may be attributable to the washout effect of high-flow gas in the upper airway and tracheal dead space, which reduces the rebreathing of expired CO₂ and improves ventilatory efficiency, thereby decreasing the risk of CO₂ retention [23]. This advantage is particularly important in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery, who often experience impaired diaphragmatic and pulmonary function due to intraoperative pneumoperitoneum, positioning, and residual anesthetic effects. Overall, HFNC can significantly optimize both oxygenation and ventilation in the early post-extubation period under deep sedation, with this benefit persisting for up to 15 min and diminishing by 30 min after extubation, suggesting that HFNC primarily exerts its protective effects during the critical early recovery phase, in line with its intended clinical application.

Third, HFNC conferred benefits for hemodynamic parameters and recovery-related adverse events [24, 25]. Our study showed that the HFNC group experienced a smaller decrease in SpO₂ and less pronounced increases in heart rate and MAP during the early post-extubation period, compared to the NC group. This suggests that HFNC can effectively attenuate the sympathetic stress response following extubation and help stabilize hemodynamics. Increases in heart rate and blood pressure after extubation are typically due to a combination of hypoxemia, CO₂ retention, and airway irritation, all of which activate the sympathetic nervous system. By delivering stable and efficient oxygen and improving both oxygenation and CO₂ clearance, HFNC reduces the cardiovascular burden [26]. Furthermore, the use of HFNC during extubation under sedation was associated with fewer cases of agitation and choking, which likely further reduced the risk of blood pressure fluctuations linked to sympathetic activation, contributing to overall hemodynamic stability. Although not all parameters reached statistical significance, the observed trends are notable and warrant further investigation in larger studies.

In addition, there were no significant differences between groups in perioperative recovery outcomes such as time to first flatus, length of hospital stay, or PACU stay. The relatively long hospital stay observed in this study likely reflects local clinical practice rather than postoperative morbidity. At our institution, patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery are commonly hospitalized until postoperative pathological confirmation and treatment planning are completed. Notably, no major postoperative complications were observed during hospitalization. This suggests that, while HFNC can improve immediate post-extubation respiratory function, its impact on the overall recovery period may be limited. This finding could be related to factors such as sample size, baseline patient characteristics, and surgical complexity.

Finally, regarding other perioperative complications, the incidence of hypotension, nausea and vomiting, and airway spasm in the HFNC group was not increased compared to the NC group, indicating that HFNC did not pose additional risks and demonstrated good safety in this patient population.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center randomized controlled trial with a relatively limited sample size, the generalizability of our findings may be restricted. Although the internal validity was strengthened by a standardized anesthesia and extubation protocol, future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm the reproducibility of our results across different institutions and clinical settings. Second, the study population was limited to patients undergoing elective laparoscopic surgery, and did not include other surgical procedures or higher-risk populations, such as patients with severe cardiopulmonary disease. Therefore, the applicability of HFNC during extubation under deep sedation in broader surgical populations remains to be determined. Third, this study focused on early postoperative outcomes during the immediate recovery period, and long-term respiratory or clinical outcomes were not assessed. Further studies with extended follow-up are needed to evaluate the potential long-term benefits of HFNC. Fourth, although extubation was performed only after clinical recovery from neuromuscular blockade and adequate spontaneous ventilation was confirmed, quantitative neuromuscular monitoring was not routinely employed. Residual neuromuscular blockade may therefore have contributed to postoperative respiratory events and should be considered in future investigations. Finally, this study did not explore the optimal HFNC settings, including flow rate, inspired oxygen fraction, or duration of therapy, and future research is needed to optimize HFNC parameters for perioperative use.

In conclusion, this study confirms that HFNC is a safe and effective approach for patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery with extubation under deep sedation. HFNC significantly reduces post-extubation hypoxemia, improves early respiratory and hemodynamic stability, and does not increase perioperative complications.
