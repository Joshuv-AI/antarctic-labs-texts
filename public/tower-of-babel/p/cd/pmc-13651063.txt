Diagnostic performance of insulin resistance indices and their association with metabolic outcomes in children with obesity: a 10-year retrospective study from a tertiary pediatric endocrinology center
Authors: Berna Eroğlu Filibeli, Gülümay Vural Topaktaş, Julide Gulizar Yildirim, Bumin Dündar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood obesity is strongly associated with insulin resistance (IR) and related metabolic complications. However, the optimal surrogate marker for identifying IR in pediatric populations remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of commonly used IR indices and to investigate their association with metabolic outcomes in children and adolescents with obesity.MethodsThis retrospective study included 899 children and adolescents with obesity followed at a tertiary pediatric endocrinology center over a 10-year period. IR was defined based on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) findings. The diagnostic performance of the fasting glucose-to-insulin ratio (FGIR), quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) and the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Associations between IR, metabolic syndrome (MS), dysglycemia, hepatic steatosis, and biochemical parameters were analyzed. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors.ResultsHOMA-IR demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance for identifying IR (AUC: 0.711 in girls and 0.700 in boys), outperforming FGIR and QUICKI. Among pubertal participants, the optimal HOMA-IR cut-off values for predicting OGTT-defined insulin resistance were 4.22 in girls and 4.18 in boys. IR was present in 76.3% of participants and was associated with an unfavorable metabolic profile, including higher rates of dysglycemia and hepatic steatosis. Hepatic steatosis was detected in 63.5% of children and was significantly more common in boys. In multivariate analysis, male sex, BMI-SDS, and ALT were independent predictors of hepatic steatosis, whereas IR parameters were not independently associated. TSH levels were higher in IR and MS groups but remained within the normal range.ConclusionHOMA-IR is a reliable and practical marker for identifying IR in children with obesity. While IR is associated with multiple metabolic disturbances, adiposity and liver-related markers appear to play a more dominant role in predicting hepatic steatosis. A comprehensive metabolic evaluation, including both fasting indices and dynamic tests, is essential for early risk stratification in pediatric obesity.Keywords: Insulin resistance, HOMA-IR, Pediatric obesity, Metabolic syndrome, Hepatic steatosis

Introduction

Childhood obesity has become one of the most important public health challenges worldwide, with a steadily increasing prevalence and substantial long-term metabolic consequences [1]. Insulin resistance (IR) is recognized as the principal risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). It is defined as a reduced biological response to insulin, particularly in terms of its ability to promote glucose uptake and utilization. In response to this defect, pancreatic β-cells increase insulin secretion as a compensatory mechanism, leading to hyperinsulinemia while maintaining normal glucose tolerance in the early stages [2].

Early identification of IR in children with obesity is of critical importance, as timely lifestyle interventions—including dietary modification, nutritional counseling, and increased physical activity—may prevent or delay the progression to T2DM. The hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp technique is considered the gold standard for assessing insulin sensitivity; however, its invasive nature and technical complexity limit its applicability in large-scale or routine clinical settings [3]. Although the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) involves multiple sampling points, it is more feasible for both individual assessment and population-based studies. In the search for less invasive alternatives, several surrogate indices have been developed and shown to correlate well with clamp-derived measurements [4]. These include the fasting glucose-to-insulin ratio (FGIR), quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) and the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). The requirement of only a single fasting blood sample, without the need for intravenous access, makes these indices simple and practical tools for routine clinical use [4].

Among these, HOMA-IR is the most widely used index in both adult and pediatric populations. A cutoff value of 2.5 has been commonly accepted in adults [5]; however, variability across different ethnic groups has been reported [6]. In children, especially during adolescence, proposed cut-off values such as 3.16 or 4 have been suggested, yet no clear consensus has been established [4, 7–9]. Therefore, despite being time-consuming and relatively burdensome, OGTT remains a frequently utilized method, particularly for diagnosing impaired fasting glucose (IFG), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), and T2DM, in addition to evaluating IR. Recently, Hammel et al. [10], developed age-, sex-, and puberty-specific reference percentiles for fasting glucose, insulin, and HOMA-derived indices across childhood and adolescence, highlighting the dynamic physiological changes in insulin sensitivity during growth and maturation. Despite these advances, the optimal clinical thresholds for identifying metabolically relevant insulin resistance in obese pediatric populations remain uncertain, particularly when compared with dynamic assessments such as OGTT.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate OGTT results in children with obesity followed in a tertiary pediatric endocrinology center for the assessment of insulin resistance, and to compare FGIR, QUICKI and HOMA-IR indices between patients with and without insulin resistance as determined by OGTT findings and metabolic syndrome (MS).

Method

Study design and participants

This retrospective study included children and adolescents aged ≥ 10 years with exogenous obesity who underwent an OGTT as part of their obesity evaluation between January 2012 and January 2022. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) above the 95th percentile for age and sex according to national reference growth charts [11]. Consecutive patients meeting the inclusion criteria and whose parents or legal guardians provided informed consent were included in the study. Participants were excluded if they had obesity secondary to endocrine disorders (such as Cushing syndrome, hypothyroidism, or growth hormone deficiency), monogenic obesity (including mutations in the melanocortin 4 receptor, leptin, or leptin receptor genes), or syndromic obesity (such as Bardet–Biedl, Prader–Willi, or Alström syndromes). In addition, patients with chronic diseases or those receiving long-term medication were excluded. For participants who underwent more than one OGTT during the study period, only the first examination was included in the analysis to avoid duplication of observations. The study was approved by the local ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Because of the retrospective design, all eligible children and adolescents meeting the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period were included in the study. To evaluate the adequacy of the available sample size, a post hoc power analysis was performed based on the parameters reported by Kurtoğlu et al. [9]. The final sample consisted of 899 participants, which was considered sufficient to provide adequate statistical power for the planned analyses.

Data collection

Anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, presence of acanthosis nigricans and striae, and pubertal status, were obtained from medical records. Laboratory data included fasting plasma glucose, fasting insulin, lipid profile [total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)], alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels. Hepatic steatosis was assessed by ultrasonography. Radiological findings, including ultrasonographic assessment of hepatic steatosis, and OGTT results were also retrieved from patient records and discharge summaries.

Assessment of anthropometric measurements and pubertal status

Anthropometric measurements were obtained during routine outpatient visits by trained healthcare personnel according to standard clinical procedures. Body weight was measured with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes, and height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer. The weighing scale and stadiometer were routinely calibrated according to institutional procedures. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Weight SDS, height SDS, and BMI SDS were calculated according to national reference growth charts [9]. Pubertal development was evaluated according to Tanner staging, based on breast development in girls and testicular volume in boys [12, 13].

Blood pressure

Blood pressure (BP) was measured during routine outpatient visits by trained healthcare personnel using an appropriately sized cuff after at least 5 min of rest in a seated position. Hypertension (HT) was defined according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines. In children aged < 13 years, hypertension was defined as systolic and/or diastolic BP ≥ 95th percentile for age, sex, and height. In adolescents aged ≥ 13 years, hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg.

Insulin resistance indices

IR indices were calculated using established formulas. The HOMA-IR was calculated as fasting glucose (mg/dL) multiplied by fasting insulin (mU/L) divided by 405 [4]. The FGIR was obtained by dividing fasting glucose by fasting insulin [14]. The QUICKI was calculated as the inverse of the logarithmic sum of fasting glucose and insulin concentrations [15].

Oral glucose tolerance test

OGTT was performed after an overnight fast using a glucose load of 1.75 g/kg (maximum 75 g). Blood samples for glucose and insulin measurements were obtained at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min [16]. OGTT-defined IR was defined as a total insulin concentration exceeding 300 µU/mL during OGTT, consistent with the approach used by Kurtoğlu et al. [9]. Based on OGTT results, patients were categorized into insulin resistance and non–insulin resistance groups. IFG and IGT were defined according to the American Diabetes Association criteria. IFG was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level between 100 and 125 mg/dL. IGT was defined as a 2-hour plasma glucose level between 140 and 199 mg/dL during OGTT. A 2-hour plasma glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL was considered diagnostic of T2DM [17].

Definition of metabolic syndrome

MS was defined according to the International Diabetes Federation consensus criteria [18]. For participants aged 10–16 years, MS was diagnosed in the presence of central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90th percentile or adult cutoff if lower) plus at least two of the following: triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; HDL-C < 40 mg/dL; systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg; or fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or previously diagnosed T2DM. For participants aged ≥ 16 years, MS was defined as central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 94 cm in males and ≥ 80 cm in females) plus at least two of the following: triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; HDL-C < 40 mg/dL in males or < 50 mg/dL in females; systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg or treatment for HT; or fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or known T2DM. Participants were categorized into groups with and without MS for further analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated as appropriate [19, 20]. The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis statistics [19, 20]. Independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons of normally distributed continuous variables, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test [19, 20]. Multiple logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with hepatic steatosis and MS [21]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of IR indices for identifying OGTT-defined IR and MS, and results were presented with 95% confidence intervals [21]. The optimal cut-off values in ROC analyses were determined using the Youden Index, which identifies the threshold that maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity [22]. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. G*Power version 3.1.3 was used for a priori sample size estimation and post hoc power analysis.

Results

The mean age of the participants was 14.06 ± 2.06 years (range: 8.59–18 years; 95% CI, 13.92–14.19). The study population consisted of 899 children, of whom 63.2% (n = 568) were girls and 36.8% (n = 331) were boys. According to Tanner staging, 866 participants (96.3%) were pubertal and 33 (3.7%) were prepubertal. Detailed Tanner stage distribution is presented in Table 1. Acanthosis nigricans was present in 77.8% of the children, while striae were observed in 75.2%. HT was identified in 26.4% of the participants. Based on OGTT findings, IR was present in 76.3% of the cohort; IFG, IGT, and T2DM were identified in 11.3%, 13.9%, and 5.8% of the participants, respectively. MS was identified in 27.5% of the participants. Hepatic steatosis was detected in 63.5% of the children, with grade 1 hepatic steatosis accounting for 53.5% of these cases (Table 1).

In comparison, fewer parameters differed according to MS status (Table 2). Significant differences were observed in fasting insulin, 120-min glucose, FGIR, QUICKI, HOMA-IR, TSH, and ALT levels, whereas anthropometric measures and hepatic steatosis were comparable between groups. Overall, IR was associated with a broader range of metabolic alterations compared to MS. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, HOMA-IR emerged as the strongest independent predictor of IR (OR = 1.268, 95% CI: 1.182–1.360, p < 0.001), while BMI-SDS also showed an independent association (OR = 1.317, 95% CI: 1.035–1.676, p = 0.025). In contrast, ALT and TSH were not independently associated with IR after adjustment.

Hepatic steatosis was detected in 63.5% of the children with obesity. The prevalence of hepatic steatosis was significantly higher in boys compared to girls (69.7% vs. 58.1%, p = 0.002). In the multivariate model, female sex was associated with a lower risk of hepatic steatosis compared with males (OR: 0.562, 95% CI: 0.354–0.892, p = 0.014). BMI-SDS showed a strong positive association, with each unit increase more than doubling the odds of hepatic steatosis (OR: 2.212, 95% CI: 1.598–3.062, p < 0.001). ALT levels were also independently associated with hepatic steatosis (OR: 1.034, 95% CI: 1.020–1.048, p < 0.001). In contrast, HOMA-IR, OGTT-defined IR, age, and pubertal stage were not independently associated with hepatic steatosis. Notably, IR parameters were not independently associated with hepatic steatosis after adjustment for BMI-SDS and ALT (Table 3).

Regarding MS, female sex and HOMA-IR remained independently associated with the outcome after adjustment for potential confounders. Female sex was associated with a 79.5% increase in the odds of MS (OR: 1.795, 95% CI: 1.164–2.767, p = 0.008), while each one-unit increase in HOMA-IR was associated with a 5.9% increase in the odds of MS (OR: 1.059, 95% CI: 1.019–1.100, p = 0.003). Age, pubertal stage, ALT, BMI-SDS, and OGTT-defined IR were not independently associated with MS (Table 3).

The diagnostic performance of IR indices varied considerably across indices and outcomes (Table 4). Among all evaluated indices, HOMA-IR demonstrated the highest discriminative ability for both OGTT-defined IR and MS in both sexes. For OGTT-defined IR, HOMA-IR showed good diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.711 in girls and 0.700 in boys. In contrast, FGIR and QUICKI exhibited poor discriminative ability (Table 4). Similarly, for the identification of MS, HOMA-IR remained the best-performing index, with AUC values of 0.614 in girls and 0.750 in boys. Other indices, including FGIR and QUICKI, showed limited diagnostic utility. Overall, HOMA-IR consistently outperformed the other IR indices in detecting both IR and MS, regardless of sex.

Sex-specific ROC curve analyses further supported the superior performance of HOMA-IR (Fig. 1). For the identification of IR, HOMA-IR showed good discriminative ability in both pubertal girls and boys. The optimal HOMA-IR cutoff value for detecting IR was 4.22 in pubertal girls and 4.18 in pubertal boys. At these thresholds, sensitivity ranged from 70.0% to 71.9%, while specificity ranged from 59.4% to 60.7%. Both FGIR and QUICKI showed poor diagnostic accuracy for defining IR, with AUC values consistently below 0.310 across both genders (p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis revealed that HOMA-IR could distinguish pubertal boys with MS from those without (AUC = 0.750, 95% CI: 0.618–0.883, p = 0.002). In Table 4, a HOMA-IR cut-off value of 4.21 offers the optimal clinical balance with 75.4% sensitivity and 70.6% specificity. Notably, FGIR and QUICKI failed to demonstrate sufficient diagnostic capability for MS in girls, yielding statistically non-significant results (AUC = 0.377, p = 0.081 and AUC = 0.386, p = 0.106, respectively). However, in boys, both the FGIR (AUC = 0.252, p = 0.002) and the QUICKI (AUC = 0.250, p = 0.002) reached statistical significance, albeit with low AUC values indicating a poor discriminative performance.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of commonly used IR indices in children and adolescents with obesity and examined their ability to identify IR and MS according to OGTT findings. Our results demonstrated that HOMA-IR consistently outperformed other indices, showing good diagnostic accuracy for OGTT-defined IR in both girls (AUC: 0.711) and boys (AUC: 0.700), whereas FGIR and QUICKI exhibited poor discriminative ability. These findings are consistent with previous studies, including those by Keskin et al. [4] which demonstrated that HOMA-IR is more reliable than other fasting-based indices in obese pediatric populations. Extending prior evidence, our study further shows that HOMA-IR maintains its superiority not only for IR detection but also in identifying MS, with consistent performance across sexes, supporting its clinical utility as a practical screening tool. Although FGIR, QUICKI, and HOMA-IR are not included in the diagnostic criteria for MS, they reflect underlying IR, which plays a central role in its pathophysiology. These findings support the notion that simple IR indices, especially HOMA-IR, may be useful for early risk stratification; however, they cannot replace established diagnostic criteria for MS.

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, HOMA-IR emerged as the strongest independent predictor of IR, even after adjustment for BMI-SDS, ALT, and TSH, further supporting its clinical value as a simple and reliable marker. While BMI-SDS also showed an independent association, ALT and TSH were not independently associated with IR, suggesting that IR is primarily driven by underlying metabolic alterations rather than isolated biochemical parameters. Notably, although both fasting- and OGTT-defined measures are commonly used to assess IR, only HOMA-IR remained independently associated with MS in the multivariate model. This finding suggests that fasting IR may be more closely related to the clustering of metabolic abnormalities that characterize MS. In contrast, the predictive information provided by OGTT-defined IR appears to overlap with that of other metabolic variables included in the model, reducing its independent contribution after adjustment.

The interpretation of HOMA-IR values in children, however, remains complex due to the absence of a universal cut-off. While a threshold of approximately 2.5 has traditionally been used in adults [5], pediatric studies have reported a wide range of cut-off values depending on age, pubertal status, and population characteristics [23]. This variability is largely explained by physiological IR during puberty, a transient condition characterized by a 30–50% reduction in insulin sensitivity and compensatory hyperinsulinemia [24, 25]. Accordingly, higher cut-off values have been proposed in pediatric populations, including > 2.7 [8] and 3.16 [26], as well as sex-specific thresholds of 5.22 in boys and 3.82 in girls [9]. In our study, the optimal HOMA-IR cut-off values were remarkably similar between girls and boys (4.22 and 4.18, respectively), suggesting a relatively consistent threshold during puberty. These findings emphasize the importance of using age- and puberty-specific cut-offs, as applying adult thresholds may lead to underestimation of metabolic risk in children.

Although HOMA-IR demonstrated the best diagnostic performance among the evaluated indices, the optimal cut-off values identified in our cohort were associated with moderate specificity (approximately 60%) and acceptable sensitivity (approximately 70%). Previous pediatric studies have reported variable sensitivity and specificity values depending on age, pubertal status, and the definition of IR. For example, Kurtoğlu et al. [9] reported pubertal HOMA-IR cut-off values of 5.22 in boys and 3.82 in girls, with sensitivities of 56% and 77.1% and specificities of 93.3% and 71.4%, respectively. In contrast, our study identified similar cut-off values for boys and girls (4.18 and 4.22, respectively) but with higher sensitivity and substantially lower specificity. These differences may be related to variations in study populations, pubertal distribution, and the criteria used to define IR. Consistent with the findings of Keskin et al. [4] and Atabek and Pirgon [8], HOMA-IR remained the best-performing fasting-based index among the evaluated surrogate markers. From a clinical perspective, the relatively high sensitivity observed in our cohort supports the use of HOMA-IR as a screening tool for identifying children at increased metabolic risk. However, the moderate specificity indicates that HOMA-IR alone may overestimate IR, particularly during puberty when physiological IR is common. Therefore, HOMA-IR results should be interpreted together with clinical findings and, when clinically indicated, complemented by dynamic assessments such as OGTT. Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of using age- and puberty-specific cut-off values and highlight the complementary roles of fasting indices and dynamic testing in the metabolic evaluation of children and adolescents with obesity.

In addition to IR, our findings highlight a substantial burden of dysglycemia in children with obesity. The prevalence of IFG, IGT, and T2DM in our cohort was higher than that reported in previous pediatric studies [27], likely reflecting the characteristics of a tertiary referral population with more severe obesity and metabolic impairment. Although IFG, IGT, and T2DM were more frequent in the IR group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, this trend suggests a clinically relevant relationship between IR and disturbances in glucose metabolism. Importantly, these findings underscore a key limitation of relying solely on fasting glucose and insulin measurements. While these parameters are commonly used in clinical practice, they may fail to detect abnormalities in postprandial glucose metabolism, particularly IGT. In contrast, OGTT provides a dynamic and comprehensive assessment of glucose homeostasis, allowing for the detection of post-load hyperglycemia and early β-cell dysfunction, both of which are predictors of future T2DM [28]. Previous studies have also shown that fasting glucose and HbA1c alone may underestimate dysglycemia in children [29]. Therefore, despite its complexity, OGTT remains an essential tool for identifying high-risk individuals in pediatric obesity.

Hepatic steatosis was highly prevalent in our cohort, affecting 63.5% of children with obesity, with a significantly higher frequency observed in boys compared to girls. According to the recently proposed nomenclature, most children with obesity and hepatic steatosis would fall within the spectrum of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, because our study retrospectively evaluated ultrasonographic hepatic steatosis rather than formally diagnosing MASLD, we retained the term hepatic steatosis throughout the manuscript. This sex difference is consistent with previous reports and international consensus statements identifying male sex as a risk factor for pediatric MAFLD [30]. In multivariate analysis, male sex, BMI-SDS, and ALT emerged as independent predictors of hepatic steatosis, highlighting the central role of adiposity and hepatic injury. The strong association between BMI-SDS and hepatic steatosis is in line with previous evidence demonstrating that increasing adiposity is a major driver of hepatic fat accumulation [31]. Similarly, ALT levels, as markers of hepatocellular injury, showed an independent association, supporting their clinical relevance in identifying liver involvement. Notably, IR parameters were not independently associated with hepatic steatosis after adjustment. This finding suggests that the contribution of IR to hepatic steatosis may be largely mediated through obesity and related metabolic factors. Supporting this, previous studies have reported that although IR is closely linked to MAFLD and related cardiovascular alterations, its independent effect may diminish after accounting for adiposity and liver-related parameters [32].

Another important finding of our study was the higher TSH levels observed in both the IR and MS groups, despite values remaining within the normal range. Previous studies have shown that TSH levels are more strongly associated with adiposity than with IR itself, suggesting that mild TSH elevations in obesity may represent an adaptive physiological response rather than true thyroid dysfunction [33]. Accordingly, the higher TSH levels observed in our cohort are more likely attributable to increased adiposity, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation of thyroid function tests in children and adolescents with obesity.

Beyond the findings presented above, an important strength of the present study is its large cohort size and the inclusion of children and adolescents evaluated over a 10-year period. To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest single-center pediatric obesity cohorts from the region assessing the diagnostic performance of multiple IR indices against OGTT-defined IR. These data provide valuable regional evidence regarding metabolic risk stratification in children and adolescents with obesity.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size and a comprehensive evaluation of multiple IR indices in a real-world clinical setting. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, and the tertiary referral nature of the cohort may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the absence of a non-obese control group precluded comparisons across different BMI categories. Second, IR was not assessed using the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp technique, the gold standard for evaluating insulin sensitivity. Third, hepatic steatosis was assessed by ultrasonography rather than liver biopsy or advanced imaging modalities, limiting precise characterization of liver involvement and precluding differentiation from other causes of increased liver echogenicity, such as hepatic glycogenosis. Furthermore, obesity was defined according to national reference growth charts, which may limit comparability with studies using alternative international references. The study period also encompassed both pre-pandemic and pandemic years; however, the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on metabolic outcomes was not specifically evaluated. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow-up precluded assessment of the long-term predictive value of the identified IR indices and their association with future metabolic outcomes.

In conclusion, HOMA-IR appears to be the most reliable and clinically applicable fasting index for identifying IR in children and adolescents with obesity. IR is associated with a broad spectrum of metabolic disturbances, including dysglycemia and hepatic steatosis, even in the absence of MS. However, adiposity and liver-related markers, particularly BMI-SDS and ALT, seem to play a more dominant role in predicting hepatic steatosis. While HOMA-IR demonstrated the best performance among the evaluated surrogate indices, OGTT provided complementary information by identifying glucose tolerance abnormalities that may not be captured by fasting measurements alone. Therefore, a comprehensive metabolic evaluation combining practical fasting indices with dynamic testing when clinically indicated may improve early risk stratification in pediatric obesity.
