Association between iron metabolism markers and triglyceride–glucose index: A cross-sectional study in China Health and Nutrition Survey
Authors: Yanan Li, Yifan Dang, Yanhui Zhou, Haidong Wang, Lisheng Zheng, Yudong Ba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Iron metabolism plays an important role in insulin resistance, and the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index has been proposed in recent years as a more accessible and cost-effective marker for insulin resistance. This study aims to evaluate the association between iron metabolism markers, including ferritin (FER), transferrin (TRF), and transferrin receptor (TFR), and the TyG index. A total of 6524 Chinese individuals aged between 18 and 75 years were included in this study. Multivariable linear models were used to investigate the association between FER, TRF, and TFR levels, and the TyG index. Further subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex were also performed. There was a positive association between FER and TRF levels and the TyG index in all 3 multivariable linear regression models, regardless of stratification by sex and age. Additionally, TFR was positively associated with the TyG index among females and those aged ≥45 years, but not among males and those aged <45 years. Our findings reveal a positive association between FER and TRF levels and the TyG index in a Chinese population, while the association between TFR levels and the TyG index showed different patterns depending on age and gender.

1. Introduction

Iron is an essential metal found in humans and all other living organisms, playing a central role in many crucial biochemical processes, including normal growth and development.[1] Since no efficient pathway exists for iron excretion, intestinal dietary iron absorption is the only means for the body to uptake and regulate iron.[2] Iron absorption, transport, storage, utilization, recycling, and excretion are controlled by regulatory metabolic pathways, homeostatic mechanisms, and related regulatory proteins. In brief, iron is absorbed from the diet through divalent metal transporters and stored in the form of serum ferritin (FER) and hemosiderin.[3] These levels reflect the iron storage conditions in healthy individuals and are vital for maintaining cellular functions such as DNA synthesis, oxygen transport, mitochondrial respiration, and cell growth regulation and differentiation.[4] In most cells, after iron binds to transferrin (TRF), transferrin receptor (TFR) delivers the majority of plasma iron into tissues or organs. When the endosome becomes acidified, TRF-bound iron is released and enters the cytoplasm from the endosome.[5–7]

Insulin resistance (IR) is a prominent feature of several metabolic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, lipid metabolism disorders, and cardiovascular disease. The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) is the most frequently used index to evaluate IR, calculated as: IR = (insulin [μU/L] × glucose [mmol/L])/22.5.[8–10] Numerous studies have focused on the association between serum FER and IR measured by HOMA-IR, with some also reporting the association between TRF and HOMA-IR.[11,12] However, previous studies have reached inconsistent and contradictory conclusions.[13–15] The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting blood glucose and fasting triglyceride levels, has been proposed as a more accessible and cost-effective marker for assessing IR than HOMA-IR in clinical practice, regardless of diabetes status.[16–18]

However, the relationship between iron metabolism markers and the TyG index has not been examined. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between iron metabolism markers, including FER, TRF, and TFR, and the TyG index in a Chinese population aged 18 to 75 years from a nationwide survey.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

Longitudinal data were drawn from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), an open, ongoing, prospective cohort study with ten rounds completed between 1989 and 2015. CHNS was approved by the institutional review boards of the University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety, China Center for Disease Control and Prevention. All participants provided written informed consent. Our study extracted data from 8209 individuals in 2009 with complete fasting triglyceride, fasting blood glucose, FER, TRF, and TFR data. Participants aged <18 or >70 years old (n = 585), subjects with anemia (n = 1061), and pregnant women (n = 39) were excluded. The remaining 6524 individuals aged 18 to 70 years (3199 men and 3325 women, mean ages 44.2 ± 13.3 years for men and 45.0 ± 12.8 years for women) were included in the final analysis. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin levels of <13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women according to WHO recommendations.

2.2. Variables

The independent variables were FER, TRF, and TFR. The dependent variable was the TyG index, which was calculated by the formula: TyG = Ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2].

Demographic variables, including age, nationality, smoking status, diabetes, and high blood pressure, were obtained from the self-reported questionnaire. Weight and height were used to calculate the body mass index (BMI = weight [kg]/height [m²]). Venous blood samples (12 mL, 3 4 mL tubes) were collected from all individuals older than 7 years old after fasting. Centrifugation was performed 30 minutes after blood collection at 3000 × g for 15 minutes to obtain the serum. All sample analyses were completed in China–Japan Friendship Hospital’s Ministry of Health Laboratory, a verified national central laboratory in Beijing (medical laboratory accreditation certificate ISO 15189:2007), under strict quality control. The details of blood and serum sample collection, processing, and preservation are available at https://www.cpc.unc.edu/projects/china.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 3.4.3, http://www.r-project.org) and EmpowerStats (https://www.empowerstats.com; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA). The total statistical analysis procedure was divided into 4 steps. Firstly, baseline characteristics of participants were analyzed by different genders. Categorical variables were expressed as a frequency or percentages while continuous variables were expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SD). Differences between different genders were evaluated using chi-squared test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA (for normal distribution) or Kruskal–Wallis H test (for skewed distribution) for continuous variables. Secondly, multivariate logistic regression models were employed to calculate the odds ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) for evaluating the association between iron metabolism markers (FER, TRF, and TFR) and the TyG index. Three models were applied to provide statistical inference: model 1, an unadjusted model; model 2, adjusted for gender, age, and nationality; and model 3, adjusted for all covariates, including smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, and BMI. Thirdly, subgroup analyses stratified by gender and age using multivariate logistic regression models were further performed to evaluate the association between iron metabolism markers and the TyG index. Fourthly, and the potential nonlinearity of the association between iron metabolism markers and the TyG index in total population and different gender and age subgroup was further explored using generalized additive models and smooth curve fittings. P < .05 was considered statistically significant in this study.

The association between TFR levels and the TyG index was unremarkable in the unadjusted model and the model adjusted for gender, age, and nationality. However, this association became significant (β = 0.034, 95% CI: 0.004–0.063) in the model adjusted for all covariates. In the subgroup analyses stratified by gender, females, but not males, showed a positive association in all 3 multivariable linear regression models. Further analyses stratified by age revealed no significant associations in the age group of ≥45 years across all 3 models. However, the age group <45 years showed a positive association in the model adjusted for gender, age, and nationality (β = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.000–0.106), which became more prominent in the fully adjusted model (β = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.012–0.095). There was no observed association in the unadjusted model for the age group <45 years.

4. Discussion

In this study, we examined a large sample size of Chinese adults to explore the association between iron metabolism markers (FER, TRF, and TFR) and the TyG index from a nationwide survey. FER and TRF levels had positive associations with the TyG index in all 3 multivariable linear regression models whether the subjects were stratified by sex or age. Our results also showed that TFR was positively associated with the TyG index among females and age ≥45 years but not among males and age <45 years.

The TyG index has been demonstrated to be a highly effective evaluation tool for various diseases and health conditions in previous studies. For instance, Khalaji A et al[19] assessed its role in predicting heart failure, finding it to be a valuable marker for evaluating the risk of heart failure incidence across different populations. Moreover, it also serves as a prognostic marker for patients with heart failure, thereby guiding preventive strategies in clinical practice. Similarly, a meta-analysis conducted by Behnoush[20] investigated the association between the TyG index and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Their findings indicated that patients with higher TyG index values are more likely to suffer from OSA, highlighting that the TyG index is an easy-to-measure marker of IR useful for both the diagnosis and prognosis of OSA. Furthermore, numerous studies have focused on the TyG index as a robust predictor of IR and related metabolic disorders, underscoring its utility in clinical settings for early detection and intervention.[21,22]

There is no efficient pathway for iron excretion; hence, intestinal iron absorption from the diet via divalent metal transporters is the sole method for the body to uptake iron and regulate its levels. Under normal conditions, iron significantly influences glucose metabolism, including insulin secretion, insulin signaling, and glucose uptake. Iron accumulation in adipocytes can lead to decreased adipogenic capacity and increased lipolysis of adipose tissue triacylglycerol stores.[23,24] Consequently, the TyG index, a newly proposed marker for IR based on glucose and triglycerides, is influenced by iron levels in the body. In most cells, plasma iron is delivered to the entire body via TFR-mediated endocytosis after binding to TRF; TRF-bound iron is released into the cytoplasm when the endosome acidifies. Intracellular iron can then be stored as FER, maintaining various physiological functions.[25,26] Therefore, FER, TRF, and TFR are crucial regulators of body iron levels and may affect the TyG index used to evaluate the degree of IR. This study explores the associations between the TyG index and FER, TRF, and TFR levels.

Previous studies have investigated the associations between FER and IR (mainly measured by HOMA-IR) in various populations, yielding conflicting results. Some studies showed positive, negative, or no associations between FER and IR. A study in Western Australia found no independent association between serum FER and IR, as assessed using HOMA-IR, in men or women without diabetes. Philipp study demonstrated strong relationships between plasma FER levels and several IR markers in young and healthy subjects from the Principality of Liechtenstein (N = 2170).[27] A cross-sectional study of 12,090 participants from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that increased serum FER concentration is associated with IR in men but not in women,[28] another cross-sectional study reported an association between serum FER and HOMA-IR in men and postmenopausal women but not in premenopausal women.[29] However, results from surveys of the Chinese population were relatively consistent. A survey of 8235 participants reported that elevated serum FER levels were associated with HOMA-IR, independent of several confounders in men and women in China.[30] Another study involving 2786 Chinese individuals with no history of metabolic disorders found a positive association between serum FER levels and HOMA-IR values in both sexes.[11] Similarly, a community-based study in Shanghai found a higher serum FER concentration to be significantly associated with IR in men and postmenopausal women but not in premenopausal women, independent of possible risk factors.[31] Our study found a positive association between FER levels and the TyG index in the Chinese population across both genders and age-stratified groups. Despite consistent results for the Chinese population, these findings must be interpreted cautiously due to limited sample sizes and differing findings from other populations.

TRF, a critical protein for iron transport in the body, has attracted attention for its relation to IR. Changes in TRF and FER were inversely associated. The Lifestyle Intervention and Impaired Glucose Tolerance Maastricht[32] study reported that changes in TRF were higher in men than women and positively associated with changes in HOMA-IR. A small study in Spain (27 men and 22 women) found that TRF saturation is negatively correlated with HOMA-IR.[15] A study based on the 2010 Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported similar results, showing lower TRF saturation is significantly associated with higher insulin levels and HOMA-IR in both sexes.[33] Our study found a positive association between TRF levels and the TyG index in 18 to 70-year-old Chinese individuals, regardless of gender, consistent with findings from the CHNS, which showed serum TRF is significantly associated with HOMA-IR in both sexes.[14] This suggests that serum TRF may be significantly associated with IR in both adults and minors in China.

Compared to FER and TRF, the association between TFR and IR has received limited attention. In vitro studies have explored the relationship of TFR with IR. One study on the molecular mechanisms underlying iron dysregulation in palmitate-induced IR found that TFR1 knockdown prevents palmitate-induced iron uptake and IR, indicating that iron overload by TFR protein 1 regulation plays an important role in palmitate-induced IR in human skeletal muscle cells.[34] Another report by Liu et al[35] demonstrated that immune-reactive insulin–TRF could achieve improved binding affinity and prolonged IR activation with the assistance of TFR binding. Different studies have reported inconsistent results regarding the association between TFR and IR. Milton et al[36] reported a significant relationship between soluble TFR and HOMA-IR, similar to Huth et al findings, which showed a significant positive correlation between soluble TFR levels and HOMA-IR in 2893 participants of the population-based Cooperative Health Research in the Region of Augsburg F4 study (Germany).[37] Contrary to these findings, a study in Spain did not find a significant correlation between soluble TFR and HOMA-IR after adjusting for sex, age, and BMI. A study based on CHNS, similar to our study, found no association between soluble TFR levels and IR in Chinese children and adolescents. Our study also found no significant association between soluble TFR levels and the TyG index in adult Chinese in the unadjusted model and the model adjusted for gender, age, and nationality, consistent with results from children and adolescents. The positive association between soluble TFR levels and the TyG index found only in females, but not in males, contrasts with a study in Croatia, which found that soluble TFR levels correlate positively with IR in postmenopausal women and men but not in premenopausal women. This suggests possible racial/ethnic differences. Our findings that the positive association between soluble TFR levels and the TyG index exists only in the age group <45 years and that soluble TFR levels have no association with IR in Chinese children and adolescents may imply that age is an important factor for evaluating the relationship between soluble TFR levels and IR.

Based on the close relationship between the TyG index and iron metabolism markers found in this study, several practical applications for primary care physicians can be identified. Firstly, the TyG index, a simple and cost-effective measure, can help identify patients at risk for metabolic syndrome, IR, and related disorders. Including iron metabolism markers in routine screenings may enhance this predictive ability. Secondly, understanding the link between iron metabolism and the TyG index allows for more effective treatment plans. Patients with high TyG index and abnormal iron markers may benefit from targeted lifestyle changes and therapies focused on glucose–lipid regulation and iron homeostasis. Lastly, regular monitoring of the TyG index can track iron metabolism progress, allowing for timely treatment adjustments and potentially reducing the risk of severe complications. These insights can improve early detection, personalized treatment, and ongoing patient monitoring in primary care settings.

Our study has several strengths, including a large sample size and the use of a nationwide Chinese cohort that represents the general population. Additionally, we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounding factors in our analysis, which enhances the reliability of our results. However, we must also acknowledge the following limitations of our study. On the one hand, this study highlights a potential relationship between iron metabolism markers and the TyG index; however, the causality cannot be determined due to the cross-sectional study design. On the other hand, other iron markers, such as hepcidin, could also influence iron status. Additionally, iron status is affected by the participants’ diet and overall health. Therefore, measuring the metabolism markers more than once would provide more convincing evidence.

In conclusion, we found that the association between FER and TRF levels and the TyG index was positive in a Chinese population, while the association between TFR levels and the TyG index varied by age and gender. Future research should prioritize elucidating the potential mechanisms underlying these relationships.
