Patient-reported outcome measures in cardiac surgery: A systematic review
Authors: Brianna K. Rosgen, Sumedh Bele, Sadia Ahmed, Kyle Kemp, Maria Jose Santana, Alexander J. Gregory
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPatient-reported outcome measures (PROM) are standardized questionnaires, often referred to as tools, allowing patients to self-report their health, quality of life, or functional status associated with the perioperative care they received. There has been recent increased emphasis on their standardized incorporation into clinical practice, quality improvement initiatives, and clinical trials.MethodsWe performed a systematic review (registration ID: CRD42021262914, no funding source) on PROM use in the adult cardiac surgery literature to summarize study characteristics, questionnaire use, and formal methodologic quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa and Joanna Briggs Institute tools.ResultsWe included 668 publications from MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and CINAHL (2000-2024), using 341 unique PROM tools, in 229,392 patients. We used descriptive statistics to summarize study and patient characteristics as well as PROM and traditional outcome use, methods, and reporting characteristics. There was heterogeneous use of PROM tools and a low rate of baseline assessments, inconsistent assessment time intervals, and infrequent reporting of questionnaire element raw values. The measured PROM results were seldom compared or associated with traditional outcomes such as mortality, length of stay, or complications.ConclusionsTo achieve the maximum utility of PROMs for optimizing perioperative care and patient-centered recovery, they must be applied in a consistent and standardized fashion within clinical practice, quality improvement initiatives, and clinical trials.

Patient-reported outcome measures: systematic review of their use in cardiac surgery.

Central Message

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROM) represent an opportunity for health care providers to measure the effectiveness and quality of their care through metrics that are meaningful to patients.

Perspective

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROM) measure quality of recovery from the patient's perspective. We systematically reviewed the use of PROM in cardiac surgery between 2000 and 2024, resulting in 668 publications and 229,392 patients. Improved consensus and standardization of PROM use is needed for more optimal clinical and research outcomes.

In cardiac surgery, the determination of quality of care and postoperative recovery has typically focused on outcomes related to major complications such as prolonged intubation, surgical-site infection, acute kidney injury, stroke, myocardial infarction, extended length of stay, reoperation, hospital readmission, and mortality.1 Although important, these measures do not fully capture postoperative recovery from the patient's perspective, potentially missing the full impact when a complication does occur.2,3 It may also provide the only measure of the patient's quality of recovery and return to function when no other complication or adverse event occurs, which for many institutions is the majority of patients.4,5 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are standardized questionnaires, often referred to as tools, allowing patients to self-report their health, quality of life, or functional status associated with the perioperative care they have received.6

There has been recent increased emphasis on their standardized incorporation into clinical practice, quality improvement initiatives, and clinical trials.5,7, 8, 9 Although traditional outcomes such as mortality and complications remain important, they fail to capture the long-term effects of surgery on patients’ physical, psychological, and functional recovery. PROMs provide complementary information that more holistically describe the experience of recovery and identify meaningful differences beyond mortality and complications. This growing focus on PROMs is reflected in a recent American Heart Association Scientific Statement on the Methodological Standards for the Design, Implementation, and Analysis of Randomized Trials in Cardiac Surgery, which includes a section on the importance and proper use of PROMs.10 Furthermore, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Society, and Enhanced Recovery After Cardiac Surgery Society recently published a consensus document identifying patient engagement through PROMs as one of the recommended perioperative care elements.11

Effective adoption of cardiac surgical PROMs requires a thorough understanding of their historical use in the literature, including identification of areas for improvement and standardization. In 2019, Mori and colleagues12 published a systematic review of 105 publications describing the use of PROMs in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The authors reported details of the patient populations, study characteristics, and outcomes reporting methodology. Information on the PROM questionnaires themselves was also included, although some specifics (ie, measurement timing and frequency of PROM assessment) were only provided for the 22 publications using the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36). Other details, such as response collection methods or rationale for choice of questionnaire, were not included. Finally, no formal study methodology quality assessment was performed.

We undertook a systematic review of PROM use in patients who underwent cardiac surgery that expands on previous work by Mori and colleagues to include recently published studies, more detailed information on PROM instrument characteristics (eg, elements included, administration, timing, and reporting methods), and incorporaes a formal assessment of study methodology quality. The objectives of this review were to describe the use of PROMs in adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery, including the types of assessment instrument used, their timing and methods of administration, reporting practices, and the methodologic quality of the studies that used them.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

The completion of this study and publication of its results received exemption and waiver of informed written consent from University of Calgary research ethics board. The protocol was drafted a priori in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (Online Data Supplement 1) and registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration ID: CRD42021262914).13 The protocol was only updated to reflect review progress, not any methodologic changes.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they (1) were primary, peer-reviewed studies; (2) used any quantitative interventional or observational design; (3) involved adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery (ie, sternotomy or thoracotomy); and (4) identified or described any PROM tool. Studies were excluded if they (1) were not written in English; (2) not peer-reviewed; (3) were classified as secondary literature (eg, systematic or narrative review); (4) used study designs including case reports, case series, study protocols; (5) used qualitative study designs because this review focused only on use, reporting, and quality assessment of quantitative PROMs; (6) involved exclusively pediatric patients, patients who underwent noncardiac surgery, or patients who underwent percutaneous cardiac procedures (eg, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, percutaneous coronary intervention, patent foramen ovale closure, percutaneous ablations, and pacemaker implantations); (7) pooled eligible patients with ineligible patients, that could not be disaggregated with data provided (eg, adult and pediatric patients, or percutaneous and sternotomy procedures); (8) did not identify or describe a PROM tool; (9) reported duplicate PROM data; (10) were abstract only or the full-text was unavailable; and (11) reported only a Canadian Cardiovascular Society or New York Heart Association classification, because these measures are clinician-assigned functional classifications of patient outcomes rather than PROMs that require direct patient self-report using a standardized questionnaire.14,15

Search

The search strategy was developed by the study team and conducted in online databases including MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, and CINAHL, from inception to December 16, 2024. The search was restricted to studies conducted on humans, published in English, and published in the year 2000 or later. Search terms included cardiac surgery and PROMs, and related synonyms and medical subject headings (Online Data Supplement 2).

Study Selection

Duplicate studies were removed in bulk by Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation) using author, title, and publication year, then additional duplicates were manually removed by a single reviewer. Two reviewers screened titles and abstracts independently and in duplicate. Full-text of studies were screened independently and in duplicate by 2 reviewers. Reviewers agreed on inclusion and reasons for exclusion at the full-text screening stage with disagreements resolved by discussion or a third reviewer.

Data Extraction and Study Quality

Two reviewers extracted data independently from studies using a standardized electronic form developed by the study team. Data were extracted independently and in duplicate for 100 articles until consensus and extraction consistency was reached. Data extraction for the remaining articles was completed by a single reviewer. Extracted elements included: study information, traditional outcome measures, and PROM questionnaire details (Online Data Supplement 3). Methodologic quality assessment was completed by 2 reviewers using an appropriate tool for the types of studies included. The Joanna Briggs Institute Checklists were used for RCTs and Quasi-Experimental Studies.16 The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for Cohort Studies was used for observational studies with multiple assessment time points (eg, retrospective cohort studies, secondary analyses of RCTs, and descriptive studies with multiple assessment timepoints).17 Finally, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for Cross-Sectional Studies was used for observational studies with single assessment timepoints (eg, cross-sectional studies).17 Additional details of each quality assessment tool, including descriptions, definitions, and grading values are available (Online Data Supplement 4).

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Analyses were conducted in Stata (StataCorp, 2009; Stata Statistical Software: Release 11; StataCorp LP). For studies in which certain data were missing or unclear, they were excluded from the pooled estimate. All included studies were eligible for the main synthesis, and no subgroup analyses were completed. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics with variability estimates (Online Data Supplement 3). Data visuals were created in Stata from summary estimates previously described. A meta-analysis was not performed because it was not compatible with the aims of the study given the prohibitive heterogeneity in outcomes reported and reporting methods. Given there was no pooling of effect estimates, funnel plots were not generated to assess reporting bias. Our study aimed to summarize the use of PROMs, not their specific effect estimates, as such no certainty of evidence assessment was done. Pooled data, extraction forms, and analytical code is not publicly available, but may be provided based on reasonable request.

Included Studies

A total of 4443 abstracts were screened after duplicates were removed, followed by full-text review of 1013 articles (Figure 1). The most common exclusion reasons at full-text screening included mixed-eligibility samples (n = 81, 23.5%), duplicate PROM data (n = 52, 15.1%), and nonpeer-reviewed or secondary literature (n = 34, 9.9%). Study Participants

Among the 668 included studies, a total of 229,392 patients were included (Table 1). The mean sample size was 343 (SD ± 693), with mean age of 64.0 years (SD ± 8.0 years), and mean proportion of male patients of 83.0% (SD ± 17.0%). Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgeries were the most frequently reported, representing 67.4% (n = 450) of included studies, followed by combined CABG/valve surgeries (n = 61, 9.1%), and isolated valve surgeries (n = 60, 9.0%). Only 5.1% of studies (n = 34) reported using minimally invasive surgical techniques.

Traditional Outcomes

Traditional postoperative outcomes were variably reported with 43.4% (n = 290) reporting mortality, 20.2% (n = 135) reporting hospital length of stay, and 16.9% (n = 113) reporting intensive care unit length of stay. Only 9.1% (n = 61) reported readmission and 4.6% (n = 31) reported discharge disposition (Table 2). Forty-four percent (n = 291) reported any postoperative complication. Outcome Measurement and Reporting

Baseline scores were reported in 57.3% (n = 383) of studies. Of these, 49.4% (n = 329) were collected preoperatively and 8.1% (n = 54) were collected postoperatively. The median number of follow-up time points was 2 (interquartile range, 1-3), ranging from 0 to 14. Common follow-up intervals included 1 to 2 months (17%), 2 to 4 months (18%), 6 to 12 months (27%), and 1 to 2 years (29%) (Figure 2).Figure 2Proportion of studies reporting patient-reported outcome measurements at various assessment time points.

Proportion of studies reporting patient-reported outcome measurements at various assessment time points.

Most PROM tools (91.8%, n = 613) were patient-reported, and a small number (2.1%, n = 14) involved proxy respondents (eg, family). The mode of administration was not reported in 50.3% (n = 336) of studies. Among studies that reported administration methods, the most common were paper-based (22.2%, n = 148), mixed modes (10.5%, n = 70), telephone (9.3%, n = 62), and in-person interviews (7.6%, n = 51).

Discussion

This systematic review represents a comprehensive analysis regarding the use of PROM tools within the cardiac surgery literature. The results highlight the wide range of tools that have been used, as well as inconsistent assessment approach and results reporting. Not only does this prohibit comparison of results across studies (either informally or through meta-analysis techniques), but it identifies areas for improvement to standardize incorporation of PROMs into cardiac surgical clinical practice, quality improvement, and research.

Although the SF-36 tool is commonly used, it still only accounts for less than 25% of the total with the second most used (European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions) being less than 10%. In total, 341 unique PROM tools were identified. All PROM tools are not the same. Some are disease specific, such as the Seattle Angina Questionnaire.18 Others may be disease agnostic but focused on a single domain, such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale.19 Finally, even within the disease nonspecific multidomain PROM tools there can be different points of emphasis, for example quality of life (eg, SF-36) versus disability (eg, World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0).20,21 The majority of publications used a single-domain PROM tool. Of those with multiple domains, certain domains are preferentially represented (eg psychological and physical) compared to others that may be meaningful to patients (eg, return of roles or cognition).3,22 In addition, there is wide variation in the time intervals when the questionnaires are being administered. The most frequently reported time interval (1-2 years) was reported in only 30% of studies, and the recommended assessment points of 30 and 90 days postoperatively were only reported in 17% and 18% of studies, respectively.23 Variability in PROM tool assessment strategy may allow for improved alignment with a particular study population, planned intervention, or outcome of interest.23,24 Although this could be valuable in specific research situations, it would be beneficial for the cardiac surgical community to achieve consensus on use of a smaller group of PROM tools, with comprehensive inclusion of multiple domains, and fixed assessment intervals for reporting of data in established registries and future major clinical trials.10,11

In 2013, an extension for use of PROMs was added to the existing Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT PRO).24 Although intended for prospective randomized clinical trials, the recommendations have broader relevance as a framework for properly using these tools across the spectrum of potential applications, both clinical and research related. Consensus recommendations include use of a validated tool, rationale for its use, details on questionnaire completion (including response rates), and reporting of domain/element raw values. The publications included in our analysis used a validated tool 93% of the time (with 97% reporting use as it was originally validated), self-reporting method was described 94% of the time, and response rates were greater than 80% in the majority of studies. However, only 50% of studies reported the mode of PROM tool administration and although 73.1% reported domain scores this was largely driven by single-domain tools with only 14.5% reporting the raw-value scores for each element of the questionnaire. Finally, the CONSORT PRO guidance emphasizes that baseline data to be collected and reported. This is particularly important in PROMs, where socioeconomic status, cultural differences and ethnicity have been shown to effect scores, sometimes as much as the medical condition.25, 26, 27 For cardiac surgery, it could be additionally valuable given anticipated differences in baseline quality of life and function between patients who are symptomatic or asymptomatic from their cardiac disease. In our review, 42.5% of studies had no baseline data which limits the analysis of interventional effect.

Our review included methodologic quality assessment. For RCTs, most scored well overall, but had weaknesses in allocation concealment, participant blinding, intervention blinding, and outcome assessor blinding. Quasi-experimental studies primary areas of weakness were dissimilarity of groups at baseline and inadequate follow-up. Observational studies with multiple assessment timepoints demonstrated key limitations such as poor representativeness of the cardiac surgery population (ie, included only one type of cardiac surgery, such as CABG), and limited comparability of cohorts (ie, did not control for confounding factors). Finally, observational studies with single assessment timepoints also included limited representativeness of the cardiac surgery population as well as sample sizes that were unsatisfactory or insufficiently justified.

There has been recent interest in concepts related to recovery, or rescue, following postoperative complications. Failure to rescue has been defined as failure to prevent postoperative mortality after the occurrence of 1 or more the following 4 major complications: prolonged ventilation, stroke, renal failure, and reoperation.28 Although this addresses the immediate period of rescue from mortality related to these complications, it does not measure the long-term effects of quality of life or a failure to recover. Negative impacts on patient-reported physical and mental health, as well as daily function, persist up to 1 year after prolonged ventilation, renal failure, reoperation, stroke, or deep sternal wound infection.2,29 Minimal clinically important differences have recently been identified in 2 common cardiac surgical PROM tools that relate to heart failure (New York Heart Association) and angina (Canadian Cardiovascular Society) symptoms, while also being associated with 1-year mortality after coronary surgery.30 Therefore, PROMs should supplement, not replace, current standard outcome measures. Importantly, both should be routinely reported so that associations can be better understood between the two. In our review, the complications linked to failure to rescue were reported between 4.8% and 22.8% of the time, mortality was reported in 43.5% of studies, and hospital length of stay and readmission was only reported 20.1% and 9.2% respectively. Furthermore, only 13.7% of studies attempted any comparison or correlation between PROMs and traditional outcomes in their analysis.

Several barriers have been identified in the incorporation of PROMs into clinical practice and research, particularly in clinical trials. In the 24-year period our systematic review covered there were only 129 RCTs among the thousands of trials that would have been conducted in that time, with even fewer using a PROM as the primary outcome (n = 71 or 11% of all included studies). Despite growing prioritization of patient-centered care, there also remains hesitation to move beyond traditional outcomes when measuring quality and value metrics. This may be resolved with more consistent reporting of quality-adjusted life year, Need-Based Model of Quality of Life, or similar measures of cost-effectiveness linked to PROMs.8 Additional challenges include increased cost and complexity for implementation and sustainability of regular PROM assessments, lack of universal interpretation guidelines or definitions of meaningful differences, need for incorporation into existing electronic medical records, and inconsistent patient response rates.31,32 Although significant, these barriers are not themselves without solutions and will likely involve leveraging emerging health care technology and software. Overall, the motivation for overcoming barriers will be greater if the methodology of using and reporting PROMs in the cardiac surgical literature can be standardized to improve the effectiveness and value of these tools.

The results of this review should be interpreted in context of several limitations. First, we used search filters including exclusion of non-English studies and studies published before the year 2000, which may have excluded potentially relevant studies. Second, although we designed our online databases searches to be inclusive and comprehensive, eligible gray literature studies may have been missed. Third, because of the exclusion of qualitative studies limits insights into patient experiences not captured by quantitative PROM tools. Fourth, due to the objective of the review and the heterogeneity in PROM tool, assessment timing, and reporting, we could not perform a meta-analysis. Finally, data extraction for a subset of studies was completed by a single reviewer after consensus and consistency was established, which introduces a risk of error.

Conclusions

Patient-reported outcomes assist in quantifying the impacts of surgery on the postoperative lives and function of patients. They are already incorporated to some degree into cardiac surgical practice and have been identified as a priority for more consistent use. To achieve the maximum benefit and optimize patient recovery outcomes, they must be applied in a more consistent and standardized fashion both clinically and as a research outcome. Consensus on a specific PROM tool (or bundle of tools) that measures quality of recovery holistically, as well as baseline measurements and predefined postoperative assessment intervals, will be critical. Concurrent collection of standard outcomes such as mortality, lengths of stay, and major complications will be additionally important. Finally, if individual domain or element raw values can be reported, even as supplemental data, then more options for interstudy data analysis will be possible.
