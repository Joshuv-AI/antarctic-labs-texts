Exploring change mechanisms of implementation strategies for providing guideline-adherent care by interprofessional teams in inpatient settings: a realist synthesis
Authors: Megan M Coe, Kyla F Woodward, Sarah Gimbel, Ferdinand C Mukumbang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWell-designed guideline implementation tools – such as job aids and documentation charts – can facilitate adherence to clinical practice guidelines among health workers and accelerate improvements in quality of care and patient outcomes. However, the mechanisms through which these tools and participatory design processes influence guideline adherence in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) hospitals remain underexplored. This realist synthesis aims to refine a theory explaining how and why these strategies work, and in what contexts, by identifying evidence to support, refute, or expand upon hypothesized explanations in an initial program theory.MethodsWe conducted a realist synthesis, a theory-driven interpretive approach to secondary research integrating empirical data with realist philosophical interpretation. This realist synthesis was conducted in five steps: (1) clarify scope, (2) search for evidence, (3) appraise studies and extract data, (4) synthesize evidence and draw conclusions, and (5) disseminate, implement, and evaluate. A program theory was developed to explain a pilot project to improve adherence to existing guidelines for inpatient care of malnourished children in Kenya. Context, strategy, mechanism, outcome (CSMO) configurations are the units of theory. Evidence was identified through a search of four databases. An appraisal of relevance, richness, and rigor determined inclusion in data extraction. We synthesized our findings to refine the program theory, resulting in a middle-range theory.ResultsWe screened 8697 titles and abstracts and reviewed 112 full-text manuscripts. Data were abstracted from 27 articles. The refined middle-range theory included eight context-strategy-mechanism-outcome configurations. Participatory processes—such as health worker engagement, user testing, and local consensus processes—were found to generate mechanisms including buy-in, tool acceptability, and team identity. Guideline implementation tools contributed through mechanisms such as role clarity, reminders, improved competence, and enhanced communication. Job aid posters and user-friendly documentation tools were particularly valued for making information accessible and supporting patient monitoring. The findings highlight synergies between participatory processes and guideline implementation tools, especially in promoting teamwork.ConclusionsOur middle-range theory describes the how, why, and in what contexts implementation strategies involving participatory processes and guideline implementation tools support clinical guideline adherence among health workers in LMIC hospitals. This refined theory can be utilized to guide implementation efforts, supporting selection and design of implementation strategies that align to context and trigger the generative mechanisms that produce results.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-026-01517-z.Keywords: Clinical practice guidelines, Guideline implementation tools, Participatory, Teamwork, LMIC, Hospital, Realist synthesis

• Outcomes of guideline implementation strategies vary; in this study we develop a mechanism-based middle-range theory that explains how and why strategies delivered in specific contexts contribute to guideline adherence by health workers.

• This theory-driven approach provides insight into which strategies should be prioritized in different contexts to optimize implementation outcomes.

• We focus on implementation of malnutrition guidelines in inpatient hospital care of children in LMICs. Few reviews of guideline implementation strategies have addressed this unique context.

• Our findings contribute new evidence of synergies when implementation strategies incorporate group decision-making activities alongside teamwork skill resources, emphasizing the critical nature of teamwork in guideline implementation.

Background

Clinical practice guidelines compile evidence-based recommendations for use by health workers and are produced and distributed by international organizations, national governments and associations, and local facilities. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) these are often based on corresponding guidelines developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. Guidelines have been credited with improving quality and safety in health care by speeding the translation of evidence into practice and reducing variation [2]. While there has been a proliferation of these guidelines around the world, their implementation has lagged behind, leading some to question their value [3, 4]. Consequently, implementation strategies that support the incorporation of guidelines into practice are needed to improve adherence to guidelines by health workers and generate the intended improvements in care [5, 6]. While a wide variety of implementation strategies can support guideline implementation, this review is focused on two types of strategies – guideline implementation tools and participatory processes.

Guideline implementation tools are resources that accompany a guideline and facilitate its implementation into practice [7]. Examples of these tools include decision-support aids, training materials, guidance for monitoring, documentation forms, and guideline summaries. These tools can help overcome a primary barrier to guideline use, poor formatting that makes guidelines hard for health workers to use [8]. A Cochrane review of guideline implementation tools found that they produced a 13.5% increase in guideline adherence, however only four short term studies conducted in high-income countries were included in the review [9].

Participatory processes engage frontline health workers individually or in groups in the process of developing and implementing guidelines and associated implementation tools. Human-centered design (HCD) approaches, such as codesign and user testing, are increasingly integrated in global health and implementation research as a method to support innovation, usability, and acceptability of guidelines and implementation tools for health workers [10, 11]. For example, an HCD approach was used to redesign the inpatient documentation chart for neonatal care in Kenya to address the needs of doctors and nurses providing care [12]. Moreover, participatory processes include efforts to engage health workers in local consensus discussions, quality improvement initiatives, and other activities.

Kislov encourages implementation scientists to contribute to the ongoing process of theory development, achieved by moving back and forth across a continuum of empirical findings, program theory (PT), middle-range theory (MRT), and grand theory [13]. A program theory is developed for a specific program, describing how each intervention component is hypothesized to contribute to the outcome [14, 15], while grand theories explain broadly generalizable social constructions [16] Middle-range theories are positioned between these, with a moderate level of abstraction, “not abstract to the point of being disconnected from the on-the-ground workings of programs, yet not so specific to pertain to one program” ([17] p.364). MRTs are grounded in data to permit empirical testing and conceptualized enough to provide transferrable lessons [13]. Development and application of theory has been particularly lacking in LMICs [18], where realist approaches have been suggested as a tool to address complexities present in these settings [19]. The realist mechanism-based approach to theory development is particularly suited to bridge the gap between context and generalizability in describing how implementation strategies work [20].

In this realist synthesis, we aimed to explain the mechanisms through which participatory processes used to develop guideline implementation tools contribute to guideline adherence in hospitals, using our findings to formulate an MRT. We developed this study to expand upon a program theory developed by our research team for a specific guideline implementation effort to optimize hospital care for children with severe acute malnutrition in Kenya. Importantly, we sought to differentiate mechanisms generated by the guideline implementation tools themselves from those generated by participatory processes used to develop those tools. Understanding the relationship between these is needed to inform dissemination of guideline implementation tools after initial development, when participatory processes may be scaled back or eliminated. With evidence from reports of implementing guidelines from hospital settings across LMICs, the resulting MRT can inform the future development and scale-up of guideline implementation strategies.

Methods

Realist synthesis is an approach to secondary research that integrates empirical data with realist philosophical interpretation to inform theory refinement [17]. Realist research methodologies, including realist synthesis, offer an approach to identifying and understanding the causal mechanisms responsible for how implementation strategies work, for whom, and why. Realist synthesis can be used to explain how interventions or strategies work when introduced in particular context condition by triggering mechanisms that generate an outcome [21]. Specifically, realist theories are structured using configurations that explain how features of the context interact with an implementation strategy, generating the mechanisms that produce an outcome [14]. These explanations are typically described in the form of context-mechanism-outcome configurations, which are hypothesized and testable units of theory [22]. We added the implementation strategy to the configuration – making it a context-strategy-mechanism-outcome (CSMO) configuration – which explicates how an implementation strategy (S) interacts with a particular context (C) and how it produces observed implementation outcomes (O) through the generative mechanisms (M) it triggers [16]. Unearthing the mechanisms of implementation strategies in a way that remains linked to the nuances of the context offers an avenue to overcome the frequently cited challenge that scaling up implementation produces disparate results across contexts [23]. Some of these challenges include intervention transferability and adaptation failures owing to contextual misalignment. Mechanism-based middle-range explanations enhance evidence transferability by re-integrating different but related causal elements from different study settings into the larger conceptual picture [16, 24].

This realist synthesis utilized a five-step process (Fig. 1) described by Mukumbang and colleagues [25] that was adapted from methods described by earlier researchers [26, 27]. The steps in this method are not firmly linear, particularly in steps two through four where an iterative process is expected to identify the most relevant literature. The protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD42023474744). The Realist And MEta-narrative Evidence Syntheses: Evolving Standards (RAMSES) publication standards guided manuscript preparation (Additional file 1) [28].

5-step realist synthesis process

Preliminary study

This realist synthesis was designed to take a program theory and findings from the Team Approach to Malnutrition Services (TeAMS) Toolkit Pilot Study and integrate them with evidence from guideline implementation efforts across LMIC settings. A formative qualitative study of barriers and facilitators to guideline adherence guided by the Theoretical Domains Framework revealed that health workers in this setting relied on nutritionists to handle feeding tasks, delaying guideline adherent care when they were not available (especially at nights/weekends) [29]. The TeAMS Toolkit Pilot Study sought to address this challenge at a county hospital in western Kenya and improve adherence to existing guidelines for inpatient care of malnourished children through a theory-driven participatory co-design process to develop a set of guideline implementation tools, informed by the Behavior Change Wheel approach [30, 31]. The TeAMS Toolkit included four guideline implementation tools: descriptions of health worker roles and responsibilities, educational content on teamwork skills, job aids that simplify treatment algorithms, and a new bedside documentation chart. A theory of change diagram was developed early in the study planning to hypothesize how the design process and each implementation strategy included in the TeAMS Toolkit would contribute to the objective.

Step 1: clarify scope

We used the theory of change from the TeAMS Toolkit study to develop the mechanism-based theories. Theory development in realist synthesis is guided by retroductive theorizing, which is concerned with building mechanism-based theories or explanatory models [32]. It involves conceiving explanations based primarily on identifying the relevant context conditions that trigger or suppress the actions of mechanisms to cause an outcome. Our retroductive process entailed asking the questions ‘how does this mechanism (M) cause this outcome (O)?’ and ‘what it is about this contextual factor (C) that trigger or drown the mechanism?’ This realist synthesis aimed to refine the program theory developed in conjunction with the TeAMS Toolkit pilot study to support pediatric treatment guideline adherence among health workers in a hospital setting in Kenya [30]. The initial program theory (IPT) underwent repeated reviews by research team members. Suggested edits were discussed in team meetings and revisions made.

Step 2: search for evidence

The search strategy identified literature that was relevant to the topic and able to contribute to theory refinement. Items addressed (1) implementation or quality improvement related to (2) clinical practice guidelines by health workers in (3) LMICs. These portions of the search strategy were informed by published search strategies for LMICs [33, 34] and implementation strategies for guidelines [5, 6]. To identify items relevant to theory refinement, articles also had to contain at least one term specific to the context, strategy, or mechanisms components of the IPT, e.g. competence, design, buy-in, role clarity (Additional file 2). We included a broad range of evidence types, including peer-reviewed research articles and reports of quality improvement (QI) activities. Reference lists of literature reviews on relevant topics were screened to identify records not identified in our search. Reports published from January 1, 2010 through December 31, 2023 were included and LMICs were defined by World Bank classifications during that time [35].

A set of 11 articles expected to be found by the search was developed and used to test and refine the search strategy, first in PubMed and then following translation to three additional databases (CINAHL, EBSCO Global Health, and SciELO). Using the refined search strategy searches were conducted on February 27, 2024. With support from a research librarian, the references from the EBSCO Global Health database were deduplicated using a process that uses combinations of fields other than digital object identifiers (DOIs) to locate duplicates [36]. The deduplicated EBSCO GH results along with the full results from other databases were imported to Covidence and underwent automated deduplication [37].

Step 3: appraise studies and extract data

Appraisal of the identified studies was conducted in an iterative process guided by three criteria – relevance, richness, and rigor [38]. Relevance was considered in relation to both the review topic and to the goal of theory development, while richness encompasses both contextual thickness (detailed description of the context or intervention) and conceptual richness (theoretical explanation of how the intervention worked) [39]. Due to the variety of data types included in realist syntheses, determining standard criteria to determine rigor is difficult, as traditional hierarchies of evidence are not appropriate. We appraised rigor by evaluating trustworthiness of the evidence (credibility of the source, appropriate use of methods, and potential for bias) and the coherence of theory presented.

Initial criteria for relevance were that that the study was (1) conducted in an LMIC and (2) addressed guideline implementation by health workers. Studies meeting these criteria were assessed on three additional dimensions of relevance - Clinical Context, Implementation Strategy, and Implementation Outcome (Table 1), which were scored individually (0–2) and added together to develop the aggregate relevance score. During full text review, an overall appraisal rating (none, low, moderate, high) was given, addressing the richness and rigor of the article and the contributions it can make to development of our theory (Table 2). This appraisal rating was adapted from Hunter et al. [40].

Full-text appraisal rating scale

Titles and abstracts were screened and assigned a relevance score. Screening began with two reviewers (MMC and KFW), with regular consensus meetings to resolve discrepancies and clarify screening criteria. After 25% of the records were screened by both reviewers, there was substantial agreement between the reviewers (k = 0.78) and the remainder were reviewed by a single reviewer. An iterative process was used to prioritize records for full-text review based on the relevance score [41]. Full text review began with studies with an aggregate relevance score of 6, followed by those with aggregate scores of 4 or 5, but with the maximum score (2) on the context or implementation strategy dimensions, as these were more likely to include the detail needed to contribute to theory refinement. An initial set of thirty articles were appraised by two reviewers (MMC and KFW), with consensus discussions conducted to clarify appraisal criteria, and the remainder appraised by a single reviewer (MMC). Throughout this process, records not in English were translated using Google Translate.

Data were extracted from each item with an overall appraisal rating of “high” in full text review. The extracted elements (Additional file 3) include information about the context, guideline, implementation strategy, and outcomes. Implementation strategy details were extracted based on guidance for naming, defining, and specifying these activities [42] and were categorized using conceptual clusters of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) Taxonomy strategies [43]. Moreover, detailed descriptions of findings relevant to each CSMO configuration in the IPT were extracted from the full text.

Step 4: synthesize evidence and draw conclusions

Descriptives statistics of characteristics of included studies were calculated using proportions and ranges. The extracted data were synthesized and analyzed to test the hypothesized CSMO configurations in the IPT. To further develop an understanding of the generative mechanisms, explanatory models were formed through retroductive theorizing [44], identifying complete or partial context-strategy-mechanism-outcome combinations in the evidence relevant to the implementation strategies in question. Combinations of CSMO elements identified in the evidence were used to distinguish areas where the evidence confirms or refutes the theory, termed abductive thinking. Our abductive process was achieved through memoing, describing the alignment between each piece of evidence and each CSMO configuration in the IPT, and then collating this by CSMO configuration and analyzing the findings across studies [45]. Our abductive process also involved using our dataset to identify constructs missing from the IPT to inform expansion of the theory. The theory was refined based on this evidence.

Step 5: disseminate, implement, and evaluate

Dissemination will aim to reach both researchers and practitioners, with results shared through presentations at local and international scientific meetings, international peer-reviewed journals, and through briefs to other stakeholders. This step will commence following publication of the theory and is not discussed in the manuscript results.

Results

Step 1: clarify scope

In preparation for this realist synthesis, the preliminary pilot study theory of change was restructured and detail added to form an IPT with realist CSMO configurations [20]. Following refinement by ten research team members, including clinical researchers based in Kenya, the IPT was finalized (Table 3). It includes 10 CSMO configurations grouped by the two types of implementation strategies included in the TeAMS Toolkit Pilot study, the participatory process (design process) and the resulting set of guideline implementation tools (TeAMS Toolkit). The CSMO configurations in the IPT converge in some areas, with multiple context and strategy combinations triggering the same mechanism, while in other areas they diverge, with the same strategy activating different mechanisms depending on the context.

Steps 2 and 3: search for evidence, appraise studies, and extract data

A search for evidence in four databases identified 10,883 records, which was reduced to 8,697 through deduplication (Fig. 2). The title and abstract review excluded 7,405 records, 6,515 that were either not in an LMIC or not related to guideline implementation and 890 with a relevance score less than four. Due to the large number of studies remaining, they were prioritized based on their relevance scores and the full texts of 112 articles were reviewed, with data extraction from 27 articles with an appraisal rating of high. Step 4: synthesize evidence and draw conclusions

Two-thirds of the 27 included studies were conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa, including three in Kenya (Table 4). The guidelines being implemented included those for severe acute malnutrition (SAM, 6), intrapartum care (6), safe surgery (4), and other conditions. These guidelines were most often created by the WHO (16) or national health ministries (7), with many of these national guidelines based upon WHO recommendations. Over half (15 of 27) of the studies collected mixed methods data, while the remainder were split equally between qualitative and quantitative data collection (6 studies each). The implementation strategies described typically included multiple components across ERIC clusters, with the most common types of strategies those that support clinicians (17 of 27) or train and educate stakeholders (13 of 27).

Participatory process

The theory explores three strategies that may be incorporated in participatory processes (a) health worker engagement, (b) user testing of tools, and (c) local consensus processes. Five CSMO configurations in the IPT (1–5) describe how these strategies contribute to the use of guideline implementation tools and delivery of guideline adherent care. After refinement, one configuration was eliminated due to lack of evidence, leaving four refined configurations in the MRT (1a-1d). Studies that contributed to refining the participatory process CSMOs described activities influenced by human-centered design methods [57, 64, 65], quality improvement [48, 49, 63], and evidence based practice [50, 70].

Health worker engagement (IPT 1; MRT 1a)

We hypothesized that, if health workers feel incompetent and do not have access to other information sources (C), engaging them in a design process to develop guideline tools (S) would generate buy-in (M) and make them more likely to use tools developed (O). Engagement was considered broadly to include any activities that involved front line health workers in needs assessment, design, or implementation planning of guidelines. In the 14 articles addressing this configuration [4, 8, 30, 46, 48, 49, 51, 53, 55, 57, 59, 60, 64, 68], evidence of the strategy-mechanism link between engagement and buy-in was extensive (10 of 14) [4, 8, 46, 48, 51, 53, 55, 57, 64, 68]. Seven articles commented on the ownership, buy-in, and motivation generated by engagement strategies [46, 48, 53, 55, 57, 64, 68]. Three others pointed to a lack of involvement among staff in the design of strategies as a barrier that hindered their buy-in and use of the tools, which also aligns with this strategy-mechanism link [4, 8, 51].

We then analyzed if studies discussed the contribution of the context features of health workers feeling incompetent or lacking access to information sources. One study of a mobile guideline app development process highlighted the limited accessibility of decision support tools as an important driver of interest in using the new tool [64]. During the TeAMS Toolkit Pilot, several health workers described engagement in the design activities boosting their excitement about the Toolkit, but others said that a brief survey was adequate for them to feel engaged, and some who did not engage in the design process were fully bought in to the importance of the Toolkit [30]. This evidence indicates that when there is strong perceived need due to a lack of access, perceived lack of competence, or other factors, individuals may buy-in to the use of a tool without any additional strategies to engage them. Meanwhile, four studies identified that the lack of a perceived need or benefit from the tool was a barrier to success [51, 59, 60, 68]. Therefore, strategies that actively engage individuals who do not already perceive a need in a process that both demonstrates the necessity of tools and involves them in developing relevant tools may generate buy-in. We refined this configuration to specify that it is in this context, as opposed to when a strong need is clear, that participatory engagement is more likely to trigger buy-in.

The data also revealed that participatory engagement was not the only pathway to buy-in among health workers. In an initiative to promote hand hygiene across 50 private hospitals in South Africa, buy-in among staff was developed, but not through the pathway in this CSMO, as washing hands is not a task hindered by incompetence and there was little engagement of front-line staff in the design of the initiative [49]. Instead, in this and other studies, prioritization of implementation by health system leaders (county, national, multi facility network) and/or facility leaders (department, hospital) played an important role in generating buy-in among health workers [48, 49, 51, 57]. This prioritization by leadership is synergistic with participatory engagement in generating buy-in to and eventual use of the guideline implementation tools.

User-testing of tools (IPT 2; MRT 1b)

Guidelines and implementation tools are designed to clarify care recommendations, but health workers often find that they do not achieve that goal [8]. User-testing is a common HCD approach that can address this, ensuring that the needs of intended users are met [10]. We hypothesized that when implementation of guidelines is suboptimal and the length and complexity of the guideline create barriers to adoption (C), a strategy that addresses usability problems and refines the tool based on feedback from target users will improve acceptability and feasibility of the tools (M). Twelve studies addressed this theory [4, 8, 30, 47, 53, 56, 57, 60, 62, 64, 65, 69]. A review of guideline development and implementation in Uganda identified that there was no systematic method of pre-testing national guidelines with target users [4]. This configuration was strongly supported by nine sources [8, 30, 47, 53, 57, 62, 64, 65, 69]. Two others suggested that user testing would have improved implementation had it been included in their strategies [56, 60]. A study of reproductive health guideline use in Ghana found that modifying national and international guidelines to match the local context and to provide information in wall charts resulted in 88% of health workers finding them easier to use than the original format [62].

Based on this evidence, we did not make substantive changes to the configuration. For clarity, we did remove one element from the context (sub-optimal implementation of guidelines), as it was an indicator of the outcome.

Local consensus processes (IPT 3, 4, 5; MRT 1c-1d)

Three CSMOs in the IPT relate to the role of participatory local consensus processes, which give healthcare teams an opportunity to agree on treatment plans and develop collaborative relationships. We hypothesized that in different contexts this strategy would generate different mechanisms and outcomes. This set of CSMO configurations was addressed in 16 sources [4, 30, 46, 48, 50–55, 57, 59, 60, 63, 69, 70].

Specific features of local consensus process strategies may make them more effective. Two studies highlighted that including specific stakeholders (e.g. hospital management representatives) was a facilitator [48, 55], while another mentioned that meetings were ineffective when key staff did not attend [51]. Participants in a study that structured meetings around the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle found that these meetings were different from what they had experienced in the past, with more active engagement as opposed to passive reporting [48].

We hypothesized that local consensus processes could overcome challenges with feasibility, as well as discrepancies in guidelines by establishing a clear plan for health workers to follow (IPT 3). There is evidence from three countries and various conditions that discrepancies exist between guidelines and even within the same guideline [4, 53, 57]. Group decision making can help teams come to agreement on a unified plan of action, achieving a common goal among health workers [48, 50, 52, 53, 55, 57, 59, 69, 70]. When feasibility of the guideline was an important challenge, group processes that established how treatments would be operationalized were highlighted [53]. Moreover, in the contexts of the lack of a guideline [50, 70] and lack of communication between hospital departments [52] local consensus processes successfully resulted in unified treatment plans.

We also hypothesized that these local consensus processes could contribute to positive team perceptions (IPT 5), which was supported by three studies [30, 46, 54]. A study of the use of partographs in India described coordination between doctors and nurses developing through engagement in QI teams [46]. In Tanzania, the Surgical Safety Checklist was introduced at 40 hospitals through a strategy of first providing training on leadership, QI, and teamwork skills to a multidisciplinary team from each hospital, before an additional technical training to the same group on the guideline [54]. These teams were able to initiate use of the checklist within their facilities, with utilization in over 90% of cases. The TeAMS Toolkit study supported this portion of the theory, with participants associating the improved team collaboration they observed with the design process that engaged them early on [30].

There is also support for IPT 5 in the examples of poor teamwork that are attributed to a lack of consensus-building among health workers [51, 60, 63]. However, there are cases when a local consensus process may not generate enhanced team perceptions. In large facilities, group decision-making processes may involve a smaller proportion of the staff, making these activities less effective in generating a strong team identity compared to when they take place in small facilities [54].

In the literature, additional mechanisms related to team functioning were reported. Findings included the phenomenon where ongoing engagement by teams developed both trust and teamwork skills among their users [48]. Separately, it was found that active facility-level teams were better able to overcome barriers as they arose [52] and that the use of checklists led to increased team communication, further improving team functioning [63]. Together, these point to a positive feedback loop at work in these teams.

During the TeAMS Toolkit Pilot, some challenges in reaching consensus appeared to be driven by resistance to change among individuals who had been trained as guideline trainers. We included a CSMO in the IPT describing a hypothesized mechanism for this observation (IPT 4). Within the final TeAMS Toolkit evaluation this challenge was not brought up by participants [30], and we did not identify any other evidence supporting this configuration. Regardless of their position as trainers, it has been noted that individuals resisting change can hinder implementation overall, but not specifically through the mechanism of resistance to adaptation in consensus-building processes [51]. Therefore, this CSMO was removed from the MRT.

Guideline implementation tools

This portion of the theory explores four types of guideline implementation tools (a) clarification of roles and responsibilities, (b) educational content on teamwork skills, (c) job aids, and (d) guideline-aligned documentation tools. The IPT contained five CSMO configurations (IPT 6–10) describing how these strategies support health worker adherence to guidelines. Four refined configurations remain in the MRT (2a-2d) after one CSMO configuration (IPT 7) was eliminated based on the synthesis of evidence.

Roles and responsibilities (IPT 6, 7; MRT 2a)

Two CSMO configurations explained how a guideline implementation tool providing a description of team roles and responsibilities accounting for urgency and role overlap would improve guideline adherence. We found little evidence of specific approaches to presenting team member roles, with only five sources addressing these configurations [30, 55, 61, 67, 70]. To prevent delays in feeding, the TeAMS Toolkit contained content related to roles and responsibilities that provided role clarity while also allowing for role overlap between cadres of health workers. This approach was successful in generating an expectation among non-nutritionists that they must contribute to feeding children with SAM when a nutritionist is not present [30]. We did not identify any other reports of tools that specifically addressed role clarity where overlap in roles is needed, however we believe this is an underrecognized and understudied phenomenon and so retained this CSMO configuration in our refined theory. We identified other strategies that improved role clarity more generally and contributed to understanding how role perceptions impact adherence to guidelines. In South Africa, as part of broader ongoing efforts to improve care for children with SAM, the national Department of Health highlighted a clear and simple message to all clinicians, that within 30 minutes of arrival these children must be started on antibiotics, fed, and kept warm [55]. This directive set a very simple expectation and included a timeframe for action. In China, the development of a new procedure for nurses to offer nutritional assessment and treatment to patients with cancer-treatment related oral mucositis created role clarity and there was improvement in completion of these tasks when audited [70]. Two studies that introduced new roles designed to support breastfeeding mothers within hospitals also provide important lessons. Following the introduction of a full time nurse to support breastfeeding in Malawi, other nurses stopped completing the tasks of breastfeeding education and assessing weight-for-length, and there were no statistically significant changes in adherence to the guideline, rate of weight gain, or inpatient mortality [67]. In Kenya, a breastfeeding peer supporter role was introduced and it was noted that the documentation tool they used contributed to clarifying the expectations of the role for these individuals and other members of the care team [61]. These studies demonstrated a need for approaches to role clarification that maintain overlap in roles and that there is a link between documentation expectations and role clarity.

Based on the evidence above, minor clarifications were made to IPT 6. On the other hand, we found no specific evidence for IPT 7, which explained how reinforcement of roles by facility leaders would improve guideline adherence. While nine of the included studies used multi-component implementation strategies that included monitoring or supervision [46, 47, 49–51, 55, 57, 59, 70], these were not tied to reinforcing specific roles and responsibilities but tended to look at implementation of the guideline more generally. This left no evidence supporting IPT 7, so the configuration was removed from the MRT.

Educational materials on teamwork skills (IPT 9; MRT 2b)

We hypothesized that educational material about teamwork skills may support collaboration among health workers. The TeAMS Toolkit contained a brief section with information about four teamwork skills: (a) communication, (b) team leadership, (c) situation monitoring, and (d) mutual support, based on the TeamSTEPPS curriculum [71]. While our evaluation did reveal improvement in some measures of team performance, participants primarily attributed these to the team engagement in the participatory design process and the overarching focus on teamwork, not on learning these skills from the educational materials [30]. Two studies highlighted the importance of developing teamwork skills through multidisciplinary training sessions for patients safety and quality improvement [51, 54]. Two additional studies described how teamwork skills were developed through group meetings, without formal training on these topics [48, 68]. These findings point to the importance of learning teamwork skills, and the varied approaches to achieving that goal. We retained this CSMO, as including this content in a guideline implementation tool may provide a visible display of teamwork as a priority and provide basic knowledge to health workers. However, having opportunities to practice those skills is also necessary to develop them, so we added this context condition in MRT 2b.

Job aid posters (IPT 8; MRT 2c)

When guidelines are inaccessible or complex, we hypothesized that posters, charts and other job aids that summarize treatment plans can help health workers operationalize guidelines. Seventeen included studies supported the hypothesized CSMO configuration [4, 8, 30, 47, 50, 54–58, 60–65, 68]. Four studies found a link between this strategy and an outcome of improved guideline use, without identifying a clear mechanism [50, 54, 57, 61]. Seven others highlighted that job aid posters worked by making the information clear and easily accessible, allowing health worker to build their knowledge and access the information when needed to feel competent in their treatment decisions [4, 8, 55, 56, 62, 64, 65]. Alone or in addition to other job aids, posters on the wall acted as a reminder to health workers [47, 55, 58, 65, 68]. These findings align with the outcomes in the TeAMS Toolkit pilot, where participants highly valued the job aid component of the Toolkit and identified both easy access to information and having a reminder on the wall as mechanisms supporting their use of the guideline [30]. A mechanism was added to MRT 2c to capture the aspect of the job aids being a reminder.

Three studies indicated that these tools were more likely to generate positive outcomes in the context of staff who do not feel competent [55, 60, 63], informing the addition of this context condition in MRT 2c. In a study that introduced the Safe Childbirth Checklist in a tertiary maternity hospital in Sri Lanka, there was moderate uptake of the guideline (used in 46% of patients with 71% of items completed) which was attributed in part to the high perceived competence at baseline among health workers [63]. Others pointed to the particular usefulness of the tools among health workers with low competence, such as those without formal training on the guideline [60] or who have recently joined a department [55].

Documentation tools (IPT 10; MRT 2d)

Our hypothesized IPT indicated that in cases where different professions currently document care in separate locations, providing a unified documentation tool aligned to the guideline would be quicker, easier, and serve as a reminder. Challenges arising from documentation that is completed by different types of health workers in different parts of patient records were described in a variety of settings [47, 58, 61]. Some reported a lack of clarity about treatment plans because different diagnoses were documented in different areas of the patient record [58, 61]. A study of partograph use in India identified that when these documentation tools were made available at the patient bedside it facilitated documentation in the moment and improved efficiency of completing documentation [46]. Others highlighted how making a documentation tool easy to find, whether at the bedside or in a conspicuous place in the patient record, made it easier for others to review and monitor progress [47, 56, 68]. These findings align with the TeAMS Toolkit study results that a bedside feeding chart allowed for quick reviews of progress and improved communication during patient handovers [30].

We identified additional features that make documentation tool strategies more likely to achieve the outcome and were added to MRT 2d – (a) being placed in a visible location, (b) avoiding duplication, and (4) being easy to use. An analogue to a bedside documentation chart was described in a study of Safe Surgical Checklist implementation, where a large chart for documenting surgical counts was drawn on the operating theater wall tiles in permanent marker and a dry-erase marker used to document them during each case [68]. The authors described the benefit of this approach in that it “allowed everyone to see the count and remain engaged with the process” ([68] p.6), so we edited MRT language from “at the bedside” to “visible”. Two studies reported that documentation tools introduced to support guideline adherence were difficult to complete or duplicated documentation required in other areas of the patient record [56, 61]. This duplication generated resistance from health workers [56] and would hinder intended efficiency gains. Therefore, we specified these strategy characteristics in MRT 2d.

Discussion

In this realist synthesis, we aimed to explain the causal mechanisms at work in an implementation strategy that used a participatory design process to develop guideline implementation tools. We developed an IPT and then tested that theory against evidence from other guideline implementation efforts in LMICs. The resulting MRT incorporates eight CSMO configurations explaining how both participatory processes and guideline implementation tools contribute to guideline adherence among health workers. Our discussion will focus on the following elements: ongoing theory development as a critical part of implementation science research, the synergies between participatory processes and guideline implementation tools in developing teamwork, and recommendations for designing guideline implementation tools to support guideline adherence.

While recommendations to use theories to guide implementation science research have become commonplace [13, 72, 73], they remain poorly utilized, especially in global health research [18]. Researchers are even less frequently engaged in the process of developing and refining these theories. When we view theories as approximations instead of finished products, we can use our research to develop, refine, and expand them, contributing to knowledge development and to improve implementation practice [13]. There is considerable debate around how to incorporate complexity and balance context and generalizability in implementation research [74]. Realist theories are particularly appropriate to addressing this challenge by focusing on the generative mechanism at play, which can be used by researchers and practitioners to identify which implementation strategies are best suited to a particular context [23, 24]. The MRT we presented explicates contexts, implementation strategies, and mechanisms that impact the provision of guideline-adherent care, offering a nuanced explanation. The propositions in the theory can guide the design, implementation, and evaluation of efforts to improve quality of hospital care through guideline implementation. It should not be considered static but should be used, studied empirically, and critiqued to continue to refine the causal explanation it provides. Studying the implementation of future efforts, with measures of each element of the CSMO configurations, would inform validation and further refinement of the theory.

Our theory addressed two broad types of implementation strategies – participatory processes and guideline implementation tools – that may be used to address guideline adherence. Teamwork skills are needed to implement guidelines effectively, so strategies to improve adherence to guidelines will benefit from attention to the development of teamwork skills among health workers [75]. Our findings identified important synergies between the participatory processes and guideline implementation tools promoting teamwork. This aligns with a systematic review showing that group problem solving activities were the most effective strategy to improve health worker practices in LMICs [76]. Developing teamwork skills within hospitals depends not only on acquiring knowledge but on having the opportunity to practice [77], and participatory processes that bring people together can create those opportunities [78]. The benefits of these participatory activities can therefore expand beyond the specific objectives of one implementation activity; they have the potential to enhance capacity for effective teamwork and promote an organizational culture that values teamwork.

Several recommendations for future tool development arise from the MRT explanations of how (through what mechanisms) specific guideline implementation tools can support guideline implementation. For example, the WHO Safe Childbirth Checklist details key actions that should be taken around the time of delivery and was studied in three included studies [56, 59, 63]. Several features of the guideline align with the MRT propositions and make it an example to emulate. First, it underwent field testing and refinement prior to launch [59]. In addition, an accompanying Implementation Guide walks through a process to engage, launch, and support implementation of this tool, addressing many of the strategies of our theory [79]. Finally, this guideline encourages users to customize the tool and makes recommendations for how to do that. This is different from many guidelines which are seen as regulations that must be implemented without adaptation to a specific context. An additional recommendation when developing guideline implementation tools is to carefully consider not only the content, but the format of these tools to ensure clarity and usability for the end user. Developers of guideline implementation tools can use the Guideline Language and Format Instrument to support development of high quality tools [80], but should augment this with user-testing to ensure clarity among target users.

This study had some limitations. Program theories are not intended to be all-encompassing, therefore we did not address every potential contributor to guideline implementation, instead focusing on the specific strategies addressed by our pilot study [81]. Implementation strategies that include ongoing monitoring of guideline adherence through internal supervision, external monitoring, coaching, audit and feedback, or other approaches are likely to support guideline implementation but are not addressed in this theory [76]. The included studies often employed multicomponent implementation strategies, making it difficult to disentangle the evidence aligned to specific strategy components. To address this, we attempted to link the CSMO elements based on qualitative responses and authors’ analyses in discussion sections, however other components of the strategies may have contributed to the outcomes observed in those studies. Moreover, several areas of the registered protocol were changed due to pragmatic concerns, including having a second reviewer for only a portion of title/abstract screening and full text reviews and stricter prioritization of the records that would undergo full text review. These changes are consistent with iterative realist synthesis methodology and did not significantly impact findings due to high inter-rater reliability during dual reviews.

Conclusions

This realist synthesis refines a program theory to develop an MRT describing how and why strategies incorporating participatory processes and guideline implementation tools work at hospitals in LMICs. The MRT highlights the contribution of both participatory processes and guideline implementation tools to support guideline implementation, through mechanisms including improved team identity, acceptability, competence, and reminders. This refined theory can guide implementation efforts that are intended to support guideline adherence through the selection and design of implementation strategies that align to context and trigger the generative mechanisms that produce sustained results.
