Electrophysiological data showing the acute alcohol effect on neuronal excitability in the murine posterior parietal cortex
Authors: Valentina Licheri, Belkis J Jacquez, C Fernando Valenzuela, Jonathan L Brigman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is well established that alcohol interacts with multiple molecular targets, including ligand- and voltage-gated ion channels and intracellular signal proteins, leading to molecular and cellular neuroadaptations in various brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellum [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite the extensive literature, little is known about the effects of alcohol in the posterior parietal cortex (PPC), a cortical region involved in multiple cognitive and executive functions [4,5]. Here, we report data showing the acute alcohol effects on neuronal excitability in pyramidal neurons and interneurons expressed in PPC. Briefly, whole-cell patch clamp recordings in current clamp mode were conducted in coronal brain slices containing PPC. Resting membrane potential, input resistance, action potential (AP) threshold, and frequency were assessed during baseline, following 10 minutes of bath application of alcohol 50 mM, and after 10 minutes of washout. The data show that alcohol negatively modulates the excitability of PPC at the cellular level, as we observed a significant decrease in input resistance and AP frequency, and an increase in rheobase in PPC interneurons. Furthermore, these effects persisted even after 10 minutes of washout, indicating sustained changes in neuronal excitability. Intriguingly, acute alcohol exposure did not alter the neuronal excitability in PPC pyramidal neurons. This set of electrophysiological data may be of interest to investigators who are studying how alcohol effects voltage-gated ion channels.Keywords: Alcohol, Neuronal excitability, Pyramidal neurons, Interneurons, Posterior parietal cortex

Data Description

Resting membrane potential, input resistance, rheobase, action potential (AP) threshold, and frequency were assessed during baseline and after 10 minutes of bath application of EtOH (50 mM), followed by 10 minutes of washout. Precisely, the AP frequency reported in the graphs was calculated by averaging the last three steps of depolarizing current (+ 200, +250, and +300 pA), which were chosen to provide a representative measure of overall excitability during the steady-state firing phase. Input resistance was calculated by measuring the membrane voltage changes in response to seven hyperpolarizing current steps (ranging from -300 pA to 0 pA) and applying Ohm’s law (V=IR), where the slope of the current-voltage relationship was used to calculate the input resistance value.

First, we evaluated the effects of alcohol on the neuronal excitability of PPC pyramidal neurons using a mixed-effect analysis, given the unequal number of recorded cells across experimental groups. The mixed-effects model analysis revealed that ten minutes of alcohol bath application (50 mM) and following washout did not significantly modulate the resting membrane potential (sex effect: F(1,19) = 0.05938, p = 0.8101, Hedges’g 0.146; exposure effect: F(2,35) = 2.962, p = 0.0648, Hedges’g alcohol exposure =0.382; Hedges’g washout = 0.098; interaction effect: F(2,35) = 0.5052, p = 0.6077) (Fig. 1A). The input resistance analysis revealed no significant main effects or interaction (sex effect: F(1,19) = 3.630, p = 0.0720, Hedges’g = -0.767; exposure effect: F(2,35) = 0.5305, p = 0.5930, Hedges’g alcohol exposure = 0.116, Hedges’s g washout = -0.266; interaction effect: F(2, 35) = 1.450, p =0.2483) (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the statistical analysis of the AP threshold did not find any significant main effects (sex effect: F(1,17) = 1.019, p = 0.3269, Hedges’g = -0.386; exposure effect: F(2,31) = 1.519, p = 0.2348, Hedges’g alcohol exposure = 0.046, Hedges’s washout = 0.289; interaction effect: F(2,31) = 2.480, p = 0.1002) (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, there were no significant difference in rheobase across the experimental groups (sex effect: F(1,17) = 1.909, p = 0.1849, Hedges’g =0.510; exposure effect: F(2,31) = 1.882, p = 0.1693, Hedges’g alcohol exposure = -0.511, Hedges’g washout = 0.192; interaction effect: F(2,31) = 1.909, p = 0.1849) (Fig. 1D). Also, no significant differences were observed in the AP frequency calculated considering the last three steps of depolarizing current (sex effect: F(1,17) = 0.5920, p = 0.4522, Hedges’g = -0.395; exposure effect: F(2,31) = 0.8949, p = 0.4189, Hedges’g alcohol exposure = 0.242, Hedges’s g washout = 0.124; interaction effect: F(2,31) = 0.7236, p = 0.4930) (Fig. 1E).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Animals

Female and male C57BL/6J mice (90 days old; n= 8 animals/group) (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) were housed in groups of 2 per cage at constant temperature and humidity under a reverse 12 hours light/dark cycle (lights off 08:00 hr) with ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory food. The animals were allowed to habituate to their housing environment at least one week before the start of the experiment.

Brain slice preparation

Female and male mice (female age: 106.3 ± 2.26 days, weight: 20.39 ± 0.37gr; male age: 105.5 ± 2.19 days; weight: 30.83 ± 0.49 gr) were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (250 mg/kg intraperitoneal) and intra-cardiac perfused with cold cutting solution containing in (mM): 92 N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG), 2.5 KCl, 10 MgSO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 Hepes, 25 Glucose, 2 Thiourea, 3 Na-Pyruvate, 5 Na-ascorbate (pH to 7.3-7.4 with HCl and bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2, adjusted osmolarity to 290-310 mOsm). The brain was rapidly removed from the skull and transferred for at least 2 min into an ice-cold cutting solution. Coronal sections (300 µm) containing PPC were cut with a Leica VT1000 plus vibratome (Leica Microsystems, Bannockburn, IL), and rapidly transferred to an incubation chamber held at 34 °C containing NMDG cutting solution and 2M NaCl were added in a step-wise fashion (250 µl at 0 min, 250 µl at 5 min, 500 µl at 10 min, 1 ml at 15 min, and 2 ml at 20 min) until a final concentration of 52 mM was reached to restore osmotic balance and improve tissue recovery gradually [6] Then, the slices were moved to recover in a holding chamber (model BSC-PC, Warner Instruments, Hamden, CT) with artificial cerebral spinal fluid (aCSF) containing in (mM): 92 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 20 HEPES, 2 Thiourea, 3 Na-Pyruvate, 5 Na-ascorbate, 2 CaCl2, and 1 MgSO4 (pH to 7.3-7.4 with a few drops of 1 M NaOH and bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2, (290-310 mOsm). Slices were then kept at room temperature (approximately 22°C) for 40-60 min before starting electrophysiological recordings.

Whole-cell recordings

After room-temperature incubation, a hemi-slice was transferred to a recording chamber with slice support (Cat. # RC-27L; Warner Instruments) to allow the flow of aCSF above and below the slice. The whole cell recordings were performed in presence of aCSF containing in (mM): 126 NaCl, 3 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 10 glucose, 2 CaCl2, and 1 MgSO4, 5 Na-ascorbate, osmolarity 290-310 mOsm, pH 7.4 bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 and delivered to the chamber at a flow rate of 2 ml/min using a peristaltic pump (Master Flex, model 7518-10, Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL). Temperature was maintained constant at 34 °C using a dual automatic temperature controller (Model TC-344B) that was also connected to an in-line solution heater (Model SH-27B) (Warner Instruments).

Cells located in layers V-VI of PPC were identified using an Olympus BX50WI upright microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA) equipped with infra-red differential interference contrast optics connected to a charge-coupled device camera (CCD100, DAGE-MTI, Michigan City, IN). Glass pipettes were prepared from filament-containing borosilicate capillary glass (outside diameter, 1.5 mm, inside 0.86 mm, catalog # BF150-86-10; Sutter Instruments) and pulled to resistance between 2.5 and 5.00 MΩ using a DMZ Universal Electrode puller (Zeitz-Instruments Vertriebs, GmbH, Martinsried, Germany).

Current clamp recordings were performed using glass pipettes filled with an internal solution (containing in mM): 100 potassium gluconate, 2 MgCl2, 1CaCl2, 11 EGTA, 10 Hepes, 30 KCl, 3 Mg2ATP, pH adjusted to 7.3 with KOH, and osmolarity to 278 mOsm with sucrose. Current steps of 2 s duration and ranging in intensity from -300 to + 300 pA (Δ=50pA) were injected to hyperpolarize and depolarize the cell membrane and measure voltage changes.

After starting the whole-cell recording, stable current-clamp responses were observed for 15 minutes. We then recorded the baseline activity, applied 50 mM alcohol, and monitored the responses at 5-minute intervals. Washout effects were characterized for 10 minutes following alcohol application (Fig. 1A). Only recordings with a stable access resistance that varied < 25% and did not exceed 25 ΜΩ were included in the analysis. The interneurons were distinguished from pyramidal neurons using the membrane capacitance as performed in our previous investigations [7,8]. Cells recorded with capacitance values > 100 pF were considered pyramidal neurons, while cells showing values < 100 pF were indicated as interneurons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.4.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, Ca). Normality and homogeneity of variances across the experimental groups were assessed using custom MatLab scripts, applying the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test. The p-values are reported in Tables 1 and 2. The Rout test (1%) did not detect any outliers. However, for both neuronal subpopulations, we had one cell that did not fire after alcohol exposure, and these cells were excluded from the analysis of AP threshold, rheobase and AP frequency parameters. During the washout condition, two pyramidal neuron recordings were lost from the male group and one from the female group. For the interneuron recordings, one cell was lost in the male group. A mixed-effect analysis followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed in Prism. The effect size was calculated using a custom Hedges’s script in Matlab. Data are expressed as means ± SEM. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
