Comparative outcomes of Adam Frame and Ilizarov external fixator in pediatric lower limb deformity correction
Authors: Tarık Elma, Mustafa Çeliktaş, Salih Marangoz, Mahir Gülşen, Çagri Ors
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of the computer assisted octopod external fixator [Adam Frame (AF)] and the conventional Ilizarov external fixator (IEF) in pediatric and adolescent patients undergoing lower limb deformity correction and lengthening.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study included 48 patients (< 18 years, 69 extremities) treated between 2009 and 2015 at two tertiary orthopedic centers. Patients were divided into AF (25 patients, 35 extremities) and IEF (23 patients, 34 extremities) groups. Demographic data, deformity characteristics, correction parameters, indices, and complications were analyzed. Primary outcomes included correction angles, external fixation indices, and complication rates.ResultsThe mean age at surgery was 12.0 years in both groups (AF range 9–14, IEF range 5–14; p = 0.309). Sex distribution was also similar (AF: 14 males, 11 females; IEF: 13 males, 10 females; p = 1.000). Femoral involvement was more common in the IEF group, while tibial involvement was common in the AF group (p = 0.046). Sagittal plane deformities were more frequent in the AF group (34.29% vs. 5.88%; p = 0.005). The sagittal correction angle (p = 0.034) and daily lengthening rate (0.80 vs. 0.70 mm/day; p = 0.038) were slightly higher in the IEF group.ConclusionBoth AF and IEF are effective for pediatric deformity correction and lengthening. The IEF technique achieved greater sagittal correction and slightly faster lengthening. However, AF facilitates multiple adjustments, which can improve outcomes in complex tibial or sagittal-dominant deformities.

Introduction

Limb deformities and length discrepancies in children and adolescents are complex conditions that are challenging to treat in orthopedic practice. These conditions may be congenital, developmental, or acquired after trauma, infection, or metabolic disorders, and can lead to impaired gait, chronic pain, and long-term functional disability [1, 2]. Beyond the physical limitations, untreated deformities may also affect psychosocial well-being and quality of life [3, 4]. Surgical management in this population requires long treatment times, continuous and comprehensive follow-up, and comes with high risks for complications [5, 6].

Historically, deformity correction was achieved through acute osteotomies stabilized with plates or intramedullary devices. Although technically simple, these classical methods were limited by frequent complications and lack of gradual correction which led to unsatisfactory outcomes or need for secondary interventions [5]. The introduction of circular external fixators revolutionized treatment by allowing gradual correction and lengthening. The Ilizarov external fixator (IEF) method made it possible to achieve controlled distraction osteogenesis and simultaneous correction of multiplanar deformities [7, 8]. Despite its proven effectiveness, the Ilizarov system has limitations. Achieving precise correction of complex and multi-apical deformities often requires repeated frame adjustments, leading to prolonged treatment times and increased technical difficulty, which resulted in incomplete correction without rigorous processes [5, 9]. Advances in fixator technology have led to the development of computer-assisted hexapod or octopod spatial frames, which allow more accurate and efficient correction [10, 11]. These systems enable simultaneous multiplanar adjustments, reduce the need for frame revisions, and improve overall precision, particularly in complex deformities, thereby shortening operative times and enhancing patient outcomes.

The Adam Frame (AF), an octopod external fixator based on Paley’s method, represents an advancement in computer-assisted technology, offering a design that may facilitate application compared to traditional hexapod system [11, 12]. Unlike hexapod frames, which typically require dedicated software for all adjustments, the octopod configuration of the AF allows for manual calculations or software use, enabling surgeons to mimic deformities more intuitively and achieve corrections without constant computer dependency [13]. This hybrid approach may provide flexibility and facilitate the learning process, which could be advantageous in pediatric patients where adaptability is important [14].

While both IEF and computer-assisted external fixators are used in clinical practice [13], there is no publication in the literature with regards to use of Adam Frame octopod computer assisted external fixator. The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate and compare the results and effectiveness of the AF with those of the traditional IEF in the treatment of pediatric and adolescent patients with lower limb deformities and/or discrepancies.

Material and methods

Study design and patients

This study was designed as a retrospective comparative cohort analysis evaluating clinical and radiographic outcomes of two circular external fixation strategies in pediatric and adolescent lower-limb deformity correction: computer-assisted Adam Frame (AF) and conventional Ilizarov external fixator (IEF). Because treatment allocation was non-randomized and surgeon-dependent, comparisons are observational and should be interpreted as exploratory. This retrospective comparative study was conducted using anonymized data from cases performed between May 2009 and March 2015 at two orthopedic clinics in Turkey.

The study population consisted of 48 pediatric and adolescent patients (under 18 years of age) who underwent a total of 69 consecutive gradual deformity corrections and/or lengthening in the lower extremities due to various etiological causes, including varus or valgus deformities, fibular hemimelia, fracture sequela, growth problems, and other conditions.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged < 18 years at the time of surgery; (2) lower-limb deformity involving the femur and/or tibia treated with circular external fixation using either AF or IEF; (3) deformities requiring correction in the coronal plane, sagittal plane, or multiplanar/biplanar planes, with or without concomitant limb lengthening; (4) availability of adequate preoperative and postoperative radiographs for angular measurements; and (5) minimum follow-up of 24 months. Exclusion criteria were: acute correction procedures without gradual deformity correction protocol, lengthening-over-nail or fixator-assisted nailing techniques, bi-level corrections in the same limb segment, inadequate radiographic documentation for planned measurements, and follow-up < 24 months. Ethical considerations

The data for this retrospective study are sourced from patients treated at two different clinics during the authors' previous working periods (May 2009–March 2015), and no original institutional review board or ethics committee approval was obtained during the initial data collection period. However, the ethics committee approval required for the publication of this study was subsequently obtained from Institutional Ethics Committee of Private Medline Adana Hospital (Approval date: 06.05.2025, decision no: 04), specifically for the use of anonymized patient data and for conducting a retrospective academic analysis. Patient identities were anonymized throughout the data collection and analysis process to ensure confidentiality, and no personal identifying information was used in the reporting of results. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments..

Examined parameters

Data were gathered retrospectively from electronic medical records, peri-operative notes, radiographic images, and clinical follow-up documentation. Variables included demographic details (age, sex), diagnostic categories, intervention types, anatomical sites (bone, laterality), deformity characteristics (plane, angle, vectorial sum), preoperative and postoperative leg length discrepancies, correction indices, external fixation durations, complications, and follow-up times. All measurements were performed using standardized radiographic protocols. Indices such as external fixation index, lengthening (distraction) index, bone healing (maturation) index, and daily correction amounts were computed based on distraction durations, length gained, and consolidation times.

Surgical planning and methodology

Clinical and radiographic evaluation

Patients were evaluated clinically through comprehensive history reviews and physical examinations, including measurements of leg length discrepancy using blocks underneath the shorter limb and by measuring the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus. Orthoroentogenograms were performed both before surgery and prior to fixator removal and during routine postoperative follow-ups to assess correction status and residual deformities.

Preoperative planning

Preoperative planning followed standardized deformity analysis principles. Standing, patella-forward, full-length anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of both lower limbs were obtained for the malalignment test. The mechanical axis deviation and joint orientation angles—including distal medial/lateral femoral angle, proximal medial/lateral tibial angle, and distal tibial angle—were measured using established methods described by Paley and colleagues for frontal and sagittal osteotomies [15]. For tibial and femoral malalignment requiring multiplanar analysis, the center of rotation and angulation (CORA) was identified on orthogonal projections, and deformity parameters were verified using the oblique plane method [16, 17]. In patients with deformities in more than one plane, frontal and sagittal components were combined using vector summation to define the composite deformity angle for planning and postoperative evaluation. Leg length discrepancy was assessed clinically by measuring the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine and medial malleolus, and radiographically on long-leg standing films. Osteotomy levels were planned at or near the CORA to allow angular correction with minimal translation, as osteotomies near the CORA in metaphyseal regions promote better vascularity and bone healing while still achieving effective correction with acceptable translation. Operative technique

Frame selection (AF vs IEF) was based on surgeon preference and deformity characteristics. In general, AF was preferentially selected for complex multiplanar deformities and/or cases with prominent sagittal plane components where computer-assisted correction planning was considered advantageous. IEF was more commonly used for relatively straightforward uniplanar corrections or when conventional circular frame adjustments were deemed sufficient. All procedures were performed by three senior orthopedic surgeons using standardized techniques. Fixator-to-bone fixation in both groups used a combination of half pins and tensioned wires. Osteotomy was carried out subperiosteally at this planned level (the CORA or near the CORA) using either multiple drill-hole corticotomy or a Gigli saw, following the principles of distraction osteogenesis [18, 19].

In the AF group, correction was planned and executed with computer-assisted software that allowed simultaneous multi-planar adjustments. When both angular correction and lengthening were indicated, they were initiated concurrently as per plan. Frame configuration and strut adjustments followed the software-derived prescription. In the IEF group, hinge placement and ring configuration (Tasarımmed, Istanbul, Turkiye) were planned preoperatively according to the CORA and the intended correction plane(s). Angular correction was performed first. When lengthening was also indicated, distraction was commenced only after angular correction.

The latency period ranged from 3 to 7 days postoperatively, based on standard approaches. The distraction rhythm was set at 1 mm/day, divided into four. Patients were allowed full weight-bearing (as tolerated) immediately following frame application. Radiographic follow-up was performed approximately every six weeks for monitoring. Frames were removed after tricortical consolidation was confirmed on anteroposterior and lateral radiographs. Measurements and index calculations

Etiology was categorized as congenital, developmental, post-traumatic, post-infectious, neuromuscular, or other/idiopathic according to medical records and preoperative assessment.

Deformities were classified by plane. These included frontal (varus/valgus), sagittal (procurvatum/recurvatum). Varus was defined as medial angulation in the frontal plane, valgus as lateral angulation, procurvatum as anterior angulation in the sagittal plane, and recurvatum as posterior angulation.

Deformity magnitudes were recorded as angles in degrees for each involved plane (e.g., frontal deformity angle, sagittal deformity angle). When deformity involved more than one plane, a vector sum deformity angle was calculated by combining orthogonal angular components into a single value. Postoperative correction angles (frontal, sagittal, and vectorial) and correction status (complete vs partial) were recorded.

Leg length discrepancy was measured both clinically and radiographically, with postoperative differences categorized as none, 1 cm, or 2 cm. Lengthening amount was documented in cases receiving distraction. We also calculated the LLRS AIM (Limb Lengthening and Reconstruction Society Activity Impairment Measure) index for the AF group to assess procedural complexity. This index is derived as the sum of scores in 7 domains: location, leg length inequality, risk factors, soft tissue condition, angular deformity, bone quality/infection, and joint motion/stability. Total scores can range from 0 (normal) to 28 (high complexity) [25].

These primary indices were computed within the overall cohort and reported separately for treatment groups and subgroups (correction only, lengthening only, and correction plus lengthening). The dataset also included several daily metrics, such as the daily external fixation index, daily correction index, daily healing index, and daily correction or lengthening amounts.

Preoperative limb length discrepancy was defined as the difference in limb length between sides measured on standardized long-standing radiographs and recorded in centimeters. The planned lengthening target was determined preoperatively based on clinical examination and radiographic assessment, taking into account functional needs and soft-tissue tolerance.

Complications

Complications were recorded and classified according to Paley’s system of problems, obstacles, and complications [26]. These included pin tract infections, soft-tissue complications, fracture, nerve palsy, recurrence, and re-osteotomy. Multiple complications occurring within the same extremity were documented descriptively but counted as a single complication event for comparative analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). p-values less than 0.05 were accepted as statistically significant. For the normality check, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used. Descriptive statistics are presented using mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, median (25th percentile–75th percentile) for non-normally distributed continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The Student's t test was used to analyze normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann Whitney U test was used to analyze non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test or Fisher-Freeman-Halton test were used to analyze categorical variables. Because of the relatively small sample size and limited number of outcome events, no multivariable or propensity-based modeling was performed, and comparative analyses were primarily based on univariate statistical tests. Effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed by an independent biostatistician. Effect sizes (Hedges g for continuous outcomes and risk ratios for categorical outcomes) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key between-group comparisons to facilitate clinical interpretation of findings.

Results

A total of 48 pediatric and adolescent patients with 69 lower extremities were included in the study, with 25 patients (35 extremities) in the AF group and 23 patients (34 extremities) in the IEF group. The mean age at surgery was 12 years (range, 9–14) in the AF group and 12 years (range, 5–14) in the IEF group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.309). The AF group consisted of 14 males (56.0%) and 11 females (44.0%), while the IEF group included 13 males (56.5%) and 10 females (43.5%), with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 1.000).

Femoral involvement was significantly more common in the IEF group and tibial involvement was more common in the AF group (p = 0.046). The AF group showed a higher prevalence of multiplanar deformities, with two-plane deformities in 25.71% versus 5.88% in IEF (p = 0.055), and sagittal plane involvement (procurvatum/recurvatum) in 34.29% versus 5.88% in IEF (p = 0.005), highlighting AF's application in more complex cases. The AF and IEF groups had similar complete correction success for both frontal (79.31% vs. 83.33%; p = 0.950) and sagittal (75% vs. 100%; p = 1.000) planes. Latency time was significantly higher in the AF group (median 7 days vs. 3 days; p = 0.013). The daily lengthening rate was slightly higher in the IEF group (0.80 ± 0.20 mm/day vs. 0.70 ± 0.10 mm/day; p = 0.038). Healing indices showed no significant differences (bone healing index: 0.86 vs. 0.60; p = 0.353; distraction index: 0.52 vs. 0.56; p = 0.519), nor did correction degrees (frontal: 20° vs. 20°; p = Discussion

The current study revealed that the computer-assisted AF and traditional IEF achieved similar overall efficacy in gradual deformity correction and lengthening for pediatric and adolescent patients with lower extremity deformities. There were no substantial differences in the majority of healing indices and the frequency of complications were similar. While the AF group was applied to more complex cases with higher prevalence of multiplanar deformities (25.71% vs. 5.88%) and sagittal plane involvement (34.29% vs. 5.88%), the IEF group achieved greater sagittal correction angles (37.5° vs. 25°) and marginally higher daily lengthening rates (0.08 vs. 0.07 mm/day), though complete sagittal correction rates were similar. Although latency periods were extended in the AF, both systems resulted in high rates of complete correction without notable variations in residual deformities or follow-up duration.

The treatment of multiplanar deformities in the lower extremities of children and adolescents requires external fixation systems that are stable and adjustable, thereby facilitating gradual correction while minimizing complications and promoting bone regeneration [5]. Traditional circular fixators like IEF have proven effective for axial and angular corrections but often necessitate sequential adjustments for multiplanar issues, which can increase treatment complexity and duration [27]. In contrast, hexapod systems such as the Taylor Spatial Frame (TSF) and Smart Correction utilize computer-assisted planning for simultaneous six-axis correction [10, 27]. In our series, the AF group had a higher prevalence of two-plane deformities (25.71% in AF vs. 5.88% in IEF) and sagittal plane involvement (34.29% procurvatum/recurvatum in AF vs. 5.88% in IEF). Nevertheless, complete correction was achieved in 79.31% of frontal and 75.00% of sagittal deformities in the AF group, which were comparable to those observed with IEF (83.33% and 100.00%, respectively). These are similar to prior reports in relatively smaller cohorts [12, 28], which draw attention to the fact that the efficacy of computer assisted fixator procedures are relatively high in complex deformities [1]. Our study contributes by demonstrating that the AF, like the TSF and Smart Correction, excels in multiplanar scenarios without increasing residual deformities, offering a less invasive alternative. This could reduce the need for staged procedures and might improve pediatric compliance through potentially shorter frame times. Compared with contemporary six-axis hexapod systems such as the Taylor Spatial Frame and Smart Correction, the AF employs an octopod configuration that combines Ilizarov principles with optional computer assistance. While hexapod systems rely heavily on software-generated algorithms for all strut adjustments, the AF allows a hybrid manual/software approach that may reduce cognitive load for surgeons familiar with classical circular frames while still facilitating management of complex multiplanar deformities.

Lengthening indices, including distraction, maturation, and external fixation indices, are pivotal for assessing the biological response to distraction osteogenesis in external fixation [11]. Conventional Ilizarov systems provide a stable environment for lengthening but may require sequential approaches in combined deformity and lengthening cases, which would appear to worsen these indices [7]. In our cohort, lengthening indices were comparable, as were maturation and external fixation indices. Notwithstanding the fact that AF can achieve simultaneous correction, which is superior to sequential adjustments in IEF, the similar findings for these metrics indicate that these two methods achieve similar efficacy. The amount of lengthening in our study (4.9 cm mean lengthening with external fixation index of 41.2 days/cm) is also similar to prior research involving external fixators [12]. Our findings show that AF results in similar outcomes to IEF in terms of success metrics in pediatric patients, but with daily lengthening slightly higher in IEF. This suggests that while AF's octopod structure may offer better multiplanar handling without compromising indices, its application in complex cases could still yield equivalent biological responses, potentially due to reduced revisions and surgeon familiarity. The longer latency period observed in the AF group and the slightly higher daily lengthening rate in the IEF group may have clinical implications. A longer latency period may support more mature regenerate formation, whereas faster daily distraction may increase soft-tissue tension, patient discomfort, and the risk of minor complications. These parameters should be considered when individualizing distraction protocols for pediatric patients.

Complications in pediatric external fixation for deformities remain a concern due to prolonged frame times and pin-site issues, with up to 18% of cases suffering from nonunion and subsequent complications [6]. In our study, overall complication rates were 28.57% in AF versus 14.71% in IEF, with no significant difference and no amputations. This finding likely reflects AF's preferential application in more complex multiplanar cases rather than an inherent increase in method-specific risk; indeed, computer-assisted frames may reduce the need for frame revisions, potentially offsetting initial complication rates over the long term. These complication rates are similar to the overall data obtained from a meta-analysis of IEF procedures [6]. While hexapod systems like the TSF remain widely available and utilized, the AF, as an octopod frame, offers distinct perceptual advantages for surgeons accustomed to Ilizarov methods, allowing intuitive deformity simulation without sole reliance on software outputs, unlike hexapods which demand strict adherence to computer instructions [13, 14]. This octopod design reduces cognitive complexity in multiplanar corrections, potentially enhancing usability and precision in complex pediatric cases [8]. Unfortunately, despite these benefits, the AF has been withdrawn from the market for various reasons, including legal disputes over patents. Future studies should systematically compare AF with hexapod systems like the TSF to validate octopod superiority, and efforts to reintroduce it could bridge gaps between traditional and advanced frames, improving accessibility for deformity correction.

This retrospective study has several limitations. The modest sample size (48 patients, 69 extremities), non-randomized treatment allocation, and reliance on medical records introduce potential biases and limit statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses; consequently, non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution due to the risk of type II error. Baseline imbalances between groups—including bone type distribution (tibial predominance in AF vs. femoral in IEF) and deformity complexity—may act as confounders, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. The inclusion of bilateral extremities in some patients may have introduced within-patient correlation, and no multiplicity correction was applied. Surgeon familiarity with IEF may have biased frame selection toward simpler cases in the IEF group. Follow-up variability may have resulted in missed late recurrences. Additionally, functional outcomes and patient-reported outcome measures were not collected, limiting clinical interpretability. Despite these limitations, comparative studies directly evaluating AF and IEF remain scarce, and AF has not been systematically compared with other hexapod systems such as the TSF. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized protocols and PROMs are warranted.

In conclusion, both the Adam Frame and the Ilizarov external fixator achieved acceptable correction and lengthening outcomes in selected pediatric cases. The AF was more frequently applied in complex multiplanar deformities, while the IEF demonstrated slightly higher daily lengthening rates. However, due to baseline imbalances between groups, potential within-patient clustering, and the retrospective nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not demonstrate superiority of either method. Future prospective studies with standardized protocols and patient-reported outcomes are warranted.
