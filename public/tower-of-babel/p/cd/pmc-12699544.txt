Effects of senescence on astrocyte to brain endothelial cell signaling
Authors: Shuzhen Guo, Gen Hamanaka, Fang Zhang, Janice Lee, Ji Hyun Park, Wenlu Li, Ken Arai, Eng H Lo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging affects almost all aspects of central nervous system (CNS) function, including the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Here, we use cell culture models to ask whether senescence, a cellular feature of aging, alters the BBB by modifying interactions between astrocytes and brain endothelial cells.MethodsHuman astrocyte and Human brain microvascular endothelial cells were subcultured and maintained for cells at low and high passages, then confirmed with senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase staining and gene expression of cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (Cdkn2a). After coculturing with astrocyte, the Alexa Fluor 488‐labeled bovine serum albumin (Alexa 488‐BSA) was used as a tracer to measure the permeability of brain endothelial cells; the expression of related proteins was measured by quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction. Reducing the angiotensinogen (AGT) by small interfering RNA (siRNA) in senescent astrocyte to test the effect of angiotensin signals on endothelial permeability.ResultsYoung astrocytes (cumulative population doublings [CPD] ≤ 4) modified the expression of barrier genes and decreased brain endothelial permeability in coculture, whereas aged senescent astrocytes (CPD ≥ 9) had no effects (45.5% ± 18.0% vs. 122.8% ± 28.6%, p = 0.0016). Angiotensin is known to alter the BBB. Its precursor, AGT, is highly expressed in astrocytes in the brain. Therefore, we asked whether angiotensin signaling may mediate the loss of endothelial barrier‐promoting properties in senescent astrocytes. Both protein and messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of AGT were increased in high‐passage senescent astrocytes. Reducing AGT levels through siRNA restored the endothelial barrier‐promoting effects of high‐passage senescent astrocytes (F (2,15) = 6.508, p = 0.0092). By contrast, brain endothelial cells at different passages did not change the expression of AGT in astrocytes.ConclusionTaken together, these findings suggest that increased angiotensin signaling from astrocytes to brain endothelium may partly mediate the decrease of BBB function in the aging CNS.Keywords: aging, angiotensin, astrocyte, blood–brain barrier, endothelial, senescence

INTRODUCTION

Clinically effective neuroprotection remains a challenging goal for central nervous system (CNS) disorders. 1 , 2 The concept of the neurovascular unit was proposed to broaden the scope of neuroprotection; saving neurons alone may not work, and all types of cells including glial and vascular cells must be protected to rescue CNS function. 3 , 4 , 5

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) comprises one of the most important aspects of a healthy neurovascular unit. 6 , 7 BBB dysfunction occurs in stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neurodegeneration. Therefore, protecting the BBB is a vital part of any CNS therapeutic approach. Another factor that contributes to CNS injury and disease is aging. An aging neurovascular unit is more susceptible to a wide spectrum of insults and disease triggers. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Rodent and human studies suggest that BBB function is degraded in the aging brain. 12 However, the underlying mechanisms are complex and incompletely understood. Goodall et al. 13 reported that the distribution of the tight junction protein zonula occludens‐1 (ZO‐1) along brain microvessels was perturbed with increased breaks in the continuity of ZO‐1 timmunostaining in old mice, yet Evans blue leakage in cortex showed no significant age‐associated change. Yang et al. 14 reported an increase in immunoglobulin G accumulation in the aging mouse brain but a decrease in the uptake of plasma proteins, suggesting that this effect was driven by the age‐related shift in BBB transport from receptor‐mediated transport to nonspecific caveolar transcytosis. Using contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging, Montagne et al. 15 showed that aging led to increased BBB leakage in the hippocampus but not in other brain areas, with worse leakage in patients with mild cognitive impairment. In contrast, Verheggen et al. 16 showed significant age‐associated BBB leakage in both gray matter and white matter in healthy participants from the age of 40–90 years. Altogether, these differences in literature highlight the significant gaps in knowledge that still exist and underscore the need to better understand how aging may induce BBB dysfunction.

In spite of all these complexities, one aspect of the BBB is well‐accepted, that is, the role of astrocytes in the regulation of BBB function. 17 , 18 Normal aging induces the expression of reactive A1‐type astrocytes that may underlie neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. 19 , 20 Is it possible that aged astrocytes may also contribute to aging‐related changes in the BBB? Since angiotensin has been previously implicated in the hypertension‐induced BBB leakage, 21 , 22 and its precursor protein angiotensinogen (AGT) is known to be highly expressed in astrocytes, 23 we hypothesized that aging may alter angiotensin signaling between astrocytes and brain endothelium, and this may be a candidate mechanism that contributes to decreased endothelial barrier tightness during cellular senescence.

METHODS

Cell culture and mice

Human astrocyte (HA) from ScienCell Research Laboratories (San Diego, CA, USA) were maintained with astrocyte media plus supplements (ScienCell Research Laboratories). Human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC) from Cell Systems Corporation (Kirkland, WA, USA) were maintained with EndoGRO‐MV Complete Culture M`edia (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) on human fibronectin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) pre‐coated surfaces. The culturing conditions were the same as suggested, at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a humid incubator. Cells were split at a ratio of 1:2 when they reached more than 90% density, and this counted as one passage. As reported, primary human astrocyte cells were regarded as young at low passages (cumulative population doublings [CPD] ≤ 4), and as senescent at high passages (CPD ≥ 9); 24 HBMEC were regarded as young at low passages (CPD ≤ 12), and as senescent at high passages (CPD ≥ 20). 25

Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA) and senescent in housing of temperature‐controlled room on 12 h light/dark cycle, with filtered cage, free access to standard pellet food and water ad libitum. All animal procedures were conducted under the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee‐approved protocol (No. 2010N000147) in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Treatments of cells

The coculture system and conditioned medium transfer were used to test the effects of astrocytes on brain endothelial cells. Before coculture, the astrocytes were seeded in the 12‐well plate and kept until the cell density more than 80%. Endothelial cells were seeded on the top of 12 mm transwell inserts (Corning, 3460), which were coated with human fibronectin, by 5 × 104 cells per insert. The next day, transwell inserts with endothelial cells were transported to the 12‐well plate with astrocytes after washing, adding fresh endothelial complete media, and then this endothelial‐astrocyte system was incubated for 3 days, followed by further measurements.

To prepare the astrocyte‐conditioned media (ACM), 6 mL of endothelial complete media was added to the confluent astrocytes at different passages in 10 cm culture dishes, incubated for 72 h. The media were then collected and centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 5 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube as ACM, to be used immediately or store at −80°C. Before using, these ACM were diluted at 1:1 with fresh endothelial complete media, directly added to brain endothelial cells seeded in inserts or multi‐well plates for treatment.

Permeability assay

Brain endothelial cells on transwell insert after incubation with astrocyte or ACM, were washed and transported to a new multi‐well plate with fresh RPMI 1640 media without phenol red (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA). The tracer 100 μg/mL Alexa Fluor 488‐labeled bovine serum albumin (Alexa 488‐BSA) (Molecular Probe Inc., Eugene, OR, USA) was added in top chamber, 100 μL media from bottom chamber were collected after 30‐min incubation and measured at wavelength of 488 nm for fluorescent intensity. 21 Higher fluorescent intensity in the bottom chamber suggests more Alexa 488‐BSA was transported from top to bottom through brain endothelial layer with higher permeability.

Water‐soluble tetrazolium salt (WST‐1) assay for cell viability

The cells were changed to fresh culture media plus 10% WST‐1 solution (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, INC., Rockville, MD, USA), then returned to incubator for 2 h. The formazan dye produced by viable cells could dissolve in the media and ready to be measured at wavelength of 450 nm. The fresh culture medium was used as blank control. When endothelial cells growing on the inserts were used for this assay, after incubation, the media with WST‐1 from both top and bottom chambers were mixed completely, then aliquots of 150 μL media were transferred to 96‐well plate for measurement.

Senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐Gal) staining

Cells at different passages were subjected to SA‐β‐Gal staining using Senescence β‐Galactosidase Staining Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) following the protocol from the manufacturer. After rinsing with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), cells were fixed for 10 min at room temperature and rinsed again with PBS. The staining solution with 1 mg/mL X‐gal (5‐bromo‐4‐chloro‐3‐indolyl‐β‐d‐galactopyranoside) at pH 6.0 was incubated with the fixed cells at 37°C for 16 h in a sealed plate. At the end of incubation, the staining solution was removed, the cells were stored in 70% glycerol after rinsing again with PBS. The blue color produced by acidic β‐galactosidase in senescent cells was observed under a light microscope, original magnification 10×10.

Western blot analysis

Cells were rinsed with ice‐cold PBS, incubated, and scraped with cell lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Boston, MA, USA) on ice. The cell lysates were collected and centrifuged at 4°C by 10,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was used to determine the protein concentration and further analysis. Twenty to thirty micrograms of protein were loaded per lane and separated on premade 4%–20% SDA‐polyacrylamide gel (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), then samples were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane for blocking with fat‐free milk and incubating with primary antibodies. The antibodies to trimethylation of lysine 27 on‐histone H3 (H3K27me3, Cell Signaling Technology, 9733), advanced glycation end products (AGE, Abcam, ab23722), AGT (Santa Cruz Biotech, SC374511), and β‐actin (Sigma, A5441) were used. Horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies (Amersham, Piscataway, NJ, USA) were used, and the signals were developed with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA), visualized by CCD imager. The optical density of each band was analyzed by program Image J (https://imagej.net/ij/).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

Mission endogenous short interfering RNAs (esiRNAs) to human AGT (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used in human astrocyte to test its loss‐of‐function effects on brain endothelial cells, and esiRNA to EGFP (enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein) was used as negative control as suggested by the manufacturer. Senescent astrocytes were seeded in 12‐well plate at high density for transfection. The 1 μL siRNA and 2 μL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were each diluted in Opti‐MEM medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), then mixed and kept at room temperature for 5 min before added to cells with fresh Opti‐MEM medium. After 4–6 h incubation, complete media were added directly to each well. One day after transfection, the astrocytes were used in coculture with endothelial cells. The protein and mRNA level of AGT in astrocytes were tested 2 days after transfection.

Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR)

RT‐PCR was used to measure the senescence marker cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (Cdkn2a, also known as Ink4a/arf), BBB genes in endothelial cells, and Agt in astrocytes. The total RNAs were prepared from cells or mouse tissue by RNeasy Plus Mini kit, the cDNAs were synthesized with QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (both from Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), following the standard protocols from manufacturer. The genes expression levels were analyzed using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix with pre‐designed primers from Applied Biosystems, performed on QuantStudio 3 system. Housekeeping gene hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (Hprt1) was used as the loading control, and 2−ΔΔCt method used to calculate the changes of gene expression.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse brain was collected after perfused with PBS, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), dehydrated with sucrose solution, embedded with OCT compound, then cut into 15 μm sections with cryostat and stored at −80°C freezer. Before staining, the glass slides with brain sections were warmed to room temperature and incubated again with 4% PFA for 10 min. The sections were then incubated with 0.1% Trypsin in PBS at 37°C for 15 min, followed with 5% BSA plus 0.1% Triton‐X100 in PBS for 2 h. The primary antibody dilutions were incubated overnight with brain sections at 4°C, the fluorescent‐labeled secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., West Grove, PA, USA) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Before and after each incubation of solution, the slides were washed three times with PBS. At last, the sections were covered with Fluoro‐Gel II with DAPI (Electron Microscopy Science, Hartfield, PA, USA) and cover slide, ready for observation with NIKON fluorescent microscope.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For data with only two groups, t‐test was used. For data with more than two groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) analysis was adopted, followed by the Tukey test for comparing between each two groups. Multiple comparation was used for PCR data analysis. The significant difference was considered by p < 0.05 or q < 0.05.

RESULTS

In the aging brain, BBB function is significantly downregulated. Therefore, we asked whether senescent endothelial cells lose their transcellular barrier tightness. High passage cells represent a standard model to investigate cell senescence in aging. 26 As expected, the senescent markers SA‐β‐Gal staining and Cdkn2a gene expression increased in high passage compared to low passage brain endothelial cells (Figure 1A and 1B). Endothelial barrier tightness was assessed by measuring the movement of the tracer Alexa 488‐BSA across endothelial layers in a transwell chamber. There were no significant differences in tracer permeability in high passage cells compared to low passage cells (Figure 1C). These findings may be consistent with previous studies showing that senescent human brain endothelial cells in an in vitro BBB model do not have major differences in trans‐cellular permeability compared to young endothelial cells. 25

Endothelial barrier tightness is improved by young astrocytes but not senescent astrocytes. (A–C) Senescence markers are detected in high‐passage aged human brain microvascular endothelial cells, including senescence‐associated beta‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐Gal) staining (original magnification 10 ×10) and increased gene expression of cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (Cdkn2a), but the BSA permeability is not significantly different between young and senescent (Aged) endothelial cells. (D–F) Senescence markers are also detected in the high‐passage aged human astrocytes, as SA‐β‐Gal staining, increased gene expression of Cdkn2a, increased trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3k27me3), and advanced glycation end products (AGE). (G, H) BSA permeability of brain endothelial cells is improved with young astrocyte coculture or by conditioned media from young astrocytes but not improved by senescent (Aged) astrocytes. *p < 0.05. OD, optical density.

Astrocytes play a critical role in supporting BBB function. Hence, we asked whether senescent astrocytes may contribute to the increased endothelial permeability with aging. Once again, we checked to ensure that appropriate markers of senescence can be detected in our human astrocyte cultures. Compared to low passage cells, high passage astrocytes showed increased SA‐β‐Gal staining, higher gene expression level of Cdkn2a, and higher levels of heterochromatin marker H3K27me3 and AGE (Figure 1D–F), as reported before. 27 , 28

To ask whether senescent astrocytes may influence endothelial barrier tightness, we used co‐cultures in our transwell chamber where brain endothelial cells were grown on the top of insert membranes and human astrocytes were grown on the bottom chamber. When cocultured with low passage astrocytes, the permeability of the endothelial layer was reduced compared to endothelial cells alone (Figure 1G), as reported before. 29 , 30 But when cocultured with high passage astrocytes, endothelial permeability was unchanged (Figures 1G, 45.5% ± 18.0% vs. 122.8% ± 28.6% for the relative percentage of BSA permeability in endothelial cells cocultured with young astrocyte or with aged astrocyte, respectively, p = 0.0016; as 100% to endothelial cells alone). These effects were also observed in a media transfer experiment. Conditioned media from low passage astrocytes decreased endothelial permeability, but conditioned media from high passage astrocytes had no effect (Figure 1H, 75.2% ± 10.1% and 114.6% ± 8.0% for the relative percentage of BSA permeability in endothelial cells cultured with young ACM or with aged ACM, respectively, p < 0.001).

Next, we examined the expression of BBB‐related genes, including those involved in transmembrane transporters, tight junctions, and caveolar transcytosis. Brain endothelial cells cocultured with young astrocytes had significant differences in gene expression compared to endothelial cells cocultured with senescent astrocytes, with higher levels of solute carrier family 2 member 1 (Slc2a1, also as glucose transporter GLUT1), Claudin 5 (Cldn5), Occludin (Ocln), ZO‐1, and major facilitator superfamily domain‐containing protein 2a (Mfsd2a); and lower level of plasmalemma vesicle‐associated protein (Plvap) (Figure 2). These changes were generally consistent with the expected effects in barrier function. Tight junction genes are known to support BBB tightness. Plvap is a caveolar protein that has been proposed as a marker for endothelial dysfunction. 31 Mutation of Mfsd2a leads to leaky barriers with more caveolar vesicles. 32

Young and senescent astrocytes induce different genes expression in brain microvascular endothelial cells, including transporters (A), tight junction proteins (B), and caveolae transcytosis‐related proteins (C). *q < 0.05. Abcb1, ATP‐binding cassette subfamily B member 1; Abcc1, ATP‐binding cassette subfamily C member 1; Ast, astrocyte; Cav‐1, caveolin 1; Cldn5, Claudin 5; Mfsd2a, major facilitator superfamily domain‐containing protein 2A; Ocln, Occludin; Plvap, plasmalemma vesicle‐associated protein; Slc2a1, solute carrier family 2 member 1; ZO‐1, zonula occludens‐1.

What is the difference between young and senescent astrocytes that dampens their ability to support endothelial barrier tightness? One initial candidate may be the angiotensin signaling system. Angiotensin II (Ang‐II) mediates the BBB‐damaging sequelae of hypertension. 21 , 22 The precursor protein AGT is known to be highly expressed in astrocytes. 23 Hence, we hypothesized that senescence upregulates AGT in astrocytes, and increased Ang‐II signaling from astrocytes to endothelial cells increases endothelial permeability. First, we checked AGT levels in mouse brain. RNA samples from mouse brain cortex showed higher levels of Agt in senescent brains compared to young brains (Figure 3A, fold change of 2.13 ± 0.36 in senescent mouse brains, p < 0.0010). In the liver, a main source of AGT production, there were no differences in Agt levels between young and senescent mice (Figure 3A). Immunostaining showed the co‐localization of AGT and the astrocyte marker glial fibrillary acidic protein, with many signals adjacent to blood vessels (Figure 3B). These vascular AGT signals were higher in senescent mice compared to young mice (Figure 3C). Consistent with these in vivo data, both protein and mRNA levels of AGT were increased in senescent high passage astrocytes compared to young low passage astrocytes (Figure 3D,E). Notably, this phenomenon was cell‐autonomous in senescent astrocytes. Coculturing young astrocytes with young or senescent endothelial cells did not alter endogenous AGT levels in astrocytes (Figure 3F, one‐way ANOVA F (2, 15) = 0.4522, p = 0.6446).

(A) The aged mouse brain has higher messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of angiotensinogen (Agt) compared to young mouse brain, but no difference is seen in the mouse liver. (B) Immunostaining shows AGT signals are colocalized with the astrocyte marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and they tend to be detected next to blood vessels (original magnification ×200, scale bar 20 μm). (C) AGT signals are higher in aged mouse brain (original magnification ×200, scale bar 20 μm). (D, E) Protein levels and mRNA levels of AGT are higher in senescent astrocytes compared to young astrocytes. (F) The mRNA levels of AGT in astrocytes are not significantly changed after coculturing with young or senescent brain endothelial cells. *p < 0.05. Ast, astrocyte; DAPI, 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole; EC, endothelial cell; OD, optical density.

Finally, to assess causality, we performed a loss‐of‐function experiment to downregulate AGT in senescent astrocytes. Two days after transfection with siRNA against human AGT (siAGT), protein levels of AGT were significantly reduced compared to normal cells (Con) and negative control (siNC) cells (Figure 4A); RNA levels of AGT were also reduced compared to negative control cells (Figure 4B, about 70% reduction). After confirming the effects of our siRNA system, senescent astrocytes, with or without siRNA, were cocultured with endothelial cells in transwell chambers, and the endothelial permeability of the tracer Alexa 488‐BSA was measured. Downregulation of AGT significantly restored the ability of senescent astrocytes to promote endothelial barrier tightness. Relative to the endothelial cells alone, the percentages of BSA permeability in endothelial cells cocultured with Con, siNC or siAGT senescent astrocytes were respectively 123.2% ± 11.6%, 110.8% ± 20.1%, and 85.4% ± 11.8% (Figure 4C, one‐way ANOVA F (2, 15) = 6.508, p = 0.0092 followed by Tukey test).

Downregulation of angiotensinogen (AGT) in senescent astrocytes restores their ability to promote brain endothelial barrier function. (A, B) With small interfering RNA (siRNA) against AGT, both protein and messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of AGT are reduced in senescent astrocytes without change in mRNA level of the senescence marker cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (Cdkn2a). (C) One day post‐siRNA transfection, senescent astrocytes are cocultured with endothelial cells in transwell systems, and astrocytes with reduced AGT expression regain their ability to promote endothelial barrier tightness against bovine serum albumin (BSA) permeability. *p < 0.05. Ast, astrocyte; Con, cells without siRNA as control; siAGT, cells with siRNA to AGT; siNC, cells with siRNA to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) as negative control.

DISCUSSION

The role of astrocytes in the development, maintenance and regulation of BBB has been studied widely. Here we showed that the ability of astrocytes to promote endothelial barrier tightness is dependent on the “age” of the astrocytes, and increased expression of AGT might partly explain the decreased ability of senescent astrocytes to promote endothelial barrier tightness (Figure 5).

Schematic summary of the astrocyte‐to‐endothelial angiotensinogen (AGT) signaling hypothesis. Increased expression of the Ang‐II (Angiotensin II) precursor AGT in aged astrocytes lead to increased Ang‐II signaling from aged astrocytes to endothelium, thus increasing blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and leakiness.

As a component in the neurovascular unit, astrocytes are known to be key players in the induction and modulation of BBB function. 17 , 33 , 34 Astrocytes can induce barrier properties in nonneural endothelial cells. Janzer and Raff reported that purified astrocytes injected into the anterior eye chamber of adult rats could aggregate on the surface of the iris and become vascularized within 48 h. These newly formed vessels show no permeability to Evans blue dye at 2 weeks after injection. 35 Using an in vitro coculture system, it has also been reported that primary rat astrocytes can induce BBB properties in human umbilical vein endothelial cells, including the expression of γ‐glutamyltranspeptidase, transferrin receptor, P‐glycoprotein and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT‐1). 36 In cell culture systems, adding astrocytes also improves the barrier function of brain endothelial cells. 29 , 30 Many factors have been proposed to mediate the barrier‐promoting actions of astrocytes. As an organizer of dystrophin‐associated protein scaffolds in astrocyte endfeet, α‐dystrobrevin is important for astrocyte‐endothelial interactions that allow the formation of BBB. 37 Sonic hedgehog secreted by astrocyte through Hedgehog receptor Patch1 on BBB endothelial cells also can promote BBB tightness, with higher TEER and lower permeability with increased expression of Cldn5 and Ocln. 38 Using astrocyte specific knockout mice, it has been shown that the neogenin/netrin‐1 pathway is important for BBB integrity and loss of this signaling pathway alters endothelial permeability, endothelial caveolae function and coverage of blood vessels by astrocyte end‐feet and pericytes. 39 Taken together, the literature suggests that astrocytes can release a wide spectrum of signals to support BBB integrity. Our present study shows the converse, that is, under aging conditions, it is not the loss of these previously described signals but rather the increase of new signal(s) that leads to BBB leakage. In senescent astrocytes, an increase of AGT increases angiotensin‐II signaling towards adjacent endothelium that then downregulates tight junction genes. This pathway may partly explain the degradation of BBB integrity within an aging brain. Whether and how angiotensin signaling modulates the entire range of BBB‐promoting mediators in astrocytes should be carefully explored.

Senescence is an important part of cellular aging, and accumulating evidence has suggested a role for senescence in the aging brain. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Astrocytes in the aging brain show many characteristics of senescence, with higher expression of p16INK4a and a senescence‐associated secretory phenotype compared to astrocytes in fetal tissues. 44 , 45 In cell culture, astrocytes can also be “aged” with high passages, and various characteristics of senescence can also be detected in these cells. 24 It has been reported that “senescent” high‐passage rat astrocytes show a decreased neuroprotective capacity, and they can even promote glutamate excitotoxicity in neuron cultures. 46 , 47 Senescent astrocytes may also lead to reduced efficiency of synaptic transmission in cocultured neurons when compared to young astrocytes. 48 The proliferation of neural progenitor cells is decreased by adding conditioned medium from old astrocytes (isolated from 13‐month‐old mice) compared to young astrocytes (isolated from 3‐month‐old mice), via Wnt‐mediated surviving signaling, and this mechanism is thought to underlie reduced neurogenesis in aging hippocampus. 49 If senescent astrocytes cannot support neurons, then it is likely that they may also lose their ability to support BBB function in brain endothelial cells. In this study, our cell culture models suggest that this may be true, and the underlying mechanism may involve elevated expression of AGT in senescent astrocytes that potentially leads to increased Ang‐II signaling towards endothelial cells, thus downregulating tight junctions and increasing trans‐endothelial permeability. Furthermore, there are reports indicating that Ang‐II could promote cell senescence or dysfunction in the aging brain through oxidative stress. 50 , 51 Hence, BBB function may be affected by multiple feedback loops between aging, elevated angiotensin signaling, and inflammation.

There are caveats, limitations, and potential future directions associated with the present study. First, the permeability measurements here are based on the tracer BSA. Future studies should examine these effects with other tracers with different molecular sizes. Second, the aging astrocyte will be changed in many ways. It is still not clear how AGT increases the expression in aging astrocyte. Besides angiotensin signaling, other pathways may also contribute to decreased endothelial barrier function. It should be interesting to examine how our hypothesis interacts with well‐established pathways of oxidative stress and inflammation in the aging neurovascular unit. Third, although we focus on astrocytes, BBB function is also supported by pericytes. How the angiotensin pathway interacts with pericyte‐endothelial signaling should be explored. Fourth, endothelial cells, astrocytes, the BBB and the circulating renin‐angiotensin system are all highly regulated by circadian rhythm, 52 , 53 so how these mechanisms are modulated by circadian mechanisms and diurnal timing should be carefully assessed. Fifth, high passage cells are a commonly used but imperfect in vitro model for aging. 54 Future experiments should check this astrocyte angiotensin hypothesis with other models and methods including the induction of senescence by gene modification, oxidative stress, epigenetic changes, or chemical reagents such as Doxorubicin and d‐galactose. 55 , 56 Finally, our experiments here use cell culture models, and our primary endpoints of transcellular tightness may only be an in vitro surrogate marker for true BBB integrity. Rigorous in vivo validation of these ideas is required.

In conclusion, this study provides proof‐of‐concept that upregulated angiotensin signaling in senescent astrocytes may partly contribute to a decrease in endothelial barrier function within the aging neurovascular unit. Insofar as the renin‐angiotensin system has been historically amenable to pharmacologic targeting, future studies should investigate whether therapeutic approaches can be developed to block these signals, rescue BBB function in vivo, and improve opportunities for neuroprotection in the aging brain.
