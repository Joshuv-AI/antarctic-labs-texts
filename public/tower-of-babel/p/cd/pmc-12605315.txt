Epidemiological and economic burden of dementia in the Middle East and North African region
Authors: Najeeb Qadi, Yara Alkhodair, Waad Alshammari, Amaal Aldakheel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia is a global health challenge projected to nearly triple by 2050, especially in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA). Understanding its epidemiology and economic impact is vital for public health planning.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to synthesize evidence on dementia, particularly the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease, its economic burden, and caregiving in MENA countries, to facilitate informed policy-making and effective resource allocation.MethodsWe performed a random-effects meta-analysis to determine the pooled prevalence of dementia from studies published between 2014 and 2025 in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Two reviewers independently selected and extracted data, assessing bias risk with the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. This review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024550935).ResultsTwelve studies met the criteria, showing dementia prevalence in MENA countries from 1.1% to 7.9%, with higher rates in Iran, Israel, and Lebanon, highlighting the need for public health campaigns. The annual estimated cost for those aged 50 and older is about USD 8.18 billion. Caregiving mainly fell to female family members, with larger socioeconomic impacts than in other regions.ConclusionsDementia is a growing public health concern in MENA, requiring improved healthcare infrastructure, increased caregiver support, and targeted research to fill knowledge gaps.

Introduction

Evolving demographics and dementia prevalence

Lifespans are increasing globally as a result of improved health care and public health initiatives, driving a significant rise in dementia prevalence. The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 55 million people have dementia today, a number that is expected to nearly triple by 2050.1,2 In the Middle East and North African (MENA) region, the number of people living with dementia is predicted to increase substantially, from approximately 4 million by 2030 to 10 million by 2050. 3 Dementia, primarily Alzheimer's disease, is characterized by progressive decline in cognition, memory impairment, activities of daily living, and behavioral changes. Despite this growing challenge, comprehensive, country-specific epidemiological data in the MENA region remains limited, highlighting an urgent need for targeted research and informed public health planning.

Challenges of dementia care in the MENA region

In the MENA region, dementia care is frequently provided at home, as nursing homes are uncommon. 4 Most caregiving responsibilities fall on female relatives, leading to significant emotional, physical, and financial stress. 5 Studies have noted the importance of socioeconomic status in providing proper dementia care, with wealthier families employing migrant worker housekeepers as caregivers.6,7 Public health interventions to increase support for informal caregivers, especially given the complexities of dementia care near the end of life, are crucial.

A systematic review by Birch and Draper identified significant challenges in providing care for people living with dementia who are approaching end-of-life. 8 One study estimating the costs of Alzheimer's disease found that indirect costs (the price of informal care arising from the uncompensated labor of family members and loss of productivity for those family members because of caregiving duties) for patients living in the community increased with severity. 9 Stress from providing care could also contribute to ill health among caregivers. 10 This dictates that immediate and long-term strategies are required to address the health care needs of those living with dementia, their families, and the social and economic impacts that will arise with the increasing number of older adults in the population. Therefore, the present review aims to delve further into the epidemiology and economic impact of dementia within the MENA region to identify critical points for intervention.

Methods

Search strategy

We utilized electronic databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, employing keywords related to dementia, epidemiology, economic impact, burden, and countries within the MENA region. Manual searches of grey literature, including conference abstracts and institutional reports, were also conducted. The definition of MENA was as per the Global Peace Index, which is comprised of 20 countries: Algeria, Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Oman, Palestine (West Bank and Gaza), Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, United Arab Emirates, and Yemen.

Synthesis of results. We conducted a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions to pool dementia prevalence across studies. Because very small or very large percentages can behave poorly in meta-analysis, we first converted each study's prevalence to the log-odds (“logit”) scale to stabilize the analysis and ensure the final pooled estimate remained within the valid 0–100% range. We then pooled the transformed values using inverse-variance weighting with the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model and quantified heterogeneity with I². Finally, we converted the pooled result and its 95% confidence interval back to percentage prevalence using the inverse of the logit transformation so that results are directly interpretable. We selected this approach because pooling raw, untransformed percentages can produce invalid results (e.g., a negative pooled prevalence); the logit approach avoids this artefact and yields robust, comparable estimates across studies.

Results of syntheses

Characteristics and risk of bias

The studies exhibited diverse methodologies and populations. Most were cross-sectional; a few were longitudinal. The risk of bias was moderate, with certain design and reporting limitations.

Statistical syntheses

Ten studies with over 1.5 million individuals were included. The pooled prevalence rate of dementia after logit transformation and back-transformation was 3.6% (95% CI: 2.3% to 5.1%) using a random-effects model. Sufficient data for pooling were only available in 10 of the 12 studies included in the review. The heterogeneity observed is likely due to methodological variation in the studies, factors such as hospital-based versus community-based settings, variations in the diagnostic criteria, and country-specific socioeconomic factors. In Figure 2, the forest plot includes the prevalence estimates.

Forest plot of dementia prevalence across MENA studies. Individual study prevalence estimates are shown with their 95% confidence intervals. The pooled prevalence (highlighted in red) is 3.6% (95% CI: 2.3%–5.1%), calculated using a random-effects model with logit transformation and back-transformation for improved accuracy.

We conducted exploratory comparisons given the noted variation in study methodologies and study populations. However, formal subgroup analyses/meta-regression were not possible given the limited granularity of the data.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the synthesized results. Excluding studies with a high risk of bias or those with small sample sizes did not significantly alter the pooled prevalence estimates.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE approach was used to assess the certainty of evidence for the primary outcomes. The overall certainty of evidence for the pooled prevalence outcome was rated as moderate to low, primarily due to heterogeneity in methodologies, variability across populations, and limited standardization of diagnostic criteria.

Prevalence of dementia in the MENA region

There is a lack of country-specific studies on dementia prevalence in the MENA region. Some countries, like Bahrain and Kuwait, have not conducted any country-specific studies on dementia. Several reviews and studies have calculated the prevalence of dementia in the MENA region.11–17,19,21 It showed that Iran has the highest prevalence at 7.9%, followed by Israel with 6.6%. Lebanon, Tunisia, and Libya are in the mid-range, all above 5%. The countries with the lowest recorded percentages are Kuwait and Qatar, below 2% (see Figure 2). This data highlights the substantial variation in the prevalence of this age-associated condition across the region, suggesting a need for targeted healthcare interventions and support systems for older people in countries with higher prevalence.

One study reported women had a higher prevalence of dementia; however, once controlled for all other factors (i.e., age, education, income, marital status), the difference was not statistically significant. 20

While reviewing the dementia prevalence articles, several discrepancies were noted. Firstly, two systematic reviews (n = 4) excluded Arabic language sources.12,14 Secondly, of the studies that were not literature reviews (n = 6), two used hospital samples, which could lead to an overestimation of dementia prevalence through selection bias.16,17 Their calculated prevalence rates were higher than country-wide estimates calculated in sources like Qassem et al. 13 Finally, the study conducted by Ibrahim et al. compared a population sample to a hospital sample. 18

Dementia caregiving in the MENA region

Six studies discuss dementia caregiving practices in the MENA region.12,15,18–21 These studies find that caregivers are overwhelmingly female relatives with a higher socioeconomic class. Interestingly, two studies mentioned that dementia was understood as a normal part of the ageing process, perhaps contributing to the high rates of informal caregiving.11,12 Phung et al. noted the use of migrant laborers employed as housekeepers serving as caregivers to people living with dementia in Lebanon. 15 Comparatively, dementia patients in nursing homes in Baghdad were found to be of lower socioeconomic status, unmarried, and often illiterate. 18 Similar results were found in other Arab nations. 4

Kane et al. noted that caring for a person with dementia at home often requires foregoing paid labor outside the house and paying for in-home support, such as a nurse or housekeeper. They also mentioned that significant societal changes, namely the move towards a nuclear family structure and away from intergenerational households, may decrease home-based caregiving in the culture. 4 This should not hinder the implementation of culturally specific interventions, such as increasing in-home support for those providing home-based dementia care.

Economic burden of dementia in the MENA region

The economic burden of dementia in the MENA region is significant and expected to rise in the coming decades. It includes direct and indirect costs impacting healthcare systems, families, and the economy, requiring strategic planning and interventions.

Direct costs

Direct costs refer to the expenses associated with medical care, including diagnosis, treatment, hospitalization, and long-term care services for dementia patients. In the MENA region, almost 90% of people with dementia receive their care in non-institutional settings, e.g., nursing homes, which presents a significant economic burden on families and community health resources. 13 Healthcare services: Medical consultations and hospitalizations: Individuals with dementia need regular consultations with specialists such as neurologists and geriatricians. Estimation of economic costs

A study estimated the total direct costs of dementia care for individuals over 50 in the MENA region at approximately USD 8.18 billion. Indirect costs, including lost productivity and informal caregiving, range from USD 2.25 billion to USD 5.67 billion, with a mean value of USD 3.98 billion. When considering these costs as a percentage of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), dementia care represents a substantial economic impact, ranging from 0.45% in Lebanon to 0.05% in Sudan. 13

Future projections

The economic burden of dementia in the MENA region is expected to increase significantly by 2050 due to demographic changes and rising life expectancy. Discussion

Bhalla et al. noted that countries with the highest life expectancy have the highest rate of dementia incidence, as age is a primary risk factor for dementia. 14 Many studies identified modifiable risk factors for dementia: hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, low income, smoking, and low level of education.12,16–18,20,22

Understanding and tackling the growing burden of dementia in the MENA region

The MENA region faces challenges from a projected increase in dementia cases, which could exceed USD 8.18 billion in costs. Recent global estimates indicate that Alzheimer's disease and other dementias rank among the top contributors to disability and death among older adults. 23 This highlights the need for comprehensive planning and interventions to tackle this health issue without worsening economic and social problems. Current estimates often overlook costs linked to dementia care, especially the indirect and intangible costs of informal caregiving that significantly affect caregivers and society. 13

Current prevalence and economic impact

Dementia rates in the MENA region are similar to or below averages, affecting 5% to 7% of individuals over sixty worldwide, 24 with variations among countries. Lebanon's prevalence is 4.88%, resulting in about 0.5% of its GDP spent on dementia management. This indicates resource dedication toward dementia care and reflects varying national support levels. Factors like wars and migration can influence these calculations; individuals developing dementia may relocate to countries with more substantial support before diagnosis. Studies should include costs from productivity loss and caregiver quality of life to guide public health strategies and cost management. 13

The economic and social burden on caregivers

There is no consideration of caregiving in the MENA studies. Additionally, dementia costing studies, which do not include indirect costs in their summary, effectively overstated the total cost.2,13 It is challenging to compare family caregiving behaviors in low-income countries given the many socioeconomic differences. Family caregiving should have contextual considerations for more accurate costing estimates and a fair comparison across countries. In the MENA region, family caregiving was almost exclusively by females, demonstrating a tremendous financial, emotional, and social burden. As illustrated in Figure 5, there is a considerable gender difference, with only approximately 75% of the caregiving completed by female caregivers, compared to male and other formal caregivers.

Limitations and challenges in current research

This assessment highlights limitations in current dementia studies in the MENA region.

Significant heterogeneity across these studies requires cautious interpretation of pooled prevalence estimates, even though a random-effects meta-analysis was applied. Additionally, reliance on English-language studies may introduce bias. Differences in study design, population characteristics, and diagnostic criteria among countries limit generalizability. Future research should prioritize standardized methodologies regionally.

Recommendations for future research and policy development

Addressing the dementia challenge in the MENA region requires integrating care into healthcare frameworks and running awareness campaigns. Public health authorities and policymakers must target risk factors and understand dementia's root causes. Supporting caregivers with financial aid, training, and respite care is vital. The economic impact of dementia in the MENA region is significant. A unified approach with policy enhancements, caregiver initiatives, public education, and ongoing research is essential for effective dementia care. Further studies should assess dementia's burden by country, its implications, and develop tailored care strategies. These efforts will enhance public health planning and benefit individuals with dementia and their caregivers. By advancing programs and research, the MENA region can better prepare for dementia and support affected communities.

Conclusion

This analysis highlights the need for tailored dementia research in the MENA region. Studies focused on individual countries are vital for accurately determining dementia prevalence and crafting public health strategies. Furthermore, research must focus on the needs of caregivers and family members, evaluating their emotional health and offering support. Further research must examine dementia's impacts, including indirect costs, to inform policy and secure funding. Studying caregiving practices will help create culturally respectful care plans.

Public health initiatives should target dementia risk factors to alleviate its burden. Awareness campaigns should promote education, detect hearing loss, and manage hypertension and smoking cessation.

Health communication efforts should reduce the stigma surrounding dementia and emphasize the need for intervention, enhancing support groups, telehealth options, and integrated care, incorporating caregiving structures. Given the significance of family-centered care, supporting informal caregivers is crucial and may be more impactful than merely expanding residential care facilities.

In summary, improving dementia care services in the MENA region is warranted. Enhancing the quality of care provided to individuals living with dementia and their families will positively impact overall public health.
