Integrated transcriptomic and metabolic analysis reveal the mechanism controlling cucumber-pumpkin grafting compatibility
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Xiao-Long Wu, Li-Li Zhang, Shi-Wei Xia, Xiao-Niu Guo, Wan-Lin Yang, Gui-Zhen Rao, Wen-Li Yang, Ling Zhang, Yu Li, Hao-Hui Yang, Xi-Xiang Li, Yun-Song Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe most widely used rootstock of cucumber grafting is pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata). The main task of pumpkin rootstock breeding is to evaluate its compatibility with cucumber scions. One of the main factors affecting grafting compatibility is the rootstock bleeding sap, which affects the wound healing of graft junction.ResultsWe evaluated the grafting affinity of 30 pumpkin rootstocks, which were F1 hybrids derived from 56 breeding lines, by grafting them with the cucumber scion FH8. Correlation analysis of grafting compatibility with phenotypic traits of rootstock seedlings, grafted plants, and biochemical characteristics of rootstock bleeding sap highlighted the positive role of soluble solids content and the negative role of the absorbance of bleeding sapsmeasured at multiple wavelengths (from 280 nm to 700 nm). Bleeding saps from three rootstocks (RS50, RS83, and RS55) with different grafting compatibility were assayed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). The predominant content in bleeding saps was amino acids. Compared with self-rooted-grafting, intergeneric grafting caused changes in the pathways associated with “abiotic stress responses”, “ribosome” and “plant hormone signal transduction” in integrated transcriptomic and metabolic analysis. When compared with the bleeding sap of RS50 (BS-RS50) with low grafting compatibility, BS-RS55 with high grafting compatibility contained much lower content of flavonoids such as nicotiflorin, lonicerin, isoquercitrin and isovitexin. A similar difference profile of these flavonoids was not observed in cucumber scions, although flavonoid pathway was also affected by grafting in scions.ConclusionPumpkin rootstocks show a big variation in grafting compatibility with cucumbers scions, which at least partially depends on the nutrients (bleeding saps) transported from rootstocks to scions. Flavonoids play a negative role in pumpkin-cucumber grafting compatibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07208-z.

Introduction

Grafting is a widely used technique in field production of vegetable crops such as cucumbers (Cucumis sativus), tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum), and watermelons (Citrullus lanatus) [1]. This is usually to enhance plant growth, disease resistance particularly soil-borne diseases, and overall productivity [2]. Environmental factors such as temperature and humidity can determine grafting survival rate [3]. For instance, grafting under high-temperature conditions reduce graft success in cucumbers [4].

Incompatible rootstocks often result in poor graft union formation, reduce nutrient transport, and stunt growth. Therefore, assessing the grafting affinity of scion-rootstock combinations is a major work in rootstock breeding [5]. Intraspecific grafting typically poses no significant compatibility issues; however, interspecific or intergeneric grafting, which holds greater potential, is significantly influenced by the phylogenetic distance between scions and rootstocks [6–8]. Graft incompatibility arises when there is an insufficient vascular connection; anatomical studies of the dynamic graft healing process have emphasized the critical role of callus and cambium formation, as well as vascular reconnections [6, 9]. Plant hormone signaling, particularly involving auxin and cytokinin, is well-known to affect grafting affinity, as these hormones are vital for vascular differentiation and callus formation [10, 11]. Furthermore, genetic modification aimed at enhancing auxin levels and reducing cytokinin expression in root-specific regions has been reported to improve grafting success in tobacco [12, 13].

Increasingly, research is uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying grafting compatibility and incompatibility through metabolic and transcriptomic analyses. Studies have been conducted on fruit crops such as apple [14], pear [15], litchi [16], and grape [17]. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 that grafting induces lipid and phenolic acid accumulation in grape, while transcriptome profiling identified differential β−1,4-glucanase expression patterns post-grafting, contributing to variation in graft compatibility [18]. In litchi, multi-omics analysis demonstrated that differential expression of phenylpropanoid pathway genes across graft combinations alters the abundance of flavonoids, phenolic acids, and lignin compounds, thereby contributing to variations in graft compatibility [19]. Delayed regulation of auxin, cytokinin, and lignin biosynthesis in pear (Pyrus spp.), coupled with pronounced upregulation of the PbBglu13 (a β-glucosidase) gene in heterografts, correlates with differences in graft survival rate compared to homografts [20]. In vegetable crops, transcriptomic analyses have revealed differential expression of genes involved in auxin and cytokinin signaling in Solanaceae [21], while metabolomic studies have highlighted significant shifts in the levels of flavonoids and phenolic compounds at the graft junction [22]. Tomato-pepper graft incompatibility arises from prolonged upregulation of nucleotide-binding and leucine-rich repeat receptors, triggering immune-associated transcriptional signatures that disrupt vascular reconnection through sustained cell death [3]. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and zeatin riboside (ZR) signal transduction pathways and hormones antioxidants biosynthesis pathways were suggested by transcriptomic analysis to affect grafting comparability of watermelon on squash rootstock [23]. Additionally, some omics studies have reported the influence of long-distance transportation of mRNA and non-coding RNAs on grafting outcomes [21, 24].

Previous studies of cucumber-pumpkin grafting compatibility focused on physiological and proteomics aspects [25]. Sucrose content and enzyme activity of bleeding saps from cucumbers and pumpkins were assayed, with results suggesting their contribution to grafting compatibility [26]. This study aims to confirm the role of exudates from rootstock by using as many as 30 pumpkin rootstocks, and profile more metabolites in bleeding saps of rootstock that may regulate grafting compatibility. An integrated analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics of scions was to elucidate the consequence of rootstock differences.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The cucumber scion FH8, a hybrid cultivar bears fruit cucumber, was grafted on 30 pumpkin rootstocks which were produced from crossing between 56 pumpkin accessions from Institution of Flowers and Vegetables, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IVF-CAAS) (Table S1). Self-rooted-grafted cucumber FH8 was used as positive control in term of grafting compatibility.

Rootstock and scion seeds were soaked in warm clean water of 50 ~ 55 °C for 15 min, kept in 30 °C clean water for 8 h, and then kept in a chamber with 28 ~ 30 °C to accelerate germination until radicle reached about 3 mm in length. The scion seeds were sown 4 days later than the rootstock seeds to ensure proper synchronous growth. Grafting was done by insertion method when both rootstock- and scion-seedlings had two cotyledons and one unfolding true leaf. Briefly, we inserted the wedge end of scion hypocotyl directly into a hole at the growth point of rootstock that was made by a grafting needle (1.8 mm in diameter) in advance. For each graft combination, there were three repeats and each repeat consists of 16 grafting plants.

In the first four days after grafting, the grafting plants were grown in a chamber at a temperature of 24 ~ 25 °C/18 ~ 20 °C (day/night) with a humidity of 85 ~ 95% under a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod and shading treatment (100–130 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR) to avoid direct sunlight exposure. Then the temperature and humidity were decreased to 22 ~ 24 °C/12 ~ 15 °C (day/night) and 75 ~ 85%, respectively. At the 10 days post grafting, the sun-shading net was removed and the grafting plants were under normal greenhouse condition.

Bleeding sap collection

Bleeding saps were collected from 32-day-old rootstock seedlings in a cool culture room. The main stem tips were cut off and stem ends were fixed into 10-ml centrifuge tubes. After 16 h, one seedling could exudate 1.57–8.91 g bleeding sap and 10 seedlings per rootstock was used to obtain adequate bleeding saps for following assays. All these bleeding saps were objected to physicochemical property assay. For rootstocks of 50, 55, and 83 (RS50, RS55 and RS83), 9-ml extra bleeding saps were collected for metabolic analysis, which were evenly divided into three portions, frozen rapidly by liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. The bleeding saps were dried up by a vacuum freeze-drying (Scientz-100 F, SCIENTZ, Zhejiag, China). Then 300-µl 70% methanol was added to solute the bleeding sap powder. The new bleeding sap solution was centrifuged at 13,800×g and 4 °C. The supernatant was filtrated by organic membrane of 0.22 μm before Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC)- Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) assay.

Physicochemical property of bleeding sap

Fresh bleeding saps were used. Soluble solid content was detected by a potable refractometer (ATAGO™ 3840, ATAGO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Full wavelength microplate reader (K6600-B, Kai Ao Technology Development Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) was used to detect the max absorbance of bleeding sap under wavelength of 230 nm, 250 nm, 260 nm, 280 nm, 300 nm, 320 nm, 350 nm, 400 nm, 450 nm, 500 nm, 550 nm, 600 nm, 630 nm, 650 nm, 700 nm, 750 nm, 800 nm, 850 nm (Fig. S1).

Scion tissue collection and sample preparation

Scion leaves of three grafting plants at 13 days post-grafting were mixed into one homogenate sample, and there were three sample repeats for each grafting. Each sample was used for both RNA-seq and metabolic assay. For each sample, 50-mg scion homogenate was used for metabolic assay. The homogenate powder was suspended by 1200-µl 70% methanol solution, which was then centrifuged at 4 °C, 13,800×g. The supernatant was filtered by 22-µm organic membrane and finally assayed by LC-MS/MS.

Metabolic assay by UPLC-MS/MS

Metabolites were detected by UPLC (SHIMADZU Nexera X2, Kyoto, Japan)-MS/MS (Applied Biosystems 4500 QTRAP, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). The chromatographic column is Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm * 100 mm). The mobile phase A is pure water with 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 5% B (initial), linearly increased to 95% B over 9 min and held for 1 min, then linearly decreased to 5% B over 1.1 min and held for 2.9 min. The other chromatography conditions were: flow velocity with 0.35 mL/min, column oven temperature at 40 °C, injection volume 4 µl. The spectrometry conditions follow to what was described by Chen [27].

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, sequencing and differential expression analysis

The same scion samples of metabolome assay were used in RNA-seq. Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. RNA degradation and contamination were monitored on 1% agarose gels. RNA purity was checked using the NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). RNA concentration was measured using the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit in the Qubit® 2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). For library preparation, 1 µg of RNA per sample was used with the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA). mRNA was purified, fragmented, and used to synthesize first and second strand cDNA. After end repair and adenylation, adaptors were ligated, and cDNA fragments of 250–300 bp were selected and purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). The library was sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Data analysis involved quality control using fastp v0.19.3 to remove adapter-containing reads, reads with high N content, and low-quality reads [28]. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome ‘9930’ using HISAT v2.1.0 [29]. Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie v1.3.4 [30] to calculate FPKM values. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 v1.22.1 [31], with the Benjamini & Hochberg method for False Discovery Rate (FDR), a corrected P-value [32], and significant differences were determined when FDR < 0.05, |log2foldchange|≥1. In addition, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed on the annotated differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Combined analysis of the metabolome and transcriptome

By integrating metabolomic and transcriptomic profiles, we established metabolite-gene regulatory networks through Pearson correlation analysis. Comparative investigation of DEGs and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) among four grafted unions revealed same pathway signatures, which were functionally characterized via KEGG ontology enrichment analysis.

Results

Components associated with grafting survival rate

The grafting compatibility of 30 pumpkin rootstocks was evaluated by grafting with the same cucumber scion FH-8 (Fig. 1A). To find the best time-point for evaluation, we made a judgment of the grafted plants based on survival rate and robust plant percentage (no wilting leaves at all) at 8th and 13th day post-grafting (Fig. 1B). Basically, the survival rate decreased after grafting. Rootstocks that have higher grafting compatibility got faster graft healing; the robust plant percentage at the 8th day after grafting ranges 41.51%−95.83% with a coefficient of variation of 18.83%, which indicates a satisfying dispersion. We screened out three rootstocks for the following studies that are representative of high grafting affinity (RS55 with robust plant percentage of 95.83%), middle grafting affinity (RS83 with robust plant percentage of 81.25%), and low grafting affinity (RS 50 with robust plant percentage of 64.58%) (Fig. 1, C-D).

To identify factors contributing to grafting compatibility, we performed correlation analysis of robust plant percentage at the 8th day after grafting with phenotypic traits of rootstock and graft plants, as well as bleeding sap of rootstocks (Table S2-S6). The bleeding sap was collected from 32 days-old rootstock seedlings. One seedling can produce 3.49 g bleeding sap on the average in 16 h with the highest yield to be 8.91 g. The value of soluble solid (SS) in bleeding sap ranges 0.26 ~ 0.66 g, with the mean value to be 0.40 g and the coefficient of variation to be 22.77%. We also assayed the max absorbance (OD value) of bleeding sap under a gradient wavelength from 230 to 700 nm by a full wavelength microplate reader (Fig. S1). The biggest difference among rootstocks occurs at a wavelength of 230 nm, 350 nm, and 700 nm. Absorbance at 230 nm is often associated with peptide bonds (proteins), phenolic compounds, or aromatic organic molecules (e.g., polyphenols, tannins); flavonoids, alkaloids, and oxidized phenolic compounds (e.g., quinones), absorb strongly in a rage of 300–400 nm [33]. Pumpkin bleeding saps show a big variation in phenolic and flavonoid compounds. Furthermore, grafting compatibility was positively correlated with soluble solid while negatively with the max absorbance of from 280 nm to 700 nm in bleeding saps (Fig. 1E).

Overview of metabolites in bleeding sap of rootstocks

The significant negative correlation between grafting affinity and rootstock bleeding sap ignited our interesting on bleeding sap composition. Bleeding saps of rootstocks RS50 (BS-RS50), RS55 (BS-RS55), and RS83 (BS-RS83) were assayed by LC-MS/MS. The PCA score plots revealed distinct clusters among the treatments, both in the metabolomic profiles (Fig. S2A) and the transcriptomic data (Fig. S2B), indicating significant metabolite alterations and gene expression changes after grafting. A total of 687 metabolites were finally detected in the three bleeding saps, which are very different to metabolites in cucumber scion tissues (Table 1). Amino acids and their derivatives were predominant, and the top five amino acids are L-Norleucine, L-Isoleucine, L-Leucine, L-Glutamine, L-Lysine (Fig. 2A). In contrast, no predominant metabolites were found in cucumber scions and the top five compounds of the 867 detected metabolites in scion tissues are Cycloleucine, 4-Guanidinobutanal, Diisobutyl phthalate, Vidarabine, and L-Pipecolic Acid (Fig. 2B).

We then profiled the content and composition of amino acids in bleeding sap and scion tissues by heating map (Fig. 2C). Basically, bleeding sap has more upregulated amino acids than scion tissues, like histidine, tryptophan, lysine, valine, isoleucine and so on. There is great difference for some amino acids among bleeding saps. BS-RS83 has impressive high content of cysteine, glutamyl-leucine, glutamylphnylalanine, and jasmonoyl-isoleucine. BS-RS50 has a specifically high content of methionine sulfoxide and homomethionine. BS-RS55 has a specifically high content of acetyl-leucine and dimethylglycine.

To generally profile the difference in rootstock bleeding sap among three graft groups, we did KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to determine if significant variations existed in amino acid metabolism. Notably, phenylpropanoid pathway particularly its branch flavonoid pathway shows the most significant enrichment in all the three rootstock bleeding saps (Fig. 2D). BS-RS50 from rootstock of lowest grafting compatibility was particularly enriched in “flavonoid biosynthesis” and “flavone and flavanol biosynthesis”. BS-RS55 from the best rootstock in grafting compatibility was enriched in “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” but not flavonoid pathways. Unexpected, no amino acid pathways were enriched.

Integration analysis of scion tissues

In order to profile the differences in metabolites and gene expression in the heterogenous grafting process, integration analysis of transcriptome and metabolome was conducted for the scions. A total of 2025 DEGs were identified when comparing with self-root-grafting (FH8-FH8), among which 343 were from FH8-RS50, 1406 were from FH8-RS83, and 610 were from FH8-RS55 (Fig. 3A). Impressively, only 57 DEGs were shared by the three graftings, indicating that heterogeneous grafting can cause exotic gene expression change, which seriously depends on rootstock. Basically, better grafting compatibility, more exotic gene expression. GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs in FH8-RS50 resulted in five pathways, which are “response to toxic substance, oxidative stress, and reactive oxygen” (Fig. 3B). Grafting rejection causes similar intracellular response to abiotic stresses.

As for metabolites, there were 325, 270, and 215 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in FH8-RS50, FH8-RS83 and FH8-RS55 (Fig. 3C). Inconsistent with transcriptomic change, predominant DAMs were shared by the scions from three rootstocks. KEGG enrichment analysis indicates the pathway “Ribosome”, “DNA replication”, “Nitrogen metabolism”, and “plant hormone signal transduction” are influenced by heterogenous grafting (Fig. 3D). The inconsistent between transcriptomic change and metabolite change in scions may due to the influence of rootstocks.

Still, integration analysis of transcriptome and metabolome was done to clarify the influence of heterogenous grafting. The top three metabolic pathways were selected for presentation based on the abundance of DEGs and DAMs within each pathway. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) found significant gene-metabolite association in pathway of “plant hormone signal transduction” (Fig. 3E), “Biosynthesis of amino acids” (Fig. 3F), and “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” (Fig. 3G). Pme2060 was correlated with CsaV3_3G047240 and CsaV3_7G034270. CsaV3_3G036910 was closely correlated with pme0278 and pme2657. CsaV3_5G023050 was closely correlated with mws4085, lmmn001643, and mws0014. Accordingly, integration KEGG analysis also support these pathways. In the pathway of Biosynthesis of amino acids, 5 DEGs and 2 DAMs were highly correlated.

We then compare FH8-RS55 with FH8-RS50 in integration analysis of transcriptome and metabolome. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) identified a total of 5 pathways. The most significant pathways are “Flavonoid biosynthesis” (ko00941) (Fig. 4A), “Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis” (ko00970) (Fig. 4B), and “Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites” (ko01110) (Fig. 4C). Integration KEGG analysis also indicate a significant enrichment of these pathways. In pathway of flavonoid biosynthesis, a total of 3 DAMs and 7 DEGs were identified by CCA, among which a chalcone (Lmsn003297) was positively correlated with the expression level of 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase superfamily protein (CsaV3_5G010170).

Metabolites account for grafting compatibility

To further clarify the role of bleeding sap in grafting compatibility, we did thoroughly comparative analysis of metabolites. There are 380 DAMs between BS-RS50 vs. BS-RS55, 396 DAMs between BS-RS50 vs. BS-RS83, and 379 DAMs between BS-RS55 vs. BS-RS83. Especially, 138 metabolites were commonly observed in all three comparison groups (Fig. 5A).

We then profiled the change of the total 489 DAMs in the three bleeding saps by taking BS-RS50 as a control (Fig. 5B). There were 54 DAMs showing corresponding gradient change with grafting compatibility change among RS50, RS55 and RS83. Specifically, 30 metabolites increased in content along with grafting compatibility increase, while 24 metabolites decreased in content along with grafting compatibility decrease. Between BS-RS50 vs. BS-RS55, all the DAMs can be classified into 11 groups and the group flavonoids had the biggest number of DAMs (Fig. 5C). Moreover, KEGG enrichment analysis indicates that the pathway of flavone and flavanol synthesis has the most DAMs among the enriched pathways (Fig. 5D). These above results suggest that flavonoids in the bleeding sap are highly likely to influence grafting affinity.

Flavonoids and grafting compatibility

Flavonoids in bleeding sap are most likely the key substances affecting grafting affinity. We profiled the content of top 11 flavonoid DAMs between BS-RS50 and BS-RS55 among the three bleeding saps (Fig. 6A). Nicotiflorin, Lonicerin, Baimaside, Cosmosiin, Isoquercitrin, Isovitexin, Vitexin and Vitexin glucoside showed significant higher content in BS-RS50 than in BS-RS55. Particularly, the contents of Nicotiflorin and Lonicerin reached 16 times of that in BS-RS55. Interestingly, most of these flavonoid compounds showed relatively moderate abundant in BS-RS83 (with moderate grafting compatibility). This indicates a basically negative correlation of flavonoids with grafting compatibility.

Discussion

Grafting compatibility is influenced by a range of metabolites and gene expression patterns that regulate stress responses, vascular formation, and cellular communication at the graft junction. Plant hormones, such as auxins, play a crucial role in promoting vascular differentiation and cell division, both of which are essential for graft healing and the successful formation of a graft union [11]. Studies on pear have highlighted the synergistic roles of abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellic acid (GA), and salicylic acid (SA), which are regulated by key genes such as ALDH3F1, YUCCA2, and CKX3 [15]. Comparative genomic analyses have suggested that higher grafting affinity is associated with the upregulation of IAA and ZR in tetraploid-grafted watermelon [23]. Notably, auxin flux from rootstocks to scions has been identified as a critical factor in grafting success for tobacco, apple and litchi [13, 14, 34]. In our study, we observed a significant enrichment of DAMs but not DEGs in the “plant hormone signal transduction” pathway in grafting scions when taking self-root-grafted plants as control (Fig. 3). This suggests that the altered plant hormone signal transduction in scions may be attributed to long-distance hormone transport from rootstocks.

The antioxidant system is another key focus in studies on grafting compatibility as demonstrated in Xijiaqiangsheng watermelon; CAT and SOD were suggested to regulate grafting compatibility by WGCNA analysis under treatments of grafting with diploid and tetraploid watermelon scions [23]. Higher antioxidant activity has been reported in intraspecific grafts, which exhibit greater grafting affinity compared to interspecific grafts [8]. Conversely, downregulation of antioxidants has been observed in rootstocks that enhance tolerance to salinity stress in orange plants [35]. The contradictory role of antioxidants in grafting is likely due to the dual nature of ROS and oxidative stress, which can act as both causes and effects in the grafting process. Notably, grafted plants or tissues collected from grafting sites are typically already under graft-induced stress, complicating the interpretation of the role of antioxidants. In this study, we observed similar functional enrichment in pathways related to the “response to reactive oxygen species” and “response to oxidative stress” in SC50 scions. Beyond the antioxidant enzyme system, some studies have explored differences in metabolite fluxes within xylem sap and phloem exudates, focusing on amino acids, lipids, and secondary metabolites [14, 36]. Amino acids were identified as the predominant compounds in pumpkin bleeding sap. A total of 10 amino acids showed a corresponding change trend with grafting compatibility, which may contribute to the compatibility variance (Fig. 5B).

Flavonoids are well-known for their antioxidant properties and are generally considered beneficial in protecting plants from oxidative stress [37]. However, some studies have suggested that flavonoids may negatively impact grafting compatibility. Transcriptomic analysis of graft union formation in grapes revealed a significant upregulation of flavonoid biosynthesis [17]. In the Solanaceae family, low grafting affinity in interspecific grafts was associated with high flavonoid content [8]. Grafting upregulated key flavonoid biosynthetic genes (DFR, EC 1.1.1.219) but downregulated ANS in tea plant rootstock rhizomes. Consequently, excessive flavonoid accumulation at the graft union impeded healing/compatibility; subsequently, rootstock flavonoids translocated to the scion, affecting development [38]. Similarly, in this study, RS50, which exhibited low grafting affinity, accumulated substantially more flavonoids compared to rootstocks with higher affinity. It is concluded that an increased amount of flavonoid compound is a common response for plants to mechanical wounding [39]. And stronger flavonoid response seems to inevitably result in decreased grafting compatibility in many plants.

By means of multi-omics and morphological anatomy, it is clear that flavonoids accumulation disrupts the graft interphase and affects wound healing [8], but the detailed and beneath mechanism of the regulation remains unknown. While flavonoids help reduce oxidative stress, they may also inhibit the formation of lignin and other cell wall components critical for establishing vascular continuity between the scion and rootstock [40]. A recent study proposed that graft rejection is the result of an immune response that induces cell death, ultimately leading to incompatible graft between tomato and pepper [3]. It is possible that wounding-induced flavonoids are involved in this immune rejection. Functional validation based on genetic engineering approaches may successfully reveal the mechanism in the near future.

Conclusions

Our study explored the grafting compatibility of pumpkin rootstocks with cucumber scion FH8, identifying soluble solids content in rootstock bleeding sap as a key factor promoting grafting compatibility. Amino acids were found to dominate the bleeding sap composition. Comparative transcriptomic and metabolic analyses revealed that rootstocks with lower grafting compatibility exhibited elevated levels of specific flavonoids, such as nicotiflorin and lonicerin, which were identified as primary negative factors affecting compatibility. Additionally, the expression of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis correlated with metabolic changes. These results advance our understanding of grafting compatibility mechanisms and provide critical insights for enhancing rootstock breeding strategies. For further study, we will conduct reciprocal grafting experiments to validate the identified compounds and develop rapid screening methods for rootstock selection, and try to explore the spatial-temporal dynamics of these metabolites during graft union formation to provide deeper insights into their roles in interspecific compatibility mechanisms.
