Complementary and substitution effects of digital finance and green finance on corporate green innovation
Authors: Sijin Tan, Shigui Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the backdrop of the “dual-carbon” goals, traditional financial models are unable to meet the urgent demands of green economic development. The coordinated development of digital finance and green finance has become a critical issue of the times. Existing research has focused more on the independent impacts of digital finance and green finance, while paying insufficient attention to their interactive effects and underlying mechanisms. Therefore, this study examines the green innovation incentive effects of digital finance and green finance, as well as their interactive relationship and underlying mechanisms, by constructing a two-way fixed effects model and applying Logit and Poisson nonlinear models. The analysis is based on data from Chinese A-share listed companies from 2011 to 2023. Research indicates that digital finance and green finance exhibit a stronger substitution effect than a complementary effect in incentivizing strategic green innovation. After testing the nonlinear model, the aforementioned conclusions remain valid. The substitution effect between digital finance and green finance is primarily concentrated in non-high-tech industries, heavily polluting industries, non-state-owned enterprises, and lower-tier cities. When corporate financing constraints are weaker, the substitution effect between digital finance and green finance becomes more pronounced. The stronger a company’s technological absorption capacity, the weaker the substitution effect between digital finance and green finance becomes. The conclusion provides empirical evidence and decision-making support for leveraging diverse financial models to promote green development and sustainable growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39152-9.Keywords: Digital finance, Green finance, Corporate green innovation, Financing constraints, Technology absorption capacitySubject terms: Economics, Economics, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Finance, Finance

Introduction

At the 2020 United Nations General Assembly, General Secretary Xi Jinping announced that China aims to reach peak carbon emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality before 2060. The introduction of the “dual-carbon” goals has established the economic foundation for China’s green and sustainable development. Green transition and development require effective financial support. In 2025, the General Office of the State Council explicitly stated that “green finance” and “digital finance”—two of the five major financial initiatives—should be developed in tandem to jointly serve economic and social development alongside the green transition. Green finance has evolved to date, yet traditional financial institutions remain the primary service providers. However, achieving the “dual-carbon” goals presents a tight timeline and demanding tasks. Traditional financial models struggle to meet the massive demand for green investments and the sophisticated requirements of carbon management. Digital finance is positioned as the technological backbone for green finance, fintech, retirement finance, and inclusive finance. Only by leveraging digital technology to empower green finance can the synergies between the two sectors be fully realized. Specifically, digital finance can leverage emerging digital technologies such as big data and cloud computing to conduct precise identification, evaluation, and risk management of green projects1. It can both enhance the operational efficiency of green finance models and prevent potential “greenwashing” risks (referring to companies or organizations exaggerating environmental achievements through false claims). Digital finance can also acquire customers at lower costs, restructure business processes, and lower the barriers to entry for traditional finance2. The synergistic development of digital finance and green finance is not only an imperative policy requirement but also a practical necessity for enterprises.

Green innovation possesses dual externalities of “green” and “innovation,” serving as the core driving force for green and low-carbon development3. Enterprises are the fundamental units of economic development. The core focus of digital finance and green finance in promoting green economic development lies in fostering green innovation within enterprises. In theory, digital finance and green finance can alleviate the funding constraints on corporate green innovation and partially resolve information asymmetry issues. In practice, however, they have not been fully leveraged. The success of a company’s green innovation initiatives depends not only on the level of financial support but also on how effectively the company utilizes those financial resources. The efficiency of resource utilization is often constrained by factors such as the company’s technological absorption capacity, willingness to innovate, industry characteristics, and the resource endowments of the city where it operates. To delve deeper into the synergistic development of digital finance and green finance, this paper seeks to explore the interactive relationship between digital finance and green finance in corporate green innovation. Is it promoting complementarity? Or mutual inhibition? What mechanisms underlie different types of interactions? Addressing the aforementioned issues will better empower enterprises in green innovation, enhance their efficiency, and achieve green, high-quality economic development.

Research on the interplay between digital finance, green finance, and corporate green innovation has primarily focused on the respective incentive effects of digital finance and green finance on corporate green innovation. Research on the synergistic development of digital finance and green finance lacks sufficient breadth and depth. At the same time, the relationship between digital finance and green finance is not only complementary but may also exhibit a substitution effect. Existing research primarily focuses on mutually reinforcing relationships, with few studies examining substitution relationships. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate both into a unified framework to study their interactive effects and the underlying mechanisms. Currently, digital finance and green finance systems remain in the developmental and refinement stages. Under the dual constraints of limited resources and the approaching “dual-carbon” goals, exploring how digital finance and green finance can synergize and how to promote corporate green innovation most efficiently holds significant practical importance.

The contributions of this study are as follows: (1)First, we examine the interactive effects between digital finance and green finance on green innovation incentives, verifying whether these effects primarily reflect substitution or complementarity. We also consider both linear and nonlinear patterns of these interactive effects. Provide new evidence on the relationship between digital finance, green finance, and corporate green innovation. (2)Second, examining the interaction effects between digital finance and green finance based on distinct corporate characteristics (high-tech versus non-high-tech industries, heavily polluting versus non-heavily polluting industries, state-owned versus non-state-owned enterprises) and tiers of cities (tier-1, tier-2, tier-3, tier-4 and below cities), we distinguish the differential impacts on substantive green innovation and strategic green innovation from the perspective of innovation motivation. (3)Finally, from the perspective of financing constraints and technology absorption capacity, this study explores the specific mechanisms underlying the interactive relationship between digital finance and green finance, clarifying why the two exhibit complementary or substitution effects in incentivizing green innovation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section “Literature review” presents a literature review; Section “Theoretical mechanisms and research hypotheses” presents the research hypotheses; Section “Model setting” introduces model construction, data sources, and processing; Section “Empirical results and analysis” presents empirical findings and mechanism analysis; Section “Conclusions and policy recommendations” presents the research conclusions, policy recommendations, research limitations, and future directions.

Literature review

Green finance primarily addresses financing for the green economy, integrating sustainable economic development with financial concerns. Digital finance is a new business model that integrates traditional finance with fintech, emphasizing the application of digital technologies to financial services. Green finance focuses on directing capital flows toward green projects and industries. Digital finance and green finance are both independent and mutually influential. Given the current context, the integration of digital finance and green finance across various scenarios remains limited. To more cautiously examine the interactive effects between digital finance and green finance in the realm of green innovation incentives, the empirical analysis excludes the influence of digital finance on green finance.

Green innovation in this article primarily refers to green technological innovation, which encompasses new or improved processes, technologies, systems, and products designed to prevent or reduce environmental harm. Unlike traditional innovation, green innovation aims to generate positive environmental benefits4. Therefore, enterprises must bear additional external environmental costs. The intensity of a firm’s innovation activities primarily depends on managers’ assessment of the risks and rewards associated with innovation5. Green innovation not only shares the characteristics of traditional innovation—long cycles, high investment, and high risk—but also incurs additional environmental costs, posing a significant challenge to corporate financial support.

Green innovation is measured primarily in two categories. The first involves constructing an evaluation system for green innovation capability indicators. Researchers have developed a green innovation capability evaluation framework by integrating the entropy weight method across four dimensions: management innovation, process innovation, technological innovation, and product innovation6. This indicator system is better suited to broader green innovation than to green technological innovation. Second, it directly measures corporate green technological innovation. Due to limitations in data availability, early measures of green technology innovation relied on R&D expenditures. With the increasing availability of patent data and the systematic, in-depth analysis of such data, patent metrics have gradually become a primary indicator of green technological innovation7,8. Numerous factors influence corporate green innovation. From an environmental economics perspective, these include various environmental regulations and environmental uncertainty9,10. From a strategic management perspective, the focus is primarily on the impact of internal corporate factors on green innovation strategies11. From an industrial organization perspective, the emphasis is on examining corporate attitudes toward green innovation in light of market structure12. As green innovation research deepens, scholars are increasingly focusing on specific factors, such as corporate ESG ratings, digital transformation, and climate risk. Corporate ESG ratings and green innovation exhibit a U-shaped relationship13. Enterprise digital transformation has the most significant impact on green innovation in manufacturing enterprises, particularly substantive green innovation14. Climate risks influence corporate green innovation efficiency through two stages: green technology R&D and green technology commercialization15.

Regarding the relationship between digital finance, green finance, and green innovation, scholars have conducted research from various perspectives. First, scholars believe that digital finance can effectively promote green innovation in enterprises. The advantage of digital finance lies in leveraging information technology to create new service scenarios and models, thereby broadening the scope of services. This approach effectively guides capital allocation to support the green innovation of financial service enterprises16. Digital finance possesses the capacity to pool fragmented capital, enabling it to support enterprises in pursuing green innovation activities by broadening their financing channels17. Research also indicates that digital finance curbs carbon emissions by promoting green technological innovation18. Green finance primarily promotes corporate green innovation through green credit. The incentive effect of green credit is first manifested at the macro level. Green credit can pool funds through differentiated monetary and financial policies, including but not limited to preferential credit allocation and floating interest rates, thereby providing financial support to enterprises19. The incentive effect of green credit is further manifested at the micro level. Green credit operations prompt financial institutions to systematically collect information on enterprises’ green projects. By integrating this data with publicly available government lists of polluting enterprises and relevant standards, such practices reduce investors’ information acquisition costs20.

Regarding the interactive effects between digital finance and green finance, they can be primarily categorized into the following two types: First, the direct effect of integrating digital finance with green finance. Using a quantitative model to evaluate green bond yields21. Or shadow banking risk exposure may have a positive impact on the resilience of green new quality productivity forces22. Research has also revealed the interconnections among digital finance, green finance, and social finance. Through conceptual modeling, it concludes that digital finance serves as a significant driving force for green and efficient financing as well as social finance23. Some articles have directly proposed the concept of “green digital finance,” defined as digitalized green finance. This approach measures the total capital invested in clean energy and solar power (including fuel cells) for research and innovation, emphasizing the impact of green digital finance on renewable energy and climate change24. Second, the indirect impact effects of digital finance and green finance interactions on third parties. Digital inclusive finance not only directly reduces urban carbon intensity and promotes renewable energy adoption, but also has indirect impacts on low-carbon emissions reduction and renewable energy utilization through green credit mechanisms25,26. Digital financial technologies can also directly enhance enterprises’ access to green financing channels, thereby synergistically advancing the development of renewable energy in China27. Considering a scenario in which green finance takes precedence and digital finance serves as a supplement, green finance policies can also influence environmental quality through digital finance28. Research has also suggested that both green bonds and digital finance positively influence green finance performance29.

Theoretical mechanisms and research hypotheses

Digital finance promotes green innovation in enterprises through the following avenues: First, digital finance can establish data platforms to enhance transparency between the financial sector and financing entities30. Digital finance reduces the degree of information asymmetry between parties and effectively mitigates credit misallocation caused by information asymmetry, ultimately achieving efficient resource allocation31. Second, digital finance can broaden financing channels and pool fragmented capital17, thereby lowering financing barriers for small and medium-sized enterprises32 and providing robust financial support for green innovation. Third, digital finance enhances corporate financing efficiency by leveraging new digital technologies33. Digital technologies can also improve enterprises’ data-driven dynamic capabilities34, providing greater convenience for corporate green innovation activities.

The incentive effect of green finance on green innovation is primarily manifested in the availability of capital. When enterprises demonstrate strong environmental performance or effective environmental risk management, they gain access to greater external financing35. When a company’s environmental pollution levels are high, or it becomes involved in serious incidents that attract public attention, it becomes more difficult for the company to secure stable, continuous funding from external sources. Green finance funds must be used for research, development, and innovation in green technologies, thereby driving technological advancement in enterprises36. For instance, green credit considers a loan project’s environmental risks during the approval process37. To ensure funds are directed toward green projects, institutions also conduct post-project oversight. Therefore, this paper proposes Hypotheses 1 and 2:

H1

Digital finance can promote green innovation in enterprises.

H2

Green finance can promote green innovation in enterprises.

The theory of corporate innovation financing posits that different financing methods exert varying impacts on corporate innovation activities38. The advantages and disadvantages of innovative corporate financing methods can be evaluated through risk-return matching, resolution of information asymmetry, and maturity matching39,40. When green finance and digital finance operate independently, each serves as an independent driver of innovation. However, when both provide financial support to enterprises and interact with each other, digital finance and green finance may produce either a complementary or a substitution effect. The complementary effect refers to how the development of digital finance can enhance the role of green finance in promoting green innovation. The substitution effect is precisely the opposite. The development of digital finance may compete with green finance, thereby hindering the growth of green finance’s marginal effects on green innovation.

When the complementary effects occur, digital finance and green finance leverage their respective strengths to compensate for each other’s shortcomings, thereby more effectively promoting corporate green innovation. In terms of risk-return matching, digital finance can only generate fixed returns, and the underlying financial institutions exhibit a lower tolerance for risk. Regarding green finance, China is the first country to establish green finance reform and innovation pilot zones41. Green finance has been incorporated into China’s national policy framework. The government will also provide corresponding policy subsidies and support. The financial institutions behind it will be better equipped to bear risks. In addressing information asymmetry, green finance faces challenges in practice, including insufficient scale of development and inefficient resource allocation, which may even suppress corporate green innovation in the short term. Digital finance effectively reduces information asymmetry between parties by integrating digital technology into financial operations42. In terms of maturity matching, innovation requires long-term financial support. Both digital finance and green finance involve fixed-term principal and interest repayment requirements, but their timing can be optimally combined to incentivize corporate green innovation.

When the substitution effect occurs, digital finance and green finance may overlap in function. This overlap can lead to resource surplus, reduced resource utilization efficiency, and low conversion rates for enterprises’ green innovation outcomes. From the perspective of funding supply for green innovation, the capital requirements for innovation projects are limited in the short term. When digital finance and green finance simultaneously provide financial support, it may lead to redundant funding. Consequently, a portion of the capital intended to incentivize green innovation could be displaced. From the perspective of green innovation outputs, green innovation often involves longer innovation cycles. The efficiency of technological innovation is constrained by internal corporate resources and capabilities, such as financial risk-bearing capacity43, firm size44, and firm absorptive capacity45. When digital finance and green finance simultaneously provide funding support to enterprises, they do not enhance corporate innovation capabilities but instead reduce the return on green innovation for businesses. Additionally, green innovation relies on substantial resource investments by enterprises, and its positive impact on corporate performance manifests over an extended period. The short-term capital returns demanded by digital finance and green finance can create immediate performance pressures for enterprises. Such pressures may lead companies to abandon green innovation activities characterized by high investment, high risk, and high uncertainty.

Complementary and substitution effects may coexist. When the complementary effect outweighs the substitution effect, the incentive effect of green finance on corporate green innovation can be enhanced by developing digital finance. When the complementary effect is smaller than the substitution effect, the incentive effect of green finance on corporate green innovation will be weakenedas digital finance develops. Therefore, this paper proposes Hypotheses 3a and 3b:

H3a

Digital finance and green finance primarily exhibit a complementary effect in incentivizing corporate green innovation.

H3b

Digital finance and green finance primarily exhibit a substitution effect in incentivizing corporate green innovation.

Due to the limited nature of corporate funds, the degree of financing constraints often becomes an obstacle to enterprises’ access to capital and financial services46. Green innovation activities carry higher risks and uncertainties than conventional innovation activities. Consequently, green innovation faces more severe financing constraints47. Digital finance leverages technologies such as big data and cloud computing to match the investment and financing needs of lenders and borrowers, thereby reducing information asymmetry and alleviating corporate financing constraints31. Ultimately, green finance promotes corporate green innovation. In contrast, green finance employs more direct means. It promotes corporate green innovation by increasing the proportion of long-term loans in corporate debt and improving corporate debt structures36. The commonality between the two lies in their impact on corporate green technological innovation by alleviating financing constraints.Under certain financing constraints faced by enterprises, digital finance and green finance can generate a complementary effect, thereby promoting green innovation within businesses. Furthermore, due to differences in the degree of financing constraints faced by enterprises, as well as their capabilities and willingness to pursue green innovation, digital finance and green finance may also exhibit a substitution effect. On the one hand, digital finance and green finance provide ample financial resources to enterprises with relatively low financing constraints, potentially leading to resource waste. On the other hand, enterprises facing financing difficulties themselves harbor multiple issues. Taking small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) as an example, the primary barriers to green innovation among SMEs include insufficient funding, knowledge gaps, uncertain returns, and weak technological capabilities48. Its green innovation efficiency is relatively low, and its resource utilization efficiency is not high. Even with financial support, it is prone to generating a substitution effect. Enterprises facing financing constraints often have weak foundations for green innovation, outdated production equipment, and low technological capabilities due to capital limitations. Under resource-constrained conditions, managers often seek to avoid risks and lack the willingness to innovate49. Therefore, for enterprises facing financing difficulties, the substitution effect of digital finance and green finance in incentivizing green innovation may be even stronger. Based on this, Hypothesis 4 is proposed:

H4

The complementary or substitution effect of digital finance and green finance on corporate green innovation incentives operates through financing constraint mechanisms.

Absorptive capacity serves as the foundation for enterprises seeking innovation and collaboration, representing the dynamic ability to identify, assimilate, and apply external new knowledge. This is the absorption capacity in a broad sense. At the technical level, absorptive capacity refers to the ability to acquire, learn, and utilizenew external technologies50, termed technological absorptive capacity. The absorptive capacity and innovation capability of enterprises often exhibit a positive correlation51. By identifying internal and external information and applying it to economic activities, enterprises can effectively transform resources into innovative outcomes52. The most crucial factor is technological absorption capacity. This capacity can promote innovation-driven growth in lagging economies by absorbing advanced technologies and outputs from abroad53. The stronger a firm’s absorptive capacity, the more conducive it is to enhancing the effectiveness of its R&D collaborations and acquisition of external technologies, thereby boosting its innovation performance54.

The strength of a company’s absorptive capacity may influence the intensity of the complementary or substitution effect. Absorption capacity serves as a bridge for integrating internal resources and external knowledge within enterprises, enabling targeted absorption of innovative knowledge to drive green innovation activities. When enterprises possess a certain level of knowledge and technology conversion capabilities, the primary constraint on their green innovation is insufficient funding. Various financial channels, such as digital finance and green finance, provide financing that alleviates enterprises’ funding constraints and can effectively promote their green innovation. However, if a company lacks sufficient absorption capacity—particularly in terms of technological absorption—it will be unable to assimilate imported technologies and advanced production practices. Consequently, it will be unable to build upon these foundations to drive innovation. Even if digital finance and green finance provide ample funding, enterprises cannot generate sufficient innovation outcomes. Therefore, at this juncture, digital finance and green finance exhibit a significant substitution effect on green innovation. Based on this, Hypothesis 5 is proposed:

H5

The complementary or substitution effect between digital finance and green finance on corporate green innovation incentives operates through the technology absorption capacity mechanism.

Other variables. Financing Constraint FC Index (FC), the FC Index focuses more on the impact of internal cash flows on a company’s financing needs. This index primarily relies on corporate financial metrics to measure the probability of financing constraints occurring each year, with values ranging from 0 to 160. The Financing Constraint WW Index (WW) incorporates industry characteristics on top of corporate financial metrics, thereby possessing a degree of exogeneity61. The general approach to measuring absorption capacity employs R&D intensity (R&D expenditure/enterprise operating revenue) as an indicator, focusing on resource allocation at the R&D level. This method fails to reflect a company’s comprehensive absorption capacity accurately. Therefore, based on the research by Xiao and Xie50, the scope of this study is limited to technological absorption capacity (TAC). The indicators of technological absorption capacity can be examined from three dimensions: (1) Initial technological level (Tech): It is assumed that the higher a firm’s initial technological level, the stronger its capacity to adopt and transform new technologies. This assesses technological absorption capability from the perspective of the technological foundation. (2) Absorption capacity (Abs), measured by a company’s expenditure on absorbing new knowledge and technologies. The greater a company’s willingness to invest in assimilating new expertise and techniques, the stronger its absorption capacity and intent to absorb. This primarily assesses technological absorption capability through the lens of a company’s willingness and urgency to absorb. (3) Technological transformation capability (Trans), measured by corporate expenditures on technological transformation. Investment in technological transformation reflects a company’s capacity to innovate by applying its acquired experience and technologies, serving as a more advanced measure of its assimilation capabilities.

Controlling variables. To minimize the impact of omitted variables, this study selected multiple variables at both the micro-enterprise and macro-levels for control. Internal firm characteristics are key influencing variables for green innovation11. Therefore, at the micro level, this includes variables related to the firm’s production and operational characteristics. For example, company age (Age): the older a company is, the less likely it is to pursue green innovation. Enterprise asset scale (Lasset): The larger the enterprise, the more resources available for green innovation. The tangible asset ratio (Tang): The structure of corporate assets influences the willingness and cost of green innovation. Cash Flow Ratio (Cashflow): The more robust a company’s cash flow, the better it can ensure investment in green innovation. Return on Assets (ROA): A high return on assets enables a company to allocate more funds toward green innovation. Furthermore, considering the impact of regional economies and markets on green innovation, we introduce regional economic development levels (GDP) and market monopoly levels (Li). Regional economic development and corporate green innovation often exhibit a positive correlation62, while market monopoly levels may also stimulate corporate green innovation63.

This study employs empirical analysis using data from A-share listed companies on the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges in China from 2011 to 2023. To enhance the representativeness of the sample, the data underwent the following processing: 1) Exclude data from listed companies in the financial sector, such as insurance and banking; 2) Exclude companies that were designated as ST, *ST, PT, or delisted during the sample period; 3) Exclude companies with significant data gaps; 4) Apply 1% tail trimming to all continuous variables to eliminate the influence of outliers. Green credit data is sourced from the China Financial Yearbook and the China Industrial Statistical Yearbook65,66. Green investment data is sourced from the China Environmental Statistical Yearbook and the China Energy Statistical Yearbook67,68. Green insurance data is sourced from the China Insurance Yearbook69. Green support data is sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook70. Green bonds, green funds, and green equity are sourced from the Wind database71. The above data also draws upon provincial and municipal statistical yearbooks and statistical bulletins. Technical absorption capacity data are sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook of Science and Technology72. Enterprise green innovation data are collected from the China National Intellectual Property Administration73. All other data originate from the CSMAR database74. Benchmark regression results

Table 5 reports the regression results for digital finance, green finance, and green innovation. As shown in columns (1) to (4), digital finance and green finance both exert positive incentive effects on both substantive green innovation and strategic green innovation. The incentive effects of digital finance and green finance are non-equilibrium, with a higher impact coefficient on substantive green innovation for enterprises. Substantive green innovation by enterprises aims to drive technological advancement and gain competitive advantages, representing their long-term development potential. In Models (1) and (3), higher Index and Green coefficients indicate that financial resources are allocated more heavily toward substantive green innovations that contribute greater value. The empirical results in columns (1) to (4) of Table 5 indicate that Hypotheses 1 and 2 hold. When incorporating the interaction term between digital finance and green finance, the interaction term for substantive green innovation is negative but not significant. The interaction coefficient for strategic green innovation is significantly negative. The results indicate that digital finance and green finance exhibit a certain degree of competitive substitution in promoting corporate green innovation. The marginal effect of green finance on strategic green innovation diminishes as digital finance grows. Hypothesis 3b holds. The substitution effect outweighs the complementarity effect and prevails. This may be attributed to the fact that, on the one hand, the capital provided by digital finance and green finance alleviates enterprises’ financing constraints, thereby promoting green innovation to a certain extent. However, the ultimate goal of strategic green innovation in enterprises is to align with government policies and environmental regulations75,76. Once a certain quantity or target is reached, enterprises may lose the motivation to continue innovating, failing to maximize the utilization of digital finance and green finance resources. Ultimately, the substitution effect prevailed. Therefore, enterprises need to continue improving the efficiency of utilizing the financial resources they have obtained. In contrast, financial institutions should focus more on enterprises’ substantive green innovation outputs rather than strategic innovation. At the same time, financial support for the real economy requires more meticulously designed policies to prevent strategic responses from enterprises and the resulting waste of resources.

Robustness test

Replace the dependent variable

For corporate green innovation, the article employs the Greinratio (proportion of green invention patent applications) and the Greumratio (proportion of green utility model patent applications) as proxies to assess the robustness of the results. Second, we employ the metrics of green invention patent applications per 10,000 residents (Greinpo) and green utility model patent applications per 10,000 residents (Greumpo) at the prefecture-level city level to measure green innovation levels, thereby eliminating the non-comparable factors introduced by differences in urban population size. As shown in columns (2) and (4) of Table 6, the interaction coefficients for Greumratio and Greumpo are significantly negative at the 5% and 10% levels, respectively, consistent with the results of the baseline regression. Upon further consideration, it takes considerable time for a company’s R&D investments to translate into tangible green innovation outcomes. Additionally, there is a time lag of several months to as long as two to three years between patent application and patent grant. Therefore, green innovation is measured by the number of green patent applications rather than the number of patents granted. Green innovation activities require sustained long-term financial investment77, resulting in a time lag in the impact of digital finance and green finance on corporate green innovation. This lag may persist for an extended period. The patent development cycle can range from several months to several years, depending on the nature of the patent. This analysis focuses solely on the immediate impacts. Therefore, we apply two-period and four-period lags to the dependent variable to verify whether the substitution effect exists. After lagging the dependent variable by two periods, the substitution effect between digital finance and green finance on enterprises’ green utility model patents persists. After a lag of four periods, the substitution effect ceases to be significant, indicating that it persists in the short-term lag.

Endogeneity treatment

Reverse Causality Issue: The dependent variable consists of micro-level data on corporate green innovation, while the independent variable comprises prefecture-level digital finance and green finance levels. Data originates from two distinct databases, which partially mitigates reverse causality concerns. Additionally, this study employs first-order lagged terms for all explanatory variables to assess how prior-period digital finance, green finance, firm-level variables, and macroeconomic variables influence a firm’s green innovation in the current period. This approach mitigates the issue of reverse causality to a certain extent. The results are consistent with the baseline regression.

Omission Variable Issue: Endogeneity concerns can be mitigated by selecting appropriate instrumental variables. We employ a 2SLS regression to test the model, treating digital finance, green finance, and their interaction term as endogenous variables. The tests are conducted using instrumental variables derived from digital finance and green finance, respectively. We employ the distance (Distance) from the prefecture-level city where listed firms are headquartered to Hangzhou as an instrumental variable for digital finance. On the one hand, Hangzhou’s digital finance development ranks among the nation’s top tier. The distance between prefecture-level cities where listed companies are headquartered and Hangzhou is closely correlated with the level of digital finance development in those cities, supporting the correlation hypothesis. On the other hand, the distance between the prefecture-level city where the listed company is located and Hangzhou neither directly affects regional digital finance development nor, by affecting regional digital finance, indirectly influences corporate green innovation activities, thereby satisfying the exogeneity assumption. The instrumental variable for green finance is represented by its lagged value. The lagged green finance variable is exogenous and unaffected by current shocks. Table 9 presents the regression results. As shown in Table 9, under the conditions of endogenous Index and Green, the interaction term coefficient in the strategic green innovation model remains significantly negative at the 5% level, indicating a significant substitution effect. When both Index and Green are treated as endogenous variables, the interaction term coefficients for both substantive green innovation and strategic green innovation models are significantly negative. The endogenousness test results are consistent with the benchmark regression. All of the above models reject the weak instrumental variable hypothesis, exhibit no identification issues, employ well-specified instrumental variables, and yield robust results.

Analysis of financing constraint mechanisms

To investigate the mechanism underlying the substitution effect between digital finance and green finance, we incorporate a variable measuring the degree of corporate financing constraints into our analysis. At the same time, enterprises face limited financing channels and rely heavily on digital and green finance for funding. This will prompt them to proactively adjust their strategies and pursue green innovation to secure additional resources. Enterprises with relatively ample capital tend to rely less on digital finance and green finance channels, and their green innovation activities are more inclined toward proactive choices. At this stage, what constrains the efficiency of green innovation in enterprises is no longer financing constraints, but rather their willingness and capacity to innovate. Analysis of technology absorption capacity mechanisms

The technological absorption capacity of enterprises at different levels is measured by their initial technological level, assimilation and absorption capability, and technological transformation capability. The interaction coefficients for Index×Green×Tech_inv and Index×Green×Tech_uma in columns (1) and (2) of Table 15 are both insignificant, indicating that a firm’s initial technological level does not influence the interaction effect between digital finance and green finance on green innovation. A possible reason is that companies initially have limited technological capabilities, leaving little room for improvement in green innovation. For strategic green innovation, technological complexity is relatively low, making it less influenced by initial technological capabilities and more strongly shaped by the willingness to pursue it80. The coefficient of the interaction term Index×Green×Abs in columns (3) and (4) is significantly positive, opposite in direction to the coefficient of the interaction term Index×Green. This indicates that the stronger a firm’s absorption capacity, the weaker the substitution effect of digital finance and green finance on green innovation. When enterprises lack the capacity to assimilate and absorb new technologies, even if financing from digital finance and green finance can introduce cutting-edge technologies, production expertise, and talent, these enterprises will be unable to digest, absorb, and master them, let alone transform them into innovative outcomes. At this point, achieving the complementary effect is difficult, while the substitution effect prevails. The coefficients for the interaction term Index×Green×Trans in columns (5) and (6) also demonstrate the role of technological absorption capacity. The stronger a firm’s capacity for technological transformation, the weaker the substitution effect becomes. A company’s technological transformation capability represents breakthrough innovation beyond its existing technical level. Without this capability, enterprises cannot effectively utilize the resources provided by digital finance and green finance, leading to an intensified substitution effect.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Green innovation incentives for enterprises are a critical issue on the path to green development and sustainable growth. Understanding the incentive effects of different financial models and their interrelationships provides essential evidence for governments to formulate green policies. This study finds that: (1) Both digital finance and green finance exhibit strong green innovation incentive effects. However, this incentive effect is uneven, exerting a more substantial influence on substantive green innovation. (2) Digital finance and green finance primarily exert a substitution effect in incentivizing green innovation. This substitution effect is most evident in strategic green innovation by enterprises. Moreover, this substitution effect exhibits heterogeneity, being more pronounced in non-high-tech industries, heavily polluting sectors, non-state-owned enterprises, and lower-tier cities. (3) The substitution effect between digital finance and green finance operates through the financing constraint mechanism. For enterprises with weaker financing constraints, the substitution effect will be correspondingly more substantial. (4) The substitution effect between digital finance and green finance operates through the technology absorption capacity mechanism. Among enterprises with insufficient technology absorption capacity, the substitution effect will correspondingly be more substantial.

Based on the above conclusions, the following policy recommendations are proposed:

At the financial institution level, state-owned commercial banks should proactively respond to national policies and take the lead in promoting the integrated development of digital finance and green finance, thereby setting industry benchmarks. The core approach is to leverage digital technology to advance green finance and alleviate its core challenges, such as environmental information asymmetry. State-owned commercial banks shall actively develop and utilize digital green financial products, establish digital green finance platforms, and encourage innovation in green financial products. Shareholding commercial banks and city commercial banks should prioritize the integrated development of digital finance and green finance based on their own growth trajectories. The business processes of green projects should first undergo digital transformation, followed by further strengthening the digital applications within these green projects.

At the corporate level, enterprises should further integrate digital finance and green finance with their production and business operations, fully leveraging financial resources to enhance the efficiency of green innovation. Overall, enterprises should prioritize resources toward substantive green innovation projects that can create long-term competitive advantages, drive substantive innovation outcomes, and cultivate core innovation capabilities. Enterprises should enhance their capacity to absorb and transform new knowledge and technologies, establish a mature system for resource input and green innovation output, and improve the efficiency of green innovation. High-pollution enterprises should proactively strengthen their commitment to environmental responsibilities, prioritize the output of green innovation projects focused on energy conservation and emission reduction, quantify environmental responsibilities as “green assets,” and achieve sustainable development.

At the governmental level, it is essential to consider the synergistic effects between digital finance and green finance policies during policy design. Digital finance and green finance systems are still evolving and improving. When formulating policies for digital finance and green finance, consideration should be given to their complementary strengths, striving to avoid overlap in coverage and functionality. Second, digital finance and green finance policies should account for differences in corporate characteristics, industries, and cities. Differentiated policies are implemented for high-tech industries, heavily polluting industries, and state-owned enterprises. For lower-tier cities with relatively underdeveloped economies, implement preferential policies to strengthen digital financial infrastructure, attract more talent, narrow the gap with higher-tier cities, and achieve coordinated development. Finally, digital finance and green finance policies should prioritize enterprises facing financing difficulties. Enterprises with a willingness to pursue green innovation but lacking access to financing should be designated as key beneficiaries of policy support.

The study has two limitations. First, as digital finance and green finance develop, greater convergence between the two may emerge. The application of digital technologies in digital finance to green finance development may even give rise to new concepts or models, such as the emerging “digital green finance platform.” Under the new development paradigm, digital finance and green finance are inseparable and must be measured as a new variable. Secondly, it lies in the limitations of the data, which primarily relies on information from A-share listed companies. However, for unlisted small and medium-sized enterprises, digital finance holds greater potential to fulfill its inclusive role, and its relationship with green finance may also transform. Future discussions may focus on the following areas. On the one hand, using the “digital green finance platform” as a new model, research its impact on corporate green innovation. Analyze the differences in green innovation incentives between independent digital finance and green finance under the new integrated model. On the other hand, collect data on small and medium-sized enterprises to re-examine the interplay between digital finance and green finance.
