Effect of obesity on endovenous ablation outcomes in the Vascular Quality Initiative
Authors: Lavender Micalo, Juan Carlos Jimenez
Published: 2026

We congratulate Levy et al1 from the University of Michigan for their recent retrospective multicenter cohort study reporting clinical outcomes following endovenous truncal ablation across the body mass index (BMI) spectrum. With rapidly emerging medical therapies for obesity, this topic needs more clinical data to fill existing knowledge gaps. Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) is a worldwide public health problem, and obesity has been identified as an independent risk factor for disease progression and severity.2,3 Nevertheless, current evidence on the impact of obesity on outcomes following endovenous saphenous vein ablation remains conflicting, which may influence practice patterns and insurance coverage policies that disadvantage patients with CVI and high BMI.4 In this issue of the Journal of Vascular Surgery Venous and Lymphatic Disorders, the investigators present a multicenter analysis of the Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) Varicose Vein Registry, examining the relationship between BMI and outcomes after endovenous truncal ablation, with implications for patient selection, counseling, and treatment decision-making.

The authors analyzed 10,494 patients who underwent truncal endovenous ablation, comparing outcomes by BMI in separate cohorts undergoing ablation alone or ablation with concomitant phlebectomy. Among patients undergoing concomitant phlebectomy, the probability of improvement in the Revised Venous Clinical Severity (rVCSS) and symptom scores was >92% regardless of BMI, and higher BMI was not associated with greater complications or recanalization. In contrast, among patients undergoing ablation alone, higher BMI was associated with an increased likelihood of recanalization and a modest reduction in the magnitude of rVCSS improvement. Based on these findings, the authors suggest that obesity alone should not preclude patients from undergoing endovenous thermal ablation.

Using a strong, clear study design, the VQI Varicose Vein Registry provides a large, nationally representative, multicenter cohort of 10,494 patients, enhancing generalizability. Analyzing the ablation alone and ablation with phlebectomy cohorts separately allows for nuanced, treatment-specific interpretation of outcomes. Importantly, using absolute rather than relative improvements in rVCSS and symptom scores addresses a key methodological limitation of previous studies, which may systematically disadvantage patients with greater disease severity upon presentation.

The authors acknowledge several limitations. The 60-day median follow-up limits the assessment of long-term durability, which is relevant for late recanalization and sustained symptom relief. The long-term complication data for approximately 50% of patients were missing, reducing statistical power for complication analyses. Although BMI is widely used as a measure of obesity, it does not capture true body composition specific to lean muscle mass, body fat percentage, and bone density. In addition, the VQI registry only captures patients who underwent procedures, but those who were not offered intervention because of BMI are missing, a potential source of selection bias.

These findings arrive at a particularly relevant moment, as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are reshaping the medical management of obesity worldwide. Their effects on patients with CVI require further investigation. Our recent review of TriNetX US Collaborative Network data demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor agonists may play a clinically beneficial role in reducing complications and improving survival in obese patients with CVI.5 As these agents drive substantial weight loss among patients with venous disease, clinicians will increasingly encounter and counsel obese patients who are actively losing weight with GLP-1 receptor agonists, or are contemplating pharmacologic therapy before proceeding with truncal ablation. This study’s demonstration that concomitant phlebectomy mitigates the BMI-associated early recanalization risk seen with ablation alone offers reassurance that obesity itself need not delay intervention pending weight loss. At the same time, the rising prevalence of GLP-1 use raises unanswered questions: does pharmacologically induced weight loss alter venous hemodynamics, or wound healing through alternative physiologic pathways that may modify procedural outcomes? Future VQI analyses stratifying by GLP-1 exposure could meaningfully extend this work.

Levy et al1 offer compelling evidence that obesity alone should not preclude endovenous ablation, particularly when paired with phlebectomy. As pharmacologic weight management reshapes the CVI population, our clinical decision-making must evolve in parallel; treating obesity not as a barrier to intervention but as a modifiable factor that demands individualized, informed procedural planning.
