Successful management of paraquat poisoning: a case report
Authors: Amarja Ashok Havaldar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParaquat is a herbicide used for weed control and is one of the lethal poisons associated with high mortality. Exposure to this compound could be accidental or as a deliberate self-harm. Clinical manifestations can range from mild symptoms initially to multiorgan failure. Owing to high case fatality (50–90%) and no specific antidote is available; different treatment regimens are proposed with variable success rates. Here, we present two patients with paraquat poisoning, their initial presentation, clinical progress, and successful management.Case presentationTwo young adults, one 29-year-old male and one 32-year-old female patient from South India, presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and worsening renal and Liver failure. Patients had already received gastric lavage at the local hospital and later transferred to our hospital. We initiated immunosuppressive therapy with cyclophosphamide at 15 mg/kg/day for 3 days and steroids. Both patients received renal replacement therapy and N-acetyl cysteine infusion. We observed improvement in clinical and biochemical parameters over a week. There were no respiratory symptoms throughout the hospitalization. At the time of discharge, both patients were off renal replacement therapy. On long-term follow-up after 2.5 years, both patients had recovered well without any renal dysfunction.ConclusionThis case report highlights the successful management of paraquat poisoning with high-dose cyclophosphamide with steroids. The timely initiation of immunosuppression prevented disease progression and facilitated prompt recovery.Keywords: Cyclophosphamide, Dexamethasone, Immunosuppression therapy, Paraquat, Plasma exchange

Introduction

Paraquat is a herbicide with a chemical composition of 1,1′-dimethyl-4,4′-bipyridinium dichloride [1, 2]. The primary use of paraquat is for weed control. Accidental or deliberate self-harm can lead to inhalation and ingestion of the compound. It is hydrophilic and does not penetrate intact skin. However, there is mention about dermal exposure [3]. Paraquat is rapidly inactivated in soil [4]

The clinical signs and symptoms can range from the development of oral ulceration, abdominal pain, reduced urine output, renal failure, jaundice, and hypoxemic respiratory failure leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [5, 6]. The mortality remains high, ranging between 50 and 90% [1, 2]. The intensivist should have a high index of suspicion as the timely initiation of treatment can significantly improve patient outcomes.

The mechanism of toxicity is due to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals. Paraquat is reduced by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). Paraquat stimulates the formation of ROS and acts on NADPH oxidase and nuclear factor kappa B (NFkB). [4]. It causes extensive inflammation, triggering a cytokine response. The respiratory system is predominantly involved. Paraquat is absorbed in the lungs through an energy-dependent process, resulting in a higher concentration in the lungs than plasma. Progressive lung damage is seen similar to ARDS [4, 7]. The ROS causes damage to proximal tubules, resulting in acute kidney injury [8]. Painful oral ulcers are a characteristic feature of paraquat poisoning [9]. A urine or plasma dithionate test helps to determine the paraquat level quantitatively [10].

We present two cases of paraquat poisoning, their initial presentation, clinical features and discuss the different therapies available. The initial management of any poisoning includes reducing further absorption, using renal replacement therapy (RRT) for rapid excretion, treating with the antidote, and supportive therapy. For paraquat, initial treatment involves gastric lavage. Early use of charcoal and hemoperfusion helps to decrease plasma levels of paraquat rapidly [11, 12]. As paraquat causes severe inflammation, immunomodulators, such as cyclophosphamide and steroids, either methylprednisolone or dexamethasone, have been used and showed mixed results [13–15]. RRT, hemoperfusion, and vitamins are studied in the treatment of paraquat poisoning [7].

Case presentation

Here, we describe two patients of paraquat poisoning from South India.

Case 1—A 29-year-old male with no comorbidities presented with alleged history of consumption of paraquat. Following this, he started complaining of abdominal pain and breathing difficulty. In the local hospital, post gastric lavage, patient had vomiting and loose stools. Owing to progressively worsening renal function and the requirement for dialysis, he was referred to our hospital. His vitals were pulse rate of 92 beats per minute, blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg, respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, SpO2 of 97% on room air. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed normal sinus rhythm, Q wave in Lead III, and aVF. Serum creatinine was 0.9 mg/dl, which gradually increased to 2.8 mg/dl.

He was initiated on a regimen of cyclophosphamide at 15 mg/kg, 1 gm for 3 days and 8 mg of dexamethasone twice a day, and also started on hemodialysis. Apart from renal failure, other organ functions, including oxygenation, remained stable during the intensive care unit (ICU) stay (Table 1). The patient required daily dialysis for 5 days. His renal function gradually improved. Dexamethasone was changed to oral prednisolone and gradually tapered over 10 days. He had a total five sessions of hemodialysis.

Case 2—A 32-year-old female, presented with accidental exposure to paraquat (24%) while spraying. Following this, she developed a burning sensation in the throat and excessive salivation. Patient developed mild fever and giddiness and was taken to a local hospital and later referred to our hospital after 3 days. Upon examination in the emergency room, her vitals were: pulse rate of 97 beats per minute, blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute, SpO2 of 97% on room air, and temperature of 37.2 °C. ECG showed normal sinus rhythm. Systemic examination was normal. Owing to worsening renal function (creatinine 5.4 mg/dl), we initiated RRT. Oral cavity examination showed a coated tongue with ulceration. Following one session of RRT, biochemical parameters were monitored and showed an improving trend.

The lung scan was unremarkable. Endoscopy showed corrosive injury, Zargar 2B. We started injection 900 mg of cyclophosphamide (15 mg/kg) for 3 days, 6 mg of dexamethasone twice a day, Vitamin E, and heparin. Dexamethasone was changed to oral prednisolone and tapered over 10 days.

Other supportive treatments like stress ulcer prophylaxis, initiation of oral feeding, early mobilization and DVT prophylaxis were initiated. As there was no respiratory involvement, early ambulation and feeding was possible.

Both patients showed improvement over a week and were discharged. We had telephonic follow up every 6 months and observed improved renal function, and patients no longer required renal replacement therapy post hospital discharge. At the end of 2.5 years, both the patients are doing well.

Discussion and conclusion

This case report highlights that initiation of immunosuppression therapy resulted in a prompt recovery in two patients. Initial treatment with gastric lavage is not widely practiced owing to the increased risk of perforation [16]. The types of therapies tried for paraquat poisoning are in the form of use of hemoperfusion, steroids, cyclophosphamide, and plasma exchange.

Charcoal or hemoperfusion helps to reduce the concentration of paraquat. A study by Guo, et al. showed that early (within 12 hours) and repeated hemoperfusion was associated with decreased 90 day mortality [12]. Early hemoperfusion within 5 hours was useful in patients with severe paraquat poisoning [17]. In this retrospective study, patients who received early hemoperfusion and repeated immunosuppressive therapy with cyclophosphamide and methylprednisolone had improved survival. We did not consider hemoperfusion as both the patients presented after 48 hours.

The animal study from Korea showed that cyclophosphamide at a dose above 15 mg/kg reduced lung injury severity [18]. Lin, et al. showed in a randomized controlled trial, reduced mortality with cyclophosphamide and methylprednisolone [19]. A subsequent study also demonstrated improved survival with repeated pulse methylprednisolone with cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone [20]. A RCT conducted by Gawarammana I, et al. evaluating the effect of high-dose cyclophosphamide versus placebo did not show any difference in the mortality; however, it suggested possible benefits with steroids [14]. Earlier studies were positive owing to premature stopping of trials on the basis of interim data, resulting in a Type I error.

An earlier Cochrane review demonstrated the benefit of cyclophosphamide and glucocorticoids in reducing mortality [13]. A meta-analysis by Gao, et al. showed immunosuppressive therapy was associated with lesser mortality and multiorgan failure [21]. However, a recent Cochrane review of four randomized controlled trials showed no difference in short-term mortality [15]. A recent retrospective study of 109 patients showed higher mortality, and the majority of patients received methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide [2]. In the current case report, we used dexamethasone instead of methylprednisolone. As there was no hypoxia at the time of presentation, we used the lowest possible dose of steroids.

The immunosuppressive therapy was not associated with infection or cytopenias in these patients. Similarly, the Cochrane review found no increase in infection rates. However, there is limited data on infection rates [13].

Several studies on survival showed plasma level of the paraquat and time from ingestion were important prognostic markers [22, 23]. In our patients, we could not do plasma levels of paraquat. The plasma levels could have helped in objectively predicting survival [22]. Other supportive therapies, such as ulcer prophylaxis and initiation of oral feeding also helped in early recovery. However presence ofrespiratory failure or extensive oral ulceration can delay timely initiation of oral or enteral feeding in non-intubated patients. Early placement of Ryle’s tube can ensure timely initiation of feeding [16]. Vitamins act as antioxidants and help in reducing oxidative stress [16].

The current case report shows a short-term and long-term mortality benefits with cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone. At the end of 2.5 year follow-up, none of the patients were on RRT. There is a paucity of data on long-term survival except for 1 case report showing long-term follow up of 6 years [24]. In our opinion immunosuppressive therapy helped in early recovery and prevented disease progression.

Future studies on establishing a set protocol or guidelines for managing paraquat poisoning will be helpful in ensuring uniform management across the globe. In addition, on the preventive aspects, safer alternatives need to be investigated for weed control [25, 26]. Developing policies on the safer use of paraquat and ensuring compliance with them will be helpful.

Conclusion—Early initiation of immunosuppressive therapy improved survival in these patients. Future studies should focus on protocolised management to ensure uniform care and to improve outcomes.
