MSC-derived exosomes improve endometrial fibrosis via the lncRNA IGF2R/ miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis
Authors: Xingyu Huo, Juntong Chen, Maojiao Qian, Qian Xue, Pengzhan Xu, Yueming Wang, Zhonglin Jiang, Qianqian Luo, Yanlian Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometrial injury (EI) is frequently associated with intrauterine adhesions, endometrial thinning, amenorrhea, and infertility. In recent years, stem cells and their exosomes have been shown to have significant tissue repair efficacy. However, the role and probable mechanism of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUMSC-Exo) in endometrial healing remains unclear.MethodsTo build an EI model, in vivo investigations were carried out utilizing C57BL/6 mechanical manipulation. Hematoxylin–eosin staining and Masson Trichrome Staining were employed to examine morphological changes in the mouse uterus. TGF-β1 treatment was employed in an in vitro experiment to create an injury model for human endometrial stromal cells (hESCs). The expression of fibrosis-related molecules in uterine tissue and hESCs was investigated using Western blot and immunofluorescence labeling.ResultsIn animal experiments, after intervention with hUMSC-Exo, the fibrotic damage and expression of receptive molecules in the uterine tissue of EI mice were significantly repaired. Bioinformatics analysis predicts the high expression of lncRNA IGF2R in hUMSC-Exo and its relationship with fibrosis-related molecule aquaporin 8 (AQP8). In cell experiments, knocking down lncRNA IGF-2R in hUMSC-Exo or directly knocking down AQP8 in hESCs resulted in a significant increase in fibrosis-related molecule expression of hESCs.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our findings imply that hUMSC-Exo can prevent fibrosis via the AQP8/miR-143-5p/lncRNA IGF2R axis, hence reducing mechanically caused EI. Our findings offer fresh approaches and plans for using hUMSC-Exo to treat EI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04611-z.Keywords: EI, LncRNA IGF2R, miR-143-5p, AQP8, hESCs, Fibrosis, hUMSC-Exo

Introduction

Following endometrial injury (EI), endometrial fibrosis frequently appears when the normal endometrial base layer is harmed and the typical proliferation capacity is absent [1]. One of the main causes of fibrosis is abnormal stromal cell function and metabolism [2–4]. Exosome therapy created from stem cells has emerged as a viable alternative source for the treatment of endometrial fibrosis and other fibrotic illnesses in recent years [1, 5, 6]. However, the exact regulatory mechanism underlying its anti-fibrosis actions is unknown.

Under normal physiological settings, the endometrium is extremely regenerative, and a proper pregnancy depends on its periodic alterations [7]. Clinical evidence indicates that the primary cause of endometrial cycle abnormality is mechanical endometrial damage brought on by hysteroscopic surgery [8–11]. Additional research has revealed that one of the main causes of endometrial fibrosis is the aberrant metabolism of human endometrial stromal cells (hESCs) during this phase, which is followed by the release of a significant amount of extracellular matrix (ECM) [4, 12]. This has also been found by Lv et al. [13]. When injury occurs, endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) will transform into myofibroblasts, resulting in excessive deposition and reorganization of ECM, producing a large number of dense fibrous tissue, and ultimately leading to endometrial fibrosis [14, 15]. In recent years, a number of clinical trials have verified the effectiveness of multiple sources of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in the treatment of endometrial fibrosis [16–18]. Therefore, the primary approaches to treating mechanically damaged endometrial fibrosis involve controlling hESCs metabolism and preventing ECM production [10, 19, 20].

Aquaporins (AQPs) are composed of six transmembrane alpha helices and two half channels [21]. Water can flow via the highly preserved aspart-proline-alanine (Asn-Pro-Ala, NPA) sequence found in each half-channel [22–24]. There are now 13 different types of AQPs (AQP0-AQP12) in mammals [21, 22]. Despite having distinct physiological roles due to varying expression locations, these 13 types of AQPs perform comparable tasks in clear water. According to recent research, tissue fibrosis and AQPs are tightly connected [25, 26]. According to Yin et al., SQW improves adenine-induced kidney fibrosis by upregulating the expression of AQP1, which decreases epithelial-mesenchymal transition [27]. The therapy of bleomycin-induced lung tissue fibrosis involves the MAPK/miR-23b-5p/AQP5 signaling axis, as Wang et al. showed [28]. According to recent research, AQPs are also crucial in the uterus [29–32]. Although they are all expressed in the uterus, aquaporins 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, and 9 have distinct localizations [33]. The function of AQPs in the development of endometrial fibrosis brought on by mechanical damage, however, has not received much attention.

A kind of extracellular vesicle with a diameter of 40–100 nm, mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (MSC-Exo) are released by MSCs. Most cytoplasmic components of MSCs, including proteins, lipids, metabolites, and nucleic acids, are carried via MSC-Exo, a crucial route involved in intercellular signal transduction and control [34, 35]. In several physiological and pathological contexts, MSC-Exo has demonstrated a positive therapeutic impact in recent years in reducing tissue fibrosis. Wang et al. showed that renal fibrosis brought on by ligation is lessened by exosome-mediated miR-29 [5]. Cheng et al. discovered that hUMSC-Exo contains miR-27b-3p, which may target YAP to ameliorate liver fibrosis brought on by 10% carbon tetrachloride [36]. Exosomes have been demonstrated in recent research to not only alleviate fibrosis in various conditions but also to be crucial in lowering the severity of endometrial fibrosis [37–39]. By degrading YAP ubiquitination, Qi et al. discovered that exosomes generated from blood stromal cells might reduce endometrial fibrosis [37]. Mansourabadi et al. demonstrated that extracellular vesicles secreted by adipose derived mesenchymal stem cells inhibit the expression of aquaporin 5 and EGFR genes in tumor cell lines, reducing tumor cell proliferation and migration [40]. Park et al. proved that the exosomes efficiently deliver the miRNA targeting AQP5 to breast cancer cells and inhibit the migration of breast cancer cells [41]. It is unclear, consequently, how hUMSC-Exo reduces mechanical injury fibrosis by controlling hESCs metabolism and regulating AQPs.

This work aimed to identify the function and molecular mechanism of hUMSC-Exo in controlling hESCs metabolism and reducing endometrial fibrosis caused by mechanical stress. It offers a fresh approach to the clinical management of illnesses associated with endometrial fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Animal models

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 7–8 weeks were purchased from Shandong Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding, Ltd (Shandong, China). Every animal experiment was authorized by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Binzhou Medical College Animal Testing Center and carried out in compliance with China’s national Standard Experimental Animal Guidelines. All the mice were free to drink and eat and lived in a standard environment with suitable temperature and humidity control. To reduce pain, the mice were sedated with pentobarbital sodium (200 mg/kg, i.p.) and sacrificed via cervical dislocation at the end of the trial. Verify mouse death if there is no heart activity, reflex activity, or spontaneous breathing.

Four groups (n = 20/group) of mice were randomly assigned: Sham group, EI group, PBS group, and hUMSC-Exo group. To make sure the mice were in the same estrus cycle, they received an injection of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG, #P9970, Solarbio, China) following a week of adaptive feeding. During estrus, mechanical uterine injury was done to simulate EI. The mice were given anesthesia, and then their middle and lower abdomens were sliced open to reveal the uterus. Carefully scrape the uterus on both sides until the blood is pooled in the eye after inserting a 7-gauge needle into the uterine junction [10]. To prevent harm to the ovaries, a vascular clamp is used to separate the uterus from the ovaries. After that, the wound was sutured and the uterus was washed with saline. Keep in mind that all of this is carried out in a sterile setting to prevent infection-related mouse deaths. Only a laparotomy without curettage was carried out by the Sham group. After modeling, hUMSC-Exo was given a week later. 150 µl of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 5 × 105 hUMSC-Exo was given intravenously in accordance with our earlier investigation [42]. 150 µl of PBS was injected into the tail vein of the mice in the PBS group. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Production and isolation of exosomes

The hUMSCs isolation method follows the previous method [43, 44]. To eliminate cellular contaminants, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 2 × 103 g for 30 min at 4 ℃ after the hUMSCs had achieved a confluence rate of 90–100%. Following a 60-minute centrifugation at 4°C at 1.2 × 104 g, the resultant supernatant was collected and centrifuged again for 70 min at 4°C at 1.1 × 105 g. To get rid of any tainted proteins, the resultant precipitate was re-suspended in PBS and centrifuged for 70 min at 4°C at 1.1 × 105 g. The collected sediment was re-suspended in PBS to measure the concentration, and finally stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Culture and induction of hESCs

The hESCs, which were cultivated in cells that included 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, #164210-50, Pricella, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, #P1400, Solarbio, China) in DMEM/F12 (#PM150312, Pricella, China) medium, were purchased from Wuhan Churuike Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (CRK Pharma, China). The cells were cultivated in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C, and their growth was observed daily [44]. To cause fibrosis, 10 ng/ml of human recombinant TGF-β1 (#PRP1223, Abbkine, China) was given to the medium [45, 46]. The intervention dose of hUMSC-Exo in cell experiments was based on previous literature reports [47]. Song et al. reported that a dose of 20 µg/ml hUMSC-Exo can effectively alleviate TGF-β1 induced hESCs fibrosis. In this study, we validated the significant therapeutic effect of 20 µg/ml hUMSC-Exo intervention and therefore chose it as the final therapeutic dose. There were three groups of hESCs: growing in regular media, growing in a TGF-β1 medium, and growing in a culture medium that contains both TGF-β1 and hUMSC-Exo.

Cell transfection

The Lipomaster 2000 Transfection Reagent (#TL201-01, Vazyme, China) was used to transfect hESCs after they had been infected in six-well plates. Following the product’s instructions, 100 µL of Opti-MEM medium was mixed with 5 µL siRNA and 7.5 µL Lipomaster 2000 Transfection Reagent. The mixture was then allowed to sit at room temperature for 5 min before being added in drops to the cell culture plate. Following 48 h of incubation at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2, follow-up tests were carried out. Western blot was used to measure the transfection effectiveness.

Lentivirus construction and transfection

GenePharma (Shanghai, China) developed the negative control and lncRNA knockdown lentivirus. The hUMSCs with an infection multiple of 50 were treated with polybrene and 25 µl of viral infection reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. Following a 24-hour transfection, the cells were cultured for 72 h to gather cells and assess the knockdown efficacy before the fresh media was replaced. Following the selection process, 1 µg/mL Puromycin Dihydrochloride (#ST551, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to screen for Puromycin Dihydrochloride-resistant cells for subsequent studies.

Hematoxylin-Eosin staining (H&E)

Mice in each group had their uteri preserved with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-micron slices. Dewaxing to water and H&E staining after baking. Under a light microscope, the uterus’s general dimensions, endometrial thickness, and gland count were examined.

Masson trichrome staining

Each group’s uterine tissue samples were collected, preserved with 4% paraformaldehyde, dried off, and then embedded. Following baking and dewaxing, the slices were subjected to Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (#G1340, Solarbio, China). Each group’s fibrosis was examined under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry staining (IHC)

Each group’s uterine tissues were removed and cut using paraffin embedding. 3% H2O2 treatment following sodium citrate (EDTA) antigen restoration. Goat serum was then applied for 30 min to seal it. After that, anti-estrogen receptor (1:200, bs-0725R, Bioss, China), anti-progesterone receptor (1:200, #bs-23376R, Bioss, China), and anti-HOXA10 (1:200, #bs-2502R, Bioss, China) were added, and the mixture was incubated for the entire night at 4 °C. The following day, the second antibody of the same species was added, incubated for an hour, and stained with hematoxylin differentiation after diaminobenzidine (DAB) development. Lastly, a microscope was used to view the tissue staining.

Immunofluorescent staining (IF)

Immunofluorescence labeling was used to identify the protein expressions of Collagen Ⅰ, Collagen Ⅲ, Fibronectin, α-SMA, and CTGF. At room temperature, 1% Triton X-100 was used to immobilize and infiltrate frozen slices of uterine tissue and endometrial stromal cells. Overnight at 4 °C: anti-Collagen I (1:100, #14695-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Collagen III (1:100, #22734-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-Fibronectin (1:100, #15613-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-α-SMA (1:100, #ab5694, Abcam, UK), and anti-CTGF (1:100, #25474-1-AP, Proteintech, China). The secondary antibodies containing goat anti- Rabbit IgG, Alexa Fluor 488 (#A23220, Abbkine, China), or goat anti-Rabbit IgG, Alexa Fluor 594 (#A23420, Abbkine, China) were incubated for one hour following rewarming the following day. Keep the DAPI (#C0065, Solarbio, China) at room temperature and out of direct sunlight for 10 min. Lastly, PBS was used to clean the seal, and an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany) was used to view the staining.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Trizol reagent (#R711-01, Vazyme, China) was used to extract the total RNA from each group’s uterine tissue and hESCs, and a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to measure the RNA’s quality and concentration. The HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (#R323-00, Vazyme, China) or miRNA 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (#AG11716, Accurate Biology, China) was then used to reverse-transcribe the RNA into cDNA. Next, either ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (#Q711-02, Vazyme, China) or SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit II (#AG11702, Accurate Biology, China) were utilized. The Quant Studio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to conduct real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR. Lastly, critical threshold (CT) comparison was used to assess transcriptional levels, and GAPDH and U6 expression were used to standardize the results. The source of the primer sequence was Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. synthesis and supply. The specific sequence of primers is shown in Table 1.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

The Bioinformatics online software LncTar and TargetScan were utilized to predict the binding sites of lncRNA IGF2R with miR-143-5p and miR-143-5p with AQP8. The miR-143-5p mimics/inhibitors and corresponding negative control (NC) were synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. The amplified products were inserted into the pmirGLO vector (Promega, USA) between the SacI and XbaI restriction sites. Human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK293T) with low endogenous miRNA expression (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) were selected. Using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA), 800 ng of wild-type or mutant reporter gene was combined with 20 µM miR-143-5p mimic/inhibitor and co-transfected into HEK293T cells. After 24 h of transfection, Firefly & Renilla luciferase activities were measured in the lysed HEK293T cells using a dual-luciferase reporter detection system (Promega, USA).

Western blot analysis

Each group’s hESCs and uterine tissue were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer. To determine a suitable loading amount, the protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (#PC0020, Solarbio, China). Using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), the target protein was separated and attached to a PVDF membrane. After being sealed with 5% skim milk for one to two hours, the strips were incubated for an entire night at 4 °C with anti-Collagen Ⅰ (1:800), anti-Collagen Ⅲ (1:800), anti-Fibronectin (1:1,000), anti-α-SMA (1:1,000), anti-CTGF (1:1,000) and anti-GAPDH (1:20,000, Proteintech, China). It was immunoblotted with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature following rewarming and TBST washing the following day. Lastly, the protein bands were observed using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China) using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (ECL) kit (#BL523B, Biosharp, China). ImageJ software was used to assess the band density.

Fertility examination

To detect fertility, mice were chosen at random from each group, the male-to-female ratio was 1:2. After mating, check for vaginal plugs every day. Male and female mice were separated once vaginal plugs were present, and two weeks later, the number of pregnancies were noted.

Statistical analysis

The independent sample T-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare the quantitative data, which were mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used to create statistical graphs of the experimental data after SPSS 26.0 software was used for analysis. A statistically significant was p value < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of hUMSC-Exo

As shown in Fig. 1A, hUMSC-Exo is shown by electron microscopy as a typical round particle with a diameter of 30–100 nm (Fig. 1A), as is nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, Fig. 1B). Western blot results showed that hUMSC-Exo was positive for Alix, Tsg101, and CD63 (positive markers for exosomes), but negative for Cyt-C (a negative marker for exosomes) (Fig. 1C). To observe whether hUMSC-Exo enters the uterus, we performed small animal imaging and exosome tracing. hUMSC-Exo were strongly expressed in the uterus and significantly expressed in mice when compared to the PBS group, as seen in Fig. 1D. In addition, IF results revealed that hUMSC-Exo entered ESCs (Fig. 1E).

hUMSC-Exo repairs uterine morphology and function in EI mice

To investigate the effect of hUMSC-Exo on EI, we conducted an animal experiment, the experimental design is displayed in Fig. 2A. H&E and Masson staining were used to assess the pathological alterations and fibrosis in the uterus of mice. As shown in Fig. 2B-D, endometrial thickness and gland count were considerably lower in the EI group than in the Sham group. In contrast, hUMSC-Exo treatment dramatically restored these alterations compared to the PBS treatment group. Furthermore, the EI group had a substantially larger fibrotic area than the Sham group, and the hUMSC-Exo treatment greatly reduced the fibrotic area when compared to the PBS group (Fig. 2E-F). After that, the effect of hUMSC-Exo administration on uterine function in EI mice was investigated. ER, PR, and HOXA10 expression levels in the EI group were significantly lower than those in the Sham group (Fig. 2G-L). After treatment, hUMSC-Exo significantly changed the expression of these three markers linked to uterine function as compared to the PBS treatment group. To track the mice’s fertility, the litter size of each group was recorded after the cage was unified. Figure 2M-N shows that the litter size of the EI group was significantly less than the Sham group, whereas the litter size of the hUMSC-Exo treatment group was much larger than the PBS group. These results imply that hUMSC-Exo transplantation significantly repairs the structure and function of the uterus in mechanically damaged mice.

hUMSC-Exo alleviates endometrial fibrosis in EI mice

Further analysis was conducted on the effect of hUMSC-Exo on endometrial fibrosis in EI mice. The EI group had considerably higher levels of fibrosis-related molecules (Collagen I, Collagen III, Fibronectin, α-SMA, and CTGF) than the Sham group (Fig. 3A-F). However, following hUMSC-Exo therapy, the expression of these fibrosis-related molecules significantly declined. Additionally, the IF results were consistent with this result (Fig. 3G). The outcomes demonstrated that hUMSC-Exo could successfully treat fibrosis in EI mice.

To explore the reasons for the significant downregulation of AQP8 expression caused by EI, we analyzed the miRNA RNA-seq results in uterine tissue before and after EI in public databases. As shown in Figs. 4C and 139 miRNAs capable of targeted regulation of AQP8 were predicted using miRWalk and TargetScan databases. Further analysis was conducted on the differential expression of miRNA in uterine tissue before and after EI in public databases. The results showed that there were 45 upregulated and 39 downregulated DEGs with Log2FC > 1 and p value < 0.05 (Fig. 4D). Figure 4E shows four miRNAs (miR-143-5p, miR-885-3p, miR-378c, miR-378d) that target AQP8 and are significantly upregulated after EI. Further analysis revealed that only miR-143-5p had high expression abundance before and after EI (Fig. 4F), suggesting that its high expression may be the key to inhibiting AQP8 expression.

We further analyzed the potential regulatory components of AQP8 in hUMSC-Exo by RNA-seq. Figure 4G displays the top 10 lncRNAs with the highest expression abundance out of the 17,093 that we screened out. Then, the interaction sites between AQP8 and miR-143-5p, as well as miR-143-5p and lncRNA IGF2R, were predicted through the LncTar website and validated through dual luciferase reporter gene experiments (Fig. 4H-K). Furthermore, we observed significantly upregulated miR-143-5p and significantly downregulated AQP8 expression levels in the uterine tissue of EI mice compared to the Sham group (Fig. 4L-O). After hUMSC-Exo intervention, the expression of miR-143-5p and AQP8 was significantly reversed (Fig. 4L-O). The above results suggest that hUMSC-Exo derived lncRNA IGF2R targeting the miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis may be the key to alleviating EI.

hUMSC-Exo in vitro alleviates TGF-β1-induced hESCs fibrotic pathway activation

To confirm the molecular mechanism by which hUMSC-Exo mitigates endometrial fibrosis caused by EI, we acquired hESCs (Fig. 5A). A fibrotic injury model of hESCs generated by TGF-β1 was created in vitro (the flow chart is shown in Fig. 5B). TGF-β1 intervention markedly increased the expression of fibrosis-related molecules of hESCs (Collagen Ⅰ, Collagen Ⅲ, Fibronectin, α-SMA, and CTGF), as seen in Fig. 5C-H. Conversely, the expression of fibrotic molecules was markedly reversed by hUMSC-Exo intervention. The foregoing findings were also supported by IF detection of molecules linked to fibrosis (Fig. 5I). These findings imply that TGF-β1-induced hESCs fibrotic pathway activation can be considerably reduced by hUMSC-Exo in vitro intervention.

AQP8 is key in controlling the hESCs fibrosis pathway

The function of AQP8 in controlling the fibrotic pathway of hESCs. To address AQP8 expression in hESCs, we utilized si-AQP8 (Fig. 6A). All three of the si-AQP8 that we created greatly reduced the expression of AQP8 in hESCs, as seen in Fig. 6B-C. The si-AQP8 that had the most inhibitory effect was chosen for further research. Following si-AQP8 expression, hESCs received TGF-β1 treatment in vitro. Figures 6D-E indicate a significant increase in the expression of fibrosis-related markers (Collagen I, Collagen III, Fibronectin, α-SMA, and CTGF) in hESCs from the si-AQP8 group against the Control group. The foregoing findings were further supported by IF detection of molecules linked to fibrosis (Fig. 6F). These findings imply that AQP8 is a crucial target for the activation of the fibrotic pathway in hESCs.

IGF2R delivery is the key for hUMSC-Exo to alleviate hESCs fibrosis via the miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis

To test the mechanism of action of hUMSC-Exo-derived IGF2R in reducing hESCs fibrosis, we treated hUMSCs with sh-IGF2R and collected hUMSC-Exo (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B, the qRT-PCR test revealed a significant decrease in IGF2R expression in hUMSC-Exo after knockdown, and the one with the greatest inhibitory effect was chosen for further investigations. TGF-β1-damaged hESCs were treated in vitro with both normal and IGF2R knockdown hUMSC-Exo. The results showed that as compared to the Control group, the expression of miR-143-5p in hESCs in the sh-IGF2R group was greatly elevated after treatment, while the expression of AQP8 was significantly decreased (Fig. 7C). The sh-IGF2R group showed considerably greater expression of fibrosis-related markers in hESCs (Collagen I, Collagen Ⅲ, Fibronectin, α-SMA, and CTGF) compared to the Control group (Fig. 7D-F). These findings show that hUMSC-Exo reduces fibrosis-induced IGF2R transmission in hESCs via the miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis.

Discussion

hUMSC-Exo has attracted a lot of interest in the treatment of EI due to its capacity to reduce inflammation, high in vitro expansion efficiency, non-toxic side effects, good tolerance, ease of preservation, and mobility [48, 49]. Recently, a large number of studies have reported the role of MSC-Exo in regulating endometrial tissue metabolism and promoting regenerative repair of damaged uterine tissue [37, 50]. Our current research suggests that hUMSC-Exo reduces uterine damage in EI mice by inhibiting fibrosis. In addition, the therapeutic effect of hUMSC-Exo on EI is mainly achieved by delivering lncRNA IGF2R targeting the miR-143-5p/AQP8 pathway. Our findings are expected to have some theoretical implications for the management of EI.

One of the main causes of gynecological and obstetric issues like infertility and aberrant hormone release is EI, which can be brought on by a variety of causes [51]. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that a single of the primary signs of EI is endometrial fibrosis brought on by mechanical injury [52, 53]. According to Sun et al., one possible biological basis of endometrial fibrosis is the aberrant expression of epithelial cells driven by the TGF-β1/Smad pathway [50]. According to this study, mechanical injury results in decreased glandular abundance, increased fibrosis, and endometrial thinning. At the same time, reproductive ability is diminished, and hormone expression is decreased.

Endometrial receptivity describes the endometrium’s capacity to permit embryo adhesion, invasion, and the induction of a sequence of alterations in the endometrial stroma, all of which eventually facilitate embryo implantation. Endometrial receptivity, implantation control, and endometrial decidualization all depend on the HOXA10 gene, which is mostly expressed in the endometrium [54]. This is what the study’s findings show. HOXA10 expression can be elevated via hUMSC-Exo transplantation. Reports state that ER and PR are crucial for endometrial healing and pregnancy [55]. Following hUMSC-Exo transplantation, we also examined the expression of ER and PR and discovered significant rise. In conclusion, these findings show that hUMSC-Exo transplantation can promote the function of uterus and improve EI brought on by mechanical injury.

Numerous studies and literature reports have been conducted to explore the factors that influence endometrial fibrosis. Excess ECM deposition has been found in studies to cause the onset and progression of endometrial damage and fibrosis. Under physiological settings, hESCs function as a cellular scaffold, generating and releasing ECM to preserve the correct structure of glandular epithelium and glands. When the endometrium’s basal layer is injured, it produces several pro-fibrotic substances, which causes an increase in stromal cells and their transformation into myofibroblasts, resulting in the production of a huge volume of extracellular matrix [4, 13]. In recent years, researchers discovered that AQPs serve a key role in regulating tissue cell fibrosis. Lv et al. revealed that AQP1 can regulate P53 in AKI, reducing inflammation and fibrosis [56]. Zhu et al. discovered that aquaporins are abundantly expressed in the female reproductive system [57]. In this investigation, we discovered that AQP8 was significantly expressed in hESCs, whereas EI was associated with a considerable downregulation of AQP8 expression. By si-AQP8, in vitro research further demonstrated the role of AQP8 in controlling the expression of genes linked to fibrosis in hESCs. These findings imply that AQP8 plays a role in the functional anomalies and uterine fibrosis damage brought on by hESCs as a result of EI.

hUMSC-Exo has been demonstrated in numerous studies to have important therapeutic effects, including anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, immune-regulating, and regenerative repair properties. The use of hUMSC-Exo in the treatment of disorders of the female reproductive system, including premature ovarian insufficiency and EI, has been extensively documented in the literature in recent years [39, 50, 58–60]. It can dramatically restore impaired reproductive ability and fix structural and functional problems in tissues and organs like the ovaries and uterus. In a rat intrauterine adhesion model, S et al. found that ADSCs-Exo restored functional endometrium [61]. Qi et al. also showed that UBR4 is a crucial molecule for MenSC-Exo repair of IUA and reduces endometrial fibrosis by encouraging YAP ubiquitination degradation [37]. Shao et al. revealed that ADSC-Exos alleviate endometrial fibrosis by regulating miR-150-5p mediated lncRNA MIAT [62]. Chen et al. demonstrated that differential expression of miR-195-5p in extracellular vesicles can reduce ECM deposition and myofibroblast differentiation, and improve endometrial fibrosis by regulating the YAP-Smad7 pathway in the Hippo signaling cascade [63]. In a similar vein, we discovered in this investigation that hUMSC-Exo may effectively restore mice reproductive capacity and repair uterine anatomical and functional defects brought on by EI. Further research has found that hUMSC-Exo can prevent fibrosis by delivering the lncRNA IGF2R to the miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis. In addition, in vitro studies that sh-IGF2R in hUMSC-Exo also validated the previously proposed regulatory mechanism.

Although our experimental results indicate that hUMSC-Exo can alleviate mechanical injury-induced endometrial fibrosis in mice, translating hUMSC-Exo to clinical application for endometrial injury still requires clarification of specific issues such as the detailed molecular mechanisms, optimal dosage, and potential side effects. These aspects will be further investigated in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that hUMSC-Exo can repair EI that is both structural and functional. hUMSC-Exo may ameliorate endometrial fibrosis via activating the lncRNA IGF2R/miR-143-5p/AQP8 axis. The results of our study have revealed important targets, offering a novel therapeutic approach for the clinical management of mechanical injury-induced uterine dysfunction and fertility decline in women of reproductive age.
