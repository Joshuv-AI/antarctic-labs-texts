Socioeconomic disparities in physiological dysregulation: a longitudinal mediation analysis of cardiometabolic multimorbidity among middle-aged and elderly Chinese
Authors: Chunyu Yan, Yingyu Jing, Qi Ruan, Ping Liu, Guang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) poses a significant health burden among middle-aged and older adults in China, yet early risk prediction remains challenging. The Composite Score of Physiological Variability (CSPV), integrating blood pressure, pulse rate, and body weight fluctuations, may offer a novel tool for assessing CMM risk, but the role of socioeconomic status (SES) in this association is unclear.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, N = 4,905) and the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, N = 4,086), we calculated CSPV based on tertile-coefficients of variation for physiological measures. Cox models and mediation analyses examined longitudinal associations between CSPV and CMM, adjusting for covariates (age, sex, lifestyle, etc.), and quantified SES mediation.ResultsThe risk of CMM was significantly higher for each 1-point increase in CSPV (CHARLS: HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.18–1.42; CLHLS: HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.06–1.31; combined analyses: HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.12–1.37). SES played a partially mediating role in the association between CSPV and CMM partially mediated, with mediation percentages of 21% (CHARLS) and 16% (CLHLS), respectively. Stratified analyses showed that the CSPV-CMM association was stronger in the low-education, low-income, and rural-dwelling populations.ConclusionElevated CSPV is an independent predictor of increased risk of CMM in middle-aged and elderly Chinese, and SES plays an important mediating role in this association. Monitoring the variability of physiological indicators and combining them with socioeconomic interventions may contribute to the early prevention and control of CMM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24698-6.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity (CMM) is a coexisting state of two or more cardiometabolic diseases (e.g., coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus) [1, 2], with significantly higher clinical burden and health risks than those of a single disease [3]. In middle-aged and elderly Chinese, the high incidence of CMM not only leads to the deterioration of individual health and the decline of quality of life, but also significantly increases medical expenditure and social economic burden [4, 5]. Although traditional risk factors, such as elevated blood pressure, obesity, and insulin resistance, have been widely studied [6], how to identify high-risk populations at an early stage and to elucidate the sunderlying mechanisms is still an urgent scientific issue to be addressed.

The Composite Score of Physiological Variability (CSPV) provides a systematic assessment tool for quantifying homeostatic imbalance by integrating multi-system physiological indicators (such as multidimensional dynamic measures of blood pressure, pulse rate, and body weight) [7]. Long-term variability in physiological indicators reflects the stress experienced by the body's regulatory systems in maintaining internal homeostasis [8]. Increased variability often signals impaired physiological regulatory function, manifesting as reduced feedback mechanism efficacy and overcorrection, ultimately leading to decreased system stability [9, 10]. Current research has confirmed that long-term variability in individual physiological indicators is independently associated with cardiovascular disease risk [11]. Regarding blood pressure, a meta-analysis of 12 studies demonstrated that for every 5 mmHg increase in systolic blood pressure standard deviation (SD), the risks of stroke, coronary heart disease, and overall cardiovascular events significantly increased by 17%, 27%, and 12%, respectively [12]. Similarly, fluctuations in body mass index (BMI) were found to increase the risk of cardiovascular events by 27% [13]. Although existing studies have confirmed the independent association between abnormalities in individual physiological indicators and cardiometabolic disease risk [14–16], the cumulative effects of multi-system physiological dysregulation on long-term health trajectories in middle-aged and elderly populations remain unclear. Particularly against the backdrop of China's rapidly aging population, CSPV may be more suitable for assessing the overall risk of CMM.

Although socioeconomic status (SES) has been shown to modify the association between individual cardiometabolic diseases and health outcomes [17, 18], systematic evidence remains lacking regarding its mediating role in the relationship between CSPV and the progression of CMM. Existing studies have primarily focused on high-income countries or single-disease scenarios [19, 20]. In China’s context of rapid population aging and urban–rural disparities in healthcare resources, SES-related factors may potentially alter the predictive value of CSPV for CMM risk. Particularly noteworthy is that China's urban–rural dual structure and its associated social security gradient may lead to divergent effects: rural low-income populations could experience exacerbated cumulative physiological variability due to delayed medical care, while urban highly-educated groups might mitigate multisystem dysregulation risks through health management behaviors [21, 22]. However, such differential effects remain unquantified in the CSPV-CMM relationship.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the longitudinal association between CSPV and CMM in middle-aged and older adults by utilizing two large prospective Chinese cohort studies—the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS). The study will further elucidate the mediating role of SES, thereby providing novel strategies for early CMM intervention.

Method

Study population and design

This study utilized data from two nationally representative large-scale cohorts: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [23] and the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) [24]. Detailed study designs, sampling strategies, and quality control measures for both surveys have been comprehensively described in the supplementary materials.

Specifically, in the CHARLS cohort study, participants were enrolled in 2011 (baseline) and completed two follow-up waves in 2013 and 2015. Only individuals with complete data at all three time points were included in the analysis. In the CLHLS cohort study, we selected data from three survey waves: 2008–2009 (baseline), 2011–2012 (first follow-up), and 2014 (second follow-up) (Fig. 1). After rigorous screening based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analytical samples were determined as follows: The CHARLS cohort initially included 17,705 baseline participants, while the CLHLS cohort comprised 16,954 baseline participants. After excluding individuals lost to follow-up, those who did not meet age criteria, participants with missing disease information, and those lacking physiological measurement data at any follow-up visit, the study ultimately included 4,905 eligible participants from CHARLS and 4,086 from CLHLS in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).Fig.1Study design

Study design

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity assessment

CMM was defined based on self-reported or physician-diagnosed conditions during baseline and follow-up surveys, including: heart disease (such as myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart conditions), diabetes (including impaired glucose tolerance and elevated fasting blood glucose), stroke, and hypertension. The presence of two or more of these conditions was defined as CMM.

CSPV measurement

We employed the coefficient of variation (CV) to quantify BMI variability (BWV), pulse rate variability (PRV), and blood pressure variability (BPV), calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation (SD) to the mean for BMI, pulse rate (PR), and systolic blood pressure (SBP), respectively. Given that systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV) demonstrates a stronger association with adverse health outcomes compared to diastolic blood pressure variability (DBPV) [11, 25], we primarily used SBPV as the measure for BPV. To comprehensively assess the overall fluctuation of individual physiological indicators, we constructed a composite variability score based on established methodologies [26]. Specifically, BWV, PRV, and BPV were stratified into tertiles, with individuals in the highest tertile assigned 1 point and those in the middle or lowest tertiles assigned 0 points. The scores for the three indicators were then summed to derive the composite variability score (range: 0–3). A higher score indicates greater instability in an individual's physiological parameters.

SES assessment

In defining socioeconomic status (SES), we considered three key dimensions: education level, residential area, and total household wealth. Education level was standardized into three categories: Primary (assigned 0 points, including no formal education and elementary school education), Middle (1 point, junior high school or vocational secondary education), and High (2 points, high school, college, and above). Residential area was classified as rural (0 points) or urban (1 point). Total household wealth was a composite measure encompassing all household net assets, including daily expenses but excluding any outstanding debts or loans. In each study, total household wealth was divided into tertiles, with scores ranging from 0 to 2 representing the lowest to highest wealth levels. The SES score was calculated by summing the points from education level, residential area, and total household wealth. Based on this cumulative score, SES was categorized into four groups: low (score = 0), low-middle (score = 1–2), upper-middle (score = 3–4), and high (score = 5) [27, 28].

Covariates

All participants underwent structured questionnaire interviews conducted by trained professionals. To account for potential confounding factors, we collected data including age, sex, marital status, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, sleep time, physiological measures at the last follow-up (such as SBP, pulse rate, and BMI), as well as SES-related factors.

Statistical analysis

For all participants across both cohorts, continuous variables of baseline characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test alongside Q-Q plots (Supplementary Figure S2). Right-skewed household income data were log-transformed (ln(income + 1)) before being included in parametric analyses. We initially employed Cox proportional hazards models to examine the association between variability in individual physiological measures (BPV/PRV/BWV) and the risk of CMM events, estimating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the composite score in relation to CMM. To assess the dose–response relationship between individual physiological measures and CMM, we conducted restricted cubic spline regression analyses using the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles to explore potential non-linear associations. Subsequently, we examined the risk association between CSPV scores and CMM by treating CSPV both as a categorical and continuous variable. In the categorical analysis, using the 0-point group as the reference category, we compared the CMM risk among participants with different CSPV scores. For the continuous analysis, we calculated the corresponding CMM risk for each 1-point increase in the CSPV score. Finally, we performed a meta-analysis by pooling data from both cohorts using fixed-effects models. We subsequently employed a mediation analysis model for further investigation [29]. First, Linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between CSPV and SES. Second, Linear regression was used to test the direct association between CSPV and CMM. After incorporating SES as a mediating variable in the model, we repeated the Linear regression analysis between CSPV and CMM to assess changes in their relationship and evaluate the mediation effect. Additionally, we applied a non-parametric bootstrap approach with 1000 resamples to determine the significance of total effects, indirect effects, and direct effects [30]. To further investigate the moderating effect of SES on the association between CSPV and CMM, we conducted stratified analyses based on age and SES (including education level, household income, and residential area). All models were adjusted for potential confounders/covariates, including age, sex, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep time, systolic blood pressure, BMI, and pulse rate. Multiple imputation handles missing data under the Missing at Random (MAR) assumption by generating missing values based on the conditional distribution of observed data. This study employed the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) method, incorporating all analytical variables to ensure model consistency. To further assess the impact of missing values, we conducted sensitivity analyses on the multiply imputed complete datasets and re-examined the mediation effects. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1), with a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Population characteristics

Association between single physiological measures and CMM

Supplementary Table S3 presents the associations between individual physiological measures and their quartiles with CMM risk. In the basic Model 1, CHARLS showed a gradual increase in CMM risk with higher quartiles of BPV and BMV (P for trend < 0.05), with BPV demonstrating the strongest association with CMM risk (HR = 1.03). In contrast, the CLHLS exhibited significant associations between increased CMM risk and both BPV and PRV (P for trend < 0.05). In Model 2, after further adjusting for age, sex, education level, residence, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, sleep time, waist circumference, SBP, pulse rate, and BMI, all physiological measures in the CHARLS showed significant associations with CMM risk. In contrast, only BPV and PRV measures remained significantly associated with CMM risk in the CLHLS. The restricted cubic spline curve analysis indicated no significant nonlinear relationships between any single physiological measure and CMM risk (Pnonlinear > 0.05, Supplementary Figure S3).

Association between CSPV score and CMM

As shown in Table 2, in the CHARLS, each 1-point increase in CSPV was associated with a 31% higher risk of CMM (HR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.18, 1.42). In the CLHLS, each 1-point increase in CSPV was associated with an 18% increased risk of CMM (HR: 1.18; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.31). Mediating role of SES in the relationship between CSPV and CMM

As shown in Supplementary Table S4, after adjusting for covariates, CSPV was significantly negatively associated with SES in both cohorts (βCHARLS = − 0.221, PCHARLS = 0.002; βCLHLS = − 0.103, PCLHLS < 0.001). In Table 3 and Table 4, Model 1 demonstrated that CSPV was significantly positively associated with CMM (βCHARLS = 0.302, βCLHLS = 0.034, P < 0.001). When SES was included as a mediating variable in Model 2, CSPV remained significantly positively correlated with CMM (βCHARLS = 0.311, βCLHLS = 0.146, P < 0.001). Thus, SES partially mediated the association between CSPV and CMM. Bootstrap analysis further revealed that in the CHARLS and CLHLS cohorts, the total effect of CSPV on CMM was 0.102 (95% CI: 0.064, 0.163) and 0.076 (95% CI: 0.019, 0.165). The indirect mediating effect through SES was 0.041 (95% CI: 0.023, 0.085) and 0.012 (95% CI: 0.006, 0.021). Stratified analysis

We re-analyzed the mediating role of SES in the relationship between CSPV and CMM using the multiply imputed dataset. Sensitivity analysis revealed minimal differences in effect sizes between imputed and complete data, suggesting that missing data had a limited impact on the primary conclusions. The results showed no substantial differences from the primary findings, further confirming the robustness of our conclusions (Supplementary Table S5 and Figure S4). To further investigate the influence of key SES factors on the relationship between CSPV and CMM risk, we conducted age-stratified analyses to systematically examine the potential moderating effects of different education levels, household income, and residential location. As shown in Supplementary Table S6, the strength of the association between CMM and CSPV scores was significantly exacerbated by all three SES indicators, low educational attainment, low income, and residence in a rural area, among older adults aged 60 years and older in both the CHARLS and CLHLS.

Discussion

This study investigated the longitudinal association between CSPV scores (constructed by integrating blood pressure, pulse rate, and body weight variability) and CMM risk among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The results demonstrated that elevated CSPV scores were significantly associated with increased CMM risk, with SES playing a partial mediating role in this relationship. Notably, findings from both cohort studies showed high consistency—despite differences in participants' age structure and baseline characteristics, the direction and effect size of the association between CSPV and CMM remained similar, enhancing the reliability of the conclusions. This suggests that integrating multisystem physiological variability into a composite score can more comprehensively reflect systemic homeostatic dysregulation, thereby facilitating the identification of high-risk populations and providing new insights for early intervention strategies.

Previous studies have conclusively demonstrated the close association between BPV [31], PRV [32], BMV [33] and the development of chronic diseases as well as health outcomes in elderly populations. However, systematic research on the mechanisms linking these physiological variability indicators to multimorbidity risk remains lacking. This study employed a longitudinal design to reveal for the first time the epidemiological association between CSPV and CMM in a Chinese population, overcoming the limitation of traditional research that focused solely on single disease outcomes. By integrating two large-scale prospective cohort studies from China, we demonstrated significant correlations between long-term physiological variability and both cardiovascular and metabolic disease risks. The increase in CSPV scores may reflect impairment in the body's regulatory functions. Mechanistically, sustained fluctuations in physiological parameters may induce cumulative damage to vascular endothelial function, metabolic homeostasis, and organ function [34, 35]. For instance, blood pressure variability may increase mechanical stress on vascular walls, accelerating arteriosclerosis progression [36], while simultaneously disrupting glucose metabolism and potentially influencing diabetes development [37]. Body weight variability likely reflects impaired metabolic regulatory capacity and shows close associations with insulin resistance and lipid metabolism disorders [38, 39]. Furthermore, long-term BMV fluctuations may promote adipose tissue inflammation [40] and alter adipokine secretion patterns [41]. Additionally, disturbances in homeostasis may further trigger systemic inflammation [42]. These pathophysiological changes collectively contribute to the development of CMM. Although this study did not establish a clear clinical intervention threshold, the data analysis revealed that for every 1-point increase in CSPV, the risk of CMM significantly increased (HRCHARLS = 1.31; HRCLHLS = 1.18). Based on this, we recommend that in clinical practice, particular attention should be paid to patients with a total CSPV ≥ 2, as well as those with abnormally high coefficients of variation in any single physiological parameter. These individuals may benefit from more frequent follow-ups (every 3–6 months) and comprehensive metabolic assessments. Future research should determine the optimal risk stratification cutoffs through external validation cohorts, further explore the impact of different CSPV groups on specific clusters of multimorbidity, and elucidate their potential biological mechanisms in depth, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the development of targeted interventions.

The mediating role of SES represents another significant finding of this study. Analyses revealed that lower SES was associated with higher CSPV scores, thereby indirectly influencing CMM risk through this pathway. This finding carries important public health implications, particularly within China's context of pronounced urban–rural disparities. A large prospective study involving 13,699 participants from the HRS demonstrated that educational attainment showed a more significant association with disease accumulation than wealth [43]. Another case–control study found that higher formal education levels reduced the risk of esophageal cancer [44]. Populations with low SES often face multiple challenges including limited healthcare access, inadequate health literacy, and greater life stressors [45, 46], which may contribute to suboptimal chronic disease management and consequently exacerbate physiological variability. Furthermore, the socioeconomic impacts of educational factors frequently originate from childhood experiences, subsequently influencing behavioral and psychological determinants such as lifestyle choices and health knowledge acquisition—factors closely tied to later-life health outcomes and disease management [47]. Improvements in personal wealth, including living conditions and healthcare resource accessibility, may further modulate individual health management practices [48]. Stratified analyses further revealed that the association between CSPV and CMM was more pronounced in rural populations and among groups with lower education levels and incomes, suggesting that socioeconomic disadvantages may amplify the negative health impacts of physiological instability. These findings align with the theory of social determinants of health [49, 50], emphasizing the importance of considering socioeconomic factors in cardiovascular disease prevention and control. Furthermore, this study extends previous research on the relationship between SES and health outcomes by quantifying through mediation analysis the degree to which SES influences the association between physiological instability and CMM, providing new insights into health disparities. This study observed that the mediating proportion attributable to SES was approximately 16–21%, indicating that, besides socioeconomic factors, other important mechanisms are also involved in regulating the relationship between CSPV and CMM. Based on the existing evidence, we believe these mechanisms may include, but are not limited to, individual-level biological factors [51] (such as genetic predispositions and epigenetic regulation); environmental exposures [52, 53] (like air pollution and noise, which act as chronic environmental stressors); health behaviors not fully captured by measurements [54] (such as subtle differences in dietary patterns and exercise habits); and psychosocial factors [55] (including chronic stress and depressive symptoms). These findings suggest that targeted, differentiated intervention strategies for low SES populations require multi-dimensional and comprehensive approaches. While improving access to primary healthcare and alleviating financial burdens, these strategies should also incorporate multi-level measures such as environmental interventions, behavioral modifications, and psychological support, in order to effectively break the vicious cycle between physiological instability and social disadvantage. Future research could further elucidate the interactions of these complex mechanisms by integrating multi-omics data and digital monitoring technologies.

The current study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although two independent cohorts were analyzed, the relatively short follow-up period may limit the assessment of long-term associations. Specifically, while our study design is longitudinal, the observed associations reflect effects within a defined time frame (4–6 years in CHARLS and CLHLS). Longer follow-up durations would be necessary to validate whether CSPV predicts CMM risk over decades or across different life stages, particularly given the chronic, progressive nature of cardiometabolic diseases. Future studies with extended follow-ups could further elucidate the temporal dynamics of physiological variability and its cumulative impact on multimorbidity. Second, the current CSPV calculation was based on measurements from only three time points. While this approach captures some degree of variability, it may not fully represent true long-term fluctuation patterns, particularly for parameters like blood pressure that exhibit substantial short-term variability. The limited number of measurements could potentially underestimate intra-individual physiological variability and affect the accuracy of risk prediction. Emerging wearable technologies enabling continuous, high-frequency monitoring may provide more robust variability assessments in future studies. Third, despite adjusting for multiple confounding factors, the observational study design cannot completely rule out residual confounding, as unmeasured variables such as genetic factors and psychological stress may influence the results. Although multiple imputation is based on the MAR assumption, unmeasured severe health issues may lead to Missing Not at Random (MNAR). Future studies could mitigate such bias by improving follow-up protocols. Finally, as the study population consisted of Chinese middle-aged and older adults, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other populations.

Conclusion

This study confirms the independent association between CSPV and CMM risk in Chinese middle-aged and older adults, while revealing the mediating role of SES in this relationship. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying multimorbidity but also provide scientific evidence for developing targeted prevention strategies. Against the backdrop of rapid population aging, this integrated research perspective combining physiological and social factors holds significant implications for promoting healthy aging.
