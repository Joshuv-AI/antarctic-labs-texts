Psychometric properties and constructs of Patient Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) in primary care: a scoping review
Authors: Véronique Lowry, Marie-Ève Perron, Pierre-Henri Roux-Levy, Cloé Beaulieu, Vanessa T. Vaillancourt, Maxime Sasseville, Marie-Eve Poitras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPositive patient experience is associated with improved health outcomes and greater adherence to recommended care. Patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) are tools developed to assess these experiences and can be used for multiple purposes, including to inform quality improvement. Patient experience is a complex concept that lacks a standardized definition, with multiple constructs described in the literature. The variety of available PREMs makes it difficult for knowledge users to select those that are valid, reliable, and suitable for their specific practice settings, particularly in primary care. The objective of this scoping review is to identify and describe PREMs developed for the primary care context, the constructs measured and their psychometric properties.MethodsFollowing the JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we conducted a scoping review of studies presenting the development or the psychometric properties of PREMs for adults in primary care in an original or a synthesis design. We searched databases (MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus) for studies between January 2019 and December 2023. Two independent reviewers screened and selected studies. They extracted PREMs constructs, psychometric properties, number of items, time of completion and tool availability.ResultsSeven studies, including three synthesis designs, were included in the review. From these papers, 23 PREMs were included for data extraction. Tools focusing exclusively on a single component of patient experience, a specific population, or a narrowly defined context were excluded. The constructs most frequently represented across identified tools included accessibility, relational aspects, and quality of clinical care or services. However, no tool covered all relevant constructs, highlighting the variability in defining patient experience. Tools ranged from 4 to 74 items, with limited reporting on completion time. Only five tools involved patients in their development, potentially limiting their applicability in routine practice. Psychometric properties varied, with most tools demonstrating acceptable validity and reliability. However, inconsistencies in reporting and testing methods were noted.ConclusionThis review highlights the diversity of PREMs validated for primary care and the variability in their constructs and psychometric properties. Improving conceptual clarity and alignment of patient experience constructs across PREMs, while ensuring meaningful patient involvement in tool development, and rigorous psychometric testing are essential to enhance their utility. This study supports the integration of PREMs into primary care quality improvement initiatives, contributing to more patient-centered healthcare delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03436-w.

Background

Patients’ experience is a complex concept that is distinct from, but related to patient satisfaction with care, a more commonly used concept in healthcare evaluation [1]. Patient satisfaction has been historically prominent in healthcare evaluation, while patient experience represents a more recent conceptual and methodological development [2]. Patients’ satisfaction is more focused on whether patients’ expectations were met while patients’ experience encompasses both subjective and objective aspects they personally encounter while receiving healthcare, including their perceptions and observations of provider and staff behaviours within the system [1–4]. A substantial body of evidence indicates a significant correlation between positive patient experience and patient-related outcomes [5–7]. These outcomes include increased adherence to evidence-based recommended care and medication, health-promoting behaviour and the use of screening services and immunizations [8–11]. Positive patient experience is also correlated with more efficient resource use, such as decreased length of hospital stay and rates of readmission as well as a lower number of primary-care visits [8–11].

Patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) are tools used to assess patients’ experience of care [12]. They differ from general satisfaction surveys which are often use to assess healthcare professional performance as they incorporate questions about healthcare organization and delivery of care which can impact quality of care [13]. PREMs also differ from patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), which are used to assess patients’ health status or quality of life [13]. PREMs and PROMs are complementary tools for improving healthcare quality and improving patients’ health status [14]. PREMs are used for micro, meso and macro levels of assessment, targeting individual, organizational and systemic performance improvement [15]. PREMs are tools that support the current healthcare system transformation, which aims at optimizing the use of resources, empowering patients and emphasizing what matters most to them, such as value-based and patient-centered care [16, 17]. The assessment of patient experience with PREMs can help identify opportunities for enhancing clinical teams and healthcare organizations and thus improve healthcare [18]. Adopting PREMs in primary care can accelerate [19] a global shift toward the current healthcare system transformation by allowing providers and policymakers to inform decision-making, drive quality improvement, benchmarking and performance assessment and enhance overall care delivery [20]. PREMs data contribute to identifying health inequities, comprehensiveness of care, and underserved populations for whom care could be realigned and consistent with the current healthcare system transformation [21, 22]. At the point-of-care level, PREMs data facilitate goal setting, communication and therapeutic approaches [23–25]. By regularly completing PREMs, patients report their experience with health services [18]. This enhances the synchronization between patients’ and providers’ needs (e.g., goal-oriented care, communication) [26–31]. When used in aggregated form (i.e., presented as a whole or organized according to groupings of patients categorized according to specific characteristics), PREMs can help identify priorities for improvement and become a powerful decision-making tool [32–34]. Despite the growing opportunity to emphasize patient experiences to support primary care organizations and improve healthcare quality, the use of validated PREMs by healthcare professionals is still limited and not routinely implemented in most primary care clinics [18, 35–37].

Several barriers hinder the implementation of PREMs, including resource limitations, staff resistance, healthcare professionals’ concerns about overburdening patients, technological challenges and professionals’ lack of awareness of PREMs [18]. Barriers also include challenges regarding the selection and use of scientifically valid tools for their clinical context because of the variety of PREMs [15, 38, 39]. Even knowledge users (e.g., clinicians, decision-makers) familiar with PREMs often struggle to identify the most appropriate measures for use in practice to inform care or quality improvement [15, 40, 41]. Indeed, the design and content of PREMs may not always align with the needs of clinical practice [42]. Finally, another significant challenge lies in the lack of consensus and conceptual alignment regarding the key constructs commonly used to operationalize and measure patient experience of care [43, 44]. This situation arises from the rapidly expanding literature and the presence of multiple operational definitions encompassing diverse constructs [44, 45]. Commonly cited constructs for defining patient experience in primary care include first-contact accessibility, relational continuity, family-centered care, population orientation and intersectoral teamwork [46].

There is a need to integrate patient experience to support primary care improvement toward value-based healthcare and minimize the challenges faced by knowledge users in adequately selecting PREMs. Summarizing PREM constructs and psychometric properties is therefore essential to address key implementation barriers, including uncertainty about tool relevance, methodological rigor, and applicability to primary care practice. Providing this information in a structured manner can facilitate informed selection and uptake of PREMs in routine care and quality improvement initiatives. The objective of this scoping review is to identify and describe the PREMs developed for the primary care context, the constructs measured and their psychometric properties.

Methods

Study design

Our scoping review followed the JBI methodology, which includes 1) developing a search strategy; 2) screening and selecting sources of evidence; 3) extracting the data; and 4) analyzing and presenting the results [47]. We reported data using the Preferred Reporting Items Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) [48].

Research question and Population, Concept, Context (PCC)

Our primary research question was as follows: What are the PREMs (concept) validated for adults (population) seeking primary care (context)?

Search strategy

In August 2023, we first performed an exploratory search with three main concepts: tools (i.e., PREMs), patient experience, and primary care in two specific databases (PubMed and Google Scholar). This research allowed us to identify two syntheses of tools for assessing patient experience [45] and healthcare quality [49] that were used to create the current scoping review’s search strategy. The added value of the current scoping review lies in its focus on patient experience tools specifically used in primary care. This contrasts with the two previous reviews: one was related to quality of care [49], a concept that is related but different from patient experience, and the other was not specific to primary care [45].

In December 2023, we identified relevant studies by performing a literature search using terms related to psychometric properties (e.g., validity, reliability, sensibility), questionnaire (e.g., survey, evaluation, measure, patient-reported experience measures, PREM), patient experience (e.g., patient opinion; patient satisfaction; healthcare quality) and primary care (e.g., general practice, primary healthcare, family medicine). The search strategy included the term patient satisfaction to increase the likelihood of identifying relevant PREMs, as these measures are sometimes labeled, indexed, or conceptualized as satisfaction instruments despite assessing patient experiences. Synonyms were combined using the OR operator, and distinct concepts were combined using the AND operator. We developed the search strategy in collaboration with a librarian, and we tested the sensitivity of the search strategy using key relevant sources (Supplementary Material 1). We conducted our search across three different databases: MEDLINE, CINAHL, and Scopus, as these databases are susceptible to covering papers published in primary care research. The search strategy was adapted for the EBSCOHost platform, as a simultaneous search in MEDLINE and CINAHL was possible within this platform. The search strategy was also adapted for the Scopus database. We limited our strategy to the timeframe between January 2019 and December 2023 to update the literature search conducted by the two syntheses identified during our exploratory search [45, 49]. The tools identified by the two syntheses were considered to be included in the review.

Eligibility criteria

To be included, studies had to 1) describe the development or the evaluation of the psychometric properties of PREMs in an original design or PREMs presented in a review, 2) focus on PREMs that can be used in a primary care context, 3) have the full-text available, 4) have been published in English or French, and 5) have been published between 2019 and July 2023 (as explained in the search strategy section above). However, papers published before 2019 could be included from the screening of references of included studies. From the included sources with a synthesis design, we screened the list of primary studies to extract the sources aligned with our objective. We excluded tools that 1) did not cover a global experience of care (e.g., covered only one patient experience construct) and that were specific to 2) a population (e.g., mental health, musculoskeletal diseases) or 3) a context (e.g., telemedicine, out-of-hours care). This decision was made to prioritize PREMs with broader applicability across primary care settings and to support comparability and usability. The first exclusion criterion allowed us to exclude instruments that assessed patient satisfaction exclusively. Studies relying solely on patient satisfaction measures (e.g., patient expectation questionnaire, checklists, quality of care only) were not considered eligible, as inclusion required the use of a genuine PREM, namely a validated instrument designed to assess multiple domains of patient experience. We also excluded tools that were not found according to the reference provided in the article or the name of the tool.

Screening and selection of studies and PREMs

Two independent reviewers (MEPe and CB) screened the eligible studies. These two reviewers have expertise in healthcare, as MEPe is a registered nurse, and CB is a social worker. For standardization, the reviewers independently assessed the titles and abstracts of 10 initial sources using the Covidence (www.covidence.org) web-based collaborative software platform for literature review. Then, they met to verify that they included and excluded the same sources, achieving consistency in their approach. The reviewers subsequently screened the titles and abstracts of all remaining relevant sources in increments of 40, meeting after each increment to discuss any discrepancies and reach a consensus. After completing the initial screening, MEPe and CB independently screened the full texts of the selected sources to confirm their eligibility for inclusion and met to resolve discrepancies. Any conflicts were resolved by a third or senior investigator (PHRL or MEPo) if necessary. Formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not calculated, as discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, in accordance with methodological guidance for scoping reviews [48].

Data extraction

Two reviewers (MEPe and CB or MEPe and PHRL) developed an Excel data-charting grid to extract data from the included sources and for the PREMs included from sources that have a synthesis design. The grid was tested by the reviewers with one included study. Each reviewer independently extracted the data, and the results were compared to ensure concordance before both reviewers proceeded with the rest of the extraction process. For the papers included in the review, we extracted the characteristics (authors and year of publication, objective, study design, context and number of tools) of the papers and identified tools that were suitable for inclusion. For all PREMs included, we extracted the constructs included, number of items, time for completion if available and availability of the PREMs. Construct names were derived from the labels used in the included instruments and informed by constructs commonly described in the literature as reflecting patient experience [46]. For each PREM included, we consulted the original paper describing the development and validation process of the tool and extracted the population and language for which the tool was tested and the psychometric properties of the tools mentioned by the authors of the studies covering the development of the tools (e.g., validity and reliability). We did not systematically search for or extract data from subsequent validation studies conducted in other populations or contexts; this choice is addressed as a limitation of the review.

Results

Selection of studies and PREMs

Our search of the included databases allowed us to identify 449 records that were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation). After duplicates were removed using Covidence (n = 86), we screened the titles and abstracts of 363 sources and 14 of these sources were assessed for full-text screening. Four papers were eligible; one report [50] and one other manuscript [51] were added, as we screened the reference lists of the included papers. These documents were not identified from our database search since they were published before 2019 [50, 51]. The flowchart of the included studies is presented in Fig. 1. The characteristics of the six included studies and the tools included and excluded from each study are presented in Table 1. From the six included papers or reports, we identified 23 PREMs that were eligible for inclusion in our review. Reasons for the exclusion of tools are presented in Supplementary Material 2. Four of the included PREMs were identified when the references of the manuscripts were reviewed (Table 1).

Patient experience or other concepts measured

In most of the manuscripts covering the development of the included tools, the concept measured is not directly referred to as patient experience. In some tools, the concepts being measured are primary care assessment [59, 63, 64, 66], patients’ evaluation of general practice [53], practice assessment [55, 60], patient perception of quality [61], patients’ satisfaction [62, 69], patients’ feedback [57, 72], quality of patients’ visit [65] and quality, costs and equity of primary health care [74]. Patient experience is generally not defined across most of the different manuscripts covering the development of tools. The authors of the manuscript covering the development of the HowRwe [71] used the definition of the Beryl Institute [44] which defines patients’ experience as: “the sum of all interactions, shaped by an organization’s culture, that influence patient perceptions, across the continuum of care”. In the patients’ experience survey instrument development tool manuscript, it is simply mentioned that satisfaction is defined by patient experience [56]. In the P3CEQ, patient experience is defined as “individual patient perspectives on a range of health and social care related experiences” [68]. In two other manuscripts, patients’ experience is simply presented as patients’ journey within the health system [67] and an essential part of the Quadruple Aim of health reform [73].

Constructs measured

We identified the constructs that were included in each PREM (Table 2). Conceptual overlap across constructs is intrinsic to patient experience; accordingly, the identified domains are not intended to be mutually exclusive but are derived from the elements reported in the included PREMs. The most common construct included in the tools was accessibility, which was included in 18 tools (78%). Relational aspects and clinical care and services are also constructs that were each included in 13 tools (57%). Integrality and patient engagement were less frequently included, being present in only five (22%) PREMs. The description of the constructs within manuscripts covering the development of tools was consistent for some constructs, such as communication, coordination, continuity, integrality and global satisfaction. Integrality was often referred to as comprehensiveness of care. Other constructs were more various in their description. For example, the accessibility construct was defined not only by this term, but also as “first-contact care”, “facilities, availability and accessibility”, “Getting through on the phone/opening hours”, “Gaining access to the practice/comfort and privacy of the practice environment” and “Distance to the primary care practice”. For the other constructs, there was some variability in the description, as presented in Supplementary Material 3.

Characteristics of the PREMs

The number of items, time for completing the questionnaire and availability of the PREMs are also presented in Table 3. The PREMs included between four and 74 items and most authors did not mention the time needed to complete the questionnaires. Some tools are available online or directly within the manuscript, but others can only be obtained by contacting the authors. Most studies covering the development of the tool did not mention whether patients were involved in the development of the PREM tool. The authors explained the involvement of patients in only five studies [54, 56, 61, 65, 66]. For three tools, the development of the PREM involved the consultation of collaborators, including patients [54, 56, 66]. In the development of two other tools, patients were consulted about questions or concepts that should be included in the context of a qualitative study [61, 65].

Psychometric properties of the PREMs

The psychometric properties identified in the manuscripts covering the development and testing of the PREMs are presented in Table 3. Only four studies indicated which approach was used to test the PREMs psychometric properties (classic test theory or item-response theory) [58, 61, 68, 72] One study used both theories [68] and three studies used item-response theory [58, 61, 72]. The sample size used for validation varied from 112 to 55 687 participants. PREMs have been validated in various countries: seven in the United States [52, 56, 59, 62–64, 66], four in the United Kingdom [54, 55, 60, 68, 71], two in Norway [58, 70], one in Greece [67], three in Canada [61, 69, 73], one in Australia [57], one in Taiwan [72] and one in Poland [65]. The EUROPEP was validated in 16 European countries [53] and the QUALICO-PC Patient Questionnaire was validated in three European countries [74]. Thirteen PREMs were validated in English and tools were validated from 1994 to 2022. Fourteen of the 22 tools included were developed and validated with a large sample of patients who consulted in primary care or general practice without further details regarding their condition [52–57, 59–61, 65–68, 73, 74]. The PEQ-GP was specifically designed and validated in patients with chronic conditions experiencing general practice [58]. The PCAS and the PCAT tools were validated in members of health insurance policies covering healthcare, including primary care [63, 64]. The PSQ-18 was validated with patients’ visiting physicians and other healthcare providers, including family physicians [62]. The GS-PEQ tool was validated in patients admitted to a Norwegian health trust, including outpatients undergoing rehabilitation and patients receiving somatic, psychiatric and alcohol and substance dependency care [70]. The HCSQ tool was validated in the context of a research program aiming to integrate services for the maintenance of autonomy in the elderly population and considered all contacts with a health worker in the last six months [69]. The HowRwe tool was validated in a pre-operative assessment clinic for patients scheduled for major orthopaedic operations, but was also designed to be used in primary care [71]. The PFQ is a tool designed to measure physicians’ performance and was validated with outpatient visitors of a medical center [72].

Regarding psychometric properties, different indicators were used to assess the validity and reliability of the PREMs. Eleven studies used only exploratory factor analysis [53–57, 59, 61, 64, 65, 67, 71], one study used only confirmatory factor analysis [52] and two studies used both methods to assess structural validity [58, 69]. Exploratory factor analysis was variable across studies, with factors explaining between 52.8% and 88.1% of the variance of results [53–57, 59, 61, 64, 65, 67, 71]. Confirmatory factor analysis, which seeks to confirm existing hypotheses related to the factors included in the tool, showed a reasonable to good fit in the three studies using this method [52, 58, 69] (Table 3). Various methods have been used to evaluate other types of validity: content validity, criterion validity (convergent or divergent) and predictive validity (Table 3). Inter-scale correlations were also used to assess the relationships between the variables measured by those scales, which could be either a high or low, depending on the goals of the research [82]. The results regarding the various measures of validity of the PREMs are presented in Table 3.

Internal consistency using Cronbach α [52, 53, 55, 56, 58–67, 69, 71, 73] and Unequal length Spearman-Brown corrected correlation [56] was the most common measure of reliability. Internal consistency is generally assessed by examining the correlations between various items on the same test or subscale [83]. There is a large variability in the measure of internal consistency across PREMs, which mostly varies between questionable (0.6 ≤ α < 0.7) and excellent (α ³ 0.9) [84]. However, in two PREMs [67, 73], the internal consistency of the accessibility subscale of the tool was considered unacceptable (α < 0.5) [84]. Test-retest reliability [60, 69, 72] and rash item reliability [66] were also measured for some tools.

Discussion

The aim of this scoping review was to identify a set of validated PREMs for the primary care context. We identified 23 PREMs that can be used in primary care and analyzed the most common constructs included in these tools and the characteristics of the tools in terms of number of items, time to complete the questionnaire and availability of the tools. We also analyzed the articles covering the development of the tools to identify the population with which the tool was validated and the psychometric properties of the tool.

The concept of patient experience itself was rarely explicitly defined, even when its dimensions were operationalized through PREMs. Previous studies have also concluded that patient experience is a complex concept that lacks an agreed-upon definition in the scientific literature [44, 85]. Some authors suggest that patient experience is interchangeable with patient satisfaction [85], but others highlight its broader nature, including all interactions across the care continuum [44]. This variation highlights the need for a more standardized definition of patient experience to increase the comparability of results across studies and health systems [86]. In our review, we identified 26 constructs, including 12 that were recurrent in at least five PREMS, which demonstrated the complexity of the concept of patient experience. Interestingly, the outcome construct was identified. However, this concept is more closely aligned with PROMs, highlighting the potential overlap between PREMs and PROMs. No tool covered all the constructs of patient experience. Global satisfaction was one of the identified constructs and was present in eight tools. However, the constructs identified encompassed different elements of patient experience, including accessibility, relational aspects and clinical care and services, which were the constructs most present in the PREMs.

The tools included in this study varied widely in terms of their length, ranging from four to 74 items, with many authors failing to report the time required to complete the questionnaires. This lack of clarity regarding the burden of completing the tools could impact their practical applicability in clinical settings, where time constraints are often a significant concern [87]. Moreover, patients were involved in the development of only four of the tools, which may limit their use in routine practice. Ten PREMs were validated in North America and ten were validated in European countries. In this context, we can consider that these tools can be used in most developed countries. However, the differences in healthcare systems, patient populations, and cultural contexts across these countries may affect the generalizability of the tools. Most tools have been validated in English, which limits the generalizability of the tools. Indeed, PREMs may need cultural and language adaptations to be used in different contexts [88]. However, as a result of our inclusion criteria, the PREMs included in this review were primarily validated in general patient populations rather than condition-specific groups, making them more applicable for assessing general patient experience in primary care.

In the development of the included PREMs, the most common measure of validity was factor analysis, which was either exploratory or confirmatory. Factor analysis is a statistical technique used to identify underlying dimensions or factors that explain the relationships among a set of variables, simplifying complex data by grouping related items [89]. It is commonly employed to develop and validate instruments by providing evidence for construct validity on the basis of internal structure and test content [89]. Exploratory factor analysis is used to examine data and formulate hypotheses about underlying factors when prior evidence is limited to identifying characteristic features and relationships without imposing a factorial structure [90]. Confirmatory factor analysis is focused on testing theories on the basis of pre-existing hypotheses or knowledge [90]. Exploratory factor analysis condenses multiple variables into a smaller number of factors and confirmatory factor analysis verifies the relationships between observed measurements and latent variables [90]. In terms of reliability, there was a great variability in the measure of internal consistency across the PREMs. This measure evaluates whether multiple items intended to measure the same underlying construct yield consistent results and correlates with one another [83]. The variability in psychometric testing methods highlights the complexity of measuring patient experience and suggests that more comprehensive approaches to validation are needed to ensure the robustness of these instruments across different contexts [91].

Implications for practice

The overarching objective of this review was to synthesize PREMs developed for primary care and to identify their constructs, characteristics, and psychometric properties. PREMs are increasingly used not only to support quality improvement initiatives at the clinical and organizational levels, but also for performance assessment, public reporting, and value-based purchasing. As such, they serve the informational needs of multiple knowledge users, including healthcare providers, organizational leadership, payers, policymakers, and patients.

Our results can support clinicians, healthcare managers and researchers in selecting the most appropriate PREMs for their intended purpose, whether for research, internal quality improvement, organizational performance monitoring, or accountability, using Tables 2 and 3.

However, not all PREMs identified in this review are equally suited to all contexts of use. Several instruments include a large number of items, assess multiple constructs, or were validated in specific healthcare systems, which may limit their feasibility for routine implementation, particularly in busy primary care settings.

From a clinical and organizational perspective, overly complex or lengthy instruments may reduce response rates, increase administrative burden, and hinder the timely use of data for quality improvement. From a system-level perspective, variability in constructs, measurement properties, and implementation approaches may also affect the comparability of PREMs across organizations, thereby limiting their utility for performance benchmarking and public reporting.

These findings underscore the importance of aligning PREM selection and development with the intended level of use (clinical, organizational, or system-level), the target population, and the decision-making context. In this regard, well-validated short forms may offer an efficient compromise between measurement rigor and feasibility [92]. Furthermore, transparent reporting of psychometric properties and implementation characteristics is essential to support meaningful interpretation by all stakeholders, including patients and payers.

Strengths and limitations

This synthesis can improve the integration of PREMs across different primary care settings. By summarizing the constructs and psychometric properties of the tools, our review supports decision-makers, clinicians, and researchers in navigating the existing landscape of patient experience measures, while recognizing that the appropriateness of tool selection ultimately depends on alignment with the specific purpose for which an instrument was developed. A key limitation is that we only used the manuscript covering the development of the tool to describe the psychometric properties. Psychometric properties were also not consistently reported across studies, limiting the comparability of findings, the generalizability of results, and the feasibility of meta-analyses [93]. Inconsistent psychometric properties can result in inaccurate or misleading conclusions from score interpretations of patient experiences [94]. Reason for development of the tool and responsiveness was not extracted because it was not reported in most included PREMs. Enhancing the reporting of the psychometric properties of PREMs could facilitate their use in primary care settings, thereby contributing to improved healthcare delivery and greater patient satisfaction with care. Additionally, some relevant sources may have been missed since our search strategy was limited to the years 2019 and onward and used previously published synthesis. The limitations of the two syntheses used could have influenced our description. Also, as the literature search was completed in December 2023, PREMs developed after this date were not included in this review. In addition, some domains identified in this synthesis, such as Communication and Coordination, represent broad, multidimensional constructs. In this review, these domains were treated as higher-order categories to support synthesis and usability rather than as exhaustive representations of all possible subcomponents. While this approach enhances interpretability, it may oversimplify conceptual complexity.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes PREMs validated for use in primary care and highlights the diversity of constructs included in the patient experience. While PREMs provide valuable insights into care delivery, there is substantial variability in how patient experience is defined, which constructs are measured, and how psychometric properties are reported. To improve the measurement of patient experience, future work could explore the potential role of standardizing key constructs, while balancing this approach with concerns related to contextual relevance and conceptual plurality [86, 95]. Strengthening these aspects will enhance the relevance and usability of PREMs in primary care, ultimately contributing to more patient-centered healthcare.
