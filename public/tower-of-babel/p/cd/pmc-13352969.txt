Clinical recognition of pediatric diphtheria in a resource-limited Somali hospital: a prospective observational study
Authors: Farah Ali Ahmed, Mohamed Nur Ali, Mohamed Yaqub Hassan, Mustafa Gahnug, Yasir Khalif Ali, Farah Abdullahi Ismail, Ali Abdi Jama, Abdullahi Mohamed Hassan Fujeyra, Mohamed Mukhtar Mohamed, Said Abdirahman Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiphtheria remains a life-threatening disease in low-resource settings where immunization coverage is poor and access to a laboratory is limited. Somalia is currently experiencing a resurgence of clinically suspected diphtheria among children, yet detailed case-level clinical data remain scarce. The purpose of this study was to illustrate clinical features, vaccination status, complications, and early outcomes of pediatric diphtheria in a resource-limited Somali hospital. Furthermore, it identified the factors associated with severity and mortality for enhanced early recognition and management.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted, including 51 pediatric patients aged < 18 years admitted with clinically diagnosed diphtheria to a tertiary referral hospital in Somalia between January 2025 and December 2025. Diagnosis was based on WHO clinical criteria due to the absence of local laboratory capacity. Demographic, clinical, treatment, and outcome data were collected prospectively. The requirement for pediatric intensive care unit admission defined disease severity. Statistical comparisons between severe and non-severe cases used appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests, and univariable logistic regression explored predictors of severity and mortality. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. This study was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to guarantee transparent, thorough, and consistent demonstration of the observational methods and results.ResultsFifty-one children met the clinical case definition (median age 6.0 years; 52.9% male). Classical airway manifestations were frequent: pseudomembrane (88.2%), bull neck (88.2%), stridor (58.8%), and airway obstruction (52.9%). Vaccination coverage was extremely low, with only 9.8% having received any diphtheria-containing vaccine. Systemic complications included neuritis in 47.1% and myocarditis in 19.6%. Eleven patients (21.6%) required PICU admission and were classified as severe. Toxin-mediated complications strongly predicted severity: myocarditis (72.7% in severe vs 5.0% non-severe) and neuritis (100% vs 32.5%). Eight children died (overall case-fatality rate: 15.7%), all in the PICU cohort. Mortality was highest among children with myocarditis (50.0%) and neuritis (33.3%), and all deaths occurred in unvaccinated children. Univariable logistic regression revealed that toxin-mediated problems such as myocarditis and neuritis were robust predictors of severe disease and mortality (p < 0.05), whereas the classical airway features and vaccination status were not substantial.ConclusionClinically diagnosed pediatric diphtheria in Somalia is characterized by profound under-immunization, high rates of classical airway features, significant toxin-mediated complications, and substantial mortality. Early identification of severe illness is essential, particularly recognition of cardiac and neurological involvement. Strengthening immunization programs, ensuring timely diphtheria antitoxin availability, and expanding critical care capacity are urgent priorities to improve outcomes.Keywords: Diphtheria, Pediatrics, Somalia, Resource-limited settings, Vaccination

Introduction

Diphtheria is an acute and potentially life-threatening bacterial infection caused primarily by toxigenic strains of Corynebacterium diphtheria [1]. The organism produces diphtheria toxin, which can lead to respiratory obstruction, myocarditis, and a range of systemic neurological complications [1, 2]. Despite the long-standing availability of effective vaccines, diphtheria continues to pose a public health threat in settings where routine immunization coverage remains low, health services are fragmented, and access to microbiological confirmation is limited [3]. In recent years, a pattern of resurgence has been reported across several low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), driven largely by conflict, population displacement, and interruptions in essential immunization programs [3, 4].

Between 2022 and 2023, global immunization coverage remained stable without further improvement, with 89% receiving the first dose of the diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis (DTP) vaccine, 84% the third dose, and 83% the first dose of measles-containing vaccine. The burden of disease has become increasingly concentrated in regions with fragile health systems, including parts of sub-Saharan Africa, and the Middle East and South Asia [5, 6]. Recent outbreaks in Yemen, Nigeria, and among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh have illustrated how rapidly diphtheria can spread in densely populated, under-immunized communities [7–10]. Somalia represents one of the most vulnerable countries to vaccine-preventable disease resurgence [11]. The country has experienced three decades of protracted humanitarian crisis, recurrent drought, internal displacement, and limited public sector financing for health, all of which have contributed to persistently low routine immunization levels [12]. Estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF suggested that DTP3 coverage in Somalia has remained among the lowest globally, with national figures fluctuating between 40 and 50% in recent years. However, real coverage may be lower due to uncertainties in reporting from nomadic and hard-to-reach populations [13, 14]. A large proportion of Somali children fall into the "zero-dose" category, receiving no routine vaccinations during early childhood [13, 15]. These gaps elevated the risk of diphtheria transmission and created challenges for outbreak control [11, 15].

Diphtheria is mostly found among those who have not received vaccinations or only partial vaccinations, especially in deprived areas where healthcare facilities are unavailable. Diphtheria is marked by specific clinical symptoms such as a pseudomembrane in the throat, a bull neck, stridor and breathing difficulties, together with life-threatening systemic signs such as myocarditis and peripheral neuritis [16, 17]. Toxigenic Corynebacterium diphtheriae produces an exotoxin that interferes with protein production through the inhibition of elongation factor-2 (EF-2), causing pseudomembranes on the infected tissue and heart damage and neuropathy due to the distribution of the toxin systemically [18].

Since 2024, national surveillance updates have indicated a growing number of suspected diphtheria cases in Somalia, with clusters reported in areas affected by population displacement and disruption of primary healthcare services [11, 19]. In this context, access to laboratory confirmation was extremely limited. Few facilities possessed culture or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) capacity for C. diphtheriae, and specimens require specialized transport and referral pathways that are not always feasible during outbreaks [20]. As a result, clinical diagnosis becomes central to case identification, guided by WHO criteria that emphasize pharyngitis, pseudomembrane formation, and cervical swelling ("bull neck"), particularly in the absence of an alternative explanation[1, 3, 21].

Recent epidemiological reports have further emphasized that Somalia's resurgence is closely linked to zero-dose children, population displacement, disrupted primary healthcare services, vaccine shortages, and restricted access to outbreak response resources.

Published Somalian studies about diphtheria remain sparse, and mostly available information comes from public health reports and aggregated case summaries rather than detailed clinical studies [11, 19]. This gap has implications for early recognition and triage in hospitals with limited airway equipment, ventilatory support, or availability of diphtheria antitoxin (DAT) [22]. In previous regional outbreaks, delays in the clinical suspicion of diphtheria have been associated with preventable severe complications, including toxic cardiomyopathy and respiratory failure [7–9]. Similarly, the absence or delayed administration of DAT, a therapy that neutralizes circulating toxin, has been consistently associated with increased mortality [2, 21]. Given these challenges, improving clinical recognition is critical for effective case management in Somalia, particularly among children who may deteriorate rapidly. Pediatric diphtheria often presents with fever, dysphagia, cervical lymphadenopathy, progressive upper airway obstruction and, in advanced cases, signs of myocarditis or peripheral neuropathy [1, 2, 22]. In low-resource settings, where pediatric intensive care beds are extremely limited, identifying early predictors of disease severity may help guide prioritization of airway management and transfer for escalation of care [22].

This study has addressed the research gaps by offering prospectively collected, case-level clinical data from Somalia, where the available data have mostly included aggregate surveillance reports lacking any description of patients’ conditions. This paper covers the deficiency of clinical diagnosis in situations that lack laboratory diagnosis based on the WHO criteria. It specifically fills the gap in clinical recognition in the absence of laboratory confirmation by applying WHO-based diagnostic criteria and documenting real-world presentations. This study focused on clinical diagnosis without laboratory confirmation, reflecting the realities of frontline practice in this environment [18, 19]. It also highlighted the opportunities for strengthening syndromic surveillance, improving vaccination uptake, and ensuring the availability of diphtheria antitoxin and supportive critical care [15, 20]. By presenting detailed bedside observations from a resource-limited setting with ongoing transmission, this study contributed to a growing body of literature on diphtheria resurgence. This study further highlighted the clinical and public health response strategies within Somalia and similar humanitarian or health-system-challenged contexts.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This is a prospective observational study conducted, including 51 pediatric patients aged < 18 years, at a single tertiary referral hospital in Somalia, which serves as the national center for pediatric emergency and critical care. The study period extended from January 2025 until the time of final data extraction (January 2025–December 2025). Due to a lack of laboratory confirmation for diphtheria in Somalia, diagnosis was based exclusively on WHO suspected case criteria, defined as upper respiratory tract illness with an adherent pseudomembrane of the pharynx, tonsils or larynx, in the absence of an alternative explanation.

Eligibility criteria

Children were eligible for inclusion if they were aged < 18 years, were admitted to the study tertiary care hospital during the study period, and fulfilled the WHO suspected case criteria for diphtheria, based on clinical diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had an alternative confirmed diagnosis explaining their symptoms, had incomplete or missing clinical or outcome data, or did not meet the WHO clinical diagnostic criteria for suspected diphtheria.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Mogadishu Somali Türkiye Training and Research Hospital's institutional review board, MSTH/23474. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and local ethical guidelines. Written informed consent for participation was obtained from each patient's legal guardian. This study was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to guarantee transparent, thorough, and consistent demonstration of the observational methods and results.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and treatment data were collected prospectively using a structured data collection form. Variables included age, sex, vaccination status, presenting symptoms, local and systemic complications (with emphasis on toxin-mediated myocarditis and neuritis), treatment interventions (including diphtheria antitoxin and antibiotics), hospital level of care and in-hospital outcome (improved, referred or died).

Assessment of myocarditis and neuritis

Myocarditis was diagnosed based on compatible clinical features together with objective evidence of cardiac involvement, including elevated cardiac biomarkers (CK-MB and/or troponin) and/or echocardiographic abnormalities documented during hospital admission.

Neuritis was diagnosed clinically by the treating pediatric team based on neurological examination findings consistent with diphtheritic neuropathy, including cranial nerve involvement, limb weakness, reduced or absent deep tendon reflexes, bulbar dysfunction, or other focal neurological deficits occurring during the course of illness.

Treatment and supportive care

Treatment consisted of diphtheria antitoxin when available, antibiotic therapy according to national or hospital guidance, airway monitoring, and supportive care. Antibiotic treatment was provided to all children; penicillin- or macrolide-based regimens were used according to clinician judgment, availability, and local guidance. Diphtheria antitoxin was administered after clinical recognition whenever supply permitted. Exact timing from symptom onset to antitoxin administration and detailed drug-level antibiotic data were not uniformly documented and were therefore not included in comparative outcome modeling.

Severity classification

Disease severity was defined a priori by pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission, representing the requirement for advanced respiratory or cardiovascular support. Children managed exclusively on the general pediatric ward were categorized as non-severe.

Outcome measures

Two primary outcomes were evaluated in this study: severe disease, defined as admission to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), and in-hospital mortality, defined as death during the index hospitalization.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Python (v3.9) and SciPy statistical libraries. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed or median with interquartile range (IQR) otherwise. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Distributional assumptions for continuous variables were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Comparisons between severe (PICU) and non-severe (ward) groups were performed using the Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test (when expected cell counts were < 5) was applied for categorical comparisons, including clinical features, vaccination status, and complications. However, exploratory univariable logistic regression was used to assess associations between selected clinical predictors (pseudomembrane, bull neck, stridor, myocarditis, neuritis, and vaccination status) and the outcomes of severe disease or mortality. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Multivariable models were not undertaken due to limited sample size and low event counts. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 51 pediatric patients met the clinical case definition for diphtheria during the study period. The median age was 6.0 years (IQR 4.5–7.5), and 27 (52.9%) were male. All diagnoses were clinical because local laboratory facilities for Corynebacterium diphtheriae isolation, PCR, or toxigenicity testing were unavailable. Classical upper airway involvement was frequent, with fever, dysphagia, pseudomembrane formation, and bull neck each observed in 45 (88.2%) children. Additional features included stridor in 30 (58.8%), and cough, neck swelling, lymphadenopathy, and airway obstruction in 27 patients each (52.9%) (Table 1).

Vaccination status and treatment

Vaccination documentation was available for all patients and demonstrated marked under-immunization: 46 children (90.2%) were unvaccinated, while only 5 (9.8%) had received any diphtheria-containing vaccine (Table 1). Diphtheria antitoxin was administered to 48 of 51 children (94.1%) after clinical suspicion, while 3 children did not receive antitoxin because of supply or documentation constraints. All children received antibiotic therapy according to national or hospital guidance. Because nearly all children received antitoxin and exact timing from symptom onset was not consistently documented, treatment-specific comparative outcome analysis was not performed. Systemic complications were common, with neuritis affecting 24 children (47.1%) and myocarditis affecting 10 children (19.6%).

Disease severity and complications

Disease severity was classified according to the requirement for pediatric intensive care unit admission. Eleven children (21.6%) required PICU care, whereas 40 (78.4%) were managed on the general ward (Fig. 1). Classical airway features did not reliably discriminate severe disease: pseudomembrane and bull neck occurred in 7 of 11 severe cases (63.6%) versus 38 of 40 non-severe cases (95.0%). In contrast, toxin-mediated complications were more frequent among children requiring PICU care: myocarditis affected 8 of 11 (72.7%) PICU patients compared with 2 of 40 (5.0%) ward patients, while neuritis occurred in all severe cases (100%) compared with 13 of 40 (32.5%) non-severe cases (Table 2).

Mortality

Eight children died, giving an overall case-fatality rate of 15.7%. All deaths occurred among the PICU group, and all were in unvaccinated children, whereas no vaccinated patient died (0/5, 0.0%). Mortality was higher among children with myocarditis (5/10, 50.0%) and neuritis (8/24, 33.3%) than among those without these complications. No deaths occurred among children without neuritis. Mortality varied by age group, with deaths occurring in 3 of 13 children aged < 5 years (23.1%), 1 of 28 children aged 5–9 years (3.6%), and 4 of 10 children aged ≥ 10 years (40.0%). Mortality differed significantly by age group and was significantly higher among children with myocarditis or neuritis, while vaccination status and airway obstruction were not statistically associated with mortality in this cohort (Table 3).

Discussion

Diphtheria is one of the most lethal infections in low-resource backgrounds due to reduced vaccination coverage, leading to severe respiratory and systemic complications. This case series provided one of the most detailed clinical characterizations of pediatric diphtheria in Somalia in recent years. It highlighted several epidemiological and clinical patterns that are consistent with reports from other African and global outbreaks. The predominance of unvaccinated children, the high frequency of classical upper airway features, and the substantial burden of toxin-mediated complications observed in this cohort underscore the vulnerability of populations living in fragile health systems. The findings align with the broader trend of diphtheria re-emergence in regions affected by prolonged conflict, large-scale displacement, and disruption of routine immunization services [23–25].

Burden among unvaccinated children

The finding that 90% of children had no documented vaccination mirrors data from diphtheria outbreaks in Nigeria, Sudan, Ethiopia, and South Sudan, where low DTP3 coverage and high "zero-dose" prevalence have repeatedly been identified as key drivers of transmission [26–30]. Similarly, studies from Yemen and Bangladesh reported that more than 70–85% of affected children in their outbreaks were unvaccinated [31–33]. Global analyses from WHO and Gavi estimated that diphtheria disproportionately affects zero-dose children in conflict and displacement settings, with Somalia consistently ranking among the countries with the lowest routine immunization coverage [34–36]. Overall, these findings emphasize the central role of immunization gaps in sustaining diphtheria transmission and support calls for targeted outreach in borderland, nomadic, and internally displaced populations [37].

Clinical presentation and airway involvement

The clinical pattern in the present series, with high prevalence of pseudomembrane formation (88%), bull neck (88%), stridor (59%), and airway obstruction (53%), is consistent with severe disease phenotypes described in African outbreaks. Reports from Ethiopia, South Africa, and Sudan have documented similar airway-dominant presentations among children, often exacerbated by late presentation and limited access to antitoxin [38–40]. In contrast, in highly immunized populations in Europe and North America, contemporary surveillance and guideline data indicate that classical respiratory diphtheria with extensive pseudomembrane and "bull neck" is now uncommon; many infections are asymptomatic or present as mild pharyngitis without membrane, particularly in vaccinated individuals [41, 42].

The findings therefore reinforce that the clinical phenotype of diphtheria in low-resource settings is often more severe and closely aligned with classical textbook descriptions.

Toxin-mediated complications were also common in this cohort, with suspected myocarditis in 20% and neuritis in 47%. The myocarditis frequency is similar to recent pediatric diphtheria cohorts, including a tertiary hospital cohort from Pakistan that reported myocarditis in approximately 20% of cases and neuropathy in 16.7%. The higher neuritis frequency observed in our cohort may reflect delayed presentation, profound under-immunization, referral bias to a tertiary center, and the possibility that severe toxin-mediated disease was overrepresented among admitted children. Because neurological assessment was conducted prospectively at bedside, mild cranial neuropathies and limb weakness may also have been captured more frequently than in retrospective cohorts relying on discharge coding or incomplete documentation. These factors should be considered when comparing the 47.1% neuritis rate with historical textbook estimates of 10–20% and with other under-immunized settings.

Although early diphtheria antitoxin administration is associated with improved survival in published studies, this study was not designed to estimate treatment effectiveness. In the present cohort, almost all children received antitoxin and exact timing from symptom onset was not uniformly documented. Therefore, any apparent absence of measurable survival benefit should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect late presentation, advanced toxin-mediated complications, limited PICU capacity, and confounding by severity rather than lack of biological benefit [43, 44].

In the current study, approximately 22% of children met the severe disease definition, which is consistent with reports from Ethiopian and Sudanese hospitals documenting severe disease in 20–28% of pediatric cases [10, 45]. The mortality rate of 15.7% in our series is comparable to estimates from African humanitarian settings (10–20%) but higher than rates reported from middle‑income countries (< 5%) where antitoxin access is reliable. All deaths occurred among children classified as having severe disease, supporting global evidence that early recognition and aggressive supportive care are critical to improving outcomes [43].

The global resurgence of diphtheria has been concentrated in low‑resource settings characterized by population movement, fragile health systems, and disrupted vaccination programs, conditions that closely mirror those in Somalia [25, 34, 46, 47]. The findings are consistent with reports showing that clinical case definitions in such settings capture more advanced disease than laboratory‑confirmed cases in high‑income regions, where immunity levels are higher and clinical features may be subtler. This underscores the need for improved syndromic surveillance and decentralized diagnostic capacity in Somalia [41, 42, 48].

Implications for practice and policy

This study highlights several priorities for Somalia. Strengthening routine immunization and targeted outreach to zero-dose communities remains essential to reduce the susceptible population. Clinical recognition training for frontline providers is important where diagnostic testing remains unavailable. Ensuring timely access to diphtheria antitoxin and standardized antibiotic therapy should be prioritized as part of outbreak preparedness. Finally, reinforcing referral pathways and pediatric intensive care capacity may help reduce mortality among children who present with severe disease.

Strengths and limitations

Myocarditis was identified using clinical assessment supported by elevated cardiac biomarkers and echocardiography, while neuritis was diagnosed clinically through neurological examination. However, formal neurophysiological testing, throat culture, blood culture, PCR, and toxigenicity testing were not routinely available, and alternative viral or streptococcal etiologies could not be systematically excluded.

Conclusion

Pediatric diphtheria in Somalia was characterized by low vaccination coverage, frequent classical airway manifestations, toxin-related complications, and considerable mortality. These findings suggest that timely recognition of cardiac and neurological involvement may support earlier escalation of care. Strengthening immunization uptake, improving access to diphtheria antitoxin and standardized antibiotics, and expanding pediatric critical care capacity may help reduce morbidity and mortality in under-immunized settings such as Somalia. Future studies with laboratory confirmation, standardized complication definitions, and larger sample sizes are needed to validate these observations and inform national response strategies.
