αIIbβ3-dependent platelet procoagulant activity promotes pulmonary arterial thrombosis triggered by circulating tissue factor
Authors: Sarah E Tashbook, Tomasz W Kaminski, Claudette M St Croix, Simon C Watkins, Prithu Sundd, Tomasz Brzoska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn situ pulmonary arterial thrombosis (iPAT), occurring without concurrent deep vein thrombosis, is a life-threatening complication associated with various pathological conditions. The etiological mechanisms underlying iPAT remain poorly understood. Several studies suggest that circulating tissue factor (cTF) may contribute to the development of thrombotic complications; however, there is no direct in vivo evidence supporting the role of cTF in the pathogenesis of iPAT. Furthermore, although in vitro studies suggest that platelet anionic phospholipids enable cTF-initiated coagulation, how platelets contribute to cTF-dependent iPAT in vivo remains unknown. In the current study, we used quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy to investigate the development of cTF-induced iPAT in live mice following intravascular administration of thromboplastin. To dissect the interplay between coagulation and platelet procoagulant activity, we assessed the effects of coagulation and platelet inhibition on iPAT development in vivo. Additionally, we conducted an in vitro clotting time assay using mouse plasma samples. Thromboplastin triggered iPAT in mice in a dose-dependent manner. IPAT involved the formation of platelet-rich thrombi at the bottle-neck junctions of pulmonary arterioles and capillaries, which was prevented by heparin. Notably, pretreatment of mice with annexin A5 or eptifibatide also completely abrogated thromboplastin-induced iPAT. These intravital microscopy findings were further corroborated by the in vitro clotting time assay. Our study provides in vivo evidence that cTF contributes to the development of iPAT. We demonstrate that the prothrombotic effect of cTF is dependent on platelet-αIIbβ3 signaling, which enhances platelet procoagulant activity, leading to accelerated coagulation and development of iPAT.Keywords: Pulmonary arterial thrombosis, Circulating tissue factor, Platelet procoagulant activity, Platelet αIIbβ3, Quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy

Introduction

De novo (in situ) pulmonary arterial thrombosis (iPAT), in the absence of concomitant deep vein thrombosis (DVT), is a life-threatening complication associated with various pathological conditions [1,2] such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [3], sickle cell disease (SCD) [4], viral infections [5,6], sepsis [7], cancer [8], trauma [9–11], and surgeries [12,13]. Despite significant advances in imaging techniques and therapeutic options, the etiological mechanisms underlying iPAT are understudied and remain poorly understood.

Tissue factor (TF) is an integral membrane protein present on the plasma membrane of many cells that are not exposed to the circulating blood [14,15]. Following vascular injury, TF is exposed to the blood, where it interacts with activated plasma factor VII (VIIa), initiating a cascade of linked enzymatic reactions that culminates in the generation of thrombin and the subsequent conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin [14,15]. Multiple studies suggest that an additional source of TF, known as circulating (blood-borne) tissue factor (cTF), may also contribute to thrombosis [16,17]. In a whole blood, cTF exists in three forms: (1) associated with most of the leukocytes and platelets [18]; (2) associated with different cell-derived extracellular vesicles [19]; and (3) as a soluble protein lacking the transmembrane domain [20]. Although elevated levels and increased activity of cTF have been associated with thrombotic complications across various pathological conditions, [21–28] there is no direct in vivo evidence supporting its role in the development of iPAT.

While TF is a primary initiator of blood coagulation, activated platelets may also play a critical role in accelerating this process by externalizing anionic phospholipids, such as phosphatidylserine (PS), that provide a catalytic surface for the assembly of coagulation enzyme complexes [29]. This phenomenon, known as platelet procoagulant activity, plays a crucial role in the propagation of coagulation and thrombus formation [29,30]. Prior in vitro studies have demonstrated that platelet anionic phospholipids regulate cTF-initiated coagulation [31], but whether platelet procoagulant activity directly contributes to cTF-dependent iPAT in vivo remains unknown. Here, we use quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy (qFILM) [32,33] in live mice to reveal that cTF initiates iPAT in vivo by triggering transient sequestration of platelet-rich thrombi in the pre-capillary pulmonary arterioles. Furthermore, using an inhibitory approach, we establish that αIIbβ3-dependent platelet procoagulant activity is essential to the initiation and progression of cTF-triggered iPAT.

Materials and methods

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6J mice (~3 months old) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Both male and female mice were included in this study. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pittsburgh.

Intravital lung microscopy in mice

Quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy (qFILM) was used to assess in situ pulmonary thrombosis (iPAT) within the intact lung microcirculation of live WT mice (Fig. 1A). The qFILM experimental setup and approach have been described elsewhere in detail [34,35]. Briefly, intravital lung microscopy was performed using a Nikon A1R Multi-Photon-Excitation (MPE) Ni-E upright motorized microscope (Nikon Instruments; Tokyo, Japan). Two-dimensional (2D) time series of intravital images were acquired with NIS-Elements software using a chirped Chameleon Laser Vision (Coherent; Santa Clara, CA) emitting an excitation wavelength of 850 nm, an APO LWD 25× water immersion objective with 1.1 NA a high-speed resonant scanning mode capable of acquisition at 512 × 512 resolution with 2× line averaging and bidirectional scanning (~15 frames per second) and four GaAsP NDD detectors. Before imaging, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine HCl (Covetrus, Portland, ME) and 20 mg/kg Xylazine (Akorn Pharmaceuticals, cat# 59399-111-50). A cannula was inserted into the right carotid artery, and a tracheotomy was performed to facilitate mechanical ventilation with 95 % O2 and maintenance anesthesia (1 % isoflurane). The left lung was surgically exposed, and a small portion of the lung was immobilized against a coverslip using a vacuum-enabled micromachined device [34].

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC) dextran (MW 70 KDa, 75 μg/mouse, Thermo Fischer Scientific, cat# D1823) and Violet 450-conjugated rat-anti mouse CD49b mAb (V450-anti-mouse CD49b Ab; clone DX5, 7 μg/mouse, BioLegend, cat# 108902) were intravascularly (IV) administered through the carotid artery catheter to enable in vivo visualization of the lung microcirculation and in vivo staining of platelets, respectively. In a separate set of experiments, Evans Blue (75 μg/mouse, Millipore Sigma, cat# E2129) was used to visualize lung microcirculation, Alexa Fluor-488-conjugated anti-fibrin mAb (AF488-anti-fibrin mAb; clone 59D8, 15 μg/mouse, Millipore Sigma, cat# MABS2155) allowed in vivo visualization of fibrin, Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated annexin A5 (AF568-ANX; 0.27 μg/mouse, Invitrogen, cat# A13202) was used to detect in vivo activated platelets exposing phosphatidylserine (PS).

Pacific Hemostasis Thromboplastin D (thromboplastin; Thermo Scientific, cat# 100356TS) was used to mimic the circulating tissue factor’s (cTF) procoagulant activity. Thromboplastin, a rabbit brain extract containing TF and phospholipids, is commonly utilized in prothrombin time (PT) assay [36]. Thromboplastin was previously used to induce thrombosis in mice [37]. Here, to test whether cTF can trigger iPAT in mice, 100 or 200 μl/kg body weight (kgbw) of thromboplastin was administrated via the carotid artery catheter. When required, 300 U/kg heparin (1000 U/ml, SAGENT Pharmaceuticals, cat# 25021–400-30), 10 mg/kg eptifibatide acetate (eptifibatide; Millipore Sigma, cat# SML1042), or 200 μg/kg annexin A5 (ANX; Millipore Sigma, cat# SRP8026) were given IV 15 min before iPAT initiation.

In each mouse, a single 2D field of view (FOV; ~118,000 μm2) containing a pulmonary arteriole (30 to 50 μm diameter) and the downstream pulmonary capillary bed was randomly selected, and then the presence or absence of platelet-rich thrombi was assessed. These observations were carried out in 3 to 4 mice per study group. Time series of qFILM images were recorded prior (t = 0 s) and up to 20 min post IV administration of thromboplastin. The experiment concluded with the euthanization of the mouse using an overdose of anesthesia. The same intravital imaging platform and methodological approach were employed to visualize the hepatic and splenic microcirculations in mice.

QFILM image processing and data analysis

Time series of qFILM images were processed and analyzed using Nikon’s NIS-Elements software (NIKON, USA) as described previously [34,35]. First, an image subtraction algorithm was used to remove autofluorescence and bleed through between fluorescent channels. Second, the signal-to-noise ratio was enhanced by using a median filter algorithm followed by smoothing and denoising algorithms. Third, each channel was pseudo-colored as follows: microcirculation as purple, platelets as green, fibrin as red, and ANX as yellow to enhance contrast and facilitate visualization. Next, pulmonary arterioles and downstream capillaries were analyzed for the quantitative assessment of iPAT as described previously [35]. Briefly, platelet-rich thrombi were defined as platelet aggregates (area ≥ 10 μm2) formed within the pre-capillary pulmonary arterioles and extending into the pulmonary capillaries. Two-dimensional sizes (areas in μm2) of platelet-rich thrombi were estimated in NIS-Elements by converting qFILM images into binary images and adjusting the threshold range of the intensity histograms uniformly over the entire FOV in each frame of the time series. The sizes of all the platelet-rich thrombi in a single frame were added to estimate the total pulmonary thrombi area, which was plotted as a function of time in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software; La Jolla, CA). This approach allowed us to assess the kinetics of the initiation and progression of pulmonary thrombosis in an observed FOV. Changes in iPAT area in a FOV over time were used to estimate the area under the curve (AUC), which is a combined measure of both the size and lifetime of platelet-rich thrombi. The same approach was applied to process and analyze the intravital imaging data from the hepatic and splenic microcirculations.

Mouse platelet-rich plasma and platelet-poor plasma preparation

Mice were anesthetized with an IP injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine HCl and 20 mg/kg xylazine. Blood was drawn through the inferior vena cava using a 1 ml syringe containing 3.8 % sodium citrate (1:10). Blood sample was diluted with an equal volume of saline (maintained at room temperature) and centrifuged (100 g for 8 min at 22 °C) to prepare platelet-rich plasma (PRP) samples. A fraction of PRP was again centrifuged (1800 g for 10 min at 22 °C) to obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP) samples. Platelet concentration in the PRP was determined using HemaVet 950FS (Drew Scientific, Plantation, FL, USA) and adjusted to 150 × 10 [3] platelets/μl using autologous PPP.

Clotting time assay

Mouse PPP and PRP clotting times were assessed using BFT II Analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Products GmbH, Marburg, Germany). Fifty microliters samples of PPP or PRP (150 × 10 [3] platelets/μl) were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the presence or absence of 0.5 mM eptifibatide, 30 μg/ml Annexin A5, or equivalent volumes of vehicle (0.9 % NaCl). Following incubation, clotting times were recorded immediately after adding 100 μl of a 1:50 dilution of Pacific Hemostasis Thromboplastin D (thromboplastin; Thermo Scientific, cat# 100356TS) in 2 mM CaCl2.

Statistics

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Pulmonary arterial thrombosis maximum area (iPAT max area), area under the curve (AUC), and clotting times were analyzed using either the Mann-Whitney test (for non-normally distributed data) or the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data). In the dot plots, error bars represent the mean ± SE. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Circulating tissue factor promotes coagulation to trigger iPAT in mice

To investigate whether cTF promotes iPAT in vivo, we intravascularly (IV) administered wild-type (WT) mice with 100 or 200 μl/kg body weight (kgbw) of thromboplastin. Thromboplastin was previously successfully used to mimic cTF procoagulant activity in vivo [37]. Quantitative fluorescence intravital lung microscopy (qFILM) was utilized to assess pulmonary microcirculation in live mice, as detailed in the Materials and methods section and elsewhere (Fig. 1A) [34,35,38,39]. Prior to thromboplastin administration, qFILM revealed no evidence of iPAT in mice, with blood (purple fluorescence) flowing unobstructed through the mouse pulmonary vasculature and erythrocytes appearing as rapidly transiting dark cells (Fig. 1B, t = 0 s and Video S1). Administration of 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin induced iPAT in mice, as evidenced by the formation of platelet-rich thrombi (green fluorescence) in the pulmonary arterioles (Fig. 1B; Video S1). Over time, the thrombi obstructed the arteriolar bottlenecks (junctions between pulmonary arterioles and capillaries), impairing pulmonary blood flow. IPAT triggered by 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin did not completely resolve over the 20-min observation period. As shown in Fig. S1 and Video S2, mice challenged with a smaller dose (100 μl/kgbw) of IV thromboplastin developed milder iPAT, suggesting that thromboplastin induces iPAT in a dose-dependent manner. Next, the time series of qFILM images were analyzed to estimate the total iPAT area over time, the maximum iPAT area, and the area under the curve (AUC) as described in the Materials and methods section and elsewhere [35]. The mean iPAT maximum area (Fig. S1D) and AUC (Fig. S1E) were significantly greater in mice challenged with 200 μl/kgbw thromboplastin than those receiving 100 μl/kgbw thromboplastin. Next, we assessed whether thromboplastin-induced iPAT is coagulation-dependent. Heparin is a potent anticoagulant that significantly accelerates the inhibition of thrombin and activated coagulation factors X, IX, XI, and XII by antithrombin [40]. Given that both unfractionated heparin and low-molecular-weight heparins are widely used for thrombosis prevention and treatment [41,42], we utilized qFILM to assess iPAT development in mice pretreated with 300 U/kg IV heparin 15 min before 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin administration (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1C, D, and Video S3, heparin successfully prevented thromboplastin-induced iPAT. The mean iPAT maximum area (Fig. 1E) and AUC (Fig. 1F) were significantly reduced compared to mice receiving IV thromboplastin alone.

To evaluate whether cTF promotes thrombosis in other organs, we performed fluorescence microscopy of the liver and spleen in WT mice following 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin administration. Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4 demonstrate the absence of platelet-rich thrombi in the hepatic and splenic microcirculations, respectively, following thromboplastin IV challenge. Notably, transient impairment of blood flow was observed in the splenic microcirculation without visible platelet-rich thrombus formation (Fig. S4B, evidenced by a temporary reduction in purple fluorescence at t = 48 s), with normal flow restored by t = 2.2 min. This brief blood flow cessation likely reflects a secondary effect of iPAT. These findings may indicate that, in our model, cTF induces in situ thrombosis specifically within the pulmonary microcirculation.

Platelet procoagulant activity enables cTF-dependent iPAT in vivo

Platelet phosphatidylserine (PS) exposure is a key component of platelet procoagulant activity, which is essential for the assembly of coagulation complexes and fibrin generation [29,30]. To determine the potential role of platelet procoagulant activity in fibrin generation during cTF-dependent iPAT in vivo, we analyzed platelet PS exposure and fibrin formation in the pulmonary circulation of mice challenged IV with 200 μl/kgbw thromboplastin in the presence of low concentration (smaller than concentration need for PS inhibition in vivo) of fluorescently labeled annexin A5 (AF568-ANX; 0.27 μg/mouse) and anti-fibrin antibody (Fig. 2A; see Materials and methods). As shown in Fig. 2C, 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin led to formation of platelet-rich thrombi (green fluorescence) in mouse pulmonary arteriolar microcirculation. Initially (t = 1 min), only a small subset of platelets exhibited PS on their surface, as indicated by the AF568-ANX binding (Fig. 2 Di, ii; yellow fluorescence). Notably, fibrin presence at this stage was also minimal and localized in close proximity to PS-positive platelets (Fig. 2 Di, ii; red fluorescence). Over the next 20 min, platelet PS exposure gradually increased, coinciding with the expansion of the fibrin network (Fig. 2 Diii, iv). At t = 20 min, the thrombus was abundant in PS-positive platelets surrounded by fibrin mesh (Fig. 2B and Dv). To further validate the importance of platelet PS exposure in cTF-initiated iPAT, we next assessed the effect of inhibiting platelet procoagulant activity using a high dose of annexin A5 (ANX; 200 μg/kg). At this concentration, ANX binds to exposed PS and inhibits coagulation by forming a protective two-dimensional (2D) crystallized shield over the platelet surface [43,44]. QFILM was used to assess iPAT development in mice IV pretreated with 200 μg/kg ANX 15 min before the IV administration of 200 μl/kgbw thromboplastin. ANX abolished iPAT development (Fig. 2E, F), with both the maximum iPAT area and the AUC significantly reduced (Fig. 2G, H).

Platelet αIIbβ3 inhibition prevents cTF-dependent iPAT

TF-initiated coagulation depends on the assembly of coagulation enzyme complexes on the surface of PS-positive platelets [29,30,45]. Importantly, platelet αIIbβ3 signaling has been shown to promote platelet PS exposure [46]. Therefore, we hypothesized that inhibiting platelet αIIbβ3 signaling could reduce platelet procoagulant activity and, consequently, attenuate cTF-induced iPAT. Eptifibatide, a potent αIIbβ3 antagonist, is clinically used to manage thrombotic complications, including percutaneous coronary intervention, acute coronary syndrome, and unstable angina [47]. Mice were pretreated with 10 mg/kg IV eptifibatide (as described elsewhere [35]) 15 min before 200 μl/kgbw IV thromboplastin (Fig. 3A). Remarkably, αIIbβ3 inhibition completely abrogated the formation of platelet-rich thrombi within the pulmonary microcirculation (Fig. 3B, C, Video S4). Both iPAT max area and AUC were significantly reduced in mice pretreated with eptifibatide (Fig. 3D, E). Furthermore, fibrin deposition was also absent in the pulmonary microcirculation of IV thromboplastin-challenged mice pretreated with eptifibatide (Fig. S2).

Next, we utilized an in vitro clotting time assay to validate our in vivo findings in mice. Platelet-poor plasma (PPP) and platelet-rich plasma (PRP; 150 × 103 platelets/μl) samples were prepared from the blood of WT mice. Clotting times were measured following the addition of thromboplastin and CaCl2 ± addition of 0.5 mM eptifibatide or 30 μg/ml ANX (Fig. 3F, see Materials and methods section). As shown in Fig. 3G, the presence of platelets in PRP (gray bar) significantly shortened mean clotting time compared to PPP alone (white bar), which was reversed following treatment of PRP with eptifibatide (blue bar) or ANX (red bar).

Discussion

In recent years, iPAT has emerged as a significant clinical pulmonary complication associated with diverse inflammatory and infectious diseases. Growing evidence suggests that iPAT occurs in the absence of DVT and involves de novo (locally developed) thrombosis in small pulmonary artery branches and arterioles, thus making it distinct from venous thromboembolism [1–7,9–13]. In the current study, we aimed to determine whether and how cTF may contribute to the pathogenesis of iPAT in vivo. To achieve this, we employed intravital microscopy of the intact lung in live mice to examine the initiation and progression of iPAT following IV administration of thromboplastin (a surrogate for cTF). Thromboplastin triggered iPAT in a dose-dependent manner, leading to the formation of platelet-rich thrombi within pulmonary arterioles. These thrombi occluded pulmonary arteriolar bottlenecks and impaired pulmonary blood flow, suggesting that cTF activity contributes to the development of iPAT in vivo.

Notably, thromboplastin-induced iPAT was partially irreversible and did not completely resolve within the 20-min duration of the experiment. We hypothesized that this prolonged life of thrombi was enabled by cTF-dependent thrombin generation, which not only promoted platelet activation and aggregation, but also stabilized thrombi through coagulation activation and fibrin formation. Indeed, intravascular administration of 300 U/kg heparin (inhibitor of coagulation) prior to the thromboplastin challenge abrogated iPAT in mice. Furthermore, qFILM studies revealed fibrin localized within thrombi, which was spatiotemporally dependent on platelet PS exposure. These findings align with our previous in vitro study, where we showed that fibrin network assembly occurs preferentially at the surface of PS-exposing platelets [31]. Therefore, we next investigated whether platelet procoagulant activity promotes cTF-dependent iPAT. Notably, pretreatment of mice with a high concentration of ANX completely abrogated thromboplastin-triggered iPAT, suggesting an essential role of platelet procoagulant activity in the propagation phase of coagulation during iPAT. Our previous study demonstrated the importance of platelet αIIbβ3 outside-in signaling in platelet PS exposure [46]. Here, we show, for the first time, that αIIbβ3 receptor antagonist effectively prevented cTF-triggered iPAT in live mice in vivo and clot formation in vitro. These findings also suggest that the αIIbβ3 inhibition prevents cTF-induced iPAT probably by impairing the ability of platelets to expose anionic phospholipids and support thrombin generation; therefore, administration of exogenous thrombin should bypass the requirement for platelet activation in iPAT development (Fig. 3H). Indeed, we have shown previously that αIIbβ3 blockade failed to prevent iPAT in mice triggered by IV thrombin challenge [35]. Taken together, our past and current findings suggest that platelets contribute to the pathogenesis of iPAT in an αIIbβ3-dependent manner by undergoing aggregation, exhibiting procoagulant activity, and promoting thrombin generation.

Our findings uncover a mechanistic link between cTF activity and αIIbβ3-dependent platelet procoagulant responses in the development of iPAT. This pathway may represent a novel therapeutic target for mitigating platelet-driven pulmonary thrombosis associated with various pathological conditions. Notably, αIIbβ3 antagonists such abciximab are already approved for clinical use in acute coronary syndromes [48], underscoring the translational potential of targeting this pathway. Nevertheless, validation of these mechanisms in human studies is required to establish their clinical relevance and to evaluate the safety and efficacy of αIIbβ3 inhibition in patients at risk of developing iPAT.

Thromboplastin was previously used as a surrogate for TF to model procoagulant state associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [49]. DIC is an acquired, multifactorial thrombo-inflammatory syndrome in which widespread activation of coagulation, endothelial injury, impaired anticoagulant systems and dysregulated fibrinolysis lead to microvascular thrombosis and organ dysfunction [50,51]. Notably, Olrik Berthelsen et al. [52] reported that intravenous thromboplastin administration causes hemostatic abnormalities but, according to the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria, does not result in overt DIC in rabbits. Thus, in the current study, we specifically focused on the prothrombotic consequences of elevated cTF, modeled by IV thromboplastin administration, that leads to iPAT. Moreover, we confirmed a lack of thrombosis in hepatic and splenic microcirculation following IV thromboplastin. These data are in agreement with previous reports demonstrating absence of thrombosis in kidneys of mice [37] and rabbits [53] challenged IV with thromboplastin. Collectively, our findings suggest that, in our model, IV thromboplastin induces in situ thrombosis specifically within the pulmonary microcirculation.

Our study has a few limitations that may inspire future investigations. First, cTF may originate from multiple cellular sources and exist in different forms [54]. Additionally, its concentration and activity in blood may vary across different pathological conditions [45,55]. To account for this variability, we used commercially available thromboplastin at different concentrations to mimic cTF activity in vivo. Future studies are needed to determine which specific fraction of cTF contributes to iPAT in various pathological scenarios. Second, multiple studies have highlighted the significance of extracellular vesicles as a source of both cTF and anionic phospholipids in thrombosis and hemostasis [56]. It is likely that extracellular vesicles, in synergy with platelets, contribute to iPAT initiation and progression. More elaborate studies need to be designed in the future to investigate this interplay between platelets and extracellular vesicles to better understand their collective role in iPAT pathogenesis across different pathological states. Notwithstanding these limitations, the current study introduces an intravital microscopy-based approach to probe the pathogenesis of iPAT in live mice. Using this approach, we provide the first real-time in vivo evidence to establish that iPAT involves occlusion of pulmonary arteriolar bottlenecks by platelet-rich thrombi and αIIbβ3-dependent platelet procoagulant activity promotes the development of iPAT in vivo (Fig. 3H).
