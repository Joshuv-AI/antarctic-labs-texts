Metastatic pulmonary calcification on bone scintigraphy of a patient with lupus nephritis and end-stage renal disease
Authors: Nina H. Cheng, Yi Li, Gang Cheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A 65-year-old male complaining of low back pain was noted to have diffuse, homogenous bilateral lung uptake on Tc-99m methylene diphosphate (Tc99m-MDP) bone scintigraphy. The patient had no prior history of pulmonary disease with no apparent respiratory symptoms at time of imaging, but did endorse a long history of lupus nephritis and end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis. Review of prior chest CT and chest X-ray imaging over the last 5 years revealed diffuse ground-glass opacities and extensive parenchymal calcifications, consistent with metastatic pulmonary calcification. These radiological findings were further corroborated by laboratory studies, which demonstrated longstanding secondary hyperparathyroidism with a most recent work-up including an iPTH level of 1251 pg/mL. The differential diagnosis of bilateral, diffuse Tc99m-MDP uptake on bone scintigraphy includes tracer contamination, pulmonary etiologies such as pleural effusion or mesothelioma, metabolic diseases such as metastatic pulmonary calcification, and genetic diseases including pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis. In the setting of longstanding renal dysfunction and chronic hypercalcemia as in this patient, such radiological findings are a classic presentation of metastatic pulmonary calcification.

Case report

A 65-year-old male underwent whole-body bone scintigraphy with Tc-99m methylene diphosphate (Tc99m-MDP) for evaluation of hip and lower back pain. Past medical history included end-stage renal disease (ESRD), vertebral compression fractures, hepatitis C virus, gout, aortic stenosis s/p transcatheter aortic valve replacement, hypertension, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Three hours after intravenous administration of 28.6 mCi of Tc99m-MDP, whole body images were obtained, and demonstrated high soft tissue uptake and no kidney uptake, consistent with known renal failure. Increased uptake in the thoracolumbar spine was consistent with degenerative changes, a typical finding for patient age. Notably, diffuse homogeneous tracer uptake was observed in both lungs (Fig. 1A), with an uptake pattern resemblant to Tc-99m labeled macroaggregated albumin (Tc99m-MAA) lung perfusion activity. As a result, the possibility of Tc99m-MAA contamination was considered. Careful review of patient history confirmed that the patient did not undergo any lung perfusion study prior to the bone scan, and radiopharmacy consultation confirmed the correct administration of Tc99m-MDP tracer. The patient's prior imaging records were reviewed. His previous chest X-ray (Fig. 1B, obtained 11 months prior to Fig. 1A) and chest CT (Fig. 1C, obtained 2 months prior to Fig. 1A) demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities and extensive parenchymal calcifications consistent with metastatic pulmonary calcifications (MPCs), which were present on multiple chest CT for at least 5 years. The findings on prior chest X-ray and chest CT explained diffuse lung MDP uptake in this bone scan. In addition, this patient has a long history of kidney disease, including ESRD and lupus nephropathy, complicated by secondary hyperparathyroidism, renal osteodystrophy, and hemodialysis for the past 12 years. Serum creatinine has ranged from 4.5 to 10 mg/dL over the past 10 years. Most recent laboratory work-up revealed an eGFR of 9 mL/min and intact parathyroid hormone level of 1251 pg/mL (normal: 10-55 pg/mL).

Discussion

Bone scintigraphy is most often indicated for the evaluation of metastatic bone disease. Other common indications for bone scan include primary bone tumors, fractures, osteomyelitis, Paget disease, complications from prosthetic hardware, etc. Tc99m-MDP is one the commonly used radiopharmaceuticals for bone scintigraphy. Soft tissue uptake on a bone scan is a common finding, often with important clinical significance. The underlying mechanism for soft tissue Tc99m-MDP uptake includes metastatic calcification, dystrophic calcification, and metabolic uptake, among others [1]. Increased soft tissue uptake (including the lungs) can be caused by localized inflammation or infection [2], sequela of ischemia/infarction or necrosis [3], and primary or metastatic malignancy [4,5]. Although Tc99m-MDP is an analogue of calcium and a bone-localizing radiopharmaceutical, increased soft tissue uptake may be noted in pathological conditions without tissue calcification or ectopic bone formation.

Diffuse elevated MDP uptake of the entire lung field may often be seen in patients with significant bilateral pleural effusions, either malignant or nonmalignant [6]. Alternatively, mesothelioma may demonstrate diffuse unilateral lung Tc99m-MDP uptake, and rarely bilateral lung fields. Otherwise, lung uptake on bone scan secondary to pneumonia, malignancy, or other infection tends to appear localized and heterogenous. In this case, diffuse and homogeneous Tc99m-MDP uptake in the entire lung field is due to diffuse calcification in the lung tissues, a typical finding for MPC.

MPC is a metabolic disease characterized by calcium deposition in normal lung parenchyma, mediated by excess serum calcium, an elevated calcium-phosphate product, and an alkaline environment [7]. The nomenclature of "metastatic" does not signal a malignant process; rather, it refers to the deposition of excess calcium in the lungs, generally secondary to nonmalignant pathological processes. MPC occurs most commonly in patients with ESRD, particularly those on hemodialysis [8] and those with secondary hyperparathyroidism or hypercalcemia [9]. Although not a common clinical manifestation, previous reports have estimated that up to 79% of patients with ESRD may have evidence of MPC upon autopsy [10]. Clinical symptoms of MPC depend on the severity of underlying disease but most patients are asymptomatic as lung function is generally not affected. The pathology of MPC may be reversible upon correction of the underlying causes, such as following renal transplantation in chronic renal failure patients, or after parathyroidectomy in patients with hyperparathyroidism. Pulmonary calcium deposition may be seen on chest X-ray or CT as diffuse or localized calcified nodules, diffuse or patchy ground-glass opacity, or confluent dense parenchymal consolidation [11]. On bone scintigraphy, the pulmonary calcification demonstrates as increased lung MDP uptake, in either a diffuse [8] or heterogenous [12] pattern. When secondary to malignancy, MPC typically arises from hypercalcemia-causing etiologies, such as multiple myeloma, parathyroid carcinoma, and osteolysis from metastases.

Diffuse lung calcification may rarely occur secondary to pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis (PAM) and may present with elevated pulmonary Tc99m-MDP uptake on bone scan similar to MPC [13,14]. A rare, autosomal recessive disorder, PAM causes widespread deposition of calcium phosphate microliths throughout the lungs without disruption of calcium metabolism. PAM is characterized by a defect in SLC34A2, a type IIb sodium-dependent phosphate cotransporter found in alveolar epithelial cells. Dysfunction of this transporter results in the failure to export inorganic phosphate from the alveolar lining fluid, leading to calcium accumulation and hydroxyapatite microlith formation [15,16]). On chest radiograph, PAM may exhibit diffuse or scattered micronodules, hyperdense infiltrates with a predilection for the lung bases, ground-glass attenuation, linear subpleural calcifications or subpleural nodules, small parenchymal nodules, dense consolidations, and a mosaic pattern of attenuation. Although often initially diagnosed as an incidental finding in asymptomatic patients, the disease may progress to pulmonary fibrosis and respiratory failure.

In summary, various pathological conditions may contribute to Tc99m-MDP uptake in the lungs and present a challenge to diagnosis. The presence of lung uptake on bone scan is not specific, and correlation with patient history and other imaging modality (most often chest CT) is necessary to establish the diagnosis. If imaging correlation is available, pulmonary Tc99m-MDP uptake on bone scan should include differentials spanning infectious, metabolic, and malignant etiologies. Finally, technical issues involving tracer administration should be ruled out, as a Tc99m-MAA lung perfusion study, if conducted immediately prior to the bone scan, may appear with bilateral lung uptake.
