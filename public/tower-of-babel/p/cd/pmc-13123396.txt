Physical Exam for Athletic Shoulder Injuries in Adolescent Patients
Authors: John E. Arvesen, Michael Kutschke, Katherine Lampe, Philip Wilson, Henry B. Ellis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In addition to a thorough history, a systematic approach to the shoulder exam is critical to understanding dysfunction in the shoulder in an adolescent athlete. Similar to other orthopaedic joint exams, a progression through inspection, palpation, strength, range of motion, and special testing can make a complex exam more efficient and reproducible. Specific to adolescents, shoulder complaints often result from physeal stress injuries, apophysitis, or hyperlaxity-related instability, conditions that are absent in skeletally mature patients. It is essential to recognize specific characteristics of the adolescent shoulder to distinguish them from pathologies commonly seen in adults. Familiarity with these diagnoses and appropriate tests can help the practitioner determine a reliable diagnosis and an optimal treatment plan.Key Concepts(1)History and mechanism of injury will direct a more focused exam.(2)In adolescents, injuries involving the physis or apophysis are common, so clinical signs like physeal tenderness or pain caused by rotational movement should take priority over diagnoses usually found in adults.(3)The apprehension and relocation tests serve as the principal assessments for anterior traumatic unidirectional shoulder instability, whereas the load and shift test is particularly valuable in the evaluation of patients presenting with multidirectional instability.(4)Examination of throwing-shoulder pathology should not be limited to the shoulder alone as etiologies may stem from poor throwing mechanics, imbalances in the kinetic chain, or underlying scapular dysfunction.(5)Proper understanding and evaluation of scapular dysfunction are critical in a growing athlete to prevent recurrence of shoulder pain.

Introduction

The physical exam of the adolescent shoulder often presents a clinical challenge due to the complex and evolving anatomy of the growing musculoskeletal system. Many conditions can cause pain, instability, and limited shoulder function in this group. After a thorough history is obtained, a stepwise, organized approach to the shoulder examination can help guide further workup, elucidate the underlying pathology, and lead to appropriate diagnosis and management. A typical musculoskeletal examination involves visual inspection, palpation, assessment of range of motion, evaluation of strength, and the use of special tests to assist in diagnosing specific pathologies. In shoulder examinations, these components are utilized to assess the shoulder, although the sequence may vary. The most common presentations of shoulder conditions in adolescents are instability, overuse injuries, and trauma. Additional resources are available in this Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America Sports Quality, Safety, and Value Initiative (POSNA-SPORTS QSVI) video on the Evaluation of the Adolescent Shoulder.

As with the evaluation of any pediatric or adolescent patient, building trust is a key component of performing a thorough assessment and allows the patient to provide a complete and reliable physical exam [1]. When evaluating the shoulder, it is important to not only examine the glenohumeral joint, but also the scapulothoracic joint, and cervical spine. Oftentimes patients may present with a chief complaint of “shoulder pain” but the primary etiology of their pain is the cervical spine or scapular dysfunction [2,3]. The physical exam of the adolescent shoulder presents distinct challenges compared to that of adults due to open physes, evolving skeletal maturity, increased joint laxity, and sport-specific overuse patterns. While many exam maneuvers overlap with adult evaluations, the differential diagnosis and interpretation of findings are heavily influenced by age-related factors. Physeal and apophyseal injuries are more prevalent than rotator cuff tears, multidirectional instability is frequent due to hyperlaxity, and repeated microtrauma commonly presents as Little Leaguer's shoulder (LLS) or coracoid apophysitis rather than degenerative conditions.

General physical exam

Visual inspection

To assess the shoulder, ensure the entire shoulder, scapula, and cervical spine are visible while maintaining modesty, especially in adolescent females. The optimal exam is performed with the patient standing for both anterior and posterior inspection. Look for swelling, deformity, or atrophy; these may indicate issues like acromioclavicular (AC) or SC joint instability, neuropathy, scapular winging, shoulder instability, or scoliosis. Begin with an anterior inspection of the sternoclavicular (SC) joint, AC joint, and clavicle for asymmetry or soft tissue changes, then inspect posteriorly to compare scapulae positions, which may reveal conditions such as static winging from neurologic causes or Sprengel's deformity. While evaluating the posterior aspect of the shoulder, an assessment of the spine should also be performed to evaluate for underlying scoliosis. Muscle atrophy in the supraspinatus or infraspinatus may point to suprascapular nerve compression from labral tears associated with paralabral cysts.

Palpation

Palpation proceeds in a comparable manner, starting from the SC joint and proceeding laterally over the clavicle to the AC joint and continuing to the anterior and lateral aspects of the proximal humerus and associated structures. In addition to evaluating bony and soft tissue structures, the examiner should consider sources of pain specific to adolescents such as those arising from physeal injuries or apophysitis in the shoulder. Common sources of pain in the anterior aspect of the shoulder can be over the biceps, associated with biceps tendonitis or even an unstable biceps tendon. Tenderness over the coracoid can be associated with coracoid apophysitis, scapular dyskinesis, or SICK (Scapular malposition, Inferior medial border prominence, Coracoid pain and malposition, and dysKinesis of scapular movement) scapula syndrome [4]. In contrast to adults, where lateral shoulder pain often suggests rotator cuff tendinopathy, point tenderness may be more consistent with LLS involving the proximal humerus physis, particularly in overhead athletes ages 11-16 [5]. Posterior joint line tenderness may be suggestive of posterior labral tears and some superior labral tears. Acromion or AC joint tenderness can be associated with AC joint instability, distal clavicle osteolysis, or symptomatic os acromiale. In addition to the bony landmarks around the shoulder, palpation along the trapezius muscles and/or rhomboids may elicit pain and is commonly seen with scapular dyskinesis.

Range of motion

As a “ball in socket” joint with relatively low bony constraint, the shoulder joint affords the most range of motion of all joints in the human body. The glenohumeral and scapulothoracic joints allow the shoulder to move in all anatomic planes, and isolating the motion of each joint can aid the examiner in identifying the pathologic process. Shoulder motion has contributions from both the glenohumeral joint and the scapula. The initial 30° of abduction of the shoulder comes from the glenohumeral joint, and then the scapula starts to contribute to the total arc of motion. Typically, for every 2° of glenohumeral abduction, the scapula contributes 1° of abduction [6]. The glenohumeral joint contributes about 90°-120° of abduction, while the scapula contributes about 60°, with total abduction in the range of 150°-180° [7].

With the patient in a standing position, active motion is assessed, focusing specifically on forward flexion, abduction, external rotation (ER) with the shoulder in 0° abduction. Internal rotation (IR) can be measured by instructing the patient to place the dorsal surface of their hand on their back, reaching as cranially as possible and noting the position of the thumb relative to the nearest vertebral level (Fig. 1).Figure 1Measurement of internal rotation.Figure 1

Passive forward flexion and abduction can be assessed similarly with the patient in a supine position with the scapula stabilized. Passive shoulder flexion and abduction are initially assessed, followed by glenohumeral rotation with the arm in 90° of abduction. One hand is used to palpate the coracoid, while the other hand externally rotates the shoulder. Glenohumeral ER and IR are measured when the examiner begins to feel the coracoid move in the direction of shoulder rotation, indicating scapular engagement. When assessing motion, it is essential to understand normal vs pathologic rotation, especially in the overhead athlete evaluation. In these athletes, it is common for the dominant arm to have more external range of motion than the nondominant arm; however, assessing the total arc of motion (ER + IR = Total Arc of Motion) can determine if you have anatomic or pathologic glenohumeral internal rotation deficit or GIRD. Anatomic GIRD is defined as symmetric total arc of motion with IR less of less than 18°-20°, while pathologic GIRD is defined as a loss of IR of more than 18°-20° and a loss of total arc of motion in the dominant arm of more than 5° [8,9]. The normal total arc of motion in the overhead athlete is typically between 160° and 210° [10].

Strength

Strength testing is used to evaluate the rotator cuff and deltoid. Rotator cuff tears are rare in the adolescent shoulder; nevertheless, it is essential to understand and perform strength testing for each muscle to ensure a comprehensive evaluation [11]. Supraspinatus strength can be isolated by positioning the shoulder in the scapular plane and internally rotating the shoulder in the standing position. This exam maneuver is called the “empty can test” and the examiner provides resistance to forward elevation. Pain with resistance may indicate rotator cuff inflammation or bursitis, whereas objective weakness is more frequently associated with a rotator cuff tear. Notably, rotator cuff inflammation and bursitis are observed more often than actual tears. Infraspinatus testing can be performed with resisted ER with the shoulder in 0 degrees of abduction. Less commonly injured, the teres minor strength can be evaluated by performing the Hornblower's test. This is done by bringing the shoulder into 90 degrees of abduction and providing resistance to ER with the arm in this position. Subscapularis strength testing is done via three main tests: the bear hug test, the lift-off test, and the belly press test [[12], [13], [14]] (Figure 3, Figure 4).Figure 3Lift off test.Figure 3Figure 4The belly press.Figure 4

Each of these tests can be helpful in evaluating specific aspects of the subscapularis. Patients who have a lesser tuberosity avulsion fracture will commonly present with positive physical exam findings that isolate the subscapularis. The lift-off test is most specific for full-thickness tears, but if the patient cannot reach that position, the belly press or lift off test can also be used [13]. The belly press test and lift-off tests are more sensitive for partial-thickness tears or upper-border subscapularis tears [14]. Special tests

Instability

After completing the inspection, palpation, motion, and strength testing portions of the exam, attention can shift to special tests designed to confirm suspected diagnoses and identify specific structural injuries. Each of these tests can be used once the differential diagnosis has narrowed to a specific pathology. Instability in the adolescent shoulder is one of the most common chief complaints that will be encountered [15]. The unstable shoulder can be broken down into two major categories: traumatic unidirectional shoulder instability or atraumatic multidirectional shoulder instability (MDI) [16].

Traumatic unidirectional instability

For traumatic anterior unidirectional injuries, the apprehension test is the initial physical exam maneuver to perform. This is done by laying the patient supine on the physical exam table and bringing the arm into 90 degrees of abduction and the arm in ER [17]. A positive physical exam finding occurs if the patient reports “apprehension” or fear with the shoulder in this position [18]. Subsequently, a relocation test can be performed by placing a posteriorly directed force on the shoulder, and the apprehension resolves with this maneuver. The surprise test or release test is performed by removing the posteriorly directed force with a positive test occurring when the patient feels instability, pain, or apprehension. Lo et al. [19] evaluated the sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value for these tests and found that the surprise test was the most accurate test, and the positive predictive value of all three tests combined was 100% for anterior instability.

The load and shift test is also used to determine how unstable the shoulder is in both the anterior and posterior direction. The humeral head is first axially loaded into the glenoid, followed by the application of an anteriorly directed force. The scapula must be stabilized during this examination as additional scapular motion can make it difficult to accurately assess translation. (Fig. 5).Figure 5Load and shift exam.Figure 5

Load and shift exam.

This test can be performed in a supine position or with the patient sitting on the exam table. While the patient is supine, the patient's arm is brought into abduction and ER; an axial load is placed through the humerus onto the glenoid, and the shoulder is then shifted anteriorly.

Patients with posterior traumatic unidirectional shoulder instability usually report pain, while those with anterior instability mainly complain of instability [9]. The key to examining posterior instability is to reproduce a posterior directed force on the humeral head onto the glenoid to reproduce symptoms or instability. The Jerk, Kim, and posterior load-and-shift tests are used to demonstrate this specifically [20]. The Jerk test is performed by placing the arm into 90 degrees of abduction and IR while applying an axial load. A positive test occurs when the arm has a sudden clunk, which can also reproduce pain. Often, patients with a posterior labral tear may have pain but not a “clunk” on exam. The Kim test, a variation of the jerk test for assessing posteroinferior lesions, involves adduction with IR of the arm, elevating it 45°, and applying a posteroinferiorly directed force (Fig. 6).Figure 6The Kim test.Figure 6

The Kim test.

A positive test is noted when pain is reproduced along the posterior joint line or a clunk is observed. The jerk test demonstrates a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 94%, while the Kim test shows a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 98%. The combined sensitivity for detecting posteroinferior labral tears is 97% [10]. Both of these tests are useful in the evaluation of posterior shoulder instability and are commonly used together to better understand the location of labral pathology. The posterior load-and-shift exam is conducted in the same manner as the anterior load-and-shift exam except using a posteriorly directed force and allows for side-to-side comparison. In the context of high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle collisions or seizure disorders, patients with a posterior dislocation frequently present with an internally rotated arm accompanied by marked restriction of ER.

Atraumatic multidirectional instability

When assessing patients with MDI, clinicians may employ the load-and-shift test as well as evaluate for the presence of a sulcus sign. The MDI is defined as increased translation of the humeral head in relation to the glenoid in more than one direction and is far more prevalent in adolescents than adults due to inherent hyperlaxity and participation in flexible sports (eg gymnastics, swimming). In patients with MDI, the load-and-shift test can be performed with the patient sitting and the examiner stabilizing the shoulder blade and providing a shift both anteriorly and posteriorly. While sitting, one hand stabilizes the scapula, while the other hand holds the proximal humerus and keeps the arm in neutral rotation. With this maneuver, an anterior directed force is placed on the humerus and the examiner uses the same grading scale previously described (Fig. 7).Figure 7Sitting load and shift.Figure 7

Sitting load and shift.

The sulcus sign can also be evaluated in this position. This is graded by inferior translation of the humeral head in relation to the acromion, measured in centimeters of translation 1+ (1 cm), 2+ (2 cm), and 3+ is (3 cm) [21]. It is important to also perform the sulcus test with the arm in neutral and 30 degrees of ER as placing the arm in 30° ER can tighten the rotator interval.

Classically, the Beighton score has been used as a surrogate for overall hypermobility in other joints. In the shoulder, the Beighton score has not been shown to be as helpful in diagnosing hypermobility when compared to hyperabduction (Gagey sign) or increased ER with the shoulder in 0 degrees of abduction [22,23]. The Gagey sign is performed by stabilizing the scapula and bringing the arm into abduction in the scapular plane. Abduction of greater than 105° is associated with hypermobility and inferior capsular laxity [24]. Increased ER past 85° with the shoulder in 0 degrees of abduction has also been identified as a more helpful surrogate for hypermobility in the shoulder than the Beighton score [25]. These two physical exam findings are commonly seen in patients with MDI. Throwers shoulder

The LLS, or proximal humerus epiphysiolysis, commonly presents with pain and decreased throwing velocity. The LLS is an overuse injury unique to adolescents, marked by pain with resisted rotation at the side and lateral physeal tenderness, distinguishing it from adult SLAP tears or impingement. Pain can be reproduced with resisted internal or ER with the arm at the side, which places torque across the proximal growth plate.

During development, the humerus begins with increased retrotorsion and becomes less with age. In adolescent throwers, repetitive stress is thought to halt the natural loss of retroversion that occurs with age [26]. Glenohumeral IR deficit, or GIRD, is frequently observed in overhead athletes due to repetitive use. While GIRD can occur in adult throwers due to posterior capsular contracture, in adolescents it frequently results from a combination of physiologic humeral retrotorsion and physeal stress, requiring side-to-side comparison and correlation with throwing history rather than assuming soft-tissue restriction alone. These bony adaptations that occur can then result in dominant vs nondominant arm differences in internal and ER. Pathologic GIRD is defined as a loss of 20 degrees in the total arc of rotational motion compared to the contralateral extremity, while isolated loss of IR may not produce functional limitations [9].

Understanding the etiology of LLS is critical for guiding treatment. Most athletes will have a combination of factors that lead to increased stress on the proximal humerus that will include range of motion deficits, scapular dyskinesia, core weakness, and reduced lower extremity mobility and/or strength. Alongside a comprehensive assessment of the kinetic chain, identifying targeted deficits within the shoulder is essential for developing individualized rehabilitation plans for each patient [26]. If the loss of IR is due to posterior capsular tightness or soft-tissue restriction, rehabilitation focuses on restoring mobility through targeted stretching, soft tissue work, and gradual return of full motion. Conversely, if IR loss results from increased humeral retrotorsion, the total rotational arc is often preserved, and stretching will not change bony limitations. Management in these cases emphasizes optimizing throwing mechanics, improving scapular and rotator cuff control, and carefully monitoring workload. In some throwers, excessive ER may develop, increasing torque across the proximal humeral physis and medial elbow; therapy in these athletes focuses on limiting over-rotation during the throwing motion, reinforcing shoulder stability, and balancing strength to protect the joint [19]. A significant proportion of these injuries are associated with athletes engaging in velocity-enhancement programs such as specialized throwing or weighted ball regimens, which frequently contribute to the occurrence of these conditions. By differentiating between soft-tissue and bony adaptations, clinicians can tailor rehabilitation strategies, prevent further injury, and maximize functional performance in adolescent throwers [27].

The overhead throwing motion is one of the most complex athletic movements, and assessing the full kinetic chain is essential as deficits in core or lower-extremity strength can force compensatory stress on the shoulder. The single-leg squat can evaluate core and hip strength, lower extremity mobility, and balance [28]. Addressing these deficits stabilizes the foundation, optimizes throwing mechanics, and helps reduce overuse injuries. In older overhead athletes, shoulder pain may result from rotator cuff pathology, superior labral tears, or scapular dyskinesis. Overuse, poor mechanics, and kinetic chain dysfunction increase injury risk. Scapular IR, coracoid pain, and kinesis or SICK scapula syndrome, described by Burkhart et al. [18], involves coracoid pain from pectoralis minor tightness, overactive upper trapezius, and scapular dyskinesis, and can contribute to rotator cuff impingement. Failure to address scapular dysfunction may lead to recurrent pain. Combining kinetic-chain assessment with evaluation of scapular and shoulder mechanics allows clinicians to identify underlying dysfunction and provide targeted rehabilitation to optimize performance and reduce injury risk.

Superior labral tears and proximal biceps pathology

Superior labral tears are commonly seen in throwers and can sometimes be difficult to differentiate between a pathologic tear and a developmental adaptation in a thrower. In a study that evaluated players at the 2023 Major League Baseball (MLB) combine, all players underwent routine shoulder magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and a superior labrum anterior-to-posterior tear or “SLAP tear” was present in 84/112 (75%) of asymptomatic pitchers and 26/107 (24%) of position players [29]. History and physical exam can help to tease through this difficult presentation [30,31]. Physical examination maneuvers aimed at biceps pathology include the O'Brien’s test, modified dynamic labral shear test, crank test, and Yergason’s test/Speed’s tests [32]. O′ Brien's test is performed by placing the shoulder into forward flexion, adduction, and IR. The examiner places resistance against the forward flexion of the shoulder. A positive test occurs when the patient reports pain and the pain resolves when the arm is placed in ER. The modified dynamic labral shear test places the arm in a throwing position with one hand holding the patient's wrist and the other stabilizing the scapula while also pressing against the posterior joint line of the shoulder. The arm is then taken through abduction, and a positive test is a click with reproducible pain. The Speed's test is conducted by positioning the patient's arm in 90 degrees of forward flexion and supinating the forearm. Resistance is applied as the patient attempts to further elevate the arm, and the test is considered positive if pain is produced. The Yergason's test involves placing the patient in a standing position with the elbow flexed to 90° and the forearm pronated. The examiner asks the patient to actively supinate the forearm against resistance, with elicited pain in the biceps groove indicating a positive result. Scapula exam

The scapula exam can at times be the most confusing and difficult to perform because of the complexity of the joint and the little formal training received on the scapula during training. The scapula moves in six planes: protraction, retraction, elevation, depression, upward rotation, and downward rotation. Scapular dyskinesis, which describes a broad pattern of abnormal scapular motion, is common in the overhead athlete, while scapular winging is usually associated with a specific protrusion of the medial or lateral border of the scapula. There is still much to learn about the scapula’s contribution to shoulder pathology, but a basic physical exam can help identify the main pathology.

Scapular dyskinesis

This is commonly seen in the overhead athlete population as well as patients with MDI. Initial evaluation is performed by inspecting the scapula symmetry at rest as well as with forward elevation. Scapular dyskinesis can be classified through type I-IV and assesses for inferior angle prominence, medial border prominence, superior border elevation, or normal motion [33,34]. To accentuate the physical exam, light resistance placed against the arms during forward flexion can aid in identifying dyskinesis. The scapular assistance test assesses whether dyskinesia causes shoulder pain (Fig. 8).Figure 8Scapular assistance test.Figure 8

Scapular assistance test.

During the test, the evaluator helps rotate and tilt the scapula, giving the rotator cuff more space under the acromion. A positive result is indicated by reduced pain and improved forward flexion with assistance.

Scapular winging

Not to be confused with scapular dyskinesis, scapular winging is defined as a pathologic prominence of the medial or lateral border of the scapula associated with dysfunction of the underlying muscles. Medial winging demonstrates an elevated medial border of the scapula and is seen with injury to the innervation of the serratus anterior via the long thoracic nerve. Injury to this nerve can be caused by direct trauma, overuse/stretch injury, or Parsonage–Turner syndrome. Lateral scapular winging presents as an elevated lateral border of the scapula and is typically caused by an injury to the spinal accessory nerve, which can be iatrogenic in the setting of a procedure that involves dissection in the neck.
