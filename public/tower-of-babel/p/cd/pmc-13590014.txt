Photothermal-driven sequential immunotherapy overcomes resistance to PARP inhibition in Brca1-deficient triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Zhenyu Xu, Shengyi Wang, Yiyang Cao, Jianing Song, Yinghuan Liu, Rong Zhang, Xulin Tang, Ya Wang, Haodi Wu, Jiasheng Tu, Qian Chen, Chunmeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) faces formidable clinical challenges due to target scarcity and a profound immunosuppressive microenvironment, necessitating innovative therapeutic strategies. Although poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors leverage synthetic lethality in BRCA-mutated tumors, their efficacy is subverted by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)-driven immunosuppression, and current combinatorial strategies fail to overcome these resistance barriers. To address this, we engineered a thermo-responsive double-layer gel for spatiotemporally coordinated delivery: mild photothermal therapy reprograms TAMs to dismantle immunosuppressive niches, poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors induce immunogenic tumor death releasing damage-associated molecular patterns, and delayed anti-CD47 antibodies release blocks the CD47-signal regulatory protein α axis. In Brca1-deficient TNBC models, this strategy demonstrated: (1) mild photothermal therapy-activated stimulator of interferon genes pathway triggering TAMs repolarization, (2) tumor-derived double-stranded DNA reinforcing the anti-tumor phenotypes, and (3) anti-CD47 antibody-enhanced phagocytosis and T cell activation via antigen cross-presentation. Consequently, this strategy controlled orthotopic/distal tumors and lung metastases by remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Our work not only unveils a novel mechanism of physical energy-mediated immune modulation but also establishes a paradigm shift for TNBC therapy, from empirical drug combinations to rationally designed spatiotemporal cascades.Key words: Drug delivery, Sequential release, Cancer immunotherapy, Triple-negative breast cancer, Drug resistance, Photothermal therapy, Tumor-associated macrophages, STING pathway

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the most aggressive breast cancer subtype, presents a serious clinical challenge due to the lack of druggable molecular targets, with 5-year survival rates falling below 15% in advanced stages1,2. While approximately 15%–20% of TNBC patients harbor BRCA1/2 mutations, theoretically sensitizing tumors to poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors (PARPi), the profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by deficient T cell infiltration and myeloid cell dominance, critically compromises PARPi efficacy3, 4, 5. This paradox highlights a fundamental therapeutic gap: synthetic lethality strategies targeting DNA repair defects fail to overcome TME-imposed immunosuppressive barriers. Furthermore, despite exhibiting immunologically favorable features—such as elevated tumor mutational burden, programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes6, 7, 8—TNBC consistently shows dismal objective response rates to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) monotherapy across disease stages, underscoring the limitations of adaptive immune activation alone9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14. Consequently, combining PARPi with ICB has emerged as a key strategy. However, pivotal clinical trials report objective response rates below 30%, with frequent acquired resistance15, 16, 17, 18. Mechanistic studies identify tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), constituting over 50% of the TME immune infiltrate, as central culprits. TAMs not only establish immunosuppressive niches via cytokine secretion but also directly mediate PARPi resistance7,19, positioning them as critical therapeutic obstacles demanding urgent intervention.

Therefore, addressing TAM-mediated immunosuppression is essential. Macrophages possess potent anti-tumor capabilities contingent on M1 polarization, enabling direct tumor phagocytosis and T cell activation20, 21, 22, 23. However, the TME in TNBC is overwhelmingly dominated by pro-tumorigenic M2-like TAMs24,25. Previous strategies have primarily focused on depleting TAMs26, 27, 28, 29. While depletion approaches have been explored, the sheer abundance of these cells makes complete elimination challenging and potentially counterproductive. More promisingly, reprogramming TAMs towards an anti-tumor M1 phenotype represents a compelling “convert enemies into allies” strategy. Our prior work demonstrated that mild photothermal therapy (MPTT) can effectively reprogram the M2-like phenotype30. Nevertheless, a critical limitation emerged: tumor cells counteract reprogramming efforts by overexpressing CD47, which engages signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on macrophages to deliver a potent “don't eat me” signal31,32. This finding reveals that phenotype reprogramming alone is insufficient, and simultaneous blockade of the CD47–SIRPα axis is essential to fully unleash macrophage phagocytic function33,34. Importantly, CD47 blockade extends beyond enhancing phagocytosis, as preclinical evidence shows it also optimizes antigen cross-presentation to activate T cell immunity, creating a bridge between innate and adaptive responses35,36.

Building on this mechanistic understanding, we propose a sequential “barrier-breaking to efficacy-amplifying” strategy designed to overcome the limitations of prior approaches. In brief, first, MPTT reprograms TAMs to disrupt the immunosuppressive barrier. Concurrently, Olaparib (OL), as a PARPi induces synthetic lethality in tumor cells while releasing immunogenic damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Finally, delayed blockade of the CD47–SIRPα axis is applied to activate cascading innate and adaptive immune responses. To achieve precise temporal coordination of these distinct therapeutic actions, we developed a double-layer gel (DLG) carrier system based on FDA-approved materials (egg phosphatidylcholine (EPC)/glycerol dioleate (GDO)) (Fig. 1A and B). The modular DLG design ensures early synergy between MPTT and PARPi while enabling programmed, delayed release of (aCD47). In Brca1-deficient TNBC models, this integrated approach demonstrated triple synergistic effects: (1) MPTT activated the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway to initiate TAM reprogramming, (2) double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) released from PARPi/MPTT-killed tumor cells reinforced and sustained M1 polarization and (3) delayed aCD47 release alleviated immunosuppression dually—by blocking CD47–SIRPα interactions to enhance phagocytosis and promoting antigen cross-presentation to activate T cell immunity (Fig. 1C). This spatiotemporally orchestrated cascade induced robust systemic anti-tumor immunity against orthotopic tumors, distal lesions, and lung metastases, while establishing long-term immunological memory, thereby providing a paradigm to overcome TNBC therapeutic challenges.

(A) Schematic illustration of the preparation process for outer and inner gel precursor. (B) Schematic illustration of the stepwise preparation process for DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47). Cells and animals

The murine breast cancer cell line (4T1), macrophage cell line (RAW264.7), human breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-436), and human macrophage cell line (THP-1) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The 4T1-OVA cell line was acquired from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). MDA-MB-436 cells were cultured in Leibovitz's L-15 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 10 μg/mL insulin, and 16 μg/mL glutathione. 4T1, 4T1-OVA, RAW264.7, THP-1, and MDA-MB-231 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% glutamine. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2, except for MDA-MB-436, which was cultured under CO2-free conditions.

Female BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from JunKe Biological Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in a controlled environment (20–22 °C, 30%–70% humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (No. 2024-07-079).

For bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), bone marrow mononuclear cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of euthanized female C57BL/6 mice and differentiated into M0-like BMDMs by culturing for 7 days in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's complete medium containing recombinant murine M-CSF (20 ng/mL, PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). M1-like BMDMs were induced by treating M0-polarized cells with LPS (200 ng/mL, PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 24 h.

Construction and validation of Brca1 knockdown 4T1 cell line

Four Virus-Free™ vectors (pPB[shRNA]-EGFP:T2A:Puro-U6) containing Brca1-targeting shRNAs were designed based on the nucleotide sequence of murine Brca1 (GenBank accession No. NM_009764.2). The specific shRNA sequences included: shRNA#1: 5′-CCAAGAAGAGGATAGTATAAT-3′; shRNA#2: 5′-CCACAGGTAAATCAGGAATTT-3′; shRNA#3: 5′-GTGCTTCCACACCCTACTTAC-3′; Scramble shRNA (negative control). Large-scale low-endotoxin plasmid DNA was extracted using an EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Electroporation was performed using the Neon™ Transfection System (MPK5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with optimized parameters (1200 V, 20 ms, 3 pulse). Transfected cells were cultured for 48 h and subsequently selected with 8 μg/mL puromycin (Psaitong, Beijing, China) for 7 days. Brca1 knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot using a rabbit anti-BRCA1 monoclonal antibody (1:1000 dilution, Cat# 22362-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The 4T1 cell line with the highest Brca1 knockdown efficiency (shRNA#1) was selected for subsequent experiments.

Preparation of OL@BP-NSs

Bulk black phosphorus was ground in a mortar with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a homogeneous suspension. The supernatant containing finely exfoliated BP flakes was transferred to amber vials and sonicated in an ice bath for 8 h using a probe ultrasonicator (400 W, 5 s on/5 s off pulse mode). The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at 2000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove unexfoliated aggregates. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the pelleted black phosphorus nanosheets (BP-NSs) were washed twice with ultrapure water and lyophilized for storage at −80 °C under light-protected conditions.

For drug loading, BP-NSs were mixed with OL under continuous dark agitation (100 rpm, 4 h). Unbound OL was removed by centrifugation (12,000×g, 30 min, 4 °C), and the OL-loaded BP-NSs (OL@BP-NSs) pellet was washed twice and resuspended in ultrapure water. Lyophilized OL@BP-NSs were stored at −80 °C. To quantify drug loading capacity, OL@BP-NSs prepared with varying OL/BP-NSs ratios were centrifuged, and unencapsulated OL in the supernatant was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Preparation of DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47)

The drug-loaded double-layer gel (DLG) precursor was fabricated using a modified “Macrosol” technique as previously reported. Briefly, the outer gel precursor LG34/66 (OL@BP-NSs) was prepared by homogenizing EPC/GDO (34:66, w/w), OL@BP-NSs, and anhydrous ethanol (10% v/v), followed by lyophilization and reconstitution in EPC/GDO (34:66, w/w). The inner gel precursor LG50/50 (aCD47) was similarly formulated by blending EPC/GDO (50:50, w/w), aCD47, and anhydrous ethanol (10% v/v), then lyophilized and redissolved in EPC/GDO (50:50, w/w). The DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) was formed by sequentially injecting LG34/66 (OL@BP-NSs) and LG50/50 (aCD47) through a dual-barrel syringe into an aqueous phase.

Characterization and stability assessment of OL@BP-NSs

The hydrodynamic diameter, size distribution, and polydispersity index of BP-NSs and OL@BP-NSs were characterized by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). To evaluate stability, OL@BP-NSs were stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 4 °C, and their hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index were monitored daily over a 7-day period using the same dynamic light scattering system. Measurements were performed in triplicate at 25 °C, and data were analyzed using Zetasizer Software (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK).

Rheological characterization

The viscoelastic properties of inner and outer lipid gel were analyzed using a DHR-2 dynamic shear rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) equipped with a 20 mm parallel plate geometry. Samples (200 mg) were loaded onto the middle of the plate, allowed to equilibrate at 25 °C for 10 min, and subjected to oscillatory temperature ramp tests from 25 to 100 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C/min. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were monitored under controlled strain (γ = 5%) and angular frequency (ω = 2 rad/s) to characterize the thermo-responsive sol-gel transition behavior.

Photothermal effect evaluation

The photothermal conversion efficiency of BP-NSs and OL@BP-NSs was assessed in vitro and in vivo using an 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser system.

In vitro concentration/power screening: Aqueous solutions of BP-NSs or OL@BP-NSs (0, 25, 50, 75, 100 μg/mL) were irradiated at 1.0 W/cm2, while 50 μg/mL solutions were exposed to varying laser power densities (0.5, 1.0, 1.5 W/cm2). Temperature changes were recorded at 30-s intervals over 10 min using a thermal imaging camera (E50, FLIR Systems).

Photothermal stability: 50 μg/mL samples underwent four consecutive heating/cooling cycles (5 min irradiation at 1.0 W/cm2 followed by 7 min natural cooling), with temperature changes monitored throughout.

In vivo photothermal imaging: 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice intratumorally injected with PBS, blank DLG, Free BP-NSs, or DLG (BP-NSs) were irradiated (1.0 W/cm2, 10 min), and tumor temperatures were captured via thermal imaging at 0, 2, 5, and 10 min.

In vitro drug release profiling

The release kinetics of OL and IgG from DLG (OL@BP-NSs + IgG) were evaluated under simulated physiological conditions (37 °C, 100 rpm). Each DLG sample (outer layer: 100 μL containing 100 μg OL; inner layer: 100 μL containing 100 μg IgG) was immersed in 3 mL PBS supplemented with 10% PEG2000 (w/v) to maintain sink conditions. For the NIR-triggered release group, samples were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 10 min at 6 h, and 2, 4, and 6 days post-incubation. At predetermined intervals (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 h; 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 days), 1 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. OL concentration was quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) using a C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) with mobile phase (acetonitrile:water = 30:70, v/v) at 276 nm. IgG release was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) against a bovine serum albumin standard curve.

In vivo drug release study

4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were intratumorally injected with either DLG (Ce6@BP-NSs + IgG), DLG (BP-NSs + AFIgG), or free Ce6/AFIgG (control). For NIR-triggered groups, tumors were irradiated with an 808 nm laser for 10 min per session on Days 0, 2, 4, and 6 post-injection. Fluorescence images were acquired on Days 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21 using an in vivo imaging system Lumina XR imaging system (Revvity, Waltham, MA, USA) under isoflurane anesthesia. Signal intensities were quantified by Living Image® Software (Revvity).

In vitro cytotoxicity evaluation

The cytotoxicity was assessed using CCK-8 assays (Cat# K1018, APExBIO Technology LLC, Houston, TX, USA) across multiple cell lines under standardized protocols. For temperature screening: 4T1Brca1−KD, RAW264.7, MDA-MB-436, and THP-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and incubated overnight. Plates were subjected to hyperthermia at 37, 39, 41, 43 and 45 °C (10 min), followed by 24 h incubation prior to CCK-8 viability assessment. For OL dose optimization assay, 4T1, 4T1Brca1−KD, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-231, RAW264.7, and THP-1 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were treated with OL (0, 0.25, 1, 2, 5 and 10 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cell viability was evaluated by CCK-8 assays. For cytotoxicity assay, 4T1Brca1−KD and MDA-MB-436 cells were exposed to PBS, Free OL (5 μg/mL), BP-NSs (50 μg/mL), OL@BP-NSs (5 μg/mL OL equivalent), Mild photothermal therapy (MPTT) (43 °C, 10 min), or OL@BP-NSs + MPTT for 24 h. Post-treatment cell viability was quantified by CCK-8 assays.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from tumor and TdLNs

Tumor tissues were minced into 1–2 mm3 fragments and enzymatically digested in RPMI-1640 medium containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and DNase I (0.2 mg/mL, Yeasen Biotechnology) at 37 °C for 45 min under orbital shaking (120 rpm). The digested tissue was mechanically dissociated using a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), and the resulting suspension was centrifuged at 350×g for 5 min. Pelleted cells were washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS to obtain single-cell suspensions.

For tumor-draining lymph nodes (TdLNs), tissues were gently homogenized through a 70 μm cell strainer, followed by centrifugation (500×g, 5 min), and pelleted cells were washed with PBS containing 2% FBS to obtain single-cell suspensions.

Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis

FCM analysis was performed using a CytoFlex S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). All antibodies and buffers were used with reference to the manufacturer's instructions. Data were analyzed using FlowJo (BD Biosciences) with gating strategies.

Co-culture experiments

Two sequential co-culture models were established using a 6.5 mm Transwell system (0.4 μm pore, Corning). Model 1: 4T1Brca1−KD cells (upper chamber) were pretreated with PBS, Free OL (5 μg/mL), OL@BP-NSs (5 μg/mL OL equivalent), MPTT (43 °C, 10 min), or OL@BP-NSs + MPTT for 48 h, followed by co-culture with M0-like BMDMs (lower chamber) for an additional 48 h. Model 2: 4T1Brca1−KD cells and M0-like BMDMs were co-cultured for 48 h before therapeutic interventions (same treatments as Model 1) were applied to the upper chamber.

To detect the phenotype of BMDMs, post-co-culture BMDMs were stained with anti-F4/80 (1:100, Cat# 565411, BD Biosciences), anti-CD11b (1:200, Cat# 101259, BioLegend), anti-CD86 (1:100, Cat# 558703, BD Biosciences), and anti-CD206 (1:100, Cat# 568273, BD Biosciences) for FCM analysis of M1/M2 polarization.

To assess the DNA damage of tumor cell, 4T1Brca1−KD cells were fixed and stained with anti-γ-H2AX (Ser139) (1:200, Cat# 613419, BioLegend) or a DNA damage detection kit (Cat# C2035S, Beyotime Biotechnology). Fluorescence intensity was quantified via confocal laser scanning microscopy (FV3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

To detect the activation of STING pathway, BMDMs from Model 2 were lysed for Western blot analysis of STING (1:1000, Cat# 19851-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-STING (Ser366) (1:1000, Cat# 72971T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and β-tubulin (1:5000, Cat# 66240-1-Ig, Proteintech). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

To investigate the mechanism through which MPTT activates the STING pathway, we quantified cytosolic mtDNA. Briefly, PBS-treated and MPTT-treated BMDMs from Model 2 were incubated with 1% NP-40 on ice for 10 min. The supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 15,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and then transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes for DNA extraction. The mtDNA levels were detected using qPCR assays. The following primers were used: D-loop forward: 5′-AATCTACCATCCTCCGTGAAACC-3′, D-loop reverse: 5′-TCAGTTTAGCTACCCCCAAGTTTAA-3′.

To analyze cytokine secretion, conditioned media from Model 2 BMDMs were analyzed for TNF-α (Cat# M3063), IFN-β (Cat# M0033), CXCL9 (Cat# M3077), and CXCL10 (Cat# M0021) via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) following manufacturer protocols.

Macrophage activation analysis

Quantitative assessment of aCD47-Mediated macrophage activation was evaluated by phagocytosis and antigen cross-presentation. To assess phagocytosis, CFSE-labeled 4T1Brca1−KD cells were pretreated with OL (1 μg/mL, 12 h) to induce dying cell status. Pretreated or untreated tumor cells were co-cultured with eFluor-670-labeled M1-like BMDMs (1:2 ratio) in Fc receptor-blocking buffer (anti-CD16/32, 1:100, Cat# 553141, BD Biosciences) and exposed to escalating aCD47 concentrations (0, 2, 5, 10 μg/mL) for 6 h. Phagocytosis was analyzed by FCM and confocal laser scanning microscopy.

For antigen cross-presentation evaluation, 4T1-OVA cells were pretreated with OL (20 μg/mL, 12 h). Pretreated or untreated tumor cells were co-cultured with M1-like BMDMs (1:2 ratio) under identical Fc-blocking conditions and exposed to escalating aCD47 concentrations (0, 2, 5, 10 μg/mL) for 6 h. Cells were stained with anti-F4/80 (1:100, Cat# 565411, BD Biosciences) and anti-H-2Kb-SIINFEKL (1:50, Cat# 12-5743-81, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The percentage of H-2Kb-SIINFEKL+ macrophages and mean fluorescence intensity were analyzed by FCM.

Orthotopic TNBC therapy and immune microenvironment analysis

Female BALB/c mice bearing orthotopic 4T1Brca1−KD tumors were randomized into six groups including: PBS, DLG, DLG (BP-NSs) + L, DLG (OL@BP-NSs) + L (OL: 500 μg/mouse), DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L (OL: 500 μg; aCD47: 50 μg/mouse), Free (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L. MPTT was administered on Days 0, 2, 4, and 6 post-treatment initiation. Tumor volumes and body weights were monitored for 60 days. On Day 17, five mice per group were sacrificed for analysis: tumors were excised, imaged, weighed, and processed for terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling assay (Cat# C1089, Beyotime Biotechnology). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were histologically examined for toxicity by hematoxylin-eosin staining. For immune profiling, single-cell suspensions from tumors and TdLNs were analyzed by FCM.

Immunofluorescence staining

On Day 10 post-treatment, primary tumors and TdLNs from three mice per group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into consecutive 5-μm slices using a Leica RM2125 RTS microtome (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0, 95 °C, 20 min), sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature. Tumor sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-iNOS (1:50, Cat# ab28365, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), rabbit anti-CD206 (1:1000 dilution, Cat# 24595T, Cell Signaling Technology), and rabbit anti-CD8α (1:1000 dilution, Cat# 98941T, Cell Signaling Technology). TdLN sections were stained with anti-CD8α under identical conditions. After washing, slides were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200 dilution, Cat# ab6721, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining (1 μg/mL, 5 min). Fluorescent images were acquired using fluorescence microscopy (Ts2R, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with consistent exposure settings across all groups.

Distal tumor growth inhibition

Orthotopic 4T1Brca1−KD tumors were established in the left fourth mammary fat pad of BALB/c mice, while 1 × 106 tumor cells were simultaneously inoculated into the contralateral fat pad for distal tumor model. Only the primary tumor received therapeutic interventions (PBS, DLG, DLG (BP-NSs) + L, DLG (OL@BP-NSs) + L, DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L, Free (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L), with distal tumors left untreated. Distal tumor volumes were measured for 21 days. On Day 17, serum was collected for cytokine quantification via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (TNF-α, IFN-β, CXCL9, and CXCL10). On Day 21, five mice per group were sacrificed for distal tumor excision and weight analysis.

Anti-metastasis study

To evaluate long-term immune memory, primary tumor-bearing mice were intravenously infusion with 1 × 105 4T1Brca1−KD cells on Day 30 post-treatment. Lungs were harvested on Day 51, fixed in Bouin's solution (Sigma–Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and metastatic nodules were quantified macroscopically. For histological validation, paraffin-embedded lung sections were H&E-stained and imaged using a microscopy (BX53, Olympus).

For immune memory profiling, TdLNs from five mice per group on Day 31 were processed into single-cell suspensions and CD8+/CD4+ T cell subsets were analyzed by FCM.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. For multi-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey's post hoc correction was applied. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyzes were conducted using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Preparation and characterization of DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47)

To achieve the dual therapeutic objectives of MPTT and enhanced tumor-selective delivery of PARPi, we engineered biocompatible black phosphorus nanosheets (BP-NSs) as a multifunctional drug carrier. BP-NSs were synthesized via liquid-phase exfoliation and subsequently loaded with OL to form OL@BP-NSs. Systematic optimization identified an optimal drug-to-carrier ratio of 2:1 (w/w), yielding an encapsulation efficiency of 91.02% and drug loading of 64.54% (Supporting Information Fig. S1). Dynamic light scattering analysis revealed that OL loading slightly increased the hydrodynamic diameter of BP-NSs from 269.57 to 282.59 nm, with polydispersity index rising from 0.184 to 0.218 (Supporting Information Fig. S2). Notably, these modifications did not compromise stability, as confirmed by sustained size and polydispersity index values over 7 days in physiological buffer (Supporting Information Fig. S3).

Building upon Macrosol technique37,38, we further constructed a spatially compartmentalized DLG system. OL@BP-NSs were incorporated into the outer gel precursor (LG34/66, EPC/GDO = 34:66 w/w), while aCD47 were encapsulated in the inner gel precursor (LG50/50, EPC/GDO = 50:50 w/w). Sequential injection through a dual-barrel syringe enabled precise spatial segregation, forming DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) at the target site. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, rapid phase transition occurred upon contact with aqueous media, generating a solid gel with distinct interfacial demarcation between layers. To optimize structural integrity, we implemented a gradient injection protocol comprising three continuous stages: rapid initial injection, slow mid-phase maintenance, and accelerated final advancement, which effectively minimized interlayer mixing and preserved outer-layer architecture.

To validate the thermo-responsive properties of DLG, rheological analyzes were performed to monitor the temperature-dependent evolution of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) for inner and outer gel precursors. As shown in Fig. 2B and C, the outer gel exhibited a sol-to-gel transition temperature at 41.92 °C (crossover point of G′ and G″), while the inner gel required a significantly higher transition temperature of 81.49 °C. This thermal gradient design ensures that the outer layer undergoes phase transition under MPTT conditions (<45 °C), enabling localized OL release, while the inner layer maintains structural integrity for delayed aCD47 delivery. The photothermal performance of BP-NSs and OL@BP-NSs was systematically evaluated under 808 nm laser irradiation (Supporting Information Fig. S4). At 50 μg/mL and 1 W/cm2, BP-NSs and OL@BP-NSs achieved temperature increments of 14.8 and 15.4 °C within 10 min, respectively. In addition, the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) values were calculated to be 26.23% for BP-NSs and 25.84% for OL@BP-NSs. Remarkably, both preparations retained stable heating capabilities across four on/off irradiation cycles, consistently exceeding the outer gel's phase transition threshold (>41.92 °C, Supporting Information Fig. S5), confirming their robust photothermal stability. In vivo photothermal efficacy was further assessed in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Infrared thermography revealed that intratumoral injection of Free BP-NSs or DLG (BP-NSs) enabled precise temperature control, maintaining tumor regions above the outer gel's transition temperature under dynamically adjusted laser power (Fig. 2D and E). In contrast, PBS and Blank DLG groups showed minimal temperature increases (2.1 and 3.0 °C, respectively), aligning with in vitro observations. These results demonstrate that the DLG system achieves spatiotemporally controlled MPTT, providing a critical foundation for its sequential drug release strategy.

Next, we utilized IgG as a surrogate for aCD47 in vitro to evaluate the controlled release properties of DLG. As shown in Fig. 2F, the outer gel exhibited sustained release of OL under non-irradiated conditions, achieving a cumulative release of 35% over 10 days. However, upon laser irradiation at 4, 48, 96, and 144 h, OL release accelerated significantly, culminating in a 55% cumulative release by Day 10. In stark contrast, IgG encapsulated in the inner gel maintained minimal release (<10% over 10 days), with no discernible impact from photothermal intervention on its release kinetics. To further characterize in vivo release dynamics, a fluorescence-tracing system was established by substituting OL with Chlorin e6 (Ce6) and aCD47 with Alexa Fluor® 790-IgG (AFIgG). Real-time monitoring via an in vivo imaging system revealed markedly prolonged Ce6 retention in the DLG-L Ce6@BP-NSs + IgG) group compared to free Ce6 controls. Notably, the DLG + L (Ce6@BP-NSs + IgG) exhibited a significant reduction in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) by Day 21 versus the DLG-L (Ce6@BP-NSs + IgG) group (Fig. 2G and H), confirming light-triggered outer-layer drug release. Concurrently, AFIgG in the inner gel demonstrated sustained release kinetics, retaining >30% fluorescence signal over 21 days regardless of irradiation, with no statistically significant difference in release rates between irradiated and non-irradiated groups (Fig. 2I and J). In contrast, free AFIgG showed near-complete signal dissipation by Day 14. These findings collectively validate that DLG enables spatiotemporally controlled sequential release.

OL@BP-NSs combined MPTT to inhibit Brca1-deficient tumor cell-driven polarization of BMDMs toward M2 phenotype

To investigate the response of Brca1-deficient TNBC cells to PARPi, we generated three 4T1 cell lines with stable knockdown of Brca1 (designated 4T1Brca1−KD) via shRNA technique and identified the most efficient clone through Western blot analysis. The KD-1 clone, showing the highest knockdown efficiency, was selected for subsequent experiments (Supporting Information Fig. S6 and S7). Given the temperature threshold of MPTT set at 45 °C, we assessed the viability of murine/human BRCA1-deficient breast cancer cells and macrophages across varying temperatures. As shown in Fig. 3A, 4T1Brca1−KD and MDA-MB-436 cells retained robust viability within 37–45 °C, while RAW264.7 and THP-1 macrophages exhibited reduced survival (<80%) above 43 °C, prompting selection of 43 °C as the MPTT treatment temperature to minimize macrophage damage. Dose–response experiments further revealed that BRCA1-deficient MDA-MB-436 and 4T1Brca1−KD cells showed heightened sensitivity to OL, with viability dropping below 80% at 1 μg/mL, whereas wild-type 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells maintained viability even at 5 μg/mL (Fig. 3B). To ensure macrophage safety, OL concentrations were capped at 5 μg/mL, which preserved >80% viability in RAW264.7 and THP-1 cells (Supporting Information Fig. S8).

Comparative cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 3C) demonstrated that BP-NSs alone or MPTT alone had minimal impact on cell viability, while OL@BP-NSs exhibited superior tumor-killing efficacy over free OL, likely due to enhanced cellular uptake mediated by the nanocarrier. The complete DLG leachate's cytotoxicity was not tested, as it would involve variable concentrations of active agents, which would complicate direct comparisons with the optimized in vitro doses. Instead, we focused on major components of the DLG (OL@BP-NSs + MPTT) to ensure dose consistency and relevance to the therapeutic regimen. Notably, all treatments elicited consistent responses across murine and human cell models, validating experimental robustness.

Building on reports that Brca1-deficient breast cancer cells polarize macrophages toward pro-tumor phenotypes, we evaluated therapeutic interventions on this process. Prior to formal experiments, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated (Supporting Information Fig. S9), and it was verified that neither Free OL, BP-NSs nor OL@BP-NSs alone had a significant effect on the M1 or M2 proportion of BMDMs (Supporting Information Fig. S10), indicating no direct effect on macrophage polarization. To assess whether treatments could counteract tumor-driven M2 polarization, two sequential co-culture models were established (Fig. 3D). In the pretreatment model, 4T1Brca1−KD cells were exposed to PBS, Free OL, OL@BP-NSs, MPTT, or OL@BP-NSs + MPTT before co-culture with M0-like BMDMs. Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis (Fig. 3E–H) revealed that PBS-treated tumor cells strongly induced M2 polarization, whereas OL@BP-NSs triggered tumor cell death and released immunostimulatory signals, dose-dependently increasing M1 polarization (Free OL < OL@BP-NSs). Notably, MPTT alone, which lacked tumoricidal activity, showed M1 polarization comparable to PBS, and its combination with OL@BP-NSs did not enhance efficacy, confirming that M1 polarization in this model primarily depends on tumor cell death magnitude. In the post-treatment model, pre-established tumor–macrophage interactions were subjected to therapeutic interventions. Here, Free OL and OL@BP-NSs lost their ability to modulate M1 polarization (Fig. 3I–L), suggesting that M2-like BMDMs protect tumor cells via paracrine mechanisms. Strikingly, MPTT uniquely reversed M2 polarization independently of tumor cell death and synergized with OL@BP-NSs to restore tumor cell sensitivity to PARPi. These findings suggest that MPTT disrupts the immunosuppressive barrier imposed by M2-like BMDMs, thereby reinstating OL's cytotoxic efficacy.

OL@BP-NSs combined MPTT to overcome acquired PARPi resistance in Brca1-deficient tumor cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which M2-like BMDMs regulate PARPi sensitivity, we focused on γ-H2AX, a hallmark of DNA double-strand breaks39. FCM analysis (Fig. 3M) revealed a gradient increase in γ-H2AX MFI across PBS-, Free OL-, and OL@BP-NSs-treated groups in the pretreatment co-culture model (Fig. 3D), indicating that OL triggers double-strand break induction and subsequent dsDNA release via tumor cell death. Notably, this damage gradient paralleled the dose-dependent increase in M1-like BMDMs (Fig. 3E and F), suggesting dsDNA acts as a DAMP to drive macrophage M1 polarization. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed these findings, showing significantly elevated γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity in OL@BP-NSs-treated tumor nuclei (Fig. 3N, Supporting Information Fig. S11A), whereas MPTT alone failed to induce DNA damage markers, consistent with its lack of direct tumoricidal activity. Crucially, the OL@BP-NSs + MPTT group exhibited DNA damage levels comparable to OL@BP-NSs alone, confirming that MPTT does not jeopardize PARPi efficacy through off-target genotoxicity.

In the post-treatment co-culture model, FCM and CLSM analyses (Fig. 3O and P, Fig. S11B) uncovered distinct mechanisms. Compared to the pretreatment model, γ-H2AX signals in Free OL and OL@BP-NSs groups were markedly attenuated, demonstrating that M2-like BMDMs protect tumor cells from PARPi-induced DNA damage. Intriguingly, MPTT treatment alone—despite not altering tumor cell DNA damage markers—significantly increased M1-like BMDM proportions (Fig. 3I and J), suggesting a dsDNA-independent pathway for macrophage repolarization. In addition, the OL@BP-NSs + MPTT combination elicited the strongest γ-H2AX signals, surpassing monotherapy groups, which indicates that MPTT disrupts M2-like TAM-mediated resistance barriers to restore and amplify OL@BP-NSs’ synthetic lethality.

OL@BP-NSs combined MPTT to activate STING signaling in BMDMs and amplify aCD47-mediated anti-tumor immunity

To dissect the role of the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS)–STING pathway in therapeutically intervened macrophage polarization, we utilized the second co-culture model that better recapitulates the in vivo TME. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4A and B, Supporting Information Fig. S12) revealed that while total STING expression remained unchanged across groups, phosphorylated STING (p-STING) levels were uniquely elevated in the MPTT group, with maximal activation observed in the OL@BP-NSs + MPTT combination. This phenomenon aligns with the MPTT-driven M1 polarization observed in the absence of tumor DNA damage (Fig. 3I and J), suggesting that heat stress activates STING through non-canonical pathways, potentially via mitochondrial oxidative stress or the unfolded protein response40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45.

Subsequently, we examined the cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) levels in BMDMs from the PBS and MPTT groups. Surprisingly, heat stress led to an increase in cytosolic mtDNA in BMDMs (Supporting Information Fig. S13), providing a mechanistic basis for MPTT-driven p-STING elevation independent of tumor-derived dsDNA. Furthermore, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay confirmed that the combination therapy significantly upregulated STING downstream effector molecules—tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-β (IFN-β), C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), and C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10)—in BMDM-conditioned media (Fig. 4C, Supporting Information Fig. S14), confirming that the synergistic effect of OL@BP-NSs + MPTT could bypass the limitations of the classical dsDNA–cGAS–STING axis (Fig. 4D).

Next, we validated the effect of temporal coordination between PARPi-treatment and delayed release of aCD47 on macrophages' antitumor function. Using OL-pretreated 4T1Brca1−KD cells as a model for dying cells (Fig. 4E, Supporting Information Fig. S15), we observed that dying tumor cells markedly enhanced the phagocytic activity of M1-like BMDMs (Fig. 4F and G), likely through dsDNA-triggered “eat-me” signaling. In addition, aCD47 dose-dependently augmented phagocytosis efficiency, effectively neutralizing the CD47–SIRPα “don't eat me” axis. CLSM imaging corroborated these findings, showing increased tumor cell internalization in aCD47-treated groups (Supporting Information Fig. S16). At the level of antigen cross-presentation, an in vitro assay was designed to reveal the critical role of aCD47 (Fig. 4H and Supporting Information Fig. S17). FCM analysis showed the M1-like BMDMs co-cultured with OL-pretreated 4T1-OVA cells (versus non-treated 4T1-OVA cells) exhibited higher surface H-2Kb–SIINFEKL complex expression (positive rate and MFI), and in an aCD47 concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4I and J, Supporting Information Fig. S18). These results suggest that CD47 blockade increases the amount of phagocytosed tumor antigen, which in turn generates more antigenic peptides for cross-presentation.

DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) combined MPTT to suppress Brca1-deficient TNBC growth and reprogram the immunosuppressive TME

Encouraged by in vitro findings, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) combined with MPTT in an orthotopic Brca1-deficient TNBC mouse model (Fig. 5A). Tumor growth kinetics revealed that there were no significant differences in tumor volume among the groups during the initial phase of treatment, but significant divergence in tumor volume emerged following Day 7 (Fig. 5B and C). The DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L group exhibited tumor volume reduction as early as 3 days post-intervention—a response unique to this cohort. By Day 21, this sequential therapy achieved complete remission in 20% of mice (1/5), while the Free (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L group showed 7.7-fold larger tumors, underscoring the critical role of DLG's temporally controlled-release properties. Final tumor weights and gross images corroborated this trend (Fig. 5D and E). To assess the contribution of spatiotemporal control, we compared DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L with a composition-matched homogeneous lipid gel (HLG) + L. The DLG treatment resulted in significantly smaller tumors than the HLG group at all endpoint assessments, reinforcing the necessity of layered programming for enhanced therapeutic efficacy (Supporting Information Fig. S19).

DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) combined MPTT to establish systemic anti-tumor immunity and immune memory to suppress distal tumor growth and lung metastasis

The advantage of cancer immunotherapy lies in its capacity to activate systemic adaptive immunity for metastatic prevention48. To evaluate whether DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) combined with MPTT extends local anti-tumor immunity to systemic protection, we established a distal tumor-bearing mouse model (Fig. 7A). Distal tumors mirrored the growth kinetics of primary tumors during the initial 7 days post-inoculation (Figure 5, Figure 7B). However, upon therapeutic intervention at the primary site (Day 7), distal tumor suppression exhibited a temporal lag, with significant growth inhibition becoming apparent only by Day 13 (6 days post-treatment) (Fig. 7B and C). Strikingly, the DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L group achieved complete remission (CR) in 20% of distal tumors, with final tumor weights reduced to 6.01% of PBS controls (Fig. 7D). Serum cytokine assays revealed that the sequential therapy significantly elevated systemic levels of TNF-α, IFN-β, CXCL9, and CXCL10 (Fig. 7E, Supporting Information Fig. S26)—key mediators of innate and adaptive immunity—confirming its capacity to synchronize local tumor control with systemic immune activation.

To further elucidate the immunological mechanism of the anti-metastatic effect, T-cell subsets in TdLNs 24 h post-tumor cell infusion were analyzed by FCM. The results showed that the DLG (OL@BP-NSs + aCD47) + L decreased naïve CD8+ T cells by 69.6% while increasing total memory CD8+ T cells by 57.9%, with effector memory (TEM) and central memory (TCM) subsets elevated 1.5- and 1.8-fold, respectively, versus PBS group (Fig. 7I–K, Supporting Information Figs. This dual activation of both T cell subsets enhances the potential for sustained immune surveillance, offering long-term protection against metastatic TNBC cells.

Discussion

This study developed a thermo-responsive sequential immunotherapy platform by integrating MPTT, PARP inhibition (OL@BP-NSs), and CD47 blockade (aCD47) to overcome intrinsic drug resistance and immunosuppressive microenvironment barriers in Brca-mutated TNBC. Unlike conventional combination therapies that rely on simultaneous drug release, DLG leverages spatiotemporally controlled release properties to achieve a dynamic cascade of MPTT-mediated TME remodeling, OL-induced tumor killing, and immune checkpoint potentiation. Specifically, MPTT activates the STING pathway in a DNA damage-independent manner to reverse the pro-tumorigenic phenotype of M2-like TAMs, without inducing tumor cell death. In contrast, OL@BP-NSs induce synthetic lethality in Brca1-deficient tumor cells while releasing DAMPs. The delayed release of aCD47 effectively blocks the CD47–SIRPα axis, thereby attenuating the “don't eat me” signal and concomitantly enhancing macrophage phagocytosis coupled with antigen cross-presentation. This multidimensional regulatory mechanism demonstrated significant systemic anti-tumor effects in both distal tumor and lung metastasis models, with therapeutic efficacy markedly superior to free drug combination regimens, thereby validating the biological necessity of temporally controlled release strategies.

Although approximately 15%–20% of TNBC patients harbor BRCA1/2 mutations49, a population theoretically sensitive to PARPi, clinical outcomes remain significantly constrained by TME-mediated adaptive resistance3, 4, 5. This therapeutic impasse has driven the exploration of combination strategies, particularly the integration of PARPi with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors15, 16, 17. Mechanistically, PARPi potentiates tumor immunogenicity through induction of genomic instability and neoantigen burden, while PD-1/PD-L1 blockade seeks to counteract T cell exhaustion. However, pivotal clinical trials demonstrate modest objective response rates of ∼30% in advanced TNBC cohorts, underscoring the prevalence of intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms16,50, 51, 52, 53. These limitations underscore the inadequacy of strategies focused solely on adaptive immune reactivation, which fail to overcome the immunosuppressive barriers erected by innate immune components like TAMs.

The innovation of this work lies in redirecting therapeutic targeting from adaptive immune checkpoints to TAMs, the dominant regulators of the TME in TNBC. While prior studies demonstrated transient anti-tumor effects through TAM depletion (e.g., colony stimulating factor 1 receptor inhibitors), our approach capitalizes on the plasticity of TAMs. By employing MPTT, we achieved functional reprogramming of M2-like TAMs into anti-tumor M1-like effectors. This strategy aligns with emerging evidence that STING pathway activation reshapes immunosuppressive microenvironments but diverges critically by identifying a non-canonical STING activation mechanism. Specifically, we reveal that thermal stimuli, unlike conventional dsDNA–cGAS signaling, activate STING through mtDNA-mediated pathways. This activation could potentially bypass immune evasion mechanisms in cGAS-deficient tumors. Furthermore, while existing nanocarriers prioritize co-delivery of synergistic agents, DLG's temporally controlled release system introduces a biologically rational sequence. This sequential delivery pattern contrasts starkly with traditional concurrent delivery, which often induces counterproductive immune exhaustion54,55. The long-term survival rate of DLG was 80% versus 0% in free drug combinations, demonstrating the superiority of spatiotemporal coordination in overcoming the TNBC's dual resistance axes (drug resistance and immunosuppression).

From a translational medicine perspective, the value of this study lies in proposing a scalable therapeutic framework for an in situ gel system constructed from FDA-approved lipid materials (EPC/GDO) that combines biosafety with controlled drug release, while its modular design allows flexible adaptation of therapeutic components based on tumor subtype37,56. For instance, PARPi could be replaced with chemotherapeutic agents for non-Brca-mutant TNBC, or supplemented with cytokines or Toll-like receptor agonists to enhance immune priming in immunologically “cold” tumors. However, clinical translation requires addressing several challenges. First, while murine models demonstrate DLG-induced long-term immune memory, TNBC patients often exhibit T cell exhaustion, a condition necessitating validation in humanized models to confirm generalizability. Second, the treatment parameters for MPTT, such as laser power and irradiation duration, will need further optimization to refine therapeutic efficacy. Third, the long-term retention of gel matrices may complicate repeat dosing regimens, mandating systematic evaluation of local tissue biocompatibility (fibrosis risk) and metabolic kinetics (carrier degradation timelines). Overall, the “local physical intervention to systemic immune activation” paradigm uncovered in this work not only provides novel insights for TNBC treatment but also establishes a transformative framework for combination immunotherapy in other immunologically “cold” tumors.

Conclusions

This study addressed the critical therapeutic challenge in Brca1-mutated TNBC, where the immunosuppressive TME, particularly M2-like TAM dominance, undermines PARPi and ICB efficacy. We developed a spatiotemporally sequential strategy delivered via a DLG platform: MPTT first reprograms TAMs toward an anti-tumor M1 phenotype via non-canonical STING activation, dismantling immunosuppressive barriers. Subsequent PARPi induces synthetic lethality and releases DAMPs, while delayed anti-CD47 blockade disrupts the CD47–SIRPα axis to enhance macrophage phagocytosis and antigen cross-presentation. This “barrier-breaking to efficacy-amplifying” cascade achieved potent systemic anti-tumor responses and long-term immune memory in aggressive TNBC models, significantly outperforming conventional combination therapies. The DLG's temporally controlled release proved essential to avoid counterproductive immune exhaustion and overcome dual resistance axes. By shifting focus from adaptive immune reactivation to innate TAM reprogramming and coordinated innate-adaptive immune engagement, this work provides a transformative paradigm for TNBC treatment.
