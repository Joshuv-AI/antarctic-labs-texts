Biodecolorization and Biodegradation of Methyl Orange by Immobilized Pseudomonas aeruginosa Bacterium into SA/PVA Matrix Integrated with MOF UiO-66 Adsorbent

ABSTRACT
Highlights•UiO-66 integrated with alginate/polyvinyl alcohol enhanced dye adsorption capacity.•Immobilized P. aeruginosa in the matrix improved dye biodegradation efficiency.•Composite beads removed 92.03 % of dye, 33.99 % higher than free bacterial cells.•MO biodegradation pathway was proposed.Keywords: Biodegradation, Immobilization, Methyl orange, UiO-66, Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Introduction

The rapid expansion of textile industry is significantly contributing to global economic growth. However, this development has led to severe environmental concerns, particularly through the discharge of dye-laden wastewater [1,2]. Among the synthetic dye widely used, azo dye accounts for approximately 60–70 % of application use due to high stability and color intensity [[3], [4], [5]]. One common type of azo dyes is methyl orange (MO), which contains a characteristic azo bond (–N=N–) contributing to water solubility and recalcitrance in the environment [6,7]. In humans, the accumulation of MO through food chains can lead to exposure in the digestive system, potentially causing mutagenic diseases [8]. MO also has adverse effects by reducing soil fertility, plant productivity, and biodiversity [9]. Furthermore, the discharge into water bodies can block sunlight penetration, disrupt photosynthesis in aquatic organisms, and reduce dissolved oxygen levels [10].

Various methods for the treatment of MO have been investigated, including coagulation, sedimentation, flocculation, and adsorption. However, the investigation has primarily focused on decolorization rather than the complete breakdown of dye molecules [[11], [12], [13]]. Other methods such as photochemistry, electrochemistry, and photocatalysis offer improved degradation efficiency but are often limited by high operational costs, complex procedures, and the generation of secondary pollutants [[14], [15], [16]]. As an alternative, biological treatment methods using microorganisms have been identified as an environmentally friendly and cost-effective strategies, capable of degrading dye, including MO, into less harmful compounds through enzymatic processes [14,[17], [18], [19], [20]].

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) is one of the bacteria that is known to have the ability to degrade MO [21]. Kishor et al. [22] reported that PA showed 99 % removal efficiency with 96 % reduction of total organic carbon. A study conducted by Khan et al. [23] reported that PA showed the highest biodecolorization efficiency among other eight bacteria, achieving a removal rate of 88.23 %. This superior performance could be attributed to the ability to produce oxidative and reductive enzymes, such as azoreductase, peroxidase, and laccase, facilitating reductive cleavage of azo bonds and converting MO into colorless aromatic amines [7,[23], [24], [25]]. Despite the effectiveness, the use of PA in free-cell form is limited by low stability and sensitivity to environmental fluctuations, which compromises biodegradation performance [14,26]. To overcome these challenges, the immobilization of PA cells in polymeric matrices has been proposed to enhance the operational stability, reusability, and overall biodegradation performance.

Sodium alginate (SA), a biopolymer derived from brown algae, has been widely explored as an immobilization matrix due to the non-toxic nature and biocompatibility. However, pure SA-based matrix shows poor mechanical strength, requiring reinforcement with other materials [[27], [28], [29]]. Previous studies by Xiang et al. [30] and Wei et al. [31] successfully modified SA with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), leading to a suitable immobilization matrix for pollutant removal. PVA offers excellent adhesive characteristics, serving as a suitable material to enhance the stability of SA-based systems [32]. Although SA/PVA-based immobilization system has been extensively used, adsorption capacity toward dye such as MO remains limited [14].

To address the limitation, this study aimed to integrate metal-organic framework (MOF), UiO-66, into SA/PVA matrix. UiO-66 is characterized by a high surface area, strong affinity toward anionic dye, and biocompatibility [33,34]. Based on Molavi et al. [35], UiO-66 shows a high affinity for anionic dye such as MO and adsorption capacity of 83.7 mg/g. Moreover, MOF possesses low toxicity and favorable biocompatibility, serving as a suitable option for use as an adsorbent in the bacteria immobilization matrix [36].

This study proposed a biohybrid system composed of SA/PVA/UiO-66 beads entrapping PA cells and combining the adsorption capacity of UiO-66 with biodegradation ability of the immobilized bacteria. The results were expected to enhance both the efficiency and sustainability of MO removal, offering a comprehensive method for dye-contaminated wastewater. System performance was also evaluated through adsorption isotherm and kinetic studies to quantify the capacity and adsorption behavior. Furthermore, material characterization using FTIR, XRD, and SEM-EDX was conducted to confirm the structural integrity and successful integration of components. The formation of metabolites was identified using LC-MS analysis during biodegradation process, providing mechanistic insights into degradation pathway of MO.

Materials and methods

Bacteria and chemicals

The materials used in this study included PA (collection of the Chemical Microorganism Laboratory, Department of Chemistry, Institut Teknologi Sepuluh Nopember), nutrient agar (NA; Merck), Luria-Bertani broth (LB; Merck), demineralized water, zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl4; Sigma-Aldrich), 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2BDC; Sigma-Aldrich), N,N’-dimethylformamide (DMF; Sigma-Aldrich), methanol (Merck), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Sigma-Aldrich), sodium alginate (SA; Himedia), calcium chloride dihydrate (Merck), and MO (Merck).

Pre-culture and culture conditions

The pre-culture of PA was initiated by preparing 20 mL of LB media in an Erlenmeyer flask. Using an inoculation loop, the regenerated PA was transferred into 20 mL of LB medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with constant shaking at 180 rpm [14,26]. Following incubation, 180 mL of fresh LB medium was prepared in a separate Erlenmeyer flask, and 20 mL of the pre-cultured PA was inoculated using a micropipette. Subsequently, the culture was incubated under the same conditions, 37 °C and 180 rpm, for another 24 h [[37], [38], [39], [40]].

UiO-66 synthesis

UiO-66 was synthesized through a solvothermal method with a slight modification of the procedure described by Hidayat et al. [41]. Initially, 1.0487 g of ZrCl4 and 0.7481 g of H2BDC were each dissolved in 45 mL of DMF solvent and stirred for 15 min until homogeneous. The two solutions were combined and stirred for an additional 30 min. The mixture obtained was heated at 120 °C for 24 h, followed by cooling at room temperature for another 24 h. The formed precipitate was separated from the supernatant by decantation. To remove residual solvents and unreacted ligands, the precipitate was soaked in fresh DMF for 24 h, decanted, and washed twice with methanol, each for 24 h. Furthermore, a decantation was performed and UiO-66 was dried at 100 °C for 24 h.

Immobilization of P. aeruginosa

To prepare the matrix solution, 3.75 g of PVA and 0.5 g of SA were dissolved in 45 mL of demineralized water under continuous stirring at 350 rpm and 100 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, 0.5 g of the synthesized UiO-66 (equivalent to 1 % (w/v) of the matrix solution) was incorporated into the mixture and homogenized using a homogenizer. Simultaneously, 5 % (v/v) of PA (3.1 × 107 CFU/mL) was added to SA/PVA/UiO-66 matrix and homogenized to obtain SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA mixture [14,28].

The prepared mixture was transferred into a 10 mL syringe and dropwise added into 200 mL of cold 4 % (w/v) CaCl2 solution under constant magnetic stirring at 200 rpm [42]. Beads obtained were maintained in CaCl2 solution for 6 h to allow ionic cross-linking and stabilization [32]. Furthermore, beads were collected and washed three times with demineralized water to remove residual ions, obtaining SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads. The entire process in this step was performed under sterile conditions in a laminar air flow to minimize the risk of contamination. The steps were schematically shown in Fig. 1 [43].

Illustration of immobilized PA in SA/PVA/UiO-66 matrix.

Adsorbent and beads characterization

XRD (Philips PW 1140/90) characterization was conducted to evaluate the crystallinity of UiO-66 in both the pristine powdered and SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA composite beads. Before analysis, beads were freeze-dried at -40 °C for 4 h, followed by grinding into a fine and homogenous powder [14]. Additionally, FTIR (Shimadzu 8400s) was used to confirm the presence of functional groups at wavelength of 4000–400 cm-1 in UiO-66 and SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads before and after biodegradation. For FTIR analysis, dried samples were finely ground with potassium bromide (KBr) powder and pressed into pellets before measurement [32]. The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of beads were examined using SEM-EDX (Hitachi Flex SEM 1000) at magnifications of 40×, 100×, 5,000×, and 10,000× [33].

MO biodecolorization and biodegradation

Biodecolorization-biodegradation test was conducted by introducing 3 g of SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads into 10 mL of 50 mg/L MO solution in a carbon-free mineral salts medium (MSM). The mixture was incubated at 35 °C for 10 days, followed by separating beads through centrifugation, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 464 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Genesys 10S). For comparison, the same test was also carried out using free cells of PA, UiO-66, SA/PVA, SA/PVA/UiO-66, and SA/PVA/PA beads. The percentage of MO removal was determined using Eq. (1), where C0 is the initial concentration and Ct is the final concentration of MO [6,7].

Kinetic and isothermal adsorption tests were conducted to evaluate the effects of UiO-66 incorporation into beads compared to the neat SA/PVA. Pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models were applied for the kinetic analysis, while Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms were used for isotherm analysis. Furthermore, selected filtrates from SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA treatment were analyzed using LC-MS (Waters) to identify the metabolic products obtained during biodegradation process [7,33,42].

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for statistical analysis to assess significant differences among the treatment groups in biodecolorization-biodegradation test. This was followed by applying a post-hoc test, namely Tukey’s HSD, to determine specific group differences. Subsequently, a probability value (p-value) <0.05 was considered significant.

Results and discussion

Immobilization of PA into SA/PVA/UiO-66 matrix

In this study, PA was immobilized using the entrapment method, a widely common method of microorganism immobilization. This method included enclosing bacteria cells in a supporting matrix, forming a cage-like structure [44]. The immobilization matrix used was composed of SA and PVA, selected due to complementary physicochemical properties. Because of biocompatibility and non-toxic nature, SA is often applied in biomedical and environmental applications, including microbial immobilization [14]. Structurally, SA consists of 1,4-linked β-d-mannuronic acid (M units) and α-l-guluronic acid (G units), capable of forming GG, MM, and MG blocks. These structures show flexibility but have insufficient cross-linking required for strong mechanical properties. Additionally, the low mechanical strength of SA is attributed to the steric effects of carboxyl groups, creating steric hindrance and preventing the compact packing of the chains. To address the limitation, combining SA with other polymers, such as PVA, is considered effective. This is because PVA is recognized for good stability and mechanical properties, particularly in terms of material strength. The rigid and crystalline structure of PVA is due to high hydrolysis degree and the presence of hydroxyl groups (-OH), facilitating strong intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding between PVA chains [28,29,32].

Despite the ability to immobilize bacteria effectively, SA/PVA polymer mixture shows limited adsorption capacity due to low surface area [45,46]. Therefore, the matrix was incorporated with UiO-66 as the additional adsorbent. UiO-66 has high surface area, ranging between 1000 and 1800 m2/g [47]. With pore sizes spanning from 8 to 11 Å, this material has exceptional porosity, facilitating the efficient adsorption of MO [48]. The presence of Zr clusters in UiO-66 provides specific binding sites for MO negatively charged molecules [6]. Additionally, UiO-66 is biocompatible and has low toxicity. Previous studies reported minimal impact on cell viability at appropriate concentrations [49]. These significant characteristics make UiO-66 a suitable adsorbent for integration into bacteria immobilization matrix.

In fabricating SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads, CaCl2 solution is often used as cross-linking agent [6]. Cross-linking process depends on divalent Ca2+ ions, which interact with the carboxyl groups of SA, replacing Na+ ions in G-block regions of SA. In comparison, M-block containing β-d-mannuronic acid contributes less to hydrogel formation due to the minimum favorable conformation for ion binding [14,50]. The ionic cross-linking of CaCl2 to SA polymer also provides a network effect, thereby improving the stability, mechanical strength, and resistance of beads to external stresses [51]. Beads characterization

The diffractogram in Fig. 3a shows sharp and well-defined peaks, at 7.21°, 8.33°, and 25.55°, indicating high crystallinity [52]. These XRD characterization results confirmed the successful formation of UiO-66. Furthermore, when UiO-66 was incorporated into the SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA composite beads, the three peaks were obtained at 7.59°, 8.65°, and 25.95°, suggesting that MOF was embedded in the polymer matrix. These results are consistent with the findings reported by Pratama et al. [14] who also observed three characteristic diffraction peaks in polymer-based composites, indicating that MOF retained good structural stability, particularly after encapsulation.

(a) XRD diffractograms of UiO-66 and SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads; (b) FTIR spectroscopy of P. aeruginosa, UiO-66, and SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads.

FTIR analysis was conducted to further verify the chemical structure and interactions in the composite. As presented in Fig. 3b, FTIR spectroscopy of pure UiO-66 showed several characteristic absorption bands, including a broad peak at 3390 cm⁻¹ corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups in the organic linker, a peak at 1400 cm⁻¹ associated with symmetric and asymmetric stretching of the terephthalate linker, and a distinct band at 746 cm⁻¹ attributed to Zr–O vibrations in the framework [33,52,53]. FTIR spectroscopy of SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA composite beads, recorded both before and after the decolorization process, showed a broad absorption band at 3200–3300 cm⁻¹. This band corresponded to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups present in SA. Additionally, a band in the 1413–1420 cm⁻¹ range was observed due to the symmetric stretching vibrations of carboxylate (COO⁻) groups in SA. A distinct band at 1523 cm⁻¹ was associated with the stretching vibrations of N–H group from PA proteins [14]. At 2925–2930 cm⁻¹ and 1023 cm⁻¹, characteristic bands were identified, corresponding to C–H and C–O stretching vibrations, respectively, indicating the presence of functional groups related to PVA [28,54]. The presence of a band in the 700–750 cm⁻¹ region confirmed the retention of Zr–O bonds, suggesting continued presence of UiO-66 framework in the composite beads [33,55]. Minor shifts in these bands after decolorization suggested interactions between MO molecules and the functional groups in beads. This provides information on the supportive role of both physical adsorption and chemical interaction in decolorization process [56,57].

The surface morphology of SA/PVA/UiO-66 beads, both before and after biodecolorization, was characterized using SEM. As presented in Fig. 4a, beads showed an intact and uniform structure, suggesting high stability attributed to cross-linking with CaCl2, which enhanced the mechanical integrity of bead matrix [32]. Additionally, Fig. 4b shows bacteria cells embedded in bead matrix, appearing as rod-shaped structures with approximately 1–5 μm in length and 0.5–1 μm in width, consistent with PA morphology [58,59]. Moreover, the cubic crystals in the matrix which are shown in Fig. 4c, confirm the successful incorporation of UiO-66 [60]. The overall surface morphology of beads before biodecolorization is presented in Fig. 5a. Based on observation, beads appear shrunken, attributed to minimal fluid uptake, while after biodecolorization images (Fig. 5b) show swelling due to MO solution absorption during the process [7].

SEM-EDX results of (a) SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA before MO removal; and (b) SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA after MO removal.

EDX analysis (Fig. 5a and 5b) showed oxygen as the dominant element (30 %) before biodecolorization, originating from SA, PVA, and UiO-66. This was followed by carbon (19 %) and sodium (7 %) from SA, calcium (23 %) and chlorine (8 %) from CaCl2, zirconium (3 %) from UiO-66, and nitrogen (3 %) and phosphorus (1 %) from PA. The results confirmed the successful integration of all targeted components into beads matrix. Specifically, the increase in nitrogen and phosphorus content after biodecolorization was indicative of microbial activity and potential degradation of MO occurring in the matrix. Similar trends were reported by Purnomo et al. [6], who observed compositional shifts in PVA-based composite beads after dye adsorption and microbial treatment, further supporting the occurrence of both adsorption and biodegradation in the system.

Biodecolorization-biodegradation of methyl orange by SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA

MO biodecolorization-biodegradation test was performed over 10 days using MSM medium, which provided essential nutrients with limited carbon sources to support bacterial growth. This test compared biodecolorization capabilities of free cells of PA, UiO-66, SA/PVA, SA/PVA/UiO-66, SA/PVA/PA, and SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA. The percentage of biodecolorization, which shows the reduction in MO concentration, is depicted in Fig. 6. Among all tested samples, SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads showed the highest biodecolorization percentage of 92.02 %, which was significantly higher than all other treatments, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA and post hoc analysis (p < 0.05). This value represented a 33.99 % improvement over the free PA system (68.68 %). The enhanced decolorization efficiency indicated a synergistic interaction among immobilized bacteria cells, polymeric matrix, and UiO-66 adsorbent. This performance can be attributed to the enhanced adsorption capacity conferred by SA/PVA matrix and UiO-66 high surface area, to improve the electrostatic interactions toward MO [14,61].

Average MO removal efficiency (%) by the treatment groups. Data represent the mean values ± standard deviation (n = 3). Different letters above the bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05).

In comparison, SA/PVA/PA beads without UiO-66, achieved 77.53 % MO removal, confirming that bacteria immobilization only contributed substantially to enhanced biodecolorization relative to free cells. Similarly, SA/PVA/UiO-66 beads without bacteria achieved 69.55 % removal, which was slightly higher than the free-cell system, showing the benefit of MOF incorporation for adsorption. Meanwhile, the use of a single system comprising only SA/PVA showed limited effectiveness, with 47.57 % MO removal. This suggested the necessity of both microbial and adsorptive components. The free-cells of PA achieved 68.68 % efficiency, while UiO-66 obtained the lowest removal at 28.02 %. The lower values showed the limited capability of MOF when used individually, functioning solely through adsorption without metabolic breakdown of the dye [14,33].

The results were in line with previous studies using hybrid bio-adsorbent system. For instance, Nabilah et al. [29] reported that embedding bentonite into SA/PVA matrix immobilizing Ralstonia pickettii and Trichoderma viride improved MB removal to 97.88 %, compared to 94.6 % in the absence of the adsorbent. Purnomo et al. [6] found that SA/PVA/UiO-66 beads immobilizing brown-rot fungi achieved 68.95 % MO removal. Meanwhile, the use of SA/PVA alone achieved 43.3 %, showing the essential role of adsorbent inclusion in enhancing decolorization performance.

The visible decolorization observed in this study further supports the quantitative results. As presented in Fig. 7a, a clear visual difference is shown between the original 50 mg/L MO solution and the filtrate obtained after 10 days of treatment with SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads. A significant color fading is observed, indicating enzymatic breakdown of MO structure by PA bacteria. This bond cleavage leads to the formation of a colorless unconjugated compound, reducing the delocalization of electrons and causing the orange color to fade [7]. Additionally, Fig. 7b and 7c show the appearance of SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads after 3 and 10 days of MO decolorization process. Initially white, beads turned orange after 3 days, showing that MO dye had adsorbed onto beads. After 10 days, orange color appeared to fade as MO dye started to degrade into simpler compounds [6,7].

As observed from Table 2, the Langmuir model produced higher correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9581 for SA/PVA and R2 = 0.9921 for SA/PVA/UiO-66) compared to the Freundlich (R2 = 0.8453 and R2 = 0.8843, respectively). This showed that the adsorption of MO on both types of beads was properly represented by monolayer on a homogenous surface [20]. Moreover, the maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) significantly increased from 4.79 mg/g for SA/PVA to 6.87 mg/g for SA/PVA/UiO-66, showing a 43.5 % improvement due to the incorporation of UiO-66. The higher KL value (0.052 L/mg) for SA/PVA/UiO-66 compared to SA/PVA (0.002 L/mg) further confirms stronger interactions between MO and the modified beads [55]. These results are consistent with previous studies on MOF-based composites. Similar trends, where higher R2 values favor the Langmuir isotherm over the Freundlich model, have been reported for UiO-66-modified materials by Alkas et al. [61], Shen et al. [68], and Lu et al. [69], thereby reinforcing the dominance of chemisorption as the primary adsorption mechanism.

Identification of metabolites from MO biodegradation

Metabolites from MO biodegradation through SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads were analyzed using LC-MS instrument. LC-MS enabled the identification of compounds based on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), allowing the elucidation of degradation pathways through various enzymatic stages. The method integrated the separation of compounds based on the retention time with the principle of LC followed by detection of the molecular mass of each component through MS [70]. Biodegradation of MO using SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads includes a series of enzyme-mediated transformation reactions facilitated by PA. The process starts with the cleavage of azo bonds catalyzed by the azoreductase enzyme, leading to the amine compound C11H19N5O2 (compound 1; m/z 254.1617). Subsequently, MO is transformed into C11H14N4O2 (compound 2; m/z 235.1195) through demethylation, generating reactive species that serve as substrates for enzymatic reactions [71]. Other enzymes in the form of monooxygenase catalyze the hydroxylation of the aromatic ring in MO structure, forming C7H11N3O4 (compound 5; m/z 202.0828) and C6H4N2O4 (compound 6; m/z 168.0093). These oxidative processes facilitate ring cleavage by dioxygenases, leading to the formation of aliphatic compounds such as C5H11N3O4 (compound 3; m/z 178.0828) and C3H10N3O4 (compound 4; m/z 152.0671). Additional oxidative reactions including monooxygenase and dioxygenase enzymes contribute to the generation of sulfur-containing intermediates like C8H19N2O2S (compound 7; m/z 207.1167) which shows a unique metabolic route in the degradation of MO [70]. The peroxidase enzyme further catalyzes the breakdown of more complex aliphatic intermediates into simpler molecules in the form of C5H11NO2 (compound 9; m/z 118.0868) [72]. The metabolites identified in this study differ from previous MO degradation reports, showing that each microorganism can use a distinct enzymatic pathway. For example, Pandey et al. [73] reported that MO degradation by Serratia marcescens ED1 bacteria produced 10 metabolites. These included 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid, 2-methyl dodecane, 2,3-butadienol, 2-oxopropionamide, 2‑methoxy naphthalene, isoborneol, 2-methyl heptadecane, hexadecenoic acid, dibutyl phthalate, and dimethyl sulfoxide. Meanwhile, MO biodegradation by Shewanella putrefaciens CN32 conducted by Zhao et al. [74] identified different products, namely N,N-dimethylaniline azobenzene sulfonamide, 4-aminobenzenesulfonic acid, N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine, N-methyl benzamide, and sodium benzenesulfonate. The differences supported that MO biodegradation was not influenced by a universal pathway or species-specific mechanisms. In this study, the detection of unique intermediates such as C11H19N5O2, C7H11N3O4, and C8H19N2O2S showed the distinct degradation route for MO facilitated by immobilized PA in the SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA matrix.

Conclusion

In conclusion, these results confirmed the successful formation of SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads. The isothermal adsorption test showed a 20.47 % improvement in MO adsorption capacity when UiO-66 was integrated into SA/PVA matrix. Furthermore, based on biodecolorization-biodegradation test, SA/PVA/UiO-66/PA beads effectively removed MO with percentage of 92.026 %, which was 33.99 % higher than free cells. These findings confirmed by the formation of nine biodegradation metabolites identified using LC-MS.
