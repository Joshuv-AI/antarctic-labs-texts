Latent profiles of nurses’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence in nursing and associated factors: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tingting Wei, Ting Wang, Jiaojiao Ruan, Xueying Pang, Peng Zhou, Zhengyiqing Wang, Mingxia Hu, Shanshan Chen, Xiumei Zhang, Chengcong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is being gradually integrated into clinical nursing practice, where it plays an important role in improving nursing quality, reducing nurses’ workload, and promoting the intelligent transformation of nursing. Nurses’ attitudes toward AI applications in nursing directly affect the promotion and implementation of this technology. Understanding these attitudes and their heterogeneity is crucial for the successful implementation of AI technology. This study aimed to identify potential types of nurses’ attitudes toward the use of AI in nursing and to explore the factors associated with profile membership with type affiliation.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 206 clinical nurses in Anhui Province, China, in July 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Attitudes Toward the Application of AI Technology in Nursing Scale, and the Multidimensional Nursing Generations Questionnaire. Latent profile analysis(LPA) was used to identify distinct attitude profiles. Univariate analyses and multinomial logistic regression were performed to explore associated factors.ResultsThree profiles were identified: positive acceptance (16.02%), ambivalent balance (9.71%), and cautious skepticism (74.27%). Multinomial logistic regression showed that educational level, computer proficiency, English proficiency, and generational characteristics were significantly associated with profile membership (all P < 0.05).ConclusionNurses showed moderate attitudes toward AI in nursing with substantial heterogeneity. Profile-tailored strategies may help nursing managers facilitate the effective and sustainable implementation of AI technologies in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04920-5.Keywords: Nurses, Attitudes, Artificial intelligence, Latent profile analysis, Generational characteristics

Introduction

Artificial intelligence(AI) refers to the use of computer algorithms and machine learning techniques to enable machines to perform tasks that traditionally require human intelligence, such as perception, reasoning, and decision-making [1]. With the rapid advancement of global information technology, AI has demonstrated substantial potential in the healthcare sector [2]. As an integral component of the healthcare service system, nursing is also undergoing the integration of AI technologies. An increasing number of healthcare institutions are incorporating AI into nursing practice, including intelligent triage systems, nursing decision-support tools, and patient condition monitoring systems [3]. The application of AI in nursing not only has the potential to enhance the quality of nursing services and alleviate nurses’ workload [4], but also to promote the transformation of nursing services toward greater intelligence, precision, and personalization. Consequently, it has become an important research focus in contemporary nursing science [5]. Within this context, nurses’ attitudes toward AI technologies directly influence their dissemination, adoption, and the transformation of nursing care models, making such attitudes a critical factor in advancing smart nursing initiatives [6].

Despite the broad application prospects of AI, its practical implementation in nursing still faces multiple challenges. Globally, AI has achieved preliminary outcomes in areas such as nursing information management, vital sign monitoring, and disease prediction; however, issues including ethical risks, data security concerns, and changes in nurses’ professional roles remain unresolved [7–10]. In China, the release of policies such as the National Nursing Development Plan (2021–2025) has provided a supportive policy environment for the promotion of AI in nursing [11]. Nevertheless, as the direct users and primary implementers of intelligent nursing technologies, nurses’ attitudes not only affect the adoption of smart nursing systems but also influence the sustainability and effectiveness of technology use. When nurses hold positive and optimistic views toward AI, they are more likely to increase their willingness to adopt AI in practice; conversely, when AI is perceived as a threat to professional autonomy or job security, nurses’ attitudes may become more negative, thereby reducing their interest in adopting AI technologies [12]. Notably, such attitudinal differences are not incidental but are shaped by a combination of social and psychological factors, among which generational differences are considered a key variable that cannot be overlooked [13]. Owing to substantial intergenerational differences across multiple dimensions, nurses’ attitudes toward and acceptance of AI technologies exhibit considerable complexity and heterogeneity.

International research on nurses’ attitudes toward AI began relatively early and has primarily focused on AI acceptance, attitudes toward nursing robots, and trust in intelligent monitoring systems [14]. Domestic studies have mainly examined nurses’ satisfaction with intelligent nursing devices and their level of awareness regarding AI applications [15]; however, many of these studies are limited by small sample sizes and relatively simplistic analytical approaches, with insufficient exploration of within-group heterogeneity. As nursing informatization continues to advance, attitudes within the nursing workforce are not homogeneous but instead present latent, unobserved subtypes, such as positive, cautious, ambivalent, or resistant profiles. Traditional statistical methods are therefore insufficient for effectively identifying the underlying structure of these attitudinal groups.

The present study is informed by the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape individuals’ attitudes and behavioral intentions toward technology adoption [16]. In recent years, latent profile analysis(LPA) has been increasingly applied in nursing research to identify heterogeneity in psychological and professional characteristics. Liu et al. [17] conducted a LPA to classify nurses’ informatics competencies and proposed differentiated training strategies based on the characteristics of each subgroup. Wang et al. [18] identified multiple empathy profiles among hospice nurses and recommended targeted management and support strategies to enhance their professional quality of life. These studies indicate that person-centered approaches can uncover meaningful subgroups that may remain concealed within traditional variable-centered analyses and provide empirical support for tailored interventions [18]. However, although LPA has been increasingly applied to identify heterogeneity in nursing competencies and psychological traits, limited research has used this method to examine nurses’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Applying LPA within the framework of technology acceptance theory may facilitate a more nuanced understanding of attitudinal heterogeneity toward AI and offer evidence for the development of profile-specific educational and implementation strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent profiles of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing and to examine the factors associated with membership in these profiles.

Methods

Study design and participants

Using a convenience sampling method, clinical nurses from China were selected as research participants in July 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) registered nurses engaged in clinical work; (2) work experience of at least six months; and (3) provision of informed consent and willingness to participate. The exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses not employed by the hospital (e.g., interns, visiting scholars, or seconded staff); and (2) those on leave during the survey period (e.g., maternity leave, sick leave).

The sample size estimation followed commonly used recommendations for multivariable analyses, suggesting a minimum of 10 participants per independent variable [19], and given that the study included 15 variables, with an additional 20% allowance for invalid questionnaires, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 180 participants.This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (Approval No.: 2025-05-99).

Measures

General information questionnaire

This section was designed by the research team and included variables such as gender, birth cohort, educational background, years of work experience, professional title, whether to hold a management position, and whether to be a specialist nurse.

The attitudes toward artificial intelligence in nursing scale (ASUAITIN)

Developed by Dilek Yilmaz in 2024 and translated into Chinese by Hu in 2025 [20, 21], this scale is used to assess clinical nurses’ attitudes toward the use of AI in nursing practice. Chinese version of the scale consists of 15 items, covering two dimensions: negative attitudes (6 items) and positive attitudes (9 items). A five-point Likert scoring method was adopted, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’, with scores assigned from 1 to 5. The total score ranges from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating more favorable attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing. Two factors explained 73.278% of the total variance, the scale’s Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.785, and the Cronbach’s α values of the two sub-dimensions were 0.920 and 0.948, respectively. The test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.91. The Cronbach’s α were 0.784 in this study. Permission has been obtained to use the Chinese-translated version of the ASUAITIN.

Multidimensional nursing generations questionnaire (MNGQ)

Developed by Finnish scholar Simone Stevanin in 2017 and translated into Chinese by Gao Xiao in 2024 [22, 23], this scale is used to investigate the generational characteristics of clinical nurses from different cohorts. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the Chinese version of the scale was 0.943, and the test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.764; the item-level content validity index ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index was 0.91. Exploratory factor analysis extracted six common factors, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 64.977%. The scale consists of 45 items across six dimensions: generational conflict, patient safety perspective, intergenerational relationships, multigenerational collaboration, direction change, and work orientation. A five-point Likert scoring method was employed, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree,’ with scores assigned from 1 to 5. Higher scores indicate more pronounced generational differences. The Cronbach’s α were 0.949 in the original research and 0.912 in this study. Permission has been obtained to use the Chinese-translated version of the MNGQ.

Data collection

In this study, an electronic questionnaire was created using Wenjuanxing. With the approval of the hospital’s nursing department, the electronic questionnaire was distributed to participants by the head nurses of each department. A standardized instruction informed participants of the study’s purpose, significance, and content. After providing informed consent and agreement to cooperate, nurses voluntarily scanned a QR code to complete the questionnaire. Each IP address was restricted to a single submission, and questionnaires could only be submitted once fully completed. To improve the response rate, send one WeChat reminder each day for three consecutive days to nurses who have not completed the questionnaire after it is distributed. After collection, the researchers reviewed the questionnaires individually and excluded those with obvious response patterns or illogical answers. A total of 212 questionnaires were collected, of which 206 were valid, yielding an effective response rate of 97.2%.

Data analysis

LPA was conducted using Mplus 8.3 with the two dimensions of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing as manifest indicators to identify potential subgroups of these attitudes. Beginning with a one-profile model, the number of profiles was progressively increased. The optimal model was determined based on the following fit indices: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), which should show a decreasing trend, with smaller values indicating better model fit [24]; an entropy value closer to 1, indicating higher classification accuracy [25]; and significant likelihood ratio tests (LMR) and Bootstrap-based likelihood ratio tests (BLRT) (P < 0.05), suggesting that a model with k profiles fits better than a model with k–1 profiles. Additionally, the smallest profile should contain no less than 5% of the sample [26]. SPSS 26.0 was used for statistical description, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, the Kruskal–Wallis H test, analysis of variance, and multinomial logistic regression analysis. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University(Approval No. PJ 2025-05-99). Study procedures followed the principles set out by the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent.

LPA of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing

LPA was performed using the mean scores of the dimensions of the Attitude Toward the Application of AI Technology in Nursing Scale as manifest indicators. A total of five latent profile models were fitted, as shown in Table 2. Starting from the one-profile model, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased progressively with the increasing number of profiles, while the entropy values increased accordingly. The optimal latent classes are selected based on a combination of fit indices and theoretical interpretability. Although entropy values can reflect classification accuracy, the minimum class size is equally crucial. While the four-class and five-class models show higher entropy values, two of the classes account for only 0.48% and 1.46% of the total sample, respectively. Such very small classes lack statistical stability and theoretical interpretability.When the model reached three profiles, both the LMR and BLRT tests showed statistical significance (P < 0.05), and the smallest class probability was not less than 5%. Therefore, the three-profile model was selected as the optimal model.Based on this model, a latent profile plot was generated according to the mean item scores of the three classes, as shown in Fig. 1. The categories were named according to their score characteristics. Class 1 had the lowest score on the negative dimension and the highest score on the positive dimension and was therefore labeled as the “positive acceptance” type (16.02%). Class 2 showed high scores on both the negative and positive dimensions and was labeled as the “ambivalent balance” type (9.71%). Class 3 had a lower score on the negative dimension and the lowest score on the positive dimension and was thus labeled as the “cautious skepticism” type (74.27%).

Analysis of factors influencing nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing

Univariate analysis showed that education level, professional title, English proficiency, managerial position, specialist nurse status, and generational characteristics of nurses had significant effects on the latent profiles of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 3.

By using the “cautious skepticism” group as the reference group, this study conceptualizes attitudinal heterogeneity as a developmental gradient, highlighting factors that may facilitate progression from skepticism to acceptance. Using the different latent profile categories of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing as the dependent variable, variables with statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis were included as independent variables: education level (junior college or below = 1, bachelor’s degree = 2, master’s degree or above = 3), professional title (nurse/senior nurse = 1, charge nurse = 2, associate chief nurse or above = 3), computer proficiency level (no computer exam taken = 1, provincial-level certification = 2, national-level certification = 3), English proficiency (not passed CET-4 = 1, passed CET-4 = 2, passed CET-6 = 3), managerial position (yes = 1, no = 2), specialist nurse status (yes = 1, no = 2), and multidimensional nursing generational characteristics (entered as raw scores). Multinomial logistic regression analysis showed that education level, English proficiency, and generational characteristics of nurses were significant influencing factors of the latent profiles of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 4.

Discussion

Current status and characteristics of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing

The results of this study showed that the score of nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI technology in nursing was 50.17 ± 6.06, with an average item score of 3.34 ± 0.40 and a scoring rate of 66.89% (between 60.00% and 80.00%, indicating a moderate level), which is consistent with the findings of Majed et al. [13]. This may be related to the limited practical application of AI in nursing at the current stage, nurses’ insufficient familiarity with new technologies, and their concerns about technological safety and controllability. As emerging technologies such as AI are gradually integrated into the nursing field—such as in real-time monitoring and personalized care—they can effectively improve nursing efficiency and reduce healthcare costs [27, 28]. Despite the advantages of using AI in nursing practice, several limitations remain, such as the inability to perform physical care and the inability to address patient needs based on priority [29].

Namdar Areshtanab et al. reported that while nurses acknowledged AI’s potential to improve care efficiency and clinical decision-making, they also expressed anxiety regarding ethical risks, data privacy, and potential role displacement. Similarly, Abdullah and Fakieh found that healthcare employees perceived AI as beneficial for workflow optimization but remained cautious due to concerns about system reliability and professional autonomy.These findings suggest that nurses’ attitudes toward AI are characterized by both optimism and concern [30, 31].

Group heterogeneity in nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing

Using LPA, this study is among the first to confirm, from a latent variable perspective, that nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing exhibit marked group heterogeneity. Three distinct profiles were identified: the “positive acceptance” group, the “ambivalent balance” group, and the “cautious skepticism” group.

Nurses in the “positive acceptance” group were predominantly those with the postgraduate and above educational background, who had Passed the national-level examination in computer proficiency, and Passed CET-6 in English proficiency. A relatively higher proportion held a management position and were specialist nurses. Most belonged to the Post-90s birth cohort.They typically demonstrate higher levels of information literacy and more favorable career development expectations, show a willingness to experiment with new technologies, and express strong endorsement of the usefulness and positive value of AI. They generally perceive AI as capable of substantially improving nursing efficiency, reducing workload, and enhancing patient safety [4].

Nurses in the “ambivalent balance” group were mainly those with a Bachelor’s degree, who had No computer proficiency examination taken and Did not pass CET-4. The majority did not hold a management position, and most were from the Post-80s and Post-90s cohorts.They reflect a dual cognition of AI: while recognizing its technological value, these nurses simultaneously express concerns regarding information security, system reliability, and potential changes in professional roles [7]. This group represents the most malleable target for AI promotion initiatives.

In contrast, nurses in the “cautious skepticism” group were primarily those with an Associate’s degree, who had No computer proficiency examination taken and Did not pass CET-4. Most did not hold a management position and were not specialist nurses. This group included nurses across all birth cohort categories.They are more attentive to potential occupational uncertainty, ethical conflicts, and implementation costs associated with AI, and remain particularly vigilant about the accuracy of intelligent systems and perceived insufficiencies in humanistic care [10]. Consequently, they tend to adopt a more conservative stance toward technology uptake. This heterogeneity suggests that, in advancing the clinical implementation of AI technologies, a “one-size-fits-all” approach to training and dissemination is inappropriate.

Instead, differentiated and multi-tiered educational strategies should be developed according to attitude profiles.In medical education, AI training modules that integrate case-based discussions and algorithm transparency explanations have been shown to improve clinicians’ trust in intelligent systems [32]. In industrial settings, digital transformation training can effectively increase employees’ technology adoption and drive industry development [33]. Drawing on these approaches, tailored AI education strategies for nurses may include, providing advanced technical training for positively accepting nurses, enhancing hands-on experience and evidence-based support for ambivalently balanced nurses, and offering psychological reassurance and career security guidance for cautiously skeptical nurses—to facilitate a gradual increase in acceptance of AI applications in nursing across diverse groups.

Analysis of factors influencing nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing

The findings indicate that nurses’ educational attainment is an important factor influencing their attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing. Compared with the “cautious skepticism” group, nurses with a master’s degree or higher were more likely to be classified into the “positive acceptance” group. Higher educational attainment is typically associated with stronger information literacy and greater research sensitivity [34], enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the developmental trends and clinical value of AI technologies. Such nurses are therefore better able to recognize the advantages of AI in enhancing nursing efficiency and improving diagnostic and therapeutic precision. In addition, highly educated nurses more often undertake managerial and research responsibilities within hospitals, use intelligent information systems more frequently, and demonstrate stronger adaptability to digitalized workflows [34]. In contrast, nurses with lower educational levels may be more prone to experiencing operational difficulties or insufficient knowledge reserves when encountering AI technologies, leading to more cautious or skeptical attitudes.

This study demonstrates a significant association between English proficiency and nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing. Nurses with higher levels of English proficiency are better able to access international cutting-edge information, such as AI-related nursing guidelines, research findings, and technical documentation [35]. This facilitates a more thorough understanding of the conceptual foundations and developmental trajectories of AI applications, thereby fostering more positive attitudes. Moreover, much of the core literature underlying current AI products and systems originates from international sources; higher English proficiency reduces barriers to information comprehension and improves the efficiency of technology acquisition. In contrast, nurses with weaker English skills may experience apprehension when engaging with technical materials, which may intensify cautious or even resistant attitudes toward AI. Therefore, enhancing nurses’ international professional competence—particularly their English reading ability and skills in retrieving professional literature—may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of AI technologies and the formation of positive attitudes.

Nurses with higher MNGQ scores tended to demonstrate more open and adaptive generational characteristics, which were associated with more positive attitudes toward AI. These nurses generally demonstrate greater openness and inclusiveness in the context of teamwork and technological innovation, and thus exhibit higher sensitivity and receptivity to new technologies, making them more likely to be categorized into the “positive acceptance” group. They typically possess stronger technological adaptability, greater motivation for learning, and a more pronounced innovative orientation, along with higher expectations regarding the potential of AI to enhance work efficiency. In contrast, nurses with lower MNGQ scores tended to be more vigilant about issues such as role substitution and patient safety that may accompany AI implementation, rendering them more likely to fall into the “ambivalent balance” or “cautious skepticism” groups. In addition, differences in values and professional identity across generations may lead to divergent evaluations of the functional benefits and potential risks of AI. Accordingly, the development of smart nursing systems should fully consider generational differences and implement stratified training strategies to enhance nurses’ technological confidence and willingness to use AI.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size met the requirements for multivariable analyses, the overall sample size (n = 206) remains relatively limited for LPA, particularly when identifying smaller subgroups. In the selected three-profile model, the smallest profile comprised 9.71% of the sample (n = 20), which may affect the stability of classification. Additionally, direct comparisons between the “positive acceptance” and “ambivalent balance” groups were not conducted due to small subgroup sizes, which may limit the stability of estimates. Future studies should increase the sample size to ensure adequate representation across different subgroups. Second, participants were recruited from a single province in Anhui Province, China, which restricts the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Future research should adopt a multicenter, stratified random sampling approach, encompassing eastern, central, and western regions of China and including nurses from tertiary, secondary, and community hospitals. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture dynamic changes in attitudes over time.Future research could use a longitudinal design to explore how attitudes toward AI change among different subgroups of nurses.

Conclusions

Using LPA, this study classified nurses’ attitudes toward the application of AI in nursing into three latent profiles—positive acceptance, ambivalent balance, and cautious skepticism—revealing significant heterogeneity in nurses’ attitudes toward AI. Overall, nurses’ attitudes toward AI applications in nursing were at a moderate level, indicating substantial room for improvement. The advancement of smart nursing initiatives should pay close attention to intra-professional differences in attitudes and establish stratified training systems to enhance nurses’ willingness to adopt and utilize AI technologies, thereby promoting the safe, efficient, and sustainable development of AI in the nursing field. In addition, the study sample was limited to Anhui Province, which may introduce regional constraints, and the cross-sectional design precludes the examination of longitudinal trends and dynamic changes in nurses’ attitudes toward AI applications. Future research should expand the sample to include multiple regions in order to improve the generalizability of the findings.
