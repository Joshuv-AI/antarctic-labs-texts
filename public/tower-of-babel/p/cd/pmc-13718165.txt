Background

Burden of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs)

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) are mucosal lesions and conditions associated with an increased risk of progression to oral cancer, particularly oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). The most common OPMDs include oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), leukoplakia, oral lichen planus, and erythroplakia [1]. Globally, OPMDs affect approximately 1–5% of the general population. Prevalence is higher in South and Southeast Asia, where tobacco and areca nut use are widespread [1].

In India alone, the prevalence of OSMF is estimated at over five million individuals. Leukoplakia affects approximately 1% of adults. Together, these conditions make OPMDs a significant public health burden [7].

These disorders often present with chronic mucosal changes. OSMF is associated with restricted mouth opening. Leukoplakia appears as white keratotic plaques. Erythroplakia presents as erythematous patches. Lichen planus shows reticular mucosal changes.

Clinical progression varies among these conditions. However, the potential for malignant transformation is well established. Erythroplakia and OSMF carry higher transformation risks, exceeding 7–15%. Leukoplakia and lichen planus have lower but still significant rates of progression [22].

Role of tobacco and areca nut

The primary risk factors for OPMDs include tobacco use (smoked and smokeless) and areca nut chewing, commonly consumed as betel quid. These substances contain carcinogenic alkaloids and nitrosamines that contribute to epithelial dysplasia and DNA damage [4].

Areca nut, in particular, contains arecoline, an alkaloid that stimulates fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, contributing to the pathogenesis of OSMF [5] Combined use of tobacco and areca nut is associated with even higher malignant transformation rates.

Despite widespread awareness of the health risks, cessation of these addictive habits is difficult due to cultural acceptance, social reinforcement, and physiological dependence. Conventional cessation programs have primarily targeted smoking cessation in high-income countries, with less emphasis on smokeless tobacco and areca nut users in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the burden of OPMDs and oral cancer is greatest [21].

Nurse-led interventions in cessation

Nurses play a pivotal role in public health, preventive care, and patient education. This positions them as key facilitators of behavioral interventions. Primarily nurse-driven, with optional multidisciplinary support.(nurses take the lead in delivering the intervention, even if they work within a team setting.)

Their accessibility, frequent patient contact, and trusted role in communities uniquely position them to deliver cessation programs [18].

Evidence from smoking cessation trials demonstrates that nurse-led interventions, including brief advice, structured behavioral counseling, motivational interviewing (MI), and referral for nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), significantly improve quit rates compared to usual care [11].

In the context of OPMDs, nurses may provide additional benefits beyond cessation. They can conduct lesion surveillance, reinforce oral hygiene practices, and support patients in coping with functional impairments caused by mucosal disease.

Nurse-led group sessions and peer-support models may also address cultural and psychosocial barriers associated with areca nut and tobacco use, particularly in resource-limited settings were physicians and dentists face time constraints.

However, despite promising evidence in general tobacco cessation, the role of nurse-led interventions in OPMD-specific population remains underexplored.. While some trials in South Asia have shown favorable results for abstinence and lesion regression, findings have not yet been systematically synthesized to inform clinical guidelines and health policy.

Rationale for the review

OPMDs represent a critical window of opportunity for oral cancer prevention. If cessation is achieved at this stage, lesion regression and reduced malignant transformation are possible, directly lowering oral cancer incidence [22].

Yet, most cessation interventions continue to be led by physicians or dentists, leaving an underutilized opportunity for task-sharing with nurses.

OPMDs impose a substantial burden in LMICs, where specialist providers are limited. Therefore, it is important to assess whether nurse-delivered cessation programs can help bridge this service gap. Synthesizing the available evidence can:

Aim and objective

This systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of nurse-led tobacco and areca nut cessation interventions in adults with OPMDs. The primary objectives are to assess the impact of these interventions on abstinence (biochemically verified), lesion regression/progression, and malignant transformation. Secondary objectives include evaluating the effects on quality of life, adherence, knowledge and attitudes, adverse events, and cost-effectiveness.

By synthesizing current evidence, this review seeks to inform clinical practice, guide policy development in high-burden regions, and contribute to the global agenda of oral cancer prevention through strengthened nursing roles.

Methods

Given the inclusion of mixed study designs, a formal Summary of Findings table with pooled certainty ratings was not generated; instead, certainty considerations were integrated narratively alongside risk-of-bias assessments.

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [16]. The protocol was registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO,registration number CRD420251136386).

The review methodology was guided by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [10].

Eligibility criteria

We included studies enrolling adults (≥ 18 years) with clinically and/or histopathologically diagnosed oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) who were current or recent users of tobacco and/or areca nut. Studies were required to evaluate nurse-led cessation interventions and report abstinence or lesion-related outcomes.

Studies were excluded if they involved non-OPMD populations, pediatric participants, established oral cancer at baseline, or interventions delivered solely by non-nurse providers. Detailed eligibility criteria are presented in Table 1.

Characteristics of included studies

Quality assurance

All screening and extraction steps were documented using PRISMA 2020-compliant tracking logs. The process ensured reproducibility and transparency, aligning with the Cochrane Handbook (Version 6.3) guidance for non-meta-analytic reviews.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers. Randomized controlled trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool Version 2 (RoB 2), which examines bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results.

Non-randomized studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I, evaluating bias due to confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting.

Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Results

A quantitative meta-analysis was initially considered but was not performed due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Differences were observed in diagnostic criteria, intervention characteristics, outcome measures, and follow-up durations.

Given these inconsistencies, statistical pooling was not appropriate. Characteristics of included studies

Eighteen studies were included, encompassing 7,856 participants across India, Sri Lanka, Hong Kong, and the United Kingdom (see Table 2). Most studies (n = 10) were randomized controlled trials, five were quasi-experimental, and three were cohort designs. Sample sizes ranged from 180 to 820 participants, predominantly adults diagnosed with oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) such as oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF), leukoplakia, or mixed lesions. Interventions varied but were consistently nurse-led, including brief advice, behavioral counseling, motivational interviewing (MI), group education sessions, and referral for nicotine replacement therapy (NRT). Comparators generally involved usual physician or dentist care, pamphlets, or minimal advice.

Primary outcomes across studies were biochemically verified abstinence and lesion regression, with follow-up durations between 6 and 24 months. Secondary outcomes included oral health–related quality of life (QoL), adherence, knowledge, attitudes, and cost-effectiveness. Notably, studies from India and Sri Lanka demonstrated strong contextual applicability for areca-nut–associated OPMDs, while UK and Hong Kong trials emphasized behavioral components and QoL improvement. Overall, most trials reported superior abstinence and lesion regression rates in nurse-led groups compared with usual care, confirming the effectiveness and feasibility of nursing interventions across clinical and community settings.

Most randomized controlled trials reported abstinence rates between 28–52% in nurse-led groups compared with 12–25% in usual care. Lesion regression ranged from 46–65% among intervention participants. Findings were consistent across quasi-experimental and cohort studies, demonstrating improved cessation and lesion outcomes in nurse-led arms.

Risk of bias summary by study

Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was judged as low to moderate risk of bias (see Table 3). Most randomized controlled trials demonstrated low risk across domains, particularly in randomization, measurement, and reporting. Quasi-experimental and cohort studies using ROBINS-I generally showed some concerns due to non-random allocation and incomplete adjustment for confounding variables. Across all studies, deviations from intended interventions and missing data were minimal, while outcome assessment was well standardized, often involving biochemical verification or clinical examination. No study was rated as high risk in any domain, indicating overall acceptable internal validity for the synthesized evidence.

Rationale for risk-of-bias judgments

Across the 18 included studies, overall methodological quality ranged from low to moderate risk of bias (see Table 4. Randomized controlled trials generally demonstrated strong methodological rigor, with appropriate random sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinded assessment. Most quasi-experimental and cohort studies showed some concerns, primarily due to non-random allocation, moderate attrition, or incomplete adjustment for confounders. Outcome measurements—particularly abstinence verification and lesion evaluation—were well standardized, often involving biochemical or independent clinical assessments. Reporting bias was minimal, and selective outcome omission was rare. Overall, the evidence base was methodologically robust, supporting high confidence in the synthesized qualitative findings.

Overview of the synthesis approach

Given the heterogeneity in study designs, intervention types, outcome measures, and follow-up durations, a narrative synthesis approach was undertaken instead of a quantitative meta-analysis. The synthesis followed guidance from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Version 6.3) and recommendations by Popay et al. (2006) for structured narrative synthesis. Studies were grouped and summarized thematically according to the type of nurse-led intervention, study design, and reported outcomes (abstinence, lesion regression/progression (Lesion regression: Reduction in lesion size, severity, or histopathological grade. Synthesis of primary outcomes

Abstinence outcomes

Across studies, nurse-led interventions consistently demonstrated higher abstinence rates than control or usual care groups. Among RCTs, abstinence verification using biochemical markers (cotinine, CO, salivary thiocyanate) was performed in 9 of 10 trials, ensuring robust outcome validity. Reported cessation rates ranged from 28 to 52% in nurse-led groups, compared to 12% to 25% in controls. Quasi-experimental and cohort studies reported similar trends, with significant self-reported abstinence sustained at 6–24 months.

Key facilitators of success included repeated follow-ups, individualized counseling, and culturally tailored messages emphasizing oral health and cancer prevention. Intervention intensity (number and duration of sessions) positively correlated with abstinence success.

Lesion regression and progression

Fourteen studies evaluated lesion outcomes using clinical or histopathological assessments. Nurse-led interventions resulted in greater lesion regression (complete or partial improvement) in 46–65% of participants compared to 25–40% in usual care. Lesion progression or worsening was notably reduced in intervention arms.

Three long-term cohort studies reported lower—but not statistically significant—rates of malignant transformation in nurse-led groups, primarily due to limited sample size and follow-up durations (< 3 years). Nonetheless, consistent patterns suggested potential long-term benefit through sustained cessation and early lesion monitoring by nurses.

Synthesis of secondary outcomes

Oral Health–Related Quality of Life (OHIP-14, GOHAI)

Six studies reported significant post-intervention improvements in oral function, comfort, and psychosocial domains. Nurse counseling focused on habit substitution, symptom relief, and coping mechanisms for mucosal discomfort, which contributed to measurable QoL enhancement.

Knowledge, attitudes, and adherence

Nearly all studies demonstrated increased knowledge regarding the link between tobacco/areca nut use and oral cancer. Participants reported greater self-efficacy and motivation to quit after repeated nurse interactions. Adherence to cessation protocols improved when nurses provided telephonic follow-up or digital reminders.

Adverse events and cost-effectiveness

Minor adverse events related to NRT (mouth irritation, sleep disturbance) were reported in < 5% of participants and were transient. Cost-effectiveness analyses (n = 2 studies) showed that nurse-led programs achieved comparable abstinence rates to physician-led clinics at 40–60% lower cost per quitter, emphasizing scalability in low-resource settings.

Cross-study patterns and contextual insights

Across all intervention types, nurse-patient rapport and continuity of care emerged as critical determinants of success. Nurses were able to integrate cessation counseling into routine care (antenatal clinics, dental check-ups, community outreach), increasing program reach and acceptability. Thematic synthesis highlighted that trust, empathy, and repeated reinforcement by nurses played a greater motivational role than brief physician consultations.

Cultural adaptation was essential: interventions using local languages, community peer champions, and culturally relevant analogies demonstrated higher engagement. Importantly, nurse-led programs also contributed to early detection of lesion changes and improved referral compliance.

Limitations of the synthesis

Due to heterogeneity in study design, population characteristics, intervention intensity, and outcome measures, quantitative pooling (meta-analysis) was not appropriate. Variability in follow-up duration (6–36 months) and reliance on self-reported data in some studies also introduced uncertainty.

Potential publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies with positive findings may be more likely to be published. Additionally, most included studies were concentrated in South and Southeast Asia, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings.

Several studies were at moderate risk of bias, particularly non-randomized designs with potential confounding and self-reported outcomes. Despite these limitations, the overall direction of evidence favored nurse-led interventions.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 18 studies—comprising randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, and cohort designs—on the effectiveness of nurse-led cessation interventions in adults with oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs). The findings demonstrate consistent evidence that nurse-delivered interventions significantly improve abstinence rates from tobacco and areca nut use compared to usual care or interventions delivered by other providers. Additionally, participants receiving nurse-led care showed higher rates of lesion regression and lower lesion progression, indicating a potential role in reducing the risk of malignant transformation.

Across diverse healthcare settings, nurse-led interventions—particularly those incorporating behavioral counseling, motivational interviewing (MI), and structured follow-up—proved effective in promoting sustained cessation. The evidence indicates that the continuity of care and trust-based communication between nurses and patients are critical to success. Moreover, the majority of included studies reported improvements in oral health–related quality of life, knowledge, and adherence, suggesting that nurse-led programs have broader psychosocial benefits beyond cessation alone.

Importantly, the results align with the theoretical underpinnings of behavioral change models such as the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) and Health Belief Model (HBM), which emphasize individualized support, perceived susceptibility, and reinforcement. Nurses, with their proximity to patients and holistic training, are particularly well-suited to implement these approaches in both clinical and community contexts.

Comparison with existing literature (Nurse vs. Physician/Dentist Roles)

Previous reviews have largely focused on physician- or dentist-led cessation interventions, with limited attention to the specific role of nurses. Physicians and dentists often face time constraints and competing clinical demands, which can limit their ability to provide intensive behavioral support. In contrast, nurses have more frequent and sustained contact with patients during follow-up visits, preventive checkups, and community programs, enhancing accessibility and continuity of care.

Consistent with earlier Cochrane Reviews [6, 18], nurse-led interventions demonstrate quit rates comparable to physician-led programs, with improved adherence attributed to greater accessibility and patient engagement. This review extends these findings by highlighting their effectiveness in smokeless tobacco and areca nut cessation among OPMD populations, where cultural and psychosocial factors play a significant role.

In many South and Southeast Asian settings, nurses serve as the first point of contact in primary care. Integrating cessation counseling into routine responsibilities—such as oral screening and chronic disease management—supports task-shifting and helps bridge service gaps. This combined clinical and preventive role enables nurses to contribute to both habit cessation and early detection of lesion progression.

While dentist-led care primarily emphasizes clinical management, nurse-led approaches focus on behavioral counseling, motivation, and habit monitoring. Together, within interdisciplinary teams, these complementary roles can strengthen oral health service delivery and improve prevention outcomes.

Implications for lesion regression and oral cancer prevention

One of the most striking findings of this review is the link between habit cessation and lesion regression. Several studies included in this synthesis demonstrated that sustained abstinence—achieved through nurse-led support—was associated with measurable improvement in lesion size, texture, and histopathological grade. These findings reaffirm that cessation not only halts further mucosal damage but may also reverse early dysplastic changes, thereby delaying or preventing malignant transformation.

Although long-term data on cancer incidence remain scarce, cohort studies within this review suggest a downward trend in transformation risk among intervention recipients. This underscores the preventive oncology value of nursing interventions, particularly in regions with high OPMD prevalence. Nurses’ involvement in lesion monitoring, reinforcement of follow-up visits, and referral for biopsy or specialist evaluation enhances the continuity of preventive care.

At the population level, incorporating nurse-led cessation into national oral cancer control strategies could yield significant reductions in morbidity and mortality, given that oral cancer in India and neighboring countries accounts for nearly one-third of global cases [7]. Early intervention during the OPMD phase represents a critical window to intercept disease progression.

Public health importance (India, Southeast Asia, Global Relevance)

Compared with physician- or dentist-led approaches, nurse-led interventions provide greater continuity of care and sustained behavioral support. In high-burden regions such as India and Southeast Asia, nurses often serve as first-line providers in primary and community healthcare settings, making them highly accessible to patients.

Integrating cessation counseling into routine nursing practice supports task-shifting and enables scalable, cost-effective service delivery. This is particularly important in regions where OPMDs are prevalent and the use of areca nut and smokeless tobacco is culturally embedded. Despite existing tobacco control policies, cessation uptake remains low due to limited access to trained counselors and social stigma.

Task-shifting to nurses and community health workers offers a practical solution to expand cessation coverage in resource-limited settings. By leveraging the existing nursing workforce across primary health centers, dental clinics, and outreach programs, behavioral interventions can be scaled without major infrastructural investment.

These findings align with global frameworks such as the World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control and the Global Oral Health Action Plan, which emphasize integrating tobacco cessation into primary healthcare. Nurse-led models also promote health equity by reaching underserved populations, including rural communities and women.

Overall, the accessibility, empathetic approach, and preventive focus of nurses strengthen patient engagement and support sustained behavioral change, reinforcing their key role in public health interventions.

Strengths and limitations of the review

A major strength of this review lies in its comprehensive inclusion of diverse study designs—RCTs, quasi-experimental studies, and cohorts—capturing the real-world applicability of nurse-led interventions. The use of established appraisal tools (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, CASP) ensured methodological rigor, and inclusion of both behavioral and lesion-level outcomes provides a holistic understanding of intervention impact.

Unlike prior meta-analyses limited to smoking cessation in general populations, this review uniquely focused on OPMD-specific outcomes, bridging a significant evidence gap in oral cancer prevention literature. The cross-context comparison of Asian and Western studies further enhances its generalizability.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity in intervention intensity, duration, and outcome measures precluded meta-analysis, restricting the synthesis to qualitative interpretation. Second, some quasi-experimental studies relied on self-reported abstinence without biochemical verification, introducing potential reporting bias. Third, follow-up durations were often limited to ≤ 24 months, insufficient to fully capture malignant transformation risk. Finally, publication bias cannot be excluded, as grey literature may include small-scale or negative studies not published in indexed journals.

Despite these limitations, the overall consistency of findings across designs strengthens confidence in the conclusion that nurse-led interventions are both effective and feasible in OPMD management.

The absence of meta-analysis limits quantitative effect estimation; however, this reflects genuine heterogeneity in OPMD definitions, intervention intensity, outcome measurement, and follow-up duration across studies.

Future directions and policy integration

Future research should prioritize longitudinal, multi-center randomized trials with standardized outcome measures, including biochemical verification and histopathological follow-up, to establish causal pathways between cessation and lesion regression. Additionally, the integration of digital and telehealth platforms offers promising avenues for scaling nurse-led interventions. Mobile apps, WhatsApp-based reminders, and tele-counseling could extend reach, particularly in rural or underserved areas.

There is also a need to develop nursing-specific training modules on oral health counseling, lesion recognition, and communication skills tailored for cessation support. Collaborative frameworks involving nurses, dentists, and oncologists should be institutionalized under national oral health and tobacco control programs.

Policy-wise, governments in high-burden countries should consider incorporating nurse-led cessation into National Cancer Control Plans and Primary Health Care (PHC) initiatives, supported by funding for nurse training, supervision, and outcome monitoring. Furthermore, future systematic reviews may employ mixed-methods approaches to capture qualitative dimensions—such as patient experience, cultural perceptions, and nurse empowerment—that quantitative data alone cannot convey.

Conclusion of the discussion

This review shows that nurse-led cessation interventions improve both behavioral and lesion-related outcomes in OPMDs. Nurses play an important role in combining cessation support with lesion monitoring across care settings. These findings support the adoption of nurse-led models as scalable strategies for oral cancer prevention.

Conclusions

This systematic review suggests that nurse-led tobacco and areca-nut cessation interventions may contribute to improved abstinence rates and lesion-related outcomes among adults with oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), including oral submucous fibrosis, leukoplakia, lichen planus, and erythroplakia. Across the included randomized and quasi-experimental studies, nurse-delivered behavioral counseling, motivational interviewing, and follow-up support were generally associated with reductions in tobacco and areca-nut use.

The reviewed studies also indicated potential improvements in oral health–related quality of life, adherence, and patient knowledge, with few reported adverse effects. However, the evidence base remains limited by variations in study design, intervention components, outcome measures, and follow-up duration. Furthermore, evidence regarding malignant transformation and long-term clinical outcomes is insufficient to draw firm conclusions.

The findings highlight the potential role of nurses in supporting cessation and surveillance efforts within oral health and primary care settings. Integrating nurse-led approaches into oral cancer prevention and tobacco control programs may be beneficial, particularly in regions with a high burden of tobacco and areca-nut use. Nevertheless, these implications should be interpreted cautiously given the limitations of the available evidence.

Future research should focus on large-scale, longitudinal studies using standardized outcome measures, cost-effectiveness analyses, and evaluation of digital-health–supported interventions. Additional research is needed to strengthen the evidence base and clarify the long-term impact of nurse-led interventions on OPMD progression and oral cancer prevention.
