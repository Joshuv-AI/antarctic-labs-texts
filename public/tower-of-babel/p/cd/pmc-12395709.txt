Introduction

Sturge-Weber syndrome (SWS) is a rare congenital phacomatosis disorder characterized by a port-wine birthmark, glaucoma, and leptomeningeal angiomatosis [1]. The most common ocular manifestations of SWS are glaucoma, affecting 30%−70% of patients, and choroidal hemangiomas (CH), occurring in 40%−50% of cases. Additional ocular abnormalities may include vascular lesions on the eyelid, abnormal vascular lesions of the conjunctiva, episcleral hemangiomas, and dilated retinal vessels [2]. Choroidal hemangiomas can present as either circumscribed or diffuse. Diffuse choroidal hemangiomas are consistently associated with SWS, while circumscribed CH, which is not linked to any local or systemic anomalies, can occasionally occur in SWS patients [3].

Recently, optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) has emerged as a novel tool for visualizing the in vivo vasculature of the fundus, particularly in the outer retina and choroid. The advancements in swept-source OCTA (SS-OCTA), which feature a higher scan speed and a 1050-nm laser wavelength, offer depth-resolved flow information, surpassing the capabilities of earlier generations [4, 5]the Wide Angle Scanning Mode facilitates a comprehensive view of the fundus. SS-OCTA has proven to be a valuable diagnostic aid in identifying retinal and choroidal tumors [6, 7].

The present study reports a case ultimately diagnosed as SWS. Ultra-widefield SS-OCTA imaging revealed a choroidal lesion identified as a circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CH), with a corresponding dark area within the affected region of the choroid.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old female complained of vision loss and visual fatigue in her right eye for seven months. She has had a Port-Wine birthmark on the upper lid of her right eye since childhood but denied any prior history of ophthalmological diseases. Her best-corrected visual acuity was measured at 20/25 in the right eye and 20/50 in the left eye. Her intraocular pressure was normal in both eyes. Examination revealed dilated and abnormally tortuous conjunctival blood vessels in the right eye (Fig. 1) and lens opacity in the left eye. No other notable abnormalities were identified during the anterior segment evaluation.

Ultra-widefield fundus photography (UWFP; Optos, Daytona, Scotland, UK) revealed dilated choroidal blood vessels in the right eye, which appeared more prominent in the red-free light image. Additionally, spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), using the enhanced depth imaging (EDI) scan, demonstrated significant thickening of the subfoveal choroid in the right eye (Fig. 2). In contrast, no notable abnormalities were observed on UWFP or SD-OCT in the left eye.

The patient underwent widefield and ultra-widefield SS-OCTA scanning to evaluate retinal and choroidal vessels. SS-OCTA images were obtained in 6 × 6 mm, 12 × 12 mm, and ultra-widefield 26 × 21 mm formats (VG200, SVision Imaging, Ltd., Luoyang, China). The ultra-widefield SS-OCTA imaging revealed a large choroidal hemangioma, with the B-scan images clearly delineating its full boundary (Fig. 3). The 26 × 21 mm ultra-widefield SS-OCTA images effectively captured the complete border of the choroidal hemangioma. The measured area of the hemangioma was 41.345 mm², with an average thickness of 885 μm, while the thickest part reached a thickness of 1118.5 μm (Fig. 4). Enhanced angiography in the 12 × 12 mm and 6 × 6 mm widefield SS-OCTA images highlighted dark regions with an abnormal vascular pattern characteristic of the choroidal hemangioma (Fig. 5).

The patient ultimately underwent a head magnetic resonance examination, which revealed no evidence of pial angiomas or other cerebrovascular malformations. Taking into account the patient’s history of a port-wine birthmark on her right eyelid, along with the findings from this visit, she was diagnosed with Sturge-Weber syndrome (type II) based on the established diagnostic criteria [1]. As her right eye maintained good visual acuity and no signs of exudative retinal detachment were detected, regular follow-up was recommended.

Discussion

CH is a common ocular manisfetation of SWS and can result in choroidal thickening [8]. Literature indicates that the choroid plays a crucial role in retinal nutrition, and thickening of the choroid in patients with CH can lead to dysfunction. This occurs because abnormalities in blood volume and flow disrupt the delicate balance between the choroid and retina, ultimately causing thinning of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)-photoreceptor layer [9]. Furthermore, complications such as serous retinal detachment, subretinal hemorrhage, subretinal fluid, and cystoid macular edema are frequently associated with CH, often resulting in visual impairment. Due to the diffuse and indistinct margins of these tumors, they are commonly diagnosed late and left untreated unless they cause symptomatic changes [10]. Therefore, early diagnosis and careful follow-up of choroidal hemangiomas are essential.

The clinical diagnosis of asymptomatic CH can be quite challenging [10]. Previous diagnostic techniques include fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) and indocyanine green angiography (ICGA). ICGA provides a clearer visualization of choroidal blood vessels compared to FFA, offering a more precise depiction of choroidal circulation, and regarded as the more convincing method for diagnosing CH [11]. However, this procedure is invasive and it may also bring some risks, such as an allergic reaction to the contrast agent [12]. Hence, OCT/OCTA’s non-invasiveness becomes its key advantage in detecting CH. Previous studies have demonstrated that SS-OCT achieves a higher detection rate for CH compared to FFA and ICGA [13]. Ultra-widefield SS-OCTA not only demonstrates superior scanning depth and coverage but also enables blood flow imaging, making it particularly valuable for diagnosing vascular abnormalities such as CH. In addition, OCTA enables layer-specific segmentation of the retina, a capability unattainable with conventional dye-based angiography systems [14]. Additionally, ocular ultrasonography holds diagnostic value for CH, typically demonstrating a thickened choroid with pronounced internal reflectivity [15]. However, compared to ocular ultrasonography, SS-OCTA provides comprehensive visualization of the entire CH lesion. Furthermore, ocular ultrasonography may fail to capture the intricate vascular architecture of the hemangioma [15].

Previous studies have characterized the OCTA features of CH. Mirzayev et al. [14]. described it as “a mixed reflective internal tumor structure featuring intertwined and irregularly organized large-caliber vessels mixed with signal void areas,” while Konana et al. [12]. reported characteristic findings including: (1) irregularly arranged choroidal vessels in both superficial and deeper choroidal slabs; (2) club-like vascular structures in deeper slabs, more prominent in active tumors; and (3) signal void areas in deeper slabs, predominantly observed in dormant tumors. Abnormal vascular patterns, like worm-like, spaghetti-shaped, and club-like also reported [15]. Using ultra-widefield SS-OCTA, we identified the patient’s lesion as a CH, with the imaging vividly revealing its extent, depth, and internal vascular structure. Although lacking supporting evidence from ICGA, the ultra-widefield SS-OCTA images comprehensively demonstrated the tumor’s full profile in both transverse and longitudinal sections, exhibiting characteristic OCTA features of CH. This confirms the diagnosis as choroidal hemangioma. Furthermore, the patient’s additional clinical features (such as eyelid port-wine stain and conjunctival vascular dilation) support the diagnosis of SWS, which in turn reinforces the identification of CH. Previous cases have demonstrated successful diagnosis of choroidal hemangioma using SS-OCTA alone without ICGA confirmation. Furthermore, SS-OCTA has been successfully applied in monitoring therapeutic response in CH [14]. Nevertheless, further studies are still required to establish the substitutability of SS-OCTA for ICGA in the diagnosis of CH. When diagnosing CH using SS-OCTA, differentiation should be made from other amelanotic tumors such as amelanotic choroidal melanoma, choroidal metastatic tumors, and lymphoma. On SS-OCTA imaging, amelanotic choroidal melanoma typically presents with ill-defined margins, showing mixed hypo-reflective tumor clusters in both the choriocapillaris layer and outer retina. The tumor vasculature demonstrates hypo-reflective changes in the central region while exhibiting hyper-reflective variations in more peripheral vascular portions [6]. Metastatic tumors appear as poorly demarcated lesions with hypo-reflective areas within generally hyper-reflective tumors, where the choriocapillaris layer reveals vascular networks of varying caliber along with vascular loops and anastomoses [6]. Xu et al. [16]. observed that primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL) manifests hyperflow signals corresponding to vertical hyperreflective lesions at the level of the deep retinal capillary plexus on SS-OCTA, which they proposed as a potential biomarker for PVRL.

Our findings demonstrate that ultra-widefield SS-OCTA provides a more comprehensive visualization of the CH, including the scleral-choroid interface, despite the significant thickness of the choroid. Furthermore, the device’s ability to measure parameters such as choroidal area, thickness, and CVI enhances our capacity to characterize hemangiomas in greater detail. These measurements enable better monitoring of CH progression and quantitative analysis of treatment efficacy.

The CH in this patient was classified as circumscribed due to its well-defined boundaries. An sudden transition from irregular arranged choroidal vessels to regularly normal arranged vessels can be observed at the margin of tumor, which was in line with findings of Konana et al. [12]. Enhanced angiographic imaging of the choriocapillaris and deeper choroid revealed more pronounced dark areas. This observation aligns with previous report of Takkar et al. [17], suggesting an abnormal vascular pattern in CH. Certainly, we must acknowledge that artifacts in OCTA can also contribute to the presentation of “dark areas,” such as pigmentation, segmentation errors, and shadowing from overlying structures [17]. Therefore, we manually corrected the automatically segmented images generated by the software, and the built-in software of SS-OCTA significantly reduced errors caused by these factors when generating en face images. This phenomenon may indicate an increase in the interstitial component of CHs accompanied by a reduction in blood flow, particularly in patients with SWS, when compared to normal choroidal tissue. However, a histopathologic study of CH in SWS patients revealed that these hemangiomas are primarily composed of cavernous, capillary, or mixed vascular types, with cavernous types being the predominant form, while most of the remaining cases fall under the mixed subtype [18]. The mixed vascular subtype displays varying blood flow rates, ranging from extremely slow to exceedingly fast, both of which can manifest as dark areas on imaging [19]. Additionally, the choriocapillaris often appears sclerotic, obliterated, and occasionally calcified [20]. This could provide an explanation for the observed reduction in blood flow signals within the CH.

Several treatments for CH have been explored, including external beam radiotherapy, laser photocoagulation, proton beam therapy, targeted radiation therapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and anti-VEGF injections [2, 10]. Since the patient exhibited none of the typical choroidal hemangioma complications (e.g., serous retinal detachment or macular edema), active treatment was not indicated in this case.

There were several limitations. First, the absence of ancillary imaging modalities such as ocular ultrasonography and ICGA may reduce diagnostic certainty, although the SS-OCTA findings were conclusive in our case. Second, choroidal thickening may lead to segmentation artifacts in SS-OCTA imaging, requiring manual adjustment of retinal-choroidal boundaries. Most importantly, there remains no established consensus on diagnostic criteria for choroidal tumors using SS-OCTA, and its standalone diagnostic capability for choroidal hemangioma requires further validation through larger prospective studies. These technical considerations highlight the need for continued research to define the precise role of SS-OCTA in the evaluation of choroidal tumors, particularly in comparison with conventional multimodal imaging approaches.

In conclusion, ultra-widefield SS-OCTA demonstrates significant diagnostic value for CH and serves as a particularly valuable adjunctive tool for diagnosing SWS in cases without glaucoma. Furthermore, ultra-widefield SS-OCTA enables precise determination of the diameter and extent of CH involvement, aiding in the selection of appropriate treatment options. This capability is crucial for gaining deeper insights into the structural characteristics of CH, monitoring disease progression, and evaluating treatment effectiveness.
