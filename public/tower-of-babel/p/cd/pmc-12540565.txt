The Impact of Breastfeeding on Maintaining Body Weight After Bariatric Surgery—A Retrospective Analysis of Maternal Outcomes of Bariatric Surgery and Pregnancy Study (MOMBARIS) Multicenter Data
Authors: Piotr Krzysztof Kowalewski, Maciej Walędziak, Piotr Małczak, Wojciech Kupczyk, Natalia Dowgiałło-Gornowicz, Anna Różańska-Walędziak, Piotr Major
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective obesity treatment. The increasing number of MBS among young patients has led to more post-bariatric pregnancies. Our study investigates how breastfeeding duration impacts postpartum weight retention and health outcomes for mothers and babies after MBS.MethodsMOMBARIS (Maternal Outcomes of Bariatric Surgery and Pregnancy Study) is a multicentre study. The population is derived from MOMBARIS 2, which focused on obstetric outcomes. It included patients who became pregnant following MBS, analysing pre-operative metrics, type of surgery, pregnancy details, and breastfeeding duration. Data analysis was conducted using Julius.ai.ResultsWe analysed 156 patients post-MBS, with 148 pregnancies leading to childbirth. Among them, 131 had laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), 12 underwent Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), and 5 had one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB). The mean age was 31.7 ± 5.45 years (range 19–48) with a pre-operative BMI of 43.9 kg/m2 ± 5.8 kg/m2 (range 31.9–68.8). The mean %EWL was 72% (± 28.6). Seventeen patients had multiple children. Breastfeeding results indicated that 116 patients (78.4%) initiated breastfeeding, with 40.5% continuing beyond 6 months. No significant differences were found in BMI metrics across breastfeeding groups.ConclusionsBariatric outcomes (%EWL, %TWL) show no statistical difference based on breastfeeding patterns, nor does breastfeeding significantly affect weight loss maintenance post-surgery. Additionally, delaying pregnancy after surgery does not guarantee more significant weight loss.

Introduction

Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) is widely recognised as the most effective treatment for obesity available today. This surgical intervention has the highest long-term success rate, which can be quantitatively measured by the percentage of excess weight loss (%EWL) and the remission of obesity-related co-morbidities [1, 2]. These improvements have been documented in adult and adolescent populations, demonstrating the procedure’s versatility and effectiveness across different age groups [3].

Previous research indicates that, in the general population, breastfeeding is linked to a modest decrease in postpartum weight retention. Evidence specific to women who have undergone metabolic-bariatric surgery (MBS) remains limited: aside from Rottenstreich et al., hardly any studies have measured postpartum weight changes in this group. Therefore, whether breastfeeding duration or intensity leads to better long-term outcomes such as %EWL or %TWL after MBS is still an open question that our study aims to resolve.

Much of the existing literature discusses the effects of MBS on pregnancy outcomes, breast milk composition, lactation, and neonatal well-being. Particular attention is given to assessing the benefits and risks of MBS for both the mother and child, occasionally considering the results of obesity treatment [4–7].

The complex interplay between pregnancy and postpartum recovery can significantly affect weight stability in this population. Our study, recognising the importance of this issue, aimed to investigate the potential impact of breastfeeding duration on postpartum weight retention and the overall weight-related outcomes following MBS. We sought to ascertain whether promoting breastfeeding could aid weight management during the crucial postpartum period, ultimately contributing to improved health outcomes for both mothers and their babies.

Aim

We aimed to ascertain whether breastfeeding children born after MBS influences maternal weight loss outcomes.

Materials and Methods

MOMBARIS Study

MOMBARIS, or the Maternal Outcomes of Bariatric Surgery and Pregnancy Study, is a multicenter retrospective study based on data from seven high-volume bariatric centers. MOMBARIS 1 evaluated the impact of previous pregnancies on bariatric outcomes. The data presented in this study derive from MOMBARIS 2, which focuses on obstetric outcomes following MBS.

Population

This study examined patients who became pregnant after undergoing MBS based on national and international criteria [8, 9]. We evaluated several factors: pre-operative weight, BMI, the specific type of surgery performed (laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (LRYGB), one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB)), the duration between surgery and conception, the number of pregnancies and births, weight and BMI at the outset of pregnancy, weight gain throughout pregnancy, weight loss post-birth, and breastfeeding status. We categorised breastfeeding status into three groups: none-breastfeeding, patients who breastfed for less than or more than 6 months.

A similar analysis of patients with more than one child was performed. Follow-up (“current/final”) was the most recent clinic visit occurring ≥ 12 months after the last recorded delivery. Measurement sources were from the clinic scale (79%) or patient self-report (21%).

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed with Julius.ai.

The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed the normality of the distribution.

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations for normal distribution or median and range for non-normal distribution.

A non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare outcomes across three breastfeeding groups (not breastfeeding, less than 6 months, and 6 months or more). The primary outcomes analysed included %EWL, total weight loss (%TWL), excess BMI loss (%EBMIL), and current BMI.

A chi-square test of independence was used to analyse the relationship between breastfeeding status and return to pre-pregnancy weight.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to quantify the linear relationship between the time from surgery to conception and the overall weight loss. The same test was used to evaluate the inter-pregnancy interval and the breastfeeding duration.

Success rates for bariatric outcomes were defined as achieving > 50% EWL. All statistical tests were performed with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Main Results

We identified 156 patients who conceived after MBS, with 148 pregnancies resulting in childbirth included in this study. Among these, 131 underwent LSG, 12 had LRYGB, and 5 had OAGB. The mean age was 31.7 years (range 19–48), and the mean pre-operative BMI was 43.9 kg/m2 (± 5.83). Sixty-eight percent had children before surgery. The median follow-up was 4 years after last childbirth. The mean %EWL of the study population was 72% (± 28.6). The median time from surgery to conception was 19 [IQR 12–34] months, and the median inter-pregnancy interval was 25 [IQR 15–41] months, with a weak negative correlation between conception timing and weight loss (Pearson coefficient: − 0.106). Seventeen patients had multiple childbirths. Breastfeeding-Related Results

In our study population, 116 patients initiated breastfeeding (78.4%). Among those who commenced breastfeeding, 47 (40.5%) continued beyond 6 months. There are no statistically significant differences between breastfeeding groups in BMI measurements (pre-op, post-op, pregnancy start, and childbirth). All groups demonstrated similar BMI trajectories throughout their surgical and pregnancy journeys.

Overall BMI change in various breastfeeding groups

When analysing records of second pregnancies, most mothers (70.6%) maintained their breastfeeding patterns from the first child to the second. Of those who breastfed for more than 6 months with their first child, 71.4% continued this practice. Among those who breastfed for less than 6 months, 62.5% sustained this duration. All mothers who did not breastfeed their first child also refrained from breastfeeding their second. Mothers who breastfed for over 6 months had shorter intervals between pregnancies, averaging 42 months. Conversely, those who breastfed for less than 6 months had longer intervals, averaging 73 months. Non-breastfeeding mothers experienced the shortest intervals, averaging 30 months. Shorter intervals between pregnancies are associated with longer breastfeeding duration (Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.459).

While 57.1% of breastfeeding mothers returned to pre-pregnancy weight after their second pregnancy, only 1.5% of non-breastfeeding mothers did (p < 0001). For the first pregnancy, 51.7% breastfeeding mothers returned to pre-pregnancy weight vs 40.6% in the non-breastfeeding group (p = 0.36).

Discussion

Few reports detail the effects of weight loss in women after childbirth. Available meta-analyses [10] indicate a possible association between breastfeeding and maternal weight loss during the postnatal period. Breastfeeding influenced postpartum weight loss (PPWR), particularly in primiparous women under 30 years of age who breastfed for more than 6 months; however, in women suffering from obesity, the effect of breastfeeding on PPWR was less pronounced. Similar conclusions regarding reduced postpartum weight gain were drawn from a group of over 700 mothers suffering from obesity with a prepartum BMI of 38.5 (± 3.3 BMI), where breastfeeding did not mitigate weight gain, unlike in breastfeeding mothers with a normal BMI [11].

On the other hand, depending on status, education and other factors, women with pre-pregnancy obesity tend to gain weight after pregnancy even if their pre-pregnancy BMI was within the normal range. Maintaining weight gain a year after the pregnancy is a risk factor for obesity in the future [12].

We identified only one study that assessed the long-term impact of pregnancy on weight loss after sleeve gastrectomy [13]. In comparison to our results, the median percentage of total body mass loss (%TWL) was 31% compared to 30% in the non-breastfeeding group, 32.5% for breastfeeding for less than 6 months, and 31% for breastfeeding over 6 months. Rottenstreich et al. report an EWL% of 72% compared to 69.3% in the non-breastfeeding group, 74.9% for breastfeeding for less than 6 months, and 70.4% for breastfeeding over 6 months. Similarly, multivariate analysis indicated that %EWL was not associated with the interval from surgery to conception, gestational weight gain, or breastfeeding. Our 52% at a longer follow-up corroborates but is slightly lower, possibly owing to higher baseline BMI.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to present an analysis of MBS outcomes taking into account the time between surgery and conception as well as the duration of breastfeeding.

Although breastfeeding duration correlated with shorter inter-pregnancy intervals, we did not detect an association with %TWL or %EWL. Therefore, delaying pregnancy should continue to be advised primarily for maternal–fetal safety rather than weight-loss optimization. Another noteworthy finding was that the average time from surgery to first conception was 23 months (± 18), with a weak negative correlation between conception timing and weight loss.

In preparing this discussion, we have conducted a thorough review of the literature and found no publications regarding the impact of postoperative breastfeeding on the weight loss effects of MBS. Given the prevalence of MBS among women of reproductive age and the lower breastfeeding rates in certain populations, this study presents unique results that address these concerns.

Study Limitations

This is a retrospective study; therefore, it may be affected by recall bias. We did not obtain detailed nutritional status and dietary habits during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Because breastfeeding duration was captured as a three-level ordinal field (< 6 months, ≥ 6 months, none) and exclusivity was not recorded, the analysis necessarily simplifies the complex spectrum of breastfeeding behavior. Our breastfeeding status was self-reported without objective verification, along with 21% of the weight measurements. We observed variable follow-up periods with follow-up ranging from 1–9 years, and we adjusted for follow-up length in multivariable models between patients. We could not control all potential confounders, such as dietary habits, physical activity, and socioeconomic factors. We acknowledge relatively small sample sizes in some subgroups, particularly OAGB (n = 5), multiple pregnancies after surgery, and long-term breastfeeding groups. The timing-of-childbirth stratification was constrained by sample size, especially in the bypass procedures. Eight women contributed ≥ 2 pregnancies and a mixed-effects model confirmed that clustering did not change the point estimates.

Conclusion

Based on this study's results, there is no statistical difference in bariatric outcomes based on breastfeeding patterns, and breastfeeding does not significantly influence weight loss after metabolic surgery. While breastfeeding was not independently associated with long-term %EWL or %TWL after adjustment, it remains beneficial for postpartum weight management and should be encouraged.
