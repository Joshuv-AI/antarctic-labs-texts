Targeting the noncanonical function of metabolic enzyme PHGDH in driving PD-L1 expression and cancer immune evasion
Authors: Juan Liu, Weiwei Wang, Yvonne Sun, Shan Huang, Arnav Borole, Haiyan Zheng, Wenwei Hu, Zhaohui Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), a rate-limiting enzyme in serine synthesis, is frequently overexpressed in cancers and promotes cancer progression. Its oncogenic role has been largely attributed to its enzymatic activity. Here, we uncover a critical noncanonical function of PHGDH in cancer; PHGDH upregulates PD-L1 expression to promote cancer immune evasion independently of its enzymatic function. Mechanistically, PHGDH binds to the serine/threonine kinase RAF1 and disrupts its interaction with 14-3-3, thereby activating RAF1 and its downstream MEK/ERK signaling to induce PD-L1 expression. Elevated PHGDH levels correlate with increased PD-L1 expression in clinical tumor samples. In preclinical mouse models, tumors with high PHGDH expression exhibit increased sensitivity to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Combining PHGDH inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade significantly improves antitumor effects compared to individual treatments. These results identify PHGDH as an important PD-L1 regulator, reveal a critical noncanonical mechanism underlying PHGDH’s oncogenic function, and propose a potential therapeutic strategy for cancers with PHGDH overexpression.

Introduction

Metabolic reprogramming plays a key role in promoting cancer progression, which is an attractive target for cancer therapy.1,2,3 In addition to enhanced glycolysis (the Warburg effect), the most well-known metabolic alteration in cancer, many cancers also exhibit reprogramming of amino acid metabolism.1,2,3 Amino acids serine and glycine (produced from serine) play a crucial role in the synthesis of proteins, nucleotides, lipids, and antioxidants, as well as in one-carbon metabolism.3,4 Many cancer cells exhibit enhanced serine synthesis to provide serine and glycine, supporting the rapid proliferation of cancer cells.3,4 Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) is the first rate-limiting enzyme in serine synthesis that produces 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate by the oxidation of 3-phosphoglycerate in cells.3,4,5 PHGDH is frequently overexpressed in many cancers, including ∼50% of colorectal cancer and ∼40% of breast cancer.5,6,7,8,9,10 Various mechanisms, including gene amplification, transcriptional activation by oncogenes or loss of tumor suppressors, and post-translational modifications (e.g., ubiquitination, palmitoylation, and methylation), have been reported to contribute to PHGDH overexpression in cancer.5,6,7,8,11 PHGDH overexpression has been reported to be associated with poor prognosis in many cancer types, including colorectal and breast cancers, and plays a critical role in promoting serine synthesis and cancer progression.5,6,7,8,9,10,12,13,14,15 PHGDH is being actively tested as a promising target for cancer therapy.5,6,7,8,9,10,12,16,17 Several small-molecule PHGDH inhibitors that specifically inhibit PHGDH enzymatic activity without affecting PHGDH levels have been developed, including NCT-503 and CBR-5884, which inhibit serine synthesis of cancer cells and suppress the progression of cancers with PHGDH overexpression in preclinical cancer models.5,7,8,9,16,17

Emerging evidence has revealed that many key metabolic enzymes exhibit noncanonical functions independent of their well-established traditional functions in metabolism, contributing significantly to cancer progression.18,19 For instance, hexokinase 2, a glycolytic enzyme that phosphorylates glucose to produce glucose-6-phosphate, can localize to the nucleus and interact with chromatin-associated regulators to enhance leukemic stem cell properties.20 The gluconeogenic enzyme fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1, which catalyzes hydrolysis of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to fructose 6-phosphate, can act as a protein phosphatase to dephosphorylate telomerase reverse transcriptase, preventing its nuclear translocation and promoting cellular senescence.21 Our recent study also showed that lactate dehydrogenase A, a glycolytic enzyme that converts pyruvate to lactate, interacts with the small GTPase Rac1 and activates it, promoting cancer progression.22 These findings highlight the importance of revealing the noncanonical functions of metabolic enzymes in cancer, which may offer opportunities for improved therapeutic strategies.

Cancer cells can activate multiple immunosuppressive pathways to evade destruction by the host immune system.23,24,25 One of the most well-characterized mechanisms involves PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1. As an immune checkpoint protein, PD-L1 binds to PD-1 on T cells, triggering inhibitory signaling pathways to suppress the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), thereby enabling cancer cells to evade immune surveillance.23,24,25 PD-L1 is frequently overexpressed in various cancers, playing a pivotal role in suppressing the anti-tumor immune responses.26,27 PD-L1 overexpression can be driven by a range of intrinsic and extrinsic factors in cancer, including genetic alterations, post-transcriptional and post-translational modifications, activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, and pro-inflammatory cytokines.26,27,28,29 Immune checkpoint blockade targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has emerged as one of the most widely used and successful strategies in cancer immunotherapy.26,27,30 Tumors with high PD-L1 expression are generally more responsive to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, and PD-L1 is currently indicated as a companion biomarker for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy.26,27,30 However, the clinical benefit of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is currently limited to approximately 20% of patients. Furthermore, PD-L1 expression is affected by many factors, and its dynamic and heterogeneous presence within tumors may compromise its reliability as a predictive biomarker.26,27,30 A better understanding of PD-L1 regulatory mechanisms in cancer, along with the development of robust biomarkers of therapeutic response, will be crucial for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.

While the oncogenic effects of PHGDH have been largely attributed to its enzymatic function in promoting serine synthesis to support the rapid proliferation of cancer cells, emerging evidence suggests that PHGDH is also involved in cancer immunity through its regulation of serine metabolism.10,31 However, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of PHGDH in cancer immunity, particularly through its noncanonical functions, remain poorly understood. In this study, we reveal a previously unrecognized non-metabolic mechanism by which PHGDH contributes to cancer progression and immune evasion. Employing liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)-based quantitative proteomic analysis, we identify PHGDH as a critical regulator of PD-L1. PHGDH drives PD-L1 expression by interacting with and activating RAF1, an upstream key serine/threonine kinase in the MEK/ERK signaling pathway,32,33,34,35 to activate the MEK/ERK signaling, which is independent of the metabolic function of PHGDH in serine synthesis. Our results further reveal that driving PD-L1-mediated immune evasion is a critical noncanonical mechanism of PHGDH in promoting cancer and suggest that combining PHGDH inhibitors that target PHGDH enzymatic activity with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade could be an effective therapeutic strategy for tumors with PHGDH overexpression.

Results

PHGDH enhances PD-L1 expression in cancer cells

Currently, the precise role and mechanisms of PHGDH in cancer immunity remain poorly understood. To investigate this, we first assessed the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH in subcutaneous colorectal tumors formed by MC38 and CT26, two widely used mouse colorectal cancer cell lines, and their corresponding cell lines with PHGDH knockout (KO) using the CRISPR-Cas9 technique, in immunocompetent C57BL/6 (for MC38) and BALB/c mice (for CT26), as well as in immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice. Notably, these two cancer cell lines displayed much higher PHGDH levels compared to normal mouse colon tissues (Figure 1A). Compared to control cells expressing wild-type (WT) PHGDH, PHGDH KO in MC38 and CT26 cells significantly reduced tumor growth in C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice, as well as in BALB/c nude mice (Figures 1B–1D). Notably, PHGDH KO displayed a much less pronounced inhibitory effect on MC38 and CT26 tumors in BALB/c nude mice, compared to tumors formed in C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice (Figures 1C and 1D). CD8+ CTLs are the principal mediators of antitumor immunity and eliminate malignant cells through the release of cytotoxic granules containing granzyme B (GzmB) and the production of effector cytokines such as interferon (IFN) γ.24,36 Notably, PHGDH KO significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration (Figures S1 and S2A) and increased the production of GzmB and IFNγ by CD8+ T cells (Figures S2B and 2C) in syngeneic MC38 and CT26 tumors. To identify the potential changes in immune-related pathways that may be modulated by PHGDH, we analyzed the expression patterns of the global protein profile following PHGDH KO in human RKO colorectal cancer cells by LC-MS/MS-based quantitative proteomic analysis. PD-L1 emerged as one of the most significantly downregulated proteins in RKO cells following PHGDH KO (Figure 1E). This result was confirmed by western blot analysis in various cancer cells, including RKO, Hs578T (human breast cancer cell), MC38, and CT26 cells. Notably, RKO and Hs578T cells also displayed much higher levels of PHGDH compared to normal human colon and breast tissues, respectively (Figure 1A). PHGDH KO in these cells greatly decreased the PD-L1 protein levels (Figure 1F). PHGDH knockdown by two different small hairpin RNA (shRNA) vectors also clearly decreased the PD-L1 protein levels in human colorectal cancer HCT116 cells and breast cancer SK-BR-3 cells (Figure 1G), both of which express high levels of PHGDH (Figure 1A). In contrast, ectopic expression of PHGDH-Flag by an expression vector markedly increased the PD-L1 protein levels in RKO and Hs578T cells (Figure 1H). PD-L1 functions only when localized to the cell membrane, where it interacts with PD-1.24,37 To assess functional PD-L1, flow cytometry-based PD-1-binding assays were performed in RKO and MC38 cells (Figure S3). Both cell-surface PD-L1 expression (Figures S3A and S3B) and PD-1 binding (Figures S3C and S3D) were reduced by PHGDH KO and enhanced by ectopic expression of PHGDH-Flag. Furthermore, LDH (lactate dehydrogenase)-based T cell killing assays, performed by co-culturing activated cytotoxic T cells with RKO cells or MC38-OVA cells expressing surface ovalbumin peptide, showed that PHGDH KO significantly enhanced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Figures S3E and S3F).

We further investigated whether PHGDH increases PD-L1 expression through mRNA regulation. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays showed that PD-L1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced by PHGDH KO or knockdown (Figures 1I and 1J) and increased by PHGDH-Flag expression (Figure 1K) in different cell lines. Consistently, western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining showed that PD-L1 protein levels were drastically reduced in tumors formed by MC38 and CT26 cells with PHGDH KO, compared to tumors formed by control cells (Figures 1L and 1M). These results suggest that PHGDH plays a critical role in promoting PD-L1 expression and immune evasion in cancer. Given the well-established role of PD-L1 in cancer immune evasion, these results also suggest that the upregulation of PD-L1 expression may contribute to PHGDH’s role in cancer immune evasion.

PHGDH upregulates PD-L1 expression independently of its enzymatic activity

We further investigated the mechanisms by which PHGDH enhances PD-L1 expression in cancer cells. To determine whether PHGDH-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 depends on its enzymatic function in serine synthesis, we first examined the effects of catalytically inactive PHGDH mutants on PD-L1 expression. RKO and Hs578T cells with PHGDH KO were transfected with vectors expressing either WT PHGDH-Flag or D175N and R236K PHGDH-Flag, two enzymatically inactive PHGDH mutants.7,9 Enzymatic inactivity was confirmed using a PHGDH enzymatic activity assay kit38,39,40 (Figure 2A). Intriguingly, like WT PHGDH-Flag, expression of D175N and R236K mutant PHGDH-Flag also restored the PD-L1 expression in PHGDH KO RKO and Hs578T cells, suggesting that PD-L1 upregulation by PHGDH is independent of PHGDH enzymatic function (Figures 2B and 2C). Currently, all existing small-molecule inhibitors of PHGDH only block PHGDH enzymatic activity without affecting PHGDH protein levels.6,8,16,17 To confirm our results, various cells were treated with NCT-503 and CBR-5884, two well-characterized small-molecule inhibitors of PHGDH.6,8,16,17 Notably, NCT-503 and CBR-5884 significantly inhibited PHGDH enzymatic activity (Figures 2D and 2E) but did not clearly affect the protein levels of PD-L1 or PHGDH (Figures 2F and 2G) or the mRNA levels of PD-L1 in these cells (Figures 2H and 2I). PHGDH binds to and activates RAF1 to drive PD-L1 expression

To reveal the noncanonical mechanism by which PHGDH upregulates PD-L1 expression, co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) followed by LC-MS/MS analysis was performed to screen for the PHGDH-interacting proteins in RKO cells with or without PHGDH-Flag expression. Through this approach, the serine/threonine kinase RAF1 was identified as a top candidate PHGDH-interacting protein that may mediate PHGDH’s effect on PD-L1 expression (Figure 3A). RAF1 was selected because RAF1 is a critical upstream activator of MEK/ERK pathway, which plays a crucial role in upregulating PD-L1 expression.26,41,42,43 The MEK/ERK pathway regulates cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and immune responses and is frequently activated in various cancer types.34,44 Once activated, ERK phosphorylates and activates its downstream transcription factors, such as AP-1, STAT3, and c-Myc, leading to PD-L1 overexpression in cancer cells.26,41,42,43 The interaction between exogenous PHGDH-Flag and RAF1-HA in RKO cells was confirmed by coIP followed by western blot assays (Figure 3B). We further determined the domain of RAF1 required for its interaction with PHGDH by expressing different deletion mutants of RAF1-HA and WT PHGDH-Flag in RKO cells for coIP assays (Figure 3E). The conserved region (CR3) domain of RAF1 was found to be required and sufficient for RAF1 to interact with PHGDH (Figure 3E). Furthermore, the regulatory domain (RD) of PHGDH was found to be required and sufficient for PHGDH to interact with RAF1 (Figure 3F). In vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay demonstrated a direct interaction between the recombinant GST-RAF1 and His-PHGDH proteins (Figure 3G). The proximity ligation assay (PLA), which detects the in situ interaction of proteins in close proximity (<40 nm) in cells,45 also confirmed the interaction between endogenous RAF1 and PHGDH in cells (Figure 3H). These results together demonstrate that PHGDH directly binds to RAF1 in cells.

Activation of the RAF1-MEK/ERK signaling pathway plays a critical role in upregulating PD-L1 expression in cancer cells.26,41,42,43 Given that PHGDH directly interacts with RAF1, we tested whether PHGDH can activate RAF1 and its downstream MEK/ERK signaling to promote PD-L1 expression in cells. RAF1 phosphorylation at Ser338 (p-RAF1 [Ser338]) is crucial for its activation and is widely used as an indicator of RAF1 kinase activity.46,47,48 Upon activation, RAF1 phosphorylates and activates MEK1/2 at serine 217 and serine 221 (p-MEK1/2 [Ser217/221]), which sequentially phosphorylate ERK1/2 at threonine 202 and tyrosine 204 (p-ERK1/2 [Thr 202/Tyr 204]), leading to MEK/ERK pathway activation.34,35 Therefore, the levels of p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221) and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) are commonly used as indicators of MEK/ERK pathway activation.34,35 Our results showed that PHGDH KO markedly reduced the levels of p-RAF1 (Ser338), p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221), and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204), but not their total protein levels, in RKO and MC38 cells (Figure 4A), as well as Hs578T and CT26 cells (Figure S4). In contrast, PHGDH-Flag expression in cells markedly increased the levels of p-RAF1 (Ser338), p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221), and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204), but not their total protein levels (Figure 4B). The activation of MEK/ERK signaling by PHGDH was further confirmed by examining the impact of PHGDH KO on the expression of several other well-known ERK target genes, including CCND1, SPRY2, and DUSP6,34,49 in RKO and MC38 cells by qPCR assays (Figure S5). To investigate whether PHGDH upregulates PD-L1 levels through RAF1 activation, RAF1 was knocked down by different shRNA vectors in RKO, MC38, Hs578T, and CT26 cells. RAF1 knockdown clearly reduced the levels of p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221) and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr 204), as well as the protein and mRNA levels of PD-L1 in these cells (Figures 4C, S6A, S6B, S7A, and S7B), demonstrating that RAF1 activates the MEK/ERK signaling to upregulate PD-L1 levels. Notably, RAF1 knockdown largely abolished the downregulation of PD-L1 expression resulting from PHGDH KO (Figures 4C, S6A, and S6B), as well as the upregulation of PD-L1 expression resulting from PHGDH-Flag overexpression (Figures S6C and S6D), at both the protein and mRNA levels. These results indicate that RAF1 mediates the PD-L1 upregulation by PHGDH. These results were validated by treating cells with small-molecule MEK inhibitors U0126 and PD9805950; both inhibitors markedly reduced PD-L1 protein and mRNA levels in various cells and largely abolished PHGDH-mediated PD-L1 upregulation (Figures 4D, S7A–S7D). Similarly, treating cells with SCH772984 and LY3214996, two small-molecule ERK inhibitors,51 dramatically reduced the protein and mRNA levels of PD-L1 in various cells and largely abolished PHGDH-mediated PD-L1 upregulation (Figures 4E and S7E–S7H). Notably, RAF1 knockdown did not affect PHGDH expression (Figure 4C). These results together suggest that PHGDH binds to and activates RAF1, which in turn activates its downstream MEK/ERK signaling to promote PD-L1 expression.

Interestingly, IFNγ has been reported to upregulate PD-L1 expression.52,53 Consistent with previous studies,52,53 IFNγ treatment significantly increased PD-L1 expression in RKO and MC38 cells (Figures S8A and S8B). PHGDH KO significantly reduced PD-L1 expression in both cells treated with or without IFNγ (Figure S8A). Similarly, PHGDH-Flag expression increased PD-L1 expression in both cells treated with or without IFNγ (Figure S8B). In addition, IFNγ treatment did not alter PHGDH expression (Figure S8C).

PHGDH activates RAF1-PD-L1 axis to promote tumorigenesis in vivo

We next investigated whether activation of the RAF1-PD-L1 axis contributes to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH in vivo using syngeneic tumors formed by MC38 and CT26 cells in C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice, respectively. KO of PHGDH, RAF1, or PD-L1 individually in MC38 and CT26 cells significantly reduced tumor growth (Figure 4F). Notably, compared with PHGDH KO alone, KO of RAF1 or PD-L1 in MC38 and CT26 cells with PHGDH KO did not further significantly reduce tumor growth (Figure 4F), suggesting that RAF1 and PD-L1 contribute greatly to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH. Consistent with in vitro results observed in different cell lines (Figures 4A–4C), KO of PHGDH or RAF1 individually markedly decreased the levels of PD-L1, p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221), and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr 204), but not the total MEK1/2 or ERK1/2 protein levels, in tumors (Figure 4G). Additionally, RAF1 KO did not affect PHGDH expression in tumors (Figure 4G). Notably, compared with RAF1 KO alone, PHGDH KO in MC38 and CT26 tumors with RAF1 KO did not further reduce the levels of p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221), p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr 204), or PD-L1 (Figure 4G), suggesting that PHGDH regulates the MEK/ERK-PD-L1 signaling through RAF1 activation in tumors.

Currently, all existing small-molecule PHGDH inhibitors, including NCT-503, which are widely used in preclinical mouse models, block only PHGDH enzymatic activity without affecting PHGDH protein levels.5,7,8,16,17 To further confirm that PHGDH promotes tumor growth through both activation of the RAF1-PD-L1 axis and its enzymatic activity, we treated mice bearing tumors formed by MC38 PHGDH KO or CT26 PHGDH KO cells expressing ectopic PHGDH-Flag with NCT-503 to block PHGDH enzymatic activity in tumors. Compared to tumors formed by MC38 and CT26 cells with PHGDH KO, expression of PHGDH-Flag in cells markedly promoted tumor growth (Figures 4H and 4I). Notably, KO of RAF1 or PD-L1 significantly reduced the tumor growth driven by PHGDH-Flag expression (Figures 4H and 4I), indicating that activation of the RAF1-PD-L1 axis contributes significantly to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH. While inhibition of PHGDH enzymatic activity by NCT-503 did not clearly affect the growth of tumors formed by PHGDH KO cells, NCT-503 significantly attenuated the growth of tumors formed by PHGDH KO cells with PHGDH-Flag expression, demonstrating the important role of PHGDH enzymatic activity in tumor progression, consistent with previous reports (Figure 4I).5,7,8,16,17 Notably, while NCT-503 partially abolished the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH-Flag in tumors formed by PHGDH KO cells, it almost completely abolished this effect of PHGDH-Flag in tumors formed by PHGDH KO cells with additional KO of RAF1 or PD-L1, suggesting that RAF1 and PD-L1 contribute to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH independently of PHGDH enzymatic activity (Figures 4H and 4I). These results from preclinical mouse models strongly suggest that PHGDH activates the RAF1-PD-L1 axis to promote tumorigenesis, in addition to its enzymatic function in serine synthesis (Figure 4J).

We further determined whether the RD of PHGDH, which mediates the PHGDH-RAF1 interaction (Figure 3F), contributes to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH. Notably, human and mouse PHGDH proteins exhibit high amino acid homology (96%), with all known catalytic and cofactor-binding residues fully conserved. Consistent with our results from human cells (Figure 3), coIP assays revealed that mouse PHGDH interacted with mouse RAF1 through its RD and deletion of the RD (ΔRD) disrupted this interaction (Figure S9A). Similar to the WT PHGDH-Flag, expression of RD PHGDH-Flag (containing only the RD), but not ΔRD PHGDH-Flag, in MC38 PHGDH KO cells clearly elevated PD-L1 protein levels and increased the levels of p-MEK1/2 (Ser217/221) and p-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr 204) (Figure S9B). We confirmed that neither RD nor ΔRD PHGDH-Flag exhibited PHGDH enzymatic activity (Figure S9C). To examine the functional impact of RAF1-PHGDH interaction on tumor growth, syngeneic tumors were established in C57BL/6 mice using MC38 PHGDH KO cells expressing WT, RD, or ΔRD PHGDH-Flag. Compared to cells expressing the control vector, expression of WT or RD PHGDH-Flag significantly promoted tumor growth, whereas ΔRD PHGDH-Flag did not (Figures S9D and S9E). Notably, WT PHGDH-Flag induced a more pronounced tumor-promoting effect than RD PHGDH-Flag (Figures S9D and S9E). These results demonstrate that the RD of PHGDH mediates PHGDH-RAF1 binding to upregulate PD-L1 expression independently of PHGDH enzymatic activity, contributing to the tumor-promoting effect of PHGDH.

PHGDH inhibits the interaction of RAF1 and 14-3-3 to activate RAF1

RAF1 is tightly regulated by different regulators in cells. Among these regulators, 14-3-3 family proteins, which consist of seven highly conserved isoforms (β, ε, γ, η, σ, θ, and ζ) and are present in all eukaryotic cells, play a crucial role in regulating RAF1 activity in cells.32,33,34,35 Under normal conditions, 14-3-3 proteins bind to the CR2 and CR3 domains of RAF1 and prevent RAF1 activation and subsequent activation of MEK/ERK signaling.32,33,34,35,54 The release of 14-3-3 proteins from RAF1 is crucial for RAF1 activation.32,33,34,35,54 Our results showed that the CR3 domain of RAF1, located in the C-terminal region of RAF1, is required for its interaction with PHGDH (Figure 3E). Interestingly, it was reported that the RAF1 activator Prohibitin binds to the C terminus of RAF1 and competes with 14-3-3 for RAF1 binding, leading to RAF1 activation.33 This raises a possibility that PHGDH may compete with 14-3-3 proteins for RAF1 binding, thereby activating RAF1. To test this possibility, RKO and Hs578T cells were co-transfected with vectors expressing PHGDH-Flag and RAF1-HA, respectively, along with vectors expressing 14-3-3ζ or 14-3-3γ, two high-affinity binding partners of RAF1 among 14-3-3 isoforms,54 and subjected to coIP assays. PHGDH-Flag expression markedly decreased the binding of 14-3-3ζ and 14-3-3γ to RAF1 in cells (Figures 5A and 5B). In contrast, PHGDH KO in cells markedly enhanced the interaction between the endogenous RAF1 and 14-3-3 proteins, as measured by an antibody that can detect all 14-3-3 isoforms55,56 (Figure 5C). Furthermore, knockdown of endogenous 14-3-3 isoforms using two independent pan-14-3-3 small interfering RNA (siRNA) pools targeting all seven 14-3-3 isoforms (Figure S10) markedly upregulated the protein and mRNA levels of PD-L1 in RKO and Hs578T cells and notably, largely abolished the promoting effect of PHGDH-Flag expression on PD-L1 expression (Figures 5D and 5E). Similarly, knockdown of 14-3-3 isoforms largely abolished the inhibitory effect of PHGDH KO on PD-L1 expression in cells (Figures 5F and 5G). The p21-activated kinase family proteins (PAKs) play a key role in activating RAF1 by phosphorylating RAF1 at Ser338.47,48,57 PAK2, which is highly expressed in RKO cells, was identified as a potential PHGDH-binding protein in RKO cells by LC-MS/MS analysis (Figure S11A), and this interaction was confirmed by coIP followed by western blot assays (Figure S11B). In vitro GST pull-down assays demonstrated a direct interaction between recombinant GST-RAF1 and His-PAK2 proteins, but not between GST-PHGDH and His-PAK2 proteins (Figure S11C). These results suggest that the interaction between PAK2 and PHGDH in cells is indirect and likely mediated by RAF1. Previous studies have shown that the RAF1 activator Prohibitin binds to RAF1 and disrupts its interaction with 14-3-3, facilitating RAF1 Ser338 phosphorylation and activation.33 These findings raise a possibility that PHGDH may similarly promote recruitment of PAK2 to RAF1 by disrupting RAF1-14-3-3 interaction, thereby enhancing RAF-1 Ser338 phosphorylation and activation. Consistent with this model, coIP assays showed that PHGDH-Flag expression markedly enhanced the interaction between PAK2-Myc and RAF1-HA (Figure S11D), whereas PHGDH KO clearly reduced the interaction between endogenous RAF1 and PAK2 in cells (Figure S11E). Together, these results suggest that PHGDH inhibits the interaction between RAF1 and 14-3-3, which in turn promotes the interaction between RAF1 and its activator PAK2, leading to RAF1 activation.

Tumors with high PHGDH expression are sensitive to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade

High PD-L1 expression in cancer is generally associated with a better response to immune checkpoint blockade.26,27,30 Given that PHGDH is frequently overexpressed in various cancers and drives PD-L1 expression, we investigated whether tumors with high PHGDH expression are more sensitive to anti-PD-1 therapy. To test this, we compared the response to the anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy in C57BL/6 mice bearing control versus PHGDH KO MC38 tumors (Figure 6A). While the anti-PD-1 therapy significantly reduced the growth of control MC38 tumors, it exhibited a much less pronounced inhibitory effect on PHGDH KO MC38 tumors, although PHGDH KO alone significantly reduced tumor growth (Figure 6B). In line with the observed effects on tumor growth, anti-PD-1 therapy significantly prolonged survival in mice bearing control tumors; however, this survival advantage was markedly reduced in mice with PHGDH KO tumors (Figure 6B). Notably, flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes showed that PHGDH KO significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells and enhanced GzmB expression in MC38 tumors, indicating that PHGDH plays a critical role in promoting immune evasion in tumors (Figure 6C). Anti-PD-1 therapy significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells and GzmB expression in control MC38 tumors but exhibited a much less pronounced effect in MC38 PHGDH KO tumors (Figure 6C). These results were also validated in CT26 tumors with or without PHGDH KO in BALB/c mice (Figures 6D and 6E). Combining PHGDH activity inhibition and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade enhances antitumor responses

As mentioned above, current small-molecule PHGDH inhibitors, including NCT-503, block enzymatic activity only without affecting PHGDH protein levels.5,7,8,16,17 Given the critical role of PHGDH in promoting cancer progression through both its enzymatic function in facilitating serine synthesis and its non-metabolic function in upregulating PD-L1 expression, we tested whether inhibiting PHGDH enzymatic activity using NCT-503 along with anti-PD-1 therapy can produce a much better therapeutic effect on tumors with high PHGDH expression (Figure 6F). Mice bearing MC38 or CT26 tumors were treated with IgG, NCT-503 alone, anti-PD-1 antibody alone, or a combination of both. Notably, compared to the monotherapies or the IgG treatment, the combined treatment significantly reduced the growth of MC38 and CT26 tumors and extended survival of mice (Figures 6G and 6H). Moreover, anti-PD-1 therapy did not produce an obvious anti-tumor effect on tumors formed by MC38 and CT26 cells with PD-L1 KO (Figures 6I and 6J), indicating that the effect of anti-PD-1 therapy on these tumors largely depends on PD-L1. Additionally, these treatments did not significantly affect the body weight of mice (Figure S12). These results together suggest that combined pharmacologic inhibition of PHGDH enzymatic activity and anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade represents a potentially effective therapeutic strategy for tumors with high PHGDH expression (Figure 6K).

PHGDH expression positively correlates with PD-L1 expression in human cancers

Discussion

PD-L1 is a key immune checkpoint protein that plays a central role in cancer immunity. Monoclonal antibodies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis have revolutionized cancer therapy. However, only approximately 20% of patients experience substantial clinical benefit, prompting efforts to improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.25,29 Therefore, it is critical to elucidate functional PD-L1 regulatory mechanisms and to develop robust biomarkers that improve prediction of patient responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

PHGDH is frequently overexpressed in various human cancers.5,6,8 Its canonical enzymatic function in facilitating serine synthesis has been shown to promote cancer progression and is regarded as an attractive therapeutic target.3,4,5 More recently, emerging studies suggested noncanonical, enzymatic activity-independent functions of PHGDH, expanding the roles of PHGDH beyond metabolism. For instance, PHGDH was reported to localize to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it interacts with ANT2 and recruits mtEFG2 to facilitate mitochondrial ribosome recycling and enhance the translation of mitochondrial DNA-encoded proteins, thereby elevating mitochondrial respiration to promote tumor growth.60 In fatty liver disease, PHGDH suppresses inflammatory signaling in macrophages by interacting with TAK1 to inhibit nuclear factor-κB activation.61 These limited recent studies suggest that PHGDH may contribute to cancer progression through non-metabolic mechanisms as additional mechanisms.

Recent studies have implicated enzymes within the serine-glycine-one-carbon metabolic network in tumor immune evasion. For example, the folate cycle enzyme MTHFD2 was reported to promote PD-L1 expression in a metabolism-dependent manner, linking one-carbon metabolic flux to immune checkpoint regulation.62 Emerging studies have suggested that PHGDH also plays a role in cancer immunity through its metabolic function.10,31 For instance, PHGDH supports the maintenance of the immunosuppressive M2-like macrophage phenotype by promoting serine synthesis to enhance mitochondrial respiration, contributing to immune evasion.31 PHGDH also facilitates serine synthesis to regulate redox homeostasis and central carbon metabolism in tumor endothelial cells, thereby driving excessive endothelial proliferation and abnormal vascular sprouting, which contributes to the formation of a hypoxic, immune-excluded microenvironment.10 Despite these emerging findings, the role of PHGDH and its precise mechanisms in cancer immunity, particularly its nonmetabolic mechanism, remain poorly defined. In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognized, metabolism-independent mechanism by which PHGDH promotes cancer immune evasion. Our results demonstrate that PHGDH binds to and activates RAF1, leading to enhanced MEK/ERK signaling and subsequent upregulation of PD-L1 expression in cancer cells. Our results from preclinical mouse models show that tumors with high PHGDH expression exhibit higher sensitivity to the anti-PD-1 therapy. Furthermore, combining PHGDH enzymatic inhibition with the anti-PD-1 therapy enhances antitumor responses in tumors with high PHGDH expression. Supporting the clinical relevance of this pathway, our results demonstrate a significant positive correlation between PHGDH and PD-L1 expression in human breast and colorectal cancer tissues and also indicate that higher PHGDH expression is associated with a better response to anti-PD-L1-based combination therapy in breast cancer patients. In summary, our results identify PHGDH as an important regulator of PD-L1 expression and reveal a critical noncanonical mechanism by which PHGDH promotes cancer progression by enhancing PD-L1 expression to drive immune evasion. Most importantly, these results suggest that inhibition of PHGDH enzymatic activity in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade may represent a therapeutic strategy for cancers with PHGDH overexpression, which is frequently observed in multiple cancer types and is associated with poor clinical outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study provides mechanistic and functional insights into the noncanonical role of PHGDH in tumor immunity and its potential impact on immunotherapy response; however, several limitations merit consideration. First, the absence of selective pharmacological inhibitors that specifically disrupt the PHGDH-RAF1 interaction limits direct assessment of the therapeutic potential of targeting this signaling axis. Although our genetic and biochemical approaches establish the functional relevance of this interaction, the development of interaction-specific inhibitors will be able to further evaluate its translational applicability. Second, although our results identify RAF1 as a key mediator of the non-metabolic function of PHGDH in upregulating PD-L1 expression and promoting immune evasion, they do not exclude the possibility that other PHGDH-interacting proteins may also contribute to PHGDH-driven immune evasion and cancer progression, which will be explored in future studies. Third, although preclinical syngeneic mouse models recapitulate key aspects of tumor-immune interactions, they may not fully capture the complexity of human tumor microenvironments or treatment responses. While analysis of a cohort of breast cancer patients treated with anti-PD-L1-based combination therapy in the TIDE database revealed that therapy-responsive tumors exhibited significantly higher PHGDH expression than therapy-resistant tumors, future studies incorporating larger, well-annotated patient cohorts across multiple cancer types are required to comprehensively assess the impact and predictive value of PHGDH for clinical immunotherapy outcomes. Despite these limitations, our findings establish a mechanistic foundation for a non-metabolic role of PHGDH in PD-L1 regulation and support dual targeting of its enzymatic and non-metabolic functions as a promising direction for future investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

RKO, Hs578T, HCT116, SK-BR-3, and CT26 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MC38 cells were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, and MC38-OVA cells were obtained from Vitro Biotech. Human and mouse CRISPR-mediated gene deletion cell lines were generated as previously described.45 Briefly, sgRNAs targeting human and mouse PHGDH, RAF1, and PD-L1 were designed using an online CRISPR design tool,63 and sgRNA sequences were inserted into the lentiCRISPRv2-puro or lentiCRISPRv2-blast vector. The sgRNA sequences are listed in key resources table. Puromycin-resistant single-cell colonies were selected. The deletions of target genes were verified through PCR amplification of the edited regions followed by sequencing, and by Western blot analysis of target proteins as well. All cell lines were authenticated by ATCC using short tandem repeat profiling and confirmed negative for Mycoplasma contamination.

Animal models

All mouse experiments were approved by the Rutgers University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (#201702554). Mice were housed under controlled conditions, including a temperature of 22 ± 1°C, 40–60% relative humidity, as well as a 12-h light/dark cycle. Both female and male mice were used for s.c. tumor formation assays in C57BL/6, BALB/c, and BALB/c nude mice. For anti-PD-1 therapy experiments, only female mice were used to reduce biological variability and ensure experimental consistency, based on well-established sex-specific differences in immune responses.64,65 MC38 and CT26 syngeneic tumors were established by s.c. injection of 5 × 105 cells into the flanks of 8-week-old C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice, respectively (The Jackson Laboratory). MC38 and CT26 xenograft tumors in nude mice were established by s.c. injection of 5 × 105 cells into the flanks of 8-week-old BALB/c nude mice (Taconic). Mice were monitored 3 times/week, and tumor sizes were measured every three days after implantation. Tumor volume was calculated by length × width2 × 0.5. Tumor weight was measured after mice were sacrificed. For NCT-503 treatment, NCT-503 was administered by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection starting the day after tumor cell inoculation, once daily at a dosage of 50 mg/kg (in 10% DMSO, 40% PEG 300, and 5% Tween 80 in 45% saline) for 12 consecutive days. For anti-PD-1 therapy, when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into two groups for treatments: control IgG antibody, and anti-PD-1 antibody. The anti-PD-1 or control IgG antibodies were given by injection (i.p.) with a dosage of 200 μg/mouse every three days for five times. For combined therapies, when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups for treatments: control IgG antibody, anti-PD-1 antibody alone, NCT-503 alone, and anti-PD-1 antibody plus NCT-503. Animals and tumor growth were monitored for 60 days, or sacrificed when tumor volume exceeded 1,700 mm3 or mice reach humane endpoints according to Rutgers IACUC policy.

Method details

Western blot assays

Standard Western blot assays were employed to analyze protein levels in cells and mouse tissues.8 Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing the protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentration in extracts was measured using Bradford reagent. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The antibodies used in this study were listed in the key resources table.

Quantitative proteomic analysis by LC-MS/MS

WT and PHGDH KO RKO cells in 10 cm dishes were lysed in 100 μL of buffer containing 2% SDS, 50 mM HEPES, and 50 mM EDTA at 2500 rpm for 90 s 5 mM DTT were added to lysate with 10 μg total protein and incubated at 60°C for 30 min to reduce disulfide bonds. Free cysteines were alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Proteins were then digested using the SP3 method with sequencing-grade trypsin in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 2 mM CaCl2, overnight at 37°C as previously described.66 Resulting peptides were dried and reconstituted in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid for LC-MS/MS analysis. Peptide separation was performed using a Nano LC-MS/MS system (Dionex Ultimate 3000 RLSCnano, Thermo Fisher) coupled to an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Peptides were analyzed using a data-independent acquisition (DIA) workflow. Full MS scans were acquired over a mass range of 400–1200 m/z at a resolution of 12,000, with an AGC target of 3e6 and automatic ion injection time. Sequential 8 m/z DIA windows were used to isolate and fragment ions, with an AGC target of 4e5 and automatic ion injection time. Fragment ions were analyzed at a resolution of 30,000 using a normalized collision energy of 30. Raw data were processed using DIA-NN version 2.0 with a predicted library based on the UniProt human reference proteome for a library-free search.67 Results were filtered with a PEP (posterior error probability) threshold <0.01 and a protein group Q-value (PG.Q) < 0.01. Protein quantification was based on the MaxLFQ values reported in the DIA-NN output.

qPCR assays

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit, and cDNA was synthesized with the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Kit. Real-time PCR was carried out using the TaqMan PCR Mixture as previously described.8 Gene expression levels were normalized to Actin, which served as the internal control.

Flow cytometry analysis of cell-surface PD-L1

Cells were harvested, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with APC-conjugated anti-PD-L1 antibody for 30 min on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed three times with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry to measure cell-surface PD-L1 levels. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

PD-L1/PD-1 binding assay

The PD-1 binding assay was performed as previously described.68 Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to ∼60% confluence, harvested by trypsinization, and washed with PBS. Cells were then incubated with recombinant PD-1 Fc chimera protein (5 μg/mL) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human IgG (H + L) antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were washed, resuspended in staining buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor cell killing assays

RKO or MC38-OVA cells were seeded into 96-well plates as target cells for CD8+ T cell-mediated cell killing assays. Human peripheral blood CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and mouse CD8+ T cells isolated from OT-1 mice (The Jackson Laboratory) using a CD8+ T cell isolation kit were activated with anti-CD3 (2 μg/mL), anti-CD28 (2 μg/mL), and IL-2 (10 ng/mL) as previously described.62,69 Human CD8+ T cells were activated for 24 h, whereas mouse CD8+ T cells were activated for 48 h to generate effector cells. Activated human or mouse CD8+ T cells (2 × 105 cells per well) were added to target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 5:1 and co-cultured for 16 h. Plates were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, and supernatants were collected for tumor cell cytotoxicity assays. Tumor cell cytotoxicity was quantified using an LDH release assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with absorbance measured at 450 nm.

Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) analysis

Three days after the final treatment of MC38 or CT26 tumors with or without the anti-PD-1 antibody, TILs were isolated and prepared by using a Tumor Dissociation Kit and a gentleMACSTM Dissociator, respectively. Flow cytometric assays were performed as previously described.70 Samples were collected using the Cytek Aurora Analyzer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed by FlowJo 10 software. The anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD8, anti-Granzyme B, and IFN γ antibodies were used. Gating strategy: gate cells by excluding dead cells and debris based on size, then gate live cells as Zombie NIR negative. Next, gate CD45+ cells, followed by gating CD45+CD3+ cells. Subsequently, gate CD45+CD3+CD8+ cells, and finally, gate CD3+CD8+ GzmB+ or IFN-γ+ cells (Figure S1).

PHGDH enzymatic activity assays

PHGDH enzymatic activity was measured using the PHGDH Activity Assay Kit, a widely used kit,38,39,40 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed in the provided PHGDH assay buffer and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove debris. Protein concentration in the lysates was determined using Bradford reagent. Equal amounts of protein (in 50 μL assay buffer) were combined with 50 μL of the PHGDH reaction mix in a 96-well plate. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured kinetically using a microplate reader, with incubation at 37°C protected from light between readings. PHGDH activity was quantified by calculating the rate of absorbance change between two time points within the linear range, based on the internal standard curve provided in the assay kit.

Co-IP and LC-MS/MS assays

The potential PHGDH-binding proteins in cells were identified by co-IP followed by LC-MS/MS assays. Assays were performed as described previously.45,71 In brief, RKO cells were infected with the pLPCX-PHGDH-Flag vector, and control RKO cells were infected with the empty vector for co-IP assays. PHGDH-Flag was pulled down by co-IP using an anti-Flag antibody and eluted with the Flag peptide. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed at the Biological Mass Spectrometry facility of Rutgers University. For co-IP of PHGDH-Flag and RAF1-HA proteins in cells with ectopic expression of PHGDH-Flag and RAF1-HA, anti-Flag and anti-HA agarose beads were used, respectively. For co-IP of endogenous PHGDH and RAF1, anti-PHGDH antibody and anti-RAF1 antibody were used, respectively. The mouse or rabbit purified IgG was used as a negative control for co-IP assays.

In vitro GST pull-down assays

In vitro GST pull-down assays were performed as we previously described.45,71 In brief, E. coli BL21(DE3) cells transformed with pGEX-5X-1-GST or pET-32a-His-Trx vectors expressing PHGDH, RAF1, or PAK2 were induced with 0.4 mM IPTG for 4 h at 37°C to express recombinant fusion proteins. Purified pGEX-5X-1-GST fusion protein (200 ng) was immobilized on glutathione Sepharose beads and subsequently incubated with purified pET-32a-His-Trx fusion protein (200 ng). GST protein alone was used as a negative control. After washing, proteins bound to the beads were analyzed by Western blot assays using an anti-His or anti-GST antibody.

The proximity ligation assay (PLA)

The PLA was used to detect the in situ interaction of endogenous PHGDH and RAF1 proteins in cells by employing DuolinkTM secondary antibodies and a detection kit (Sigma-Aldrich) as previously described.22 In brief, cells were fixed and incubated with PHGDH, and RAF1 antibodies together with Duolink secondary antibodies. Ligation steps were performed to ligate the secondary antibodies that are in close proximity in cells. 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used for counterstaining the nucleus.

IHC assays

Human colorectal tumor TMAs CO6161 (n = 294) and CO20814a (n = 192) were obtained from US Biomax, with 16 duplicate tissues excluded from CO20814a during analysis. Breast tumor TMAs BR2161 (n = 208) and BR2085d (n = 160) were also obtained from US Biomax, with 8 duplicates excluded from BR2085d during analysis. All specimens were deidentified. The IHC staining was performed as we previously described.45,71 The IHC results were scored by using a scoring system from 0 to 9 as previously described.45,71 In brief, the scores were obtained by multiplying the intensity of signals with the percentage of positive cells (signal: 0: no signal; 1: weak signal; 2: intermediate signal; 3: strong signal; percentage: 0: 0%; 1: <25%; 2: 25–50%; 3: >50%). Samples with a score ≥6 were defined as high, whereas those with a score <6 were defined as low. CD8+ T cell staining was assessed as previously described.72 Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells were quantified separately in intraepithelial and stromal regions using ×200 magnification. Three high-density fields were selected per sample, and the average cell count was calculated. Expression was classified as low when fewer than five CD8+ T cells per field were observed, and high when five or more cells were present.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were obtained from at least three replicates and were expressed as mean ± SD as indicated in the figure legends. Two-tailed Student’s t test was used for statistical analysis between two groups. Analyses of variance (ANOVAs) followed by Dunnett’s, or Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were applied for multiple group comparisons. The survival information of mice was summarized by the Kaplan-Meier plots and the difference was analyzed by the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.
