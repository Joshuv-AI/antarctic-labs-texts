Integrated Management of Malignant Arrhythmias in Pediatric LMNA-Related Cardiomyopathy Using Active-Fixation CRT-D
Authors: Yujia Wang, Huafeng Wang, Haiyan Xu, Fangqi Gong, Ying Zhou, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric LMNA-related dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) carries extreme risks of malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. History of Presentation

An 11-year-old boy (height: 150 cm, weight: 30.1 kg, body surface area: 1.09 m2) with a known diagnosis of LMNA-related dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) collapsed suddenly after minor trauma while running. The patient presented with recurrent ventricular fibrillation resulting in sudden cardiac arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated by emergency medical services, and successful defibrillation restored spontaneous circulation. The patient was subsequently transferred to our institution for further management.

During hospitalization, the patient remained hemodynamically stable after resuscitation. Given the presence of severe left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction, complete left bundle branch block, and documented malignant ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) implantation was strongly recommended. However, the patient's parents expressed significant concerns regarding transvenous lead implantation and declined device therapy at that time.

Despite intensive medical management, the patient experienced recurrent, unprovoked episodes of sudden cardiac arrest due to ventricular fibrillation during the same hospitalization. Emergency extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support was initiated to stabilize him. After multidisciplinary discussions and reassessment of the risk-benefit balance, the parents consented to CRT-D implantation.

Past Medical History

The patient had been diagnosed with DCM 1 year earlier, with echocardiography showing an ejection fraction of 39% and ventricular enlargement. Genetic testing revealed a de novo heterozygous LMNA variant (c.1622G>A, p.R541H). Holter monitoring had shown multiple polymorphic ventricular premature beats and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (VT). The patient had been on continuous guideline-directed medical therapy, including ARNi, beta-blockers, MRA, and SGLT2i, prior to the current admission. The family initially declined implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement given procedural risk concerns.

Differential Diagnosis

The primary consideration was LMNA-related DCM with malignant ventricular arrhythmias. Alternative considerations included other genetic cardiomyopathies, such as desminopathy or sarcomeric mutations, as well as secondary arrhythmogenic conditions like myocarditis or electrolyte disturbances.

Investigations

On admission after resuscitated ventricular fibrillation, electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with complete left bundle branch block and a QRS duration of 150 ms, indicating marked electrical dyssynchrony (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe biventricular dilation with profoundly reduced LV systolic function; the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 16% by Simpson's method (Figure 1). No structural congenital heart disease was identified.

Continuous rhythm monitoring documented recurrent malignant ventricular arrhythmias. Collectively, these findings defined a high-risk electromechanical phenotype requiring urgent definitive therapy.

Management

Given the patient's high-risk profile, CRT-D implantation was performed under ECMO support via left axillary vein puncture (Figure 2A). Coronary sinus venography identified the anterior branch of the great cardiac vein as the target vessel (2.2-mm diameter) (Figure 2B). A 65-cm active-fixation LV quadripolar lead (Medtronic Attain Stability Quad, 4798) was successfully positioned in the distal target vein under BMW guidewire guidance (Figure 2C). Electrical parameters were optimal: LV threshold 0.8 V at 0.5 ms, R-wave 11 mV, impedance 900 Ω, with no phrenic nerve stimulation. A right ventricular defibrillation coil lead and right atrial lead were placed, and the CRT-D generator was implanted. The procedure lasted 82 minutes, with 7.3 minutes of fluoroscopy and no intraoperative complications (Figures 2D to 2F).

Despite successful resynchronization, the patient experienced refractory VT. Malignant arrhythmias persisted despite sequential trials of intravenous lidocaine and amiodarone, as well as oral mexiletine, propafenone, propranolol, metoprolol, and sotalol. Epicardial radiofrequency ablation targeting low-voltage regions on the LV anterior wall was performed successfully (Figure 3). Sustained VT terminated after substrate modification and ablation. The technical complexity of this integrated procedure—combining ECMO-supported CRT-D implantation with epicardial ablation—required specialized equipment adapted for the pediatric anatomy, as detailed in Figure 4. All invasive procedures, including CRT-D implantation and subsequent ablation, were performed during a single hospitalization owing to continuous clinical instability.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was successfully weaned off ECMO and extubated. Three months postoperatively, LVEF improved to 35.9%, QRS duration decreased to 120 ms, and device interrogation showed stable pacing parameters: LV lead threshold was 0.875 V at 0.4 ms with impedance 665 Ω; right ventricular lead threshold was 0.625 V at 0.4 ms with impedance 532 Ω; and atrial lead threshold was 0.5 V at 0.4 ms with impedance 399 Ω. No VT recurrence was observed. Clinical symptoms were markedly improved, and the patient was discharged in stable condition (Figure 5). Postdischarge management included intensified guideline-directed medical therapy by adding vericiguat and amiodarone to his baseline regimen (sacubitril/valsartan, metoprolol, spironolactone, and dapagliflozin).

Discussion

Pediatric LMNA-related DCM is characterized by rapid progression, malignant arrhythmias, and high risk of sudden cardiac death, representing an “electromechanical dual-phenotype.”1,2 The family's initial hesitation to proceed with device implantation, reversed only after life-threatening ventricular fibrillation, underscores the risks of a “watch-and-wait” approach.3 Early CRT-D implantation in these patients is supported by the 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society and 2022 Pediatric and Congenital Electrophysiology Society/Heart Rhythm Society consensus recommendations for secondary prevention in high-risk LMNA-mutation carriers.3,4

CRT-D implantation in children is particularly challenging given small body size, variable coronary sinus anatomy, and limited availability of pediatric-specific devices.5,6 Traditional passive-fixation LV leads are prone to dislodgement, especially in low-weight children.7,8 In this case, the use of an adult active-fixation quadripolar LV lead allowed stable placement in a 2.2-mm vein, with optimized pacing vectors to avoid phrenic nerve stimulation and preserve future growth potential. The quadripolar design also provided flexibility in vector selection, ensuring effective resynchronization in a compact pediatric chest. Active-fixation leads offer superior stability over passive ones, though limited pediatric data on long-term extraction remains a clinical concern. This trade-off between immediate lead performance and future extractability requires careful clinical balancing.

Despite successful resynchronization, the patient experienced recurrent, drug-resistant VT. In LMNA-related DCM, arrhythmogenic substrates often reside in the mid-myocardium or epicardium, particularly around basal septal and perivalvular areas.9 Endocardial ablation alone may be insufficient, highlighting the need for a combined therapeutic approach. Epicardial substrate modification targeting low-voltage regions on the LV anterior wall successfully controlled malignant arrhythmias.10 This dual-track strategy—device implantation plus substrate ablation—demonstrates a comprehensive approach for managing high-risk LMNA-related DCM patients, integrating mechanical support and arrhythmia control.

This case contributes novel knowledge by demonstrating the feasibility of adapting adult active-fixation technology to pediatric patients, potentially informing device selection and procedural planning in similar cases. Limitations include the lack of long-term data regarding lead performance during somatic growth, and the need for ongoing follow-up to monitor device stability, ventricular remodeling, and arrhythmia recurrence. Nevertheless, this report provides practical guidance for clinicians managing pediatric patients with LMNA-related DCM and highlights the importance of proactive, guideline-aligned, and technically innovative strategies for reducing morbidity and mortality.3,4

Conclusions

Active-fixation LV leads can be safely used in pediatric CRT-D implantation. Integrating device therapy with epicardial ablation offers an effective strategy for managing malignant arrhythmias in high-risk children with LMNA-related DCM.
