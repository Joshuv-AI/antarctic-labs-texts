Molecular and cellular landscapes of the immune microenvironment and multiomic biomarker-sets in platinum-resistant recurrent ovarian cancers
Authors: Xiaoxia Feng, Yanjun Zhang, Ousman Bajinka, Serge Yannick Ouedraogo, Yuping Zhang, Na Li, Xianquan Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer is the deadliest gynecological malignancy, posing a significant threat to women’s health worldwide. Platinum-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of ovarian cancer treatment. Although most patients initially exhibit platinum sensitivity, recurrent disease frequently develops platinum resistance following multiple recurrences. Platinum-resistant recurrent ovarian cancer (PRROC) is associated with a poor prognosis. Current treatments offer limited clinical efficacy, yielding unsatisfactory outcomes overall. Across disease stages, from primary tumors to recurrent disease, the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a critical role in cancer progression, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that tumor metabolism is essential not only for supporting tumor growth and survival but also for shaping the TME through metabolite release. Furthermore, multiomics technologies, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, provide novel insights into the immune and molecular mechanisms underpinning tumor initiation, progression, immune escape, and drug resistance, suggesting new therapeutic avenues. This review explores how tumor metabolic reprogramming influences the TME and examines the role of the TME in facilitating immune evasion. We also discuss how multiomics approaches can deepen our understanding of the mechanisms by which tumors subvert immune surveillance through TME modulation and identify potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Metabolic reprogramming, Molecular landscape, Cellular landscape, Multiomics, Biomarker

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy and the deadliest gynecological cancer in women. According to the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) database, the 5-year survival rate for OC is 51.6%. This is largely a consequence of the challenges in achieving early and specific diagnosis, which leads to the majority of cases being detected at an advanced stage. High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) represents the predominant histological subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), counting for approximately 75% of all cases [1]. Approximately 80% of patients with HGSC initially respond well to platinum-based chemotherapy. Therefore, the first-line chemotherapy regimen for these patients typically includes a platinum agent in combination with a taxane drug, such as paclitaxel or docetaxel [2]. However, the majority of these patients eventually experience disease recurrence, leading to the development of acquired platinum resistance [3].

A key determinant of this acquired resistance is the dynamic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). In platinum-resistant recurrent ovarian cancer (PRROC), the TME is not a static physical backdrop but an active, dynamically evolving shaper that co-evolves with the tumor and sustains its malignant traits. It comprises a complex network of cellular and non-cellular components: the cellular fraction includes cancer cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and various immune cells; the non-cellular compartment encompasses the extracellular matrix (ECM), cytokines, metabolites, and other factors [4]. These components collectively facilitate cancer cell survival and invasion [5–7], and play crucial roles in key processes such as the uptake and transport of platinum-based drugs, the maintenance of cancer cell viability, and the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways [8–11].

As the tumor progresses, the TME undergoes significant remodeling. Fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells modify the ECM to form physical barriers and secrete paracrine factors such as growth factors and cytokines, thereby promoting tumor proliferation. Concurrently, an abnormal vascular network supplies the tumor with oxygen, nutrients, and growth factors, while immune cells facilitate tumor immune escape through immunosuppressive mechanisms [4]. This dynamic heterogeneity further drives tumor evolution—variations in tumor type, stage, and patient-specific biological characteristics lead to spatiotemporal heterogeneity in TME components (e.g., immune cell subsets, ECM composition). These interconnected elements collectively promote tumor growth, immune evasion, angiogenesis, and therapy resistance, ultimately influencing tumor invasion, metastasis, and responses to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [5–9].

Tumor metabolism is a key factor in sustaining malignant progression. Through metabolic reprogramming, cancer cells alter their metabolic pathways to support unrestricted growth and survival. Resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy is closely linked to alterations in glucose, amino acid, and fatty acid metabolism [12]. These changes lead to the accumulation of specific metabolic intermediates—such as lactate, kynurenine, prostaglandins, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)—within the TME. These compounds not only help maintain cellular energy homeostasis but also contribute to tumor progression and immune escape [13, 14]. Notably, immune cells within the TME of PRROC also exhibit distinct metabolic adaptations (Table 1).

To decipher the complex metabolic-immune crosstalk network in PRROC, a research strategy at the systems level is essential. In recent years, the advancement of multiomics technologies—including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—has facilitated their increasing application in the study of the TME of PRROC. These technologies enable the systematic mapping of molecular and cellular landscapes within the TME of PRROC, not only aiding in the elucidation of underlying causal mechanisms but also providing novel perspectives for a deeper understanding of tumor immune evasion. Concurrently, they open new avenues for innovative therapeutic strategies targeting PRROC [41–44]. This review will specifically focus on PRROC, aiming to address the following core questions: systematically analyze how tumor cells and stromal cells drive the formation of an immunosuppressive TME through metabolic reprogramming; detail the consequent critical metabolic dysregulation and functional impairment of key immune cells; and further assess the value of integrated multiomics analytical approaches in identifying PRROC-specific biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets. By synthesizing these aspects, we aim to construct a systematic conceptual framework, thereby providing a theoretical basis and directional guidance for targeting the metabolic-immune interface to overcome platinum resistance.

Metabolic reprograming shapes immunosuppressive microenvironment in PRROC

Glucose metabolism reprograming drives TME acidification and immunosuppression

Regarding the core metabolic patterns in PRROC, a unified consensus has yet to be established in academia, with different theoretical perspectives prevailing. Since Otto Warburg proposed the phenomenon of highly efficient aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells—known as the Warburg effect [45]—this distinctive metabolic feature of cancer cells has long been regarded as a potential target for cancer therapy. The classical “Warburg effect” theory posits that, even under adequate oxygen conditions, cancer cells tend to preferentially generate energy through glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation. In rapidly proliferating ovarian cancer cells, this active glycolytic state is particularly pronounced, and its enhancement is closely associated with platinum resistance and poor prognosis in patients [46–48].

Nevertheless, subsequent studies have provided alternative evidence: in PRROC cells, to sustain long-term survival and stress resistance, cells instead rely more heavily on the more efficient mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation pathway [49, 50]. Moreover, activation of the pentose phosphate pathway has also been identified as a key mechanism in resisting platinum-induced damage and adapting to nutrient fluctuations [51–53]. These findings indicate that the metabolism of PRROC exhibits marked “heterogeneity” and “context-dependency.” Metabolic heterogeneity is reflected in the diverse metabolic pathways employed not only between different patients but even among different cells within the same tumor. Context-dependency refers to the ability of tumor cells to dynamically shift their dominant metabolic states in response to conditions in the microenvironment, such as oxygen tension, nutrient availability, and drug pressure. Notably, regardless of which major metabolic pathway predominates, the overproduction and accumulation of lactate within the TME is a common phenomenon. Consequently, lactate serves as a critical entry point for understanding how metabolic reprogramming in PRROC influences the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 1).

Cancer cell glycolysis in the TME generates lactate and metabolites that acidify the milieu, impairing immune cell function. Elevated lactate concentrations suppress NK cell cytotoxicity, inhibit cytotoxic T cell activation/proliferation, and impede dendritic cell (DC) maturation. Additionally, glycolysis disrupts macrophage polarization, neutrophil activity, and other immune subsets, collectively fostering an immunosuppressive environment conducive to tumor progression

Lactate is the end product of glycolysis, generated from pyruvate via catalysis by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). LDH is composed of two subunits, LDH-A and LDH-B, which reversibly catalyze the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, and vice versa [54]. LDH promotes the proliferation and metastasis of ovarian cancer cells, and its elevated expression is closely associated with poor patient survival outcomes [55]. The expression level of LDH positively correlates with the stage and pathological grade of OC, while inversely correlating with patient prognosis. Therefore, LDH may represent a clinically useful biomarker capable of aiding in the accurate prediction of PRROC development and patient prognosis.

The substantial lactate produced by cancer cells via the Warburg effect is predominantly exported by monocarboxylate transporters 1 (MCT1) and 4 (MCT4). Compared to normal tissues, both MCT1 and MCT4 show an upregulation trend at the mRNA level in PRROC tissues. However, multiple lines of evidence suggest that MCT4 holds more prominent clinical significance: its high expression is not only associated with poorer patient survival outcomes but also correlates negatively with survival in mouse models [56, 57]. Furthermore, its expression positively correlates with genes involved in cancer cell invasion, migration, and immune evasion, positioning it as a potential key therapeutic target [56, 57].

Nevertheless, a large-scale parallel evaluation study provides an important complementary perspective. This study simultaneously quantified multiple metabolic markers, including MCT1 and MCT4, using tissue microarrays from over 300 patients [58]. The results revealed that none of the markers, including MCT4, demonstrated independent prognostic value, and their expression showed no association with clinical benefit from current chemotherapy/anti-angiogenic therapies [58]. Such discrepancies may stem from intrinsic tumor heterogeneity, the complex relationship between protein expression levels and functional activity, as well as the high plasticity of metabolic pathways. Therefore, relying on the expression of a single metabolic protein as a universal prognostic biomarker has limitations. Future research needs to integrate spatial multiomics and dynamic metabolic analyses to systematically decipher the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of tumor metabolism.

The substantial accumulation of lactate within the TME leads to a significant decrease in extracellular pH. This acidosis exerts direct and broad inhibitory effects on the function of effector immune cells, thereby promoting tumor immune escape. Both lactate and the associated low pH reduce the cytotoxicity of NK cells, concurrently decreasing the production of perforin and granzyme B, which are essential for the cytotoxic activity of NK cells [59, 60]. Tumor-derived lactate exerts multifaceted inhibitory effects on the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Lactate not only suppresses CTL proliferation in a dose-dependent manner and reduces the production of key cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ [61, 62] but also markedly impairs their cytotoxic efficacy. Specifically, in tumor co-culture environments, lactate decreases the number of T cells capable of releasing cytotoxic granules and reduces the intensity of granule release, leading to a significant decline in the secretion of cytotoxins like perforin, granzyme A, and granzyme B [63]. Mechanistically, lactate interferes with cytokine secretion and lytic granule release by inhibiting the activation of p38 and c-Jun N-terminal kinases [64]. T cells exposed to a lactic acidotic environment exhibit diminished metabolic capacity, failing to efficiently utilize glucose for energy metabolism. Their responsiveness to anti-CD3 stimulation is also attenuated, alongside a reduced extracellular acidification rate. These factors collectively likely contribute to their decreased cytotoxic activity [63]. Studies have demonstrated that lactate can directly inhibit several key functions of CD8⁺ T cells, including the secretion of cytokines such as interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interleukin-2, all of which are crucial for an effective anti-tumor immune response [65]. Under high-lactate conditions, the proliferative capacity and cytotoxicity of CD8⁺ T cells are significantly impaired, while their susceptibility to activation-induced cell death is increased, collectively undermining their anti-tumor efficacy [66, 67]. Notably, regulatory T cells (Tregs) not only adapt to but can even enhance their immunosuppressive function in a high-lactate environment [68, 69]. Elevated lactate levels within the TME enhance Treg survival, increase the Treg-to-effector T cell ratio, thereby amplifying overall immunosuppression.

Acidosis within the TME establishes the physicochemical foundation for immunosuppression and is thus a critical prerequisite for ovarian cancer cells to achieve immune escape. In the TME, the role of lactate extends far beyond creating an acidic milieu that indirectly suppresses or modulates immune cell function. It also acts as a key signaling messenger, directly regulating immune cell functions through receptor-mediated signaling pathways and systemically driving immunosuppression. Lactate can enhance the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) on tumor cells via its receptor GPR81, thereby inhibiting the cytotoxic activity of CD8⁺ T cells and promoting immune tolerance within the TME [70]. Furthermore, lactate upregulates ESM1 expression, which in turn enhances SCD1 activity, thereby suppressing the anti-tumor response of CD8⁺ T cells. The ESM1-SCD1 axis activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, leading to increased P-glycoprotein expression, which confers resistance to platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin in ovarian cancer cells [71]. Lactate also impacts the CTLA-4 signaling pathway, further exacerbating T cell dysfunction. Increased extracellular lactate promotes the expression of the FOXP3 gene in regulatory T cells, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that impedes the activation of effector T cells through CTLA-4-mediated inhibitory mechanisms [72]. Additionally, lactate inhibits the transcriptional activity of nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), a key regulator of IFN-γ production, consequently reducing IFN-γ generation in NK cells as well [73].

Certain key metabolites, such as lactate, glucose, and glutamine, are able to directly regulate the direction of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) differentiation (Table 2). TAMs are highly plastic, core immune cells within the TME. Traditionally, based on their activation states and functions, they have been simplified into a dichotomous polarization model: the anti-tumor M1 type (classically activated) and the pro-tumor, immunosuppressive M2 type (alternatively activated) [74, 75]. However, this dichotomy cannot fully capture the true heterogeneity and dynamic changes of macrophages in the TME. In recent years, with the development of technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing, researchers have discovered a broader spectrum of functionally diverse and continuously transitioning macrophage subsets in the TME, moving beyond the traditional binary paradigm (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, in the field of metabolic immunoregulation, particularly in studies investigating the impact of lactate on TAMs, the M1/M2 model remains widely used due to its conceptual clarity, with most research on lactate-mediated macrophage regulation still employing this framework to describe their pro-tumor (M2-like) or anti-tumor (M1-like) functional shifts. Therefore, while utilizing this classic terminology, this section will incorporate an understanding of the functional complexity of macrophages to systematically elaborate on how lactate drives the reprogramming of TAMs towards an “M2-like” phenotype characterized by immunosuppression, pro-angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling within the TME of ovarian cancer.

Macrophage polarization comprises two primary forms: M1-like and M2-like, each induced by distinct stimuli. M2-like macrophages further differentiate into four subtypes (M2a, M2b, M2c, and M2d). Unique surface markers distinguish these populations, while divergent cytokine secretion drives opposing functional roles in disease contexts

Lactate acts as a key signaling molecule, driving the polarization of TAMs towards an M2 phenotype and sustaining their functions through multi-layered mechanisms. Overexpression of the Myc gene in PRROC cells activates HIF1α, increases lactate secretion, and induces immunosuppressive M2-type macrophages via the Gpr132 receptor, thereby impairing CD8⁺ T-cell function and promoting immune escape [112]. This provides a novel potential therapeutic target (e.g., targeting Gpr132) for OC, particularly the Myc-high subtype. Furthermore, lactate can promote PRROC progression by inducing H3K18 lactylation in macrophages, which in turn activates CCL18 expression [113]. Clinical data show that serum lactate levels correlate with tumor stage and poor prognosis, and CCL18 expression positively correlates with serum lactate levels. Functionally, lactate-induced M2-like macrophages promote OC cell proliferation and migration in a Gpr132- and CCL18-dependent manner, while inhibition of the relevant genes or the use of anti-CCL18 antibodies can reverse these effects. On the other hand, lactate can also activate the inflammasome in macrophages, promoting IL-1β release. IL-1β subsequently upregulates PD-L1 expression in tumor cells via the NF-κB pathway and increases CCL2 secretion, further recruiting immunosuppressive macrophages and inhibiting CD8⁺ T-cell function, thereby establishing a positive-feedback immunosuppressive loop. This study reveals an IL-1β-centered TAM-tumor cell interaction mechanism and suggests that IL-1β is a potential target for reversing immunosuppression and enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in PRROC [114]. In most tumors such as PRROC, lactate strongly drives M2 polarization and promotes invasion through pathways such as mTORC2-AKT [115, 116].

In summary, the metabolic reprogramming in PRROC constitutes a multi-layered, dynamically plastic network system. Regardless of the preferred upstream energy metabolism mode in tumor cells, the persistent accumulation of lactate within the TME and its systematic remodeling of immune cells represent a clear and critical common pathway linking metabolic dysregulation to immune evasion. The profound immunosuppressive microenvironment established by lactate directly results in the failure of the host’s “immune-mediated clearance” mechanisms. Consequently, even though platinum-based agents may effectively eliminate a portion of tumor cells during initial therapy, residual tumor cells can survive and proliferate within the lactate-fortified “immune-privileged” niche, ultimately leading to clinically observed disease recurrence and the development of acquired resistance. This mechanism reveals that targeting the lactate axis. For instance, through the development of LDHA inhibitors [117, 118], small-molecule inhibitors or blocking antibodies against MCT1/4 [56, 119, 120], glycolysis inhibitors (like 2-DG, 3-BG) [121], or the use of buffers such as sodium bicarbonate to neutralize intratumoral acidity [122] —is not merely an immunotherapeutic strategy aimed at reversing immunosuppression. More importantly, it may represent a therapeutic approach to fundamentally disrupt the protective TME, thereby overcoming platinum resistance.

Amino acid metabolism abnormality produces tryptophan and glutamine as mediators of immune tolerance

Amino acids, as essential nutrients for the survival of all cells in the body, provide the building blocks for macromolecules such as nucleotides, thereby supporting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion. The dysregulation of amino acid metabolism is closely linked to both tumorigenesis and antitumor immunity. Metabolic pathways of representative amino acids, such as tryptophan and glutamine, synergize with lactate metabolism to collectively establish a metabolic barrier that fosters immune tolerance and therapy resistance in OX. In vivo, over 95% of tryptophan is metabolized via the kynurenine (KYN) pathway, with the remainder processed through the serotonin and indole pathways [123]. Tryptophan is catabolized into a series of biologically active metabolites, including kynurenine, with tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO2), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-1 (IDO1), and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-2(IDO2) serving as the three key rate-limiting enzymes regulating this pathway [124].

Tumors exploit the mechanism of IDO1 to foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Upon stimulation by inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ, IDO1 is activated. It promotes the formation of immune tolerance by depleting tryptophan in the TME and generating tryptophan metabolites, such as kynurenine. This process impairs the function of effector T cells, thereby enhancing tumor survival. In the TME of ovarian cancer, the enzymatic activity of IDO1 not only alters metabolic pathways like tryptophan catabolism, but its metabolite kynurenine (KYN) also acts as a signaling molecule to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) in T cells. This drives the upregulation of PD-1 expression on CD8⁺ T cells and other T cell subsets, promoting T cell exhaustion and thereby augmenting immunosuppression [125]. OCs with high IDO1 expression can actively recruit immunosuppressive cells, such as Tregs and myeloid-derived cells, to the tumor site by altering the chemokine profile in the microenvironment. This leads to a decreased proportion of antitumor CD8⁺ T cells and further reinforces the immunosuppressive state [126, 127]. While IDO1 plays a well-defined role in immunoregulation, its homolog IDO2—though sharing some substrates—is considered to have significantly lower catalytic efficiency in tryptophan catabolism, and its precise biological functions remain incompletely understood [128]. However, this does not imply that IDO2 is functionless. Emerging research suggests that IDO2 may contribute to tumor progression through mechanisms independent of its enzymatic activity. For instance, AhR signaling-mediated IDO2 expression has been shown to potentially promote tumor growth and help shape a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [129]. Similarly, IDO2 expression has also been associated with cancer cell evasion of immune surveillance [130]. These findings demonstrate that the role of IDO2 in tumors may extend beyond classical tryptophan metabolism, involving more complex signaling or immunomodulatory mechanisms that warrant further investigation.

TDO2 promotes tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and reduces sensitivity to cisplatin by activating the AhR signaling pathway and upregulating genes such as SPARC [131]. Its expression is significantly higher in PRROC tissues compared to normal tissues and is upregulated by oncogenic signals like RasV12. Overexpression of TDO2 directly drives the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ovarian cancer cells. This indicates that TDO2 is not merely an immunomodulator of the TME but also a direct driver of the intrinsic malignant behavior of tumor cells, making it a promising independent therapeutic target [132]. Furthermore, both IDO1 and TDO2 can directly suppress NK cell-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), thereby impairing the efficacy of antibody-based therapies [133]. Notably, studies have revealed that in HGSOC, the role of TDO2 may be more critical than that of IDO1. Its activity is closely associated with poor patient prognosis and tumor growth, and inhibiting TDO2 can more effectively reduce kynurenine levels and suppress tumor growth [134]. However, compared to targeting a single enzyme, the use of dual IDO1/TDO2 inhibitors may more completely ablate this metabolic axis. Preclinical studies have shown that such dual inhibitors, when combined with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, can synergistically inhibit tumor growth and significantly prolong survival. This provides a novel “metabolic-immune” combination strategy for overcoming the immunotherapy bottleneck in PRROC [126, 135].

The rapid proliferation of ovarian cancer cells is highly dependent on glutamine. As a crucial nitrogen source for the synthesis of proteins, purines, and pyrimidines, glutamine has become an indispensable “conditionally essential amino acid” in their proliferation process. Driven by oncogenes, the overexpression of glutamine metabolism-related enzymes and transporters collectively promotes substantial uptake and utilization of glutamine, thereby providing essential energy and metabolic substrates for their accelerated growth [136]. Deprivation of glutamine suppresses the production of perforin-1 and granzyme B in CD8⁺ T cells, while also inducing mitochondrial damage and apoptosis [137, 138]. Moreover, reduced glutamine levels further decrease glutathione (GSH) levels in NK cells, impairing their cytotoxic function and enabling cancer cells to evade immune attack [139]. Most studies indicate that following platinum-based treatment, both glucose and glutamine metabolism are enhanced in platinum-sensitive ovarian cancer cells. In contrast, platinum-resistant cells exhibit a strong metabolic reliance on glutamine, characterized by upregulated expression of the glutamine transporter ASCT2 and glutaminase (GLS), as well as increased oxygen consumption rate, suggesting heightened dependence on the tricarboxylic acid cycle [60]. Functional experiments confirm that sustained activation of glutaminase can induce acquired platinum resistance. Conversely, inhibiting this enzyme can restore the sensitivity of resistant cells to platinum agents. Combined use of the glutaminase inhibitor BPTES with platinum-based drugs synergistically suppresses ovarian cancer cell growth [140, 141]. However, other studies have found that in some cisplatin-resistant cells, glutamine is primarily utilized for nucleotide (purine and pyrimidine) biosynthesis rather than replenishing the TCA cycle. Consequently, inhibiting GLS may paradoxically lead to intracellular glutamine accumulation, promoting nucleotide synthesis and accelerating the proliferation of resistant cells. This may occur because blocking the conversion of intracellular glutamine to glutamate maintains glutamine at levels that support nucleotide biosynthesis [142]. For such cases, directly targeting nucleotide synthesis pathways with antimetabolites—such as the thymidylate synthase inhibitor 5-fluorouracil or the ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor clofarabine—either alone or in combination with platinum-based agents, may represent a more effective strategy to enhance ovarian cancer cell sensitivity to platinum drugs.

Furthermore, research confirms that aberrant activation of the MYC oncogene is a key driver of chemotherapy resistance in PRROC. The high expression of its downstream effector molecule, GLS, directly confers intrinsic resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy [143]. Therefore, for this subset of MYC-driven, GLS-high resistant OCs, the combined application of GLS inhibitors and PARP inhibitors has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy with translational potential. Currently, research data on glutamine metabolism mechanisms in PRROC remain relatively limited. Inhibiting glutamine metabolism pathways in combination with platinum chemotherapy may represent a novel approach for treating resistant ovarian cancer [144].

Based on the above discussion, amino acid metabolic reprogramming—particularly involving the tryptophan and glutamine pathways—plays a pivotal role in PRROC progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain in this field, including the heterogeneity of metabolic pathways, the precise roles of different enzyme isoforms, and the exploration of optimal combination therapies. Future research should employ multiomics technologies and preclinical models to deeply dissect the heterogeneity of metabolic dependencies in PRROC. This will facilitate the development of personalized combination treatment strategies based on metabolic subtyping, thereby opening new avenues for overcoming drug resistance and improving patient outcomes.

Lipid metabolism reprogramming plays key roles in immunosuppression and drug-resistance of PRROC

Aberrant lipid metabolism represents a critical feature of metabolic reprogramming in PRROC. The remodeling of lipid metabolism within the TME not only supplies essential building blocks for biomembrane synthesis and energy reserves for cancer cells but also profoundly influences ovarian carcinogenesis and progression by modulating cholesterol metabolism and key fatty acid oxidation pathways. Furthermore, it is closely associated with the development of chemotherapy resistance.

Cholesterol plays a significant regulatory role on immune cell function within the TME, acting as a key player in shaping an immunosuppressive milieu. Studies demonstrate that ovarian cancer cells promote cholesterol efflux from macrophage membranes, which disrupts their lipid raft integrity and suppresses IFNγ response. This process drives the polarization of TAMs towards a pro-tumorigenic phenotype, thereby promoting tumor growth [145]. Additionally, in cholesterol-rich TME, CD8⁺ T cells uptake excessive cholesterol, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress and the activation of its key sensor, X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1). This ultimately results in the functional exhaustion of CD8⁺ T cells, fostering an immunosuppressive environment conducive to tumor progression [146]. Key proteins and enzymes involved in cholesterol metabolism drive PRROC progression, and their homeostatic regulation directly impacts tumor sensitivity to platinum-based drugs. Research has revealed a unique cholesterol metabolic reprogramming in PRROC cells: their endogenous biosynthesis capacity is diminished, while they exhibit heightened dependence on exogenous cholesterol uptake. Silencing the TRAP1 gene mimics the metabolic alterations observed in resistant cells. Statin-mediated inhibition of cholesterol synthesis reduces cisplatin-induced apoptosis, whereas inhibiting the lipid metabolism enzyme LIPG or removing lipid components from the culture medium enhances sensitivity to cisplatin. This suggests that targeting cholesterol uptake and utilization, rather than its synthesis, may be an effective strategy for reversing platinum resistance [147].

Elevated levels of 27-Hydroxycholesterol (27HC), a major metabolite of cholesterol, have been established to correlate with resistance to carboplatin-based drugs in ovarian cancer cells [148]. Furthermore, metabolic precursors in the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway, such as isoprenoids, can function as critical cellular second messengers. They are capable of triggering signaling cascades associated with multidrug resistance, promoting the gene transcription of drug efflux transporters, and enhancing metabolic reprogramming in resistant cells [149]. In PRROC, increased cholesterol levels in resistant cells can activate the cholesterol receptors LXRα/β, leading to the upregulation of drug efflux pump proteins, such as MDR1 and ABCG2. This process subsequently induces multidrug resistance to agents like cisplatin and paclitaxel. Experimental evidence indicates that depleting cholesterol using MβCD or knocking down LXRα/β with siRNA can effectively reverse the resulting chemotherapy resistance and suppress the upregulation of these efflux pumps. Therefore, targeting the ascites cholesterol–LXRα/β–MDR1 signaling axis represents a plausible therapeutic approach for improving chemotherapy efficacy in PRROC [150].

To adapt to the glucose-deficient and hypoxic TME, ovarian cancer cells enhance fatty acid uptake and activate the critical metabolic pathway of fatty acid β-oxidation. This metabolic reprogramming enables cancer cells to efficiently utilize fatty acids as an energy source, thereby sustaining survival under the oxidative stress induced by cisplatin-based chemotherapy [151]. The rate-limiting enzyme for fatty acid β-oxidation, CPT1A, is highly expressed in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) and is associated with poor prognosis. Its inhibition or knockout can resensitize HGSOC cells to platinum-based drugs [152]. Further research has identified the solute carrier transporter Ct-OATP1B3 as a key upstream regulator of this metabolic pathway that drives metastasis. It coordinately enhances the expression of CPT1A and mitochondrial metabolic proteins, significantly boosting the mitochondrial metabolic activity of cancer cells—including fatty acid β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation. This process generates substantial ATP, supplying the necessary energy for tumor cell migration and invasion, ultimately accelerating ovarian cancer metastasis [153]. Furthermore, another key enzyme in fatty acid metabolism, ACSL1, is closely linked to the malignant progression of ovarian cancer. Elevated ACSL1 expression correlates with poor prognosis, metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance. ACSL1 promotes the survival of ovarian cancer cells and facilitates their peritoneal dissemination. Specifically, ACSL1 enhances the stability and membrane localization of FSP1 through myristoylation, thereby helping cells resist ferroptosis. In clinical samples, ACSL1 expression shows a positive correlation with FSP1 and a negative correlation with ferroptosis markers, confirming that the ACSL1/FSP1 axis is a critical pathway enabling ovarian cancer to adapt to the microenvironment, acquire drug resistance, and promote metastasis [154]. Notably, ovarian cancer exhibits a propensity for metastasis to the omental adipose tissue, shaping a unique TME rich in lipids. This environment not only provides ample lipid substrates for the metabolic reprogramming described above but has also been demonstrated to promote tumorigenesis, facilitate immune evasion, enhance chemoresistance, and contribute to recurrence [155].

Cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism play pivotal roles in ovarian cancer metastasis, the development of drug resistance, and the construction of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Consequently, interventions targeting these lipid metabolic pathways have emerged as promising strategies to overcome current clinical challenges. Beyond inhibiting cholesterol synthesis, other strategies that modulate cholesterol homeostasis, such as blocking its uptake, promoting its efflux, or accelerating its consumption, have also demonstrated therapeutic potential. For instance, cholesterol-lowering drugs such as statins have demonstrated a preliminary trend toward improved patient survival in clinical studies of ovarian cancer. On the other hand, targeting the fatty acid oxidation pathway holds therapeutic value as well; specifically, inhibiting its rate-limiting enzyme, CPT1A, can effectively induce cancer cell apoptosis [152, 156]. Furthermore, fatty acid oxidation inhibitors like etomoxir have exhibited clear anti-tumor activity in patient-derived xenograft models [152, 157]. Collectively, these preclinical and clinical findings indicate that targeting cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism represents a valid therapeutic strategy for high-grade serous ovarian cancer, offering a new direction for metabolic intervention to break through existing treatment bottlenecks.

Glycogen metabolism abnormality regulates immune cell “hunger” and nutritional competition

Glycogen, a macromolecular polymer composed of glucose, serves as an intracellular glucose reservoir. Within the TME, cancer cells engage in abnormally active glycogen metabolism to rapidly deplete and compete for the limited available glucose, thereby depriving anti-tumor immune cells of their energy supply. Concurrently, the substantial accumulation of lactate produced by this metabolism creates an acidic microenvironment that persistently suppresses the functional activity of immune cells. These two mechanisms work in synergy to systematically undermine the body’s anti-tumor immune response by operating at two levels: “energy deprivation” and “functional suppression.”

Although elevated glycogen content had been observed in various cancer cell lines as early research indicated [158], the specific role of glycogen metabolism in cancer progression was not extensively explored for a long time. Recent studies have revealed that glycogen metabolism is crucial for sustaining cancer cell survival under nutrient-scarce and hypoxic conditions within the TME [159–161]. These advances highlight the critical role of glycogen metabolism in tumor biology and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target in cancer. Studies have shown that ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) is characterized by significant intracellular glycogen accumulation. Under hypoxic conditions, these cells exhibit reduced sensitivity to cisplatin and doxorubicin, indicating an association between glycogen accumulation and drug resistance [162]. Similarly, in drug-resistant cervical cancer stem cells, the key gluconeogenesis/glycogen synthesis enzyme PCK1 promotes glycogen breakdown by phosphorylating PYGL. This redirects metabolic flux towards the pentose phosphate pathway, significantly increasing NADPH production. This enhanced antioxidant capacity facilitates ROS scavenging, ultimately helping cells survive chemotherapy pressure and develop resistance [163]. Glycogen metabolism also indirectly influences cancer progression by modulating the TME. Glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3), a key enzyme involved in glycogen synthesis, exerts multifaceted regulatory effects within the TME. By influencing immune cell functions (such as T cells, NK cells, TAMs) and metabolic reprogramming, GSK-3 has emerged as a potential target for tumor immunotherapy [164].

Specifically, its subtype GSK-3β can promote tumor development by regulating macrophage polarization: it suppresses the function of anti-tumor M1-type macrophages while enhancing the activity of pro-tumor M2-type macrophages, thereby cooperatively promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression [165]. Furthermore, GSK-3β plays a critical role in promoting cancer cell proliferation itself, making it a promising therapeutic target against cancer cells. Studies have shown that GSK-3 is overexpressed in various tumors, including ovarian cancer, and inhibiting its activity has become one of the effective strategies for treating ovarian cancer [166].

In PRROC, upregulation of the key glycogen metabolism enzyme glycogen phosphorylase B (PYGB) can drive cancer cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and tumorigenicity in vivo by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Further research found that miR-133a-3p directly targets the 3′ untranslated region of PYGB to inhibit its expression, thereby blocking this pro-oncogenic pathway. This provides new insights for PRROC therapy targeting the “miR-133a-3p/PYGB/Wnt–β-catenin” axis [167].

In summary, under stress conditions, glycogen metabolism plays a crucial role in sustaining tumor cell survival. This metabolic process is intricately regulated by hypoxia and various kinase signaling pathways, with its biological effects exhibiting multifaceted and dynamically interconnected characteristics. It is hypothesized that glycogen metabolism serves both as a primary energy source within the TME and as a critical foundation for the tumor itself and its response to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. For example, the hallmark pathological feature of OCCCs is abundant intracellular glycogen accumulation, and it often presents clinically with aggressive behavior. However, the role of glycogen metabolism in other subtypes, such as the high-incidence HGSOC, has not yet been fully investigated. Approaches under investigation include direct inhibition of key enzymes such as glycogen phosphorylase [167, 168] or with upstream signaling hubs like the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [167, 169]. These interventions are frequently designed to be combined with standard chemotherapy or targeted agents, aiming to reverse or circumvent drug resistance. Notably, preclinical studies highlight their potential efficacy in hypoxic, treatment-refractory settings. While most of these strategies remain at the preclinical stage, targeting glycogen metabolism represents a rational and forward-looking avenue to address persistent challenges in ovarian cancer therapy.

NNMT-mediated metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation in PRROC

In PRROC, metabolic reprogramming mediated by Nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT) represents a key mechanism. NNMT catalyzes the methylation of nicotinamide, consuming the methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and affecting epigenetic modifications, while simultaneously inhibiting the NAD+ salvage synthesis pathway, exacerbating NAD+ depletion and metabolic dysfunction. This process not only sustains tumor cell survival but can also remodel the TME to suppress immune responses, offering a new direction for metabolic-targeted therapy in ovarian cancer.

This metabolic remodeling directly promotes tumor cell proliferation and survival while also altering the composition of metabolites within the microenvironment—such as the accumulation of the methylated product 1-methylnicotinamide. This impacts the functional state of infiltrating immune cells, for instance, by inhibiting T cell activation and effector function [170], recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [170], and polarizing macrophages towards an M2 phenotype [171], thereby synergistically establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Consequently, targeting NNMT and its downstream metabolic signaling holds promise for restoring methylation homeostasis and NAD+ balance, reshaping anti-tumor immune responses, and emerging as a novel strategy for metabolism–immune combined therapy.

Notably, NNMT is overexpressed in various tumors, including high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma. Its protein levels are significantly higher in metastatic and recurrent lesions compared to primary tumors, while being nearly undetectable in normal ovarian and fallopian tube tissues [172–174]. Furthermore, high NNMT expression is associated with aggressive behavior in ovarian cancer and predicts poor prognosis. Inhibiting NNMT effectively blocks the proliferation of ovarian cancer cells under metabolic stress conditions, such as low glucose availability [172, 175].

The activity of NNMT is also precisely regulated by upstream factors. Research has found that the fat mass-associated protein FTO (an N6-methyladenosine RNA demethylase) influences the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to platinum-based drugs by modulating NNMT activity. Reduced FTO expression correlates with platinum resistance, whereas forced expression of FTO enhances platinum sensitivity, manifested as increased DNA damage and elevated apoptosis rates [176]. In high-grade ovarian cancer, loss of BRCA1 function drives metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells by upregulating NNMT expression, manifesting as reduced mitochondrial respiration and decreased ATP levels. This metabolic abnormality renders cells significantly more sensitive to drugs targeting energy supply, such as WZB117 (which inhibits glucose uptake) and VLX600 as well as tigecycline (which inhibit mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation). This finding elucidates a direct link between BRCA1 deficiency and NNMT-mediated metabolic vulnerability, offering a novel precision therapeutic strategy targeting metabolism for ovarian cancer patients with BRCA1 defects or NNMT overexpression [177]. Notably, the pro-resistance role of NNMT also exists in specific subtypes—in OCCC, high NNMT expression is associated with chemotherapy resistance, and suppressing its expression enhances cancer cell sensitivity to paclitaxel [178]. This suggests NNMT may serve as a new therapeutic target for OCCC. In colorectal cancer patients, elevated NNMT expression has been linked to chemotherapy resistance [179]. Similarly, studies in ovarian cancer indicate that NNMT expression is higher in drug-resistant patients compared to drug-sensitive patients, hinting at a potential association between increased NNMT expression and drug resistance [175]. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

In summary, NNMT acts as a critical node connecting genetic alterations, metabolic reprogramming, and therapeutic response, demonstrating significant regulatory functions and therapeutic targeting potential across different ovarian cancer subtypes and genetic backgrounds. It has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for reversing malignant phenotypes such as platinum resistance in ovarian cancer. Current major strategies include developing small-molecule inhibitors that directly inhibit its enzymatic activity (such as JBSNF-000088) [180], using RNA interference to knock down its expression at the genetic level [181], and indirectly interfering with its function by supplementing with metabolic sensitizers like nicotinamide [182]. However, all these investigations remain at the preclinical stage, facing challenges such as tissue selectivity due to the dual roles of its enzymatic activity and bottlenecks in in vivo delivery efficiency. Therefore, the most promising future direction lies in combining NNMT inhibitors with standard chemotherapy, PARP inhibitors, or immunotherapy, aiming to overcome resistance through multi-pathway synergy.

Multiomics insights into the TME in PRROC

The TME embodies heterogeneity and is intricately linked to tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. While tumorigenesis was once primarily attributed to cancer cell mutations, research increasingly highlights the TME’s critical role. Heterogeneity significantly contributes to drug resistance. Multiomics, integrating transcriptomics, genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, offers high-resolution, comprehensive insights (Table 3). Analyzing multiomics alterations uncovers PRROC molecular features, facilitating innovative diagnostics and treatments.

Application of genomics

Genomics investigates an organism’s genome, concentrating on DNA sequence determination, assembly, annotation, and variation analysis to unravel genome structure, function, and regulatory mechanisms [195]. Advances have revolutionized disease understanding, including cancers, and facilitated targeted treatments. Genomic instability is a cancer hallmark [196, 197]. In OC, nearly half exhibit defects in DNA repair pathways, with homologous recombination (HR) most frequently affected [198, 199]. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) are primary techniques for analyzing PRROC’s genomic landscape. Recent studies identified a genomic signature predicting platinum resistance in HGSOC. WGS/WES detected common alterations, including TP53/TTN mutations and HR defects, in platinum-sensitive and resistant patients [200]. A study employing CloneSeq-SV technology, through long-term tracking of 18 patients, revealed that resistance in high-grade serous ovarian cancer originates from pre-existing minor clones present at diagnosis rather than from newly emerged clones during treatment. These clones undergo selective expansion under therapeutic pressure and are characterized by genomic alterations such as chromosomal deletions, whole-genome duplications, and amplifications of specific oncogenes (such as CCNE1, RAB25, MYC, and NOTCH3). This process leads to reduced clonal complexity at recurrence, demonstrating that drug resistance is essentially a phenomenon of clonal evolution. These findings provide a critical foundation for future adaptive treatment strategies based on dynamic clonal monitoring [201]. However, this study has notable limitations. For instance, it cannot comprehensively identify all clones present at diagnosis, struggles to precisely determine the timing of resistance emergence, and does not fully account for critical influencing factors such as the TME. Future research should incorporate more frequent sampling and integrate multidimensional data to enable a more complete understanding of clonal evolution dynamics. A model investigating paclitaxel/carboplatin chemoresistance was developed, identifying differential expression of HLA-DPA1, SAP25, AKT3, and PIK3R5, along with TMEM205 and POLR2A mutations, as critical in chemoresistance development [202]. Genomic instability is a key prognostic marker linked to tumorigenesis and progression [203, 204]. Germline BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations exacerbate genomic instability [205], and APOBEC3A as a protective factor and important genomic instability indicator holds significant potential for predicting prognosis and immunotherapy response of PRROC [206, 207]. Spatial functional genomics, using Perturb-map technology to knock out 34 receptor/ligand genes predicted in cancer-macrophage communication via CRISPR-Cas9, revealed that Plaur, Serpine1, and Mif deletion damaged tumor growth, while Ccl7, Ccl3, and Calr knockout enhanced tumor growth [208].

Continuous improvement of single-cell genomics technology enhances the study of tumor cellular heterogeneity, though most research focuses on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) [209–211]. scRNA-seq is pivotal for dissecting HGSOC TME. Previous research demonstrated JAK/STAT pathway inhibition significantly suppresses tumor growth [212]. Another study classified three tumor immune phenotypes based on T cell infiltration: immune-infiltrated, immune-excluded, and immune-deserted [213]. A recent investigation characterized key stromal cell phenotypes in HGSOC TME regulation, including TGFβ-driven CAFs, lymphatic endothelial cells, and mesothelial cells [214]. Mapping ecosystem landscapes of early/late-stage HGSOC revealed tumor cells defined by EMT-associated gene markers; NOTCH1, SNAI2, TGFBR1, and WNT11 were a critical poor-prognosis gene signature [215]. Genomics deepens PRROC molecular mechanism understanding and provides a molecular foundation for precision medicine. Its breakthroughs are evident in cancer diagnosis/treatment and complex diseases [216–218]. However, genomic sequencing data are vast and complex. Current analytical techniques incur high costs and struggle with accurate interpretation. For example, in repeat expansion diseases like Huntington’s, long repeat strings exceed short-read sequencing read length, hindering accurate region reconstruction [219]. The MHC region also challenges short-read sequencing due to the absence of a comprehensive haplotype catalog and phase information [219]. Although long-read approaches span structural variants and preserve phase information, high cost and excessive DNA requirements (> 1 µg) limit clinical adoption. Iterative technological improvements and cost optimization remain critical. At the clinical level, there remains a lack of clear evidence that genomic profiling significantly improves patient quality of life or reduces mortality, a challenge particularly pronounced in highly heterogeneous tumors such as ovarian cancer. A single biopsy often fails to capture the full clonal architecture of the tumor, while resistance mechanisms typically involve multiple mutations and pathways that vary across different subtypes. This complexity not only increases the difficulty of developing effective targeted therapies but also limits the clinical utility of precision genomic analysis.

Application of proteomics

Proteomics, emerging as a significant biological research field following genomics, focuses on comprehensive, systematic protein analysis in cells, tissues, or organisms. The proteome encompasses all investigable protein isoforms from alternative splicing and post-translational modifications (PTMs). This domain facilitates elucidation of individual protein functions, protein-protein interactions, and dysregulated pathways, achieving profound biological system understanding and complementing genomics [220]. Investigating disease-associated proteins offers insights into pathological mechanisms, facilitating diagnostic biomarker identification and therapeutic target development. Proteomics has broad applications in cancer tumor marker research: constructing tumor specimen profiles for patient stratification; aiding prognosis assessment; revealing disease mechanisms; and searching for circulating tumor markers.

Proteomic techniques include mass spectrometry (MS), two-dimensional gel electrophoresis (2DGE), and reverse-phase protein arrays (RPPA). High-resolution MS enables protein quantification across a vast dynamic range. MS-based liquid biopsy demonstrates utility across diverse malignancies [221]. A recent study applied microscaled TMT-MS-based proteomic/phosphoproteomic workflow to 30 HGSC patients, quantifying 7,290 proteins across 87 tumor specimens and 12,914 phosphosites in 66 specimens; 101 proteins were significantly altered in patients with differential neoadjuvant chemotherapy responses [222]. Characterizing the proteogenomic landscape of 242 HGSOC samples (refractory/sensitive) identified a 64-protein signature predicting platinum-based chemotherapy resistance [223].

Numerous dysregulated proteins are identified between platinum-resistant and sensitive OCs. Researchers developed three homogenic EOC models of acquired platinum resistance (TOV-112D, OVSAHO, and MDAH-2774) through unbiased proteomics. HSP90 was upregulated in platinum-resistant cells and recognized as a central hub within protein networks. Targeting HSP90 resensitized platinum-resistant cells to cisplatin. Combining cisplatin with an HSP90 inhibitor showed synergistic antitumor effects in xenograft models [224]. Proteomics also uncovers differential markers in rare OC subtypes; Morita et al. used 2DGE/MS to identify potential markers distinguishing clear cell from mucinous adenocarcinoma [225].

Studying proteins and their PTMs provides valuable HGSOC tumor biology insights. Beyond basic proteome characterization, proteomics investigates specific PTMs in OC, including phosphorylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination. The most extensive PTM study was by CPTAC, integrating TCGA OC genomic/transcriptomic data with proteomic/phosphoproteomic analyses of 169 HGSOC tumors [226]. Recently, CPTAC conducted extensive proteogenomic/phosphoproteomic analysis of 83 HGSC tumors and 20 fallopian tube precursors [227], validating TCGA findings on HRD and histone acetylation influencing prognosis, and highlighting mitotic/CDKs as therapeutic targets. Proteomic quantitative analysis investigated the mitochondrial phosphoproteome in 8 EOC and 11 benign ovarian tissues, offering new insights into mitochondrial protein phosphorylation in PRROC pathogenesis [228].

Blood is an accessible biofluid rich in diagnostically relevant molecules. Plasma proteomics enables mechanistic disease insights, with LC-MS-based quantitative proteomics the primary workflow for novel cancer biomarker discovery. Kohli et al. identified serum ADT response biomarkers in advanced prostate cancer via iTRAQ-based profiling, identifying 47 candidates including elevated β-estradiol and estrone sulfate [229]. Implementing serum proteomic biomarkers for high-risk OC screening faces challenges, as tumor-specific loss-of-function mutations/downregulation impede reliable detection despite high plasma target protein abundance [230, 231]. A deep proteomics on 813 EOC tissue/plasma samples was conducted, detecting 10,715 proteins, which identified eight malignant phenotype-associated tissue proteins as circulating plasma biomarkers and developed targeted detection systems for 12 tissue and 7 plasma proteins to address clinical EOC diagnostics/therapeutic targeting limitations [232].

Proteomics significantly enhances PRROC biology understanding by providing an additional functional information layer. Undoubtedly, proteomics remains crucial for deciphering PRROC complexity and, integrated with other disciplines, plays a vital role in improving patient outcomes and advancing personalized care. However, its translational application faces multiple interconnected challenges, which collectively constitute significant obstacles in moving from basic discovery to clinical implementation. Proteomics workflows are complex, where subtle variations in sample handling, instrument platforms, and data analysis can all introduce bias and lead to divergent results. Studies have pointed out that some early reports of “near-perfect” protein signature diagnostic models for ovarian cancer could not be reproduced in subsequent independent validations. While high-throughput mass spectrometry can identify thousands of proteins in a single run, the task of filtering this vast amount of data to identify biomarkers with genuine biological significance and clinical value remains a formidable challenge. The path from initial discovery to final validation is lengthy, and each step—such as validation using Western blotting or immunohistochemistry—can introduce new biases or errors. Consequently, the vast majority of candidate proteins emerging from initial studies stall at the discovery phase, failing to progress into rigorous, large-scale clinical validation cohorts. This creates a substantial “translational gap” between publication and clinical utility.

The inherent biological characteristics of ovarian cancer—particularly its pronounced tumor heterogeneity—further amplify these technical limitations. There are substantial molecular differences within a single tumor (intra-tumor heterogeneity) and between different patients (inter-tumor heterogeneity). Traditional single-site biopsies or “bulk” proteomic analyses can only capture the molecular profile of a specific tumor region or provide an averaged protein expression profile, failing to reflect the complete molecular landscape of the tumor [233]. This incomplete view directly impacts the accuracy of proteomics-based molecular subtyping and may lead to the oversight of critical therapeutic targets, ultimately limiting its potential to guide personalized treatment.

Application of metabolomics

Metabolomics focuses on the comprehensive, quantitative study of small molecular metabolites (< 1,000 Da), such as sugars, organic acids, lipids, amino acids, and aromatic hydrocarbons. Complementing genomics and proteomics, metabolomics offers insights into biological system metabolite profiles.

This approach shows great promise in cancer research, with MS and NMR spectroscopy being common techniques [234]. MS is highly reliable for metabolite identification and analyzing cancer cell metabolism/reprogramming pathways [235, 236]. GC-MS-based metabolomics reported increased saturated fatty acids and decreased unsaturated fatty acids in EOC patient serum versus healthy controls. An MS-based study analyzing 232 lipids identified significant increases in specific ceramides (C16, C22, C24) in EOC patients [237]; elevated levels may link to heightened ceramide synthase (CerS6, CerS2) activity supporting tumor progression by inhibiting apoptosis [237–239]. A large-scale metabolic analysis was conducted for 448 ovarian cancer patient plasma samples using UPLC/MS [240], identifying metabolic signatures and potential biomarkers for early detection and disease stage differentiation.

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI), a label-free spatial omics technology, exhibits unique TME research advantages. Mapping spatial metabolite distributions in tissue sections enables assessment of tumor heterogeneity, cellular plasticity, and molecular interactions [241]. MSI accurately classifies HGSOC subtypes via biomarker-driven signatures, facilitating precision therapeutic stratification [242]. Vibrational spectroscopic techniques, including infrared and Raman spectroscopy, are pivotal metabolomics tools, characterizing biochemical composition by detecting molecular vibrational transitions to generate spectral fingerprints capturing biochemical states [243]. Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) amplifies signals via plasmonic nanomaterials, elevating detection sensitivity to single-molecule level [244]. A study based on SERS technology enabled effective prediction of postoperative platinum-based chemotherapy sensitivity in ovarian cancer patients by visualizing polysulfides in tumor tissues. The research further revealed that cystathionine-γ-lyase (CSE) can serve as an independent biomarker to distinguish chemotherapy non-responders from responders. Using SERS, the research team detected significant polysulfide signals in tumor tissues of non-responders and demonstrated that these compounds protect cancer cells from death by alleviating cisplatin-induced DNA damage, thereby driving chemoresistance [245].

TME metabolite availability crucially regulates tumor growth and immune cell function [246]. Cancer cells outcompete other cells for nutrients and produce immunosuppressive metabolites. Consistent with the Warburg effect, lactate is consistently upregulated across tumor types [247]. A study utilized proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H NMR) metabolomics to comparatively analyze the serum metabolic profiles of 17 patients with HGSOC and 14 patients with benign gynecological conditions. Through multivariate statistical methods such as orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), the research revealed that serum levels of lipids, choline, branched-chain amino acids, 3-hydroxybutyrate, and acetoacetate were significantly lower in HGSOC patients, while lactate levels were markedly elevated [248]. Additionally, analysis using high-resolution magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (HR-MAS NMR) spectroscopy revealed that, compared to the stromal tissue of benign tumors, the fibrotic stroma surrounding HGSOC lesions exhibited significantly elevated levels of several amino acid metabolites. These included glutamine, glutamate, acetate, lysine, alanine, leucine, and isoleucine [249]. TNBC exhibits elevated GLS1 expression and serum glutamine levels, rendering cells vulnerable to glutamine deprivation and underscoring GLS1’s critical role [250, 251]. Multiple studies observe alterations in glycolysis, FAO, oxidative stress responses, and increased metabolites like tocopherols and glutathione [252–256]. These metabolomic changes provide deeper OC metastatic microenvironment insights. Integrated with other methodologies, findings help identify specific TME molecule roles, enhancing tumor biology understanding.

Although metabolomics is not yet as routinely adopted in clinical settings as other omics technologies, it holds significant potential in cancer screening, diagnosis, and treatment. Realizing this potential requires a deeper understanding of how metabolic measurements reflect physiological states and the underlying mechanisms of cancer biology. Metabolomics achieves its optimal utility when integrated with multi-omics platforms and hypothesis-driven research to identify alterations of functional significance, elucidate molecular mechanisms within the TME, and refine existing paradigms in cancer biology.

Applications of transcriptomics

ScRNA-seq is a widely adopted method for individual cell transcriptome analysis. Droplet-based platforms (10x Genomics) and microwell-based systems (BD Rhapsody™) are extensively utilized [257]. Intertumoral heterogeneity is critical for personalized treatment; intratumoral heterogeneity drives chemotherapy resistance and therapeutic failure in OC. These landscapes influence treatment response disparities [258]. Single-cell sequencing in OC identifies specific cell markers for classification, analyzes differential gene expression to reveal functional roles, uncovers key functional gene signatures, and explores cell-gene associations to uncover regulatory networks and pathways (Fig. 3). Liang et al. analyzed scRNA-seq data from eight HGSOC cases, identifying 20 tissue-specific cell clusters. Leveraging OC immune microenvironment heterogeneity, they developed a dual-gene prognostic stratification system for clinical risk evaluation [259].

The application of single-cell sequencing technology in ovarian cancer

scRNA-seq has elucidated the complexity of tumor heterogeneity by identifying stage-specific biomolecular ecosystems—characterized by distinct signatures, regulatory networks, and intercellular crosstalk—thereby advancing our understanding of ovarian cancer pathogenesis, therapeutic response, disease progression, and drug resistance [212, 215, 259–261]. Most OC transcriptomic studies using scRNA-seq focus on cancer cells; few investigate TME heterogeneity, including crucial immune cell types for patient stratification, targeted treatment, and prognosis prediction. Research at single-cell resolution via scRNA-seq reveals new immune cell differentiation regulators and deciphers immune system heterogeneity. scRNA-seq characterizes chemoresistance evolution in HGSOC; comparative analysis of pre-/post-chemotherapy specimens reveals stress-adapted cancer cell subpopulations pre-exist and undergo subclonal expansion during treatment [215].

A recent study, employing single-cell transcriptomic profiling of matched primary and metastatic lesion samples, revealed the cellular heterogeneity and molecular features of HGSOC. A total of 13,571 cells were analyzed, identifying six major cell types: epithelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells. Notably, an aggressive epithelial subpopulation with high proliferative potential and drug-resistant properties was discovered. Multiple novel markers specifically upregulated in epithelial cells were validated for their roles in tumor growth and migration [262]. A highly immunosuppressive microenvironment was depicted in HGSOC, revealing that the majority of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T lymphocytes exhibit terminal exhaustion and impaired effector function, while macrophage populations undergo a dynamic phenotypic shift, losing their immune-recruiting capacity and enhancing the secretion of pro-tumorigenic factors during tumor progression [215]. This finding underscores the pivotal role of the TME in disease evolution. Recently, a study employing scRNA-seq identified a critical tumor cell subtype in HGSOC—the C2 IGF2⁺ subtype—closely associated with poor prognosis. This subtype is characterized by high copy number variation and terminal differentiation features, and it interacts with fibroblasts in the TME through the MK signaling pathway, thereby shaping an immunosuppressive milieu. The study further identified PRRX1 as a core driver of malignant phenotypes such as proliferation, migration, and invasion in this subtype. Based on these insights, a multi-gene model was constructed that can evaluate prognosis, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity, offering new targets and tools for personalized therapy [263]. Notably, technologies such as scRNA-seq can not only characterize such immune microenvironments but also be applied to dissect the mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance. In this context, scRNA-seq has been employed to delineate the evolution of chemoresistance in HGSOC. Comparative analysis of pre- and post-chemotherapy samples revealed that stress-adapted cancer cell subpopulations pre-exist prior to treatment and undergo subclonal expansion during therapy [215]. Another study employing RNA sequencing revealed significant upregulation of Aurora-A gene expression in cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer organoids. Further mechanistic investigation demonstrated that Aurora-A drives resistance through a dually coordinated pathway: on one hand, it suppresses the cellular senescence pathway, as evidenced by the downregulation of senescence-related genes such as p53, p16, and p21; on the other hand, it promotes glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, marked by the upregulation of glycolytic genes including HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA, along with increased levels of related metabolites. Collectively, these findings indicate that escape from cellular senescence and enhanced glucose metabolism are pivotal processes underlying cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. Ultimately, the study integrated these phenotypes and attributed them to a novel Aurora-A/SOX8/FOXK1 signaling axis. Activation of this axis enhances chemotherapy resistance in ovarian cancer by cooperatively inhibiting senescence and promoting metabolic remodeling. This discovery not only elucidates a new molecular mechanism through which Aurora-A promotes drug resistance but also provides a potential therapeutic target for reversing platinum resistance in ovarian cancer [264].

Recent advances in the application of single-cell sequencing have enabled high-resolution dissection of intratumoral heterogeneity, inter-tumor diversity, and mechanisms of tumorigenesis. However, inherent limitations persist. A fundamental drawback of scRNA-seq is the complete loss of spatial context—the original tissue location of cells is not preserved, complicating the study of cell-cell interactions and microenvironmental niches. Moreover, the technique is subject to notable technical noise and bias, including poor capture efficiency for low-abundance genes, batch effects, and uneven cell-capture efficiency, all of which can compromise quantitative accuracy. Additionally, the harsh tissue dissociation process may disrupt the native gene expression state of cells. Finally, scRNA-seq does not allow targeted analysis of specific morphological regions within intact tissue architecture. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) emerges as a critical solution to reconstruct spatially resolved gene expression architecture, mitigating scRNA-seq contextual deficit. ST delivers spatially resolved molecular profiling, enabling transcriptome-wide detection of hundreds of genes while precisely mapping their histological distribution, facilitating investigation of cellular typology, functional states, and intercellular interactions within tumors/microenvironments. ST provides a robust framework for elucidating tumor cell genetic/phenotypic diversification, quantifying spatial gene expression heterogeneity, and establishing multidimensional tumor biology perspectives.

An analysis of single-cell data across multiple cancer types has identified broadly conserved functional-state modules in cancer cells, such as interferon response and stress states. Spatial profiling further revealed that the interferon-responsive state of cancer cells shows a distinct spatial association with T cells and macrophages in the TME. Mouse model experiments confirmed that this state is modulated by the local tumor niche and the presence of lymphocytes. From a systems perspective, the study proposes that these conserved cancer cell states, through dynamic crosstalk with the microenvironment, collectively shape system-level tumor properties, including immune evasion, drug resistance, and metastasis [265]. A study comparing spatial transcriptomics with single-cell sequencing revealed that in the TME of short-term survivors with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), a distinct subtype of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)—characterized by αSMA⁺VIM⁺PDGFRβ⁺ expression—exhibits high density and elevated expression of periostin and CD36. Located at the tumor-stroma interface, these CAFs function through APOE-LRP5 signaling. They more effectively promote malignant progression, stemness, and chemoresistance in tumors, thereby elucidating a key mechanism underlying differences in patient survival [266].

Another study focusing on OCCC, through integrated spatial transcriptomics and in vitro co-culture experiments, identified a chemotherapy-resistant cell subpopulation localized to CAF-enriched regions, whose resistance is associated with HIF-1α signaling activation. The research further uncovered a positive feedback loop between HIF-1α-activated cancer cells and PDGF-activated CAFs, which underpins chemotherapy resistance in OCCC [267]. To address the unresolved question of how intercellular interactions in the TME influence survival rates in HGSOC patients, researchers developed an innovative SpatioImageOmics (SIO) multi-omics analytical pipeline. This pipeline integrates imaging mass spectrometry, spatial transcriptomics, and deep learning to automatically and precisely identify the spatial distribution and adjacent interactions among tumor, stromal, and immune cells, from which it extracts key features and biomarkers associated with patient survival. The analysis revealed high diversity in both tumor and stromal cells within HGSOC, identified prognostically relevant cellular phenotypes, and clarified how these features shape a microenvironment that simultaneously promotes tumor growth and suppresses immune responses [268]. Furthermore, by combining single-cell RNA sequencing, bulk transcriptomics, and spatial transcriptomics, researchers discovered that a novel CAF subtype known as STAR⁺ cells is enriched in chemotherapy-responsive serous ovarian cancer patients. These cells are metabolically active, located adjacent to tumor cells, and may enhance chemotherapy sensitivity by suppressing WNT signaling within tumors. Protein expression levels of STAR⁺ cells positively correlate with chemotherapy response and favorable prognosis, and chemotherapy can dynamically increase their abundance, suggesting that they are not only a key factor in improving chemotherapeutic efficacy but also a potential biomarker for predicting treatment response [269]. While ST technology continues to evolve, its maturity remains relatively low despite significant advances in basic research. A primary limitation of ST is its restricted resolution, as most current platforms generate mixed signals from multiple cells rather than true single-cell data and lack subcellular localization information, often necessitating complex deconvolution analyses for inference. Furthermore, the sensitivity and throughput of the technology are generally limited, with fewer genes captured compared to single-cell RNA sequencing, difficulty in detecting lowly expressed genes, and insufficient whole-transcriptome coverage in some targeted approaches. Additionally, spatial data analysis is complex, costly, and challenging due to the high-dimensional structure and interaction modeling required. Coupled with expensive experimental procedures and specialized analytical expertise, these factors currently constrain its broader application. Enhancing the resolution, sensitivity, and development of cost-effective workflows capable of achieving single-cell resolution will significantly impact cancer research, benefit clinical practice, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Integrative multiomics analysis

Single-omics approaches fail to comprehensively elucidate tumorigenesis and progression due to lacking synergistic information across omics layers. Empirical evidence shows isolated analysis of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic data is insufficient. Integrated multi-omics analyses are essential for accurate biological conclusions. Tumor multi-omics overcomes single-modality limitations, resolves heterogeneity, identifies oncogenic drivers, and deciphers molecular interactions within the TME, providing a foundation for precision targets and clinical decisions [270, 271].

Proteogenomics anchors on genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics [272]. It formulates mechanistic hypotheses via genomic variant interpretation, validates functional consequences with proteomics, deciphers tumor-specific protein abundance and PTM dynamics, and integrates data for precision phenotype prediction. Recent studies used integrated single-cell spatial proteomics (CyCIF) and spatial transcriptomics (GeoMX) to dissect the tumor-immune ecosystem in HGSOC [273]. CyCIF mapped immune populations and microenvironment states, enabling spatially guided ROI selection for transcriptomics. This revealed immune suppression and microenvironment remodeling in fallopian tube precursors.

A multi-omics study integrating RNA sequencing, proteomics, and nascent transcriptome analysis confirmed that STAT3 depletion suppresses ovarian cancer cell proliferation, migration, stemness, and tumorigenicity in vivo. Mechanistically, STAT3 knockout remodeled the expression profile of the STAT family, suppressed genes associated with EMT, cell cycle, and the E2F pathway, and concurrently affected stem-cell markers. This work systematically delineates the core network through which STAT3 drives ovarian cancer progression, providing a critical foundation for its development as a therapeutic target [274]. Multi-omics analysis combining single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial protein profiling revealed that diminished type-I interferon (IFN-I) signaling is a key driver of platinum resistance in chemotherapy-refractory ovarian cancer, and hypoxia pathway activation further exacerbates this resistance. Single-cell and spatial analyses confirmed that cancer cells with high IFN-I activity are more sensitive to chemotherapy, and exogenous IFN-α could restore cancer cell sensitivity to cisplatin. This study offers a potential biomarker and combination therapy strategy based on the interferon pathway for chemotherapy-refractory ovarian cancer [275]. Integrated metabolomic and proteomic analysis demonstrated that ovarian cancer cells co-cultured with adipocytes undergo profound lipid metabolic reprogramming, orchestrated by the lipid chaperone FABP4. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that inhibiting FABP4 not only reduces tumor burden but also resensitizes ovarian cancer cells to carboplatin [147]. By integrating transcriptomic, translatomic, and proteomic analyses, a study systematically elucidated how p53 mutation affects protein expression through a multi-layered regulatory network encompassing transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels. The research found that NDRG1 protein stability is markedly increased in R273H-mutant cells, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for platinum-based chemotherapy resistance in p53-mutant ovarian cancer. This discovery provides a new molecular basis and research direction for personalized treatment strategies targeting this ovarian cancer subtype [192].

Advances in computational science accelerate AI integration into cancer research. Machine learning (ML) interrogates multi-dimensional datasets for unrecognized patterns. Hu et al. used ML-driven multi-omics to integrate proteomic and glycoproteomic data from OC, identifying distinct glycosylation profiles associated with molecular tumor clusters [276]. These patterns reflect microenvironment-driven adaptations, providing novel OC pathogenesis insights. ML techniques will play an increasingly important role.

Multiomics technologies demonstrate transformative potential in multiple diseases including PRROC [277–284]. Omics integration reshapes disease understanding and treatment, revealing deep molecular mechanisms and opening paths for precision medicine. However, clinical translation faces challenges: data heterogeneity, fragmented platforms, and biological system complexity. Breaking through bottlenecks requires standardized workflows, AI-multiomics integration, and interdisciplinary collaboration to bridge research and clinical applications [270, 271].

Future perspectives

Patients are classified as PRROC or platinum-sensitive based on a platinum-free interval (PFI) shorter or longer than 6 months. NCCN recommends non-platinum chemotherapy and targeted therapy for PRROC [285]. PFS shortens with successive therapies, leading to PRD. The TME, a complex ecosystem of immune cells, stromal cells, and cancer cells, plays a pivotal role in tumor evolution and host immune interplay [286–290]. Research aligns with understanding microenvironmental influences on progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response.

However, our functional understanding of various immune and stromal cell types within the TME remains incomplete. Beyond extensively studied cell types like TAMs and Tregs, the precise roles of neutrophils, dendritic cells, B cells, and specific subsets of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the onset and progression of PRROC are still not well defined. For example, while the STAR⁺ CAF subtype has been shown to enhance chemosensitivity, the CAF subpopulations that drive resistance and their dynamic crosstalk with immune cells represent a significant knowledge gap [269]. These unknowns urgently require elucidation through high-resolution single-cell and spatial omics technologies. Furthermore, the precise mechanisms by which intense metabolic competition within the TME regulates immune cell function remain largely unelucidated. Tumor cells impair effector T cell and NK cell function while promoting the activation and polarization of immunosuppressive cells, such as M2 macrophages and Tregs, by avidly consuming glucose, producing lactate, and sequestering amino acids like arginine and methionine. In this process, metabolite transporters, receptors, and metabolic enzymes (such as MCT1/4, Arg1, and CD36) may serve as critical “metabolic checkpoints“ [56, 291–294]. They represent potential targets for reversing immunosuppression, yet their regulatory networks within PRROC are still not clearly mapped.

Another significant gap in current TME research lies in the shift from analyzing metabolic reprogramming in isolated cell types toward systematically elucidating the dynamic “metabolic ecosystem” jointly formed by tumor cells, immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and other stromal components. The essence of this system lies in the fierce competition among diverse cell types for key nutrients such as glucose and glutamine, coupled with their active exchange of metabolites including lactate, lipids, and tryptophan. This interplay spatially and functionally establishes an integrated network that sustains immunosuppression and therapy resistance. For instance, lactate is not merely a metabolic waste product but also a critical signaling messenger that regulates macrophage and T cell functions through receptors such as GPR81 [295–297]. Similarly, reprogramming of lipid metabolism is closely linked to T cell exhaustion [298–300]. The tryptophan-kynurenine metabolic axis, primarily driven by rate-limiting enzymes such as IDO1 and TDO2, depletes tryptophan in the TME and leads to the accumulation of kynurenine. This process subsequently activates the AhR signaling pathway, which directly promotes T cell exhaustion, enhances the immunosuppressive function of Tregs, and suppresses the activity of NK cells [126, 301–303]. Meanwhile, dysregulated cholesterol metabolism and heightened FAO not only provide energy and biosynthetic precursors for tumor cells but also profoundly affect immune cell functionality. Abnormal cholesterol accumulation in tumor cells can trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress and functional exhaustion in T cells, while enhanced FAO is closely linked to increased tumor invasiveness and the development of therapy resistance [146, 304, 305]. Notably, metabolic enzymes such as NNMT can modulate epigenetic landscapes by altering the ratio of SAM to S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) or influencing cellular NAD⁺ levels [180, 306–308]. These changes directly affect histone modifications, such as methylation and acetylation, leading to a reprogramming of gene expression and cellular function at the epigenetic level.

Deciphering this complex network presents substantial technical challenges. It necessitates moving beyond conventional bulk sequencing to develop and integrate cutting-edge technologies that preserve spatial information, such as single-cell metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics, and spatial proteomics. These should be combined with computational models to infer intercellular metabolite exchange and signaling crosstalk, thereby identifying key metabolic nodes (e.g., MCT4, ARG1) that drive malignant phenotypes as potential therapeutic targets.

Concurrently, a major bottleneck between fundamental discovery and clinical application is how to translate the resulting massive, multi-dimensional genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and spatial omics data into simple biomarkers or actionable predictive models that can guide clinical decision-making. This challenge stems from the high dimensionality, heterogeneity, and extreme biological complexity of the data, which often leads to overfitted and poorly interpretable models. The fundamental path to overcoming this bottleneck involves developing advanced cross-modal data fusion algorithms that integrate not only molecular data but also pathological imaging, radiomics, and clinical electronic health records to achieve integrative multidimensional analysis. Furthermore, leveraging AI technologies to construct fusion models capable of predicting prognosis and treatment response, followed by rigorous validation and iterative optimization through prospective clinical cohorts and experimental platforms, is essential. This closed-loop system—from data to knowledge to clinical validation, supported by functional validation using platforms such as organoids or patient-derived xenografts—is critical for driving the genuine clinical translation of TME research.

Conclusion

This review has systematically outlined the TME, energy metabolic reprogramming, and the immunosuppressive milieu shaped by immune cells in PRROC, while also reviewing relevant multi-omics technologies. Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that our understanding of this highly heterogeneous and dynamically evolving microenvironment remains incomplete. Only through interdisciplinary collaboration and the application of advanced technologies to deeply explore the aforementioned unknowns can transform the complex TME landscape into effective clinical targets, ultimately offering breakthrough therapeutic hope for patients with PRROC.
