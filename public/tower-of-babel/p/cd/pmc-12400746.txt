Patient-reported symptom burden and health-related quality of life in patients with aggressive Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiayue Jin, Simeng Ren, Wei Zhang, Ping Yang, Jingqi Yang, Hongkun Xu, Baojin Han, Wenzheng Zhang, Jie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-reported health-related quality of life (HRQoL) impacted by symptom burden is predictive of overall survival in patients with aggressive Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL).MethodThis cross-sectional study investigated the Core Symptoms Burden Set (CSBS) significantly affecting the HRQoL in patient with aggressive NHL and identify a single cutoff point to separate clinical difference. The MDASI-TCM and EQ-5D-5 L were used to assess the symptom (severity and interference) and HRQoL. The t test, chi-square test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to determine differences in symptoms burden between during and after cancer therapies. A multivariate linear regression model was used to identify associations between the CSBS and HRQoL. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to identify optimum cutoff point considered the grading scale (0 vs. 1–3) of the ECOG as the anchor.ResultsTotal 116 eligible patients with aggressive NHL were analyzed. The CSBS were disturbed sleep (47.4%), fatigue (44.3%), and difficulty remembering (40.9%). The EQ-5D index value ranged from − 0.294 to 1.0 and the median of EQ VAS was 80(Interquartile: 25). There were 22.4% of patients with “11111” health state. The CSBS demonstrated significantly influence in the physical function (B = 0.442, 95%CI 0.260–0.623, P < 0.001), psychological function (B = 0.674, 95%CI 0.495–0.853, P < 0.001), EQ-5D index value (B = -0.014, 95%CI [-0.027]-[-0.001], P = 0.033) and EQ VAS (B = -3.047, 95%CI [-5.028]-[-1.066], P = 0.003) for the patients with aggressive NHL. The best cutoff point of CSBS with significantly clinical difference was 2.50, with a sensitivity of 82.4% and specificity of 64.6%.ConclusionsThe Core Symptoms Burden Set of patients with aggressive NHL impacted their HRQoL significantly whose score of 2.50 or higher would be a potential preliminary indicator to be emphasized and intervened.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14730-8.Keywords: Aggressive Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Symptom burden, Health-related quality of life, Clinical difference

Introduction

For non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) in China, the disability-adjusted life years (DALY) and age-standardized DALYs increased by 100.9% and 15.6%, respectively, from 1990 to 2019. The age-standardized incidence rates also increased by 21.9% during the same period. Notably, the disease burden was substantially higher than the global average (DALYs: 9.18% vs. 1.4%) [1]. With continuous improvements in disease management strategies and the implementation of clinical practice guidelines incorporating individualized risk stratification, survival outcomes have improved significantly in some regions of China, achieving 5-year and 10-year overall survival (OS) rates of 62% and 52%, respectively [2]. In detail, the 5-year OS of patients with mature BCL, and PTCL changed from 49% to 41% in 1996–2000 to 65%, and 51% in 2011–2015, respectively (P = 0.002, and P = 0.592, respectively) [2]. Moreover, Thompson C et al. found that quality of life (QoL) is independently predictive of OS and QoL should be assessed as a prognostic factor in patients with aggressive lymphoma [3]. These findings underscore the importance of improving QoL in NHL patients.

However, comparative analyses reveal that NHL patients experience significantly greater symptom burdens—including fatigue, sleep disturbance, shortness of breath, anorexia, and diarrhea—relative to normative populations, with fatigue and sleep disturbances demonstrating particular persistence over time. The persistent symptoms burden adversely affects the QoL, and the severity of symptoms burden is negatively correlated with physical functioning and role functioning [4, 5]. However, the extensive sites of lymphoma invasion and complex pathological classification result in highly variable and often nonspecific symptoms, making them challenging to fully characterize.

A Patient-reported outcome (PRO) refer to any report of a patient’s health status provided directly by the patient, without interpretation of the patient’s response by a clinician or anyone else [6]. PRO can capture the symptom burden in patients more sensitively and accurately than clinicians reporting outcomes or observers (parents or caregivers) reporting outcomes. It had been reported that 80% of patients with lymphoma want a PRO report and receive feedback from their doctor on their symptoms burden and QoL [7]. In order to reduce data missing and improve the interpretability of PRO results, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommended that investigators would explore approaches to reduce the burden on patients evaluation, including the use of electronic PRO (ePRO) capture in non-medical Settings. Hongfan Yu et al. [8] found that ePRO could improve patients compliance and data quality compared with longitudinal data collection by paper and pencil.

This cross-sectional study investigated the Core Symptoms Burden Set (CSBS) significantly affecting the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with aggressive NHL measured by ePRO. Moreover, this study also aimed to identify a potential cutoff point to separate clinical difference establish a clinically meaningful cutoff point to differentiate symptom severity levels. Such a threshold would assist clinicians in prioritizing symptom management by distinguishing burdens with true clinical relevance, thereby enabling more targeted and scientifically informed care for aggressive NHL patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

Every patients with aggressive NHL identified in the outpatient and inpatient department at Guang’anmen Hospital and China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences between December 2022 and January 2024 would be invited to participate in our questionnaire. Olny participants who provided the written informed consent could receive the HRQoL questionnaires and all data will be kept strictly confidential in this cross-sectional observational study. This study was approved by the independent ethics committees of Guang’anmen Hospital and China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences.

Outcome measures

MDASI-TCM

The MD Anderson Symptom Inventory for traditional Chinese medicine (MDASI-TCM) is a patient-reported outcomes assessment tool designed to measure symptoms experienced by patients with cancer who opt for TCM [9, 10]. Along with the core MDASI’s 13 symptom items and 6 interference items (physical functioning [work, general activity, walking] and psychological.

functioning [relations, enjoyment of life, mood]), the MDASI-TCM also assesses 7 symptoms specific to Traditional Chinese Medicine approaches (Sweating, Feeling cold, Constipation, Coughing, Palpitations [racing heartbeat], Heat in the palms or soles of the feet, Bitter taste in the mouth). The recall of all items is over the past 24 h. Zhandong Li et al. had verified the MDASI-TCM is valid, reliable, and concise to capturing symptomatic changes in Chinese cancer patients and survivors receiving TCM-based care with 0.90 Cronbach α coefficients for symptom-severity items and 0.93 for interference items [9]. Known-group validity was also substantiated by the MDASI-TCM’s detection of differences in symptom severity according to performance status (P < 0.001) and interference levels by cancer stage (P < 0.05) [9]. All items are measured on a scale of 0 to 10, with 0 being “not present” and 10 being “as bad as you can imagine.”. A moderate-to-severe symptom burden was defined as any symptom or symptom interference scored as 4–10 on a 0–10 scale [11].

EQ-5D-5 L

The EQ-5D-5 L [12, 13] essentially consists of 2 pages: the EQ-5D descriptive system and the EQ visual analogue scale (EQ VAS). The descriptive system comprises five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has 5 levels: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems and extreme problems. This decision results in a 1-digit number that expresses the level selected for that dimension. The digits for the five dimensions can be combined into a 5-digit number that describes the patient’s health state. The EQ VAS records the patient’s self-rated health on a vertical visual analogue scale where the endpoints are labelled ‘The best health you can image’(100 score) and ‘The worst health you can image’(0 score).

Sample size

Based on the previous study [14], fatigue is the highly incident phenomenon in patients with lymphoma, whose incidence was 40.7% in China [15]. Before conducting this study, our pre-experiment also found that the incidence of moderate to severe (≥ 4 points) fatigue was 53.3%. One hundred eleven patients were required to detect the occurrence of fatigue at a 0.95 confidence level with desired margin of error to 0.1, accounting for a 10% non-response rate.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the participants’ demographic factors and patient-reported outcomes(MDASI-TCM and EQ-5D-5 L). The t test and chi-square test were used to determine differences in symptoms burden between during and after cancer therapies. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used if the normal distribution assumption was not met. The symptoms with top 3 in the both the average scores and proportion of moderate-to-severe symptoms on MDASI-TCM at both during and after therapy were defined as the Core Symptoms Burden Set (CSBS). The chi-square test, non-parametric rank sum test, and Spearman correlation analysis were used for univariate analysis to identify the influence of participants’ demographic factors and clinical factors of diseases on HRQoL (physical functioning, psychological functioning, EQ VAS and EQ-5D index value [16]). A multivariate linear regression model was used to identify associations between the CSBS and HRQoL with adjustment for statistically significant variables (potential confounding factors: P < 0.200) in univariate analysis. For the missing data, the data after multiple imputation is used for the sensitivity analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to identify optimum cutoff point considered the grading scale (0 vs. 1–3) of the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) as the anchor. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26, using 2-sided statistical testing at the 0.05 significance level.

Results

Study sample and baseline characteristics

Among 116 eligible participants with aggressive NHL, the average age of the participants was 51 years and 50.9% (n = 59) of participants were male. There were similar proportion of patients in married (n = 93, 80.2%) and covered by medicare (n = 98, 84.5%). In our study, 85.3% (n = 99) of patients had B-cell lymphoma, especially diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (n = 91, 78.4%), and nearly half (n = 57, 49.1%) of patients were at Ann Arbor stage III–IV. 58.6% of patients (n = 68) had an ECOG PS of 1–3. The most patients (75.9%, n = 88) had completed the treatment about more than six months earlier (median days since completed treatment: 191.5 days). Functioning status and quality of life

More than 20% of patients reported moderate-to-severe levels (4 to 10 of 10) of physical functioning and psychological functioning (21.84% and 22.41%, respectively). The most severe symptom interference was with “relations with other people” (Table 2).

This population reflects a response rate of 74.1% (n = 86) in EQ-5D-5 L among eligible participants. The EQ-5D index value ranged from − 0.294 to 1.0 and the median of EQ VAS was 80(Interquartile: 25). And there were 22.4% of patients with “11111” (meaning no problem of mobility, self-care, activity, pain and anxiety) health state. The result of univariate analysis indicated the potential impact of marital status, ECOG, health insurance, cancer stage, age, and comorbidity on quality of life (P < 0.200, supplement 2. for details).

The EQ-5D-5 L exhibited a 25.9% data missing due to the potential measurement burden. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in the impact of CSBS on EQ-5D-5 L by multiple imputation. Core symptoms burden set score cutoff point with clinical difference

With the grading scale (0 vs. 1–3) of ECOG as the anchor and the maximal Youden index of ROC curve, the best cutoff point of Core Symptoms Burden Set with significantly clinical difference was 2.50, with a sensitivity of 82.4% and specificity of 64.6% (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study evaluated the symptoms burden, function interference and quality of life in adult survivors of aggressive NHL, while also examining association between Core Symptoms Burden Set and HRQoL by ePRO tool. Our findings demonstrate that the Core Symptoms Burden Set of patients in aggressive NHL is disturbed sleep, fatigue, and difficulty remembering, which impacted their HRQoL (physical functioning, psychological functioning, EQ-5D index scores and EQ VAS) significantly. Moreover, this is the first report of potentially clinically meaningful cut point in composite symptom score of patients with aggressive NHL. We identified 2.50 points or higher in Core Symptoms Burden Set (mean score of disturbed sleep, fatigue, and difficulty remembering) as the clinical significance score. These results have important clinical implications for assessing and managing CSBS, while establishing a foundation for integrating practical, clinically meaningful PRO measures into NHL management throughout the treatment continuum.

Previous study showed that patients with NHL experienced more fatigue, dyspnea, sleeping problems, appetite loss, diarrhea and financial problems (all P ≤ 0.001 and clinically relevant) compared with the normative population; and mean fatigue scores remained significantly stable over time [4]. Beside the similar finding in reporting more fatigue and disturbed sleep, our study also found that the difficulty remembering was the core symptoms burden set which impacted their HRQoL. A prospective cohort study [17] reported that more than 60% of survivors were not satisfied with their present ability to concentrate or remember things. Moreover, long-term survivors with NHL showed a higher incidence of memory problems while patients with more symptoms burden and worse quality of life before treatment [17].

Despite a 25.9% missing data rate in EQ-5D measurements, sensitivity analyses by multiple imputation confirmed the impact of CSBS on EQ-5D-5 L, and the baseline was balanced between responders and non-responders.

The study RT3 specified that DLBCL patients have statistically significant but clinically marginally relevant (< 10 points) worse scores on physical functioning, role functioning, cognitive, and social functioning (P<0.01 or P<0.05) compared to a French normative population [18]. Our study also found more than 20% of patients reported moderate-to-severe levels (4 to 10 of 10) of physical functioning and psychological functioning (21.84% and 22.41%, respectively). Furthermore, the Core Symptoms Burden Set showed significant impact on physical functioning (B = 0.442, 95%CI 0.260–0.623,P < 0.001), psychological functioning(B = 0.674, 95%CI 0.495–0.853,P < 0.001), and quality of life (EQ-5D index value: B = −0.014, 95%CI [−0.027]-[−0.001], P = 0.033; EQ VAS: B = −3.047, 95%CI [−5.028]-[−1.066], P < 0.003). A similar conclusion was drawn by Tingting Liu et al. [19] in a cross-sectional study, who showed that symptom burden scores of patients with lymphoma after chemotherapy were negatively correlated with global health status, pain, physical functioning, and emotional functioning. Cecilie E Kiserud et al. [20] also found that chronic fatigue symptoms were significantly correlated with lower physical function, physical health, social function, and emotional problems (P < 0.001) in male patients with lymphoma. The high level of anxiety symptoms in lymphoma patients is also an influential factor for their quality of life (P < 0.01) [21].

The MDASI-TCM scale was used to identify and measure the symptoms burden in patients with cancer; and previous studies [11, 22–24] showed the statistically optimal cutoff points were 4 for moderate symptoms burden and 7 for severe symptoms burden. However, the cutoff point 4/7 was for evaluating the severity of the single symptom item which couldn’t define the composite symptoms burden severity categories. Wendy H. Oldenmenger et al. [24] found that ROC curve and regression models (such as multivariate analysis of variance, general linear model, etc.) were mostly used to define the threshold by system review about how to define the cutoff point of symptom burden with clinical significance. The Core Symptoms Burden Set in this study was the composite symptoms (fatigue, sleep disturbance, and difficulty remembering), and there was no clear evidence as to what the optimal cut points with clinically significance are. Therefore, our study used ROC curves to identify optimum cutoff point considered the grading scale (0 vs. 1–3) of the ECOG as the anchor. This is the first report that the Core Symptoms Burden Set score ≥ 2.50 is a potential trigger for a more attention and management. When the cutoff point would be as a definitive recommendation by external validation, it indicated when to start symptom management and could be used to evaluate the efficacy of the intervention. Each patient with aggressive NHL should be screened termly for CSBS to ensure that those with symptom burden with clinical significance are identified and treated promptly and effectively. When the CSBS score is more than 2.50, a more focused history and physical examination should be conducted to find the main symptom burden in CSBS. According to different main symptom burden in CSBS, such as fatigue, we would suggest the patients to enhance the physical activity [25] or join the yoga therapy program [26]; for sleep disturbance, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and sleep hygiene education should be included in the initial treatment of all NHL with sleep disorders [27]; for difficulty remembering, we could give patients instruction in self-management and coping strategies and routine physical activity [27, 28].

The cross-sectional study design constrained our analysis to contemporaneous evaluations of symptom burden and HRQoL at baseline assessment, which were intrinsically unable to account for dynamic changes in disease progression and symptom evolution over time. Moreover, the generalizability of cut-off point should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional nature of the study and its limited sample size, both of which may contribute to bias.

However, our study has some limitations. The missing data in EQ-5D could affect result interpretation, though we have mitigated this limitation through sensitivity analyses. This study has a cross-sectional design which does not allow a causal link to be established. And the external validation need to be conducted for the wide application of the cutoff point. Therefore, this cutoff point was only a potential preliminary indicator. In the future, a longitudinal study with larger samples would be conducted to validate the relationship between the Core Symptoms Burden Set and survival, and the specificity and sensitivity of cutoff point furtherly.

Conclusions

The Core Symptoms Burden Set of patients in aggressive Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is disturbed sleep, fatigue, and difficulty remembering, which impacted their HRQoL (physical functioning, psychological functioning, EQ-5D index scores and EQ VAS) significantly. The Core Symptoms Burden Set score of 2.50 or higher would be a potential preliminary indicator to be emphasized and intervened because of its clinical difference measured by ECOG. External validation in future research is required to establish the applicability of the cutoff point.
