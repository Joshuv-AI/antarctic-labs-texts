The cream-skimming behaviors of tertiary hospitals under medical alliances: evidence from China
Authors: Zixuan Peng, Xu Chen, Peter C. Coyte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile previous studies have delved into the formation and development of medical alliances in China, there has been limited focus on investigating inequity in the referral rates and the quality of care received provided to patients with experience of referral and those without under the introduction of medical alliances. This study explored: (1) inequity in the odds of being referred to healthcare institutions within medical alliances; and (2) inequity in the quality of care received between the referred and non-referred patients.MethodsThis study employed a dataset comprising 440,950 individuals who had at least one outpatient visit at healthcare facilities in Hangzhou city, Zhejiang province, China from January 1, 2020 to September 24, 2021. Quality of outpatient care was measured by the odds of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare institution. Binary regression models combined with random effects were constructed to examine inequity in the referral rates and the quality of care received. A set of sensitivity analyses were conducted to check the robustness of study findings.ResultsThis study has three key findings. First, outpatients’ insurance status, rather than their specific diseases and health conditions, was identified the most significant determinant driving healthcare institutions’ referral decisions. Compared with outpatients covered by public health insurance programs, those without such coverage were more likely to be referred by tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities (coefficient = 1.33; 95% CI: 0.56–2.11) while being less likely to be referred by primary care facilities to tertiary hospitals (coefficient = -2.00; 95% CI: -3.08 - -0.92). Second, the referred outpatients received poorer quality of care, as indicated by higher odds of having all-cause follow-up encounters within seven days at any healthcare institution, compared to those non-referred outpatients. Third, outpatients with chronic diseases and public health insurance coverage not only experienced higher referral rates but poorer quality of outpatient care after being referred from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities, compared to their counterparts.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that tertiary hospitals “siphoned-off” outpatients with public health insurance coverage from primary care facilities. Outpatients who were older, were male, with chronic diseases, and with public health insurance coverage were more likely to experience not only higher referral rates but poorer quality of outpatient care after being referred from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities. Tailored policies are required to protect and compensate the most vulnerable population groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02549-5.

Introduction

In 2009, the Chinese government launched a new round of nationwide reforms to ensure affordable and equitable access to high-quality basic health care for all its citizens [1]. Plenty of studies have shown that the healthcare reform since 2009 in China has yielded promising outcomes, such as the development of the primary care delivery system [2]. Nevertheless, China still grapples with major challenges in transforming its underperforming hospital-centric, fragmented healthcare delivery system into one that delivers high-quality and efficient care [1]. Specifically, a three-tiered healthcare delivery system (village clinics in rural areas and street hospitals in urban areas are the first level, township healthcare centers in rural areas and community healthcare centers/stations in urban areas the second level, and county hospitals in rural areas and city hospitals in urban areas the third level) has been established in China [3], with healthcare facilities in each tier undertaking distinct functions and roles. Although China has made remarkable efforts to strengthen the quality of its primary care facilities during the past decade, the performance of primary care institutions has not significantly improved [4–7]. Meanwhile, without the universal implementation of a formal hierarchical diagnostic and treatment system, Chinese patients always bypass primary care facilities to seek out care at secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities regardless of episodes of care [8–10]. Under such a twisted delivery system, limited medical resources in tertiary hospitals were seriously occupied by common diseases, while medical resources in primary care facilities were left underutilized, jointly resulting in a huge waste of medical resources [9].

With the goal of enhancing the efficiency of medical resources utilization and mitigating health inequalities across different regions in China [9, 11], the State Council of China released the ‘Guiding Opinions on Promoting the Construction and Development of Medical Alliances” in 2017 [12]. Medical alliance (also referred to as “medical consortium” in the literature) refers to medical organization formed by different levels of healthcare providers through vertical referral, or by the same level of healthcare providers through horizontal referral of the same or different specialties [13]. Specifically, four specific medical alliance modes have been introduced and widely implemented in China, including 1) urban medical alliance that is usually led by tertiary public city hospitals and joined by community healthcare facilities and nursing homes, etc.; 2) county medical alliance that is spearheaded by public county hospitals and joined by township healthcare centers and village clinics; 3) specialized medical association that is established to promote cooperation between special departments across different regions; and 4) remote care system that is designed to offer healthcare services for individuals in poverty-stricken areas [14]. A total of 118 cities [14] and 567 counties [15] started to pilot the medical alliance modes in China in 2019 [16]. By the end of 2023, more than 18,000 medical alliances had been established in China [16].

While the practical development of medical alliances has been flourishing, there has been a notable scarcity of Chinese studies exploring research topics regarding medical alliances1. One branch of research has focused on the behaviors of healthcare facilities during the implementation of the medical alliance modes. A study conducted in 2020 found that while a closer relationship was established between (tertiary and secondary) hospitals and community healthcare centers, a lack of reciprocal relationship persisted between either tertiary hospitals or township and secondary hospitals [7]. Some studies have demonstrated that the adoption of the medical alliance modes facilitated two-way referrals [8], although the rates of downward referral (referring patients from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities) were notably lower than the rates of upward referral (referring patients from primary care facilities to tertiary hospitals) [17–19]. Another branch of research has estimated the health effects of implementing the medical alliance modes (or its specific measures). For instance, some studies have shown that integrated nursing care [20], the Star Family Doctors Training Program (i.e., a comprehensive continuing professional development program for general practitioners) [21], health information technologies [22], and intensive intervention [23] implemented in medical alliances were conducive to patients’ health. A study conducted in 2020 demonstrated that the adoption of the medical alliance modes promoted the utilization of healthcare services in county and township hospitals within medical alliances [11]. Other studies, however, have concluded that although the adoption of the medical alliance modes enhanced the utilization of healthcare services at primary care facilities, its implementation failed to control the rising medical costs [24]. Despite this preliminary evidence, few research has explored inequity in the referral rates and the quality of care received by outpatients during the implementation of the medical alliance modes in China. As such, the goal of this study is twofold: 1) to investigate inequity in the odds of being referred to other healthcare institutions within medical alliances; and 2) to explore inequity in the quality of care received between the referred and non-referred outpatients under the implementation of the medical alliance modes in China.

Methods

Data sources

This repeated cross-sectional analysis took place at Xiaoshan, an urban district of Hangzhou city, Zhejiang province, China, with a population of 1.82 million population in 2019 [25]. Xiaoshan has a total of 57 hospitals and 782 primary care facilities (including 328 community healthcare centers/stations, 102 village clinics, and 352 other healthcare institutions) in 2019 [25]. In 2019, the Xiaoshan government established four medical alliances that are spearheaded by four tertiary hospitals (including the Xiaoshan Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, the Xiaoshan First People’s Hospital, the Xiaoshan Hospital, and the Xiaoshan Third People’s Hospital) and joined by other hospitals and primary care facilities in Xiaoshan (more details can be seen in Supplementary File S1).

We used visit-level administrative data collected by Xiaoshan Health Commission as part of its routine operations2. This dataset comprises encounter data on individuals who had outpatient visits at all healthcare facilities in Xiaoshan from January 1, 2020 to September 24, 2021. The dataset contains individuals’ demographic information and data on visit-level diagnosis. After removing observations with missing values, we finally obtained an analysis dataset comprising 440,950 individuals who had at least one outpatient visit at healthcare facilities in Xiaoshan from January 1, 2020 to September 24, 2021. Since an individual may visit different healthcare facilities, our sampled outpatients may have multiple observations during the study period.

Study variables

Referral rates and quality of outpatient care were two outcome variables assessed. Referral rates were measured by three binary variables, including: (1) two-way referral (equals 1 if the patient was referred to a healthcare institution within medical alliances and 0 otherwise); (2) upward referral (equals 1 if the patient was referred from a primary care facility to a tertiary hospital and 0 otherwise); and (3) downward referral (equals 1 if the patient was referred from a tertiary hospital to a primary care facility and 0 otherwise). We used the odds of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility as a proxy for the quality of outpatient care based on two reasons: (1) readmissions over short periods of time could reflect the quality of care provided and has been widely used by previous research as an indicator of quality of care [26]; and (2) we were unable to access information on patients’ clinical details and could only adopt utilization-based quality indicators.

We also included a set of control variables that have been shown to be determinants of the referral rates and the quality of outpatient care [8], including years of age, sex (female or male), insurance status (whether the patient had public health insurance coverage3 or not), occupation (public sector employees4, blue-collar workers, farmers, or other), marriage status (married or not), comorbidity degree (high, moderate, or low [27]5), chronic diseases status (whether the patient had chronic diseases [28] or not), and hukou status (whether the patient had a local hukou6 or not).

Statistical analyses

A set of statistical methods were used to explore inequity in the referral rates and the quality of outpatient care. First, a descriptive analysis was conducted to report the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values of the key study variables. Second, considering that both the referral rates and the quality of care were measured by binary variables, binary regression models were deemed suitable for analysis. Specifically, we estimated the impacts of age, sex, marriage status, occupation status, chronic diseases status, comorbidity degree, insurance status, and hukou status on the referral rates. We then compared differences in the odds of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility between the referred and non-referred outpatients. Since we had a longitudinal dataset, we adjusted the impacts of unobserved individual-, institution-, and day-level factors though fitting random effects models. Finally, to check for the robustness of our research findings, we conducted a set of sensitivity tests: (1) we instead fitted a model where the odds of having seven-day disease-specific follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility were included as the dependent variable; and (2) we tracked the degree of heterogeneity in the impacts of having referral experience on the quality of outpatient care received through performing subgroup analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using R language statistical software version 4.4.0 [29]. This study involves human participants and was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of Zhejiang Lab (reference 2023 No. 115).

Results

The descriptive results

Figure 1 displays the geographical distribution of medical alliances in Xiaoshan. Primary care facilities (represented as the pin) were located around the four tertiary hospitals (represented as the circle). Table 1 reports the descriptive statistics of the study variables. About 62.23% of the sampled outpatients had referral experience during the implementation of the medical alliance modes, among which 4.96% were referred from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities and 4.64% from primary care facilities to tertiary hospitals. About 67.09% and 29.27% of the sampled outpatients had seven-day all-cause and disease-specific follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility, respectively. The mean age of the sampled outpatients was 70 years (standard deviation = 7 years), with ages ranging from 1 to 106 years. Most of the sampled outpatients were female (50.35%), unmarried (66.93%), blue-collar workers (88.69%), had a low degree of comorbidity (91.32%), did not have chronic diseases (51.53%), had public health insurance coverage (87.66%), and had a local hukou (95.69%).

Inequity in the referral rates

Table 2 reports the impacts of the proposed factors on the referral rates. Outpatients who were older (coefficient = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.35–0.53), male (coefficient = 0.10; 95% CI: 0.08–0.12), married (coefficient = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.40–0.44), retired (coefficient = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.10–0.25), and had chronic diseases (coefficient = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.26–0.29) were more likely to be referred to healthcare institutions within medical alliances than their counterparts. In contrast, outpatients who had a lower degree of comorbidity (coefficient = -1.18; 95% CI: -1.36 - -1.00), were not covered by public health insurance programs (coefficient = -0.99; 95% CI: -1.07 - -0.90), and had a local hukou (coefficient = -0.06; 95% CI: -0.10 - -0.01) were less likely to be referred to healthcare institutions within medical alliances.

Inequity in the quality of outpatient care

Table 3 reports the differences in the odds of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility between the referred and non-referred groups. Compared with the non-referred outpatients, the referred outpatients were more likely to have seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility (coefficient = 4.62; 95% CI: 4.59–4.65). Specifically, outpatients who were referred from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities had higher odds of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility (coefficient = 7.51; 95% CI: 6.81–8.20) than those who were not. Outpatients who were referred from primary care facilities to tertiary hospitals also had a higher likelihood of having seven-day all-cause follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility (coefficient = 7.19; 95% CI: 6.71–7.68) than those who were not.

Discussion

This study combined binary regression models with random effects to explore inequity in the odds of being referred to healthcare institutions within medical alliances and in the quality of care received by outpatients with and without referral experience during the implementation of the medical alliance modes. Notably, patients’ insurance status emerged as the most significant determinant driving healthcare institutions’ referral decisions during the implementation of the medical alliance modes in China. This research also discovered that the referred outpatients experienced poorer quality of outpatient care (measured by an increase in the odds of having subsequent follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility within seven days) than their counterparts.

This study found that although primary care facilities referred those with a higher degree of comorbidity and public health insurance coverage to tertiary hospitals as expected, tertiary hospitals practiced cream skimming by choosing patients based on their insurance status other than their need for care. Cream skimming refers to healthcare providers’ practices of selecting patients for some characteristics other than their need for care [30], a concern that has been highlighted by previous studies as a persistent issue in the healthcare market [31, 32]. In our study, we discovered evidence indicating that tertiary hospitals exhibited a strong inclination to retain outpatients employed by public agencies and covered by public health insurance programs—typically individuals with stable employment and better socioeconomic conditions. In the meantime, these tertiary hospitals preferred to refer blue-collar workers, farmers, the retired, and individuals without public health insurance coverage to primary care facilities. These findings are at accordance with previous research that stressed the need to avoid that the leading hospitals within medical alliances may seize resources from township hospitals within medical alliances in China [11, 33]. The Chinese government has set a lower level of funding for insurance reimbursement to primary care facilities than tertiary hospitals [4]; hence, primary care facilities would be willing to refer those with public health insurance coverage to tertiary hospitals after spending their limited insurance budget. In comparison with primary care facilities, although hospitals within medical alliances are all public hospitals, financial support from the government was very limited and they needed economic income to support its development [11]. Individuals who worked for public agencies and covered by public health insurance programs tended to have more financial resources in China [34] and therefore, may incur higher medical costs that could support the operation of tertiary hospitals. This may offer explanations for the cream skimming behaviour of tertiary hospitals during the implementation of the medical alliance modes in China.

Another key finding of this study is that after adjusting for health status factors (including comorbidity and chronic health conditions), the referred outpatients were more likely to experience poorer quality of outpatient care, measured by an increase in the odds of having seven-day all-cause or disease-specific follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility. This finding may be attributed to the disruption of continuity of care due to referrals. Different medical service organizations may have contradictions in management concepts, performance appraisal principles, salary standards, hospital culture, and risk control capabilities [35]; hence, these institutions may offer different treatment plans to patients. Our subgroup analyses highlighted outpatients who were older, were male, with chronic diseases, and with public health insurance coverage were more likely to experience not only higher referral rates but poorer quality of outpatient care after being referred from tertiary hospitals to primary care facilities. These findings suggest that these particular population groups were at a greater risk of suffering from the unintended side effects of implementing the medical alliance modes in China.

The outcomes of this study offer a foundation on which to construct actionable policy recommendations. Notably, our research highlighted patients’ insurance status as the foremost determinant influencing healthcare institutions’ referral behaviors during the implementation of the medical alliance modes. We therefore suggest the establishment of a standardized referral mechanism centered around patients’ disease conditions and health status to reduce inequity and curtail the profit-seeking behaviors of tertiary hospitals [36]. Furthermore, our investigation underscored that the referred outpatients were at an elevated risk of experiencing poorer quality outpatient care when implementing the medical alliance modes. In light of this problem, we advocate for the construction of a quality monitoring system and a performance evaluation system [37]. Moreover, our subgroup analyses revealed that outpatients who were older, were male, with chronic diseases, and with public health insurance coverage faced the greatest susceptibility to experiencing the negative consequences of implementing the medical alliance modes. As a remedy, policy decision-makers may consider realigning the inappropriate incentives as part of China’s fee-for-service payment system [38] and initiating compensation policies (such as introducing financial incentives for healthcare providers tied to the achievement of specific predetermined quality benchmarks [39], including improvements in health outcomes and reductions in health inequity) to counteract harms caused to these vulnerable population groups.

This study has some notable strengths. First, although some studies have explored research topics related to medical alliances in China, a majority of them focused on the establishment and progression of diverse medical alliance modes. In contrast, little has been dedicated to exploring inequity in the referral rates and the quality of care received between the referred and the non-referred outpatients. Our study, to the best of our knowledge, constitutes the first analysis that examined the cream-skimming tendency of tertiary hospitals and the associated health consequences during the implementation of the medical alliance modes in China. Another commendable aspect of this study is its substantial practical implications for both China and countries currently exploring the establishment of medical alliances (such as the US and the UK [7]).

Certain limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, our research was conducted using datasets from a specific administrative region in China, which could potentially limit the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the datasets encompassed observations from all residents who underwent outpatient visits during the study period and included all the healthcare institutions in Xiaoshan7, which greatly enhanced the generalizability and representativeness of our study findings. The implementation of medical alliances was initiated by the central Chinese government and shares a large degree of similarity across different regions. Even though our study was conducted in one region in China, we anticipated that the insights this study offered hold relevance for other regions in China. Second, due to data unavailability, our study adopted utilization-based quality indicators. In our study, based on the assumption that readmissions within a short period after discharge often reflect poor quality of outpatient care, we used the occurrence of having seven-day all-cause or disease-specific follow-up encounters to any healthcare facility as a proxy for the quality of outpatient care. One potential concern with using readmission as a measure of outpatient care quality is that readmissions to primary care facilities after discharge from tertiary hospitals may not necessarily indicate poor quality outpatient care. In many countries, primary care serves as the first point of contact and plays a key role in coordinating follow-up care [40]. In China, however, despite significant government investment in strengthening the primary care system, patients often lacked trust in primary care providers and tend to seek care directly from hospitals for a broad range of health concerns [6]. Therefore, in the absence of strict enforcement of a hierarchical diagnosis and treatment system, we anticipated that any readmission within a short period after discharge would signal poor-quality outpatient care, with biases associated with the readmission measure being very limited. We strongly recommend that future investigations incorporate clinical indicators (encompassing both process and outcome measures), as well as subjective patient feedback (such as perceived quality of care and satisfaction levels), to offer a more comprehensive picture on the health outcomes of implementing the medical alliance modes. Finally, our study findings revealed the cream-skimming behaviors of tertiary hospitals that they lean towards retaining patients with financial advantages when implementing the medical alliance modes. Regrettably, we were unable to compare differences in healthcare expenditures between the referred and the non-referred outpatients as a result of lacking data. We therefore encourage future research to integrate data on patients’ healthcare costs to offer a deeper analysis of the potential profit-driven behaviors exhibited by tertiary hospitals when implementing the medical alliance modes.

Conclusion

This study revealed the behaviors of tertiary hospitals towards different groups of outpatients within the context of medical alliance implementation. Surprisingly, it was patients’ health insurance status, rather than their diseases and health conditions, determined healthcare institutions referral decisions. More problematically, individuals with chronic diseases and with public health insurance coverage experienced not only higher referral rates but poorer quality of outpatient care. These findings revealed the potential repercussions associated with the implementation of the medical alliance modes. Our study findings stressed the necessity for the Chinese government to devise policies aimed at diminishing inequity in both the referral rates and the quality of care provided to the vulnerable population groups when diffusing the medical alliance modes across China.
