Planar cell polarity complex internalization is dependent on Celsr1 cis-dimerization
Authors: Sarah G. Latario, Thomas Saba, Brandon Trejo, William Giang, Stephanie E. Zimmer, Danelle Devenport, Sara N. Stahley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPlanar cell polarity (PCP) coordinates collective cell polarity along a tissue plane through the asymmetric localization of junctional complexes comprised of core transmembrane proteins: Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2. Basal cells of embryonic mouse epidermis undergo numerous cell divisions to support the developing tissue while also maintaining tissue-level polarity. To achieve this, junctional PCP complexes are selectively co-internalized by the dividing cell, facilitating the restoration of tissue polarity upon mitotic exit. In Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis, we identify aberrant mitotic internalization of Celsr1 and Fz6, and a failure to co-internalize Vangl2. Mixed-cell in vitro assays reveal Crsh is deficient in mediating trans-endocytosis of Celsr1 and Vangl2. These defects are rescued through forced dimerization of Crsh, supporting the hypothesis that Celsr1 adhesive interactions stabilized by cis-dimers are required for both PCP establishment and maintenance via trans-endocytosis of PCP complexes. Overall, these findings establish a Celsr1 dimerization-dependent mechanism for control of tissue polarity during cell division.

Introduction

Planar cell polarity (PCP), the collective polarization of structures along a tissue plane, is essential for tissue formation and organization during development.1 Disruptions in PCP lead to developmental and patterning defects across organ systems, from disruptions in stereocilia organization in the inner ear to defects in neural tube closure to misaligned epidermal appendages.1,2,3 PCP is orchestrated by a conserved set of three transmembrane proteins: Celsr1, Frizzled6 (Fz6), and Vangl2.4 These proteins display polarized localization with respect to the tissue axis, with Vangl2 on the anterior and Fz6 on the posterior of the cell.4,5 This protein asymmetry is organized by homotypic interactions of Celsr1, which is found on both the anterior and posterior membranes of adjacent epithelial cells.2,4,5

Celsr1 is an atypical cadherin and adhesion GPCR, with a large extracellular domain containing nine cadherin repeats in addition to multiple EGF-like and laminin-G-like domains.6,7,8,9 These domains drive intercellular interactions that regulate Celsr1 junctional localization as well as co-enrichment of PCP proteins.10,11,12,13 Recent structural studies indicate the Celsr1/2 cadherin domains can form antiparallel dimers with only trans-interactions, as well as trimers and tetramers where Celsr is both cis- and trans-dimerized.14,15,16 However, the precise role of proposed Celsr1 adhesive interactions in regulating planar polarity remains unresolved.

In the murine epidermis, basal layer keratinocytes are planar polarized while also rapidly dividing during development and tissue homeostasis, presenting a challenge for polarity maintenance.11,17 This challenge is met through bulk internalization of the PCP complex during mitosis and restoration of planar polarity following cell division.17,18 PCP proteins are internalized both in cis from the membrane of the mitotic cell, and in trans across the cell junction from the neighboring cell.19 Proper tissue-level polarity is lost when mitotic internalization is hindered, demonstrating a vital role for this internalization pathway in polarity maintenance during development.17,18 Mitotic internalization of the PCP complex occurs through junctional trans-endocytosis via a process dependent upon the Celsr1 extracellular domain.19

Numerous studies have demonstrated the importance of the Celsr1 (Flamingo, Drosophila; fmi-1, C. elegans) cadherin domains for conserved functions from tissue polarity to neuronal dendrite morphogenesis.12,13,20,21 The Celsr1 variant Crash (Crsh, D1040G) harbors a single point mutation in the 8th cadherin repeat that maps to a calcium binding site between the 7th and 8th cadherin domain.22 Celsr1Crsh/Crsh mice present with numerous and severe developmental defects including craniorachischisis, misoriented inner ear stereocilia, misaligned hair follicles, and abnormal lung and kidney development.11,22,23,24 Studies suggest that this variation creates a flexible region within the cadherin repeats and disrupts cis-dimerization of Celsr1.12,22

Here, we utilize dimerization-deficient Crsh to elucidate the role of Celsr1 cis-interactions in regulating proper mitotic internalization of the PCP complex. Through immunofluorescence localization of endogenous PCP proteins in vivo, mechanistic dissection of cis-versus trans-interactions in vitro, and rescue approaches, we find Celsr1 dimerization is required for mitotic PCP complex internalization. Importantly, we find that Celsr1 cis-dimerization in both neighboring cells is necessary for trans-internalization of Celsr1 and Vangl2, while intercellular Celsr1-Fz6 interactions are sufficient for Fz6 trans-internalization. These findings indicate that Celsr1-Fz6 extracellular interactions contribute to trans-endocytosis and demonstrate a previously unrecognized role for Celsr1 cis-dimerization in PCP complex mitotic internalization in the mammalian epidermis.

Results

Aberrant mitotic internalization of PCP complexes in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis

During cell division, PCP complexes undergo endocytosis with Celsr1 co-internalizing both Fz6 and Vangl2. To determine if PCP complex internalization requires Celsr1 cis-interactions, we utilized the murine Celsr1 mutant Crsh. The Crsh variant mediates cell adhesion via trans-interactions but fails to mediate lateral cis-dimerization.12 In both Celsr1+/+ and Celsr1Crsh/Crsh embryonic back skin immunostained for endogenous Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2, mitotic cells of the basal epidermis displayed prominent cytoplasmic PCP protein puncta (Figures 1A and 1B). As previously reported, mitotic Celsr1 puncta co-localized with early endosomal marker EEA1 (Figure S1A).17 In Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis, mitotic cells displayed a significant increase in the number of Celsr1 and Fz6 puncta compared to Celsr1+/+ (Figures 1B and 1D). In contrast, Vangl2 mitotic puncta were strikingly absent in basal keratinocytes of Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis (Figures 1B and 1D, arrowheads). The relative differences in the number of Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2 puncta between Celsr1+/+ and Celsr1Crsh/Crsh were consistent across all stages of mitosis, from prophase through anaphase/telophase (Figure S2). However, the number of Celsr1 and Fz6 puncta in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh decrease in telophase relative to prophase and metaphase, suggesting a potential defect in the retention of puncta throughout mitosis typically observed in Celsr1+/+.17,18 While internalized puncta contained all three core PCP proteins in Celsr1+/+ epidermis, we find a significant decrease in the fraction of Celsr1 puncta that colocalized with either Fz6 or Vangl2 in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh (Figures 1C, 1E, and S3). Crsh does not substantially alter PCP protein internalization in cis

COS-7 cells were co-transfected with Fz6-mCherry constructs and either Celsr1WT-EGFP or Celsr1Crsh-EGFP (Figure 2A). This experiment was repeated with Vangl2-EGFP co-transfected with either Celsr1WT-mCherry or Celsr1Crsh-mCherry to determine if Crsh can cis-internalize Vangl2. While a significant portion of Vangl2 remains at the membrane and is not co-internalized to the same degree as Fz6, Vangl2 co-internalization by both Celsr1WT and Celsr1Crsh was still observed (Figure 2C). However, we note a significant decrease in co-localized Celsr1-Vangl2 mitotic puncta for Crsh compared to WT (Figure 2D). In cultured mouse keratinocytes, similar Celsr1-Fz6 and Celsr1-Vangl2 co-internalization with both Celsr1WT and Celsr1Crsh was observed (Figure S5B). These data indicate that Celsr1 cis-dimerization is not required for co-internalization of either Celsr1-Fz6 or Celsr1-Vangl2 complexes in cis. Therefore, reduced cis-internalization is unlikely to be causing the aberrant mitotic internalization seen in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh mouse embryonic back skin.

Crsh is deficient in trans-endocytosis

Since the Crsh mutation did not substantially disrupt internalization of PCP proteins in cis, deficient trans-endocytosis might underlie the aberrant mitotic internalization phenotype observed in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh embryonic back skin. Thus, we hypothesized that Celsr1 dimerization is required to stabilize the junctional complex for trans-endocytosis. To determine if trans-endocytosis is perturbed by the Crsh mutation, COS-7 cells were transfected with either Celsr1WT-EGFP or Celsr1WT-mCherry. After plasmid uptake, the independently transfected cells were mixed and co-cultured. Transfected Celsr1WT and Celsr1Crshwere expressed at similar levels as assessed by immunofluorescence intensity (Figure S6A), and endogenous Celsr1 was undetectable via antibody labeling in non-transfected cells (Figure S6B). As expected, mitotic cells transfected with Celsr1WT-EGFP also contained Celsr1WT-mCherry puncta (100% of mixed-cell pairs), resulting from trans-endocytosis of Celsr1 from the neighboring cell into the mitotic cell (Figure 3A; Table 1.1). However, when Celsr1Crsh-EGFP and Celsr1Crsh-mCherry transfected cells were similarly mixed, no trans-endocytosed mitotic puncta (0% of mixed-cell pairs) were detected (Figure 3A; Table 1.1). To determine if Celsr1 cis-dimerization is required on both sides of the cell-cell junction for trans-endocytosis, we mixed Celsr1WT-EGFP with Celsr1Crsh-mCherry transfected cells. Mitotic cells transfected with Celsr1WT were unable to trans-endocytose Celsr1Crsh (Figure 3B; Table 1.1). Similarly, mitotic cells transfected with Celsr1Crsh were unable to trans-endocytose Celsr1WT (Figure 3B). Relative to Celsr1WT, cells expressing Celsr1Crsh displayed a larger junctional area indicating that deficient trans-endocytosis by Crsh is not due to a lack of sufficient cell-cell contact (Figure S6C). These results indicate that Celsr1 cis-dimerization is required on both sides of the cell-cell junction for trans-endocytosis to occur.

Fz6 endocytosis in trans is independent of Celsr1 cis- and trans-dimerization

The defect in Crsh trans-endocytosis suggested that a broader defect in PCP complex internalization underlies the phenotype observed in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh mice (Figure 1). To determine if cis-dimerization of Celsr1 is required for Fz6 trans-endocytosis, we performed mixed-cell internalization assays. For these experiments, COS-7 cells were transfected with either Celsr1WT-EGFP or Celsr1Crsh-EGFP and mixed with cells only transfected with Fz6-mCherry. Surprisingly, Fz6 was trans-internalized into approximately 70%–80% of mitotic cells transfected with either WT or Crsh (Figure 4A; Table 1.2). Vangl2 trans-endocytosis requires Celsr1 cis-dimerization on both sides of the cell-cell junction

Since Celsr1 cis-dimerization is required for Vangl2 trans-internalization, we next asked on which side of the cell-cell junction Celsr1 cis-dimers act to mediate this process. Additional mixed-cell assays were conducted with Celsr1 and Vangl2 co-expressing cells opposing a Celsr1-only expressing cell. Upon mitosis, clear Vangl2 trans-endocytosis occurred in cells expressing Celsr1WT (Figure 5A; Table 1.3). However, Celsr1WT cells mixed with cells co-expressing Celsr1Crsh and Vangl2 exhibited mitotic Vangl2 trans-endocytosis in only 9% (3/32) of cell-cell pairs (Figure 5B; Table 1.3). Similarly, in Celsr1Crsh cells mixed with cells co-expressing Celsr1Crsh and Vangl2, only 1 cell-cell pair out of 29 (3%) showed Vangl2 trans-endocytosis (Figure 5C; Table 1.3). These outcomes are notable decreases compared to Vangl2 mixed-cell assays with Celsr1WT (WT-WT: 96% compared to WT-Crsh: 9% and Crsh-Crsh: 3%). Induced dimerization of Crsh rescues both Celsr1 and Vangl2 trans-endocytosis

To directly test if the loss of Celsr1 and Vangl2 trans-endocytosis is the result of impaired Celsr1 dimerization, we used the FKBP dimerization system in which a small molecule dimerizing agent drives lateral cadherin clustering of FKBP fused proteins,26,27,28 thereby rescuing Crsh cis-dimerization defects as reported previously.12 Using this system, we assessed trans-endocytosis in the presence or absence of forced Crsh dimerization (Figure 6A). COS-7 cells were transfected with Celsr1WT-EGFP or Celsr1Crsh−FKBP-HA and mixed. These cells were then treated with dimerizer or vehicle control. Notably, dimerizer treatment itself does not alter interphase junction assembly, nor mitotic cell number in Celsr1-transfected cells between vehicle or dimerizer treated cells (Figure S7). Celsr1WT mitotic cells treated with dimerizer showed discernable co-localized puncta containing Celsr1Crsh−FKBP-HA resulting from trans-endocytosis, while vehicle treated cells had no trans-endocytosed puncta (Figure 6B). Similarly, Celsr1Crsh−FKBP-HA cells undergoing mitosis also had trans-endocytosed puncta after dimerizer treatment, which was not observed in vehicle-treated cells (Figure 6C). Discussion

Mitotic PCP complex internalization serves as a mechanism to ensure asymmetric protein distribution and preserve polarity in the highly proliferative epidermis.17,18 During mitosis, Celsr1 co-internalizes PCP proteins both in cis (from the mitotic cell) and in trans (from the neighboring cell).19 Here, we demonstrate that Celsr1 dimerization is essential for trans-internalization of both Celsr1 and Vangl2. In contrast, Celsr1 dimerization is neither required for Fz6 trans-internalization nor PCP protein internalization in cis. These findings reveal new insights into the mechanism by which the Crsh mutation impairs the formation and maintenance of tissue polarity and demonstrate an essential role for Celsr1 cis-dimerization in PCP complex internalization during mitosis.

Previous work using a truncated Celsr1 lacking the extracellular domain demonstrated that this deletion mutant failed to cis-internalize Vangl2.19 Using full-length variants of Celsr1, we find that Celsr1 mediates Vangl2 cis-internalization and that this process is not substantially perturbed by the Crsh mutation. These findings suggest that protein-protein interactions mediated by the Celsr1 extracellular domain are necessary for Vangl2 cis-internalization but that this process does not require Celsr1 cis-dimerization. Similarly, Fz6 is also cis-internalized with both Celsr1WT and Celsr1Crsh (Figure 2), as well as the extracellularly truncated Celsr1 variant.19 These data indicate that Celsr1 co-internalization of Vangl2 and Fz6 in cis does not require Celsr1 adhesion or Celsr1 cis-dimerization, yet reveal differing requirements for the presence of the Celsr1 extracellular domain. Interestingly, previous studies suggest that Vangl2 stabilizes Celsr1 at the membrane and reduces Celsr1 internalization.19,29 This agrees with our observations that although we observe Vangl2 internalization in cis, a significant portion is retained on the membrane. In contrast, Fz6 is more readily cis-internalized than Vangl2 and displays a more prominent association with Celsr1 during mitosis (Figures 2B and 2D). Together, these findings support a model in which cis-internalization of PCP complex proteins is not dependent on Celsr1 cis-dimerization.

Unexpectedly, our mixed-cell assays for trans-internalization reveal the presence of extracellular interactions between Celsr1 and Fz6 that occur across cell-cell junctions (Figure 4A). These interactions are sufficient to mediate Fz6 internalization in trans. This sufficiency is a possible explanation for the increased number of Fz6 mitotic puncta in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh back skins (Figure 1D). Although the Crsh cell-cell junction is unstable,12 Fz6 is still trans-internalized via extracellular, cross-junctional interactions with Crsh. More endosomes may be required to internalize the diffuse PCP protein pools arising from the un-dimerized Crsh junctions compared to those needed for endocytose intact asymmetric WT junctions. Therefore, increased trans-internalization of Fz6 likely accompanies increased internalization of the diffuse Crsh pool. Thus, resulting in a selective increase in Celsr1-Fz6 mitotic puncta, but not Vangl2 puncta, which fails to be trans-endocytosed by Crsh. Further, the internalization mechanism and trafficking itinerary is presumably perturbed by the Crsh mutation since there is a considerable loss of colocalization between Celsr1-Fz6 in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis (Figure 1E).

Research using Drosophila PCP components has demonstrated Fz cis-interactions with Flamingo (Fmi; Celsr1 in vertebrates) and extracellular domain interactions of Fz with Vang (Van Gogh; Vangl in vertebrates).30,31,32,33 Our data indicate Celsr1 and Fz6 interact across the cell-cell junction in a manner sufficient for Celsr1 to trans-endocytose Fz6 from the neighboring cell, even in the absence of Celsr1 homophilic adhesion. These observations reveal additional aspects of mammalian Celsr1 function and the mechanism of PCP transmembrane protein interactions. For example, Celsr1-Fz6 trans-interactions may facilitate polarity re-establishment in daughter cells and support junction formation and stabilization of PCP complexes after cell division. Further research is needed to define the mechanism of this interaction and to determine its broader role in stabilizing and maintaining planar polarity during development.

While Celsr1 and Fz6 are capable of extracellular interactions, our data suggest Celsr1 tetramers are the minimum functional unit required for trans-endocytosis of the junctional PCP complex. Structural studies suggest Celsr1 can form antiparallel dimers that engage only in trans-interactions, as well as trimers and tetramers in which Celsr1 is both cis- and trans-dimerized.14,15,16 This tetrameric arrangement aligns with published data in Drosophila wings suggesting a 1:1 ratio of Flamingo (Fmi, homolog to Celsr1) across the junction.34,35 The requirement for a Celsr1 tetramer is supported by the lack of Vangl2 trans-internalization observed in both our in vivo (Figure 1) and in vitro (Figure 5) experiments. Notably, Vangl2 overexpression is known to stabilize Celsr1WT during mitosis,19 thus it is possible that Vangl2 is similarly able to stabilize Crsh, resulting in marginal, yet observable levels of Vangl2 trans-endocytosis, even in the absence of Celsr1 cis-dimerization (Table 1.3). Though Vangl2 trans-internalization via Crsh is extremely minimal, it is worth noting that a cis-dimer on one side is slightly more efficient at Vangl2 trans-internalization (Table 1.3; 9% vs. 3%), suggesting that the stability generated by Celsr1 cis-dimers, even on one side of the junction, is greater than any imparted by Vangl2 interactions. Further, because Vangl2 is primarily internalized in trans,19 the loss of Vangl2 puncta in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh back skin suggests Celsr1 cis-dimerization is required for trans-endocytosis of the Celsr1-Vangl2 complex. The tetrameric model is further supported by the lack of trans-endocytosis in Crsh mixed-cell assays (Figure 3) and rescue experiments (Figures 6 and 7) where we find Celsr1 dimers must be present in both interacting cells for Celsr1 and Vangl2 trans-internalization.

In addition to driving internalization of the adhesive junctional complex, Celsr1 cis-dimerization likely maintains PCP complex integrity post-endocytosis. In our Celsr1WT mixed assays, trans-internalized proteins resulting from a stable cell-cell junction show clear colocalization. However, our cis- and trans-internalization assays indicate a loss of Celsr1Crsh-Vangl2 (Figure 2D) and Celsr1Crsh-Fz6 colocalization (Figure 4A). These findings support our in vivo data revealing a loss of Celsr1-Fz6 colocalization upon internalization in Crsh (Figure 1E). Though some puncta co-localize, many internalized Fz6 and Vangl2 puncta are not closely associated with Celsr1 puncta in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis (Figures 1 and S3). This reduced association suggests the lack of a stable Celsr1 cell-cell junction disrupts complex integrity and proper trafficking itineraries. The alteration in trafficking pathways in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh epidermis is further supported by the decrease in puncta number in cells undergoing telophase relative to metaphase (Figure S2). As PCP complexes are known to be both recycled and degraded upon mitotic internalization,17,18,19 our results suggests a shift in the balance of PCP complex recycling verses degradation in Celsr1Crsh/Crsh mutants. Together, these data suggest Celsr1 cis-dimerization helps preserve PCP complex association during internalization and may play a key role in directing the trafficking itinerary of the PCP complex. We speculate that internalized PCP complexes containing Fz6 versus Vangl2 may be differentially targeted for recycling or degradation. While this warrants further investigation, differential complex trafficking, particularly of Celsr1-Fz6, may play a role in restoring polarity upon the completion of mitosis in conjunction with the retention of Vangl2 on the anterior membrane as previously proposed.19

Overall, our data suggest Celsr1 and Fz6 interact via their extracellular domains forming a complex capable of trans-endocytosis. At minimum, Celsr1 forms a tetrameric complex stabilized via cis-dimerization. This tetrameric complex is required for functional trans-endocytosis of Celsr1 and Vangl2 and for retention of the internalized PCP complex throughout mitosis. Furthermore, we show PCP complex internalization in vivo is severely disrupted by the Celsr1 variant Crsh with Vangl2 being particularly affected. With Crsh associated with severe neural tube defects, our findings are consistent with numerous studies linking disruptions in Vangl2 internalization and trafficking to neural tube defects and impaired tissue development.36,37,38,39,40 Therefore, the disruption in Vangl2 internalization identified in Crsh mutants likely contributes substantially to the overall loss of tissue polarity and developmental defects, and ultimately sheds light on the importance of Celsr1 dimerization in polarity maintenance in rapidly dividing tissues. Elucidating the direct contributions of internalization and trafficking defects to the loss of tissue polarity in PCP mutants will be an important focus for future studies.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations that could be addressed in future work. Our trans-endocytosis experiments have only been performed in COS-7 cells and have not been validated in vivo, for which a mosaic system would be required. Focusing on the trans-endocytosis defect, this study does not directly address differences in the mechanism of endocytic uptake for Crsh vs. WT, nor the postulated trafficking itinerary alterations, both which warrant further investigation. Additionally, by relying on transient transfections of fluorescently tagged proteins, our in vitro assays could result in protein levels that may not fully reflect the behavior of endogenous proteins. We also utilize the Celsr1Crsh mutation as a proxy for dimerization loss, but structural studies confirming cis-dimerization defects are still lacking. Other conformational changes and altered protein-protein interactions resulting from the mutation may also be contributing to the phenotype. Lastly, we solely focus on endocytosis of core transmembrane PCP proteins in the present study, though acknowledge key cytoplasmic and effector proteins likely play roles in regulating PCP complex internalization.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse lines and breeding

All mice were housed in an AAALAC-accredited facility in accordance with the guide for the care and use of Laboratory Animals. Animal maintenance and husbandry were in full compliance with the laboratory Animal Welfare Act. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Penn State University College of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol number PROTO202102020. Celsr1Crsh/+, gifted by Jennifer Murdoch, were backcrossed on C57BL/6 for at least 10 generations prior to use in the experiments reported. All experiments were performed on both male and female E14.5 and E15.5 embryos obtained from timed matings of heterozygous mice.

Cells and cell lines

CD1 mouse keratinocytes (MKs) were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in E-medium supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.05 mM Ca2+. For immunofluorescence experiments using CD1 MKs, cells were cultured on #1.5 glass coverslips coated with fibronectin. Following transfections with Effectene (QIAGEN), cells were incubated for 24 h prior to a 12 h switch to 1.5 mM Ca2+ to allow for junction assembly and mitosis. Cells were then fixed and stained following the immunofluorescence procedure described below. CD1 MK cells were free of mycoplasma. COS-7 cells (gifted from Andrew Kowalczyk) were free of mycoplasma and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS. For COS-7 immunofluorescence experiments, cells were cultured on #1.5 glass coverslips with no additional extracellular matrix. Transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagents (Invitrogen). Exact experimental conditions varied between assays and are stated below.

Molecular cloning and constructs

Vangl2 with N-terminal EGFP (pEGFPC2-Vangl2) was created by inserting full length Vangl2 in Clontech pEGFPC2 vector using EcoR1 and Sal1 sites. Fz6 with C-terminal mCherry (pEGFPN1-Fz6-mCherry) was created by introducing Xho1 and Not1 sites flanking Fz6-mCherry for subsequent insertion into pEGFPN1.

Method details

Immunofluorescence staining of mouse back skin

Dorsal skin from E14.5 and E15.5 embryos was dissected in PBS+ and fixed in 4% PFA in PBS+ at room temperature for 1 h. Dissected skins were labeled with Hoechst and processed for Celsr1, Fz6, Vangl2, EEA1, and phosphorylated Histone 3 immunofluorescence as indicated and previously described.12,45 Following fixation, embryos were washed three times and dissected in PBS. They were blocked and permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 with all subsequent washes performed with PBT. Skins were then incubated in primary antibody solutions at 4 °C overnight, washed three times for 30 min and incubated for 3 h in secondary solutions at room temperature. The skins were subsequently washed and mounted in ProLong Diamond antifade reagent (Molecular Probes).

Cis-internalization assay

COS-7 cells and CD1 MKs cultured for cis-internalization immunofluorescence experiments were seeded onto #1.5 glass coverslips and incubated for approximately 12 h. Cells were then co-transfected with a 1:1 mixture of Celsr1-EGFP and Fz6-mCherry or Celsr1-mCherry and EGFP-Vangl2 constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 reagents (Invitrogen) for COS-7 cells and Effectene reagents for CD1 MKs (Qiagen). Cells were fixed and stained approximately 24 h after transfections to ensure many transfected cells were undergoing mitosis. Staining protocol and antibodies used are described below in immunofluorescence staining and materials table.

Trans-internalization assays

COS-7 cells were grown to 80% confluence then equally split into two dishes and incubated for approximately 15 h. They were then independently transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 reagents (Invitrogen) and incubated for an additional 6–8 h. The cells were then trypsinized and mixed at a 1:1 ratio, seeded onto #1.5 glass coverslips, and incubated for 18–20 h or until roughly 30 h had passed since transfection to achieve transfected mitotic cells. The cells were then fixed and stained according to the protocol below. The occurrence of trans-endocytosis was defined by the presence of 3 or more puncta in the mitotic cell having originated in the neighboring cell. This criteria was used to quantify the number of mitotic cells that underwent trans-endocytosis in each experimental condition. FKBP rescue and control experiments were performed using the same cell mixing assay except the addition of dimerizer (AP20187) or ethanol as a vehicle control. After mixing, the cells were incubated for approximately 16 h and then treated with dimerizer or vehicle for an additional 6 h before fixation. For consistency between mixed-cell experiments, the cells were still fixed roughly 30 h post-transfection.

Immunofluorescence staining of in vitro assays

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS+ for 10 min at room temperature. Directly following fixation, cells were washed three times in PBS and permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (PBT) with all subsequent washes performed with PBT. Cells were then incubated at room temperature for 1 h in primary antibody solutions in PBT, washed with PBT, and incubated in secondary antibody solutions in PBT for 30 min. Coverslips were then washed with PBS and mounted in ProLong Diamond antifade reagent (Molecular Probes).

Confocal microscopy

Confocal images were acquired on an inverted Nikon Ti2-E microscope equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-X1 spinning disk confocal unit, a Hamamatsu Fusion BT sCMOS camera, and a piezo stage using a 0.2 μm step size. Four laser lines (405, 488, 561, and 640 nm) and four objectives were used for imaging including a Plan Apo λ 20x/0.75 NA air, Plan Fluor 40x/1.3 NA oil, Apo TIRF 60x/1.49 oil, and Apo TIRF 100x/1.49 oil objectives. Skin images were acquired using the 60x and 100x oil objectives while in vitro assays were imaged with the 40x, 60x, and 100x oil objectives. Identical acquisition settings were utilized for every image within each experiment. All cells imaged and analyzed had similar construct expression levels.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Image analysis and statistics

All image processing and analysis was performed using Fiji unless otherwise noted.46 Embryonic mouse back skin images were deconvolved using Microvolution41 and were pseudo flat-field corrected using BioVoxxel 3D Box. Cells within the basal layer were segmented using the pretrained model (cyto3) in Cellpose (v3.0.10)42,47 with stitch threshold = 0.5, diameter = 80, anisotropy = 1.85 using two channels (Hoechst and Fz6). Custom ImageJ macros were used to filter label images for mitotic cells before generating cropped binary masks and fluorescence images with pixels outside the cell segmentation set to 0. For 3D segmentation of Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2, ilastik43 pixel classifiers were trained for each protein. An object classification workflow was used to identify internalized puncta compared to puncta at the cell-cell borders. An ImageJ macro was used for further image processing and analysis. Probability thresholds (Celsr1: 0.5, Fz6: 0.5, Vangl2: 0.5) were used to further refine puncta segmentations prior to the generation of 3D objects using a 26-connected components analysis. To remove under-segmented objects, only 3D objects below a certain voxel size (Celsr1: 200, Fz6: 200, Vangl2: 200) were kept. For each 3D object corresponding to Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2, intensity values were measured from their respective fluorescence images as well as other proteins’ segmented images (3D Suite ImageJ).44 Puncta from one channel were considered to be positive for other channels if there was any overlap in segmentation. Statistical analysis of back skin results was conducted on three independent experiments (back skin from one Celsr1+/+ and one Celsr1Crsh/Crsh embryo from 3 independent litters) with a minimum of 10 mitotic cells per replicate. Interphase quantification of single cells and cell-cell junctions was performed in ImageJ. The cell periphery or junctional regions were manually identified to obtain desired regions of interest. Average intensity and junctional area were measured and compared between Celsr1WT, Celsr1Crsh, and Celsr1Crsh−FKBP expressing cells. All cis-internalization assays were analyzed using ilastik and Fiji. The Celsr1, Fz6, and Vangl2 channels were segmented and classified in ilastik and co-localization analysis of the segmented channels was performed using 3D Suite ImageJ as previously performed. All trans-internalization assays were classified by the occurrence of trans-internalization, defined as the presence of 3 or more puncta in the interior of the mitotic cell assumed to have originated from the neighboring cell. This allowed for the occurrence of cellular debris or antibody precipitants to not skew the data. See figure legends for details on statistical analysis.
