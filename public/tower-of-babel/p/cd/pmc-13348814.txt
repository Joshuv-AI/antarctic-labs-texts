ABSTRACT
The AAEV (American Association of Extracellular Vesicles) Annual Meeting at the John P. McGovern Commons in Houston, TX convened over 300 leading researchers, clinicians, and industry experts from around the world to advance the rapidly evolving field of extracellular vesicle (EV) science. EVs, nanoscale lipid-bound particles released by all prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, have emerged as crucial mediators of intercellular communication, trans- porting proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids that influence a wide spectrum of physiological and pathological processes. Their involvement in immune modulation, tissue regeneration, cancer progression, metabolic regulation, and other complex biological functions positions EVs as promising diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic delivery agents in precision medicine and personalized healthcare. However, significant challenges persist, including the heterogeneity of EV populations, complexities in isolation and purification, and the pressing need for standardized characterization protocols.The 2024 AAEV’s annual gathering provided a pivotal forum for exchanging insights and cultivating collaborations. The meeting featured keynote addresses delved into the intricate heterogeneity, biogenesis pathways, and immu- nomodulatory capabilities of EVs, as well as their contributions to disease progression.Subsequent sessions covered a broad range of topics, showcasing cutting-edge technologies for EV isolation and characterization, revealing novel mechanisms by which EVs modulate immune responses and disease states, and presenting innovative EV engineering approaches for delivering therapeutics. Industry presentations complemented academic discussions by introducing scalable EV production systems, automated isolation methods, specialized analytical tools, and strategies to navigate regulatory pathways. Alongside these presentations, the association supports dissemination of the latest discoveries and methodologies through its flagship publication, Extracellular Vesicle (EV).Collectively, the insights shared at the AAEV Annual Meeting underscored the remarkable progress in understanding EV complexity, refining isolation and analysis techniques and translating fundamental discoveries into clinically actionable solutions. Speakers highlighted advanced isolation platforms, refined bioengineering methods, and efforts to integrate EV-based diagnostics and therapeutics into existing clinical frameworks. As the field matures, the forward momentum reflects a transition from theoretical potential to tangible applications. By fostering global collaboration, strengthening ties between academia and industry, and providing platforms like the EV journal, for ongoing dialogue, the EV community is well-positioned to surmount current challenges and accelerate the integration of EV-based approaches into mainstream healthcare.

Keynote address I- biological properties and function of native and engineered extracellular vesicles in health and disease

Dr. Raghu Kalluri’s keynote address centered on the evolving understanding of EVs, particularly exosomes, in biology, diagnostics, and therapeutics. He began by highlighting the intrinsic heterogeneity and complexity of EV populations, emphasizing that EVs-ranging from exosomes of roughly 100 nm in size to larger microvesicles-carry a diverse array of proteins, nucleic acids (including DNA, mRNA, and miRNA), and lipids.1 Drawing on over a decade of research, Dr. Kalluri described the technical challenges and solutions to EV isolation and purification, such as employing size-exclusion chromatography and FPLC-based methods to obtain highly pure EV preparations suitable for clinical applications.2 He noted that EV production and cargo composition are influenced by cell type, physiological state, and environmental conditions and that standardized isolation and characterization protocols are essential to move the field forward.

A key focus of his talk was the functional significance of EVs in intercellular communication. Using mouse models engineered to label EVs in vivo, Dr. Kalluri’s team has observed extensive trafficking of EVs between tissues, including unexpected routes such as pancreatic EVs influencing distant organs like the brain. These findings suggest a pervasive network of EV-mediated signaling that may contribute to normal tissue homeostasis, aging, and disease processes including cancer progression.

Another highlight was the translational aspect of EV research. Dr. Kalluri described how EVs can be harnessed as non-invasive liquid biopsies to detect molecular signatures of cancer and other diseases. He shared examples of how machine learning and proteomics have identified EV protein and nucleic acid markers that distinguish cancer-derived EVs from healthy counterparts.3–6 On the therapeutic front, his lab’s work developing exosome-based delivery systems of siRNA to target oncogenic KRAS mutations in pancreatic cancer has progressed from bench to bedside.7 This exosome-based RNA interference therapy has completed Phase I clinical trials and showed promising safety and target engagement, laying the groundwork for Phase II trials and the potential for broader EV-based therapeutics (NCT03608631).

In summarizing, Dr. Kalluri emphasized the synergy of fundamental EV biology and rigorous clinical translation.8 By deepening our understanding of EV composition, formation, and biodistribution, and by leveraging advanced isolation and bioengineering tools, EVs stand poised to transform diagnostics, prognostics, and treatments in oncology and beyond.

Both Dr. Kalluri and Dr. Raposo highlighted that EV research has matured significantly, revealing sophisticated biogenesis routes, intricate cargo-sorting mechanisms, and profound biological and clinical implications. From fundamental cell biology and immunology to advanced translational and clinical endeavors, EVs stand at the frontier of biomedical innovation.

Following the compelling keynote addresses by Dr. Raghu Kalluri and Dr. Graca Raposo, the conference delved into a series of thematic sessions that spanned the breadth and depth of EV research. These sessions illuminated the multifaceted roles of EVs in intercellular communication, disease mechanisms, immune modulation, and regenerative medicine, while also showcasing cutting-edge advancements in EV isolation, characterization, and engineering technologies. By bridging foundational discoveries with applied innovations, these discussions underscored the transformative potential of EVs in both basic science and clinical applications. The subsequent sessions provided a platform for researchers to share insights into the complexities of EV biogenesis, the diversity of their cargo, and their dynamic roles in health and disease, as well as exploring novel approaches to harness EVs as powerful tools for diagnostics and therapeutics.

Biogenesis and biodiversity of EVs

This session explored the complexity of EV biology, with a special focus on the diverse origins, sorting mechanisms, and functional heterogeneity of EVs. Presentations spanned new insights into how EVs derive from specialized organelles, such as cilia and mitochondria, and how tetraspanin proteins, ubiquitination pathways, and key cargo loading strategies regulate EV biogenesis. Further, the session highlighted engineered EV systems for delivering bioactive cargoes, including genome editors, underscoring the translational potential of EV-based technologies.

Dr. Asa Gustafsson’s presentation delved into how cells maintain mitochondrial quality control via EV release, particularly when lysosomal function is impaired.9 Under normal conditions, damaged mitochondria are removed by autophagy and lysosomal degradation. However, when lysosomal activity is compromised, portions of dysfunctional mitochondria can be sorted into large EVs and secreted extracellularly. This process, dependent on Rab7 and the endosomal pathway, ultimately allows peripheral macrophages to engulf and degrade mitochondrial components, preserving cellular homeostasis. Such findings highlight the adaptive versatility of EV biogenesis in organelle maintenance.

Dr. Steve Gould’s talk addressed fundamental questions in EV biogenesis at the plasma membrane. By focusing on tetraspanin proteins (CD9, CD81, CD63), he demonstrated that distinct sorting signals, especially endocytosis motifs, influence whether cargoes are incorporated into EVs.10 The presence or absence of plasma membrane endocytosis signals determined cargo enrichment in EVs, and blocking endocytosis enhanced EV budding at the cell surface. This work suggests that EV biogenesis is guided by a delicate interplay of cargo sorting signals, tetraspanin-lipid interactions, and the cell’s endocytic machinery. Ultimately, exosomal tetraspanins serve as a driving force for vesicle formation, providing a mechanistic framework for EV engineering.

Dr. Keisuke Ito introduced the role of EVs in the maintenance and self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs).11 HSCs produce EVs that can carry lipids, proteins, and RNA to neighboring cells, influencing the hematopoietic niche. Manipulating EV formation or altering their cargo impacted HSC proliferation and lineage commitment. These findings position EV-mediated communication as a critical regulator of stem cell fate, with potential applications in regenerative medicine and transplantation.

Cilia are specialized organelles essential for signal transduction, and cilia-derived EVs (cEVs) add another dimension to the EV biogenesis landscape. Dr. Mingli Zhu demonstrated that the ESCRT-0 complex, specifically proteins like HRS, sorts ubiquitinated receptors (e.g., Smoothened) into cEVs. Intriguingly, cEV budding is independent of ESCRT-III and instead relies on phase separation and clustering of ubiquitinated cargo at the ciliary tip. This provides a novel model of membrane scission and cargo packaging distinct from canonical endosomal intraluminal vesicle formation.

The session concluded with a presentation by Dr. Xiuming Liang on using EVs as delivery vehicles for therapeutic proteins, including the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing machinery.12 By exploiting viral glycoprotein VSV-G and intimin-like domains for cargo loading and membrane fusion, the researchers significantly improved intracellular cargo release. Through systematic optimization of guide RNAs and Cas9 variants, they achieved high editing efficiencies in vivo, demonstrating the translational promise of EV engineering for gene therapy.

Collectively, these studies underscore the remarkable complexity and versatility of EV formation and cargo selection. The biogenesis processes extend beyond traditional endosomal routes to include plasma membrane budding, ciliary membrane remodeling, and organelle-derived vesiculation. Improved mechanistic understanding fosters new tools for therapeutic delivery by engineering EVs to carry genome editors or other high-value payloads that hold great clinical promise. With advancing knowledge of EV heterogeneity, it becomes increasingly feasible to tailor EVs for disease-specific interventions, ultimately bridging the gap between fundamental biology and medical applications.

Functions I: emerging new functions

This session introduced a spectrum of innovative research uncovering how EVs and their cargoes-particularly various classes of RNAs and even DNA-are driving newly discovered biological functions. Topics ranged from the extraordinary complexity of extracellular RNAs identified in human biofluids to EV-mediated regulation of filopodia and synapse formation, to inter-kingdom RNA communication between organisms, and the therapeutic promise of EVs displaying antibodies on their surface. Collectively, the studies showcased fresh perspectives on EV biology, highlighting EVs as critical players in gene regulation, immune modulation, development, and precision medicine.

Dr. Aleksandar Milosavljevic introduced the Extracellular RNA Atlas (exRNA Atlas) projecťs latest findings, revealing a tremendous level of complexity in extracellular RNAs across human biofluids.13 The most recent analyses uncovered millions of extracellular genomic loci expressed as extracellular RNAs (exRNAs), many of which are short fragments of unknown function. These data suggest that what we currently know, such as the commonly studied small RNAs, is only a fraction of the complexity. Understanding this molecular dark matter may open up new diagnostic biomarkers and deepen our understanding of EV-mediated signaling processes.

Dr. Alissa Weaver’s presentation uncovered that small EVs (exosomes) can induce filopodia formation, actin-rich protrusions fundamental for cell migration, neuronal synapse formation, and possibly cancer metastasis.14 Her team identified proteins, including endoglin and THSD7A, as key EV cargoes that trigger filopodia and dendritic spine development. This work underscored the importance of EVs as orchestrators of cell shape changes and synaptic architecture, revealing an entirely new layer of EV function in tissue patterning and connectivity.

Dr. Li Yu introduced the concept of migrasomes, a novel class of large EVs formed at retracting edges of migrating cells15–17. Migrasomes serve as signaling hubs, distributing molecules such as cytokines and mitochondrial components. His findings linked migrasomes to key biological events, including angiogenesis, immune response modulation, and developmental morphogenesis. Such insights into migrasomes expand our understanding of EV diversity and their roles in shaping the cellular environment, immune regulation, and ultimately entire tissue architectures.

Dr. Hailing Jin’s research demonstrated that EVs play an essential role in cross-kingdom RNA trafficking between plants, fungi, and even bacteria.18 EVs protect RNAs from degradation in the extracellular environment and facilitate uptake into the destination cells and organisms. Plant and fungal pathogens exchange small RNAs and mRNAs using EVs, influencing immunity and virulence. This RNA warfare extends beyond plant-fungi interactions to include bacteria-fungi communication, illustrating how EVs are pivotal in mediating host-microbe evolutionary arms races. Her discoveries offer potential avenues for sustainable crop protection and set a foundation for understanding cross-kingdom or cross-organismal communication strategies that drive ecological and evolutionary dynamics.

Dr. Mengying Hu’s findings revealed that activated T cell-derived EVs, enriched with extracellular DNA, enhance antigen presentation in dendritic cells and tumor cells, leading to stronger anti-tumor T cell responses. By introducing EV DNA into the tumor microenvironment, these EVs amplify immune responses and synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors. This approach may inspire a new class of immunotherapies leveraging EVs as immunostimulatory agents, broadening the repertoire of tools in cancer immunotherapy.

Dr. Oscar P.B. Wiklander presented a strategy for decorating EV surfaces with monoclonal antibodies, enabling precise targeting of tumor cells.19 By binding full-length antibodies, such as those targeting HER2 or PD-L1, to EVs, his team achieved dramatic improvements in tumor targeting and retention. Loading EVs with chemotherapeutics like doxorubicin further led to potent anti-tumor effects in mouse models. This flexible, modular platform may be adapted to various antibodies and cargoes, potentially revolutionizing EV-based targeted therapies.

The talks delivered in this session collectively underscored a paradigm shift in EV research. EVs emerge not just as carriers of small, well-studied RNAs, but as vessels enriched with a multitude of complex RNA species, proteins, and even DNA. Their functions transcend boundaries, influencing neural development, cancer immunity, tissue morphogenesis, and inter-kingdom communication. As these insights accumulate, future work may focus on integrating these new functions to develop more sophisticated therapeutics, optimize EV cargo selection and targeting, and unravel the intricate molecular dialogues orchestrated by EVs in health and disease.

EV cargos and biomarkers

This session underscored the complexity and promise of EV cargoes as sources of clinically useful biomarkers, as well as the engineering of EVs for targeted therapeutic delivery. Talks explored a range of topics, from advancing EV isolation methods for high specificity and throughput to characterizing EV content that may offer early disease detection or improved tissue targeting. Presentations emphasized the importance of strict assay validation, careful selection of isolation and analysis techniques, and robust statistical and clinical validation to harness the full potential of EV-based biomarkers and therapies.

Dr. Tony Ye Hu showcased the development of novel, isolation-free, nanoplasmonic assays that enhance the detection and profiling of EV-associated biomarkers directly from blood.20 By integrating nanotechnology, machine learning, and multiomics analyses, his team achieved greater diagnostic sensitivity and specificity than conventional approaches such as CA19–9 in pancreatic cancer.21 Their isolation-free strategy simplifies workflows, reduces sample volume, improves reproducibility, and may be implemented in clinical pathology labs, holding promise for global health applications including tuberculosis diagnostics.22

Dr. Devika Soundara Manickam discussed large EVs that carry functional mitochondria, focusing on stroke.23 By delivering mitochondria-containing EVs to the blood-brain barrier endothelium, her team restored metabolic function, reduced cell death, and improved stroke outcomes in animal models. These large EVs, enriched in mitochondria, overcame ischemic damage and enhanced neuronal and endothelial health, suggesting a new line of EV-based therapies for cerebrovascular diseases.

Dr. Carlos Salomon addressed the urgent need for early ovarian cancer detection. His group analyzed EV proteins and RNAs from plasma to identify biomarker panels capable of diagnosing early-stage ovarian cancer with very high sensitivity and specificity, surpassing that of CA125.24 This approach involved large patient cohorts, careful data cross-validation, and eventually translated to the development of standardized protocols with biotech partners, paving a path toward clinical implementation of EV-based biomarkers in ovarian cancer screening and early detection. A crucial step toward integration into routine pathology workflows is the automation of EV isolation and analysis, ensuring scalability, reproducibility, and clinical-grade throughput.

Dr. Susmita Sahoo presented innovative strategies for EV-based gene delivery and uncovered EV roles in cardiorenal disease. First, she illustrated how EV-encapsulated AAV vectors can deliver genes to cardiomyocytes more effectively, even in the presence of neutralizing antibodies, improving heart function post-myocardial infarction.25 She also revealed that in chronic kidney disease models, kidney-derived EVs carrying pathological microRNAs and proteins can induce cardiotoxicity, leading to heart failure.26 Her work highlights how engineered EVs can improve gene therapy, while better understanding EV signaling between kidney and heart may inspire therapies for cardiorenal syndromes.

Dr. Louise Laurent explored the complexity of EV and non-EV carriers that transport RNAs and proteins in human pregnancy.27 Using advanced single-particle techniques, mass spectrometry, and immunomagnetic separation, her team identified unexpected heterogeneity among placental EV populations. Some placental markers and pregnancy-associated RNAs reside in non-EV carriers, raising caution against focusing only on EVs for pregnancy-related biomarker discovery. Her results underscore the necessity of carefully characterizing different extracellular carriers and adopting multiple analytical methods to ensure accurate biomarker identification.

These talks collectively highlight the enormous diversity and complexity of EV cargos, introduce next-generation isolation and analysis techniques, and describe how EV biomarkers can improve early disease detection. By bringing sophisticated technological tools and rigorous validation to bear on these problems, the field is moving closer to the reliable clinical implementation of EV-based diagnostics and therapeutics.

Lightning talks from the above session

Dr. Yong Tao’s group engineered exosomes to carry active catalase and positively charged coatings for treating retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury. In both mouse and mini-pig models, these tailored exosomes delivered catalase to the diseased retina, reducing apoptosis and improving visual function. Dr. Antje Maria Zickler presented a system for engineering EVs with RNA-binding motifs to load them with target mRNAs, along with fusogens to ensure delivery into recipient cells. Using these EVs in cancer models achieved potent immunotherapeutic responses at lower doses compared to lipid nanoparticles, offering a safer and more efficient platform for RNA-based treatments. Dr. Loïc Steiner focused on urinary EVs as biomarkers for bladder cancer, demonstrating that EVs provide unique proteomic signatures distinguishing healthy from cancerous conditions, and even differentiating muscle-invasive from non-muscle invasive disease, paving the way for improved bladder cancer screening. Dr. Janos Zempleni described programmable milk exosomes engineered via genetics and bio-orthogonal chemistries. These dairy-derived EVs can incorporate guide RNAs, proteins, or other payloads, and display synthetic peptides that enhance homing, retention, and endosomal escape, thus promising versatile, natural, and scalable delivery vehicles.

These lightning talks illustrate the field’s rapid innovation and diversity, from novel EV isolation techniques and engineered exosome therapeutics to biomarkers for cancer, vision impairment, and prenatal environmental exposures. By combining advanced materials, genetics, and orthogonal chemistries, researchers are expanding EVs’ diagnostic and therapeutic potential across a wide spectrum of diseases.

Novel technology in EV studies

This session explores cutting-edge methodologies designed to improve the isolation, characterization, and analysis of EVs for research and clinical applications. Presentations in this session illustrate how advancements in instrumentation, innovative assay frameworks, and refined isolation techniques not only simplify workflows but also enhance reproducibility, sensitivity, and standardization. By integrating novel analytic pipelines with automated and scalable purification strategies, researchers are forging pathways toward more robust EV-based diagnostics, meaningful biological insights, and ultimately, smoother transitions into clinical use.

Dr. Jennifer Jones discussed approaches to simplify and standardize EV flow cytometry.28 While single EV resolution and specialized instrumentation are ideal for certain questions, Dr. Jones emphasized that bead-based multiplex EV assays on standard cytometers can also yield valuable data, particularly for evaluating marker repertoires and performing longitudinal studies. She highlighted the importance of instrument calibration and consistent protocols to ensure the reproducibility and comparability of EV data across multiple experiments, instruments, and institutions. By employing standardized calibration beads, reference materials, and thoughtful assay design, researchers can confidently integrate EV flow cytometry data into large-scale clinical and translational studies, even without specialized EV-only flow cytometers.

Dr. Fei Liu presented novel ultrafiltration and size-fractionation methods, implemented in a technology termed EXODUS, to efficiently isolate EVs from a variety of biofluids, including blood, urine, and even tears.29 By using oscillatory filtration methods to prevent membrane clogging, EXODUS enables continuous, scalable separation of EVs and their further characterization via proteomics and RNA profiling. In clinical research, Dr. Liu’s team successfully applied these methods to identify EV-derived biomarkers for early cancer detection, including prostate and bladder cancers and even explored tear-derived EVs as a potential source of biomarkers for neurological and ocular conditions. The platform’s ability to achieve high EV purity, size stratification, and broad applicability to different sample types highlights the promise of combining innovative isolation methods with advanced molecular profiling to accelerate EV-based diagnostics and disease monitoring.

Together, these two talks showcased how improved isolation, fractionation, and analysis technologies, ranging from standardized EV flow cytometry protocols to innovative filtration systems, are paving the way for more reliable, informative, and clinically relevant EV research.

EV functions II: immune response

This session delved into how EVs orchestrate various aspects of immune modulation, shaping cellular responses that influence both health and disease. The speakers showcased the pivotal role of EVs in scenarios ranging from early pregnancy maintenance to complex tumor-immune interactions. By illuminating the ways EVs guide immune cell behavior, influence inflammatory cascades, and carry functionally active proteins, cytokines, and cargoes, the talks collectively underscored the versatility and power of EVs in immune regulation.

Dr. Susanne Gabrielsson’s talk highlighted EVs as promising immunotherapeutic agents against cancer.30 By delivering antigens and immunostimulatory signals directly to antigen-presenting cells, EVs can activate robust, tumor-specific T-cell responses.31 They incorporate adjuvants, tumor antigens, and checkpoint modulators into EVs to boost their therapeutic potency. Through proteomic profiling, they aim to identify optimal EV types that overcome immunosuppression and enhance anti-tumor immunity. Ultimately, EV-based cancer immunotherapies offer a novel, targeted, and more effective approach to improving patient outcomes.

Dr. John Yu focused on how glycosphingolipid-enriched EVs from embryonic or cancer cells can dampen T-cell proliferation and promote trophoblast invasion, providing a window into how EVs manage immune tolerance and tissue remodeling at the fetal-maternal interface.32 This manipulation of host immunity through EV-bound glycosphingolipids highlights a potential mechanism for maintaining pregnancy equilibrium and, by analogy, how tumors might similarly hijack such pathways to avoid immune destruction.

Shifting to the realm of inflammatory cell death, Dr. Vijay Rathinam described how pyroptotic cells release EVs bearing fully formed gasdermin pores.33 These EVs deliver pore complexes to bystander cells, extending inflammatory cell death beyond the initial site. This revelation of EV-mediated propagation of pyroptosis suggests a novel dimension in inflammation’s self-perpetuating cycles and may inform strategies to mitigate excessive tissue damage.

In allergic lung inflammation, Dr. Heather Pua’s work showed that Th2 cell-derived EVs carry surface-bound IL-3, which significantly enhances eosinophil survival.34 The concept that cytokines associated with EV membranes could be more potent than free cytokines raises intriguing questions. This could represent a fine-tuned EV-based mechanism to stabilize certain immune cell subsets and sustain inflammatory conditions, particularly in allergic responses.

Dr. Ferdinando Pucci’s presentation connected EV biology to the phenomenon of epitope spreading in anti-tumor immunity.35 By studying EVs engineered to carry defined B-cell antigens, Dr. Pucci’s team demonstrated that B cells can use EV-derived antigens to mount broader and more versatile antitumor responses. This broadening of the B-cell repertoire suggests a critical role for EVs in stimulating epitope diversity and enhancing tumor-specific immunity, offering insights into how robust and adaptable antitumor immune responses can be generated.

Finally, Dr. Ananth Kumar Kammala introduced exofection, wherein fetal-derived EVs restore the expression of a key transporter protein (BCRP) in maternal cells under inflammatory conditions.36,37 This result highlights yet another dimension of EV-mediated homeostatic maintenance during pregnancy, ensuring proper molecule transport and immune stability at the fetal-maternal interface.

Together, these talks illustrated that EVs are far more than inert containers; they are dynamic, context-dependent agents orchestrating immune cell behavior, sustaining inflammatory states when needed, preserving tissue function in delicate physiological scenarios, and even enhancing the immune repertoire in cancer. As a whole, the session emphasized the promise of harnessing EV-driven pathways to mitigate inflammatory damage, sustain healthy pregnancy, enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy in cancer, or preserve homeostasis in complex immune environments.

EV functions III: disease mechanism

This session centered on the diverse ways EVs influence disease processes and offered insights into innovative EV-based interventions. Collectively, the speakers demonstrated how careful engineering of EVs and systematic understanding of their organotropism and cargo dynamics can pave the way for new strategies to treat a wide range of pathologies, from fibrotic disorders to neurological and metabolic diseases.

Dr. Yanlian Yang explored the importance of single EV analysis, showing how EV properties such as mechanical stiffness and protein secondary structures correlate with tumor aggressiveness.38 By integrating multifaceted EV characterization at both the ensemble and single-particle levels, Yang’s team uncovered signatures reflecting tumor malignancy and provided valuable diagnostic clues. She also discussed the use of EVs for cancer therapy, specifically functionalized EVs encapsulating epigenetic regulation siRNA for targeting and modulating immune responses against tumor cells.39

Dr. Lucia Languino’s talk delved into the intricate role of integrins in EV-mediated intercellular communication, with a particular focus on how integrins are selectively incorporated into EVs and contribute to their functional specificity.40 Her studies demonstrate that integrin-rich EVs play critical roles in tumor progression by facilitating cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions in the tumor microenvironment. Using advanced imaging and proteomic approaches, Dr. Languino highlighted how integrin-specific EV subpopulations regulate processes such as angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metastasis.

Dr. Takahisa Nakamura addressed obesity-related complications and their mechanistic underpinnings involving EVs.41 By examining EV circulation in pre- and post-bariatric surgery patients and animal models, Dr. Nakamura found that liver-derived EVs convey metabolic and inflammatory signals that modulate the states of immune cells in specific metabolic tissues. These findings highlight how nutrient sensors modulate EV biogenesis and cargo, ultimately contributing to systemic inflammatory responses in obesity.

Turning to fibrotic disease, Dr. Mengrui Liu presented a novel therapeutic strategy against idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).42 Dr. Liu showed that targeting and deactivating myofibroblasts via nanoparticle-based delivery of anti-fibrotic cargo could shift the fibrotic microenvironment back toward normalcy. Crucially, fibroblast-derived EVs produced after this “re-education” process further support the attenuation of fibrosis by modulating the cellular and molecular landscape. This self-reinforcing feedback suggests a powerful therapeutic loop for treating IPF.

In sum, the talks in this session collectively highlighted the intricate roles of EVs in disease progression and control. By capturing their roles in shaping metabolic landscapes, immune responses, and tissue remodeling, the speakers underscored how deeper molecular and engineering insights into EVs can fuel the development of cutting-edge diagnostics and therapeutics.

EV therapeutics I: metabolism and regenerative medicine

This session highlighted how EVs are being leveraged and engineered to enhance therapeutic delivery and improve outcomes in metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, as well as in regenerative medicine. The speakers presented innovative strategies to harness EVs for tissue-specific cargo delivery, inflammation modulation, and improved regenerative capacity.

Dr. Saumya Das emphasized the potential of circulating EV-derived RNAs to serve as liquid biopsies for tissues that are challenging to sample directly.43 Using deep sequencing and single-nucleus reference atlases, his lab has identified tissue-enriched transcripts in EVs that reflect disease-specific changes in organs like the heart. By mapping EV transcriptomes to single-nuclear data, Dr. Das’s team can track dysregulated pathways in chronic disorders, including diabetes and heart failure, non-invasively. This molecular insight holds promise for the development of personalized diagnostics and targeted therapies, enabling clinicians to better tailor interventions based on a patienťs unique transcriptomic signature.

Dr. Ramkumar Menon’s talk presented a novel approach to combating preterm birth-a condition with high mortality and morbidity. He described a microphysiological “organ-on-a-chip” platform simulating the maternal-fetal interface. Through this platform, Menon’s team tested exosome-based therapeutics, particularly IL-10-loaded EVs, to quell intrauterine inflammation.44 These engineered EVs effectively passed through complex barriers, reduced fetal inflammation, and improved pregnancy outcomes in animal models. This humanized testing system could reduce reliance on animal models and expedite drug development, ultimately helping prevent premature births.

Dr. Juliane Nguyen introduced a unique Velcro-inspired targeting strategy for EV delivery to ischemic myocardium.45 Recognizing that conventional targeting methods offer only incremental improvements, Dr. Nguyen’s lab developed leucine zipper-based Zippersomes to amplify targetable binding sites. By layering EVs onto previously bound EVs, they expanded the available receptor space, enhancing EV accumulation and retention at injury sites in the heart. This multi-layer docking approach allowed for improved therapeutic efficacy and more durable retention, paving the way for minimally invasive, systemic EV therapies for heart repair.

Dr. Robert Raffai explored how interleukin-4 (IL-4)-polarized macrophage-derived EVs can reverse atherosclerosis.46 These EVs induce favorable metabolic and inflammatory gene signatures, enhance monocyte oxidative phosphorylation, and foster efferocytosis-key steps in plaque regression. Notably, the ApoE content within these macrophage EVs reshaped microRNA networks, orchestrating immunometabolic reprogramming, which reduced necrotic cores, promoted collagen enrichment that collectively stabilized plaques. Such EV-based immunotherapeutic strategies offer a promising avenue for reversing advanced cardiovascular diseases.

In summary, the session showcased the potential of EV-based therapeutics across diverse contexts: from non-invasive molecular diagnostics of chronic metabolic and cardiac conditions, to improved maternal-fetal outcomes, innovative tissue-targeting strategies for regenerative cardiology, and immunometabolic remodeling of the atherosclerotic microenvironment. Collectively, these talks underscored the versatility of EVs as a platform for precision medicine and regenerative therapies.

Lightning talks from the above session

During the lightning talks, speakers highlighted an array of innovative EV applications in regenerative medicine, metabolism, and disease detection. Dr. Jose Francis-Oliveira showed that T cell-derived EVs carrying microRNAs cross the blood-brain barrier to modulate neuronal activity and rescue sociability deficits in Rag1 knockout mice, suggesting a new avenue for neurobehavioral interventions. Dr. Zankruti Dave presented a strategy to enhance EV targeting by swapping VSVG with other viral glycoproteins to achieve cell-selective EV uptake. Dr. Michail Spanos described a novel approach to isolate and analyze heart-specific EVs using two unique protein markers (POPDC2 and CHRNE), enabling EV-based cardiac disease profiling in conditions like heart failure and myocardial infarction. Mr. Martin Ng demonstrated that IL-4-polarized macrophage EVs improve survival and cardiac function in mice post-MI, by downregulating type I interferon pathways and inflammatory leukocyte subsets, suggesting a promising therapy for heart failure. Dr. Marta Garcia-Contreras emphasized the role of non-vesicular extracellular nanoparticles in neuroinflammation, identifying microglial-derived exomeres and supermeres that reflect specific inflammatory states and overlap with Alzheimer’s patient profiles. Dr. Ana Salazar Puerta found that EVs are crucial mediators of cell lineage reprogramming, enhancing fibroblast-to-endothelial and myoblast-to-neuron conversions, paving the way for EV-assisted regenerative therapies. Dr. Shikha Rani showcased EV lipidomics as an early biomarker for ovarian cancer detection, while Dr. Hongyan Liao found that TB-infected macrophage EVs promote Th1 polarization of CD4+ T cells, highlighting EVs’ potential in future TB vaccines. Finally, Dr. André Görgens underscored a previously underappreciated EV clearance mechanism involving RBC and platelet binding, crucial for refining EV-based therapeutic designs.

EV in vivo modeling

This session showcased cutting-edge research exploring the origins, roles, and therapeutic applications of EVs in complex disease models. From understanding how tumor-derived EVs reprogram the microenvironment to how EVs mediate metabolic dysfunction, and from optimizing EV engineering for tissue repair to developing biomaterial-based EV delivery systems, these talks collectively illustrated the evolving landscape of in vivo EV studies and their translational potential.

Dr. Kathleen McAndrews examined the fate and function of cancer cell-derived EVs, specifically focusing on tetraspanin CD9-positive vesicles in pancreatic cancer. Using a genetic EV fate mapping approach, Dr. McAndrews showed that CD9 EVs are locally transferred to the pancreatic tumor stroma, accumulating in fibroblasts and macrophages early in disease progression. She demonstrated that this EV uptake reprograms stromal and immune cells, modulating transcriptional profiles and influencing tumorigenesis. This work emphasizes the complexity of EV-mediated intercellular communication in the tumor microenvironment and suggests that dissecting these EV subsets could reveal new EV functions in cancer.47

Dr. Wei Ying’s study focused on EVs derived from gut microbiota that translocate into the host circulation, particularly in obesity-related metabolic disorders.48 He found that bacterial DNA-rich EVs pass through an impaired intestinal barrier in obese mice, promoting tissue inflammation and insulin resistance. CRIg-positive macrophages and complement factors serve as a crucial defensive mechanism, attempting to clear these EVs and mitigate their metabolic effects.49 By highlighting the interplay between gut microbial EVs and host metabolic homeostasis, Dr. Ying’s work underscores the importance of EV-driven crosstalk between microbiota and host immune cells in chronic disease.

In a model of impaired wound healing in diabetes, Dr. Brian Eliceiri deployed sponge implants to identify EV populations driving tissue repair.50 By comparing wild-type and diabetic EVs from macrophage-rich microenvironments, Dr. Eliceiri’s team pinpointed key proteins and microRNAs that promote pro-reparative functions. Testing these engineered EVs in diabetic wounds demonstrated significant improvements in wound closure and tissue remodeling. This research highlights a translational opportunity: rationally engineered EVs tailored with beneficial factors (e.g., protease inhibitors, miRNAs) can be harnessed as biologically active therapeutics to accelerate wound healing and other reparative processes.

Dr. Junjie Xiao introduced an innovative injectable pericardial hydrogel patch loaded with EVs enriched in miR-222 and guided by mechanical cues.51 In a model of ischemia-reperfusion-induced cardiac injury, these EV-laden hydrogels provided targeted EV delivery to the heart, mitigating damage, and enhancing cardiac function. Dr. Xiao’s approach not only demonstrates the feasibility of mechanotransduction-based EV therapies but also merges biomaterials with engineered EV cargo to create biofunctional patches. Such EV-based biomaterial systems have the potential to improve therapeutic specificity, retention, and efficacy in regenerative medicine applications.

The talks in this session collectively underscored the power and complexity of EV biology in vivo. Dr. McAndrews illuminated how tumor-derived EVs manipulate local stroma and immunity, Dr. Ying revealed that microbial EV translocation drives metabolic inflammation, Dr. Eliceiri showcased the therapeutic potential of precisely engineered EV payloads in diabetic wounds, and Dr. Xiao combined EV engineering with biomaterials for cardiac repair. Together, these speakers framed a future in which understanding and manipulating EV release, content, and targeting can unlock new frontiers in combating cancer, metabolic dysfunction, and organ injury through more precise and effective treatments.

EV therapeutics II: EV engineering

This session focused on cutting-edge strategies to enhance the therapeutic potential and stability of EVs. Speakers explored novel engineering techniques to stabilize fragile cargoes, target disease tissues with greater specificity, and exploit EVs’ natural biocompatibility for diverse clinical applications. These talks demonstrated that rationally designed EV platforms could significantly advance the therapeutic index and durability of EV-based medicines.

Dr. Samir El Andaloussi’s presentation focused on engineering EVs for enhanced therapeutic delivery.19,52 His group develops strategies to load EVs with specific RNAs and proteins, improving stability, targeting, and functional potency. By manipulating surface ligands, they guide EVs to diseased tissues, while engineered cargoes modulate disease-relevant pathways. Employing advanced methods like genetic modifications and click chemistry, they achieve highly controlled EV compositions. Dr. Andaloussi also addressed large-scale manufacturing, quality control, and safety considerations for clinical applications. Ultimately, their goal is to establish EVs as versatile, precision-engineered drug delivery systems, enabling more effective and personalized treatments for patients.

Dr. Ke Cheng described the development of EV-based inhaled therapies to treat lung and heart diseases.42,53–56 Starting from clinical trials using lung stem cells and their EV derivatives, Dr. Cheng’s team discovered that inhaled EV therapies can stabilize lung function in IPF patients, potentially outperforming current drugs. Additionally, inhaled EVs were shown to redistribute from the lung to the heart, providing cardioprotective effects following myocardial infarction. Mechanistic studies revealed that endothelial metabolism shifting (via down-regulation of CD36) plays a major role in cardioprotection. Beyond regenerative medicine, Dr. Cheng’s lab engineered EVs to deliver ACE2 decoys for neutralizing SARS-CoV-2 and to encapsulate mRNA or protein-based therapeutics.57–59 Novel dry-powder EV formulations further expand ease of delivery. Collectively, this work highlighted how EV engineering, through inhalation routes, functional cargo loading, and powder formulations, can accelerate the translation of EV therapies for lung and heart diseases.

Dr. Yong Tao focused on engineered EVs for treating vitreoretinal diseases such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and uveitis.60 By engineering EVs with VEGF antibodies and MMP-sensitive peptides, Dr. Tao’s group created EVs that target choroidal neovascular lesions, sustaining drug retention and reducing off-target effects. In animal models, these EVs significantly curbed pathological neovascularization and inflammation. Another approach utilized EVs coated with cerium oxide nanoparticles to scavenge reactive oxygen species and modulate immune responses in posterior uveitis. Dr. Tao also introduced artificial cells that mimic EV release, enabling controlled, stable intravitreal delivery. These engineered EV systems improved structural preservation and vision-related outcomes in preclinical models, demonstrating the promise of EV-based precision drug targeting and sustained release for ocular therapeutics.

Dr. Najla Adel Saleh’s presentation addressed the challenge of stabilizing interleukin-10 (IL-10), a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine, using EVs.61 By overexpressing IL-10 in EV-producing cells, her team produced EVs that present both monomeric and oligomeric IL-10 forms. These IL-10-enriched EVs retained their cytokine’s functional stability under various stress conditions (temperature changes, freeze-thaw cycles) far better than free recombinant IL-10 protein. When tested on pro-inflammatory macrophages, IL-10 EVs more effectively suppressed inflammatory gene expression than soluble IL-10. Interestingly, even naïve EVs showed some anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting EV-intrinsic bioactivities. Dr. Saleh’s work supports EVs as a robust platform to stabilize and deliver cytokine therapeutics with improved functionality and durability.

The talks in this session illustrated how engineering EVs can unlock their full therapeutic potential. Dr. Cheng demonstrated inhaled EV formulations for lung and heart repair, including viral decoys and mRNA delivery. Dr. Tao presented strategies for ocular EV therapies, combining VEGF inhibition, antioxidant nanoparticles, and artificial EV-releasing implants, to achieve targeted, durable treatments for retinal disease. Dr. Saleh showed how EV loading can stabilize fragile cytokines like IL-10, enhancing their anti-inflammatory effects. Together, these presentations revealed how careful EV engineering, adjusting cargo, surface properties, and formulation, can yield transformative medicines for a wide range of diseases, from respiratory and cardiac conditions to ocular and immunoinflammatory disorders.

Session 10: clinical application of EVs

This session highlighted translational progress in employing EVs as therapeutic agents or biomarkers to tackle challenging diseases. Speakers presented data on EV-based solutions for improving drug delivery, gene therapy, organ and wound repair, and immune modulation. By leveraging the native complexity and biocompatibility of EVs, these studies aim to deliver targeted therapies with minimal toxicity, address chemotherapy-resistant tumors, enhance tissue regeneration, and shape the immune microenvironment. While regulatory and mechanistic hurdles remain, these cutting-edge efforts underscore the promise of EV-based interventions moving closer to clinical implementation.

Dr. Mei He discussed the development of an EV-based delivery system for drugs and gene therapies.62 By using bioinspired and biologically compatible approaches, her group aims to overcome longstanding challenges in drug delivery, such as targeting specific tissues and ensuring controlled release. Dr. He highlighted how EVs, produced under 3D culture conditions and optimized manufacturing pipelines, can be employed to carry therapeutics. Whether inhaled, combined with patches, or engineered to contain RNA or protein cargoes, these EVs demonstrated improved stability, enhanced targeting, and promising efficacy in models of organ and wound repair. The work sets the stage for scalable, off-the-shelf EV therapeutics with broad clinical applications.

Dr. Valérie Kalluri presented a journey from the discovery stage to a phase one clinical trial using exosomes engineered with siRNA targeting the Kras G12D mutation in pancreatic cancer patients.7 These engineered EVs, derived from standardized MSC cultures, delivered targeted anti-oncogenic cargo that effectively suppressed tumor growth, including therapy-resistant clones, in preclinical studies. The clinical trial confirmed no detectable toxicity and initial hints of target engagement in patients. Dr. LeBleu emphasized lessons learned about manufacturing scale-up, characterization, and regulatory navigation. This pioneering effort showcases that engineered EV therapeutics can advance rapidly from bench to bedside for precision cancer therapy.

Focusing on MSC-derived EVs, Dr. Bernd Giebel discussed their clinical potential and translational hurdles.63 Although MSCs have shown promise in multiple disease models, donor variability and aging limit their consistency. Dr. Giebel’s group demonstrated that MSC-EVs retain multifaceted immunomodulatory and regenerative functionalities critical for treating conditions like graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and ischemic stroke. By establishing a robust potency assay and working toward MSC cell lines to reduce batch variability, their approach seeks to achieve a reliable and scalable EV product. This strategy aims to produce reproducible, potent MSC-EVs for future clinical applications where single-molecule drugs fall short.

Addressing glioblastoma’s complex microenvironment, Dr. Irene Bertolini highlighted the role of microglia-derived EVs in recruiting bone marrow–derived myeloid cells to the tumor site.64 By manipulating microglia under tumor conditions, her team showed that these EVs carry cargo that expands inflammatory monocytes in the bone marrow and acts as chemoattractants to the tumor. This work uncovers a new dimension in how EVs modulate immune cell infiltration in brain tumors, potentially offering novel intervention points. Inhibiting microglia EV-mediated recruitment could complement current therapies and improve outcomes for glioblastoma patients.

Ameya Chaudhari presented an innovative auxetic patch platform for enhanced wound healing and organ repair, with a focus on diabetic foot ulcers.65 These 3D-printed patches are ultra-elastic, biodegradable, and instantly adhesive, conforming to dynamic tissue movements and overcoming the challenges of traditional wound dressings. Loaded with MSC-EVs, the patches demonstrated sustained EV release, significant wound closure in vitro and in vivo, and upregulation of genes involved in healing. The patches also successfully repaired lung punctures and can be adapted to deliver diverse therapeutics, offering broad clinical applications in regenerative medicine.

This session showcased transformative progress in utilizing EVs as therapeutic agents and biomarkers to tackle complex diseases. The presentations spanned advancements in EV-based solutions for drug delivery, gene therapy, organ and wound repair, and immune modulation. By leveraging the biocompatibility and functional versatility of EVs, these studies demonstrated their potential to deliver targeted therapies with minimal toxicity, combat chemotherapy-resistant tumors, enhance tissue regeneration, and modulate the immune microenvironment. While challenges such as regulatory hurdles, manufacturing scale-up, and mechanistic insights remain, the session highlighted how innovative strategies are accelerating the clinical translation of EV-based interventions, setting the stage for their integration into next-generation precision medicine.

Advanced technology in industry

Dr. Steve Greco introduced PranaX, an early-stage technology company focused on healthy aging, wellness, and inflammatory disorders. PranaX envisions leveraging exosomes as a central component of next-generation therapies and preventive healthcare solutions. The firm’s business model includes three main lines: (1) Exosome banking-storing personal EV samples for future use, providing a form of bio-insurance; (2) Engineered MSC-derived exosomes as health supplements for overall wellness; and (3) EV-based therapeutics targeting age-related diseases. PranaX aims to overcome complex regulatory hurdles, enhance scalability, and improve safety profiles, ultimately making exosomes more accessible as advanced wellness and therapeutic products.

Dr. John Nolan presented practical approaches to EV characterization using spectral flow cytometry and single-vesicle analysis tools. Working closely with Cytekbio’s Aurora and Northern Lights platforms, he emphasized how spectral flow cytometry enables multi-marker EV phenotyping, enhanced sensitivity, and resolution. This allows researchers to parse EV heterogeneity and quantify surface markers more reliably. Additionally, Dr. Nolan introduced Cellarcus Vesicle Flow Cytometry (vFC) Assay reagent kits, standards, and protocols designed to streamline EV assays across various flow cytometers. These tools help calibrate EV measurements, confirm single-vesicle resolution, and yield quantitative data on size, cargo, and structure, facilitating reproducible and insightful EV research.

Dr. Jason Lowery introduced Beckman Coulter’s CytoFLEX Nano, a purpose-built flow cytometer optimized for nanoparticle and EV analysis. With enhanced scatter sensitivity and up to six fluorescence channels, it can detect particles as small as ~40 nm. The instrumenťs automation and intuitive software simplify EV workflows, from experimental setup to data acquisition. This dedicated EV analyzer provides fast, consistent measurement of EV size, concentration, and phenotypic markers, greatly improving efficiency and accuracy for EV researchers.

Dr. John Cadwell presented FiberCell Systems’ hollow fiber bioreactor technology for high-density 3D cell culture and EV production. FiberCell’s artificial capillary design mimics physiological conditions, achieving in vivo-like cell densities without frequent passaging. This cytocentric and even tissue-centric approach yields concentrated EVs with improved bioactivity, consistency, and reduced contaminants. FiberCell’s long-term, stable cultures (up to years) can produce large EV quantities suitable for clinical-scale EV therapeutics. The controlled environment and scalable design position FiberCell’s hollow fiber bioreactors as ideal platforms for commercial EV manufacturing.

Dr. Zheng Zhuang introduced Exodus Bio’s automated exosome isolation systems powered by novel dual-membrane nanofiltration cartridges. These devices enable rapid, label-free concentration of EVs from diverse biofluids (including plasma, urine, and conditioned media) and even from bacteria or viruses. Exodus’s systems can handle volumes up to 10 L (in GMP-compatible models) and yield intact EVs with excellent purity and recovery. Their user-friendly design and reproducible results aim to reduce complexity, save time, and accelerate EV research from discovery to application.

In summary, these industry speakers showcased a range of cutting-edge EV technologies-from large-scale, physiologically relevant bioreactors for EV production and spectral flow cytometry instrumentation, to novel chip-based isolation systems and engineered EV therapeutics. Collectively, these solutions promise more efficient, reproducible, and clinically scalable EV workflows, driving forward the translation of EV research into real-world diagnostics, therapeutics, and preventive healthcare.

Conclusion

The AAEV 2024 Annual Meeting highlighted the remarkable advancements and interdisciplinary collaborations propelling extracellular vesicle research forward. Keynote addresses by Dr. Raghu Kalluri and Dr. Graca Raposo laid the foundational understanding of EV heterogeneity, biogenesis, and their pivotal roles in intercellular communication and disease progression. Subsequent sessions delved into innovative technologies for EV isolation, characterization, and engineering, demonstrating how these tools are overcoming longstanding challenges related to reproducibility, scalability, and functional specificity. The integration of advanced analytical techniques, such as spectral flow cytometry and nano-scale analysis, with scalable production platforms like hollow fiber bioreactors and automated chip-based systems, underscores a concerted effort to standardize and streamline EV research and therapeutic application.

Industry presentations further emphasized the translational potential of EV technologies, showcasing platforms that enhance EV isolation purity and throughput, as well as engineered EVs tailored for targeted delivery and therapeutic efficacy. Clinical application sessions provided compelling evidence of EVs’ utility in diagnostics and as therapeutic agents across a spectrum of diseases, from cancer and metabolic disorders to regenerative medicine and immune modulation. Collectively, these insights illustrate a dynamic and rapidly evolving field poised to integrate EV-based solutions into mainstream clinical practice, offering novel diagnostics, personalized therapies, and regenerative treatments that leverage the innate biocompatibility and functional versatility of EVs.

Future directions

As extracellular vesicle research continues to mature, several key trajectories are poised to shape the next phase of innovation and clinical integration:

Harmonization of standards and protocols:

Continued efforts to establish rigorous, standardized protocols for EV isolation, characterization, and functional assays will be crucial. Community-driven initiatives to develop reference standards and calibration materials will enhance data comparability and reproducibility across laboratories and studies, facilitating broader clinical adoption and regulatory approval.

Integration of artificial intelligence and automation:

Leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) for data analysis and automating EV workflows will streamline the entire EV pipeline, from sample processing to data interpretation. AI-driven algorithms can enhance biomarker discovery, optimize EV engineering processes, and enable real-time quality control, thereby accelerating the translation of EV research into clinical applications.

Enhanced cargo engineering and targeting:

Advances in genetic and chemical engineering will enable more precise loading of therapeutic cargos, such as siRNAs, CRISPR components, and therapeutic proteins, into EVs. Additionally, surface modification techniques will improve EV targeting specificity, allowing for the delivery of therapeutics to precise tissues or cell types, thereby maximizing efficacy and minimizing off-target effects.

Scalable and GMP-compliant manufacturing:

Developing scalable, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant platforms for EV production will be essential for commercializing EV-based therapeutics. Innovations in bioreactor design, automated isolation systems, and robust quality control measures will support large-scale, reproducible EV production necessary for clinical trials and commercial deployment.

Comprehensive understanding of EV biodistribution and mechanisms:

Expanding our knowledge of EV biodistribution, uptake mechanisms, and in vivo functionality will inform the design of more effective EV-based therapies. Advanced imaging techniques, single-particle tracking, and in vivo modeling will provide deeper insights into how EVs interact with target cells and tissues, enabling the rational design of EV therapeutics with enhanced therapeutic indices.

Translational and clinical validation:

Collaborative efforts between academia, industry, and clinical institutions will drive the rigorous testing and validation of EV-based diagnostics and therapeutics. Conducting well-designed clinical trials to evaluate safety, efficacy, and therapeutic outcomes will be paramount in establishing EVs as reliable clinical tools.

Expanding therapeutic applications:

Beyond oncology and regenerative medicine, EV-based therapies hold promise for treating a wide range of conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular disorders, autoimmune diseases, and infectious diseases. Exploring these diverse applications will broaden the impact of EV research and therapy development.

Regulatory and ethical considerations:

Navigating the regulatory landscape and addressing ethical considerations related to EV-based therapies will be critical for their successful translation into the clinic. Engaging with regulatory bodies early in the development process and adhering to ethical guidelines will facilitate smoother approval pathways and public acceptance.

In conclusion, the convergence of fundamental biological insights, technological innovations, and clinical research is propelling extracellular vesicle research toward a transformative era. By addressing current challenges and capitalizing on emerging opportunities, the field is set to unlock the full potential of EVs in precision medicine, offering ground-breaking solutions for diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of a myriad of diseases.
