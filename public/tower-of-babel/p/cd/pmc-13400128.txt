AI-ECG for Detecting Left Ventricular Systolic Dysfunction in Chagas Disease
Authors: Clareci Silva Cardoso, Kathryn Mangold, José Luiz Padilha da Silva, Cláudia Di Lorenzo Oliveira, Ariela Mota Ferreira, Lea Campos Oliveira da Silva, Maria do Carmo P. Nunes, Wanessa Campos Vinhal, Ana Carolina de Oliveira Gonçalves, Paulo Rodrigues Gomes, Pablo Perel, Itzhak Zachi Attia, Ester Cerdeira Sabino, Francisco Lopez-Jimenez, Antonio Luiz Pinho Ribeiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) is the main predictor of mortality in Chagas disease (ChD). Although LVSD can be treated with affordable medications, its diagnosis relies on cardiac imaging, which is often unavailable in resource-limited settings.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to evaluate an artificial intelligence–enabled electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) for detecting LVSD and predicting mortality and incident LVSD in ChD.MethodsA previously developed AI-ECG LVSD model was fine-tuned in an external ChD sample and applied to SaMi-Trop, a Brazilian prospective ChD cohort. Diagnostic performance for LVSD, confirmed by echocardiography, was compared with N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). Prognostic performance for all-cause mortality at 2 and 9 years was assessed using Cox models, and incident LVSD over 7 years using log-binomial models.ResultsAmong 1,304 participants, AI-ECG showed high accuracy for LVSD detection (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.85-0.93), similar to NT-proBNP (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.87-0.93; P = 0.52). Among 1,547 patients with Chagas cardiomyopathy, AI-ECG predicted all-cause mortality at 2 and 9 years with performance comparable to NT-proBNP (9-year C-index: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.74-0.82 vs 0.77; 95% CI: 0.74-0.79). AI-ECG could replace NT-proBNP in an established ChD mortality risk score with minor loss of accuracy. Incident LVSD occurred in 8.4% over 7.3 years, and AI-ECG predicted incident LVSD with performance similar to NT-proBNP.ConclusionsA fine-tuned AI-ECG model showed high accuracy for LVSD detection and was independently associated with long-term mortality and incident LVSD. Despite slightly lower performance than NT-proBNP, it may serve as a substitute in settings where NT-proBNP is unavailable. (Longitudinal Study of Patients With Chronic Chagas Cardiomyopathy in Brazil [SaMi_Trop Project] [SaMi-Trop]; NCT02646943)

Chagas disease (ChD), caused by Trypanosoma cruzi infection, remains a significant global public health challenge due to its high morbidity and mortality burden.1 Despite the possibility of a cure and advancements in transmission control, ChD continues to be classified as endemic across continental Latin America, with new estimates of more than 10 million infected persons2 and an estimated 30,000 new cases and 10,000 ChD-related deaths are reported annually.3

Many individuals with ChD remain asymptomatic for years with symptoms may manifest only at a later stage of the disease. The disease has an acute phase, typically lasting 4 to 8 weeks, which is often mild or asymptomatic, followed by a chronic phase. If left untreated, the infection can persist for a lifetime; however, timely treatment can reduce the risk of disease progression.1,4,5 An estimated 30% of individuals with untreated ChD will develop heart disease, often progressing to serious and life-threatening complications that may require specialized care. This condition is called chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy (ChCM) characterized by increasing fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and electrical and mechanical heart dysfunction.6 Clinically, ChCM is identified by an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG), with or without left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD).1

LVSD is the main predictor of mortality in ChCM.7 This condition can be treated with affordable drugs that improve symptoms and survival. Diagnosing LVSD requires cardiac imaging tests like echocardiography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance, which are often unavailable in most endemic and remote areas.8 The ECG, a low-cost and widely available tool in primary care,9 plays a key role in ChD evaluation.10 Although many ECG abnormalities are linked to worse prognosis and the need for further care, only a fraction of those with abnormal ECGs have LVSD. In addition, LVSD can occur with mildly abnormal ECG changes, limiting its utility in identifying candidates for treatments.8

Significant barriers to screening, diagnosis, and treatment of ChD persist, contributing to the under-reporting and invisibility of cases.11 Early identification of LVSD may help prevent its progression to symptomatic heart failure (HF).12,13 In patients with a high risk of HF, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP)-based screening followed by team-based care can be useful to prevent the development of LVSD or new-onset HF.12 In ChD, BNP or NT-proBNP is an accurate biomarker for the presence of LVSD14 and an independent predictor for the risk of death.15 However, BNP or NT-proBNP are not available at the primary care level in most endemic countries for ChD, limiting their clinical utility.

Recent studies have explored the use of artificial intelligence–enabled ECGs (AI-ECGs) images for the detection of LVSD.8,16,17 Our group demonstrated that an AI-ECG algorithm originally derived and validated in the general population of a high-income country (and not in patients with ChD), when enriched with basic clinical information and ECG parameters, may accurately predict LVSD in ChD patients.8 Although promising, the AI-ECG model demonstrated suboptimal accuracy when used alone. In the present study, we propose to assess the performance of an improved AI-ECG model in detecting LVSD and its prognostic value for incident LVSD and mortality in patients with ChD. First, we will fine-tune the AI-ECG model to improve its ability to identify LVSD in ChD patients and compare its diagnostic accuracy with the standard-of-care NT-proBNP. Second, we will evaluate whether this AI-ECG model predicts death during up to 9 years of follow-up. It will also test whether we can replace NT-proBNP with the AI-ECG output in an existing mortality risk score,18 thereby simplifying the risk stratification process. Finally, we will assess the AI-ECG's ability to predict new-onset LVSD, in comparison with NT-proBNP and 12-lead ECG, in individuals with a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) (LVEF), as detected by echocardiography during follow-up. We hypothesize that the enhanced AI-ECG model will accurately detect LVSD and predict both mortality and new-onset LVSD, providing a tool for assessing disease progression in patients with ChD.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This study was conducted with patients from the SaMi-Trop project, a prospective cohort study initiated in 2013 by a network of collaborating scientists in the states of Minas Gerais and São Paulo, Brazil.19 The cohort comprises individuals with a confirmed seropositive diagnosis for ChD, including patients from 21 municipalities in Minas Gerais, an endemic region. The baseline study visit took place in 2013 to 2014, followed by the first follow-up (FU1) in 2015 to 2016 and the second follow-up (FU2) in 2019 to 2022.15 The SaMi-Trop cohort was originally established to develop a prognostic algorithm that integrates simple ECG measurements with clinical information and NT-proBNP levels to predict disease progression and mortality in patients with ChD.

This project leverages the coverage of the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais,20 which provides the technological infrastructure for acquiring, cloud-storing, and analyzing ECG patterns and echocardiogram (Echo) images. In this program, all patients' ECGs and clinical data are sent to a central reading unit center, which also collects clinical data, such as the history of ChD. Using this database, we selected 21 municipalities within a limited region in the northern part of the State of Minas Gerais where the prevalence of patients with chronic ChCM was expected to be high.19 Eligible patients, selected based on the ECG, performed in 2011 to 2012, were contacted by telephone or letter and invited to participate. Interviews were conducted at primary health care units in each municipality. The interview protocol included a structured questionnaire collecting demographic and clinical information, blood sampling for NT-proBNP measurement, and the acquisition of ECG and, for FU1 and FU2, Echo. Mortality data from each follow-up were subsequently verified both by information of the patient’s relatives or health professionals and through record linkage with the Brazilian Mortality Information System.21

Chagas model AI-ECG fine-tuning and application to SaMi-Trop

In a previous study published in 2021,8 we applied and evaluated an AI-ECG algorithm developed for the detection of LVSD in the general population, published in 2019 (AI-ECG-2019), to 1304 SaMi-Trop ChD patients from the FU1 visit, who had both ECG and Echo exams. For the current study, we fine-tuned the model using the Retrovirus Donors II (REDS II) data set, part of the National Epidemiology Study of REDS II,22 as an external sample of ChD patients. The REDS II data set was split by patients into training (70%), testing (10%) and validation (20%). The REDS II data set and the fine-tune process are described in detail in the supplementary material. The new REDS II fine-tuned model (AI-ECG), parsed into 2-second windows was applied into 2 waves of SaMi-Trop, baseline (1.906 patients) and SaMi-Trop FU1 (1.304 patients).

Study outcomes and covariates

The outcomes were prevalent LVSD in FU1 in the cross-sectional study, all-cause mortality at 2 years, and at 9.3 years of follow-up (FU1 and FU2), and disease progression, defined as the development of LVSD among those with preserved LVEF at baseline (FU1) who progressed to reduced LVEF at FU2. LVSD was identified by LVEF ≤40% on Echo. Deaths were reported by family or health professionals and verified through record linkage with the Brazilian Mortality Information System.

Age was considered continuous, and sex was categorized as a binary variable (male/female) based on sex assigned at birth. Cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, were self-reported and classified as binary variables. Functional capacity was assessed using the NYHA functional classification based on a standardized physical activity questionnaire, categorized into 4 classes (I–IV) and dichotomized as I–II vs III–IV.12,23 Serial measurements of NT-proBNP were also obtained and was treated both as a continuous and binary variable, categorized according to age-specific cut points for HF.24

Standard 12-lead ECGs were acquired using uniform protocols and centrally interpreted by trained cardiologists blinded to clinical data, according to the Minnesota Code criteria.8 ECG findings were classified as normal, minor abnormalities, or major abnormalities based on the predefined criteria (Supplemental Table 1).25 Transthoracic echocardiographic evaluations were performed at FU1 and FU2 using commercially available ultrasound systems. Echocardiographic images were digitally stored and analyzed offline by a centralized reading center following the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography.26 The echocardiography physician did not have access to the AI-ECG results.

Statistical analysis

Before analysis, data underwent routine cleaning, consistency checks, and verification of linkage across ECG, epidemiological, and serological records. ECG tracings with inadequate technical quality and records with invalid or missing key identifiers were excluded. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means and SDs or medians and ranges.

For the diagnostic evaluation, discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Four models were evaluated (NT-proBNP, AI-ECG-2019, AI-ECG-ChD, and NT-proBNP + AI-ECG-ChD), and correlated receiver operating characteristic curves were compared using the DeLong test. Diagnostic accuracy measures (sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values) were estimated for AI-ECG in identifying LVSD, using an echocardiographic EF ≤40% as the reference standard. Nonparametric percentile bootstrap with 5,000 replicates was used to obtain 95% CIs. Diagnostic ORs were estimated using logistic regression, with 95% CIs derived from standard asymptotic methods. Calibration was evaluated using calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, and the Brier score. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis to estimate net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities.

For predicting the risk of death, the modeling process was performed in 2 main steps. First, Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to illustrate survival according to AI-ECG status and NT-proBNP for both 2-year mortality (FU1) and overall follow-up. Next, Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association of variables with death. Two sets of models were fitted for the 2 main outcomes: death within 2 years (FU1) and death over the entire follow-up period. The AI-ECG–based screening status (positive or negative) was used as the main independent variable.

Mortality models were specified a priori based on combinations of baseline variables (age, NYHA functional class, heart rate, QRS duration, and NT-proBNP). The original model corresponded to the SaMi-Trop mortality score, including all 5 variables.18 An updated model replaced NT-proBNP with AI-ECG while maintaining the remaining variables. A simplified model included only 3 variables: age, NYHA functional class, and AI-ECG.

Covariates were defined as follows: age (per decade); NYHA functional class (I vs II–IV); heart rate (≥80 bpm); QRS duration (≥150 ms); NT-proBNP (normal vs age-adjusted abnormal levels); and AI-ECG screening status (positive vs negative). HRs with 95% CIs were estimated, proportional hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and model discrimination was assessed using Harrell C-index. Internal validation was performed with 1,000 bootstrap resamples, with optimism-corrected C-index and calibration slope reported. Model selection and comparison were additionally guided by the Akaike information criterion, with lower values indicating better relative model fit and lower information loss.

Univariable and multivariable log-binomial models were used to predict new LVSD from FU1 to FU2. The models included variables measured at the FU1 visit, including AI-ECG status, abnormal NT-proBNP levels, and major ECG abnormalities, all treated as binary variables. We also tested these variables in pairwise combinations within the same model, namely AI-ECG positivity with major ECG abnormalities, NT-proBNP with major ECG abnormalities, and AI-ECG positivity with NT-proBNP. All 6 models were further adjusted for sex, age, diabetes, and hypertension. Discrimination was assessed using the AUC. Relative risks with 95% CIs were estimated for each factor. Calibration was evaluated using calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, and the Brier score. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed using Poisson regression with robust variance and a log-time offset to account for varying follow-up time. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis.

The data were assumed to be missing at random. Specifically, it was assumed that missingness was unrelated to the outcome, conditional on the observed data. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3), using the packages tidyverse, table1, surminer, rms, pROC, and dcurves.

This cohort study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee and by the Brazilian National Research Ethics Commission under protocol number 179.685/2012. The SaMi-Trop study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02646943). This work was funded by the National Institutes of Health under grants U01AI098461 and 1UIAI68383. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, interpretation, or dissemination of this study.

The data set used to assess the performance of the fine-tuned AI-ECG model included 1,304 patients with ChD, with a mean age of 60 years (SD 12.5); 67.0% were females, and 42.8% were illiterate. NYHA functional class III-IV was found in 35.6%, a heart rate of <80 (87.5%), QRS interval <150 (84.3%), and normal NT-proBNP levels in 86.0% of these patients. Major ECG alterations were observed in 59.5%. Diabetes (11.0%) and hypertension (64.4%) were the most prevalent comorbidities (Table 1). AI-ECG to predict incident LVSD

We evaluated the ability of the AI-ECG from FU1 to predict new LVSD, as detected by echocardiography at FU2. Discussion

This study further advances the knowledge of the potential value of AI-ECG, showing that: 1) a model explicitly fine-tuned for the ChCM has an excellent performance on the detection of LVSD, comparable to the standard method, NT-proBNP; and 2) this model has strong prognostic value, serving not only as a robust biomarker for 2-year and long-term mortality risk but also as an independent predictor of disease progression over long-term follow-up, slightly inferior to NT-proBNP based models. Indeed, when incorporated into a simplified ChD mortality score, it effectively replaced NT-proBNP with minimal loss of predictive power.

It is widely recognized that high-quality deep neural networks, when trained on 1 ECG data set, may not perform as well on different data sets or in various clinical environments. In our study, the accuracy of the AI-ECG model for detecting LVSD improved significantly from the baseline AUC of 0.839 (0.793-0.885)8 with the original model, trained on non-Chagas U.S. data, to 0.89 (95% CI: 0.85-0.93) after fine-tuning in ChD patients. This level of accuracy was previously achieved in our earlier study only by incorporating NT-proBNP values and clinical variables into the model. Indeed, the AUC of the AI-ECG for detecting LVSD was comparable to that of the NT-proBNP, the standard biomarker recommended for screening in patients at high risk for HF. This enhancement in accuracy makes the AI-ECG approach for screening LVSD highly appealing, especially considering the low cost and widespread availability of ECGs in many endemic regions.12,27 Early identification of LVSD is key, as timely initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy can alter the disease trajectory, prevent progression to overt HF, and improve life expectancy.28 The availability of low-cost screening for ventricular systolic dysfunction can enable the optimized use of limited imaging resources, allowing patients to benefit from the early initiation of effective therapies, such as beta-blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors.12,16 However, it remains to be proven whether screening for LVSD using AI-ECG can be equally efficient in reducing the risk of progression to overt HF, as shown by the strategy using natriuretic peptides.29

We demonstrated, for the first time in ChD, that the AI-ECG for detention of LVSD has strong independent prognostic value for the middle- and long-term risk of death. Since both the AI-ECG and NT-proBNP are biomarkers of LVSD and HF, we tested whether we could substitute NT-proBNP into our previously described risk score for ChCM, the SaMi-Trop score. BNP/NT-ProBNP are established prognostic markers of death risk in ChD, but the lack of access to primary care physicians has been regarded as an obstacle to utilization of SaMi-Trop score in clinical practice.29 On the other hand, the established Rassi score relies on clinical features, ECG, X-ray, Echo, and Holter monitoring, as well as stress testing, which are typically restricted to tertiary centers where all these exams are available. Thus, our goal was to further simplify the SaMi-Trop score,18 which is designed to depend on a few complementary exams and to be helpful for primary care. Our simplified SaMi-Trop mortality score meets this expectation, demonstrating excellent predictive ability with only 3 variables: age, NYHA functional class > I, and AI-ECG information.

It is also remarkable that the AI-ECG flagged patients with initially preserved EF who later developed systolic dysfunction, suggesting its sensitivity to early, subclinical electrical, or structural changes. This phenomenon was described in the original 2019 low EF paper and confirmed recently by other researchers for the general population.16,17 Again, AI-ECG and NT-proBNP models, when adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes, had similar accuracy in predicting future LVSD, with the AUC reaching over 0.75 for both strategies when the standard ECG is included in the model. Whether AI-ECG-identified high-risk patients would benefit from targeted therapeutic interventions remains an important question for future prospective studies.

This study demonstrates that the AI-ECG algorithm is an effective, low-cost screening tool for LVSD when integrated directly into standard ECG platforms. Because it leverages existing infrastructure, it can be deployed at scale to screen large populations, streamline mortality-risk scoring, and track ChD progression across diverse clinical settings.

Limitations and strengths

Our study has limitations and strengths that warrant consideration. First, we did not collect data on hospitalizations, which could serve as a direct marker of ChD progression and its clinical impact. Second, there is a risk of under-reporting deaths due to loss of follow-up, potentially leading to an underestimation of the actual mortality risk. To mitigate this bias, in addition to death reports from health care professionals and family members, we cross-referenced mortality with the Brazilian Ministry of Health’s Mortality Information System, which has an estimated under-registration rate of only 3.65%.30 Third, although the original SaMi-Trop risk score has been validated in an external cohort,18 we did not validate the simplified score and the prediction of future LVSD in external cohorts, a necessary step for using the AI-ECG in clinical practice. Fourth, a further limitation is that the distribution of NYHA functional class in this cohort differs substantially from that of the data set used for model fine-tuning, as the present cohort consists of patients with suspected cardiomyopathy, whereas REDS II; used for fine-tuning; primarily includes asymptomatic blood donors. This discrepancy in clinical severity may influence model performance across populations with different functional profiles. Finally, in this study, in addition, NYHA functional class was assessed using a standardized questionnaire administered by trained nurses; however, inconsistencies were identified during data review. Because NYHA functional class was not central to the primary outcomes, it was ultimately categorized as class I-II vs III-IV following data review. Conversely, the study also has notable strengths. Most data are primary, systematically collected at baseline and during follow-up interviews according to standardized protocols and conducted by a specialized team.

Conclusions

In this study, a fine-tuned AI-ECG model demonstrated high accuracy for detecting LVSD in patients with ChD and was independently associated with long-term mortality and disease progression. Its performance was comparable to, although slightly lower than, NT-proBNP across diagnostic and prognostic analyses (Central Illustration). These findings support the potential role of AI-ECG as a pragmatic alternative to NT-proBNP, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to biomarkers and imaging is restricted.
