Cardiorespiratory fitness modulates the effect of acute exercise on vigilance performance in trained soldiers
Authors: Jose Luis Bermejo Ruiz, Bruno Ribeiro do Couto, Gonzalo Monfort Torres, Francesc Llorens Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is known to influence cognitive performance, particularly in tasks requiring sustained attention. However, the acute effects of high-intensity exercise on vigilance performance across different CRF levels remain underexplored. This study examined the impact of CRF level and a single bout of maximal incremental running on vigilance. Thirty-four male soldiers were assigned to either high- or low-CRF groups (n = 17 each). Participants performed a psychomotor vigilance go/no-go task at rest and immediately following exercise to exhaustion. Reaction time (RT), commission errors (CE), and omission errors (OE) were recorded and analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. Both groups showed improved RT after exercise compared to baseline. Participants with higher CRF consistently exhibited faster RTs across all task blocks. However, the low-CRF group demonstrated a greater magnitude of improvement post-exercise, suggesting a larger acute response to exercise. CRF modulates the cognitive effects of acute high-intensity exercise. Although all participants benefited, individuals with lower CRF showed relatively larger improvements in vigilance performance. These findings have implications for enhancing cognitive readiness in physically demanding settings, such as military operations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55140-5.

Introduction

Cardiovascular fitness, commonly operationalized through cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), is a key indicator of cardiovascular health and an important predictor of overall physical performance and disease prevention, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity1. Although the terms cardiovascular fitness, cardiovascular capacity, and cardiorespiratory fitness are sometimes used interchangeably in the literature, CRF typically refers to the ability of the circulatory and respiratory systems to supply oxygen during sustained physical activity and is commonly assessed through maximal oxygen uptake or performance during graded exercise tests. Over the last decades, the relationship between cardiovascular capacity and cognitive performance has attracted considerable scientific interest2–4. However, despite growing evidence linking physical fitness and cognitive functioning, important questions remain regarding the mechanisms underlying this association and the specific conditions under which exercise may enhance or impair cognitive performance.

In general, previous studies suggest that higher cardiovascular capacity is associated with better cognitive performance across a range of domains5. Physical activity (PA) is considered an effective strategy for improving cardiovascular capacity and may consequently enhance cognitive functioning6,7, particularly in tasks requiring sustained attention or vigilance. Vigilance refers to the ability to maintain attention and respond to stimuli over prolonged periods of time, a cognitive function that is especially relevant in operational environments such as military contexts. For example, González-Fernández et al.8 demonstrated that eight weeks of physical training improved vigilance performance and efficiency, while Luque-Casado et al.9 reported a positive association between aerobic fitness and sustained attention. These findings suggest that higher CRF may support cognitive processes that are critical for maintaining alertness and rapid responses during demanding tasks.

This issue is particularly relevant in military settings, where soldiers are often required to maintain vigilance and inhibitory control under conditions of intense physical exertion, in which fatigue and operational demands may compromise response execution and cognitive control10,11. In this context, tasks combining sustained attention and response inhibition may offer a more ecologically valid approach to studying post-exercise cognition in trained military personnel.

Several neurobiological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the relationship between physical activity and cognitive function. Regular PA is associated with increased levels of neurotrophic and growth factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), insulin-like growth factor-1, and vascular endothelial growth factor, which facilitate cognitive functioning through cellular adaptations including synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and improved cerebral blood flow12–14. Empirical evidence further suggests that individuals with higher CRF tend to exhibit greater cortical volume in frontal, temporal, and parietal regions as well as in the hippocampus8,15,16. These structural adaptations may contribute to improved memory, attention, processing speed, and executive functioning compared to individuals with lower CRF14,15,17.

In addition to long-term adaptations, acute bouts of physical activity can also influence cognitive performance. Electroencephalography (EEG) studies have shown increased neural activity in the prefrontal cortex and improved performance on visual attention tasks following acute exercise18. Such findings suggest that individuals with higher CRF may exhibit reduced neural latency in the prefrontal and parietal cortices during reaction-time tasks, indicating more efficient neural processing19. These results support the idea that physical activity may improve cognitive performance through attention-related mechanisms20, particularly in tasks requiring executive control and sustained attention.

Acute high-intensity exercise may also produce transient reductions in prefrontal cortical resources, a mechanism that has been described within the framework of the transient hypofrontality hypothesis21. According to this view, metabolic and attentional resources are temporarily redistributed toward motor and autonomic demands during strenuous exercise, which may contribute to inconsistent post-exercise cognitive outcomes. In line with this interpretation, some studies have reported impaired working memory or reduced prefrontal activation after acute high-intensity exercise22,23, whereas others have found improvements or no clear changes12,24, suggesting that the final cognitive outcome may depend on moderators such as fitness level, timing of assessment, and task demands25.

These divergent findings suggest that the relationship between physical activity and cognition is complex and moderated by several factors25. Among these, physical fitness level appears to be a particularly important moderator. Additional factors include the type of cognitive task, the timing of cognitive assessment (during exercise, immediately after exercise, or following a recovery period), participant characteristics such as age, and the characteristics of the exercise stimulus itself, typically described through the FITT framework (frequency, intensity, time, and type)24,26,27.

Within this framework, it is important to distinguish between the acute effects of a single bout of exercise and the long-term adaptations resulting from regular training. Acute exercise can induce short-term and reversible physiological and neurocognitive responses6,28,29, whereas structured exercise training programs may lead to more persistent changes in brain structure and cognitive functioning (Northey et al. 2018). While there is relatively consistent evidence that moderate-intensity exercise of short to moderate duration can enhance vigilance performance8, the cognitive effects of high-intensity exercise are less clear30.

Some studies have reported that acute high-intensity exercise may facilitate cognitive performance31,32, particularly during exercise performed to exhaustion, which may temporarily increase alertness and neural activation27,33. These effects may selectively benefit executive functions such as inhibitory control and attention34,35. Inhibitory control refers to the ability to suppress inappropriate responses and is often assessed through tasks such as the go/no-go paradigm. Chang et al.6 examined cardiorespiratory fitness as a moderator in a meta-analysis by comparing acute-exercise effect sizes across fitness strata. Their results suggested that statistically significant cognitive benefits were more consistently observed in individuals with moderate and high fitness, supporting the view that fitness level may influence the magnitude of acute exercise effects rather than acting as a uniform determinant of cognitive performance. This interpretation is consistent with empirical work categorizing participants according to fitness level and examining fitness-by-condition effects on cognition31. Furthermore, physiological responses triggered by high-intensity exercise emerge rapidly but tend to decline shortly after exercise cessation. Consequently, the cognitive benefits associated with these responses may also be transient6,36, and in some cases cognitive performance may even deteriorate if exercise intensity or duration becomes excessive27.

Another important consideration is the nature of the cognitive task used to assess performance. Different cognitive tasks engage different neural networks35,37, and many studies examining exercise–cognition interactions have relied on relatively simple tasks that may not fully capture the cognitive demands encountered in real-world contexts such as military operations or competitive sports29,38. In this context, the go/no-go task is particularly useful because it allows simultaneous assessment of basic information processing speed and inhibitory control processes32, which are essential for rapid decision-making and sustained attention in operational environments.

Despite growing research interest in this field, studies directly comparing individuals with different fitness levels remain limited, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the moderating role of CRF in acute exercise–cognition interactions27,31. This limitation is particularly relevant in physically demanding contexts such as military operations, where personnel are frequently required to perform cognitively demanding tasks under conditions of intense physical exertion and accumulating physiological strain10,11. Under such conditions, understanding whether CRF modulates post-exercise vigilance and inhibitory control may have practical implications for cognitive readiness and operational performance2,9.

In this context, the go/no-go psychomotor vigilance task is particularly useful because it allows the simultaneous assessment of response speed and inhibitory control, two cognitive functions that are highly relevant in operational environments32. In addition, the acute effects of vigorous or exhaustive exercise on cognition appear to be heterogeneous, suggesting that fitness level may act as an important moderator rather than a uniform determinant of post-exercise performance6,25,27,30,31.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to examine whether cardiorespiratory fitness modulates vigilance performance following a single bout of exhaustive exercise in trained soldiers. Vigilance was assessed using a modified Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT) indexed by reaction time (RT), whereas inhibitory control was assessed through commission errors (CE) and omission errors (OE). Based on previous literature, we hypothesized that: (i) individuals with higher CRF would demonstrate better cognitive performance than those with lower CRF in both pre- and post-exercise conditions; (ii) acute exhaustive exercise would induce transient changes in vigilance performance; and (iii) although both groups might benefit from the acute physiological response to exercise, individuals with lower CRF would exhibit larger pre–post changes, potentially reflecting greater room for transient performance gains6,27,31,32.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-four male soldiers [age range: 25–40 years, mean (standard error) 32.24 (± 3.8) years, 178.7 (± 5.9) cm, 79.2 (± 8.1) kg, with 14.35 (± 3.6) years of rifle shooting experience] voluntarily participated in this study. All participants provided written informed consent before participation, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Valencia on 25/07/2014 (Ref: H1402563451425), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All volunteers were considered fit to participate based on their previous medical examination and Annual Military Physical Fitness Test (AMPFT) results. These data were used as inclusion criteria to ensure that participants could safely perform the maximal incremental treadmill test and as a preliminary screening measure for group allocation. Participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no physical, neurological, sensory, postural, or gait disorders, and no history of neuropsychological impairment. The sample was ethnically homogeneous, as all participants were Spanish soldiers, with only one participant reporting Arab ancestry.

All participants completed a maximal incremental treadmill test to exhaustion and were then categorized according to age-adjusted normative cardiorespiratory fitness standards provided in the ACSM Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription39 into two CRF groups: low-CRF and high-CRF (Table 1). For men aged 30–39 years, VO2peak values between 40.7 and 43.9 mL kg−1 min−1 correspond to the Fair category, whereas values between 48.3 and 51.7 mL kg−1 min−1 correspond to the Excellent category. Accordingly, the mean VO2 peak of the low-CRF group (40.74 ± 3.27 mL kg−1 min−1; range = 36.1–44.6) fell within the Fair range, whereas the mean VO2peak of the high-CRF group (48.7 ± 2.82 mL kg−1 min−1; range = 45.3–53.5) fell within the Excellent range, indicating a clear physiological distinction between groups. This classification ensured a meaningful physiological separation between groups while maintaining ecological validity within a trained military population.

All participants followed the same physical-military training program (6–10 h per week). The sample size (n = 34) was determined by an a priori power analysis performed using G*Power 3.1.9.6 (University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The calculation assumed a repeated-measures ANOVA design with one between-subject factor (CRF group) and two within-subject factors (time and block), an alpha level of 0.05, and power of 0.80. The primary outcome used for the calculation was reaction time (RT), and the assumed effect size was Cohen’s f = 0.28, based on previous work examining acute exercise effects on vigilance performance36 and on the magnitude of effects typically reported in meta-analytic work on acute exercise and cognition32. This trial was retrospectively registered at ISRCTN (ISRCTN18115608) on 05/08/2025.

Procedure

Participants were tested on two experimental days separated by 48 h: a familiarization day and an experimental day. They were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for 48 h before each testing session and to refrain from stimulants (e.g., caffeine) and alcohol for 24 h before testing.

Day 1: familiarization day

Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants were fitted with a Polar M400 heart rate monitor (Polar Electro Ltd., Kempele, Finland). They then completed a brief relaxation phase in the supine position on a stretcher. Next, participants performed a 1-min familiarization version of the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT) adapted to a Go/No-Go paradigm. Immediately afterwards, they completed the maximal incremental treadmill test to exhaustion used to determine VO2peak and subsequent group allocation.

Day 2: experimental day

Forty-eight hours after the familiarization day, participants returned to the laboratory and were again fitted with a Polar M400 heart rate monitor. After repeating the relaxation phase, baseline cognitive performance was assessed using the PVT Go/No-Go task. Once pre-exercise assessments were completed, participants performed the maximal incremental treadmill protocol to exhaustion. Immediately after exercise termination, they repeated the same cognitive task in the post-exercise condition. The transition interval was limited to the minimum time required to remove the Oxycon Mobile II system and assume the shooting position while holding the rifle (i.e., less than 1 min). Both the treadmill test and the PVT setup were performed in adjacent locations, thereby minimizing any recovery time before post-exercise testing. The study setup is shown in Fig. 1.

Measures and materials

PVT go/no-go task

Participants completed a modified Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT) adapted to a Go/No-Go paradigm in both pre- and post-exercise conditions. They were instructed to stand in a bipedal shooting position while holding an assault rifle (HK G36 C, Heckler & Koch GmbH, Oberndorf, Germany; weight: 2.82 kg; length: 72 cm) horizontally with both hands. Visual stimuli were generated and presented using E-Prime® software40 and projected onto a screen positioned at a distance of 4 m from the participant. In our version of the task, a black Gabor stimulus with a diameter of 0.30 m was presented against a light grey background, corresponding to an approximate visual angle of 4.20° × 4.20°. A push-button switch was mounted on the rifle trigger and connected to the computer via a USB cable, allowing trigger presses to be recorded as behavioral responses in E-Prime. This setup is also illustrated in Fig. 1.

The total duration of the task was 720 s, divided into four blocks of 180 s, allowing participants to respond to a sufficiently large number of stimuli in accordance with the recommendations for the psychomotor vigilance task41. No rest interval was provided between blocks, as the task was performed continuously and the block division was used for analytical purposes only. In each trial, a Gabor patch (4.20° × 4.20°) appeared centrally on the screen with an initial horizontal orientation. The target change occurred at randomized intervals between 2 and 10 s. Two stimulus conditions were used: a No-Go condition, in which the Gabor rotated 45° to one side and participants were instructed not to respond (25% of trials), and a Go condition, in which the Gabor rotated to the opposite side (75% of trials) and participants were instructed to press the trigger as quickly as possible with their dominant hand. The Go rotation side was counterbalanced between participants36.

Based on the task logs, participants completed an average of 110.62 ± 3.48 stimuli in the pre-exercise condition and 110.74 ± 4.15 stimuli in the post-exercise condition. This corresponded to 82.94 ± 2.62 and 83.03 ± 3.20 Go trials, and 27.68 ± 0.98 and 27.71 ± 1.06 No-Go trials, respectively.

To minimise learning-related influences, all participants completed a 1-min familiarization version of the task on Day 1 before the experimental session. In addition, the randomized timing of stimulus presentation reduced predictability, and the PVT has been reported to show negligible aptitude and practice effects42.

Oxycon mobile II

Gas exchange was measured using the second-generation Oxycon Mobile system (Oxycon Mobile II, Jaeger, Viasys Healthcare, Germany), a portable breath-by-breath gas analysis device previously shown to provide valid measurements of VO2peak43. The system consisted of a facemask with turbine, integrated O2 and CO2 sensors, and a pulse oximeter.

Before each test, after 15 min of acclimatization, ambient temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure were recorded. The turbine and gas analysers were then calibrated using a two-point calibration procedure. Immediately before each exercise test, the turbine was calibrated with a 3-L syringe (Hans Rudolph Inc., Kansas City, USA), and the gas analysers were calibrated using a certified gas mixture containing 5% CO2, 16% O2, and 79% N2 (Pangas, Dagmersellen, Switzerland). The device was warmed up for at least 60 min before testing.

The Oxycon Mobile II is a telemetric system composed of a measuring and transmitting unit and a fixed receiving unit. The measuring unit was attached to the participant’s back using a harness (total weight: 950 g). Gas exchange and ventilatory variables were recorded breath-by-breath and averaged over 15 s intervals. All measurements were performed by a trained investigator.

Cardiovascular test and maximal incremental protocol

Participants performed a maximal incremental treadmill test to exhaustion using a motorized treadmill (H/P Cosmos Quasar, H/P/COSMOS Sports & Medical, Nussdorf-Traunstein, Germany) and breath-by-breath gas analysis to determine VO2peak. The protocol was based on the Bruce graded exercise test (Bruce et al. 1973), with modifications adapted to the present study design. Specifically, the initial running speed was set at 5 km h−1, and speed was increased by 0.5 km h−1 every 30 s, while treadmill incline was kept constant at 1% throughout the test. Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was recorded at the end of the maximal incremental treadmill test using the Borg 6–20 scale44. The test was supervised by a trained investigator. Participants were continuously monitored using the portable gas analyser described above. VO2peak was defined as the highest oxygen uptake value reached during the test. The test was considered maximal when at least three of the following four criteria were met: (i) inability to continue increasing running speed, (ii) respiratory exchange ratio (RER) > 1.1, (iii) achievement of age-predicted maximal heart rate, and (iv) no further increase in oxygen uptake despite increasing running speed. The main physiological variables retained for analysis were VO2peak, final treadmill speed, time to exhaustion and peak RPE.

Data analysis

Three primary indicators of cognitive performance were computed: mean reaction time (RT), commission errors (CE), and omission errors (OE). RT was used as a measure of response speed, whereas CE and OE were used as indicators of response accuracy. RT values were calculated exclusively for correct responses, excluding trials with RTs shorter than 100 ms (anticipatory responses) or longer than 500 ms (classified as omission errors). A commission error was recorded when a participant pressed the trigger in response to a No-Go stimulus and was expressed as the absolute number of commission errors in the condition. An omission error was recorded when the participant failed to press the trigger following a Go stimulus or responded after more than 500 ms, and was expressed as the absolute number of omission errors in the condition.

To evaluate possible temporal variations in performance, the 720-s task was divided into four blocks of 180 s, and all performance variables were calculated for each block. In addition, task cost was calculated as the difference between pre-test and post-test RT for each block (pre-test RT − post-test RT), so that higher values indicated greater pre–post change in performance. Variable definitions and calculation criteria were based on previous work36,42.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.23 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). First, descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. The assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All cognitive performance variables showed an approximately normal distribution, whereas some physiological variables (i.e., VO2peak, final treadmill speed, and time to exhaustion) did not.

To examine the effects of CRF condition, time, and block on vigilance performance, a repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with block (1, 2, 3, and 4) and time (pre- and post-PVT) as within-subject factors and CRF condition (low vs. high CRF) as the between-subject factor. Separate analyses were performed for RT, CE, and OE. When significant effects were found, Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were applied. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

To examine task-related changes in response speed, a repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on RT cost across blocks 1–4, with CRF condition as the between-subject factor.

Group differences in physiological variables were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test. In addition, Spearman correlations were conducted to explore the association between RT cost and physiological performance variables (VO2peak, final treadmill speed, and time to exhaustion).

Finally, to complement the categorical CRF comparisons, additional linear regression analyses were conducted using VO2peak as a continuous predictor of pre–post changes in vigilance performance. For RT, the change score was calculated as pre-test RT − post-test RT. For CE and OE, pre–post change scores were calculated as post-test − pre-test. Additional regression analyses were also conducted for RT change in each task block. In addition, between-group differences in pre–post change scores (ΔRT, ΔCE, and ΔOE) were explored using Mann–Whitney U tests.

Results

Effects of cardiorespiratory fitness and acute exercise on vigilance performance

In the first analysis, multivariate contrasts revealed significant main effects of CRF condition (low vs. high CRF) (F2,31 = 18.7, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.55), time (pre vs. post PVT) (F2,31 = 8.08, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.35), and block (blocks 1–4) (F6,27 = 5.55, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.55) on the set of dependent variables. In addition, a significant CRF condition × time × block interaction was observed (F6,27 = 2.91, p = 0.026, partial η2 = 0.39), indicating that the pattern of change across the task differed according to fitness level and exercise condition.

Reaction time

Univariate analyses revealed significant effects of CRF condition (F1,32 = 38.58, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.55), time (F1,32 = 16.6, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.34), and block (F3,96 = 16.61, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.34) on RT. Overall, participants in the high CRF group exhibited faster reaction times than those in the low CRF group across all blocks. In addition, RT values were significantly lower in the post-exercise condition than in the pre-exercise condition, indicating faster responses following the maximal exercise protocol. In descriptive terms, RT decreased by 6.06 ms in the high-CRF group and by 16.94 ms in the low CRF group from pre- to post-exercise.

Across the duration of the task, RT progressively increased from the first to the later blocks, indicating slower responses as the vigilance task progressed. Pairwise comparisons illustrating these differences are presented in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Descriptive statistics for RT in the pre- and post-exercise conditions are presented in Table 2.

Accuracy

No significant main effects of CRF condition, time, or block were observed for the accuracy variables (commission errors and omission errors). Likewise, no significant interaction effects were found for these variables. However, descriptive inspection of the data indicated an increase in commission errors in the post-exercise condition, whereas omission errors remained relatively stable. Specifically, commission errors increased by 1.82 errors in the high CRF group and by 1.94 errors in the low CRF group, whereas omission errors changed by + 0.59 and − 0.41 errors, respectively. Descriptive statistics for commission and omission errors are presented in Table 2.

RT cost analysis

In the second analysis, a significant main effect of block on RT cost was observed (F3,30 = 4.36, p = 0.012, partial η2 = 0.30). No significant main effects of CRF condition or interaction effects were found. Pairwise comparisons showed a significant difference between the costs of block 1 and block 3, with block 3 showing a higher cost than block 1 (p < 0.01). These results suggest that the performance cost associated with the task was not constant across time, but increased in the later stages of the vigilance task. Correlations

Spearman correlations were conducted to explore the association between task cost and physiological performance variables. In the high CRF group, a significant positive correlation was observed between task cost in block 1 and VO2peak (rs = 0.57, p = 0.019), indicating that higher aerobic fitness was associated with higher task cost values in the initial block. No other significant correlations were found across the remaining blocks or within the low CRF group. Correlation coefficients for all variables are presented in Table 3.

Continuous VO2 peak analyses

To complement the categorical CRF comparisons, additional linear regression analyses were conducted treating VO2peak as a continuous predictor of pre–post changes in vigilance performance. For overall reaction time, VO2peak showed a negative association with RT improvement (pre-test RT − post-test RT), although this trend did not reach statistical significance (β = − 1.02, 95% CI [− 2.08, 0.03], p = 0.056, R2 = 0.11). No significant associations were found between VO2peak and changes in commission errors (β = − 0.001, 95% CI [− 0.11, 0.11], p = 0.990, R2 < 0.001) or omission errors (β = 0.10, 95% CI [− 0.13, 0.34], p = 0.385, R2 = 0.02).

At the block level, higher VO2peak was significantly associated with smaller RT improvement in block 1 (β = − 1.75, 95% CI [− 3.32, − 0.18], p = 0.030, R2 = 0.14) and block 3 (β = − 1.81, 95% CI [− 3.35, − 0.28], p = 0.022, R2 = 0.15). No significant associations were observed for block 2 (β = − 0.77, 95% CI [− 2.33, 0.79], p = 0.323, R2 = 0.03) or block 4 (β = 0.18, 95% CI [− 1.19, 1.56], p = 0.787, R2 = 0.002).

To complement these analyses, between-group differences in pre–post change scores were also examined using Mann–Whitney U tests. Descriptively, the low-CRF group showed a larger improvement in ΔRT than the high-CRF group (16.94 ± 18.58 ms vs. 6.06 ± 14.52 ms), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (U = 197.5, p = 0.070). No significant between-group differences were observed for ΔCE (U = 126.5, p = 0.537) or ΔOE (U = 132.0, p = 0.677). These complementary analyses were consistent with the main repeated-measures results.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and vigilance performance following a single bout of vigorous exercise. The main findings indicate that individuals with high CRF demonstrated better vigilance performance than individuals with low CRF both before and after the exercise bout. These findings are consistent with previous research showing that higher levels of aerobic fitness are associated with improved cognitive functioning, particularly in tasks involving attention and executive control1,4.

One possible explanation lies in the structural, functional, and neurophysiological adaptations associated with higher CRF. Previous studies have shown that individuals with higher cardiorespiratory fitness tend to exhibit greater cortical volume in frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, and that these structural differences correlate positively with cognitive performance15,16. These regions play a central role in attentional control and executive functioning, which are essential for vigilance tasks. In addition, regular physical activity has been linked to neurophysiological adaptations such as increased cerebral blood flow, synaptic plasticity, and neurogenesis, which may further support cognitive performance across the lifespan13,14. Together, these adaptations may help explain why individuals with higher aerobic fitness often display superior performance in cognitively demanding tasks such as vigilance.

The present findings also contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the cognitive effects of acute vigorous exercise. Previous studies have reported heterogeneous findings, with some showing improvements in cognitive performance following acute exercise, whereas others have reported no change or temporary impairments depending on exercise intensity and the timing of cognitive assessment6,25,38. One theoretical explanation for these inconsistencies is the transient hypofrontality hypothesis, which proposes that during vigorous exercise neural resources may be temporarily redirected from higher-order cognitive processes toward motor and physiological systems required to sustain physical effort21. Under such conditions, the cognitive outcome may depend partly on an individual’s physiological capacity to tolerate the metabolic demands of exercise. In the present study, reaction time improved after exercise in both CRF groups, suggesting that the exercise bout did not produce a generalized post-exercise cognitive impairment. This pattern may be partly explained by the high training status of the participants and by the fact that cognitive performance was assessed immediately after exercise, when arousal-related mechanisms may temporarily facilitate response speed.

From an applied perspective, these findings are particularly relevant in military operational contexts. Military personnel are often required to perform cognitively demanding tasks such as monitoring, threat detection, and rapid decision-making immediately after periods of intense physical exertion. Previous research suggests that higher levels of aerobic fitness may help maintain cognitive performance under such conditions2,9. Consequently, individuals with higher CRF may be better able to sustain vigilance and cognitive readiness following vigorous exercise in operationally demanding situations. This interpretation is also consistent with military-style studies showing that physical exertion and load carriage can impair vigilance and response control, particularly when cognitive demands must be sustained under accumulating physiological strain11,45.

Another possible interpretation relates to the ceiling hypothesis. Individuals with higher CRF may already operate close to their optimal level of cognitive performance, leaving less room for further improvement following acute exercise6,30. In contrast, individuals with lower CRF may display greater variability in cognitive responses because their baseline cognitive efficiency is lower. In military contexts, such variability could translate into greater fluctuations in vigilance following intense physical activity. This interpretation is consistent with the present findings, in which lower CRF participants exhibited greater pre–post changes despite poorer absolute vigilance performance. A similar pattern was reported by Wang et al.46, who found that following exhaustive exercise both higher-fit and lower-fit individuals responded faster, whereas the lower-fit group showed reduced information processing capacity, suggesting that aerobic fitness may act as a resilience factor under exhaustive cognitive-physical demands. One possible interpretation is that individuals with higher CRF may already operate closer to their optimal level of attentional performance, leaving less room for transient post-exercise gains. This pattern may also be consistent with previous work suggesting selective benefits of higher fitness for cognitive performance at rest47,48. Thus, the present findings may reflect greater baseline attentional efficiency in the high-CRF group together with greater scope for acute pre–post change in the low-CRF group.

When CRF was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher VO2peak values were associated with smaller pre–post improvements in reaction time in blocks 1 and 3. This result complements the group-based findings, showing that the high-CRF group maintained faster RT overall, whereas participants with lower CRF showed greater acute changes after exercise. One possible explanation is that individuals with higher aerobic fitness may already perform closer to their upper level, thus showing less room for transient improvement. Previous studies have also suggested that fitness level may shape the cognitive response to acute exercise, particularly under demanding conditions31,49. At the same time, broader exercise–cognition reviews indicate that the moderating role of fitness is not consistently observed across all paradigms and may depend on factors such as exercise intensity, task demands, and the timing of post-exercise assessment6,27,32. This may partly explain why such moderation appears more clearly under high-strain or exhaustive conditions, such as those examined in the present study.

It is also important to note that the RT cost analysis did not reveal significant differences between CRF groups across task blocks. However, the complementary analyses treating VO2peak as a continuous variable suggest that aerobic fitness may still modulate the magnitude of pre–post-performance changes, indicating that the influence of CRF may be better captured when fitness is considered along a continuous physiological spectrum rather than solely through categorical group comparisons. The results confirm previous studies, suggesting that maintaining the attention span for long periods of time requires considerable mental effort leading to a state of mental fatigue50, which has been evidenced in our study by performance cost increasing in the later stages of the vigilance task in both groups. It is also well established that mental fatigue impairs top-down attentional capacity51. The increase in RT cost in the later blocks may therefore reflect the combined influence of progressive attentional fatigue during sustained task performance and the transient recovery dynamics occurring immediately after exhaustive exercise.

Descriptive data also indicated an increase in commission errors in the post-exercise condition, whereas omission errors remained relatively stable. Although these changes were not statistically significant in the main inferential analyses, this pattern may suggest a speed–accuracy trade-off, whereby faster responses following exercise occurred at the expense of inhibitory accuracy. This interpretation is consistent with previous work showing that acute exercise may facilitate response speed and arousal without necessarily producing uniform improvements in executive control, particularly after strenuous or exhaustive 18,27,34,52-55 (McMorris and Graydon 2000). In operational contexts, this distinction is important because faster responses following intense exertion may not necessarily reflect a uniformly beneficial cognitive effect if response control is compromised.

Taken together, these findings suggest that cardiorespiratory fitness not only influences absolute vigilance performance but also modulates the magnitude and pattern of the acute cognitive response to exhaustive exercise. In our sample, participants with higher CRF maintained faster reaction times overall, whereas those with lower CRF exhibited larger acute changes following exercise. This pattern supports the view that fitness moderates the transient cognitive effects of acute exercise rather than exerting a uniform influence on post-exercise cognition6,30,31 .

This study has some limitations. First, the sample consisted exclusively of male soldiers, which limits the generalizability of the findings to women, civilians, or less-trained populations. Second, the use of a maximal incremental treadmill protocol to exhaustion provides ecological relevance for physically demanding contexts but does not allow direct comparison with individualized exercise prescriptions based on ventilatory thresholds or relative physiological intensity domains. Third, although participants completed a familiarization session and the Psychomotor Vigilance Task is known to show minimal practice effects, the absence of a non-exercise control condition limits the ability to fully exclude residual learning or repeated-testing influences42. Finally, future studies should investigate the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these transient post-exercise effects by combining behavioral outcomes with physiological and brain-based markers.

In conclusion, the present study contributes to the growing body of research examining the interaction between aerobic fitness and cognitive performance under physically demanding conditions. Individuals with higher CRF demonstrated better vigilance performance both before and after vigorous exercise, highlighting the importance of aerobic fitness for maintaining cognitive performance when physical demands are high. These results support the idea that aerobic fitness may play a critical role in sustaining cognitive readiness in operational environments such as military settings.
