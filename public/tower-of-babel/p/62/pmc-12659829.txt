Ocular manifestations of systemic lymphoma
Authors: Tingting Jiang, Xinyi Zhou, Junxiang Gu, Qing Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:A comprehensive understanding of the ocular presentations of systemic lymphoma (SL) is needed for early recognition and treatment to improve outcomes.Design:A retrospective study.Methods:The ocular manifestations of 17 patients with SL were retrospectively summarized.Results:Among 17 SL patients, 12 (21 eyes) presented with secondary vitreoretinal lymphoma (SVRL) and five (8 eyes) presented with viral retinitis. Among those with SVRL, 16 eyes (76.2%) exhibited only vitreoretinal involvement, three eyes (14.3%) had both choroid and vitreoretinal involvement, and two eyes (9.5%) showed lacrimal gland and vitreoretinal involvement. Ocular symptoms occurred at a median interval of 63 months (range: 4–220 months) after SL diagnosis. Ocular manifestations included keratic precipitates in 10 eyes (47.6%), anterior chamber cells in 10 eyes (47.6%), vitreous cells in 18 eyes (85.7%), subretinal infiltrates in six eyes (28.6%), optic nerve involvement in three eyes (14.3%), neovascular glaucoma in five eyes (23.8%), and ciliary body/choroidal involvement in three eyes (14.3%). Patients received intravitreal methotrexate injections and systemic chemotherapy. The 3-year survival rate was 47.5%. Viral retinitis manifested as vitritis, peripheral retinal focal necrosis, and retinal vasculitis, and among those five patients, the median interval from SL diagnosis to ocular involvement was 15 months (range: 9–28 months). These patients received antiviral therapy and intravitreal ganciclovir injections.Conclusions:SVRL and viral retinitis are two ocular manifestations of SL. Vitreous biopsy and PCR testing are valuable for distinguishing between them. Our findings support the importance of long-term ocular surveillance in patients with SL.

Various ocular manifestations, including non-granulomatous anterior uveitis, iris-ciliary body and choroidal infiltration, vitreoretinal involvement, and optic nerve infiltration, have been reported to occur in association with systemic lymphoma (SL).[12345678] Early detection and management of ocular manifestations of SL are critical for improving patient outcomes. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of ocular presentations in SL is essential for clinicians to be able to recognize and address potential complications early.

Traditionally, SL has been assumed to metastasize to ocular structures, including the iris, ciliary body, and choroid, through hematogenous spread. This process results in infiltration of these structures and can lead to choroidal lesions. However, more recent studies have demonstrated the notable occurrence of secondary vitreoretinal lymphoma (SVRL) in patients with SL.[18910] SVRL differs from primary VRL in that it represents metastatic involvement of the retina and vitreous driven by lymphoma cells originating from SL. However, the exact mechanism of SVRL remains unclear and requires further investigation. This will be particularly relevant for clinicians managing patients with SL, as early recognition of SVRL can lead to more effective and targeted interventions.

Additionally, because SL patients typically undergo chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which induce an immunocompromised state, these patients are susceptible to a range of opportunistic infections, including viral retinitis.[6] Therefore, clinicians must also remain vigilant in monitoring for ocular complications that occur in immunocompromised individuals.

In general, the ocular manifestations of SL are relatively understudied, and the increasing incidence of SVRL presents a growing challenge in clinical practice. Awareness of the potential for secondary involvement is crucial for the timely diagnosis and appropriate management of affected patients. Through the summary of ocular involvement in patients with SL, this study aimed to provide insights to inform clinical decision-making and improve patient care. The findings of the present study can serve as a reference for clinicians and provide guidance for the management of SL-related ocular complications. Early recognition and targeted therapies are expected to improve the prognosis.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of patients with SL treated in the Ophthalmology Department of the Hospital between March 2019 and August 2023. All patients were without HIV infection and had no history of other malignancies. The collected data included patient demographics (age and gender), as well as the site and pathological type of SL, follow-up duration, and mortality. The recorded results of ocular examinations included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), diagnostic procedures, treatments, and the results of slit-lamp microscopy examination, ocular ultrasonography, and fundus photography. The ethics committee of the hospital approved the study, which was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The diagnosis of SVRL is based on a comprehensive assessment, incorporating the patient’s medical history of SL, vitreous biopsy, and analysis of intraocular fluid cytokines and molecular markers. The diagnosis of viral retinitis relies on the clinical manifestations of patients and PCR detection of intraocular fluid.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics Software Version 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test. For normally distributed data, differences between two groups were assessed for statistical significance by using Student’s t-test. Nonparametric analysis was applied for non-normally distributed data. Differences with P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 17 patients with SL were included in this study (7 males and 10 females). Among them, 12 patients (21 eyes) presented with SVRL and five patients (8 eyes) presented with virus retinitis. The mean follow-up duration was 28.2 ± 13.1 months (range: 6–48 months).

The 12 cases with SVRL included five men and seven women. The average age of these patients was 57.5 years (range: 33–77 years), with nine of the 12 patients (75%) exceeding 50 years of age. Nine patients (75%) had bilateral ocular involvement. Of the 21 affected eyes, 16 eyes (76.2%) exhibited only vitreoretinal involvement, three eyes (14.3%) had both choroid and vitreoretinal involvement, and two eyes (9.5%) showed lacrimal gland and vitreoretinal involvement. Primary SL among these patients included nasal NKT lymphoma in five patients, mediastinal T-cell lymphoma in one patient, and diffuse large B-cell lymphomas in the other six patients, which originated in the lymph nodes (2 cases), testis (1 case), breast (1 case), stomach (1 case), and ethmoid sinus (1 case) [Table 1]. For all 12 patients, ocular symptoms occurred after the diagnosis of SL, with a median interval from diagnosis to symptom onset of 63 months (range: 4–220 months). The median time from B-cell lymphoma diagnosis to ocular involvement was 66.3 months (range: 4–190 months), and that from T-cell lymphoma diagnosis to ocular involvement was 24 months (range: 12–220 months).

Among the 12 cases with SVRL, ocular manifestations included keratic precipitates in 10 eyes (47.6%), anterior chamber cells in 10 eyes (47.6%), vitreous cells in 18 eyes (85.7%), subretinal infiltrates in six eyes (28.6%) [Fig. 1], optic nerve involvement in three eyes (14.3%), neovascular glaucoma in five eyes (23.8%), and ciliary body/choroidal involvement in three eyes (14.3%) [Fig. 2] [Table 2]. Intraocular fluid samples were obtained from all patients for interleukin-10 (IL-10)/IL-6 analysis and cytological examination. An elevated IL-10/IL-6 ratio (>1) was observed for five eyes. Lymphoma cells were identified in vitreous biopsy samples from four cases. Central nervous system lymphoma (CNSL) occurred in six patients (50%). In two of these cases, CNS involvement and ocular involvement were diagnosed simultaneously upon ocular examination. In two cases, intracranial involvement preceded ocular involvement, with an average interval of 21 ± 8.5 months, and in the other two cases, ocular involvement was detected prior to intracranial involvement, with an average interval of 6 ± 1.4 months.

All 12 patients with SVRL received intravitreal methotrexate (MTX) injections in combination with systemic chemotherapy. The treatment regimen for intravitreal MTX was twice a week in the induction period for 2 weeks, once a week in the consolidation period for 1 month, and once a month in the maintenance period. The duration of treatment was determined based on the concentration of interleukin-10 (IL-10) in the intraocular fluid. The abnormalities improved following intravitreal injections [Fig. 3]. Among these patients, the baseline BCVA was 1.04 ± 0.8 (LogMAR), and the final BCVA was 0.56 ± 0.64 (LogMAR) (P = 0.13). At the end of the follow-up period, five patients had died. Accordingly, the 3-year survival rate was 47.5%. The mean time from ocular involvement to death was 23.8 ± 17.5 months.

Viral retinitis associated with SL was observed in five patients (8 eyes), including two males and three females. Three cases had bilateral involvement. The average age of these patients was 46.8 years (range: 24–64 years). The primary lesions were located in the lungs, lymph nodes, spleen, and stomach, and comprised both B-cell and T-cell lymphomas. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of intraocular fluid revealed the presence of cytomegalovirus (CMV) in six eyes and Epstein–Barr virus in two eyes. The CD4 counts of all patients were within the normal range. Ocular manifestations among these patients included keratic precipitates in four eyes and vitreous cells in all eight eyes. Viral retinitis manifested as vitritis, peripheral retinal focal necrosis, and retinal vasculitis [Fig. 4]. The mean interval from SL diagnosis to ocular involvement among these patients was 15 ± 7.7 months (range: 9–28 months). All the patients received oral valganciclovir combined with intravitreal ganciclovir injections. Retinal findings showed a good response to treatment, and retinitis regressed. Among the patients with viral retinitis, the initial BCVA was 0.79 ± 0.77 (LogMAR), and the final BCVA was 0.64 ± 0.77 (LogMAR) (P = 0.7) [Table 3]. After 5–6 months of treatment, no ocular recurrence was observed.

Discussion

Originally, SL was known to predominantly disseminate through hematogenous routes, resulting in secondary involvement of the iris, ciliary body, and choroid.[1] However, recent research demonstrated that SL can also metastasize to the vitreous and retina, leading to SVRL, with an incidence ranging from 5% to 28%.[78910] The exact pathogenic mechanism of SVRL remains unclear, and its diagnosis requires comprehensive assessment, incorporating the patient’s medical history of SL, vitreous biopsy, and analysis of intraocular fluid cytokines and molecular markers.[1]

Mashayekhi et al.[10] found that the most common manifestation of SVRL is vitreous cell infiltration. In a study by Cao et al.,[1] all 12 cases of SVRL exhibited vitritis, with five cases showing retinal or subretinal infiltration. Consistent with previous studies, vitreous cell infiltration was also the most frequent presentation of SVRL in the present study. In another study, no significant differences in ocular manifestations were found between cases of SVRL and primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL).[10]

Apart from ocular involvement, CNS lesions can also occur in SL.[8] Mashayekhi et al.[10] reported that in PVRL, CNSL occurred before ocular symptoms in 83% of cases, whereas in SL, CNSL occurred after ocular symptoms in 78% of cases. This timing may be a distinguishing feature between PVRL and SVRL. However, in the present study, CNSL occurred before ocular symptoms in 32.8% of PVRL cases,[11] whereas CNSL occurred after the presentation of ocular symptoms in 16.7% of SL cases. These findings are inconsistent with those of Mashayekhi et al., and this discrepancy may be attributed to the limited sample size of the present study. Future studies with large sample sizes are needed.

In the present study, all cases of SVRL occurred after the diagnosis of SL. However, a previous study reported that 22% of SVRL cases presented ocular involvement before systemic involvement, with a relatively short interval between the different presentations.[10] Therefore, for patients with intraocular lymphoma, monitoring of other systems in addition to the CNS is necessary.

In the literature, the interval between SL diagnosis and ocular involvement varies greatly, ranging from 4 to 120 months.[1910] Compared with B-cell lymphoma, T-cell lymphoma exhibits stronger invasiveness and shorter intervals to ocular symptom development. Consistent with previous research, the average interval between systemic T-cell lymphoma diagnosis and ocular symptom development in our study was 24 months, whereas that between systemic B-cell lymphoma diagnosis and ocular symptom development was 66.3 months. Treatment for SVRL includes local therapy (intravitreal MTX/rituximab injection), systemic therapy (radiotherapy and chemotherapy), and combination therapy.[8910] All of the patients in the present study received intravitreal MTX injection along with systemic chemotherapy. A previous study reported a 3-year survival rate of 60% for SVRL.[9] In the present study, five patients died during follow-up, resulting in a 3-year survival rate of 47.5%. This indicates a poor prognosis for SVRL. Moreover, even after treatment, recurrence can occur in the eye, CNS, and throughout the body.[8]

CMV retinitis (CMVR) is the most common opportunistic infection in the eye and a common occurrence in HIV patients.[12] However, it can also occur in other immunocompromised states such as those resulting from radiotherapy, chemotherapy, use of immunosuppressive agents after organ transplantation and in other contexts, or genetic immunodeficiencies.[1314] Currently, only a few reports have described CMVR in SL.[151617181920] Research has indicated differences between CMVR in SL patients and HIV patients. First, in HIV patients with CMVR, the CD4 T-cell count is often less than 50 cells/mm3,[12] whereas in SL patients, the CD4 T-cell count is typically normal.[1820] In the present study, the CD4 T-cell counts of all patients were within the normal range. This is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies. The occurrence of CMVR in SL patients with a high CD4 T-cell count may be due to T-cell exhaustion and senescence, which are major dysfunctional states in the tumor microenvironment.[21] Second, the ocular manifestations differed between the two groups. Viral retinitis often presents with severe retinal hemorrhage and edema in HIV patients, with relatively mild anterior chamber and vitreous inflammation. Conversely, CMVR in SL tends to present as indolent retinitis, with less retinal hemorrhage and edema but more severe anterior chamber and vitreous inflammation compared with that in HIV patients.[161718] In the present study, SL with CMVR mainly manifested as vitritis, peripheral retinal focal necrosis, and retinal vasculitis.

For HIV patients, indications to stop antiviral treatment include a CD4 T-cell count greater than 100 cells/μL and regression of retinal lesions.[22] However, for SL patients with CMVR, due to the presence of a normal CD4 T-cell count, there is no accepted criterion for stopping therapy. Therefore, determination of the treatment duration for CMVR in these patients can present a dilemma. High-risk periods for CMVR recurrence in SL patients appear to be during the reintroduction of chemotherapy or upon cessation of anti-CMV therapy, typically occurring within 2 months of treatment cessation.[19] Currently, continuation of antiviral treatment is recommended for at least 3–6 months.[17] These patients require regular monitoring to detect and treat recurrence. In the present study, all patients received antiviral therapy for more than 5 months, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up.

The present study had several limitations, including its retrospective design and small sample size. A small sample size may affect statistical power. Studies with large sample sizes are needed to achieve in-depth investigations on ocular involvement of SL in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, this retrospective study describes two common ocular manifestations of SL: SVRL and viral retinitis. The patient groups with these two manifestations showed no significant differences in age, gender, onset time, ocular inflammation, or visual acuity. Thus, when a patient with SL presents with vision loss, both conditions should be considered. If the ocular manifestations are difficult to differentiate, vitreous biopsy and intraocular fluid PCR testing are valuable for distinguishing between the two conditions. The treatment of patients requires multidisciplinary cooperation. The long interval between systemic disease detection and the onset of ocular symptoms emphasizes the importance of performing thorough and long-term ocular surveillance as a part of systemic surveillance in all patients with SL.
