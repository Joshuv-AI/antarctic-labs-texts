Lactate-induced miR-7-5p/TRIM33 reprograms metabolic flux to suppress tumor growth and viral reactivation
Authors: Yifei Xu, Xuehua Min, Zihan Zhang, Yulin Zhang, Xiaoting Chen, Xinyu Wang, Caixia Zhu, Erle Robertson, Qunling Zhang, Yin Tong, Liming Zhang, Yuyan Wang, Qiliang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer metabolic reprogramming generates a hostile tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by lactate-driven acidosis, fostering tumor progression. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), an oncogenic herpesvirus linked to multiple malignancies, thrives under such stress, yet how it interacts with and contributes to an acidic TME remains poorly understood. Here, we identify miR-7-5p as a lactate-downregulated microRNA with diagnostic potential in plasma from patients with EBV-associated diffuse large B cell lymphoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. TRIM33, a previously uncharacterized lactate-inducible transcriptional regulator of glycolytic flux, is directly targeted by miR-7-5p. Integrative multi-omics analyses reveal that lactate promotes TRIM33 binding to specific motifs in the promoters of key glycolytic genes, including SLC16A1, which enhances their expression and reprograms metabolism. Importantly, the loss of TRIM33 accelerates tumor growth, enhances EBV reactivation, and confers resistance to lactate-transporter inhibitors. Our finding provides circulating miR-7-5p as a noninvasive biomarker and reveals TRIM33 as an integrator of lactate stress, metabolism, and viral oncogenesis, offering a novel dual-targeting therapeutic strategy.Keywords: lactate, miR-7-5p, TRIM33, glycolysis, EBV

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is shaped by profound metabolic alterations in cancer cells, leading to extracellular acidosis driven largely by lactate accumulation. This acidic milieu, a hallmark of the Warburg effect, not only supports rapid tumor growth but also promotes immune evasion and therapy resistance.1 Lactate is now appreciated as more than a metabolic by-product—it functions as a signaling molecule that influences multiple aspects of tumor biology.2 Clinically, high lactate levels correlate with aggressive diseases and poor outcomes, prompting interest in lactate metabolism as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target.3,4,5 Inhibitors of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) or monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) have shown efficacy in preclinical models, particularly when combined with conventional therapies.6,7,8,9

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are highly conserved, tissue-specific, small non-coding RNAs that maintain cellular homeostasis by negatively regulating gene expression.10 In mammalian cells, miRNAs direct the miRNA-induced silencing complex to partially complementary target sites, primarily located in the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of mRNAs, resulting in translational repression with or without mRNA degradation.11 miRNAs regulate vital biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and cellular metabolism, and have become promising diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic biomarkers in cancer.12 Recent studies have implied that miRNAs fine-tune gene expression in response to metabolic stress and have emerged as critical regulators of glycolysis and TME communication. For example, miRNAs such as miR-124 and miR-210-3p have been linked to lactate metabolism in solid tumors, highlighting their crucial roles in regulating glycolysis.13,14,15 However, the landscape of lactate-responsive miRNAs (LRMs) and their functional targets, particularly how they influence metabolic reprogramming and facilitate communication between tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironmental stress, in viral-associated malignancies remains to be investigated.

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a human γ-herpesvirus first identified in 1964,16 is causally linked to >250,000 cancers annually,17 including diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC),18 thrives in acidic niches. The virus establishes lifelong latency in B cells but can reactivate into lytic replication upon expression of key viral transactivators such as BZLF1 and BRLF1, leading to the production of infectious virions.19,20,21 During latency, EBV expresses several oncogenic proteins (e.g., EBNA1, LMP1) that drive survival and immune response evasion.20,22 DLBCL, the most common type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, frequently presents with widespread disease, making diagnosis and monitoring difficult. Current dependence on invasive biopsies is restricted by tumor heterogeneity, necrosis, and infection-related complications.23,24 In the EBV-immortalized B lymphoblastic cells, our previous work showed that lactate stabilizes EBV latency by suppressing viral miRNA transcription25 and triggers cleavage of mature tRNA at the anticodon loop (i.e., 5′-tRNAHis-GUG half) for promoting tumor cell growth,26 yet how host factors sense lactate stress to modulate both tumor metabolism and viral fate is unknown. Critically, current diagnostics for EBV+ lymphomas rely on invasive biopsies, underscoring the urgent need for plasma-based biomarkers reflecting real-time TME dynamics. As such, miRNA profiles and their targets, which reflect the metabolic state of EBV+ malignancies, especially in response to the acidic TME, are the key to enhance early detection and evaluation of treatment response.

In this study, we identified miR-7-5p as a lactate-downregulated miRNA with diagnostic potential in plasma from DLBCL and NPC patients. Mechanistically, TRIM33 (an E3 ligase involved in immunity and viral defense), modulating both host metabolism and viral gene expression, is directly targeted by miR-7-5p. Knockdown of TRIM33 accelerates tumor growth, enhances EBV reactivation, and confers resistance to lactate-transporter inhibitors. This pathway not only offers a non-invasive biomarker but also unveils dual-targeting strategies against EBV-associated cancers at both host metabolism and viral latency levels.

Results

Identification of lactate-associated miRNA signatures in DLBCL and NPC

Accumulating evidence links elevated systemic lactate levels to tumor aggressiveness.27 Due to their stability in circulation, miRNAs have emerged as promising biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and prognosis.28 To identify miRNAs reflective of metabolic stress in EBV-associated malignancies, we performed small RNA sequencing on B cell lines treated with PBS or lactate (Figure 1A, left). Distinct miRNA expression profiles were observed between EBV+ and EBV− cell lines under both basal and lactate-exposed conditions. Four miRNAs (miR-7-5p, miR-7974, miR-210-3p, and miR-4521) exhibited significant differential regulation (Figure 1A, right). Among these, miR-210-3p was selectively upregulated in EBV+ cells, whereas miR-4521 was downregulated specifically in EBV− cells. In contrast, miR-7-5p and miR-7974 were consistently downregulated upon lactate treatment irrespective of EBV status.

To determine whether these responses were driven by lactate anions or extracellular acidification, we exposed lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) to PBS, HCl (pH-matched control), lactic acid (LA), or sodium lactate. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) revealed that miR-7-5p and miR-210-3p responded more robustly to lactate than to low pH alone, whereas miR-7974 showed no clear preference (Figure 1B). We next analyzed plasma samples from patients with DLBCL. Circulating miR-7-5p levels exhibited a significant inverse correlation with plasma lactate concentrations (Figure 1C). Trends for miR-210-3p (upregulation) and miR-7974/miR-4521 (downregulation) aligned with in vitro observations.

To assess the broader relevance of this signature across EBV-driven cancers, we examined plasma from NPC patients. A linear relationship was observed between plasma lactate and all four miRNAs, most prominently for miR-7-5p (Figure 1D), suggesting a conserved lactate-responsive regulatory network in EBV-associated malignancies. Notably, the lactate sensitivity of miR-210-3p and miR-7974 was significantly higher in EBV+ individuals compared to EBV− controls (Figure 1E), implying that these miRNAs play a role in virus-modulated metabolic adaptation.

To validate their relevance in solid tumors, we performed in situ hybridization on nasopharyngeal biopsy tissues. miR-7-5p expression was inversely correlated with LDHA (lactate dehydrogenase A), a marker of glycolytic activity, whereas miR-210-3p showed a positive correlation (Figure 1F). Collectively, these findings support miR-7-5p and miR-210-3p as sensitive in vivo indicators of markers of lactate-driven metabolic reprogramming.

Lactate-mediated downregulation of miR-7-5p directly activates TRIM33-dependent tumor suppression

miRNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to the 3′ UTR of target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation or translational repression.29 They fine-tune critical cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis.30,31,32 To explore the functional roles of miR-7-5p and miR-210-3p, we used a pan-cancer miRNA-pathway database (http://cis.hku.hk/miRNACancerMAP) to map their regulatory networks. Both miRNAs were enriched in pathways associated with hypoxia, interleukin-2/STAT5 signaling, transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), and tumor necrosis factor α/nuclear factor κB activation (Figure 2A) and were linked to diverse biological functions across multiple cancer types (Figure 2B), underscoring their broad impact on tumor biology. Functional validation confirmed that lactate treatment consistently downregulated miR-7-5p and upregulated miR-210-3p in lymphoma, NPC, and cervical cancer cell lines (Figure 2C), reinforcing their conserved response to metabolic stress. These results position miR-7-5p and miR-210-3p as leading candidates for non-invasive monitoring of EBV-associated tumor progression and metabolic dysregulation, and potentially therapeutic targets.

Given that miRNA target specificity can be context dependent,33,34,35 we integrated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data with predictions from TargetScan, miRDB, and StarBase to identify genes preferentially regulated by miR-7-5p in a lactate-rich microenvironment. This approach yielded 28 high-confidence candidate targets (Figure 2D; Table S1). Efficient modulation of miR-7-5p levels was confirmed by qPCR following transfection with mimics or inhibitors (Figure 2E). Among these candidates, TRIM33 emerged as a top hit; its expression was enhanced by lactate treatment or miR-7-5p inhibition and suppressed by miR-7-5p overexpression (Figures 2F and S1A). Consistent with post-transcriptional regulation, increasing doses of miR-7-5p mimic progressively reduced TRIM33 protein levels, whereas the inhibitor increased them (Figure 2G). To validate direct targeting, we identified conserved miR-7-5p binding sites in the TRIM33 3′UTR and generated luciferase reporter constructs (Figure 2H, top). Co-transfection of the wild-type (WT) reporter with miR-7-5p mimic significantly reduced luciferase activity, an effect abolished by mutating the seed sequence (Figure 2H, bottom), confirming direct interaction.

Given the role of TRIM33 as a negative regulator of TGF-β1 signaling in fibrotic diseases,36 we examined whether the miR-7-5p-TRIM33 axis modulates TGF-β isoform secretion. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) revealed that miR-7-5p overexpression decreased TGF-β1 but increased TGF-β2 and TGF-β3 secretion, with opposite effects observed upon inhibition (Figure 2I). TRIM33 knockdown, confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure S1B), recapitulated this isoform switch; it elevated TGF-β2/3 without affecting TGF-β1 (Figure S1C), indicating that TRIM33 mediates isoform-specific regulation downstream of miR-7-5p. These findings establish TRIM33 as a direct and functionally relevant target of miR-7-5p under lactate-rich conditions through which the axis reshapes the TME in EBV-associated cancers.

To elucidate the functional role of TRIM33 under lactate stress, RNA-seq of TRIM33-knockdown cells revealed enrichment in pathways related to apoptosis and viral response (Figure S2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further implicated TRIM33 in cell proliferation, glycolysis, central carbon metabolism, and viral replication regulation (Figure S2B). Functionally, in LCL cells cultured under lactate-rich conditions, miR-7-5p overexpression reduced late apoptosis (Figure S2C) and enhanced proliferative capacity (Figure S2D), suggesting a pro-survival role in metabolically stressed environments. TRIM33 knockdown similarly decreased late apoptotic cells (Figure S2E), phenocopying the anti-apoptotic effect of miR-7-5p overexpression. Consistently, both colony formation (Figure 2J) and CCK-8 assays (Figure 2K) demonstrated that miR-7-5p overexpression significantly promoted cell proliferation. Notably, the pro-proliferative effect of miR-7-5p was partially abrogated by reintroducing a TRIM33 construct lacking the 3′ UTR (TRIM33-Δ3′UTR), confirming that TRIM33 acts as a growth suppressor targeted by miR-7-5p. Moreover, TRIM33 knockdown alone increased colony formation, further supporting its tumor-suppressive function. These data indicate that lactate-induced downregulation of miR-7-5p upregulates the expression of TRIM33, thereby activating the tumor-suppressive program of TRIM33. Thus, the lactate-miR-7-5p-TRIM33 axis critically regulates tumor cell proliferation.

Lactate-induced miR-7-5p downregulation activates TRIM33-mediated suppression of glycolysis

Aerobic glycolysis is a hallmark of aggressive cancers and correlates with poor prognosis.37 Given that lactate upregulates the transcription factor TRIM33 and our RNA-seq linked TRIM33 to central carbon metabolism, we sought to define its metabolic role. We employed an integrated multi-omics strategy combining transcriptomics (RNA-seq), targeted metabolomics of central carbon metabolites, and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) to map TRIM33 genomic binding dynamics under lactate stress (Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment confirmed the involvement of TRIM33 in carbon utilization and energy production (Figure 3B). Transcriptomic analysis of TRIM33-knockdown cells identified significant alterations in central carbon metabolism genes: BSG, SLC16A1, SLC2A3, HK2, and PGK1 were downregulated, while TIGAR, ALDOA, and DLAT were upregulated (Figure 3C), validated by qPCR (Figures 3D and S3A). Given that TRIM33 plays a bidirectional role in central carbon metabolism, it needs a direct assessment of glycolytic flux. We therefore measured mitochondrial ATP production (mitoATP), extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in both TRIM33-knockdown and miR-7-5p-overexpressing cells. The metabolic effects were partially reversed by restoring TRIM33 expression (Figures 3E and 3F), confirming that miR-7-5p acts through TRIM33 to modulate cellular energetics. The results showed that both miR-7-5p overexpression and TRIM33 knockdown reduced mitoATP (Figure 3G), increased ECAR, and decreased OCR (Figure 3H), indicative of enhanced glycolysis and impaired oxidative phosphorylation. Reduced ECAR and increased OCR upon TRIM33 expression further support its role in suppressing glycolysis and promoting oxidative metabolism. Thus, the miR-7-5p/TRIM33 axis reprograms metabolic flux in response to lactate.

TRIM33 redirects carbon flux from glycolysis to the TCA cycle to reshape energy metabolism

Metabolic intermediates serve as key indicators of cellular bioenergetic status.38 To define TRIM33-dependent metabolite changes, we performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics. Pathway enrichment confirmed the pivotal role of TRIM33 in central carbon metabolism (Figure 4A). Matchstick and radar plots revealed distinct intracellular metabolic profiles in TRIM33-knockdown cells under lactate conditions, with significant bidirectional shifts in key metabolites (Figures 4B and 4C). Analysis of culture supernatants showed that TRIM33 knockdown altered the secretion of multiple metabolites, including increased levels of urate, whereas α-ketoisovalerate was decreased (Figure 4D), demonstrating that TRIM33 shapes both intracellular and extracellular metabolic landscapes.

To map carbon flow across major glucose-utilizing pathways, we quantified metabolites in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). TRIM33 knockdown increased intracellular 6-phosphate gluconic acid and fructose-6-phosphate, early glycolytic key intermediates, while reduced lactate and NAD+, metabolites linked to redox balance and energy homeostasis (Figure 4E). Similar patterns were observed in both miR-7-5p-overexpressing and TRIM33-knockdown LCL cells (Figure S3B). We propose a model in which TRIM33 functions as a metabolic switch under lactate stress, redirecting carbon flux to support adaptive responses (Figure 4F). Metabolic pathway mapping revealed that TRIM33 loss enhanced glycolytic intermediates while depleting TCA cycle metabolites, indicating that TRIM33 suppresses glycolysis and promotes mitochondrial oxidation. To directly trace glucose fate, we performed U-13C-glucose flux analysis. TRIM33 knockdown increased glycolytic flux but decreased labeling in TCA cycle and PPP intermediates (Figures 4G and 4H), confirming TRIM33 as a negative regulator of aerobic glycolysis. Given that the Warburg effect supports rapid tumor proliferation, the TRIM33-driven shift toward TCA cycle dominance may compromise metabolic fitness in lactate-rich microenvironments, thereby contributing to its tumor-suppressive function.

TRIM33 drives SLC16A1 expression in a lactate-dependent manner

To determine whether TRIM33-mediated transcriptional regulation of metabolic genes requires lactate exposure, we performed ChIP-seq in LCL cells treated with PBS or lactate for 24 h (Figure 5A). Genome-wide analysis revealed TRIM33 binding across promoters, introns, exons, and intergenic regions, with approximately 20% of peaks localized to promoter regions (Figure 5B). A chromosomal heatmap illustrated non-uniform binding density, indicative of regional specificity (Figure 5C). De novo motif analysis identified three enriched DNA sequences under lactate stress: CTCCTGM, CTCCCRA, and TGTADT (Figure 5D). Notably, these motifs were highly enriched in the promoter of SLC16A1 (which encodes MCT1, a key lactate transporter), suggesting regulatory control (Figure 5E). Lactate exposure altered TRIM33 occupancy at transcription start sites (TSSs) of target genes, as evidenced by shifts in average ChIP-seq signal profiles (Figure 5F). Differential binding analysis (visualized via Integrative Genomics Viewer) and ChIP-qPCR confirmed altered TRIM33 recruitment to the promoters of BSG, SLC16A1, SLC2A3, HK2, PGK1, and TIGAR upon lactate treatment (Figures 5G and 5H), indicating lactate-dependent transcriptional rewiring.

Integrative analysis of RNA-seq and metabolomic profiles following TRIM33 knockdown revealed coordinated downregulation of SLC16A1 and associated metabolic perturbations, prompting focused investigation of the role of TRIM33 in regulating this lactate transporter. This hypothesis was supported by in vitro DNA pulldown assays, demonstrating interaction between TRIM33 and the identified promoter motifs (Figure 5I). Consistent with this regulatory role, knockdown of TRIM33 markedly reduced both mRNA and protein levels of MCT1 (Figure S3C). Critically, analysis of DLBCL patient tissues revealed a significant positive correlation between TRIM33 and SLC16A1 mRNA levels (Figure 5J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that lactate reprograms the global transcriptional landscape in a TRIM33-dependent manner, with SLC16A1 emerging as a central effector through which TRIM33 modulates lactate transport and metabolic adaptation.

TRIM33 promotes EBV latency and suppresses lytic reactivation

Viruses frequently hijack host metabolic pathways to support their life cycle. EBV infection is known to drive a metabolic shift in B cells from oxidative phosphorylation toward aerobic glycolysis.39 Consistent with this, 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate/sodium butyrate (TPA/NaBu [T/NB]) treatment, which could promote EBV lytic, significantly increased glucose consumption, lactate production, and total ATP levels, hallmarks of enhanced glycolytic flux (Figure 6A), while reducing mitoATP output, indicating mitochondrial suppression (Figure 6B). Furthermore, T/NB markedly elevated the ECAR and suppressed the OCR (Figure 6C), confirming impaired oxidative metabolism. Given that TRIM33 restrains glycolytic activity under lactate stress, we hypothesized that it may antagonize viral lytic reactivation, which is often favored under high-glycolysis conditions. To test this, we quantified EBV virion release by measuring viral DNA in culture supernatants. TRIM33-knockdown cells released significantly more EBV virion particles than shCtrl controls (Figure 6D), indicating that TRIM33 suppresses lytic replication. qPCR analysis of viral transcripts revealed that TRIM33 knockdown downregulated latent genes (EBNA1 and LMP1) and upregulated immediate-early lytic transactivators (BZLF1 and BRLF1) (Figure 6E). In contrast, lactate treatment (TRIM33 upregulation) enhanced EBNA1 and LMP1 expression while modestly suppressing BZLF1 and BRLF1, effects that were partially reversed by TRIM33 knockdown. This suggests that TRIM33 stabilizes viral latency. ChIP-qPCR confirmed that lactate increased TRIM33 occupancy at the promoters of EBNA1 and LMP1, while decreasing its binding to BZLF1 and BRLF1 (Figure 6F), supporting a role for TRIM33 in reinforcing latency and silencing lytic switch genes. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of lactate transporters using AZD3965, Su3118, and VB124 significantly reduced EBV copy numbers in lactate-stimulated cells (Figure 6G), linking metabolic suppression to antiviral effects. Moreover, immunohistochemical analysis of NPC tissues showed higher EBNA1 protein levels in TRIM33high tumors compared with TRIM33low tumors (Figure 6H), underscoring the clinical relevance of this regulatory axis.

TRIM33 loss and miR-7-5p overexpression drive tumor progression in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo impact of TRIM33 suppression, we established a xenograft tumor model by intraperitoneal injection of luciferase-expressing LCL cells into NOD/SCID mice (Figure 7A). Cells were transfected with miR-NC (control), miR-7-5p agomir, or miR-7-5p agomir together with a TRIM33-Δ3′UTR construct resistant to miR-7-5p-mediated repression. Agomir efficiency of miR-7-5p was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure S4A). Bioluminescence imaging revealed that miR-7-5p overexpression significantly increased tumor burden at both 1 and 3 weeks post-inoculation compared with controls (Figures 7B and 7C). Co-expression of TRIM33-Δ3′UTR partially but significantly attenuated this pro-tumorigenic effect. Consistently, miR-7-5p-overexpressing mice exhibited markedly reduced survival (Figures 7D and S4B), whereas TRIM33 restoration modestly but significantly prolonged survival, although not to control levels. Serum lactate levels were elevated in the miR-7-5p group, and this increase was partially reversed by TRIM33 re-expression (Figure 7E). Conversely, additional knockdown of TRIM33 in miR-7-5p-overexpressing cells further accelerated tumor growth in vivo (Figure 7F). Mice receiving miR-7-5p agomir or bearing TRIM33-knockdown tumors displayed significantly higher tumor burden (Figures 7G and 7H) and elevated plasma lactate levels (Figure 7I), suggesting that loss of TRIM33 reprograms tumor metabolism toward heightened glycolytic output. Both interventions significantly shortened overall survival (Figures 7J and S4C). Plasma biochemical profiling revealed disturbances in lipid and energy homeostasis (Figure 7K), highlighting the systemic metabolic consequences of dysregulating the miR-7-5p/TRIM33 axis during tumor progression.

TRIM33 loss confers resistance to lactate transporter inhibitors in vivo

To determine whether the anti-tumor efficacy of lactate transporter inhibitors depends on TRIM33, we repeated the xenograft experiment using LCL-Luc cells stably expressing shCtrl or shTRIM33 (Figure 8A), followed by treatment with AZD3965 (MCT1 inhibitor), Su3118 (dual MCT1/MCT4 inhibitor), or VB124 (MCT4 inhibitor), agents known to disrupt key lactate transport nodes in cancer metabolism.40 Treatment with AZD3965 significantly reduced plasma lactate levels (Figure 8B) and EBV viral titers (Figure 8C). However, TRIM33 knockdown markedly attenuated the anti-tumor effects of all three inhibitors, as demonstrated by representative bioluminescent images (Figures 8D and S4D) and longitudinal tumor flux quantification at baseline, week 1, and week 4 (Figure 8E). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed that TRIM33-deficient mice exhibited shorter survival despite treatment, indicating diminished therapeutic benefit (Figure 8F). Body weight remained stable across groups (Figure S4E), and comprehensive toxicity assessments revealed no overt adverse effects. Renal function markers (creatinine, uric acid, and urea nitrogen), liver enzymes, and lipid profiles were within normal ranges (Figure 8G), ruling out major organ toxicity. These data demonstrate that the anti-tumor activity of lactate transporter inhibitors is critically dependent on TRIM33, which leads to propose that TRIM33 sustains the functional reliance on MCT1 by maintaining their expression or metabolic context, and the loss of TRIM33 renders these transporters non-essential or bypassable, thereby conferring therapeutic resistance.

Discussion

Eukaryotic viruses are known to extensively reprogram the cellular metabolism of the host to support their replication, persistence, and dissemination. Common metabolic adaptations include enhanced aerobic glycolysis, increased fatty acid synthesis, and glutaminolysis processes that supply essential energy, biosynthetic intermediates, and redox balance for viral propagation.41 These virus-induced metabolic shifts offer attractive targets for developing novel antiviral strategies. In this study, we identify a critical regulatory pathway, the miR-7-5p/TRIM33 axis, that links lactate-driven metabolic stress with tumor suppression and maintenance of viral latency in EBV-related cancers. Lactate, a key component of the acidic TME, was found to suppress miR-7-5p expression, leading to TRIM33 upregulation. Elevated TRIM33 levels promote SLC16A1 (MCT1) transcription, enhancing lactate uptake and causing intracellular acidosis. This metabolic shift redirects energy production from glycolysis toward oxidative phosphorylation, ultimately inhibiting tumor cell proliferation. Notably, in the absence of TRIM33, cells rely more heavily on aerobic glycolysis, supporting unchecked tumor growth. These findings highlight the role of TRIM33 as a key mediator linking external metabolic signals with internal bioenergetic reprogramming.

Beyond its role in tumor metabolism, we uncovered a previously unrecognized function of TRIM33 in maintaining EBV latency. TRIM33 binds to promoters of critical viral genes such as EBNA1 and LMP1, reinforcing latent infection while suppressing lytic transactivators such as BZLF1 and BRLF1. Knockdown of TRIM33 leads to significant induction of EBV lytic replication and virion release, indicating that TRIM33 acts as a guardian of viral quiescence. Importantly, using pharmacological inhibitors AZD3965 (targeting MCT1), Su3118 (targeting both MCT1 and MCT4), and VB124 (targeting MCT4), we demonstrated substantial suppression of both tumor growth and EBV reactivation in vivo. These results suggest that the lactate-TRIM33 axis could simultaneously inhibit tumor progression and prevent EBV reactivation, a major risk factor for relapse in EBV-infected cancers.

Although miR-7-5p had been linked to pH-responsive pathways, such as the miR-7/TGF-β2 axis involved in acid-enhanced metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer, its specific role in sensing lactate within EBV-driven tumors remained unclear.42 Our work establishes miR-7-5p as an LRM in DLBCL and NPC, and identifies another lactate-regulated miRNA miR-210-3p. These correlations suggest that these miRNAs could serve as non-invasive biomarkers for assessing tumor metabolic activity. While the exact mechanism behind lactate-induced miRNA dysregulation is still under investigation, preliminary data suggest that lactate may interfere with miRNA biogenesis by affecting the expression or activity of key enzymes such as Drosha and Dicer.43 Further studies should explore how metabolic signals influence miRNA maturation and stability in a lactate-rich microenvironment.

Mechanistically, our study confirms TRIM33 as a functional target of miR-7-5p through bioinformatic prediction and experimental validation. TRIM33 significantly alters cellular energy metabolism, promoting oxidative phosphorylation. Metabolomic profiling reveals notable changes in central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis, TCA cycle intermediates, and amino acid pools, underscoring the broad regulatory roles of TRIM33 in cell metabolism. In particular, our focus on lactate dynamics highlights the need for deeper exploration into functional roles of other metabolites altered by TRIM33 deficiency to fully understand their contributions to tumorigenesis and viral control.

While it has been reported that the TRIM family proteins, including TRIM24, TRIM28, and TRIM33, can form complexes that suppress EBV lytic reactivation by repressing BZLF1 expression,44 whether TRIM33 influences EBV lytic replication through the modulation of host cell metabolism remains to be determined. In this study, we show that TRIM33 also modulates the host metabolic landscape to favor viral latency, particularly by regulating the expression of key viral genes such as EBNA1 and LMP1. This dual role in epigenetically repressing lytic genes and regulating metabolism positions TRIM33 as a crucial integrator of environmental signals and viral fate decisions.

Increasing evidence supports targeting cancer metabolism as a promising therapeutic approach. The Warburg effect makes many tumors reliant on glycolysis, making enzymes such as HK2, PFK1, and LDHA attractive therapeutic targets.45,46,47 However, clinical translation has faced challenges due to toxicity and compensatory pathways. In contrast, the selective MCT1 inhibitor AZD3965 disrupts lactate shuttling and shows promise in DLBCL and other metabolically vulnerable lymphoma.48 Similarly, agents of Su3118 (dual MCT1/MCT4 inhibitor) and VB124 (MCT4 inhibitor) effectively inhibited tumor growth and EBV replication in a TRIM33-dependent manner without evident systemic toxicity in our models, evidenced by stable body weight and normal renal and hepatic function. These findings support their further development as targeted therapies against metabolism in EBV-associated cancers. Although AZD3965 has been independently reported to exert anti-tumor effects in lymphomas, our study reveals TRIM33 to be a critical determinant of its therapeutic efficacy, suggesting that the functional integrity of the miR-7-5p/TRIM33 axis may serve as a predictive biomarker for patient stratification in future metabolic-targeted therapies.

In conclusion, our study describes a lactate-miR-7-5p-TRIM33-SLC16A1 signaling axis that integrates tumor metabolism with the control of the EBV life cycle. This pathway not only provides insight into how metabolic stress influences tumor formation and viral persistence but also opens new avenues for miRNA-based diagnostics and combination therapies targeting both tumor cells and latent virus. Future research will investigate the conservation of this axis across other virus-associated cancers and evaluate its utility in guiding precision medicine approaches.

Materials and methods

Human subjects

Plasma samples were collected from patients diagnosed with DLBCL at Shanghai Cancer Hospital, Fudan University. Clinical details are provided in Table S2. Additional plasma samples were obtained from patients with NPC at Jiangxi Cancer Hospital (Table S3). Nasopharyngoscopy tissue biopsies were also collected at Jiangxi Cancer Hospital, with patient ID and chemotherapy regimens summarized in Table S4. The use of residual blood and tissue specimens was approved by the Medical Ethics Committees of respective institutions, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional review board guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell lines

HEK293 (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], no. CRL-1537), HEK293T (ATCC, no. GDC187), C666-1 (ATCC, no. CRL-3209), HeLa (isolated from a female patient, ATCC), and C33-A (ATCC) cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 6% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invigentech), 1% penicillin (Sangon Biotech), and 1% streptomycin (BBI Life Sciences). EBV+ LCLs (LCL and LCL-luciferase), maintained as described previously,43 along with EBV+ B95.8 (ATCC, no. CRL-1612), EBV− Ramos (ATCC, no. CRL-1596), and EBV− BJAB (a gift from coauthor E.R.), were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 8% FBS, 1% penicillin, and 1% streptomycin. All cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Animal ethics and husbandry

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Chinese Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University. Female NOD/SCID mice (5 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. Mice were housed in pathogen-free micro-isolation cages (up to five per cage) with ad libitum access to sterile water and standard rodent chow throughout the study.

Antibodies and reagents

The primary antibodies used were as follows: mouse monoclonal anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (catalog no. 60004-1-lg, ProteinTech), anti-β-actin (catalog no. 66009-1-Ig, ProteinTech), anti-TRIM33 (catalog no. 55374-1-AP, ProteinTech), ChIP-grade anti-TRIM33 (catalog no. A301-060A, Bethyl Laboratories), anti-LDHA (catalog no. 2012S, Cell Signaling), anti-EBNA1 (catalog no. SC-81581, Santa Cruz), anti-MCT1 (catalog no. 20139-1-AP, ProteinTech). The secondary antibodies used were as follows: IRDye 800-conjugated anti-mouse (catalog no. 926-32210) and anti-rabbit (catalog no. 925-32211) (Li-COR Biosciences). Other reagents included protease inhibitors (PMSF, aprotinin, leupeptin, and pepstatin; Amresco); NP-40 (catalog no. 1108A23, Shanghai Fusheng Industrial); Coomassie blue (catalog no. A100472, Sangon Biotech); nitrocellulose membranes (catalog no. 10600002, Cytiva); LA (catalog no. 27714, Sigma-Aldrich); and sodium lactate (catalog no. BCCD7998, Sigma-Aldrich), TPA (catalog no. P8139, Sigma-Aldrich), and NaBu (catalog no. S5826, Sigma-Aldrich).

DNA constructs

The full-length TRIM33 was amplified by PCR using cDNA from LCL cells as template and cloned into pEGFP-C1 (via KpnI and BamHI), pA3F (BamHI and NotI), and pA3M (KpnI and NotI) vectors. Oligonucleotide sequences are listed in Table S5.

Cell transfection

HEK293 and HEK293T cells were transfected using polyethyleneimine (PEI, molecular weight 40,000; Yeasen Biotechnology) at a ratio of 1 μg DNA to 3 μL PEI (1 mg/mL). For miRNA modulation, miR-7-5p mimics, inhibitors, or negative control RNAs were delivered using GP-transfect-mate reagent (G04008, GenePharma) at a 1:1 (v/w) ratio of reagent to RNA. The miR-7-5p mimic, inhibitor, and agomir were synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma. The miR-7-5p agomir was chemically modified to enhance stability and cellular uptake, featuring 3′-cholesterol conjugation on the antisense strand, phosphorothioate linkages at both termini, and 2′-O-methoxyethyl modifications throughout the sequence.

Generation of stable cell lines

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting 293T cells with transfer plasmids (pLVX for overexpression or pLKO.1 for knockdown) and packaging plasmids (pRSV.REV-Kana, pMD2g-Kana, and pMDLg-pRRe-Kana) using PEI. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45-μm membranes, and used to infect LCL, LCL-luciferase, or HeLa cells. Infected cells were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin for 2 weeks. Knockdown or overexpression efficiency was validated by qPCR and immunoblotting. Two shRNAs targeting TRIM33 were designed using the Sigma online tool (https://www.sigmaaldrich.cn/), annealed, and cloned into the pLKO.1 at the AgeI and EcoRI sites. The sequences were shTRIM33-1, 5′-TCGATACCAAACTACTATAAA-3′ and shTRIM33-2, 5′-GTACTAGTTGTGAAGACAATG-3′. All oligonucleotides are detailed in Table S5.

RNA-seq and miRNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and quantified on a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Libraries were prepared and sequenced by Lianchuan Biological Technology. Raw reads were processed with Cutadapt to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38), transcripts were reconstructed, and gene expression levels were estimated. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with a false discovery rate <0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed using the HifairIII First Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (Yeason). qPCR was performed with Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeason) on a real-time PCR system. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal control. For miRNA detection, stem-loop RT-PCR was employed. Stem-loop primers were designed to match the 3′ end of each miRNA, enabling specific first-strand cDNA synthesis. qPCR was then performed using miRNA-specific forward primers and a universal reverse primer. Primer sequences are provided in Table S5.

Luciferase reporter assay

WT and mutant fragments of the TRIM33 3′ UTR were amplified from LCL cDNA and cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector downstream of the firefly luciferase gene using XbaI and FseI sites. Mutation were introduced to disrupt predicted miR-7-5p binding sites. HEK293 cells were co-transfected with reporter constructs, miR-7-5p mimics (or negative control), and a Renilla luciferase control plasmid (pRL-TK). After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using a FlexStation 3 microplate reader. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla activity for each sample.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged (14,500 rpm, 5 min, 4°C), and supernatants were mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. Protein was separated by electrophoresis, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye 800CW-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and imaged using an Odyssey CLx system.

ChIP

Cells were lysed in ChIP lysis buffer on ice for 30 min. Nuclei were pelleted, fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 30 min, and quenched with glycine. After washing, nuclei were resuspended in SDS lysis buffer and sonicated on ice to shear chromatin to 200- to 500-bp fragments. Lysates were diluted in ChIP dilution buffer, pre-cleared with Protein A/G agarose beads, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-TRIM33 antibodies or control immunoglobulin G (IgG). Immune complexes were captured with Protein A/G beads; washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and Tris-EDTA buffers; and eluted. Crosslinks were reversed at 65°C with 5 M NaCl, followed by RNase and Proteinase K treatment. DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction, precipitated with isopropanol and glycogen, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Purified DNA was analyzed for qPCR or subjected to ChIP-seq.

For ChIP-seq, reads were quality filtered, aligned to the human genome (hg38), and peak calling was performed using MACS2 (version 2.1.1). Differential binding analysis was conducted with diffReps (version 1.55.6). Peak annotation and functional enrichment of promoter-associated genes was performed using ChIPseeker (version 1.42.0) and clusterProfiler (version 4.12.6), respectively.

Cell Counting Kit-8 assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at appropriate densities. After 0, 24, 48, 60, and 72 h post-treatment, Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (10% of culture volume) was added, and plates were incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed relative to untreated controls.

Colony formation assay

Cells (1 × 104 or 3 × 103) were seeded in 10-cm dishes containing 10 mL DMEM with 6% FBS. Medium was replaced every 3 days. After 14 days, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, air dried, and scanned using a Canon scanner.

Apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate) and propidium iodide (PI) were added (1:50 dilution), and samples were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Apoptotic cells were analyzed by flow cytometer (BD FACS Calibur) and processed using FlowJo (version 10).

Measurement of lactate in plasma

Whole blood (5 mL) was collected in EDTA tubes, allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min. Plasma was diluted 5-fold in assay buffer and incubated at 37°C (CO2 free) for 30 min. The reaction was stopped with 0.5 M acetic acid, and absorbance at 490 nm was measured. The lactate concentration was calculated from a standard curve.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 20 min) to remove debris. Samples were added to ELISA plate wells, sealed, and incubated at 37°C for 50 min. After washing, the biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated for 50 min. After three additional washes, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate was added, and reactions were processed in the dark at 37°C. The reaction was stopped with the stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves after subtracting blank well values.

Real-time ATP, glycolysis, and mitochondrial stress assays

Seahorse XF sensor cartridges were hydrated overnight in XF Calibrant (Agilent) at 37°C in a CO2-free incubator. For metabolic assays, compounds were loaded as follows: ATP rate assay (port A: oligomycin; port B: rotenone/antimycin A); ECAR (glycolysis) assay (port A: oligomycin; port B: rotenone/antimycin A, port C: 2-deoxy-d-glucose); OCR (mitochondrial respiration) assay: (port A: oligomycin; port B: carbonylcyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone; port C: rotenone/antimycin A). Cells were seeded in XF96 plates, centrifuged briefly (200 × g, 1 min), and equilibrated in XF Base Medium (supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose, pH 7.4) before analysis on the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer. Data were analyzed using Wave Desktop Software (version 2.4.2).

Intracellular and extracellular metabolite profiling

Cells were harevested by centrifugation (200 × g, 5 min, room temperature), and supernatants were cleared (2,500 rpm, 10 min). Metabolites were extracted by adding ice-cold methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v/v) to cell pellets or supernatants. Internal standards were spiked in, and samples were sonicated (5 min, ice bath), incubated at −80°C for 30 min, and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4°C). Supernatants were transferred to glass vials for LC-MS analysis. Metabolites were separated on a Waters BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μM) using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography. The mobile phase proceeded as follows: A, 15 mM ammonium acetate, 0.3% ammonia in water; B, 15 mM ammonium acetate, 0.3% ammonia in 90% acetonitrile; flow rate: 0.3 mL/min; and column temperature: 40°C. Metabolites were identified and relatively quantified based on accurate mass and retention time.

Metabolic flux analysis by 13C-glucose tracing

Cells were incubated in glucose-free medium for 2 h, then switched to medium containing 13C-labeled glucose for 3.5 h. Suspension cells were harvested (1,500 rpm, 5 min), washed three times with cold PBS, and lysed in 2 mL pre-chilled 80% (v/v) methanol (−80°C) for 2 h or overnight. An additional 1 mL 80% methanol was used to rinse the tube. Combined lysate was transferred to a 1.5-mL tube and centrifuged (14,000 × g, 20 min) at 4°C; supernatants were dried under N2 or in a speed vacuum at room temperature without heating. Dried extracts were stored at −80°C until reconstitution for LC-MS analysis.

Metabolites were separated on a Synergi Hydro-RP column (2.0 × 100 mm, 2.5 μm) using a 25-min gradient (5%–90% methanol) with ion-pairing mobile phases (10 mM tributylamine/15 mM acetic acid in water vs. methanol), and the data were processed using TraceFinder 3.2.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 15 min. Sections were blocked with 5% normal serum for 1 h, incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Signals were developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with Harris hematoxylin. Slides were dehydrated, mounted, and imaged by light microscopy.

In situ hybridization

FFPE sections were treated with Proteinase K to enhance probe accessibility. Digoxigenin-labeled RNA probes were used: miR-7-5p, 5′-ACAACAAAATCACTAGTCTTCCA-3′ and miR-210-3p, 5′-TCAGCCGCTGTCACACGCACAG-3′. Probes were denatured and hybridized overnight at 37°C in a humidified chamber. After stringent wash, signals were detected with anti-digoxigenin-HRP and DAB. Expression was scored by H-score: H = P × I, where P = percentage of positive tumor cells (0–4 scale) and I = staining intensity (0–3 scale). Scores were categorized as low (0–4), moderate (6–8), or high (9–12).

DNA pull-down assay

Biotinylated double-stranded DNA probes corresponding to TRIM33-binding motifs were synthesized. Whole-cell lysates were pre-cleared with streptavidin magnetic beads (20 μL, 4°C, 2 h). Supernatants were incubated with a 1-μg biotinylated probe overnight at 4°C, followed by the addition of streptavidin beads for 3 h. Beads were washed three times with cold Tris-buffered saline, and bound proteins were eluted in 2× SDS-PAGE loading buffer. TRIM33 enrichment was detected by immunoblotting.

Tumor xenograft and drug treatment

Female NOD/SCID mice (5 weeks old) were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Each mouse was injected intraperitoneally with 1 × 107 cells in 200 μL PBS, resulting in diffuse tumor spread within the peritoneal cavity. Starting on day 2 post-inoculation, mice were administered sodium l-lactate (0.5 mg/g body weight) via intraperitoneal injection every other day. From day 10 onward, miR-7-5p agomir or a negative control (2 nmol per administration) was delivered intraperitoneally every 96 h. Tumor growth was monitored by in vivo bioluminescence image following intraperitoneal injection of d-luciferin (150 mg/kg). Bioluminescent signals were quantified as total flux (photons/second) within a defined region of interest.

In a separate therapeutic intervention study, mice were inoculated on day 0 with 1 × 107 cells in 200 μL PBS and treated with sodium l-lactate (0.5 mg/g, every other day). Beginning on day 13, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of either vehicle (PBS) or one of the following compounds (all at 5 mg/kg and all from MedChemExpress): AZD3965 (catalog no. HY-12750), Su3118 (catalog no. HY-N4115), or VB124 (catalog no. HY-139665).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses, including Student’s t test, ANOVA, Pearson’s correlation analysis, chi-square test, and Kaplan-Meier analysis, were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data from cell-based experiments represent at least three independent replicates unless otherwise indicated. Sample sizes were determined based on prior studies and published literature to ensure adequate statistical power. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t test. For experiments involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by the appropriate post hoc test was applied. Tumor growth curves were analyzed by two-way ANOVA to account for both treatment and time effects. Linear correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the differences between survival curves were evaluated by the log rank test. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
