Fatty acid regulation and phosphatidylethanolamine biosynthesis are important for hepatitis E virus replication
Authors: Kush K. Yadav, Menuka Bhandari, Hassan M. Mahsoub, Young Bin Park, Huong Van Le, Jae Mun Choi, Hannah M. Brown, Gireesh Rajashekara, Scott P. Kenney, Xiang-Jin Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHepatitis E virus (HEV) infection is associated with severe maternal and fetal complications during pregnancy, yet the underlying mechanisms driving this vulnerability remain poorly defined. We demonstrate that fatty acids and lipids play an important role in modulating HEV replication, that increasing fatty acid concentrations enhance HEV replication in susceptible cell cultures, and that the lipid levels are elevated in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits. Lipidomic profiling from infected cell culture and pregnant rabbits identified oleic acid and phosphatidylethanolamine as key lipid drivers for enhanced HEV replication. We further reveal that placental lactogen boosts HEV replication by increasing fatty acid availability. Together, these findings uncover a potential lipid-driven pathway underlying HEV-associated adverse clinical outcomes during pregnancy.

Hepatitis E virus (HEV) is a leading cause of acute viral hepatitis worldwide, although it also causes chronic hepatitis, particularly in immunocompromised individuals (1–3). HEV, a single-strand positive-sense RNA virus, is classified in the family of Hepeviridae which consists of two subfamilies, Orthohepevirinae and Parahepevirinae (4). The species Paslahepevirus balayani within the genus Paslahepevirus of the Orthohepevirinae subfamily comprises at least eight distinct genotypes (HEV-1 to HEV-8). HEV-1 and HEV-2 infect humans, whereas HEV-3 and HEV-4 are zoonotic infecting humans and several other animal species (5, 6). The genome of HEV contains three main open reading frames (ORF) (7, 8): ORF1 encodes a nonstructural polyprotein with putative functional domains, ORF2 encodes viral capsid protein, and ORF3 encodes a multifunctional protein that is essential for viral replication and virion release (9–11). An ORF4, which overlaps with ORF1, was reported in HEV-1 and also other strains (12, 13), and reportedly encodes a stress-induced protein that enhances viral replication (12). HEV excretes in feces of infected individuals as nonenveloped virions, although the virions in the circulating blood are quasi-enveloped with lipid membranes (14, 15).

HEV infection disproportionately impacts on pregnant women causing pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes, such as fulminant hepatic failure and maternal mortality, particularly in the third trimester (16–18). This gestation-associated enhancement of disease appears to be mainly associated with HEV-1. However, recent reports suggest that other HEV genotypes may also be associated with enhanced manifestation of diseases during pregnancy (19–21), and that HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits also displayed pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes (22–24).

The pregnancy-associated high mortality during HEV infection has garnered great interest since the phenomenon was first reported (25). Unfortunately, the underlying mechanism remains largely undefined and is thought to attribute to a complex interaction among viral, host, immunological, and hormonal factors (26). Additionally, ORF4 in some HEV-1 strains enhances HEV replication during times of endoplasmic reticulum stress such as during pregnancy (27, 28). More recently, strains of HEV have been shown to replicate in placental cells (29), placental trophoblast cells and stromal cells with HEV-1 replicating more efficiently in these cells (19, 30, 31), but the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

The HEV ORF1 reportedly contains a potential fatty acid–binding domain (FABD) within putative papain-like cysteine protease (PCP) domain (32). Although the functional relevance of this FABD and the potential role of free fatty acids (FFAs) in HEV replication remain largely unstudied, lipid metabolism may be an important yet overlooked host factor in HEV pathogenesis. This possibility is particularly compelling in the context of pregnancy, a physiological state characterized by elevations in circulating FFAs, especially during the third trimester when HEV-associated disease severity is more pronounced. Notably, HEV-infected humans (17, 26, 33), rabbits (24), and gerbils (34) all exhibit pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes. All these species have a hemochorial placenta in which maternal blood directly contacts fetal trophoblasts, and placental lactogen (PL) is produced by syncytiotrophoblast during parturition in gerbils, rabbits, and humans. In contrast, other HEV natural hosts such as pigs, which do not experience pregnancy-related mortality (35), possess a diffuse epitheliochorial placenta that maintains distinct maternal and fetal epithelial layers throughout gestation (36) and PL production. Primates uniquely synthesize PL from a gene duplicated within the GH locus (37), linking pregnancy specific endocrine signals to metabolic remodeling. These physiological differences may help explain species-specific HEV susceptibility but do not fully account for why only certain hemochorial hosts develop HEV-associated adverse outcomes during pregnancy.

Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) is a major phospholipid enriched in cytoplasmic membranes and viral replication organelles, where it contributes to membrane curvature, fluidity, and the formation of specialized viral replication compartments used by some positive-sense RNA viruses (38). Structurally, PE consists of a glycerol backbone esterified to two fatty acids and linked to an ethanolamine headgroup, making it highly responsive to cellular fatty acid availability and metabolic remodeling (39). Numerous RNA viruses rely on PE rich microenvironments to stabilize viral replication complexes, promote membrane invagination, and facilitate genome synthesis (40, 41). Since HEV-specific lipid remodeling and pregnancy itself drives increase in circulating fatty acids that feed directly into PE biosynthesis, understanding how PE abundance, composition, and membrane-shaping properties may influence HEV replication is important for defining potential metabolic dependencies of HEV.

In this study, we first demonstrated that fatty acids and lipids, such as oleic acid and PE, contribute to enhanced HEV replication during pregnancy. We also showed that an increase in PL hormonal-mediated fatty acid enhanced HEV replication. Mechanistically, we revealed that oleic acid directly promotes HEV replication in cell cultures, while knockout/knockdown of PE synthesis reduces HEV replication. We further show that pregnancy hormones capable of modulating fatty acid metabolism influence HEV replication in accordance with their ability to increase or decrease fatty acid availability. Collectively, our findings provide important mechanistic insights into how pregnancy-associated metabolic changes may potentially contribute to HEV-associated adverse outcomes.

Results

Placental Lactogen (PL) Enhances HEV Replication in Liver and Placental Cells In Vitro.

PL is produced by the syncytiotrophoblast in increasing quantities throughout parturition. Since natural HEV infection in pregnant women and experimental HEV infection in pregnant gerbils and rabbits resulted in adverse clinical outcomes (16, 24, 34), therefore it is logical to assess the potential effects of PL on HEV replication. We found that treatment of Huh-7 S10-3 cells with physiologically relevant levels of PL (12 ng/µL) led to an increase in detectable HEV-3 (strain P6) ORF2 antigen by IFA with neglectable cytotoxicity from PL treatment (Fig. 1 A–D). There are higher numbers of HEV ORF2-positive liver and placental cells in HEV-3-infected and PL-treated group than in HEV-3-infected and untreated group (Fig. 1D). Quantification of the HEV ORF2-expressing cells using IFA and flow cytometry revealed a statistically significant increase of 10% in the numbers of ORF2-positive cells when the infected cells were treated with PL (Fig. 1 A and B). HEV-3 RNA was significantly increased in PL-treated liver cells beginning on day 2 posttransfection and in the supernatant with a similar trend observed in JEG-3 cells (Fig. 1 E–H). The HEV-3 RNA on day 0 after transfection establishes the baseline of HEV RNA present in the samples. We further showed an enhanced viral replication utilizing HEV-1 and HEV-3 GFP replicons, pSKHEV2-EGFP (HEV-1), and pSHEV3-EGFP (HEV-3) (42). We observed significantly higher numbers of GFP signals in PL-treated Huh-7 S10-3 cells with both pSKHEV2-EGFP and pSHEV3-EGFP (Fig. 1 I and J), suggesting that the mechanism is potentially conserved between HEV-1 and HEV-3. We observed more pronounced enhancement of HEV-3 replication in liver cells (Fig. 1I) and HEV-1 replication in placental cells (Fig. 1J).

HEV Infection and PL Increase Free Fatty Acid Concentration in Cells.

A primary function for PL during gestation is to regulate maternal metabolism, ensuring adequate nutrient supply to the growing fetus by mobilizing glucose, amino acids, and lipids (43, 44). Therefore, we investigate whether PL alters cellular metabolism in a manner that would favor HEV replication. PL’s primary effector target is the prolactin (PRL) receptor but it can also bind to the growth hormone (GH) receptor with a weaker affinity (45). We first biochemically measure the host cell changes during HEV infection with and without PL treatment. We found that HEV-3 P6 infection significantly increased the levels of fatty acids by almost threefold in JEG-3 placental cells. A similar increase of fatty acids, though to a lesser extent and not statistically significant, was also observed in Huh-7 S10-3 liver cells (Fig. 2 A and B). Treatment of Huh-7 S10-3 cells and JEG-3 cells with PL [12 ng/µL] resulted in an increase in fatty acid concentrations over time which became statistically significant by day 4 posttreatment in JEG-3 cells and day 6 posttreatment in Huh-7 S10-3 cells, suggesting that both cell types are responsive to PL-mediated signaling (Fig. 2 C and D). HEV-3 infection of Huh-7 S10-3 cells and JEG-3 cells resulted in statistically significant increase in fatty acid concentration for at least 12 d before returning to normal levels by day 15 (JEG-3) and day 18 (Huh-7 S10-3) (Fig. 2 E and F).

To further validate the in vitro observation in JEG-3 and Huh7 S10-3 cells, we measured the fatty acid concentration in sera of HEV-3 (US-2 strain)-infected pigs collected from a previous study (46) and observed a significant increase in serum fatty acid concentrations at 7, 21, 28, and 35 d postinfection (Fig. 2G). We also found a strong correlation between HEV-3 RNA loads in rectal swabs (r = 0.96, P = 0.0018) and in serum (r = 0.8986, P = 0.0149) and free fatty acid levels in sera (Fig. 2H). There is no additive or synergistic effect in FA levels when the cells were treated with PL and also infected with HEV-3, even though the FA increase remained elevated and statistically significant over untreated cells (Fig. 2 A and B).

In Silico Docking Analysis Reveals High Binding Energy and Affinity of the Fatty Acid–Binding Domain (FABD) in HEV ORF1 to Oleic Acid.

A FABD within the PCP region was previously predicted in HEV-1 (strain Pakistan) ORF1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B) (32). To determine the potential role of the FABD in PL and fatty acid-mediated enhancement of HEV replication, we utilized the Pharmaco-Net platform (https://pharmaco-net.org/: Calici Co LTD, Cambridge, MA), an AI-based drug discovery platform based upon in silico protein interaction modeling, to assess the binding kinetics of oleic, linoleic, palmitic, and mycolipenic acids which were upregulated during PL treatment and HEV infection. Additionally, we also utilized 31 Methyl 24Z dotriaconten 32 olactone, which was downregulated during PL treatment and HEV infection, to compare binding affinities within the FABD (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Our AI-DOCK modeling analysis suggested a stronger binding affinity for oleic, heptadecanoic, palmitic, and linoleic acids ranging from −5.38 kcal/mol (heptadecanoic acid) to −5.89 kcal/mol (linoleic acid) while mycolipenic acid and 31 Methyl 24Z dotriaconten 32 olactone were less favorable to bind within the pocket with 0.98 kcal/mol and 185000000.0 kcal/mol, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S1). Each fatty acid is predicted to form distinct packing within the FABD, leading to prediction of differential hydrogen bonding within the pocket (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 G–I). Oleic acid was predicted to form hydrogen bonds with serine 168 (designated S683 in full-length HEV-1 ORF1 numbering), and glutamic acid 158 (designated E673) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), while Linoleic acid and Palmitic acid were predicted to form hydrogen bonds with glycine 159 and leucine 161 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E and F).

The FABD Is Present in Species P. balayani But Not in Species Avihepevirus or Rocahepevirus.

The FABD regions among HEV-1, HEV-2, HEV-3, and HEV-4 within the species P. balayani are relatively conserved with 50.6% amino acid sequence identities (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), whereas HEV-1 FABD region has only 6.2% amino acid sequence identities with that of Avihepevirus magniiecur and Rocahepevirus ratti (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). Amino acid residues S683 and E673, implicated in hydrogen bonding in the molecular modeling studies, are conserved among the HEV genotypes within species P. balayani (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). However, the serine and glutamic acid residues are not present in species A. magniiecur (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B) while species R. ratti has a serine and glutamic acid spaced 10 amino acids apart but offset by 3 AAs from the same motif in the HEV-1 sequence alignment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). In general, the full-length HEV ORF1 polyproteins from species A. magniiecur and R. ratti are shorter (1531 and 1636 amino acids, respectively) than that from species P. balayani (1,693 amino acids for HEV-1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). De novo structural modeling of HEV ORF1 utilizing Alphafold 3 predicts the presence of FABD structure only in P balayani strains whereas the structures of species A. magniiecur and R. ratti are predicted to be mainly unstructured regions within the polyprotein, suggesting that the FABD is likely unique to HEV within the species P balayani which comprises the majority of human-infecting strains of HEV. Utilizing iTASSER (47–49) with the HEV-1 ORF1.pdb file as the overlay constraint also produced a favorable model (C-score 0.37) with the heterologous amino acids corresponding to the FABD as disordered rather than structured as a FABD. Three less favorable predicted structures (C-scores −0.1, −1.51, and −3.95) produced ordered structures within the amino acids aligned to the FABD region (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D–F), however, none appeared to recapitulate the distinctive FABD structure found in species P balayani protein structures.

Lipidomic analyses were performed in HEV-3-P6-infected and uninfected Huh-7 S10-3 cells and a total of 1,463 lipids were detected. The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis showed the significantly different lipid profiles between HEV-3-infected versus uninfected Huh-7 S10-3 cells (Fig. 3A). In total, 74 lipids were significantly different in the HEV-3-infected versus uninfected group (P < 0.05, VIP score >1, and fold change ≥1.5 or ≤0.667): 19 increased lipids, and 55 decreased lipids, showing that the overall level of lipids decreased after HEV-3 infection (Fig. 3B). Among them, 13 PEs were downregulated, 3 FAs were also downregulated (Fig. 3C) including FA(18:1)-Oleic acid, FA(16:1)-Palmitoleic acid, and 3 FAs were upregulated including FA(18:0)-Stearic acid and FA (16:0)-Palmitic acid (Fig. 3D).

HEV-3ra Infection in Pregnant Rabbits Increases Fatty Acid Concentrations in Serum, Liver, and Placental Tissues.

To understand the potential role of lipids in pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes during HEV infection, we evaluated the fatty acid concentrations in serum, liver, and placental tissue samples collected from a previous study that reported a significant fetal loss of 23% in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits (24). Irrespective of pregnancy status, the fatty acid concentrations were increased during HEV-3ra infection (Fig. 4 A and B). We found that the serum fatty acid concentrations were increased on day 7, 14, 21, and 28 in both HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected pregnant rabbits, although the difference was statistically significant only on day 21 and 28 in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the fatty acid concentrations in the serum, liver, and placenta were the highest in the three rabbits (#5, #7, and #32) that were also tested positive for viral RNA in the placenta in the previous study (24). In addition, we found a similar trend in the serum fatty acid concentrations in HEV-3ra-infected nonpregnant rabbits when compared to uninfected nonpregnant rabbits, and there are significant differences on day 14, 21, and 28 in HEV-3ra-infected nonpregnant rabbits compared to uninfected nonpregnant rabbits.

We further studied the liver and placenta tissues to understand whether lipid accumulation is a potential strategy for HEV-associated pathogenesis in pregnant rabbits. We found high levels of lipid depots in the liver of HEV-infected pregnant rabbits when compared to the uninfected pregnant rabbits (Fig. 4C). Quantification of liver fatty acid concentrations supported the above findings with higher levels seen in HEV-infected pregnant rabbits (Fig. 4D). In placenta, qualitative differentiation was not possible between the HEV-infected pregnant rabbits and uninfected pregnant rabbits (Fig. 4E), although there is statistically significant difference in the quantification of placental fatty acid concentrations in HEV-infected pregnant rabbits when compared to the uninfected pregnant rabbits (Fig. 4F). ImageJ analysis to quantify the lipid depots in the liver and placenta of HEV-infected and uninfected pregnant rabbits demonstrates statistically significant differences only in the liver samples of HEV-infected pregnant rabbits compared to noninfected pregnant rabbits (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Targeted Lipidomic Profile Analyses in the Serum, Placenta, and Liver of HEV-3ra-Infected Pregnant Rabbits Reveal Upregulated Fatty Acids and PE’s.

To examine the effect of HEV infection on the host lipids, we performed targeted lipidomic profiling in the serum, liver, and placenta tissue samples of both HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected pregnant rabbits that were collected at 35 d postinoculation with HEV-3ra from a previous study (SI Appendix, Table S2). The abundance of lipids was significantly higher in the serum samples of the HEV-3ra-infected rabbits compared to the PBS mock-inoculated group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In sera, a total of 1,135 lipids belonging to six major lipid categories, including fatty acyls (FA), glycerolipids (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), prenol lipids (PR), sphingolipids (SP), and sterol lipids (ST), were detected in both groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Further in-depth bioinformatic analysis reveals that the lipids belong to 43 subclasses (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis revealed significantly different lipid profile in sera between HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected rabbits (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). In total, 174 lipids were significantly different in the sera of HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected groups (P < 0.05, VIP score >1, and fold change ≥1.5 or ≤0.667) with 145 increased lipids and 29 decreased lipids, showing that the overall level of lipids increased after HEV infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). We noticed that the level of lipid subclass PE (63/174) was one of the enriched lipids, with 58 of them upregulated and only five downregulated in HEV-3ra-infected group compared to PBS control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 G–I). Similarly, the level of the three FFAs was significantly different, with two of them being upregulated and one downregulated in HEV-3ra-infected group.

In the liver, the abundance of lipids was not significantly different between groups (Fig. 5A). A total of 1,325 lipids belonging to six major lipid categories were detected in both groups (Fig. 5B). Further analysis reveals that these lipids belong to 43 subclasses (Fig. 5 C and D). The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis showed a significantly different lipid profile in the livers between HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected rabbits (Fig. 5E). In total, 172 lipids were significantly different in the liver of HEV-3ra-infected and uninfected groups with 129 increased lipids and 43 decreased lipids, showing that the overall level of lipids increased after HEV-3ra infection (Fig. 5F). We further noticed that the level of lipid subclass PE (27/172) was one of the enriched lipids with 26 upregulated PEs and only one downregulated PE during HEV-3ra infection (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, 20 FFA’s were upregulated in liver samples infected with HEV-3ra, including FA(16:0)-Palmitic acid, FA(18:1)-Oleic acid and FA(18:2)-Linoleic acid (Fig. 5H). Additionally, while comparing the total upregulated PE’s in HEV-3ra-infected rabbits, nine PE’s were identical between serum and liver, fifteen were identical between liver and placenta and seventeen were identical between placenta and serum. Interestingly, out of all upregulated PE’s from serum, liver, and placenta, we found only five identical PE’s present in all three groups (Fig. 5I).

Overall, the abundance of lipids was significantly higher in the placenta of HEV-3ra-infected rabbits when compared to the PBS control group (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). A total of 1,334 lipids belonging to six major lipid categories were detected in both groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), and these lipids belong to 43 subclasses (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). Supplementation of Oleic Acid Enhances HEV Replication in Huh-7 Liver Cells.

To assess the effects of the fatty acids identified in this study during PL treatment in Huh-7 S10-3 cells and in the liver of HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits on HEV infection, selected commercially available fatty acids including palmitic acid (PA), oleic acid (OA), and linoleic acid (LA) were supplemented to Huh-7 S10-3 cells that were infected with HEV-3 P6. We found that LA had no effect on HEV-3 P6 replication, OA significantly increased the number of HEV-3-infected cells while PA reduced the number of HEV-3-infected cells (Fig. 6A). The stimulatory effect of OA was also evident in IFA staining (Fig. 6B). Additionally, treatment of cells with C75, a highly specific B-ketoacyl synthase fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitor, decreased the number of detectable HEV-3-infected cells (Fig. 6A). The amount of viral RNA copies in HEV-3-infected Huh-7 S10-3 cells significantly increased with OA treatment and significantly decreased with C75 treatment (Fig. 6C). We further confirmed the results by utilizing fluorescent-tagged HEV replicons, showing an increase in the mean fluorescence intensity for both HEV-1 and HEV-3 in the presence of supplemented OA (Fig. 6D). To determine the potential role of FASN in viral replication, the supernatants derived from C75-treated HEV-3 P6-infected Huh-7 S10-3 cells were used to infect HepG2/C3A cells (Fig. 6E). We found a significant decrease in infectious virus titer as evidenced by fewer IFA-positive cells when compared to the HepG2/C3A cells that were infected with supernatants from either OA-treated and HEV-3-infected Huh-7 S10-3 cells or only HEV-3-infected Huh-7 S10-3 cells (Fig. 6E).

PE Knockout (KO) or Knockdown (KD) in HepG2/C3A Cells Decreases HEV Replication.

From the lipidomic analyses, we found that PE’s are upregulated in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits. To further confirm the effects of PE on HEV replication, we KO/KD the phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (PISD) gene and phosphoethanolamine cytidylyltransferase (PCYT2) gene, which are essential for the PE synthesis in HepG2/C3A cells (Fig. 7A). Using CRISPR/cas9 gRNA, we knocked out the PISD/PCYT2 genes, which were expressed as GFP-positive cells. We utilized the FACS cell sorting system to isolate GFP-positive cells, either PISD KO or PCYT2 KO, and revive them (Fig. 7 B and C). We found that there is a significant decrease in the HEV-3 P6 luciferase activity with the PISD KO and siRNA PISD KD. While we observed a significant decrease with HEV-3 P6 luciferase activity with the siRNA PCYT2 KD, we did not observe a significant difference with the PCYT2 KO (Fig. 7D). In contrast, there is a significant decrease in the HEV-1 Sar55 luciferase activity with the PCYT2 KO and siRNA PCYT2 KD. In case of PISD KO, we did not see a significant difference, although a significant difference was observed in the luciferase activity with the siRNA PISD KD (Fig. 7E). We further utilized the PISD/PCYT2 KO cells and treated them with PISD/PCYT2 siRNA, but failed to detect any statistically significant differences between the groups (Fig. 7 F and G). Furthermore, we also performed a double KO (both PISD and PCYT2 genes) in HepG2/C3A cells, but failed to successfully revive the double KO cells.

Pregnancy Hormones Enhancing Lipolysis or Availability of Fatty Acids Increases HEV Replication.

Pregnancy hormones, such as progesterone which increases lipolysis, enhance HEV replication (50), while estrogen which inhibits lipolysis has apparently no significant effect on HEV replication (51). To determine whether pregnancy-associated hormones that broadly promote lipolysis can influence HEV replication, we evaluated the effects of progesterone, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), and prolactin on HEV replication in Huh-7 S10-3 cells. Following HEV-3 P6 RNA transfection, IFA staining of the transfected Huh7 cells showed an increase in HEV-positive cells in the progesterone and hCG treated groups, whereas estrogen and prolactin treatment resulted in fluorescence level comparable to HEV-3 P6 RNA transfection alone (Fig. 8A).

Consistent with these observations, HEV-3 RNA titers in the supernatant on days 3 and 6 were significantly elevated in progesterone treated and hCG treated cells relative to HEV-3 RNA transfection alone. In contrast, estrogen and prolactin did not alter viral RNA titers compared to the untreated HEV group. However, both hormones treated groups produced significantly lower HEV RNA titers than hCG treatment group (Fig. 8B). Analysis of the amount of intracellular HEV RNA on day 6 further confirmed an increased viral replication in progesterone treated and hCG treated cells, with no significant difference between estrogen, prolactin, and HEV RNA transfection alone. As with supernatants, intracellular HEV RNA levels in estrogen and prolactin treated cells were significantly lower than those in the hCG treated group (Fig. 8C).

To further assess the impact of hormonal treatment during active HEV infection, we infected Huh-7 S10-3 cells with HEV-3 P6. On day 3 postinfection, progesterone and hCG significantly increased viral RNA titers in the supernatants compared to HEV-3 infection alone, whereas estrogen and prolactin again showed no significant effect (Fig. 8D). Similar to the transfection model, estrogen-treated and prolactin-treated groups exhibited significantly lower viral RNA titers than hCG treated group. By day 6, progesterone no longer showed a difference from HEV infection alone, but hCG treatment produced significantly higher viral titers than progesterone. Estrogen and prolactin remained indistinguishable from HEV infection alone, yet both were significantly lower than hCG (Fig. 8D). Intracellular viral RNA quantification on day 6 mirrored these trends, estrogen, prolactin, and HEV infection alone groups showed no significant differences, whereas progesterone and hCG treatments resulted in an increase in the amounts of intracellular HEV RNA (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

HEV infection is associated with adverse clinical outcomes in pregnant women, particularly in the third trimester, yet the underlying mechanism remains largely unknown. Pregnancy-associated hormonal fluctuations reportedly impact HEV replication, but prior studies did not address the broader metabolic remodeling that complements gestational period changes (50–52). Many RNA viruses utilize host lipid pathways to support membrane rearrangements, replication complex formation, and immune evasion (53, 54). Studies investigating pregnancy driven lipid remodeling and HEV pathogenesis have not been described thus far. Through lipidomic approaches, this study provides evidence that hormone-driven lipid remodeling contributes to pregnancy-associated HEV pathogenesis.

The results from this study introduce a hormone–lipid–HEV axis, which demonstrates how pregnancy hormones are capable of modulating intracellular fatty acid abundance that impacts HEV replication and pathogenesis. Progesterone reportedly enhances HEV replication in human hepatoma cells whereas estrogen did not have significant effect, via modulation of cell-mediated immune response (50, 51). Progesterone is known to increase intracellular fatty acid synthesis and availability in the placenta (55) and liver (56), whereas estrogen decreases fatty acid concentrations (57, 58). This difference in lipid metabolism led us to speculate that the differential effects of progesterone and estrogen on lipid biosynthesis and regulation may alter HEV replication. By delineating the effects of pregnancy hormones on lipid remodeling, in this study we attempted to delineate the potential role of fatty acid availability in HEV pathogenesis.

By utilizing HEV infection culture systems and HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits recapitulating pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes, we showed that HEV infection increases lipid content, particularly OA and PE, leading to enhanced HEV replication. Mechanistically, we showed that HEV replication is enhanced by OA, while disruption of PE synthesis reduces viral loads, suggesting a lipid-dependent pathway that HEV favors during infection. Similar host lipid remodeling has been reported for several positive-sense RNA viruses including hepatitis C virus (59), dengue virus (60), and Zika virus (61). A recent study reported high HEV titers in the liver of gerbils when fed with a high fat diet compared to a low fat diet, supporting the theory that fatty acid abundance and availability enhances HEV replication (62). Interestingly, elevated viral titers in the liver did not significantly enhance liver pathology, implying that lipid availability itself is likely insufficient to drive HEV-associated disease severity. The results from the study supports the notion that severe disease manifestation requires more than simply enhanced lipids, rather hormonally driven lipid remodeling, which is highly abundant during pregnancy. In addition, elevated lipid levels in sera are reported in acute hepatitis E patients, suggesting that HEV induces lipid accumulation as a potential strategy for sustaining viral replication (63). In this study, we found similar elevation of serum fatty acids in pigs during acute HEV infection. Unique to the acute hepatitis E cases, PE levels were lower when compared to healthy humans (63). We found in this study a similar downregulation of PE in HEV-3 P6-infected cells on day 6. In contrast, in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits, we observed higher PE levels in serum, placenta, and liver tissues with five of the PE’s being identical in all three samples, suggesting a potential HEV-related disease severity dependency on PE. However, specific to gestation, hormones such as progesterone and PL intensify fatty acid synthesis to meet the demands of the growing fetus that could be a situational benefit synergizing viral lipid demands.

Additionally, in vitro and in vivo differences observed in this study may reflect temporal dynamics, cell type specificity, and infection stages rather than mechanistic contradiction. First, lipidomic analysis in other viral infections show that PE and related phospholipids are dynamically regulated, often remodeling at replication membranes followed by later compensatory upregulation in whole tissues as infection and host responses progresses (64). Second, our in vitro HEV infection system captures a homogeneous hepatocytes population under controlled conditions with the lipidomic analysis done at day 6 postinfection, whereas the liver tissues from infected pregnant rabbits represent a more complex tissue composition including hepatocytes, nonparenchyma liver cells, and stromal components each with distinct basal lipid metabolism and virus-induced remodeling. Similar complex cell heterogeneity is also present in placental tissues. Third, the in vivo samples in this study reflect a later, clinically relevant stage of infection and pregnancy, where systemic hormonal changes and inflammatory signals drive hepatic and placental phospholipid synthesis and remodeling, including increased PE content. Hence, in vitro PE downregulation may reflect localized utilization and remodeling at sites of virus replication in a simplified cellular context, whereas in vivo PE upregulation in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits may reflect the combined complex outcome of viral replication, host metabolic compensation, multiple hormonals signaling, and tissue heterogeneity. Therefore, the results from this study lend credence to the concept of hormone-driven lipid remodeling process contributing to HEV pathogenesis.

In this study we identified PL as a key hormonal driver that increases intracellular and extracellular fatty acids to enhance HEV replication, thereby offering a potential explanation for the trimester-specific adverse outcomes in HEV-infected pregnant women. PL production during pregnancy is seen exclusively in species containing hemochorial placentas, where maternal blood is in direct contact with fetal trophoblasts. Two of the few available animal models recapitulating HEV pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes, rabbits (24) and gerbils (65), both contain hemochorial placentas and also synthesize PL (66), thus suggesting potential physiological relevance of PL in HEV pathogenesis during pregnancy. Although PL has never been studied for its role in HEV replication, its serum concentration is known to progressively rise from early gestation throughout the end of term (67). PL is also known as a potent regulator of lipid metabolism, elevating fatty acid synthesis and availability for the growth of placenta and fetus (68). In this study, we showed that PL treatment elevates fatty acid abundance in human placental (JEG-3) and human hepatoma (Huh-7 S10-3) cells and enhances HEV replication, thus suggesting a potential role of PL in HEV pathogenesis.

The interplay between hormone, lipids, and HEV infection is further supported by our in vivo findings. Placenta and liver from HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits exhibited accumulation of lipid droplets (LD). Similar observations have also been described in gerbils where LD’s were found to be important for HEV replication (62). Mechanistically, HEV pORF3 is a central LD-biogenesis inducer and an essential virulence factor in vivo (62). Recent studies demonstrate that HEV pORF3 undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation to form condensates that are associated with the LD phospholipid monolayer. This upregulates cholesterol anabolic pathways promoting triacylglycerol and cholesterol ester synthesis leading to expansion of LD stores, and creates a permissive environment for virus replication (62). Extensive remodeling of host metabolism, including the TCA cycle, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), FA metabolism, and β-oxidation has been reported in HEV-infected hepG2/C3A-MAVS-knockdown cells (69). The findings also demonstrated increases in long-chain neutral lipids, LD abundance, and proinflammatory oxylipins, along with functional dependency on lipid-fueled OXPHOS rather than glycolysis for efficient HEV replication (69). These findings of HEV replication dependency on lipid remodeling align with the results of elevated fatty acids and PE in serum, liver, and placenta of HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits in this study. We showed that disruption of PE synthesis by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockouts of PISD and PCYT2 genes in HepG2/C3A cells significantly reduces replication of both HEV-1 and HEV-3, thus indicating an important role of PE-dependent membrane dynamics in the HEV life cycle.

The presence of a FABD within HEV ORF1 (32), supported by our findings in this study demonstrating in silico docking of OA and further validated by HEV replication enhancement in cell culture, gives credence for a potential link between host lipid availability and viral replication machinery. Although pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes are mainly associated with HEV-1 (70), there have been reports of pregnancy-associated severe complications due to HEV-4 infection (71). Under experimental conditions, HEV-3 also causes pregnancy-associated fetal loss in pregnant rabbits (24). HEV-1 exhibits approximately 58.5% amino acid sequence identity with HEV-3 and 62.9% identity with HEV-4 in the FABD, a relatively conserved region among different HEV genotypes. Our results from this study and the existing knowledge suggest a potential functional relevance of this FABD. Coupled with the differential effects we observed on HEV replication when susceptible cells were supplemented with fatty acids (OA, LA, PA), differential fatty acids occupying the FABD binding pockets may potentially alter global protein conformations that regulate HEV replication. We speculate that the fatty acid binding to the FABD may coordinate with zinc binding allowing the HEV PCP domain to function. A recent study reported the presence of six cysteine residues at the HEV PCP region that are critical for viral replication (72). It was demonstrated that when one of the cysteines was mutated to alanine, C563A (present at the FABD), HEV replication is decreased. In general, fatty acid binding to its domain causes rearrangement of amino acid side chains and strengthens the hydrogen bonding network (73) which we speculate in case of OA and LA. In case of palmitic acid, however, the disruption to the cysteine might have caused a decrease in viral replication. Taken together, the results from this study suggest a hormone–lipid–HEV axis in which pregnancy hormones, particularly PL, drive lipid remodeling that synergizes with HEV’s intrinsic lipid exploiting strategies.

Fatty acid synthase (FASN) drives fatty acid biosynthesis (74), and its ketoacyl synthase activity is inhibited by the compound C75. C75 treatment leads to rapid accumulation of malonyl-CoA, metabolic stress, and suppression of endogenous fatty acid production (75). In this study, we showed that inhibition of FASN with C75 significantly reduced HEV replication in Huh-7 S10-3 cells, indicating an important role of de novo fatty acid synthesis in HEV life cycle. FASN inhibition also reduces hepatitis C virus replication (76). A plethora of other viruses also require host FASN-dependent de novo fatty acid biosynthesis, including viruses from Coronaviridae, Flaviviridae, Herpesviridae, Picornaviridae, Retroviridae, and Togaviridae families (77). Importantly, flaviviruses such as HCV have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, suggesting that lipid-driven viral replication may be a shared vulnerability among different viruses. This raises the possibility that pregnancy-specific hormonal upregulation of fatty acids may uniquely potentiate replication of hepatotropic viruses, including HEV and HCV. Similarly, higher abundance of OA has also been reported in HCV-infected cells (59). Therefore, targeting lipid biosynthesis or lipid-virus interfaces may represent a potential broad antiviral strategy.

Although the results from this study suggested a lipid-mediated enhancement of HEV replication, future in-depth mutagenesis studies of the predicted FABD and in vitro and in vivo functional studies of mutants in the lipid-interacting residues of the FABD are necessary to definitively determine whether FABD and lipid interactions are required for virus replication and for the proviral effect of OA. Additionally, targeting the PL-growth hormone receptor axis and modulating PL levels, interfering with receptor engagement or attenuating downstream signaling, may reduce pregnancy-associated lipid surges and thereby potentially limit HEV replication in vivo. The predominantly in vitro nature of our mechanistic experiments is a limitation of the study. Although the cell culture systems provide controlled platforms to dissect lipid-dependent enhancement of HEV replication, they cannot fully recapitulate the complex hormonal, metabolic, and immunological environment of pregnancy in vivo. Furthermore, given the complexity of pregnancy-associated lipid remodeling and its potential to enhance HEV replication in vivo, future comprehensive studies using PL treatment and oleic acid supplementation in infected pregnant animals are warranted in order to more definitively determine whether hormonally induced or exogenously elevated lipid levels enhance viral replication, and to validate the physiological relevance of the lipid-dependent mechanism suggested from this study. Interestingly, serum profiles of normal pregnant women show a significant increase in the OA from the first trimester to the third trimester (78), which could be advantageous for HEV to enhance its replication causing pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes. However, the current lack of lipid profiling datasets from HEV-infected pregnant women represents a major limitation in the field for comparative analysis of our in vivo study in HEV-3ra-infected pregnant rabbits. Finally, pregnancy is accompanied not only by hormonal and lipid changes but also by a distinct immune and inflammatory milieu that can also enhance viral replication and contribute to tissue injury and fetal loss (24). Thus, to better understand the driving factors contributing to the hormone–lipid–HEV axis, future comprehensive studies are necessary to determine how metabolic and immune pathways crosstalk may contribute to HEV-associated adverse outcomes during pregnancy.

HEV-1 is mainly associated with disproportionately higher maternal mortality, while HEV-3 and HEV-4 are generally associated with milder diseases (70). However, pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes have been documented in other HEV genotypes, including confirmed cases associated with HEV-3 (79, 80) and HEV-4 (19). The proposed PL–lipid–HEV axis in this study represents one potential conserved mechanism across different HEV genotypes with differing pathogenic consequences due to additional genotype-specific viral and host factors. The HEV-3 system used in this study, both in vitro and in vivo, provides a tractable model to dissect lipid-dependent virus replication mechanisms. In addition to HEV-3, in this study we also utilized multiple HEV-1 in vitro systems, including HEV-1 GFP replicon, HEV-1 Gluc replicon, and HEV-1 HiBiT infectious clone systems, to demonstrate the lipid-dependent pathways in both HEV-1 and HEV-3. A major limitation currently in the field is the lack of a tractable HEV-1-specific pregnant animal model that can consistently reproduce pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes. We have shown that the FABD is relatively conserved across HEV-1, HEV-3, and HEV-4, thus supporting a potential shared dependency on lipid-driven virus replication, and that in silico substitution of key hydrophobic residues in the active sites of FABD sequences of HEV-3 and HEV-4 may alter the pocket depth (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), leading to differential binding potential between genotypes. While lipid remodeling as demonstrated in this study appears to provide a common replication advantage for HEV, the clinical phenotype may be the result of more complex interplay between specific lipid requirements and genotype-specific pathogenic traits. HEV-1 and HEV-2 possess stronger antagonists of type I/III interferon signaling (81), exhibit more efficient placental tropism (31), and uniquely express ORF4 (12) which may potentially synergize with the lipid rich metabolic state of late pregnancy to drive adverse disease outcomes. In contrast, HEV-3 and HEV-4 appear to be more sensitive to residual antiviral immunity and less efficient in placental replication (19, 29, 34), which may reflect their milder clinical phenotype. Additionally, OA increases lipid depots and induces a slower increase in mitochondrial fragmentation (82) to reduce MAVS signaling and antiviral response (83), allowing virus to maintain a more permissive metabolic environment for replication while avoiding antiviral defense (84). We showed in this study that HEV-3ra infection in pregnant rabbits has elevated levels of FFA’s and PE’s. Whether HEV-1 and HEV-2 exploit lipid metabolic availability and pathways more efficiently than HEV-3 and HEV-4 to impact HEV pathogenesis in pregnant women remains to be determined. Thus, the proposed hormone–lipid–HEV axis in this study may utilize a combination of host responses and viral elements to impact the disease outcomes during pregnancy.

Extensive metabolic rewiring has been suggested during HEV infection that includes upregulation of fatty acid synthesis, β-oxidation, and OXPHOS pathways (69) as a necessary component for meeting the demands of HEV replication. HEV infection reportedly increases long chain neutral lipids and lipid droplet abundance, further suggesting a reliance on lipid metabolism (62). The results in this study demonstrated that pregnancy hormones, particularly PL, enhance fatty acid availability and PE synthesis, thereby underscoring lipid requirements for HEV replication. Collectively, the results indicate hormonal lipid remodeling as an important factor in pregnancy-associated adverse outcomes during HEV infection.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement.

All virus studies were approved by Institutional Biosafety Committees from Virginia Tech (IBC approval #22-023) and from the Ohio State University (IBC #2016R00000082). The original study in rabbits from which convenient samples were used in this study was approved by the Virginia Tech Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC # 19-047) (24).

Cells.

A subclone of the Huh-7 cell line, Huh-7-S10-3 (85), was used for transfection with in vitro-transcribed HEV genomic RNAs to produce quasi-enveloped HEV virions. Human hepatoma cell line HepG2/C3A, isolated from human liver carcinoma [ATCC # HB-8065], was used for HEV infectivity assay as described previously (28). JEG-3, isolated from human placental carcinoma [ATCC #HTB-36], was made by serial cloning of BeWo cell. Cells were cultured and maintained as described previously (46, 86).

HEV Replicons and Reporter Assays.

To determine the HEV replication kinetics in the presence of fatty acid and in the absence of the lipids, HEV-1/HEV-3 green fluorescent proteins (GFP) replicons (87), HEV-3 P6 luciferase replicon, and HEV-1 Sar55(HiBiT) infectious reporter virus were used as described previously (34, 88, 89).

HEV-3ra Infection in Pregnant Rabbits.

The pregnant rabbit HEV infection study was reported previously (24), and convenient tissue samples from the previous study were utilized in the present study. In brief, a total of 32 HEV-negative, 6.5-month-old, NZW female rabbits (16 pregnant and 16 nonpregnant) were randomly divided into four groups of eight rabbits per group: control nonpregnant (PBS-NP), control pregnant (PBS-P), infected nonpregnant (HEV-NP), and infected pregnant (HEV-P) (24). In this present study, the serum, liver, and placental tissue samples from PBS-P and HEV-P groups were used for fatty acid assays, lipid staining as well as for targeted lipidomic analysis.
