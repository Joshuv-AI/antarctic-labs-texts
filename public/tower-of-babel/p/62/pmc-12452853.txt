Key messages

Osteoporosis is commonly linked to aging and hormonal changes, but recent research suggests that immune responses, particularly those triggered by viral infections, may also play a role. This study provides new insights by identifying a causal relationship between immune responses to Epstein-Barr virus, Human herpesvirus 6, and Varicella zoster virus and reduced bone mineral density. These findings open up potential therapeutic approaches targeting viral infections and could influence both clinical treatment strategies and public health policies regarding osteoporosis.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a prevalent systemic skeletal condition characterized by increased bone fragility across multiple anatomical sites, leading to a higher susceptibility to fractures. In advanced stages, osteoporosis can result in incapacitation and even mortality (Ensrud and Crandall, 2017; Miller, 2016). Osteoporosis is typically asymptomatic for many years and is often diagnosed only after a fragility fracture occurs. (Glaser and Kaplan, 1997). In older adults diagnosed with osteoporosis (T‑score ≤ −2.5), especially those with previous vertebral or hip fractures, clinical guidelines recommend initiating treatment—such as antiresorptive therapy and fall prevention measures—to reduce the risk of subsequent fractures. Given the etiologic focus of this work, we minimize clinical management details and instead emphasize immune pathways regulating bone remodeling that are pertinent to the present analyses.” (Lane et al., 2000; Black and Rosen, 2016). Traditionally, osteoporosis has been attributed to hormone deficiency/aging-induced bone remodeling dysfunction (primary osteoporosis), or as a secondary consequence of diseases/drugs (secondary osteoporosis). However, a more profound comprehension of the immune system reveals its significant involvement in various forms of osteoporosis (Zhang et al., 2022).In the context of postmenopausal osteoporosis, estrogen deficiency induces immune cell alterations that variably promote osteoclast activation (Föger-Samwald et al., 2020; Fischer and Haffner-Luntzer, 2022). Senile osteoporosis is characterized by age-related sustained low-level immune system activation, disrupting immune homeostasis and ultimately leading to bone loss (Khandelwal and Lane, 2023).

Virus-specific antibody titers reflect prior exposure and the intensity of humoral immune activation, and thus can serve as epidemiologic proxies for immune stimulation relevant to bone metabolism (Hjelm et al., 2006; Goulet and Atkins, 2020). Antibody-related immune responses are mediated by immunoglobulins (Ig). Rather than detailing antibody biochemistry, we leverage GWAS of antibody responses to use genetically proxied serologic phenotypes as instruments in Mendelian randomization (Napodano et al., 2023).The stability, specificity, and adaptability of antibody frameworks render protein-based antibodies as one of the most crucial biological therapies. Antibodies not only have the inherent ability to bind antigens and endogenous immune receptors, but they also show great potential for protein engineerin (Goulet and Atkins, 2020). In recent decades, the immune response has played a pivotal role in bone regeneration by initiating the corresponding process in the early stage of bone regeneration and creating an appropriate inflammatory microenvironment for this purpose. Furthermore, there is an intricate interaction between the immune system and bone metabolism (Chen et al., 2022).An imbalance in the immune system may contribute to inflammatory stimulation, which, in turn, may lead to a disruption in bone metabolism and trigger various pathological conditions, including osteoporosis. The activation of Th1 and Th17 subpopulations of helper T cells can induce the differentiation of bone resorptive cells, while regulatory T cells (Tregs) are responsible for immune suppression and can inhibit the differentiation of bone resorptive cells. Additionally, certain pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), may also contribute to an imbalance in bone metabolism by both triggering it and inhibiting it (Tanaka, 2019).

Over the past decade, there has been a substantial increase in the execution of large-scale GWAS, and MR has effectively identified numerous genetic variants associated with osteoporosis. These studies leverage genetic variants as a natural experimental designed to pinpoint risk factors linked to outcome variables (Larsson et al., 2023). MR serves as a valuable tool, particularly when randomized controlled trials are not feasible for examining causality, and observational studies yield biased associations due to confounding or reverse causality. The use of genetic variants as a test variable for exposure addresses these issues: the alleles of this exposure-related genetic variant are randomly assigned and remain unaffected by reverse causality (Sekula et al., 2016; Bowden and Holmes, 2019).The risk factors for osteoporosis typically exert their influence over an extended period of time, making MR studies preferable to traditional observational studies for investigating the causal effects of long-term lifestyle-related risk factors on osteoporosis (Zheng et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023). In this study, we prespecified Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), human herpesvirus-6 (HHV-6), and varicella-zoster virus (VZV) antibody phenotypes as primary exposures to test whether genetically proxied virus-specific immune responses are associated with osteoporosis/bone mineral density (BMD) using two-sample Mendelian randomization, and we triangulated these patterns in NHANES 2003–2004.

Material and methods

Study design

This study employed a two-sample MR analysis. Initially, instrumental variables (IVs) associated with 46 immune response traits were identified using summary statistics from GWAS. These IVs were then applied to an independent dataset to obtain unbiased estimates of the relationships between exposure and outcome. The analysis aimed to establish a causal link between immune responses and osteoporosis. For genetic variants to serve as effective IVs in MR analysis, they must meet three key criteria: first, they must have a robust and consistent association with the risk factor under study; second, they must be independent of any known or unknown confounding factors; and third, their influence on the outcome must be mediated solely through the risk factor, without affecting the outcome through any other direct pathways (Jansen et al., 2014)(Fig. 1).

Solid lines represent statistically significant relationships identified in the study, whereas dashed lines denote non-significant relationships, which are excluded from the final analysis.

Data sources

The data on antibody-mediated immune responses were sourced from a 2020 study by Guillaume et al., which investigated immune responses to various infectious disease pathogens(DOI:10.1093/ofid/ofaa450). This study utilized the UK Biobank cohort, encompassing case-control phenotypes for 8735 individuals and incorporating up to 10,000 serological measurements of infectious diseases, along with whole-genome genotyping. The study identified 46 phenotypes based on data from 13 different infection sources, which included 15 case-control seropositive phenotypes and 31 quantitative titration phenotypes. The analysis confirmed the presence of multiple genetic variants associated with 13 distinct infections, many of which are biologically plausible and could serve as potential therapeutic targets or candidates for further biological research. These findings provide important insights that can guide future scientific exploration and the development of new therapeutic strategies, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1, which describes the 46 antibody-mediated immune responses.

To minimize selection bias, we selected three osteoporosis-related traits from the UK IEU database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) and the Finnish FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results). These databases contain a wide range of genomic data, covering various osteoporosis-related phenotypes. We specifically selected these traits to ensure their representativeness and to accurately reflect the potential causal relationship between immune responses and bone metabolism. The specific phenotypes and outcome details are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

To ensure the representativeness and reliability of the data, we used data from the 2003–2004 cycle of the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey). NHANES is a long-running program designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. It collects a broad range of health, nutrition, and demographic information through interviews and physical examinations. Specifically, this study focused on individuals with confirmed cases of EB Virus, Varicella, and Shingles, and extracted data on viral infection statuses and bone mineral density for detailed statistical analysis. Given the different impacts of each virus on the immune system, we specifically focused on these three viruses' potential role in immune-mediated osteoporosis.

We categorized participants into standard adult age groups—young adults (<45 years), middle-aged adults (45–64 years), and older adults (≥65 years)—consistent with common definitions in developmental and gerontological research. This classification is based on commonly used standards to ensure that we could more precisely analyze the impact of viral infections on bone mineral density across different age groups. Specifically, the elderly cohort had a relatively smaller sample size, which may influence the statistical power of the analysis for this group. We have acknowledged this in the results and conducted power calculations to ensure the reliability of the elderly cohort data.

Genetic instrumental variable selection

We first selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that were strongly and independently associated with both the exposure and outcome to serve as instrumental variables (IVs). Generally, SNPs are chosen based on a strict threshold (P < 5 × 10^−8), but we relaxed this to P < 5 × 10^−5 when necessary to ensure we had enough relevant IVs. We then applied linkage disequilibrium-based pruning to exclude SNPs that were in linkage disequilibrium (r^2 > 0.01) within a window size of 10,000 kb. Additionally, we aligned the exposure and outcome datasets by removing palindromic SNPs with allele frequencies close to 0.5 to avoid potential ambiguities.

We evaluated the strength of the instrumental variables (IVs) in the MR analysis using the F-statistic., which helps ensure the reliability of our causal estimates. Typically, an F-statistic value above 10 is recommended to avoid biases that could result from weak instruments. This metric indicates how well the IVs are associated with the exposure under study. The F-statistic is calculated using the formula: F = R^2 × [(N-1-k) / k] × (1-R^2) (Butler-Laporte et al., 2020; Burgess and Thompson, 2011), where R^2 represents the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by the IVs, N is the sample size, and k is the number of SNPs used. Additionally, we used minor allele frequency (MAF) and beta (β) values to assess the effect sizes of SNPs on the exposure.

To improve the accuracy of our results, we applied a statistical adjustment known as FDR correction, which helps control for multiple comparisons and reduce the likelihood of false-positive results (Glickman et al., 2014; Curtin and Schulz, 1998).

MR analysis

In our MR analyses, we primarily used the IVW method to explore the causal relationship between antibody immune responses and osteoporosis. This method is highly regarded for its accuracy when key assumptions are met. To strengthen our results, we also applied other complementary methods, including the weighted median method, simple mode, weighted mode, and MR-Egger regression, which help to ensure that our findings are robust and reliable (Slob and Burgess, 2020; Bowden et al., 2015; J Bowden et al., 2016; Burgess and Thompson, 2017).

The weighted median method provides robust causal estimates even when up to 50 % of the IVs are invalid or affected by pleiotropy. This makes it a powerful tool in scenarios where some IVs may not fully meet the assumptions required for the IVW method (Hartwig et al., 2017). The simple mode and weighted mode methods provide alternative approaches for estimating causal effects, offering further sensitivity to the results. MR-Egger regression is used to detect and adjust for pleiotropy, helping to ensure that the causal estimates are not biased by horizontal pleiotropy (Verbanck et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

To ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted an MR-Egger regression analysis (Greco et al., 2015). MR-Egger is particularly useful for detecting and adjusting for pleiotropy, where IVs may influence the outcome through pathways other than the exposure of interest. The presence of pleiotropy can undermine the validity of the causal relationship between exposure and outcome.

Additionally, we performed tests for heterogeneity, including the Q test and Cochran’s Q test, to assess the consistency of the IVs. We also applied the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis within the MR-PRESSO framework to identify and exclude results influenced by multicollinearity and outliers. This approach helps to ensure that our findings are not driven by any single IV or by significant heterogeneity within the data (Srivastava and Deal, 2002).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2, with Mendelian randomization performed using the "TwoSampleMR" package. Data visualization was carried out using the R graphics library, ensuring that the presentation of results was both clear and accurate.

Results

Preliminary results of Mendelian analysis

Initially, we performed five Mendelian randomization analyses on 46 antibody immune responses in relation to three osteoporosis outcomes. Afterward, we aggregated the results across these three osteoporosis outcomes to identify potential causal relationships between specific antibodies and osteoporosis. Our analysis revealed causal associations involving three specific antibodies: Epstein-Barr virus EA-D antibody levels, Human herpesvirus 6 IE1A antibody levels, and Varicella zoster virus glycoproteins E and I antibody levels (Fig. 2).

Blue shading indicates low P values, signifying stronger evidence for a causal relationship, while red shading represents high P values, indicating weaker evidence. Red boxes highlight antibody immune responses that are causally associated with all three types of osteoporosis studied, underscoring their potential as targets for therapeutic intervention.

The causal relationship between the three antibody immune responses and osteoporosis was further verified

To further assess the robustness of the associations between antibody immune responses and osteoporosis, we conducted additional MR analyses on the antibody immune responses identified in the initial investigation across three subtypes of osteoporosis(Table 1). For the ukb-b-177 outcome, we observed a causal relationship with 11 antibody immune responses (Fig. 3A). For the Osteoporosis (ukb-b-12,141) outcome, a causal relationship was identified with 8 antibody immune responses (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, for the finn-b-M13_OSTEOPOROSIS outcome, we found causal relationships with 5 antibody immune responses (Fig. 3C).

Sensitivity analysis

While the IVW method is highly effective in establishing causality between exposure and disease outcomes, it is known to be vulnerable to bias from weak instruments. To further evaluate the robustness of our causal inferences, we conducted additional sensitivity and multiple-effect analyses. We employed three distinct approaches to assess sensitivity and detect potential multi-effect issues, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Our findings revealed that for Human herpesvirus 6 IE1A antibody levels and Varicella zoster virus glycoproteins E and I antibody levels, the P-value for the pleiotropy test related to osteoporosis (Osteoporosis (ukb-b-12,141) ) was <0.05. This suggests the presence of pleiotropy in this specific case. However, for all other analyses, the P-values were greater than 0.05, indicating no significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy in the remaining results.

Impact of viral infections on bone density: nhanes 2003–2004 data analysis

In the in-depth analysis of NHANES 2003–2004 data, we found a significant association between viral infections and bone mineral density in specific populations.This study analyzed the impact of viral infection status on bone mineral density (BMD). The results showed that the presence of antibodies against Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and varicella virus was significantly associated with changes in bone mineral density. Specifically, among young men, individuals infected with EBV had lower BMD in both the lumbar vertebrae and pelvis than those uninfected, and the differences were statistically significant (p values were 0.028 and 0.010, respectively). Among middle-aged women, individuals infected with varicella virus antibodies had lower BMD levels in multiple bone sites (such as the left arm, left leg, right arm, right leg, ribs, thoracic vertebrae, etc.) than uninfected individuals, and the differences were statistically significant (p values ranged from 0.002 to 0.045). In addition, among elderly women infected with varicella virus antibodies, the BMD in their left arm, right arm, lumbar spine and other parts also showed significant differences (p values ranged from 0.012 to 0.039). These results suggest that viral infection may be a potential factor for decreased bone mineral density, especially in middle-aged and elderly women (Table 2).

Discussion

Osteoporosis is one of the major clinical problems faced by elderly men and women, as it leads to increased bone fragility, making fractures more likely to occur throughout the body, resulting in high medical costs, physical disability, a decline in quality of life, and an increase in mortality rates (Tella and Gallagher, 2014). As people age, the incidence of osteoporosis also rises, making it crucial to diagnose, prevent, and manage its complications. Over the past decade, advances in our understanding of bone biology have facilitated the development of novel treatments specifically designed to further prevent and treat osteoporosis (Castelo-Branco and Soveral, 2014). Unlike previous studies that broadly implicated immune dysregulation, our analysis is the first to demonstrate genetic evidence that EBV, HHV-6, and VZV antibody responses are causally associated with reduced BMD, thereby identifying specific viral triggers of immune-mediated bone loss. In recent years, numerous studies worldwide have continuously emphasized the significant role of immune responses in the occurrence and development of osteoporosis. For instance, Zheng et al. found through genetic correlation and Mendelian randomization studies that circulating inflammatory proteins have a significant causal relationship with osteoporosis and fractures (Zheng et al., 2024). Similarly, Fischer and Haffner-Luntzer also pointed out that the activation of Th1/Th17 cell subsets can promote osteoclast differentiation, thereby accelerating bone loss (Zhang et al., 2022; Fischer and Haffner-Luntzer, 2022). Compared with these studies, our work not only reconfirms the crucial role of inflammation and immunity in bone metabolism, but also provides for the first time genetic evidence of the association between EBV, HHV-6 and VZV specific antibodies and osteoporosis. This combined MR analysis with NHANES data approach makes our findings have stronger causal inference power and population representativeness. Notably, we have revealed the significant impact of viral infections on specific populations (young men and elderly women), which provides new stratified evidence for international related research and suggests that future intervention measures should consider individualization and population differences.

The notion of immune aging represents the alterations in cellular and serological immune responses related to age, and it influences the body's capacity to respond to both exogenous and endogenous antigens. As individuals age, immune system function declines, leading to increased susceptibility to infections, reduced vaccine efficacy, and a chronic low-grade inflammatory state. This immune dysregulation is closely related to various age-related health issues, such as atherosclerosis and Alzheimer's disease (Reid and Billington, 2022). Currently, certain monoclonal antibodies are employed in the management of osteoporosis with notable efficacy. For instance, Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting RANKL, effectively inhibits the formation and function of osteoclasts. However, while Denosumab’s anti-fracture effects are similar to those of bisphosphonates, its suppression of bone resorption significantly diminishes approximately seven months after the last administration, potentially leading to vertebral compression fractures (Rachner et al., 2011; Cui and Snapper, 2021). Therefore, it is imperative that new treatments are grounded in a profound understanding of bone biology when devising them.

In our investigation, we utilized large-scale GWAS data to conduct a bivariate MR analysis, exploring the potential causal relationship between antibody immune responses and osteoporosis. Our findings suggest that three types of antibody immune responses—specifically, Epstein-Barr virus EA-D antibody levels, Human herpesvirus 6 IE1A antibody levels, and Varicella zoster virus glycoproteins E and I antibody levels—may act as causal factors in the development of osteoporosis. Importantly, we found no evidence supporting a reverse causal relationship. Notably, Epstein-Barr virus EA-D antibody levels and Human herpesvirus 6 IE1A antibody levels were negatively associated with bone mineral density, indicating a potential protective effect on bone density (Song et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024).

EBV is widespread globally and has been associated with an increasing number of acute, chronic, malignant, and autoimmune disease syndromes. Previous studies have explored the humoral immune responses to EBV for diagnostic, pathogenic mechanisms, and protective purposes, such as vaccine development (Kwan Tat et al., 2004). Moreover, humoral immune responses to EBV-encoded tumor antigens LMP1, LMP2, and BARF1 have provided new options for targeted immunotherapy (Deng et al., 2018).Our study identified a significant causal relationship between EBV EA-D antibody levels and osteoporosis. This finding suggests that EBV infection might influence bone health through chronic immune activation and persistent inflammation. Previous studies have shown that EBV can remain latent in B cells, leading to ongoing low-level immune responses that induce chronic inflammation. This chronic inflammation may promote osteoclast activation, increasing bone resorption and reducing bone density, thereby heightening the risk of osteoporosis. Additionally, EBV infection could alter immune cell functions within the bone marrow microenvironment, further disrupting bone metabolism. Therefore, strategies targeting EBV infection could represent novel approaches in managing osteoporosis.Immune responses play a crucial role in the onset and progression of osteoporosis. The activation of immune cells, particularly Th1 and Th17 cell subpopulations, has been shown to promote bone resorption, contributing to the development of osteoporosis. Studies have demonstrated that Th1 cells, through the secretion of cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ), stimulate osteoclastogenesis, leading to increased bone loss . Similarly, Th17 cells, by producing cytokines like IL-17, play a significant role in the process of bone resorption . On the other hand, regulatory T cells (Tregs) protect bone density by inhibiting osteoclast activity . Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have been found to accelerate bone resorption during immune activation, further exacerbating the progression of osteoporosis . These immune-mediated mechanisms provide a novel perspective for understanding the impact of viral infections on osteoporosis and highlight the dual role of immune responses in bone metabolism (Middeldorp, 2015; Bagg, 1991).

HHV-6 also known as Human B lymphocyte virus type 6, was first isolated from peripheral blood lymphocytes of lymphoid hyperplasia patients in 1986. Although HHV-6 differs from other human herpesviruses, DNA studies have revealed similar genomic features to cytomegalovirus (Ablashi et al., 1988). The prevalence of HHV-6 antibodies in the general population is approximately 26 %, but significant variations exist among different populations. Higher prevalence and titers of HHV-6 antibodies have been observed in the serum of patients with certain malignant tumors, Sjogren syndrome, multiple sclerosis, and nodular lesions (Voumvourakis et al., 2010; Laing et al., 2018).We also found that HHV-6 IE1A antibody levels are significantly associated with osteoporosis. Similar to other herpesviruses, HHV-6 triggers prolonged host immune responses, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Chronic HHV-6 infection may lead to increased bone resorption and impaired bone formation through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Notably, the persistent infection of HHV-6 could alter the bone marrow microenvironment, keeping immune cells in an activated state, thereby accelerating bone loss. HHV-6 could play a critical role in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, and further research into its mechanisms is vital for developing new therapeutic approaches.

VZV causes clinically significant diseases during both acute and recurrent infections, accompanied by powerful innate and acquired immune responses. During the primary infection, VZV hijacks T cells and spreads to the skin, establishing a latent period in the ganglion cells. The persistent T cell and B cell memory, which is strengthened through reactivation or re-exposure, can lead to postherpetic neuralgia, spinal encephalitis, meningitis, and VZV vasculopathy. As our MR results indicate, these mechanisms may affect the incidence of osteoporosis (Nagel and Gilden, 2014; Bowden et al., 2016). These findings suggest a potential link between antibody immune responses and osteoporosis.This study further supports the critical role of the immune system in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, aligning with recent findings in the field. For instance, previous research has established an association between EBV and other immune-mediated diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis, but its link to bone density has been less clear. Our study extends this association by providing new evidence of EBV antibody levels potentially affecting bone density. Additionally, while HHV-6 and VZV infections have been shown to induce chronic inflammation, their specific roles in osteoporosis had not been thoroughly investigated before. Our study provides the first genetic evidence supporting the causal role of these virus-specific antibodies in the development of osteoporosis. This finding not only fills a gap in the existing literature but also opens new avenues for future research.

It is important to consider that the effect of virus-mediated immune-inflammatory activity may be context-dependent. Latent viral infections such as EBV or VZV may synergize with intrinsic host conditions—such as chronic inflammation, immunosenescence, or metabolic imbalance—to amplify osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption (Sansoni et al., 2008; Vincenzi et al., 2024). Alternatively, viral factors might act more like modulators of the host’s immune microenvironment, in a manner comparable to diet or microbiome effects, subtly shifting the physiological baseline without being the sole driver of pathogenesis (McMiller et al., 2024; Lawson et al., 2001). Future mechanistic studies are needed to delineate these interaction pathways in more detail. Our MR findings linking EBV and HHV-6 antibody responses with reduced BMD support the concept that virus-driven immune activation—through cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6—may directly accelerate osteoclast activity, providing causal evidence that complements prior observational reports.

This study, by combining Mendelian randomization methods with observational analysis of NHANES 2003–2004 data, has revealed potential causal relationships between specific viral infections and changes in bone mineral density. This finding is significant, as it not only validates the hypothesis that EBV and VZV may influence bone health through chronic inflammatory responses but also provides clearer evidence of the relationship between viral infections and osteoporosis. The significant associations observed in young men and elderly women suggest that the impact of viral infections may vary across different populations, offering insights for the development of personalized intervention strategies. Further validation and extended research on these findings will enhance our understanding of the role of viral infections in the pathology of osteoporosis and could lead to the exploration of new prevention and treatment approaches. Our subgroup analysis revealed that EBV-related bone loss was most evident in young men, while VZV effects predominated in elderly women. These sex- and age-specific patterns refine current understanding and suggest that immune responses to latent viruses may differentially affect skeletal health across the lifespan.

Experimental models provide biologically plausible pathways linking EBV and HHV-6 to bone metabolism. EBV infection in humanized mice induces human osteoclast activation and bone erosion, with osteoclast-like multinucleated cells positive for cathepsin K and MMP-9, indicating direct osteoclastogenesis (Nagasawa et al., 2021). In vitro, EBV has been shown to drive differentiation of RAW264.7 cells into osteoclast-like cells through pro-inflammatory signaling (Yokoe et al., 2022). HHV-6 latency has been detected in CD34⁺ bone marrow progenitor cells in vivo, suggesting sustained immune perturbation in the hematopoietic niche (Luppi et al., 1999). Together, these observations support our interpretation that latent herpesvirus infections may contribute to osteoporosis via chronic immune activation, although confirmation in skeletal models remains important.

This study has several important limitations. First, instrument validity and measurement error: although Mendelian randomization (MR) reduces confounding, its validity relies on strong and specific associations between genetic instruments and the exposures of interest. For EBV and VZV infection status, the instrument–exposure relationship may be imperfect, which can weaken causal inference. In addition, we used serological antibody levels as proxy measures of immune exposure; as downstream markers, antibody titers do not fully capture immune variation and can be influenced by vaccination history, prior infections, and individual immune status, introducing potential misclassification.Second, study design and residual confounding: the NHANES 2003–2004 data are cross-sectional, capturing associations at a single time point and limiting insight into temporal dynamics or disease progression. While MR mitigates confounding by design, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. In our NHANES analyses we did not fully adjust for key covariates (e.g., BMI, smoking, vitamin D, physical activity, socioeconomic status), which may affect both infection and bone mineral density.Third, sample size and statistical power: subgroup analyses—particularly for certain age–sex strata—had relatively small sample sizes, reducing power and increasing the risk of type II error (i.e., missing smaller effects). Findings in these strata should therefore be interpreted cautiously.Fourth, geographic and ancestry coverage: the GWAS resources (e.g., UK and Finnish cohorts) predominantly reflect European ancestry, and the observational component draws on a U.S. population (NHANES 2003–2004). This constrained geographic and ancestry range may limit generalizability to other populations. Multi-ancestry GWAS and non-U.S. cohorts are needed to assess external validity.Fifth, lack of functional/experimental validation and clinical data: our conclusions are based on genetic epidemiology and observational analyses; we did not perform mechanistic experiments (e.g., in vitro functional assays, in vivo models) or clinical validation. Future work should include functional studies to test proposed mechanisms and longitudinal/clinical datasets to evaluate translational relevance.Finally, data vintage and analysis scope: although NHANES 2003–2004 provides standardized phenotypes, its age may limit contemporary applicability. Due to data-availability constraints, we could not perform additional supporting analyses (e.g., extensive multivariable adjustments, colocalization, or comprehensive sensitivity analyses), which may affect precision and generalizability.Collectively, these limitations argue for cautious interpretation and motivate future research that incorporates multi-ancestry cohorts, longitudinal designs, rigorous multivariable adjustment (or multivariable MR), colocalization and pleiotropy-robust methods, as well as functional and clinical validation to triangulate the observed associations.

By integrating MR analysis with cross-sectional data from the NHANES 2003–2004, this study offers an unprecedented exploration of the potential causal relationship between EBV and VZV infections and bone mineral density in different populations. Our research not only supports the hypothesis that viral infections are significantly associated with osteoporosis but also reveals the substantial impact of these infections on bone health in specific populations, such as young men and elderly women.These findings suggest that the identified molecules may represent potential candidates for further exploration in therapeutic contexts.

Conclusion

The novelty of this study lies in the combination of MR—a powerful tool for causal inference—which addresses many limitations of traditional observational studies, such as confounding bias and reverse causality. Additionally, the use of nationally representative NHANES data provides a robust foundation for the generalizability of our findings. This research provides new evidence for the role of viral infections in the pathology of osteoporosis, highlighting the need for larger longitudinal studies and mechanistic research to further validate and expand upon our findings. This study provides foundational data and a framework that could inform future vaccine or antiviral research efforts.
