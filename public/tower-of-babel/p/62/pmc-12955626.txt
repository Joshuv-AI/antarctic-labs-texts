Microbiome and metabolite biomarkers of CAR T-cell therapy outcomes in relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma∗
Authors: Sang Eun Yoon, Woorim Kang, Junhun Cho, Hyeon Ji Cho, Mauricio Chalita, Hyun-Seok Oh, Dong-Wook Hyun, Sujeong Han, Hyun Kim, Hojun Sung, Jae-Yun Lee, Bon Park, Kyung Ju Ryu, Hyun-Young Kim, Duck Cho, Won Seog Kim, Seok Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Bacteroides fragilis and inosine biosynthesis are key CAR T-cell response biomarkers, supporting microbiome-based strategies for treatment.•CAR T-cell AEs were linked to specific microbes and serum fatty acids, with higher acetate and butyrate in patients without AEs.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape for B-cell lymphoid malignancies.1,2 In particular, CD19-targeted CAR T-cell therapy is widely used as a salvage treatment for patients with relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (R/R-DLBCL).3, 4, 5 However, several challenges remain, including the management of life-threatening toxicities associated with CAR T-cell infusion and the high rates of disease progression or relapse due to resistance.6,7 A recent multicenter, real-world, prospective study of 485 patients with R/R-DLBCL who received the CD19-targeted CAR T-cell products axicabtagene ciloleucel or tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel) reported that ∼55% to 65% of patients experienced disease relapse or progression within 1 year after treatment.8 The efficacy of CD19 CAR T-cell therapy is influenced by multiple factors, such as target antigen loss and limited in vivo persistence of CAR T cells.9

To improve outcomes, novel CAR T-cell therapies with different targets and mechanisms of action are under development. However, optimizing the in vivo efficacy of existing CD19 CAR T cells by modulating the host environment is also a promising strategy. Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiome plays a role in shaping immune responses and influencing CAR T-cell efficacy.10 In a study of patients treated with CD19 CAR T-cell therapy, responders exhibited higher levels of anaerobic gut commensal bacteria and an enrichment of peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathways, suggesting a potential link between specific microbiome compositions and CAR T-cell responses.10 Given the role of the gut microbiome in regulating immune function and the observed variations in microbiome composition across different ethnicities and geographic regions, investigating the gut microbiome in Asian patients receiving CD19-targeted CAR T-cell therapy may provide valuable insights for microbiome-driven strategies to enhance treatment efficacy. Here, we present an analysis of the gut microbiome in patients undergoing CD19 CAR T-cell therapy and explore its potential impact on clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients

We prospectively enrolled 47 patients with R/R-DLBCL scheduled to undergo CD19 CAR T-cell therapy. Among them, tisa-cel was administered as part of routine clinical practice, whereas anbalcabtagene autoleucel (anbal-cel) was administered within a clinical trial. Both tisa-cel and anbal-cel are second-generation CAR T-cell products that share the same extracellular antigen-binding domain and costimulatory domains (4-1BB and CD3ζ). However, anbal-cel incorporates CRISPR/Cas9–mediated dual knockout of programmed cell death protein 1 and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains, designed to enhance T-cell persistence and mitigate exhaustion, as previously reported.11 Following informed consent, fecal and serum samples were collected at 2 time points: baseline prior to apheresis (pretreatment; n = 47) and 1 month after CAR T-cell infusion (posttreatment; n = 32) (Figure 1A). Although predefined exclusion criteria were established to eliminate patients with conditions that could significantly disturb the microbiome (eg, active or chronic inflammatory bowel disease; acute infectious disease within 1 month before apheresis; recent use of antibiotics, steroids, or immunosuppressants within 1 month before apheresis; history of gastrointestinal tract surgery; or receipt of artificial nutrition), no patients who met these criteria were enrolled. Therefore, no participants were excluded based on these eligibility restrictions. Baseline microbiome composition in patients with R/R-DLBCL was assessed and compared with that in age-matched healthy controls (HC) and patients with newly-diagnosed DLBCL (ND-DLBCL). Microbiome data from HC and ND-DLBCL were obtained from previously reported data sets (Figure 1B).12 Pretreatment serum and fecal samples from patients with R/R-DLBCL (n = 47) were analyzed to evaluate associations with response to CAR T-cell therapy and the occurrence of immune-related adverse events (AEs). Serum samples were used for metabolomic analysis, and fecal samples were used for microbiome and metagenomic profiling. Microbiome composition was also compared between pretreatment and posttreatment fecal samples in the subset of patients with available posttreatment (n = 32). Clinical and laboratory data collected at baseline included sex, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, complete blood count, serum lactate dehydrogenase, ferritin, and C-reactive protein at the time of apheresis. Clinical history reflecting tumor aggressiveness at diagnosis, including Ann Arbor stage and prior lines of therapy, was also recorded. Response to CAR T-cell therapy was evaluated using computed tomography (CT) and fludeoxyglucose-18 positron emission tomography/CT at 1 and 3 months after infusion, based on the Lugano response criteria.13 Responders were defined as patients who achieved complete remission or partial response. The occurrence and severity of immune-related AEs, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), were recorded within 30 days of CAR T-cell infusion according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy consensus guidelines.14 All CRS and ICANS events, regardless of grade, were included in the analysis. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Samsung Medical Center (approval number: 2022-05-135) and was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Whole-genome shotgun metagenomic sequencing

For whole-genome shotgun sequencing, metagenomic libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Library size and concentration were assessed using a Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform (2 × 250 bp read length) at CJ Bioscience Inc, Seoul, Korea, with a minimum sequencing depth of 10 Gbp per sample to ensure high-quality data acquisition. Whole-genome shotgun metagenomic sequencing data for patients with ND-DLBCL and HC were obtained from a previous study.12 For comparative analysis with R/R-DLBCL, a subset of 47 patients with ND-DLBCL and 47 age- and sex-matched HC was selected. Taxonomic and functional analyses were performed by shotgun metagenomics (see supplemental Methods).

Inosine analysis and luminescence-based cytotoxicity assay

Serum short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) and inosine concentrations were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, and detailed analytical procedures are described in the supplemental Methods. Primary CAR T cells were generated from healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and their cytotoxic activity against Nalm6 cells was evaluated using a luminescence-based assay, as described in the supplemental Methods. Functional assays assessing the effects of inosine and A2A receptor inhibition on CAR T-cell cytotoxicity were conducted as detailed in the supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using nonparametric tests, with significance set at P value <.05. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival were analyzed with Kaplan–Meier methods and multivariable Cox regression, where feasible. Microbiome diversity was assessed by Shannon and Bray–Curtis metrics, with group differences evaluated using PERMANOVA. Differential abundance was analyzed with ANCOM-BC, and functional associations were explored using MaAsLin2 with false discovery rate correction. Predictive modeling employed random forest with threefold cross-validation. Given the limited sample size and subgroup imbalances, all subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Where feasible, clinical covariates were incorporated into multivariable Cox regression models, and microbiome and metabolome features were evaluated using frameworks with internal normalization and multiple-testing correction. Subgroup analyses with particularly limited sample sizes (eg, antibiotic exposure, AEs) were explicitly considered exploratory and are presented as hypothesis-generating. Detailed statistical methods are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes

Among the 47 patients included in the analysis, 24 (51%) were aged <60 years, and most were male (n = 28; 60%). The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status was 0 to 1 in 94% of the patients. At diagnosis, 28 (60%) patients presented with advanced disease (stage III/IV). Laboratory findings showed elevated C-reactive protein in 8 (17%) , increased ferritin in 9 (19%), and elevated lactate dehydrogenase in 34 (72%) patients. The median number of prior treatments was 2 (range, 1-4), and 34 (72%) patients received CAR T-cell therapy as third-line treatment (Table 1). All patients received CD19 CAR T-cell therapy; 29 received tisa-cel in clinical practice and 18 received anbal-cel in a clinical trial.15 Baseline characteristics before CAR T-cell therapy did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (supplemental Table 1). At the 1-month follow-up, the overall response rate, defined as the sum of complete remission and partial response, was 78.7% (n = 37/47). At the 3-month follow-up, overall response rate decreased to 48.9% (n = 23/47). CRS and ICANS were observed in 36.2% (17/47) and 14.9% (n = 7/47) of patients, respectively.

Gut microbiota characterization in R/R-DLBCL patients

To characterize gut microbiota communities in patients with R/R-DLBCL (n = 47), we compared them with an equal number of age- and sex-matched patients with ND-DLBCL and HC (Figure 1B). Beta-diversity analysis showed significant differences in overall microbiome composition in R/R-DLBCL compared with both ND-DLBCL (P < .001) and HC (P < .001; Figure 1C). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria (also referred to as Pseudomonadota) exhibited the most notable difference, with significantly higher levels in R/R-DLBCL than in HC (P = .006). By contrast, no significant difference was observed when comparing R/R-DLBCL with ND-DLBCL (P = .3124; Figure 1D). The beta-diversity distance from HC was also more pronounced in R/R-DLBCL than in ND-DLBCL (Figure 1E), suggesting more severe dysbiosis in the gut microbiota of patients with R/R-DLBCL.

Building on our previous findings that higher levels of Enterobacteriaceae negatively correlated with immunochemotherapy response in ND-DLBCL, we hypothesized that patients with R/R-DLBCL would also exhibit elevated Enterobacteriaceae levels. Supporting this hypothesis, patients with R/R-DLBCL showed elevated Enterobacteriaceae levels similar to those observed in nonresponders (NR) with ND-DLBCL (ND-DLBCL) following first-line immunochemotherapy. In contrast, patients with R/R-DLBCL differed significantly from responders with ND-DLBCL (ND-DLBCL) and HC (Figure 1F).

Gut microbiome analysis and response to CAR T-cell therapy

Using data from 32 patients with pre- and posttreatment samples, we conducted a comparative analysis of the gut microbiome, examining β-diversity before CAR T-cell therapy and 1 month after infusion. CAR T-cell therapy did not induce statistically significant changes in the overall composition of the gut microbiome (P = .3542; Figure 2A). To evaluate potential confounding by CAR T-cell product type, we compared gut microbiome community structures between tisa-cel and anbal-cel recipients. No significant differences were observed at baseline (PERMANOVA, P = .6426) or after infusion (PERMANOVA, P = .8741), suggesting that the biomarker associations identified in this study were not attributable to product heterogeneity. We next assessed the association between gut microbiome composition and response to CAR T-cell therapy. Of the 32 patients with paired pre- and posttreatment samples, 22 demonstrated a clinical response at the 1-month evaluation, whereas 5 did not. The remaining 5 patients could not be assessed at 1 month because of early clinical deterioration from disease progression; these patients were classified as NR. β-Diversity analysis revealed a significant difference in microbiome composition between responders and NR at 1 month (P = .007; Figure 2B). To identify microbial taxa contributing to these compositional differences, we performed an ANCOM-BC analysis. At both pre- and posttreatment time points, Bacteroides fragilis was the most distinguishing taxon associated with CAR T-cell therapy response (Figure 2C). After the initial 1-month response assessment, a second evaluation was conducted at 3 months to monitor disease status. Patients who relapsed after initially responding were categorized as having a “relapse,” whereas those who maintained their response were categorized as having a “maintained response.” All 10 patients classified as NR at the 1-month evaluation—including the 5 who could not be assessed because of early clinical deterioration—remained without clinical response at the 3-month assessment (progressive disease, stable disease, or death within 1.7-2.6 months after infusion). Thus, the NR classification was consistent across both time points. When we analyzed Bacteroides fragilis abundance in pre- and posttreatment samples according to response durability, patients with a maintained response exhibited consistently higher levels of Bacteroides fragilis at both time points. In contrast, those who relapsed by 3 months showed low Bacteroides fragilis abundance, similar to NR (Figure 2D). Building on these observations, we hypothesized that the presence of Bacteroides fragilis might be associated with improved PFS. Indeed, patients with detectable Bacteroides fragilis in their gut microbiota prior to treatment had significantly longer PFS compared with those without it (P = .022; Figure 2E). In addition, given prior reports suggesting that antibiotic use disrupts the gut microbiota and potentially lowers CAR T-cell therapy response rates, we examined the impact of antibiotic use on the microbiota.10 Because no patients received antibiotics within 1 month before CAR T-cell therapy, we evaluated the impact of antibiotic exposure during the first 30 days after infusion using available fecal samples (n = 32). Postinfusion antibiotics (cefepime, meropenem, piperacillin–tazobactam, vancomycin, teicoplanin, and levofloxacin) were administered for febrile neutropenia or documented bacterial infections. We compared 25 patients who received antibiotics after infusion with 7 who did not. No significant differences in overall microbiome composition were observed (P = .6672; Figure 2F), nor was the 1-month response rate affected by antibiotic use (P = .2096; Figure 2G). However, the small sample size and group imbalance (25 vs 7) limit these findings; thus, results should be interpreted cautiously, and larger studies are needed to clarify the effect of antibiotics on CAR T-cell therapy outcomes.

Metagenomic functional profiling and predictive modeling of CAR T-cell therapy response

To explore metabolic differences tied to CAR T-cell therapy response, metagenomic functional profiling of pretreatment fecal samples was performed using HUMAnN3, with pathway-level biomarkers subsequently validated via MaAsLin2 (Figure 3A). Our analysis revealed significant enrichment of the inosine biosynthesis pathways (PWY-5695, PWY-6123, PWY-7234) and the L-glutamine biosynthesis pathway (PWY-6549) in responders. By contrast, L-isoleucine biosynthesis pathways (ILEUSYN-PWY, PWY-5103) and the palmitoyl ethanolamide biosynthesis pathway (PWY-8055) appeared more prevalent in NR. Leveraging these functional profiles, we developed a random forest model to predict response to CAR T-cell therapy. This model exhibited robust predictive performance, achieving an area under the curve of 0.88 (Figure 3B). Key features linked to response prediction included the biosynthetic pathways of inosine (PWY-5695, PWY-6123, PWY-6124), pantothenate (PANTOSYN-PWY, PANTO-PWY), arginine (ARGINIE-SYN4-PWY), and glutamic acid (PWY-6549). Pathways associated with NR encompassed those involved in the biosynthesis of propionic acid (P108-PWY) and L-isoleucine (ILEUSYN-PWY, PWY-5103; Figure 3C).

The inosine biosynthesis pathway emerged consistently as a key feature in both the random forest model and the MaAsLin2 analysis, underscoring its potential relevance in distinguishing responders from NR. Stratification by median reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads values of the inosine biosynthesis pathway showed that the enriched group (top 50%) had significantly longer PFS than the depleted group (bottom 50%; P = .002; Figure 3D). Building on the recognition of the inosine biosynthesis pathway as a critical feature in responders, we further investigated whether its influence extended to measurable differences in pretreatment serum inosine levels. Serum inosine concentrations were analyzed to assess whether higher levels could be attributed to microbial synthesis and subsequent host absorption. Our analysis showed significantly higher inosine levels in the responder group relative to NR (P = .042; Figure 3E). Pretreatment serum inosine levels showed a significant positive correlation with Bacteroides fragilis abundance by linear regression (P < .001; R2 = 0.3550; Figure 3F), a biomarker enriched in responders. To assess inosine’s effect on CD19 CAR T-cell antitumor activity, we cocultured CAR T cells with Nalm6 cells in the presence of inosine (Figure 3G). Cytotoxicity assays indicated that inosine may augment CAR T-cell–mediated killing of Nalm6 cells (Figure 3H). CAR T cells pretreated with 10mM inosine for 48 hours showed a trend toward increased cytotoxicity after 24-hour coculture compared with untreated cells (Figure 3I-J). This effect was attenuated by inhibition of the adenosine A2A receptor, suggesting a potential mechanistic link (Figure 3K). In the univariate analysis, significant predictors included age >60 years, absolute lymphocyte count <1000, ferritin ≥1200 ng/mL, and submedian serum inosine (Table 2). Other variables were not significant in the univariate model. In the multivariate Cox regression analysis to identify predictive factors for PFS, age >60 years, ferritin ≥1200 ng/mL, and submedian serum inosine remained significant independent predictors of PFS (Table 2). Because Bacteroides fragilis abundance was highly correlated with serum inosine levels (Figure 3F), including both variables in the multivariate Cox model could have introduced collinearity and increased the risk of overfitting in this limited cohort. We therefore selected serum inosine as the representative biomarker, given its stronger functional plausibility.

Subgroup analysis of AEs and gut microbiome metabolism

To assess the impact of CAR T-cell therapy–related AEs, we performed a subgroup analysis by stratifying patients based on the presence or absence of CRS and ICANS using microbiome data from pretreatment fecal samples (n = 47). A comparative analysis was conducted to identify bacterial species biomarkers by evaluating patients who experienced AEs against those who did not. Significant differences emerged between patients with and without AEs (P = .0338; Figure 4A). Subsequent comparisons highlighted distinct microbial taxa associated with AEs (Figure 4B). Notably, species from the genera Bifidobacterium, Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia—all recognized producers of acetate and butyrate—were more prevalent in patients without AEs. Building on these microbial differences, we analyzed serum fatty acid profiles to explore their metabolic implications. Higher levels of acetate and butyrate were observed in patients without AEs, whereas patients who experienced AEs displayed elevated levels of isovalerate, a branched-chain fatty acid (Figure 4C).

To explore the link between microbial metabolism and serum fatty acid levels, we analyzed gut microbiome functional gene profiles. Genes involved in acetate (ackA), butyrate (but), and isovalerate (IVD) biosynthesis correlated with corresponding serum metabolites. Spearman correlation showed that ackA abundance was positively associated with serum acetate (ρ = 0.5561, P = .0008), but with butyrate (ρ = 0.7485, P < .0001), and IVD with isovalerate (ρ = 0.4766, P = .0050; Figure 4D). In the subgroup analysis, ackA and but genes were more abundant in the non-AE group, aligning with the higher serum levels of acetate and butyrate. Conversely, IVD was more abundant in the AE group, consistent with the elevated serum levels of isovalerate (Figure 4E).

Discussion

In this investigation, we detected significant alterations in the gut microbiota of patients with R/R-DLBCL, characterized by an increased abundance of Proteobacteria and Enterobacteriaceae, alongside a more pronounced degree of microbial dysbiosis relative to ND-DLBCL. Moreover, the gut microbiota composition of patients with R/R-DLBCL closely resembled that of patients with ND-DLBCL who failed to respond to first-line immunochemotherapy. Our previous study established Enterobacteriaceae as a prognostic biomarker for treatment response in patients with ND-DLBCL receiving rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone.12 However, in patients with R/R-DLBCL—in whom Enterobacteriaceae levels remained consistently elevated—no clear association emerged between their abundance and response to CAR T-cell therapy. This widespread prevalence of Enterobacteriaceae in R/R-DLBCL may have obscured their prognostic significance in this context.

By contrast, our study revealed an enrichment of Bacteroides fragilis in responders to CAR T-cell therapy. Bacteroides fragilis is recognized for its immunomodulatory properties, primarily through the production of polysaccharide A, which fosters dendritic cell maturation and induces regulatory T-cell development.16, 17, 18 Such effects contribute to a more balanced immune environment, potentially enhancing CAR T-cell function and yielding improved treatment outcomes. Although Bacteroides fragilis emerged as a species-level biomarker for CAR T-cell response, overall Bacteroidetes abundance did not differ significantly among patients with R/R-DLBCL, patients with ND-DLBCL, and HC. This likely reflects the metabolic and taxonomic diversity within the Bacteroidetes (Bacteroidota) phylum, underscoring the importance of species-level resolution for identifying clinically relevant microbial signatures. In addition, functional metagenomic analysis suggested that responders to CAR T-cell therapy were enriched for inosine biosynthesis pathways. Consistent with this, functional profiling with HUMAnN3 indicated that Bacteroides fragilis may contribute to inosine biosynthesis, raising the possibility that microbial metabolism could influence CAR T-cell responses. Inosine, a pivotal microbial metabolite, has been shown to modulate immune responses and enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.19,20 Microbiome-derived inosine activates T cells via the adenosine A2A receptor, bolstering antitumor immunity. Furthermore, immunotherapy-induced gut permeability facilitates inosine translocation into systemic circulation, thereby amplifying T-cell activity and therapeutic outcomes. Recent studies have also demonstrated that inosine directly improves CAR T-cell functionality by promoting T-cell proliferation and differentiation in glucose-deprived tumor microenvironments.21 Mechanistically, this effect is driven by overexpression of adenosine deaminase, which converts adenosine to inosine. This reprograms metabolism by reducing glycolysis, enhancing mitochondrial function, and promoting glutaminolysis and polyamine synthesis, thereby supporting CAR T-cell stemness and antitumor activity. In line with this, our in vitro experiments suggested that inosine may enhance CAR T-cell cytotoxicity—an effect attenuated by A2A receptor inhibition. In our in vitro assays, inosine concentrations of 10mM to 30mM were used, consistent with prior studies designed to elicit measurable cellular effects under controlled conditions.20, 21, 22 Although these concentrations exceed physiological serum levels, they provide a standardized experimental framework for exploring potential functional effects. Taken together, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conversely, NR displayed higher levels of L-isoleucine biosynthesis pathways. Elevated branched-chain amino acids such as isoleucine have been associated with immune suppression and may inhibit CAR T-cell activity.23 These metabolic distinctions underscore the potential of targeting microbial metabolic pathways to optimize CAR T-cell therapy outcomes.

With respect to immunotherapy-related toxicities, patients who developed AEs exhibited distinct gut microbiota profiles compared with those who did not. More specifically, SCFA-producing bacteria—including Bifidobacterium, Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia—proved more abundant in patients who did not develop AEs. SCFAs such as acetate and butyrate support intestinal barrier integrity and regulate immune responses, potentially mitigating excessive inflammation.24, 25, 26 Intriguingly, although Faecalibacterium prausnitzii was identified as a negative biomarker for CAR T-cell response, its well-documented anti-inflammatory properties may help reduce immunotherapy-related toxicities.27 This observation suggests that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii may have differential effects on treatment efficacy and toxicity, highlighting the complex interplay between the gut microbiome and host immunity in CAR T-cell therapy. Additionally, patients who experienced severe AEs showed elevated levels of isovalerate, a branched-chain fatty acid linked to proinflammatory effects. Such an imbalance—favoring proinflammatory metabolites—may predispose patients to severe immunotherapy-related toxicities. Modulating the gut microbiome to enhance SCFA production while reducing proinflammatory metabolites could offer a potential strategy to prevent or mitigate these toxicities.

Notably, antibiotic use did not significantly affect gut microbiota composition or CAR T-cell therapy response in our study. This contrasts with a previous report suggesting that broad-spectrum antibiotics deplete metabolically active commensals whose metabolites enhance CAR T-cell function.28 However, this discrepancy should be interpreted with caution. Our result may have been influenced by the limited sample size and by the study design excluding patients who received antibiotics shortly before apheresis, creating an approximately 6 to 8-week antibiotic-free interval before infusion. This interval likely mitigated short-term microbiome perturbations and may partly account for the differing observations. In addition, variations in the timing of antibiotic administration, baseline microbiome composition, and host-related factors such as ethnicity, diet, and microbial resilience could further contribute to interstudy differences.

In summary, our study highlights a significant link between gut microbiome and CAR T-cell outcomes in R/R-DLBCL. Bacteroides fragilis and inosine pathways emerged as potential biomarkers of response, whereas immune-suppressive metabolic pathways were enriched in NR and patients with AEs. These findings suggest that the microbiome may function both as a predictive biomarker and a therapeutic target, and its integration into clinical practice could enable more personalized and effective CAR T-cell therapy.

Nevertheless, this study has limitations. First, although survival analyses adjusted for clinical covariates, similar adjustments were not applied to microbiome or metabolome data given the limited sample size. The observational design also restricts causal inference. Second, immune-related AEs were analyzed as binary outcomes; further stratification by subtype and severity was limited by the small number of ICANS cases. Third, 2 CD19 CAR T-cell products were used, and although no significant differences were observed in baseline features, efficacy, toxicity, or microbiome composition, potential confounding cannot be fully excluded. Last, functional validation relied on in vitro cytotoxicity assays without phenotypic CAR T-cell characterization, and confounders such as diet, lifestyle, and host genetics were not fully considered. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, multiethnic cohorts and elucidate the mechanistic links between specific microbial species and CAR T-cell function. A deeper understanding of microbiome-mediated immunomodulation may ultimately inform microbiome-based interventions to enhance CAR T-cell efficacy and mitigate toxicity.
