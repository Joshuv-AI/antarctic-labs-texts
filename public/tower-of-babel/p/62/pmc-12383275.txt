BioFuse: A programmable timer switch of gene expression
Authors: Cheng Huang, Jie Zhao, Zichun Tan, Shang Dai, Binqiang Wang, Zhenming Xie, Furong Zhang, Yulong Zhou, Ning Yu, Chunhui Cai, Yonghua Yao, Baojun Wang, Bing Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current gene circuits designed to time gene expression depend on the intricate interactions among various regulators and their targets, which confines them to a limited range of temporal tunability. Here, we report a programmable timer switch of gene expression termed BioFuse, which allows the reaction time ranging from hours to days. BioFuse comprises a series of fuse-like tandem DNA cassettes that can be sequentially edited by the adenine base editors (ABEs), resulting in either the activation or deactivation of a downstream gene once the editing is complete. Adjusting the number of DNA cassettes incorporated allows precise programming of BioFuse’s reaction time. Applying BioFuse to control carotenoid biosynthesis genes decouples lycopene production from growth in E. coli and increases lycopene yield without external inducers. Using BioFuse in a bacterial autolysis system enables timely and efficient protein release. BioFuse offers a versatile tool for precise, wide-range timing of gene expression and metabolic activities in bacteria, with potential applications in industry and biomedicine.

INTRODUCTION

Time is perceived differently by bacteria and multicellular organisms. For Escherichia coli, a period of 20 min could encompass its entire life span. Behaviors of E. coli, including growth, division, and morphogenesis, are under the precise control of orchestrated temporal expression of multiple genes. This gene expression regulation pathway is intricately linked to its growth cycle and is constrained by its brief generation time (1, 2). Meanwhile, in most application scenarios, like industrial fermentation, agriculture, and biomedical engineering (3–5), bacteria are used over hours to days, much longer than their natural life cycles. Thus, there is an urgent need for a wide-ranged precise temporal control method, which is now hindered by the high complexity and poor orthogonality of cellular genetic pathways (6).

Traditionally, temporal control of gene expression involves promoter-attached operons, which are regulated by chemical inducers, such as isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), arabinose, and tetracycline (7–9), or by environmental inducers like cold shock (10). This approach requires real-time human intervention and is limited by the number of available exogenous inducers. Quorum sensing is also used to regulate the timing of gene expression (11), but the timescale is limited by the confined thresholds of cell concentration. In addition, some genetic oscillators (6, 12–15) can modulate gene expression periodically, but their inherent characteristics constrain the timescale of regulating cycles, preventing precise gene expression control after a specific time interval and restricting their practical applications. Recent advances in CRISPR-Cas–based gene editors, such as adenine base editors (ABEs) (16), cytosine base editors (17), and prime editors (18), have provided approaches for DNA-based cellular signal recording. A number of systems were developed to edit specific DNA targets in response to stimuli, enabling the capture of temporal information (19–22), recording of endogenous signals, e.g., CRISPR-mediated Analog Multi-Event Recording Apparatus (CAMERA) (23), DNA-based Ordered Memory and Iteration Network Operator (DOMINO) (24), DNA Typewriter (25), and estimation of elapsed time [DNA clock (26)], indicating the potential of using DNA as a medium for long-term and quantitative genetic programming.

To address the aforementioned challenge of gene expression temporal controlling, we developed BioFuse, a programmable transcription regulatory apparatus for precise time control of gene expression in engineered bacteria. BioFuse is composed of two components: the ABE-dCas9 fusion protein apparatus and a fuse-like DNA repeats upstream of the target gene promoter for sequential ABE editing. Once the editing process is complete, the downstream promoter is activated or deactivated to regulate target gene expression accordingly. The reaction time of BioFuse can be adjusted by changing the length of the fuse-like DNA repeats, allowing for precise temporal control of the target gene expression within a wide range of time. The BioFuse presents a reliable and long-term strategy for presetting bacterial metabolic change and behavior change, which holds great promise for applications in industrial fermentation and biomedical engineering (Fig. 1).

RESULTS

Principles and construction of BioFuse

BioFuse is constructed with tandem overlapped DNA cassettes for sequential ABE editing (Fig. 2, A and C). Starting from the first DNA cassette, the ABE-dCas9 mediates base mutations (A·T to G·C) of the fuse-like DNA repeats sequentially, followed by mutations in the downstream target gene promoter regions, which either deactivate (switch off) or activate (switch on) the downstream genes. Since this regulatory apparatus resembles the operation of a fuse, it is designated “BioFuse.”

Each repeating cassette of the fuse-like DNA, termed “push site,” consists of a 20–base pair protospacer sequence: 5′-NBBTAABBBBNNNNNNNNBB- 3′ (N = A/T/C/G; B = T/C/G). The three base pairs at the 5′ and 3′ ends of each push site overlap with their respective upstream and downstream push sites. All push sites are recognized by a single sgRNA (single guide RNA), termed “push-sgRNA” (psgRNA). On the basis of this paradigm, we designed and evaluated a highly functional push site sequence: 5′-AGCTAACTGCTGTCACTAGC-3′, which we designated as “α–push site.” Once the 3–base pair potential protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) at the far 3′ end is edited, a new PAM will be formed for the next push site (Fig. 2A) for subsequent editing by ABE. Downstream the push sites, we constructed a “switch site” containing a potential editing site in the −35-box region of the target gene promoter, which can be edited to activate or deactivate gene expression. For example, the −35 box of σ70 promoter is 5′-TTGACA-3′, which will be edited by ABE to 5′-CCGACA-3′ to deactivate the gene expression (from on to off) (Fig. 2A). Alternatively, we can preconstruct an inactive −35 box as 5′-TTGATA-3′, which will be edited by ABE to 5′-TTGACA-3′, resulting in activation of gene expression (from off to on) (Fig. 2C). The corresponding sgRNA for the switch site is named “switch sgRNA” (ssgRNA). We screened promoters that can be used for either switching off (fig. S1, A to C) or switching on (fig. S1, D to F) expression of targeted genes and ligated the promoters subsequently to BioFuse constructs.

Using mCherry as a reporter gene, we constructed BioFuse variants with different numbers of overlapped push sites, e.g., BioFuse Off-1 to Off-5 and On-1 to On-5 (table S1), where the number denotes the push sites within the BioFuse, while “On” and “Off” indicate its regulatory function. The efficient ABE8e-dCas9 (27) was used for BioFuse editing (table S2). We then cotransferred the respective plasmids containing the BioFuse apparatus into E. coli BL21 (DE3), which were inoculated into LB medium and continuously cultured with transfer (1/1000th of the total culture volume) performed every 24 hours.

Following each transfer, a bacterial sample was centrifuged and photographed (figs. S2G and S3G), and the fluorescence intensity of mCherry was measured (Fig. 2, B and D). The editing efficiency of BioFuse was further characterized using Sanger sequencing (figs. S2 and S3). In strains containing BioFuse Off-0 to Off-5 for temporal inactivation of mCherry expression, data show that longer BioFuse sequences delayed the decrease in mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 2B and fig. S2G). Conversely, in strains containing BioFuse On-0 to On-5, longer BioFuse sequences delayed the increase in mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 2D and fig. S3G). In addition, the mCherry fluorescence intensities correlated with the editing frequency of the switch sites (figs. S2 and S3), confirming that BioFuse controlled the mCherry expression as expected and that the timing of gene expression can be predetermined by adjusting the length of BioFuse.

BioFuse-mediated multiplex regulation of gene expression

Having demonstrated that BioFuse can regulate the temporal expression of a single gene, we aimed to use multiplex BioFuses with varying lengths to achieve temporal control of multiple genes in a single strain. Using both enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) and mCherry as reporters, we expressed the two combinations of BioFuses in E. coli: On-2–mCherry with On-4–eGFP (Fig. 2E and fig. S4A) and Off-2–mCherry with On-4–eGFP (Fig. 2H and fig. S4D). The transformed E. coli cells were plated onto LB agar, followed by continuous imaging every 12 hours under a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 2, F and I).

For colonies with BioFuses On-2–mCherry and On-4–eGFP, the mCherry expression was initiated at 24 hours, while the eGFP expression commenced at 48 hours (Fig. 2F), indicating that the BioFuse efficiently regulated the temporal expression of the two genes. This sequential gene activation led to a notable flower-like phenotype with red fluorescence showing primarily in the central colonies, and the yellow fluorescence was observed in the peripheral colonies (Fig. 2, F and G, and fig. S4, B and C). On the other hand, the colonies with BioFuses Off-2–mCherry and On-4–eGFP showed a different fluorescence pattern: The mCherry expression ceased after 24 hours, while the eGFP expression commenced at 48 hours (Fig. 2, I and J, and fig. S4, E and F). These results demonstrate the scalability of the BioFuse system to exert precise temporal control over the expression of multiple genes, highlighting its potential in constructing multitiered cellular regulatory networks.

BioFuse-mediated decoupling of cell growth from production

In the context of expressing exogenous products in a microorganism, it is a common practice to use an inducible expression strategy to decouple cell growth from production (28). Nevertheless, the current strategies for inducible expression have inherent limitations, including the requirements for chemical inducers (29, 30) and modifications to the culture medium and environmental conditions (10, 11, 31). Moreover, all these factors complicate the fermentation process and increase production costs. In contrast, the BioFuse enables the timed expression of exogenous genes at preset intervals without the need for extracellular signals, offering a powerful approach for timing and switching gene expression, thereby simplifying bioengineered fermentation processes.

To verify the regulation potentials of BioFuse, we take lycopene (32, 33) as an example, which is a natural carotenoid with a relatively high economic value. By using BioFuse On-2, we constructed an engineered E. coli strain (On-2–lyco) that switches on the expression of the lycopene synthetic gene cluster (crtE, crtB, and crtI) (34) after a predetermined time (Fig. 3, A, C, and D, and table S3). Compared with control strains, which consistently express the lycopene synthesis genes, the On-2–lyco exhibited notable enhanced growth in 24 hours before the lycopene gene cluster was activated (Fig. 3, B and C). The tandem push sites and switch sites were sequentially edited by ABE (Fig. 3D), which switched on the expression of lycopene synthetic gene (Fig. 3C). Ultimately, a significant increase (approximately fivefold, P < 0.001) of lycopene yield in the On-2–lyco at 48 hours was observed compared with the control strain (Fig. 3, E and F). These results demonstrate that BioFuse can regulate the timing of carotenoid production, thereby decoupling cell growth from production and consequently enhance the yield of carotenoids. This illustrates the potential of BioFuse in bioengineering applications, including microbial fermentation.

BioFuse-mediated temporal control of bacterial autolysis

Controllable bacterial lysis is essential in bioengineering, such as preparing crude enzyme solutions (35), extracting intracellular products (10), bacteria-based delivery systems (3, 36–38), and bacteria-suicide pathways (39). On the basis of BioFuse, we developed an autolysis apparatus capable of controlling the timing of E. coli autolysis without the need for human intervention or external signals.

To achieve high lysis efficiency, our strategy leverages the concept that the lysis of a single bacterium can trigger a chain reaction, leading to the lysis of surrounding bacteria (Fig. 4A). In this system, human C-type lysozyme (40, 41) is expressed at a constantly low level using a weak promoter to prevent premature lysis and ensure that the growth of bacteria was not affected (fig. S5A). To control the initiation of the chain lysis reaction, BioFuse was incorporated to a low-leakage on-promoter (fig. S6) of the ΦX174E toxin (36, 39, 42). After a predetermined time interval, ΦX174E was expressed, disrupting the bacterial cell wall, causing bacterial cell lysis and further releasing lysozyme. The lysozyme then degrades the cell walls of neighboring bacteria, triggering the chain lysis reaction (Fig. 4A).

We evaluated the autolysis efficiency of E. coli strains carrying two BioFuses with different lengths (On-2–lysis and On-3–lysis). These strains (table S4) also expressed mCherry, allowing us to track their lysis efficiency. An E. coli strain expressing only mCherry was used as a control (CK). Analysis of the release of mCherry fluorescence revealed that the On-2–lysis strain had a much higher lysis ratio (>90%) than the CK (<10%) at 72 hours (Fig. 4B) and lysed faster than the On-3–lysis strain (Fig. 4, B and D). This result aligns with the BioFuse site editing frequency (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, electron micrographs showed that the bacterial lysis rate negatively correlated with the length of the BioFuses (Fig. 4D and fig. S5B). These findings confirm that BioFuse-mediated autolysis apparatus can control the timing of E. coli autolysis without external intervention or signals, exhibiting great potential in relevant fields like bioproduct collection and bacteria-based drug delivery for targeted therapy.

Enhancing tunability and robustness of BioFuse system

To broaden BioFuse’s application scenarios and improve its robustness and tunability, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of key factors affecting its editing efficiency and implemented strategic improvements to achieve more precise temporal control of gene expression. First, we explored how the length of BioFuse influences the editing process. We compared the single-target editing efficiencies of BioFuses with varying lengths (Off-3, Off-4, Off-5, and Off-9) and focused on the editing efficiency of the third push site (push site 3) over time. We found that the shorter BioFuses (Off-3 and Off-4) exhibited higher editing efficiency of push site 3 (Fig. 5A). In contrast, editing efficiency was notably reduced in Off-5 and nearly stagnant in Off-9. The results indicate that the length of BioFuse directly affects both editing efficiency and propulsion limits, underscoring the need to investigate additional factors to make BioFuse more fine-tunable.

Next, we designed two additional BioFuses with distinct push site compositions: 5′-AGCTAAGTGCGTTCATGAGC-3′ (β) and 5′-AGCTAACTCCTTCGACTAGC-3′ (γ). These BioFuses were structurally like the α-type BioFuse except for the sequences and the corresponding psgRNA (Fig. 5B). We compared the editing efficiency of BioFuse Off-4, Off-β4, and Off-γ4 (Fig. 5, A and B, and figs. S2E and S7) and found that γ push sites showed the highest editing efficiency. The introduction of distinct push sites improves the programmability of BioFuse and enables the construction of a multiplex BioFuse system with minimal cross-talk.

The concentration of sgRNA and Cas protein has been reported to influence the efficiency of target editing in certain cellular recorders (23, 43). In the BioFuse system, two sgRNAs are used: psgRNA, which directs ABE8e-dCas9 protein to the push sites, and ssgRNA, which guides the ABE8e-dCas9 protein to the switch site promoter region. To evaluate how the expression levels and ratios of these sgRNAs affect the performance of BioFuse, we constructed a series of BioFuse Off-9 plasmids with various promoters to express psgRNA and ssgRNA at different levels (Fig. 5C). Our results showed that PS119 (psgRNA: ssgRNA = 1:1) exhibited the lowest average editing efficiency (27.1%), whereas PS110 (psgRNA: ssgRNA = 1:0.33) showed the highest average editing efficiency (96.5%) at day 7 (Fig. 5D and fig. S8), indicating that an appropriate ratio of psgRNA and ssgRNA can improve the editing efficacy of BioFuse. Then, we induced various expression levels of the ABE-dCas9 protein using a T7-lac promoter with different IPTG concentrations (0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 mM). The results demonstrated that increased expression levels of the Cas9-ABE protein led to enhanced editing efficiency of BioFuse (fig. S9, A and B).

In addition, we observed that the strains carrying On-3 exhibited a lower switch site editing frequency compared with those carrying Off-3 (fig. S10A). This phenomenon is likely due to differences in survival pressures (44) and competitive interactions (45) between subpopulations with varying levels of exogenous protein expression. To further investigate the underlying mechanism, we developed an enhanced OnE promoter, which exhibited a 29% increased expression level compared with the On promoter when activated (fig. S1, D to F). Our results revealed that OnE-5–PS110 exhibited lower switch site editing efficiency compared to On-5–PS110 (fig. S10B). Furthermore, we cocultured two E. coli strains: one carrying mCherry overexpressed plasmid (Off-CK) and the other carrying mCherry silenced plasmid (Off-∆GG) (fig. S1A) and found that the mCherry-silenced strain became dominant in the population after 48 hours (fig. S10C). These findings suggest that competition between subpopulations may also be a factor influencing the propulsion of the BioFuse system.

To further improve the robustness of long BioFuse in controlling gene expression, we tested various psgRNA/ssgRNA ratios for BioFuse Off-5 and On-5, both of which showed high rates of incomplete editing during propulsion (Fig. 2, B and D, and figs. S2F and S3F). In BioFuse Off-5, we incorporated an additional J23119-psgRNA framework, while in BioFuse On-5, we transcript the ssgRNA with J23110, and they both exhibited effective site editing as well as improved activation or deactivation of mCherry expression (Fig. 5, E to G).

Building on these improvements, we constructed a long BioFuse (Off-9–PS110) and analyzed the editing efficiency of nine biological replicates for each target site over time (Fig. 5H). The result indicated that the extended BioFuse exhibited robust stability and a highly broad dynamic editing range. Furthermore, we determined the mean editing efficiency of the push targets in relation to culture time by using an exponential decay model (Fig. 5I). The average editing efficiency of the push sites steadily increased over time, suggesting that BioFuse can reliably edit the Fuse-like sequence and is scalable for regulating gene temporal expression. These advancements allowed us to precisely calibrate BioFuse’s reaction time, enhancing its scalability and robustness.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed a powerful transcription regulatory apparatus, BioFuse, which enables precise temporal control of gene expression by presetting its length. By optimizing fuse-like sequences and fine-tuning sgRNA ratios, BioFuse precisely regulates the expression of multiple genes in bacteria. This apparatus addresses the limitations of classical gene regulation methods, offering a more straightforward modular design with minimal resource requirements. We also successfully switched the growth mode and production mode in industrial fermentation strains using the BioFuse, resulting in a significant enhancement of lycopene yield (P < 0.001), demonstrating its strong potential for industrial applications.

In previous researches, sequential gene editing technologies have notably advanced the capacity to dynamically track and record multiple molecular events at tandem DNA targets, such as DOMINO (24) and DNA Typewriter (25). In addition, sequential activation of Cas9 editing at different DNA targets has also been enabled through specially design of engineered guide RNAs (proGuides), offering controlled timing for gene knockouts (46). While these existing systems are optimized for their specific applications, the BioFuse system introduces an approach that enhances both scalability and stability, rendering it particularly suitable for applications requiring extended and precise temporal gene regulation. BioFuse uses ABE to introduce new PAMs at the initial target site, thereby sequentially activating subsequent targets and enabling precise control over the expression of downstream genes. This architectural design ensures that fully edited targets become incompatible with sgRNAs, thus eliminating competition with unedited targets. Compared to other sequential editing systems, BioFuse is distinguished by its substantial scalability across a wide range of timescales, from hours to days. Besides, we systematically investigated factors influencing the editing efficiency of BioFuse, such as the ratio of psgRNA to ssgRNA, which can be optimized to improve the system’s stability and controllability. Further elucidation of how these factors affect BioFuse’s functionality is deserved to develop predictive models that enhance its performance and expand its potential applications.

In recent years, engineered bacteria have been widely used in medical research (3, 4, 37, 38). The utilization of multiplex BioFuse systems allows for the precise regulation of multiple gene expressions, thereby facilitating the design of complex and orchestrated strategies. For example, BioFuse could be used to control colonization-related genes under specific situations, which were involved in bacterial colonization in hosts (47, 48). It could also be used for the timed release of therapeutic agents through bacterial autolysis. Moreover, BioFuse does not require external inducers, allowing complex regulatory frameworks without concerns about the pharmacokinetics or toxicity of inducers, offering a strategy for manipulating engineered bacteria in medical applications.

The BioFuse system holds great potential for further development and broader applications in biological systems beyond bacteria since ABE has been reported to work well across multiple domains of life (49–55). BioFuse may also serve as a filter for biological signal noises, which only outputs a signal when the strength of the input surpasses a specific threshold. BioFuse could enhance the robustness and stability of synthetic signal pathways and can precisely adjust the threshold by simply modifying the length. In addition, constructing BioFuse with emerging base editors could expand its programmability (56–59), making it applicable to a broader range of scenarios.

In conclusion, BioFuse offers a programmable, highly dynamic, and scalable apparatus for accurately regulating temporal gene expression. It operates autonomously without requiring manual intervention or external inducing signals, thus having great potential for applications in diversified areas across various hosts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains and cultural conditions

The cloning strain used in this study was E. coli Trans5α chemically competent cells from TransGen Biotech. Unless otherwise specified, liquid cultures were grown in LB medium (containing 10 g of NaCl, 5 g of yeast extract, and 10 g of tryptone). In contrast, solid cultures were maintained on LB agar plates (supplemented with 10 g of NaCl, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of tryptone, and 7.5 g of agar). Stock solutions of chloramphenicol (34 mg/liter), streptomycin (40 mg/liter), and IPTG (0.1 M) were prepared, and unless otherwise indicated, these stock solutions were added to the culture media at a 1:1000 dilution.

Plasmid construction

In the construction of plasmids, the ABclonal Gloria Nova HS 2 × Master Mix (RK20717) was used for the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the fragments (tables S1 to S5). Primers used for gene cloning and plasmid construction were listed in table S8. The desired products were purified after amplification using the Sangon Biotech’s SanPrep Column DNA Gel Extraction Kit. The purified fragments were ligated into plasmid backbones using ABclonal’s 2 × MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (RK21020). The resulting plasmids were transformed into Trans5α chemically competent cells, and selection was carried out on LB agar plates containing either chloramphenicol (34 mg/liter) or streptomycin (40 mg/liter), depending on the plasmid resistance profile. Subsequently, single colonies from the selection plates were transferred to LB medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 24 hours. The resulting liquid cultures were subjected to Sanger sequencing. Following sequence validation, plasmids were extracted using the ABclonal’s AFTSpin EndoFree Plasmid Mini Kit (RK30102) and stored at −20°C. Bacterial strains were preserved at −80°C.

Competent cell preparation and cotransformation

To enhance the efficacy of cotransformation with two plasmids and to mitigate the influence of impure cultures during experimentation, E. coli BL21(DE3) purchased from TransGen Biotech was prepared as a batch of ultracompetent cells using the Sangon Biotech’s Ultra-Competent Cell Preps Kit. For cotransformation, 100 μl of the ultracompetent cells were drawn into a 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tube, which was then placed on ice. Subsequently, 500 ng of each plasmid was introduced into the tube. Following a 30-min incubation period on ice, the tube was subjected to heat shock treatment in a 42°C water bath for 90 s. This was followed by a 5-min incubation period on ice. Subsequently, 500 μl of LB medium was added, and the tube was incubated on a shaker at 37°C and 220 rpm for 2 hours. Then, for T7lac-ABE strains, 200 μl of the culture was plated on LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol (34 mg/liter) and streptomycin (40 mg/liter). Following a 24-hour incubation period, individual colonies were selected for further passaging culture.

For ABE constant express strains, 200 μl of the culture was transferred to 10 ml of LB medium in a tube (lycopene production strain) or 500 ml of LB medium in a conical flask (autolysis strain). Then, the lycopene production strain in the tube was cultured in a shaker at 30°C and 220 rpm, while the autolysis strain in the conical flask was cultured in a shaker at 37°C and 220 rpm.

Bacterial passaging culture

Single colonies obtained from cotransformation were inoculated into 10 ml of LB medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (34 mg/liter) and streptomycin (40 mg/liter). The cultures were agitated at 37°C and 220 rpm. At 24-hour intervals, a 1/1000 volume (10 μl) of the culture was transferred to a fresh medium, with the transfer process taking less than 5 min. Following the transfer, 4 ml of the bacterial culture was retained. Subsequently, 500 μl was combined with 40% glycerol in a 1:1 ratio and stored at −80°C for future utilization. Furthermore, 1.5 ml of the culture was centrifuged at 12,000g for 1 min, after which the resulting bacterial pellet was collected. A 1.5-ml aliquot was used for Sanger sequencing, while 200 μl was used for fluorescence intensity measurement.

Fluorescence intensity measurement

A 200-μl sample from the culture medium was transferred to a 96-well microplate, and the fluorescence intensity of mCherry was quantified using a SpectraMax M5 multimode microplate reader with excitation at 587 nm and emission at 610 nm.

Sanger sequencing and site editing frequency analysis

BioFuse comprises tandem DNA cassettes with repetitive, fuse-like sequences. Quantifying gene editing efficiency in such regions is challenging due to the limitations of next-generation sequencing (NGS), which struggles with accurate read mapping in repetitive sequences (60–62). NGS exhibits reduced accuracy as repeat numbers increase, yielding fewer reliable reads (see folder “Off-359-NGS” in the dataset: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15278618). In contrast, Sanger sequencing offers a cost-effective and precise alternative for characterizing editing efficiency in repetitive regions (24). Bacterial samples obtained through passing culture was amplified using site-specific primers (table S6) with Gloria Nova HS 2 × PCR Mix, and the resulting amplicons were sequenced by Tsingke Biotech Co. Ltd. using an Applied Biosystems 3730xl sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., MA, USA). To analyze editing frequencies, we developed San-analysis, a Python script for processing Sanger sequencing chromatograms. This tool automates the analysis of chromatogram files from a specified input directory, calculating site-specific editing frequencies and exporting results to an Excel file. Chromatogram data were analyzed with San-analysis as detailed in note S1 and illustrated in fig. S11. High-throughput sequencing

Bacterial samples obtained through passaging culture were lysed by heating at 95°C for 3 min. Then, PCR amplification of each bacterial culture samples was performed using the following primers: G-NGS-1: 5′-GGAGTGAGTACGGTGTGCCGC-ATGCCCAGGCAGGTCTTTAA-3′ and G-NGS-2: 5′-GAGTTGGATG CTGGATGGCCAACGGAATTAGTGCCGATGGCTAA-3′ (table S7) with Gloria Nova HS 2 × PCR Mix for NGS produced by ABclonal Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The amplified samples were then subjected to high-throughput sequencing and analysis using the Hi-Tom HTS sequencing service. The sequencing was performed using an Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, USA), and the result was analyzed by Hi-Tom online service (http://121.40.237.174/Hi-TOM/) (63). The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Zenodo database under the accession number: 10.5281/zenodo.15278618.

Fluorescence imaging

Bacterial colony fluorescence was captured using a Zeiss SteREO Lumar.V12 epifluorescence microscope equipped with an X-CITE 120 lamp (Lumen Dynamics). The Lumar 38 filter was used for green fluorescent protein detection (excitation, 470/40 nm; emission, 525/50 nm), and the Lumar 31 filter was used for red fluorescent protein detection (excitation, 565/30 nm; emission, 620/60 nm). Fluorescence values were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.53k).

Lycopene extraction and analysis

Lycopene production from engineering E. coli strains was measured according to the method described previously (64). Lycopene was extracted from the engineering E. coli strains as follows: Cell pellets obtained were extracted three times with 1 ml of acetone/methanol (7:2, v/v) in the dark, and the supernatant was collected. Lycopene in the pooled extracts was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography using a Waters Alliance 2690 system and a Hypersil C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Alltech). A mixture of acetonitrile/methanol/2-propanol (40:50:10, v/v/v) was used as the mobile phase. Lycopene was identified by retention time and absorption spectra compared with the standard compound. The lycopene yield (milligrams per liter) was determined from the area under the peak detected at 472 nm using a calibration curve of the lycopene standard (Sigma-Aldrich).

SEM analysis

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 2000g for 3 min and washed three times with deionized water. The cells were then processed according to the previously described method (65). The samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The dehydrated samples were coated with gold-palladium and observed using GeminiSEM 560 (ZEISS, Germany).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Fresh bacterial cultures (100 μl) were inoculated in LB medium and grown to an OD600 (optical density at 600 nm) of 1.0. Total RNA was extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech), followed by immediate reverse transcription of the total RNA into cDNA with the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen). The cDNA was stored at −20°C for future use. The cDNA was then diluted fivefold and used as a template for quantitative reverse transcription PCR with the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara). The amplification reaction was carried out on the STRAGENE Mx3005P system. The average of three replicate cycle threshold (CT) values was used for samples with consistent melting curves across treatment groups. The relative change in target gene transcription levels was calculated using the 2 -ΔΔct method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical parameters, including the definitions, number of experiments, deviations, statistical significance, and analysis method, are reported in the related figures and corresponding figure legends. Statistical analysis including data deviations and statistical significance was performed using the OriginPro software (2024b; OriginLab Corporation, MA, USA). Unless otherwise specified in figure legends, data are presented as mean ± SD, based on at least three independent biological replicates. P values were calculated using two-tailed Student’s t test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Images in Figs. 1, 3A, and 4A were created in BioRender.
