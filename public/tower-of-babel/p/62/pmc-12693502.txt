An SpC editor targeting pre-mRNA splicing for precise CRISPR control and enhanced antitumor efficacy
Authors: Xiaorui Shi, Chong Hu, Lijun Jia, Zhen Lei, Bin Guo, Jie Zhou, Fu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe CRISPR/Cas9 system is a powerful genome editing tool that has the potential to be applied to a variety of biomedical applications. Despite the considerable potential of this gene editing technology, there are numerous safety concerns including the possibility of unpredictable off-target effects. The splicing process, which involves the removal of introns from pre-mRNA and the alignment of exons to produce mature transcripts, is a critical step in gene expression in most eukaryotes. In this study, we present a spliceosome-responsive CRISPR/Cas9 (SpC) editor that utilizes the splicing inhibitor pladienolide B (PB) to regulate pre-mRNA splicing and control the expression of the anti-CRISPR protein AcrIIA4, thereby modulating the activity of the Cas9 nuclease. This approach allows for precise regulation of the gene editing process, thereby effectively mitigating off-target effects. The reliability and robustness of the SpC editor were demonstrated through in vitro and in vivo bioluminescence imaging. Furthermore, a dual-target sgRNA was designed to target the diphtheria toxin A gene, resulting in apoptosis induction and growth inhibition of tumor cells across various types of cancer cells. Our results indicate that this SpC editor has the capacity to precisely regulate tumor cell growth, thus providing new insights and significant implications for cancer gene therapy.

Introduction

Gene therapy for tumors is a treatment approach that involves the direct targeting of cancer cells or the immune system through the application of genetic engineering technology, thereby effecting a gradual transformation in the landscape of tumor treatment [1, 2]. With advancements in the gene delivery technology and gene editing tools, gene therapy is expected to become a fundamental component of precision cancer medicine in the future [3, 4]. The CRISPR/Cas (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated) system is a revolutionary gene-editing tool inspired by an adaptive immune defense mechanism that bacteria and archaea have evolved over hundreds of millions of years [5, 6]. It is widely utilized in gene therapy for various diseases, including tumors. By mimicking this natural system, CRISPR was successfully combined with the Cas9 nuclease, creating “molecular scissors” that precisely target and modify specific DNA sequences using single-guide RNA (sgRNA) [7, 8]. This technology, characterized by its high efficiency, programmability, and relatively low operational cost, has fundamentally transformed the research paradigm in the field of gene editing [9, 10]. Although the CRISPR/Cas system has demonstrated disruptive potential in medical treatments, such as the repair of genetic defects and the construction of disease models [11, 12], there are also challenges associated with off-target effects and safety concerns. This necessitates the development of more efficient and controllable CRISPR systems [13–15].

Pre-mRNA splicing is a crucial component of gene expression and contributes to proteome diversity in higher organisms [16]. Utilizing the pre-mRNA splicing process to achieve conditional control of gene expression is essential for studying various aspects of the cell or organism. By incorporating the 5' splice site into the stem structure of a tetracycline-sensing aptamer, researchers have developed a synthetic switch that regulates gene expression through variable splicing. When applied in Caenorhabditis elegans, this switch enabled high levels of functional protein expression with minimal background levels [17]. Another approach, based on a universal splitting intron, has led to the development of a class of transcriptional splicing regulators known as SENTRs for gene regulation [18]. The simultaneous use of multiple orthologous SENTRs to regulate a gene was successfully demonstrated in Escherichia coli. Additionally, intron-mediated trans-splicing denoisers were employed to design fluorescent biosensors with excellent detection limits [19]. These advancements illustrate the wide range of applications derived from mRNA splicing regulation in biotechnology. Furthermore, the central role of splicing in regulating human gene expression and its association with various diseases, combined with its biochemical complexity, makes it an attractive target for small-molecule therapeutics [20]. Pladienolide B (PB), a splicing regulator, serves as an effective inhibitor of tumor cell proliferation [21, 22]. By directly targeting spliceosome-associated protein 130 (SAP130) in a dose-dependent manner, it inhibits the spliceosome factor 3B subunit (SF3B1) and disrupts the pre-mRNA interaction with U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (U2 snRNP) [23]. Currently, small-molecule drugs that modulate spliceosome activity are widely employed in the treatment of cancer and other diseases [24, 25].

To effectively control the activity of Cas9 and minimize the off-target effects and unnecessary background leakage, we propose a spliceosome-responsive CRISPR/Cas9 (SpC) editor. This editor regulates the expression of the Cas9 nuclease expression through spliceosome activity, thereby facilitating target gene editing. The anti-CRISPR (Acr) protein AcrIIA4 [26–28] was inserted into the spliceosome introns, and the splicing was regulated by the PB inhibitor. The feasibility and reliability of the SpC editor in regulating Cas9 activity were evaluated using bioluminescence imaging. Subsequently, the design of sgRNA was enhanced, resulting in the development of a dual-target sgRNA that targets the diphtheria toxin A-chain gene (DTA) to further minimize off-target effects. Finally, the SpC editor system demonstrated its efficient and precise gene editing capabilities in various tumor cells by inducing tumor cell apoptosis and achieving gene therapy in tumor cells.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and sequences

To construct a plasmid for regulating the expression of the AcrⅡA4 gene using the spliceosome, the exon 6–intron 6–exon 7 sequence of human phosphoglycerate isomerase (TPI) minigene was synthesized. The AcrⅡA4 gene was then inserted into intron 6 of TPI, and the AcrⅡA4-containing TPI sequence was subsequently cloned into the BamHI/EcoRI site of the pcDNA3.1 vector, resulting in the construction of the Sp-Acr (splicing-AcrⅡA4) plasmid. To construct sgRNA expression plasmids targeting firefly luciferase (Fluc), three distinct groups of sgRNA sequences were designed and subsequently inserted into the PHB-pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro vector. When constructing therapeutic plasmids that express functional genes, the DTA gene was inserted into the BamHI/EcoRI site of the pcDNA3.1 vector to create the downstream tumor apoptosis-inducing plasmid pDTA, which is regulated by Cas9 nuclease. The sgRNA1 and sgRNA2 targeting DTA were inserted into the pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) vector to construct the dual-target plasmid sgRNADTA. The sequences of sgRNA are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells, HepG2 cells, HeLa cells, A549 cells, and 4T1 cells were maintained in our laboratory. Cell identity was confirmed by STR profiling, and mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested with negative results. HEK293T cells, HepG2 cells, HeLa cells, and A549 cells were cultivated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Hyclone). 4T1 cells were cultivated in RPMI 1640 medium (Hyclone) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were incubated on plates for 24 h with culture medium prior to transfection. The plasmids were transfected into cells using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Luciferase activity assay

Two hours after cell transfection, PB (Cayman Chemical), dissolved in DMSO (MP Biomedicals), was added to the cells, which were continuously treated for 24 h. After removing the supernatant from the cells, the attached cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma–Aldrich). Subsequently, add 100 μl of 1 × cell lysis buffer and lyse the cells at room temperature while shaking for 20 min. For the luciferase activity assay, mix 10 μl of cell lysis buffer with 10 μl of Luciferase Reaction Reagent (TransGen Biotech). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, luciferase activity was measured using the GloMax 20/20 luminometer (Promega).

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

HEK293T cells were cultured in six-well plates for 24 h. The experimental group was transfected with the plasmid for 2 h and subsequently treated with PB for an additional 24 h. Total RNA was extracted from the cell samples using Trizol (Invitrogen), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse transcribed and synthesized from the total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNA product was utilized for PCR amplification, employing the designed Sp-Acr primer sequences: forward (F): 5′-AGGTGGAGTGGGCTGAGAA-3′; reverse (R): 5′-GGGAAGGGAGCAGAACAAG-3′. The cycling conditions for PCR were as follows: 94°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 62°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. A final extension at 72°C was performed for 7 min. The resulting product was loaded onto a 10% agarose gel and electrophoresed for 30 min at a voltage of 40–50 V, followed by photographic analysis.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

The animal experiments were conducted and approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Six-week-old female BALB/c mice were selected for the in vivo imaging experiments. HEK293T cells transfected with the plasmids of the system were subcutaneously implanted into the right hind limb of mice after being treated with PB for 24 h. Transfected cells without PB treatment were implanted in the left hind limb as controls. Following an intraperitoneal injection of 15 mg/ml d-luciferin (potassium salt) (Maokangbio) in the mice for 6–8 min, bioluminescence signals in vivo were collected using the Xenogen Lumina Ⅱ system (Caliper Life Sciences). The bioluminescence units in the region of interest were quantified by photons per second per cubic centimeter per steradian (p/s/cm2/sr).

Cell proliferation assay

The viability of the cells was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8 kit, DiyiBio). Approximately 5 × 103 cells per well were inoculated into a 96-well plate and pre-cultured for 24 h. The plasmids in different groups were transfected into cells for 48 h. Then, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm for each well was determined using a microplate reader. At least three duplicate wells were prepared for each group.

Wound healing assay

The migratory capacity of the cells was assessed using a wound healing assay. A suitable number of cells were inoculated into a 12-well plate. Once the cells were fully adherent and reached a density of 70%–90%, the cells were scratched with the tip of a 10-µl pipette and washed twice with PBS to remove any debris. The cells were then subjected to transfection or treatment with PB. Microscopic images were captured and recorded at 0, 24, and 48 h, respectively. The extent of wound healing was analyzed and quantified using ImageJ software. The scratch width method was used to calculate the cell migration rate. The calculation is as follows: Cell migration rate = [(scratch width at 0 h) − (scratch width at 24 or 48 h)] / (scratch width at 0 h) × 100%.

Transwell analysis

After resuspending the specified cells in serum-free medium, 200 μl of the suspension was added to the upper chamber of an 8-μm Transwell chamber (Millipore), which had been evenly coated with Matrigel and solidified at 37°C for 1 h. Then, 800 μl of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. Following incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h, non-invading cells in the upper chamber were gently removed with a cotton swab and washed three times with PBS. The cells in the lower chamber were fixed with 4% methanol for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Five random fields were selected and photographed under an inverted microscope.

Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis analysis

A549 or HepG2 cells were inoculated in six-well plates. When the cell density reached 70%–90%, transfected with the plasmids of system. Subsequently, PB was added, and the cells were incubated for 48 h. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and digested with trypsin. The cell suspension was then transferred to a 1.5-ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min. Following centrifugation, 195 μl of Annexin V-FITC binding solution, 5 μl of Annexin V-FITC (Beyotime Biotechnology), and 10 μl of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution were added sequentially. The cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The apoptosis rate of the cells was analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACS Calibur), and the data were processed with FlowJo 10.8 software.

Cell cycle assay

Cell cycle determination was performed using a Cell Cycle Analysis kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). The transfected or drug-treated cells were digested with trypsin and subsequently collected in clean 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes. Centrifuge the samples at 1000 × g for 5 min to precipitate the cells. Resuspend and wash the cells twice with PBS, then add 1 ml of 70% ethanol while keeping the tubes in an ice bath, and fix the samples at 4°C overnight. After centrifugation, precipitating, and washing the cells, add 0.5 ml of PI staining solution to each tube and incubate at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence was detected at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm using flow cytometry (BD FACS Calibur), and quantitative data were analyzed with FlowJo 10.8 software.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data from at least three independent experiments are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA were used to analyze statistical significance between experimental groups, followed by Tukey’s test to correct for multiple comparisons. Statistically significant differences were expressed as follows: *P < .05, **P < .01, and ***P < .001.

Results

Design of spliceosome-activated Cas9 editor

To achieve precise and tunable gene editing within cells, an SpC editor was developed, which is regulated by the small molecule PB. Specifically, the pre-mRNA spliceosome is combined with the Cas9 antagonistic protein AcrⅡA4 within the CRISPR–Cas9 system. By modulating the splicing process with PB, the expression of the AcrⅡA4 protein in the splicing product is controlled, thereby regulating the activity of the Cas9 enzyme. The work principle is as follows (Scheme 1): The human phosphoglycerate isomerase (TPI) minigene containing exon 6–intron 6–exon 7 was constructed. The AcrⅡA4 gene was then inserted into intron 6 of TPI to obtain Sp-Acr vector. In the absence of PB treatment, the pre-mRNA of Sp-Acr is spliced normally. This process removes the intron containing AcrⅡA4 and connects exon 6 and exon 7. Consequently, with AcrⅡA4 deleted, the Cas9 nuclease is expressed normally and functions effectively. It targets and binds to the sgRNA, forming a complex that cleaves the target gene, resulting in a downregulation of the gene of interest (OFF state). In contrast, when PB is administered, the splicing of Sp-Acr is inhibited, leading to the retention of introns. The AcrⅡA4 gene contained within the intron is expressed, which inhibits the activity of the Cas9 protein. This prevents the formation of the complex between Cas9 and target gene, thereby the expression of the gene of interest is upregulated (ON state). Thus, this editor indirectly regulates the gene editing process by modulating the activity of the spliceosome through splicing inhibitors.

Schematic representation of spliceosome-activated Cas9 editor. When PB is absent, splicing occurs normally. When AcrⅡA4 is inactivated, the CRISPR system executes its gene-editing function, and the downstream gene of interest (GOI) is not expressed (OFF). In contrast, following the addition of PB, splicing becomes abnormal. When AcrⅡA4 is activated, the activity of Cas9 is inhibited, and the expression of the downstream GOI is turned ON.

The feasibility and optimization of SpC-F editor

We initially assessed the feasibility of the Sp-Acr reporter by bioluminescence imaging, selecting firefly luciferase (Fluc) as the downstream target gene. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, in the absence of PB induction, AcrⅡA4 is spliced out with the intron, and Cas9 is normally expressed, cleaving downstream Fluc under the guidance of sgRNA. At this stage, the bioluminescence signal is relatively low. In contrast, following PB treatment, the splicing of Sp-Acr is abnormal. The AcrⅡA4 protein retained within the intron antagonized Cas9, allowing downstream Fluc to be expressed normally, resulting in an increased bioluminescence signal. To examine the gene editing efficiency of the SpC-F system, three sgRNAs targeting Fluc were designed. The pGL-3 luciferase plasmid, Cas9 plasmid, and these three sgRNAs were transfected into HEK293T cells. Then the luciferase activity was measured 24 h later (Fig. 1B). The results demonstrated that the bioluminescence intensities in the sgRNA groups were significantly lower than that of the without sgRNA control group, suggesting that the complex formed by the combination of Cas9 and sgRNA effectively cleaved the target gene and led to a substantial downregulation of Fluc expression. Of note, the bioluminescence activity in the sgRNA1 group was lower than that in the other two sgRNA groups, indicating that sgRNA1 exhibited the most effective targeting. Consequently, sgRNA1 was selected for subsequent experiments.

The transfection condition for the plasmids was then optimized to achieve the most effective editing with the minimum packaging load. The SpC-F system containing various plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells (Fig. 1C), and PB was added for induction 2 h later. Under transfected cells with equal amounts of Cas9, sgRNA, and pGL-3, the 500 ng of Sp-Acr construct exhibited the highest luciferase signal increase, which was 2.8-fold (Fig. 1D). When transfected with equal amounts of Cas9, sgRNA and Sp-Acr, 200 ng of pGL-3 was sufficient to achieve the maximum increase of Fluc activity (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, to investigate whether the gene targeting efficiency of the complex formed by the combination of Cas9 and sgRNA is influenced by the plasmid ratio, the ratio of sgRNA to Cas9 was optimized while maintaining the optimal concentrations of Sp-Acr and pGL-3 plasmids. As shown in Fig. 1F, when the plasmid ratio of sgRNA to Cas9 was 1:1, the activation capability of the system reaching 3.9-fold. Therefore, this optimized ratio was utilized in subsequent experiments.

In vitro and in vivo validation of SpC-F editor

To assess the specific response of SpC-F system to PB, PB and other inhibitors were added into the SpC-F transfected HEK293T cells. As shown in Fig. 2A, the expression of Fluc was significantly increased following PB treatment but not in other groups. We then investigated the regulatory effect of PB drugs on the spliceosome through reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis (Fig. 2B-C). Following PB treatment, the inhibiton of splicing process led to a reduction in the spliced mature products. These findings confirm that PB can effectively regulate the dynamics of the spliceosome, thereby influencing the activity of AcrⅡA4 within its introns.

To further investigate the dose dependence of the SpC-F system on PB, different concentrations of PB (0, 10, 100, 1000 nM) were added to the HEK293T cells transfected with SpC-F. However, the luciferase activity did not increase when the drug concentration was 1000 nM (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We speculated that the drug concentration (1000 nM) was too high and the range was too wide, which may have resulted in a cytotoxic effect of PB on cells. Consequently, we narrowed the drug concentrations, adjusting the range from 0 to 500 nM (Supplementary Fig. S1B and Fig. 2D). We found that luciferase activity increased with the PB concentration within the range of 0 to 20 nM. This indicates that the SpC-F editor is regulated by PB in a dose-dependent manner within this concentration range.

In order to validate the feasibility of in vivo regulation of gene editing with SpC-F, HEK293T-Fluc cells transfected with the SpC-F were implanted on both sides of the hind limbs of mice and treated with either PB or not (Fig. 2E). As illustrated in Fig. 2F, there is a significant increase (6.2-fold) in the bioluminescence signal in the PB-treated group compared to the control group. These findings were consistent with the luciferase results obtained in cells, indicating that the PB-regulated SpC-F editor can achieve precise and controllable gene editing in cells and animal models.

Construction and optimization of SpC-D editor for gene therapy

After successfully verifying the feasibility of SpC-F editor both in vitro and in vivo, we envision utilizing this system to regulate tumor cells and implement gene therapy. DTA is a bacterial toxin produced by the pathogenic strain Corynebacterium diphtheriae, known for its potent cytotoxic effects. By catalyzing the adenosine diphosphate ribosylation and inactivation of elongation factor 2 (eEF2), DTA can inhibit protein synthesis and induce apoptosis at very low concentrations [29]. Therefore, we utilized the function of DTA to develop a spliceosome-responsive Cas9 editor (SpC-D) aimed at inducing apoptosis in cancer cells (Fig. 3A). Similarly, when treated with PB, the abnormal splicing of Sp-Acr activates the expression of AcrⅡA4, which prevents the cleavage of the target gene by the CRISPR system. Concurrently, the expression of the DTA protein increases, leading to apoptosis in tumor cells. Furthermore, we optimized the sgRNA targeting DTA to construct a dual-target sgRNADTA (Fig. 3B), aiming to potentially reduce the off-target effects of sgRNA and improve the accuracy of gene editing.

(B) Scheme of SpC-D system plasmids: a plasmid expressing the AcrⅡA4, a plasmid expressing two sgRNAs targeting the DTA, a plasmid expressing the Cas9 protein, and a plasmid expressing the DTA protein. We first verified the reliability of the SpC-D in inhibiting cell growth. After transfecting the SpC-D into A549 cells, we found that the cell viability of the PB treatment group was significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig. S2A). We also assessed the impact of DTA or PB on the A549 cells. Although a certain inhibitory effect on cell growth was observed when transfected with only pDTA plasmids or treated with only PB, the inhibitory effect was most pronounced in cells treated with both SpC-D and PB (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Subsequently, we optimized the ratio of sgRNA to Cas9 plasmid, testing ratios of 1:2, 2:1, and 1:1 across four different tumor cell lines. As shown in Fig. 3C–F, the SpC-D effectively inhibited cell proliferation in various tumor cells. Among them, when the ratio of sgRNA to Cas9 was 1:2, the most significant decrease in cell viability was observed. Notably, the most substantial effects were found in A549 and HepG2 cells, which exhibited ~2.7–5.7-fold downregulation. Therefore, we prioritized these two cell types and selected a 1:2 ratio of sgRNA to Cas9 for subsequent experiments.

We then investigated the impact of the concentration of the therapeutic gene DTA on the cells. By transfecting with the same amount of SpC-D system plasmids but varying concentrations of pDTA plasmids in A549 and HepG2 cells, we observed that the cytotoxic effect in the PB group became more pronounced with increasing pDTA concentration (Fig. 3G and H). However, it was also noted that at excessively high pDTA concentrations (800 or 1000 ng), the cell viability in the control group was inhibited to some extent. We speculate that this may be due to the saturation of the CRISPR editing effect when plasmid concentrations are excessively high, leading to a degree of DTA overexpression that is not regulated by the system, consequently producing a background cytotoxic effect on the cells.

SpC-D system enables regulating the tumor cell behaviors

To further evaluate the impact of SpC-D on tumor cell behavior, we assessed its effect on tumor cell migration using a wound healing assay. The changes in the scratch area of A549 (Fig. 4A) and HepG2 (Fig. 4C) cells among different treatment groups were recorded and quantified at 0, 24, and 48 h. It demonstrated that the migration rate of the single DTA group and the PB group was slower than that of the control group. Notably, the SpC-D + PB experimental group exhibited the lowest migration rate in both cell lines, showing an approximate 40% reduction compared to the control group in A549 cells at 48 h (Fig. 4B) and an approximate 35% reduction in HepG2 cells under the same conditions (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we evaluated the effect of the SpC-D on tumor cell invasion using transwell analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, the number of invasive cells decreased in both the single PB treatment group and the single DTA group for A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and HepG2 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Notably, the SpC-D + PB group exhibited the lowest number of invasive cells. These findings suggest that the activated expression of DTA may inhibit the migration and invasion of tumor cells following the treatment of PB.

The SpC-D editor system regulates the migration behavior of tumor cells. (A) A549 cells or (C) HepG2 cells treated as indicated and the scratches were photographed at 0, 24, and 48 h, respectively. Panels (B) and (D) were the quantitative analyses of panels (A) and (C), respectively. [Data are mean values ± SD (n = 3), ***P < .001.]

We then conducted flow cytometry analysis to examine the apoptosis in SpC-D transfected A549 cells before and after PB administration (Supplementary Fig. S4). The results indicated that the apoptosis rate of A549 cells increased to 60% after treatment with the PB. Subsequently, we established a single PB group and a single DTA group in both A549 (Fig. 5A) and HepG2 (Fig. 5B) cells to further investigate the effect of the SpC-D system on tumor cell apoptosis. Flow cytometry results demonstrated that the induction of tumor cell apoptosis was most pronounced in the SpC-D + PB experimental group, although a slight amount of apoptosis also occurred in the DTA or PB treatment groups. These results indicate that SpC-D can regulate the editing of the CRISPR system through spliceosome activity, effectively promoting the apoptosis of tumor cells. We then investigated whether apoptosis is accompanied by the changes in cell cycle. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, a significant reduction in the S phase and an increase in the G0/G1 phase were observed in the SpC-D + PB experimental group compared to the other group. We hypothesized that this cell cycle arrest result was primarily attributable to the intracellular expression of DTA, which inhibits protein synthesis and induces both cell progression blockade and apoptosis. Taken together, this SpC-D editor targeting DTA could significantly inhibit cell migration and induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in various tumor cells.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an SpC regulated by the small molecule PB. The AcrⅡA4 gene was incorporated into the spliceosome to control the expression of the Cas9 protein, while the splicing inhibitor PB was utilized to modulate the splicing of the spliceosome, thereby achieving dual control of target gene expression. We observed that this SpC-F editor exhibited a dose-dependent response to PB, with increased luciferase signals corresponding to higher concentrations of the drug. And in vivo imaging further demonstrated the system’s effectiveness. Moreover, the downstream DTA gene was modulated to induce cell migration, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest in tumor cells, thereby enabling the SpC-D editor to effectively kill tumor cells. In summary, we have successfully constructed a spliceosome-responsive CRISPR/Cas9 editor, which provides a potential tool for gene editing and therapy.
