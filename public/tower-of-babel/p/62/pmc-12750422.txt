A Dual Approach to Rehabilitation of an autoimmune ataxia : A case study involving the Adult-Onset Opsoclonus-Myoclonus-Ataxia Syndrome
Authors: Joon Sin Ser, Kee Jin Loh, Sing Mei Chung, Mei Xian Tan, Evangeline Jia Yun How, San San Tay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpsoclonus-Myoclonus-Ataxia Syndrome (OMAS) is a rare inflammatory neurologic condition predominantly described in pediatric populations. Adult-onset OMAS presents significant rehabilitation challenges because of limited literature and established treatment protocols. We report a middle-aged man with postinflammatory OMAS who underwent intensive inpatient rehabilitation using both conventional therapy and adjunct interventions, including robot-assisted gait training and hydrotherapy. The patient presented with characteristic opsoclonus, truncal ataxia, and bilateral dysmetria after an upper respiratory tract infection. After 22 days of intensive rehabilitation, the patient demonstrated remarkable functional improvement with a FIM efficiency of 1.77 and effectiveness of 0.84. Berg Balance Scale scores improved from 25 of 56 to 53 of 56, and the patient achieved modified independence in activities of daily living. While recovery occurred during rehabilitation, causality cannot be established, as improvement may also reflect the natural disease trajectory. Nevertheless, this case demonstrates the potential efficacy of combining conventional rehabilitation with targeted adjunct therapies for adult-onset OMAS, providing a rationale for a treatment strategy in rare neurologic conditions.KEYWORDS: Case report, Functional outcome, OMAS, Rehabilitation, RAGT, Inflammatory ataxia

Opsoclonus-Myoclonus-Ataxia Syndrome (OMAS) is a rare inflammatory neurologic syndrome characterized by a triad of opsoclonus (rapid, chaotic, multidirectional saccades without intersaccadic pauses), myoclonus, and ataxia seen in the pediatric population. While well-documented in pediatric populations as a paraneoplastic phenomenon associated with neuroblastoma, adult-onset OMAS remains exceptionally rare and is confined to isolated case reports and small case series.1 The clinical presentation in adults mirrors that observed in children, encompassing opsoclonus, balance impairment secondary to ataxia, myoclonus, tremor, and weight loss.1 However, adults rarely exhibit the behavioral abnormalities and sleep disturbances characteristic of pediatric cases.2

The etiology of adult-onset OMAS remains poorly understood, with reported associations in the literature, including postinfection states (Mycoplasma, Coronavirus disease 2019, Epstein-Barr virus) and paraneoplastic syndromes (small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, ovarian teratoma, Hodgkin’s lymphoma).3,4

Diagnosis relies on clinical criteria requiring at least 3 of the following features: opsoclonus myoclonus (diffuse or focal body), truncal titubation with or without ataxia, and other cerebellar signs.4 Despite treatment, which usually involves corticosteroids and immunoglobulins, Klaas et al1 reported that symptoms still rapidly progress over weeks, resulting in debilitating impairments, with patients reaching a neurologic nadir at 18 weeks. The majority require wheelchair assistance or 2-man assistance for ambulation, necessitating prolonged intensive inpatient rehabilitation.

Currently, no literature exists describing rehabilitation approaches for patients with OMAS. This case report represents the first comprehensive description, as far as we know, of a rehabilitation program for a patient with adult-onset OMAS. This case report aimed to provide a rationale for a treatment strategy integrating conventional and adjunctive therapeutic strategies for patients with rarer neurologic conditions.

Case presentation

This case report adheres to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines with appropriate informed consent obtained. Institutional Research Ethics Board approval was not required. This case report presents a middle-aged male patient in his 40s with premorbid independence in activities of daily living (ADL) and community ambulation without assistive devices. His medical history included chronic kidney disease (stage 3) and Philadelphia-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia treated with a haploidentical transplant in 2024, and he was maintained on dasatinib while in remission.

The patient worked as an aerospace technician, with occupational demands including manual labor, operation of heavy machinery, spray painting of equipment, drilling, and sawing of raw materials. His role required prolonged standing, lifting loads of 10-20 kg, and administrative tasks, including documentation and filing.

In early 2025, he presented with a 1-week history of nonvertiginous giddiness, upper limb tremors, gait unsteadiness, and visual blurring after an upper respiratory tract infection. He did not report weakness, altered sensation, or sleep disturbances.

Clinical examination demonstrated opsoclonus with multidirectional nystagmus, gait unsteadiness with a positive Romberg sign, truncal titubation, bilateral upper limb postural tremor, and dysmetria. The Medical Research Council score was 5 in all limbs. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed no intracranial abnormalities accounting for his symptoms. Nerve conduction studies suggested possible demyelinating polyneuropathy and radiculopathy. Lumbar puncture excluded infectious etiologies, demonstrated no cytoalbuminologic dissociation, and cytology was negative for malignancy. Antiganglioside antibodies were negative, and the malignancy screen was negative.

After exclusion of alternative diagnoses and the presence of characteristic opsoclonus, truncal titubation, and cerebellar ataxia, OMAS with postinflammatory etiology was diagnosed. He received a 5-day course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Despite treatment, his symptoms persisted, except for mild improvement in opsoclonus.

Prior to transfer to inpatient rehabilitation, he required 2-man assistance with an Arjo walker (a type of mobility aid requiring 2-person assistance with a U-shaped table for support and a suspension strap that prevents falling) for ambulation because of poor balance from truncal ataxia and postural tremors of the lower limb. He was transferred to inpatient rehabilitation after 14 days in the neurology ward. Initial inpatient rehabilitation assessment revealed mild opsoclonus, bilateral dysmetria, postural tremors, and truncal ataxia with preserved limb strength (Medical Research Council grade 5) and intact sensation. Functional assessment demonstrated a requirement for a 2-person minimum assistance to ambulate 12 meters with rested breaks in between, and contact-guard assistance for standing ADL.

Rehabilitation program

The rehabilitation program targeted specific impairments, including bilateral upper limb dysmetria and reduced fine motor control, truncal ataxia, impaired balance, and gait instability. Activity limitations encompassed the inability to perform his ADL that required fine motor control, including but not limited to buttoning his shirt, writing, and using his mobile phone.

A dual approach incorporating conventional and adjunctive therapy was implemented. He received 120 minutes daily of conventional physiotherapy and occupational therapy, supplemented by adjunct therapy, including robot-assisted gait training (RAGT; using a patient-guided suspension system robotic device, ANDAGO (manufactured by Hocoma) and hydrotherapy.

Conventional physiotherapy comprised trunk-limb coordination exercises, core and lumbopelvic stability training targeting truncal ataxia and balance impairment, standing balance exercises, and gait training addressing gait instability. Conventional occupational therapy included ADL retraining, trunk control training (in supine, sitting, and standing positions), weighted exercises, which consisted of using weights while performing reaching functional tasks such as writing and picking up objects, using proprioceptive feedback to improve limb ataxia and truncal balance, and dual task exercises combining physical and cognitive demands to enhance coordination and balance, such as simulated cooking classes and functional tasks while communicating (fig 1).

Timeline of various rehabilitation therapies during inpatient rehabilitation.

Nine hydrotherapy sessions of 60 minutes each were conducted between days 4 and 19 (3-4 sessions weekly), focusing on ambulation, side-step walking, and static exercises, including lunges. Nine daily ANDAGO sessions of 30 minutes each occurred between days 4 and 14 of rehabilitation admission (fig 1).

Outcome measures

Standardized assessments included FIM, evaluating functional disability across 18 items in motor and cognitive domains (maximum score=126); the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), assessing balance using 14 items (maximum score=56); the Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT), measuring fine and gross motor dexterity; the Comprehensive Coordination Scale (CCS), evaluating multisegment coordination (maximum score=69); EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) quality of life assessment; and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (table 1).

Outcome measures at baseline, on discharge, and at follow-up.

Baseline assessments on day 2 demonstrated a FIM score of 84 of 126, a BBS score of 25 of 56, PPT scores of 9 (right hand) and 11 (left hand), both below normal ranges, an assembly score of 16 (below normal), a CCS score of 54 of 69, HADS D1A1, and EQ-5D-5L scores indicating significant mobility limitations with a self-perceived health rating of 80 (table 1).

Opsoclonus resolved by the end of the first week of inpatient rehabilitation, with slight improvements in truncal ataxia, dysmetria, and balance. After completion of ANDAGO on day 14, ambulation improved to walking independently; however, he still required supervision for stair climbing. Hence, ANDAGO was stopped, and the focus was shifted to endurance and stair climbing practice.

After completing hydrotherapy at the end of 3 weeks of inpatient rehabilitation, he achieved modified independence for his ADL and was able to ambulate steadily and independently without an assistive device. Truncal ataxia, postural tremors, and dysmetria resolved, with marked balance improvement. He was discharged after 22 days of inpatient rehabilitation, with a FIM score of 124 of 126 (efficiency=1.77 and effectiveness=0.84), a BBS score of 53 of 56, PPT scores of 13 (right hand) and 15 (left hand) within normative ranges, an assembly score of 22 (below normal), a CCS score of 64 of 69, HADS D1A0, and EQ-5D-5L scores indicating complete functional independence with a self-perceived health rating of 100. No adverse events occurred during ANDAGO or hydrotherapy interventions (table 1).

Follow-up

Two-month postdischarge outpatient review demonstrated continued improvement with successful environmental reintegration.

Assessments showed a FIM score of 126 out of 126, a BBS score of 56 of 56, a CCS score of 66 of 69, HADS D1A0, and EQ-5D-5L scores indicating maintained functional independence with a self-perceived health rating of 95. He was certified fit to return to work with light duties and gradual resumption of regular occupational responsibilities over 2-3 months (table 1).

Discussion

Rehabilitation of rare neurologic conditions presents significant challenges because of limited available guidelines and resources, contrasting markedly with established protocols for common conditions such as stroke and spinal cord injury.5 This case demonstrates the potential efficacy of a dual approach combining conventional and adjunctive rehabilitation strategies.

His remarkable improvement, reflected by a FIM efficiency of 1.77, substantially exceeds established benchmarks for neurologic rehabilitation. FIM efficiency exceeding 0.6 is considered clinically significant, indicating effective functional improvement relative to the length of stay.6 Comparative studies report an average FIM efficiency of 0.61-0.64 for patients with stroke in inpatient rehabilitation settings, highlighting the exceptional outcomes achieved in this case.6,7

The combined approach in this case study had a longer total duration of daily therapy (180–210min) compared with conventional therapy (120min) alone, potentially contributing to the marked improvement in the patient’s function. However, therapy duration alone is unlikely to fully account for his outcome. This is supported by a study by Kimura et al,8 who found that there was no statistical association between duration of daily therapy and FIM gain in younger patients with stroke below 70 years old.

The ANDAGO is classified as a patient-guided suspension system and allows overground ambulation.9 RAGT was selected based on extensive literature supporting its efficacy as adjunct therapy in stroke, multiple sclerosis, and spinal cord injury, with documented improvements reported in rare conditions, including Becker muscular dystrophy.10,11 A meta-analysis demonstrated that RAGT, by providing adequate training intensity and duration, significantly improved balance outcomes in patients with stroke.12 Given the absence of specific literature on OMAS rehabilitation, evidence from neurologically similar conditions was used as the closest therapeutic approximation.

The proposed mechanisms underlying RAGT efficacy include somatosensory facilitation and repetitive lateral weight shifting, which enhance gait symmetry and muscle coordination, particularly demonstrating superior balance outcomes when combined with conventional physiotherapy in patients with infratentorial stroke.13 The harness safety system and partial weight support facilitate implicit motor learning by enabling patients to focus on gait pattern correction without fear of falling.14 This approach allows for high-intensity task-specific training with increased repetition frequency, potentially promoting neuroplasticity and motor recovery through sustained practice of physiological movement patterns.

Hydrotherapy selection was informed by evidence demonstrating functional improvements, enhanced joint mobility, balance enhancement, and pain reduction in rheumatic conditions and chronic low back pain.15 Balance improvements have also been reported in patients with Parkinson’s disease after hydrotherapy.16 The marked BBS improvement from 25 of 56 to 54 of 56 supports hydrotherapy’s efficacy in this case. We postulate that the water resistance during movement and water flow around the body provides continuous neurofeedback, enhancing proprioception, balance control, and motor coordination.

These findings highlight the promise of adjunct rehabilitation strategies targeting specific impairments, though further research is required for validation. Had opsoclonus persisted beyond the first week, potential interventions might have included virtual reality or neurofeedback for eye movement control, as described in vestibulopathy rehabilitation literature.17

Although the patient improved during rehabilitation, attributing recovery solely to the rehabilitation program is not possible. As noted in prior literature, patients with this condition may have some level of spontaneous recovery after reaching clinical nadir.1 The patient might also have had a delayed response to the IVIG received. The observations should therefore be interpreted with caution, and future studies are needed to better disentangle the contributions of natural recovery, immunotherapy, and rehabilitation interventions.

Study limitations

This single case report limits the generalizability of the findings. The patient’s relatively young age, good premorbid function, and absence of significant comorbidities may have contributed to favorable outcomes. A major limitation of this case report is the inability to determine causality. It remains unclear which specific components of the rehabilitation program contributed to recovery. Improvement may have been because of the natural disease trajectory (eg, reaching nadir) or a delayed therapeutic effect of IVIG administered prior to rehabilitation. Additionally, the postinflammatory etiology may respond differently to rehabilitation compared with paraneoplastic cases. Long-term follow-up data beyond 2 months would strengthen the outcome assessment.

Conclusions

This case report demonstrates the potential efficacy of combining conventional rehabilitation with targeted adjunctive therapies for adult-onset OMAS. The remarkable functional improvements achieved suggest that intensive, multimodal rehabilitation approaches may significantly benefit patients with rare neurologic conditions. This combined approach provides a foundation for future treatment protocols while highlighting the need for further research to validate these preliminary findings and establish evidence-based guidelines for OMAS rehabilitation.
