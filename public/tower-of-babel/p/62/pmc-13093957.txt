Endoscopic puncture and recanalization in a child with corrosive esophageal stenosis and atresia: case report and literature review
Authors: Ming-tang Ye, Al-wajih Hamzah, Ji-rong Qi, Zhi-qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorrosive esophageal stenosis and atresia represent severe late complications of caustic alkali ingestion, often leading to discontinuity of the esophagus and loss of oral feeding capability. While traditional surgical reconstruction is highly invasive and carries significant morbidity, endoscopic minimally invasive recanalization may represent a potential alternative in highly specific scenarios.Case presentationWe report a case of an 11-year-old boy who developed long-segment esophageal stenosis and complete atresia following accidental alkali ingestion. Successful esophageal recanalization was achieved using a combined bidirectional endoscopy approach under fluoroscopic guidance, involving puncture, balloon dilation, and the placement of a fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FCSEMS). Following the procedure, the patient resumed an oral diet and demonstrated favorable outcomes at midterm follow-up.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that endoscopic recanalization appears to be a feasible option for selected pediatric patients with short-segment atresia and suitable anatomy. Its success hinges on meticulous preprocedural assessment, multidisciplinary collaboration, and strict adherence to indications. Surgery is not the only pathway, and treatment must be individualized based on specific patient conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06735-4.

Introduction

Corrosive esophagitis, resulting from the ingestion of corrosive agents such as strong acids or alkalis, causes severe injury to the esophageal wall. One of the most serious long-term complications during its chronic phase is the development of long-segment esophageal stenosis and complete obstruction [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that among patients with severe corrosive esophagitis, the incidence of long-segment stenosis (> 5 cm) can reach 15%–20%, with a subset of these cases progressing to complete atresia, leading to the total loss of oral feeding capacity and severe nutritional impairment [2, 3].

The severity of the condition is directly determined by the nature, concentration, and duration of contact with the corrosive substance. Clinically, patients typically present with a clear history of ingestion. Following the acute phase, they may experience progressively worsening dysphagia, initially with solid foods and potentially advancing to an inability to swallow liquids. This is often accompanied by malnutrition and respiratory symptoms due to reflux and aspiration. Diagnosis primarily relies on a detailed patient history, contrast esophagography, and endoscopic evaluation. Endoscopy is particularly crucial, as it does not only assess the depth and extent of acute injury but also serves as a key tool for diagnosing and managing strictures in the later stages.

Managing corrosive esophageal strictures remains challenging. For short-segment strictures, endoscopic dilation with balloons or bougies is the first-line treatment. However, traditional management for long-segment stenosis or complete atresia has largely depended on major surgical interventions, such as esophagectomy with colonic or jejunal interposition [4, 5]. These procedures are highly invasive, carry significant morbidity, and profoundly impact the quality of life, especially in pediatric patients. Despite aggressive intervention, these strictures are characterized by a high recurrence rate, with poorer prognoses associated with long-segment or multiple strictures. Furthermore, areas of corrosive injury are considered high-risk for the development of esophageal carcinoma, necessitating lifelong endoscopic surveillance for patients.

Recent advances in endoscopic techniques, including bidirectional endoscopy, puncture recanalization, and temporary stent placement, have expanded the therapeutic spectrum for selected cases of esophageal obstruction. However, these approaches remain technically demanding, highly operator-dependent, and applicable only under strict anatomical and clinical conditions.

Herein, we report a pediatric case of corrosive esophageal stenosis complicated by a short-segment complete atresia, successfully managed through a staged bidirectional endoscopic puncture recanalization. Rather than proposing a new standard of care, this report aims to illustrate the feasibility, decision-making process, and technical considerations of this approach in a carefully selected patient, while highlighting its limitations and potential risks.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old boy was admitted to a local hospital with retrosternal burning pain following the accidental ingestion of a strongly alkaline cleaning agent. Initial management included gastric lavage, fluid resuscitation, and nutritional support. Due to the severity of the esophageal injury, he was transferred to a tertiary care center where he underwent multiple endoscopic dilatations. However, these procedures were discontinued after an esophageal perforation occurred during one session. Consequently, a gastrostomy and jejunostomy were performed for enteral nutrition. Despite this, the patient’s esophageal stricture progressively worsened, eventually leading to complete atresia.

Nine months after the initial injury, the patient was referred to our institution. Gastroscopy via the gastrostomy revealed severe stenosis at the cardia, preventing passage of the endoscope. Contrast esophagography identified a long-segment stenosis of approximately 10 cm in the lower esophagus, with the narrowest diameter being about 3 mm (Fig. 1A). Initial dilatation was performed using a 12-mm balloon to address the lower esophageal stricture (Fig. 1B). While the endoscope could then pass through this initially stenotic segment, it could not proceed beyond the proximal site of complete atresia. Subsequent bidirectional endoscopy, including both oral endoscopy and endoscopy via gastrostomy, revealed a single blind-end pouch within the esophagus. This blind end was identified 22 cm from the incisors during oral endoscopy and was approximately 10 cm above the cardia when approached via gastrostomy (Fig. 2A and B). Radiological imaging confirmed a minimal distance of only 2–3 mm between the two blind ends (Fig. 3).

A second procedure for endoscopic recanalization was performed two weeks later. Under the combined guidance of bidirectional endoscopy and fluoroscopy, the short distance (approx. 2 mm) between the two ends was reconfirmed. A mucosal needle knife was introduced via the oral endoscope to puncture the center of the proximal blind end, successfully traversing the thin tissue barrier and entering the lumen of the distal esophageal segment (Fig. 4A). A guidewire was then advanced, followed by sequential balloon dilatation (10 mm and 12 mm) of the newly created channel. A FCSEMS, 14 cm in length and 16 mm in diameter, was subsequently placed across the recanalized segment (Fig. 4B). The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful. Postoperative management included prophylactic antibiotics to prevent infection and a one-week course of proton pump inhibitors for acid suppression. The gastrostomy tube was removed following the successful resumption of oral intake. Dietary progression was carefully managed, advancing from liquids to a semi-solid diet, and finally to a regular diet. Patients were specifically instructed to chew food thoroughly before swallowing and to drink water after meals to prevent food debris from adhering to the stent. Following the removal of the stent, the patient has effectively returned to normal oral nutrition.

Discussion

Literature review

The potential for catastrophic clinical presentations and lifelong complications following the ingestion of corrosive substances makes this condition one of the most challenging clinical entities in gastroenterology. This pathology is most frequently observed in two distinct populations: young children who accidentally ingest corrosive agents and adults who intentionally consume them with suicidal intent. Among corrosive injuries, alkaline and acidic agents are the most common culprits. Ingestion of such substances can cause burns to multiple sites, including the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, esophagus, and stomach [6, 7]. The resultant tissue damage can trigger a cascade of severe complications, including airway compromise, esophageal or gastric perforation, sepsis, and even death. In some cases, the subsequent development of esophageal strictures is inevitable, leading to the patient’s inability to swallow food normally.

A range of therapeutic approaches has been proposed in clinical practice; however, significant controversy persists in the literature regarding optimal patient management strategies, and a standardized consensus has yet to be established. For patients with chronic stenosis, repeated esophageal dilation is typically employed to maintain an adequate luminal diameter. In cases of severe stenosis or complete obstruction, dilation therapy may be limited by procedure-related complications or insufficient efficacy, necessitating consideration of surgical intervention. Management of esophageal stenosis caused by ingestion of corrosive substances

Fourteen studies reported on the management of esophageal strictures following corrosive substance ingestion. Among the 1125 patients with strictures, treatment was endoscopic (n = 804, 71.4%) or surgical (n = 309, 27.4%). Surgical intervention, typically involving esophageal replacement with a gastric or colonic conduit, was primarily reserved for cases with perforation or full-thickness necrosis detected on diagnostic evaluation. In these scenarios, conservative management was generally associated with poor outcomes and was therefore avoided.

Once an esophageal stricture develops, endoscopic dilation with or without stent placement constitutes the primary therapeutic approach. Data presented in the table above indicate that this modality successfully resolves dysphagia in approximately 75% of cases. Achieving an optimal outcome typically necessitates multiple dilation sessions, with only a minority of patients requiring surgery as a first line intervention. The decision to proceed with primary surgery is often influenced by the experience of the local medical team and the presence of specific criteria, such as multiple strictures.

Compared to major surgical procedures like esophageal replacement, which carry substantial risks of morbidity and mortality, endoscopic dilation represents a safer initial treatment strategy. Complications, such as perforation, are relatively uncommon; moreover, most perforations are minor in nature and carry a favorable prognosis, resulting in an acceptable risk benefit profile. Furthermore, surgical intervention remains a viable option for cases refractory to repeated endoscopic dilation.

Pathological basis and therapeutic challenges in corrosive esophageal stenosis or atresia

The damage inflicted by alkaline corrosive agents on the esophagus is considerably more severe than that caused by acidic substances. By binding to tissue proteins, alkalis induce deep liquefactive necrosis and saponification, potentially penetrating the full thickness of the esophageal wall. This process is accompanied by vascular thrombosis, leading to tissue ischemia, which subsequently initiates a reparative phase characterized by fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. The final outcome is the formation of dense, poorly vascularized scar tissue, causing severe luminal contracture and even disruption of esophageal continuity. This distinct pathophysiological process explains why corrosive strictures are often long, fibrotic, respond poorly to simple dilation, and have a high recurrence rate [22, 23].

When the condition progresses to complete atresia, conventional endoscopic therapies become ineffective. Patients face the challenge of long-term dependence on enteral feeding via a stoma, significantly impairing their quality of life. In this context, surgery often becomes the only remaining therapeutic option for the majority of patients.

Value and limitations of conventional surgical reconstruction

For patients with multiple esophageal strictures, long-segment stenosis, or complete atresia, esophagectomy combined with colonic or jejunal interposition has been traditionally regarded as the definitive curative procedure. This approach aims to completely remove the diseased esophagus and restore alimentary tract continuity using an autologous intestinal conduit, theoretically enabling long-term oral intake. However, this procedure is associated with substantial risks, including severe complications such as anastomotic leakage, anastomotic stricture, graft necrosis, and intrathoracic infection [4, 24]. In adult patients, and particularly in children who are still undergoing growth and development, the procedure entails significant trauma, prolonged recovery, and potential long-term impacts on thoracic/abdominal organ function and psychological development. Consequently, surgical intervention is often considered a last resort rather than a first-line option [25].

Evolution and indications of endoscopic minimally invasive recanalization techniques

Advancements in endoscopic technology have introduced minimally invasive approaches, such as bidirectional endoscopy with rendezvous technique, electrocautery incision, and puncture recanalization, offering a promising alternative for selected patients with corrosive esophageal atresia. These techniques aim to reestablish the continuity of the native esophageal conduit while avoiding the need for highly invasive transthoracic surgery.

However, endoscopic recanalization is not suitable for all cases of atresia. Analysis of technical decision-making and innovations in the present case

The successful treatment of a pediatric patient with long-segment corrosive esophageal stenosis and atresia via a staged, bidirectional endoscopic puncture recanalization in this case provides a valuable example for discussing the indications, technical nuances, and treatment strategies associated with this technique. It exemplifies a staged, multi-modality approach to precision minimally invasive therapy.

Staged procedural strategy

The initial procedure focused solely on dilating the lower esophageal stenosis. This step aimed to establish a passage, thereby creating the necessary conditions for the subsequent rendezvous procedure. This staged approach avoided the potential for excessive trauma and edema that could result from a single-session recanalization in the context of a severe, complex stricture. The management of the atretic segment was specifically addressed in the second procedure, enhancing both the focus and safety of the intervention.

Endoscopic puncture recanalization technique and safety assurance

The core technical achievement of this case lies in the bidirectional endoscopic puncture recanalization. The oral and gastrostomy endoscopes were advanced to the upper and lower blind ends, respectively, achieving precise apposition under fluoroscopic guidance. This real-time, visual navigation was critical, as it minimized blind puncture, allowed for recanalization along the presumed original esophageal axis, and substantially reduced the risk of transmural penetration into the mediastinum and injury to major vessels or adjacent organs.

Rationale for stent placement

The decision to place a FCSEMS following recanalization was based on a profound understanding of the underlying pathology. The newly created channel is essentially a dilated tract within fibrotic scar tissue, possessing a strong tendency for elastic recoil and restenosis. The stent provides sustained radial force to counteract this contracture, thereby maintaining luminal patency and allowing time for mucosal re-epithelialization over the tract. The fully covered design was selected to facilitate subsequent stent removal and to mitigate the risk of tissue embedding by the stent mesh.

Controversies regarding stent selection and future perspectives

Although the metal stent played a pivotal role in the present case, its application in benign esophageal conditions remains controversial [28]. Primary concerns include stent-related discomfort, and potential long-term complications such as pressure-induced ulceration, hyperplastic tissue reaction, and even the formation of new strictures [29]. Consequently, bioabsorbable stents (BDS) are regarded as a more promising alternative. BDS can provide sufficient radial force while degrading in vivo over time, thereby obviating the need for a second removal procedure and avoiding long-term complications associated with a permanent foreign body. However, challenges persist, particularly regarding their mechanical strength and the precise matching of the degradation profile with the tissue healing process, which represent key hurdles in current research and development [30, 31]. The use of a metal stent as a bridging therapy in this case was a rational decision based on locally available medical resources. Nevertheless, with anticipated advancements in BDS technology, they hold significant promise as a superior future option for such patients.

Conclusion and limitation

Corrosive esophageal stenosis and atresia represent one of the most severe long-term sequelae of alkali ingestion, characterized by dense fibrotic scar tissue formation and complete disruption of esophageal continuity. While conventional surgical reconstruction can restore alimentary continuity, its significant invasiveness and high complication rates have spurred the search for less invasive alternatives.

This case successfully achieved anatomical and functional reconstruction through staged bidirectional endoscopic puncture recanalization and stent placement. It demonstrates the potential of this technique as a feasible and effective alternative in meticulously selected patients, particularly those with short atretic segments and favorable anatomy. However, its success is critically dependent on multidisciplinary collaboration, precise image guidance, and substantial operator expertise. Given its technical complexity and inherent risks—including perforation, mediastinitis, and restenosis—this procedure must not be generalized. It should be reserved only for a highly selected subset of patients with short atresia, well-defined blind ends, and access to specialized care. For the majority of patients, especially those with long-segment atresia or unfavorable anatomy, surgical reconstruction remains the definitive treatment option.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as a single case report, the findings are inherently anecdotal and preclude generalization to broader populations. Second, the literature review incorporated both adult and pediatric studies without age-stratified analysis. This introduces heterogeneity, as adult and pediatric populations differ fundamentally across multiple domains, including etiology (accidental ingestion in children versus intentional ingestion in adults), anatomical characteristics, physiological response to injury, healing potential, nutritional requirements, and long-term risk of malignancy. The paucity of robust pediatric-specific evidence constrains the strength of our recommendations and highlights the pressing need for multicenter prospective studies dedicated exclusively to children with corrosive esophageal injuries.

Looking forward, advancements in materials science, such as bioabsorbable stents, along with the accumulation of long-term outcome data, may expand the role of endoscopic recanalization. Ultimately, improving outcomes for affected children will depend on enhanced disease awareness, strengthened early intervention, and carefully individualized treatment strategies.
