Diep flap with anterior fascial island: The latest fashion for reconstruction of massive soft tissue defects
Authors: Trần Thiết Sơn, Phan Tuấn Nghĩa, Phạm Thị Việt Dung, Tạ Thị Hồng Thuý
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe DIEP flap has become the first choice for autologous breast reconstruction after total mastectomy and is also used for reconstructing various body parts. However, the reliance on a single vascular pedicle poses a risk of skin compromise in zone IV, potentially leading to necrosis. To reduce this risk, the extended DIEP transversal flap technique ensures sufficient blood supply to zone IV by utilizing dual deep inferior epigastric vessels or the superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA). This study outlines our experience in performing DIEP flap harvest in conjunction with the rectus fascia technique. In our retrospective analysis of ten patients—six for breast reconstruction and four for soft tissue defects—we found an average BMI of 20.1 kg/m² and an average flap size of 29 × 13 cm. The length of the flap's pedicle ranged from 9 to 14 cm. The anterior rectus sheath averaged 6.1 × 11.7 cm. All flaps survived, though one patient experienced 2 cm of necrosis at the distal edge of a pedicled DIEP flap. The conservation of the fascial plexus between the two perforators of the DIEP flap can provide the extra supply to the distal part of the flap, making zone IV viable. The total or extended DIEP flap, without additional anastomosis, helps reduce operation time, surgical complications, and other complications. The extended DIEP flap technique reduces operation time and complications, providing a viable option for reconstructing massive defects and breast reconstruction after mastectomy.Keywords: Diep flap, Rectus anterior fascia, Massive soft-tissue defect, Reconstruction, Microsurgery

Introduction

The deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap was introduced by Koshima in 1989 for head and neck reconstruction. In 1994, Allen utilized the free DIEP flap for breast reconstruction following cancer.1,2 Since then, the DIEP flap has become the preferred choice for breast reconstruction after total mastectomy, owing to its abundant volume and reliable vascular supply, while providing the added benefit of an abdominoplasty with minimal donor site deformity.3,4 Beyond breast reconstruction, the DIEP flap, in horizontal, vertical, or oblique forms, is also suitable for addressing small to moderate defects in pediatric and male patients.5, 6, 7 However, the reliance on a single vascular pedicle for the DIEP flap presents a risk, as the skin in zone IV—according to the Holm-Hartrampf classification—can be compromised, potentially leading to necrosis. To address this concern, an extended DIEP transversal flap has been proposed to ensure adequate blood supply to zone IV. This technique allows for the reconstruction of large defects in the limbs, head, and neck by combining two perforating or supercharging forms of the deep inferior epigastric vessels or utilizing the superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA).8, 9, 10 In this article, we share our experience with the extended bilateral DIEP flap technique, where the distal part of the flap receives sufficient blood supply without requiring additional vascular anastomosis. This is achieved by preserving a strip of rectus abdominis fascia within the flap. We apply this method to cover extensive defects in the neck and lower limbs, as well as for breast reconstruction following mastectomy.

Patient and methods

Between 2017 and 2024, we conducted a retrospective analysis of ten patients who underwent reconstruction using the rectus anterior fascia DIEP flap. All patients were women, with a mean age of 45.6 years ± 9.7 (range, 28–60) and an average BMI of 20.1 kg/m² (range, 16.3–23.8 kg/m²). Among these patients, six had a BMI of 20.6 kg/m² (range 18.5 to 23.8 kg/m²) and underwent breast reconstruction following modified radical mastectomy. The remaining four patients had a BMI of 19.4 kg/m² (range 16.3 to 21.8 kg/m²) and presented with soft tissue defects. These included two patients with trauma injuries to the leg and foot, one patient with a burn scar on the neck and anterior chest, and one patient with a synovial sarcoma in the thigh.

The average interval between the previous surgery and reconstruction was 19.8 months (4 to 36 months). This included an average of 8.5 months (range 4 to 20 months) for reconstructive coverage and 27.3 months (range 22 to 36 months) for breast reconstruction. Flap harvesting technique

Preoperatively, we identify and mark the perforators in the lower abdomen using a hand-held Doppler. We perform the rectus fascial DIEP technique only when two perforators are located on either side of the flap. The size of the skin flap matches the size of the defect, and we also designed the relative fascia strip between the two perforators.

First, the skin incision is placed at the lower edge of the flap, followed by the incision at the upper edge of the flap. The skin flap is dissected with electrocautery above the fascia from lateral to medial until the rectus sheath over the muscle is reached. Continue the dissection below the rectus sheath until the two perforators are identified. Choose the two largest perforators located on either side of the rectus sheath. When perforators over the muscle are encountered, the rectus sheath is incised, leaving a large fascial piece around two perforators. This fascia strip will be harvested with the skin flap. Select the appropriate perforator and dissect the DIEA pedicle in the rectus muscle up to its origin, achieving a maximum length of 12–14 cm. When using a pedicle flap, the perforator is chosen ipsilateral to the defect. Unlike a pedicled flap, the free flap pedicle is taken from the contralateral side with the soft tissue defect to be reconstructed. The opposite perforator is dissected below the fascia layer and then cut. This perforator is ligated only after confirming bleeding at the distal end of the flap, corresponding to the IV zone. A fascial island measuring approximately 5–8 cm in width and 8–12 cm in length is dissected from the rectus abdominis muscle layer with both perforators. The flap is then separated from the abdominal wall as a flap based on one DIEA vessel. Primary flap thinning is performed in cases requiring coverage by removing the superficial fat layer. The extent of thinning is limited to the fascia strip. For breast reconstruction, additional adipose tissue is needed at the superior and inferior edges to increase the flap volume. The flap is then transferred to the recipient site.

Reconstructive technique

If a free DIEP flap is selected for reconstruction, it is elevated from the donor site and transferred to the recipient site. Anastomosis is performed between the flap pedicle and the recipient vessel using a 10–0 suture under a microscope. After anastomosis, patients receive an intravenous infusion of 5000 units of heparin. The flap is then placed over the defect or rolled up to recreate the breast shape in breast reconstruction. A drain is also placed, and the sutures are applied in two layers with a 4–0 suture.

Donor site: The closure of the donor site depends on the area of fascia that was taken. The rectus sheath is closed with a running large-caliber non-braided suture for a small fascial island. In cases where a larger fascial island is taken, polyethylene mesh is used to close the abdominal wall. After elevating the flap, the abdominal skin above the flap is undermined to the costal margin. The donor site is then closed in layers over suction drains, similar to an abdominoplasty.

Postoperation: Flap reflux should be monitored every three hours. Antibiotics are administered for three days, and the drains are removed on day seven. Sutures are taken out after 12 to 14 days.

Results

The patient's surgical details are summarized in Table 2. The flap type was a pedicled DIEP flap in one patient and a free DIEP flap in nine. A transverse flap design was used for all DIEP flaps. We used DIEP flaps for coverage reconstruction of soft tissue defects, including a pedicled DIEP flap (in one patient) and three free DIEP flaps (in three). Six free DIEP flaps were used for breast reconstruction (in six patients). All DIEP flaps were harvested based on one dominant perforator from the deep inferior epigastric pedicle. The DIEP flaps averaged 29 ± 3.3 cm in length (range, 26 to 36) and 13.3 ± 1.7 cm in width (range, 9 - 16 cm). The length of the flap's pedicle ranged from 9 to 14 cm (mean, 11.9 ± 1.6 cm). The anterior rectus sheath averaged 6.1 ± 1.3 cm in width (range, 26 to 36) and 11.7 ± 0.6 cm in length (range, 10 - 12 cm). The flap pedicle was anastomosed end-to-end with the anterior tibial artery in two cases, the fascial artery in one case, and the internal mammary artery in six cases. All patients had their donor sites closed primarily. All flaps survived completely, while a 2 cm necrosis at the distal flap edge occurred in one patient who underwent reconstruction with a pedicled DIEP flap measuring 36 cm in length. Two patients of reconstructive surgery required secondary flap defatting. There were no reported early or late complications at the donor site, including abdominal wall hernia. The median follow-up duration was 32.7 months. All patients were satisfied with the appearance of the reconstruction. The DIEP flaps provided reliable soft tissue volume and improved contour of the original defects, and no functional problems were observed in the main motions of the foot, ankle, and neck. The flaps in the reconstructive coverage area resembled the neck, leg, and foot skin texture. Discussion

Microsurgical flaps in the reconstruction of extensive soft tissue defects

In 1982, Hartrampf introduced the transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous (TRAM) flap, which uses a superior deep epigastric vascular pedicle and includes the rectus abdominis fascia and the muscle flap.11 In 1989, Koshima et al. first described the DIEP flap, which is based on the perforators of the deep inferior epigastric artery and does not involve the rectus abdominis muscle, notably reducing abdominal wall morbidity.2 Since then, the DIEP flap has gained widespread acceptance and has become the preferred option for breast reconstruction and soft tissue defects. The classic DIEP flap, particularly advantageous for women who have given birth, offers a large volume, a long vascular pedicle, and suitable skin quality for reconstructing significant defects in the head, face, neck, and limbs.1,7,12, 13, 14 However, using the DIEP flap with a single vascular pedicle poses limitations due to an unreliable blood supply on the opposite side.15 Alternatives include combining multiple microsurgical flaps, such as the DIEP flap with the ALT flap or the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIP) flap.8,16 However, these combinations come with complications such as complicated surgical procedures, the risk of anastomotic occlusion, and extended surgical times. An extended DIEP flap, which utilizes perforators from both sides through a technique known as supercharging or pre-expansion, has also been introduced to enhance flap volume.17, 18, 19, 20 This method improves blood supply to zone IV, an area vulnerable to necrosis. Alternatives include combining multiple microsurgical flaps, such as the ALT flap with the latissimus dorsi flap, the DIEP flap with the ALT flap, or the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIP) flap.8,16 However, these combinations come with complications such as complicated surgical procedures, the risk of anastomotic occlusion, and extended surgical times.

The role of rectus fascia in DIEP flap blood supply

The role of the rectus abdominis fascia has not been typically highlighted when discussing the dissection of the DIEP flap. The rectus abdominis muscle sheath is one of the nine components of the abdominal wall. Below the arcuate line, the aponeuroses of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis contribute solely to the anterior rectus sheath. The Deep Inferior Epigastric Artery (DIEA) and the Superficial Inferior Epigastric Artery (SIEA) provide blood supply to the lower abdomen, with the DIEA playing a significant and relatively constant role. Perforators originate from the DIEA, passing through muscle or septal structures to supply blood to the skin.15,21, 22, 23, 24 The number and location of these perforators can vary between individuals and even from one side of the body to the other in the same individual. As each perforator crosses the abdominal muscles, it either passes through the fascia or travels beneath it temporarily. From this point, the perforators branch into three layers: the fascia plexus, which supplies blood to the fascial and the subfascial fat layer; the subdermal plexus; and the subcutaneous plexus, which supplies blood to the skin and subcutaneous layers. These vascular systems are closely interconnected. The direct large perforators pass through the fascia and terminate in the subdermal and subcutaneous plexuses.24,25 The suprafascial and subfascial plexus systems also provide alternative pathways for blood flow to other vessels within the flap. Each perforator forms independent perforasomes under normal physiological conditions.26, 27, 28 Direct flow through the linking vessels of the fascia plexuses communicates directly with adjacent perforators. These linking vessels open only when there is an interruption in the blood supply to the adjacent perforasome. In the course of muscle to skin, the perforators divide into four plexuses: pre-facial, suprafacial, subcutaneous, and subdermal plexus. The fascial plexus is related to the subcutaneous and subdermal plexuses, with connections between the perforators existing only within fasciocutaneous flaps. In a fascial preserve flap, the presence of both the suprafascial and subfascial plexuses provides an alternative pathway, improving the opening of direct and indirect flow channels on the opposite side of the flap. These plexuses connect to the subcutaneous and subdermal plexuses via direct and indirect linking vessels, which are essential for flap perfusion, especially in the contralateral lateral adjacent area. (Figure 4). We typically leave a significant section of fascia, measuring between 5 and 10 cm in width and 8 and 12 cm in length, depending on the positioning of the two perforators on either side of the flap. This technique conserves the fascial plexus between the two perforators. The presence of a single perforator does not increase the perfusion potential on the opposite side of the flap; therefore, the blood supply in that area is comparable to that in the perforator area.

During clinical operations, we observed bleeding at the flap edges in Zone IV after ligating the perforator on one side. We classified the area on the contralateral side of the flap as IIa, while the ipsilateral adjacent area is IIb, where the blood supply primarily comes from direct and indirect connections without involvement from the fascial plexus. Zone IV, according to Hartrampf's classification, connects to Zone IIa and transitions into Zone III next to Zone II (according to our classification) (Figure 5). For coverage, we performed primary thinning by removing the entire fat layer beneath the superficial fascia while preserving the fascia itself. This technique maintained the integrity of the subcutaneous and subdermal plexuses throughout the flap.29 We believe that the blood supply to Zone IV (as per Hartrampf's classification) was successfully extended for three reasons: (1) the use of a fascial strip between the two perforators; (2) thinning that reduced the flap's volume and (3) anastomosis of the pedicle to the other artery, where the arterial pressure is greater than that at the donor site. Thinning is not typically performed with breast reconstructions to maintain volume. Since patients undergoing breast reconstruction usually have a low BMI, a single-pedicle DIEP flap might not provide sufficient volume compared to the contralateral breast. The rectus fascial DIEP flaps, however, offer a large volume of tissue and feature long pedicles that are favorable for microsurgical anastomosis. This is particularly important in cases where the recipient's vessels are not suitable for microsurgical anastomosis, making pedicled flaps the preferred choice for such lesions in the thigh.30,31

The main limitation of this report is its small sample size, consisting of only ten patients, which means that no firm conclusions can be drawn. Another limitation is the absence of a group with fascia that includes only one perforator. Additionally, there is no existing PDE system to verify the expansion of the flap’s perfusion based on a single perforator. For patients with a BMI greater than 26, the volume of the DIEP flap is sufficient for unilateral reconstruction and does not require expansion. However, male or pediatric patients, as well as those with a straight lower abdominal incision, are not suitable candidates for this technique. Furthermore, fascia preservation is of little benefit for patients with multiple small perforators from the DIEA on one side of the flap.

Conclusion

The rectus fascial island DIEP flap offers a viable alternative for the reconstruction of large body defects, as well as for breast reconstruction following mastectomy. Preserving the rectus abdominis fascia connecting the perforators from both sides of the DIEP flap may improve blood perfusion to the distal portion of the flap.
