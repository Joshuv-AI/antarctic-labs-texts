High-quality, haplotype-resolved reference genomes of the Dutch warmblood horse and Friesian horse using trio binning
Authors: Marije J Steensma, Bart J Ducro, Bert Dibbits, Harmen P Doekes, Job G C van Schipstal, Ted Kalblfleisch, Martien AM Groenen, Martijn F L Derks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn horses, genetic diversity is predominantly observed between breeds, with little variation within breeds. The studbooks of the two largest horse populations in the Netherlands, the Dutch Warmblood horse and Friesian horse population, have ongoing conservation projects including collecting large-scale genotype and sequence data. The current reference genome, derived from a Thoroughbred horse can lead to bias in genetic analyses of other horse breeds. Therefore, the aim of this study was to create high-quality breed-specific reference genomes of Dutch Warmblood and Friesian horses.ResultsWe performed nanopore long-read sequencing (R10.4, Q20+) of an F1 cross between a Dutch Warmblood horse and a Friesian horse to create two breed-specific reference genomes by trio binning. This resulted in high-quality, haplotype-resolved reference genomes with contig N50 of 37 and 35 Mb and single copy gene completeness of 99.2 and 99.3% for the Friesian and Warmblood, respectively. The majority of the chromosomes contained telomeric and /or centromeric sequences. The Ensembl gene annotation resulted in 19,750 and 19,872 protein coding genes for the Friesian and Warmblood, respectively. No large chromosomal rearrangements were observed between the Friesian and Warmblood genomes. However, a total of 722 large structural variations (> 10 kb) were identified, of which 14 affect the coding sequence of protein-coding genes.ConclusionThe novel breed-specific reference genomes provide a valuable resource for future genetic analysis and breed conservation efforts and will contribute to ongoing equine pangenome efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11985-0.Keywords: Reference genome, Breed-specific, Friesian horse, Warmblood horse, Trio binning, Haplotype-resolved

Background

The horse (Equus caballus) is distinguished in 400–500 different breeds which strongly vary in phenotypic features due to centuries of selection on a variety of breeding goals [1–3]. The ongoing evolution in genomics technologies has led to insights in the genetic background of the phenotypic differences among horse breeds. For instance, up till now, over 260 equine hereditary traits have been discovered [4]. An important resource for genetic analysis and fine-mapping of those phenotypic features, is the development of the equine reference genome which has shaped equine genomic studies in the past 15 years [5].

The first equine genome assembly (EquCab2.0) [6] based upon the DNA of the Thoroughbred mare Twilight, was published in 2007. By current standards, this assembly would be considered as a draft assembly [7]. This reference genome was generated using the best technology available at the time and included Sanger sequencing (6.8X) of Twilight, BAC end sequences of Twilight’s half-brother [6] and available radiation and hybrid cytogenic maps [8]. In 2018, the genome of Twilight was re-sequenced and re-assembled, resulting in EquCab3.0 [7]. This assembly was built upon the solid foundation of EquCab2.0 and augmented with short-read data (Illumina, 45X), long-read data (PacBio, 16X), Chicago Hi-C proximity ligation data [7], providing a higher-quality equine genome assembly. Since the development of the equine reference genomes, many studies have used the reference genome to study genetics underlying various performance and health traits [9–12], and the evolution of horses [13–15]. Recently, a reference genome for the Finn Horse (EquCab_Finn) [16] and a Thoroughbred telomere to telomere (T2T) assembly have been developed, of which the latter is expected to serve as the main equine assembly in the near future [17].

All published equine reference genomes, except EquCab_Finn, are from a Thoroughbred horse [6, 7]. Consequently, when assessing the DNA-profile in other breeds than the reference, the detection of polymorphisms will be biased towards the sequence structure of the reference genome and breed-specific variation will be missed [18–20]. This is particularly an issue in horses due to their high inter-breed and low intra-breed genetic diversity [2, 11, 21]. Thus, there is a need for breed-specific reference genomes, which will improve mapping quality and thereby aid in the detection of breed-specific (structural) variation. In addition, those breed-specific reference genomes will be a valuable resource for the future equine pangenome currently underway.

The studbooks of the two largest Dutch horse populations, the Dutch Warmblood horse and the Friesian horse population, are routinely collecting genotypes (70 K SNP array). In addition, whole genome sequence data of influential stallions (N = 50 per breed) from both studbooks will soon become available. Therefore, breed-specific reference genomes of those breeds will be highly valuable for improving fine-mapping results. Especially for the Friesian horse, as this breed is genetically most distinct from other Dutch horse breeds [22] and suffering from inbreeding related disorders [23–25].

In this study, we utilized trio binning of an F1 outbred horse descending from a Friesian stallion and a Dutch Warmblood mare to generate high-quality haplotype-resolved reference genomes of the two largest Dutch horse breeds. In addition, we also compared the two genomes to identify structural variants. The presented breed-specific equine reference genomes provide an important contribution to the collection of equine reference genomes.

Methods

Data generation

We used the trio-binning approach [26] to assemble the diploid genome of a female horse. The F1 was sequenced with long and short reads, while the parents were sequenced with only short reads to employ trio-binning. A two-year-old mare descending from an outcross between a purebred Friesian stallion and a purebred Dutch Warmblood dressage mare, was used as the F1. The F1 and both parents were privately owned by the same individual breeder in the Netherlands. The F1 and the mother were registered by the “Koninklijk Warmbloed Paardenstamboek Nederland” (KWPN) and the father was registered by “Koninklijk Friesch Paarden Stamboek (KFPS). Blood of the F1 and both parents was drawn with a 9.0 ml EDTA tube. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Puregene blood kit from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Isolated DNA from the F1 was used for Oxford Nanopore Sequencing (ONT, 88X). Short read elimination was performed on 7 µg of high molecular weight DNA from the F1 with the SRExs kit from PacBio (Menlo Park, CA). A single Nanopore library was prepared on 3 µg SRExs treated DNA using the Ligation sequencing kit V14 (SQK-LSK114). The library was sequenced on two PromethION R10.4.1 flow cells (FLO-PRO114M) on an in-house Oxford Nanopore Sequencer PromethION 24. The raw reads of Nanopore were basecalled by Guppy (v6.5.7). The reads had a mean quality score of 18, which corresponds to a base call accuracy of 98.4%. Reads were filtered with the MiniKNOW v23.04.5 with the default cutoff of a minimum average Phred q-score of 7.

DNA (2.5 µg) of each parent and the F1 was send to BGI Genomics (China) for whole genome sequencing. DNA libraries of each parent were developed by BGI Genomics following the MGIEasy PCR-Free DNA Library Prep Set User Manual version A2. DNA libraries were whole genome sequenced by BGI Genomics on DNBSEQ-T7 (50X), with 150 bp read length.

Trio Binning haplotype genomes

The accuracy of trio binning to distinguish parental haplotypes is higher at greater heterozygosity in the F1 compared to F1 individuals with lower heterozygosity. Therefore, first, heterozygosity of the F1 was estimated using GenomeScope2.0 [27] with the short reads of the F1.

The trio mode in Canu (TrioCanu, v2.2) [26] was used to bin the parental reads to construct parental haplotype assemblies. Triocanu was run with the short reads from each parent and the ONT reads (fastq file, Q > 7) from the F1 with the following options: -p asm genomesize = 2.5 g. The F1 long reads were assigned to the haplotype with the most matching haplotype-specific k-mers (see [26] for details). Ambiguous reads with a tie or too few haplotype-specific k-mers (frequency < 7) were making up 2.7% of the total bases and were not assigned to a haplotype. There were 1,274,515 reads (0.3% of all F1 bases) that were too short (length < 1,000) and were filtered out.

The assigned parental ONT reads were each separately assembled and scaffolded using Flye v2.9 [28] with the options: –nano-hq < fasta file> –read-error 0.03 –asm-coverage 40 –genome-size 2.5 g –scaffold. This resulted in two assemblies, one paternal haplotype assembly (Friesian horse) and one maternal haplotype assembly (Dutch Warmblood mare). Each parental assembly was then further reference-guided scaffolded with RagTag v2.1.0 [29], using the Thoroughbred T2T assembly (GCA_041296265.1) as the reference [17]. The scaffold module from RagTag v2.1.0 was used with default parameters.

Validation of scaffolded assemblies using linkage disequilibrium information

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was calculated between SNPs to validate the order and orientation of the reference-guided scaffolded assemblies and correct for any bias towards the equine T2T assembly. Genotype data from the GGP Equine 70 K Plus Beadchip (Neogen/Illumina) containing 71,589 SNPs was available for 4,065 Friesian horses. For the Dutch Warmblood dressage horses, the provided pre-filtered (–maf 0.01 –set-me-missing –set-hh-missing –not-chr X, Y and only keeping one SNP if it was in LD (r2 > 0.5) with another SNP) genotype data of the GGP Equine 70 K Plus Beadchip containing 56,830 SNPs was available for 5,367 horses. The SNP positions were converted from their original position on EquCab2.0 to the novel assemblies. First, EquCab2.0 was aligned to both Friesian and Warmblood assembly using MUMmer4 [30]. Then, SNP positions were lifted over from EquCab2.0 to the Friesian and Warmblood assembly using CrossMap [31], and SNPs that mapped to multiple positions in the same assembly were removed. This resulted in 68,136 SNPs and 55,170 SNPs mapping to the Friesian horse and Warmblood horse assembly, respectively. Then, pairwise LD between SNPs was calculated for each breed separately using Plink v.1.90b3.38 [32] with the parameters: –mind 0.6 –maf 0.05 –geno 0.1 –r2 inter-chr ld-window-r2 0.5. These quality control parameters left 38,584 SNPs for the Friesian horse assembly and 49,616 SNPs for the Warmblood horse assembly for calculating LD.

The mean r2 value and the total number of SNPs that were in LD (r2 > 0.5) with contigs or scaffolds were calculated. Based on the mean r2 value, the total number of SNPs in LD, and on the length of the contig/scaffold in LD, we checked its order as defined by the reference-guided scaffolded assemblies. Next, orientation for all contigs/scaffolds larger than 2 Mb was assessed. Mean base pair location of the SNPs in LD (including standard deviation) at contigs/scaffolds was calculated and compared to the total length of the contigs/scaffolds. Based on the orientation in the reference-guided scaffolded assemblies, SNPs at the end of a contig/scaffold should be in LD with SNPs at the beginning of the next contig/scaffold.

Polishing

The short reads of the parents were aligned to their parental assembly (Friesian or Warmblood) using BWA-MEM2 v2.2.1 [33]. SAMBLASTER v.0.1.26 [34] was used to mark duplicates and SAMtools v1.17 [35] to sort and index the BAM files. Pilon v1.24 [36] was used to polish indels based on the parental short read BAM files specific for each parental assembly with the parameters -mindepth 0.8 -fix indels -changes. Quality values (QV) of the final polished assemblies were computed using Merqury v1.3 [37] to provide estimates of assembly consensus accuracy. Finally, one contig with size < 20 bp was removed in the Friesian assembly.

Quality control and assessment

Assembly completeness was evaluated by compleasm v0.2.2 [38] to assess the quality of each parental haplotype assembly before and after polishing. Compleasm was run with the mammalia (mammalia_odb10, version 2021-02-19) dataset using the flag -l < dataset>. Assembly statistics were generated using assembly_stats [39].

Genome alignment

Genome assembly alignments were generated using D-GENIES v1.5.0 [40], with minimap2 v2.23 as the aligner and repeatedness was set to many repeats. The chromosomes were sorted on order.

Repeats

The BuildDatabase tool from RepeatModeler v2.0.5 [41] was used to build a de novo repeat library from our assemblies using the Recon and RepeatScout tools. RepeatMasker v4.1.5 [42] was then used to identify repeats together with the custom build repeat library from RepeatModeler. In addition, tandem repeats were identified using the TRF tool v4.09 [43]. BLAST [44] was used to detect the known horse satellite DNA sequences (BIT score > 450), 37cen (GenBank accession: AY029358), 2PI (GenBank accession: AH010654), EC137 (GenBank accession: JX026961), present in all equine chromosomes except chromosome 11 [45, 46]. First, a blast database was built with the parameters: makeblastdb -in < fasta>- out < name_output>-parse_seqids -dbtype nucl. Then, blastn was run with the following parameters: blastn -query < query_sequence>-db < name_output_blastn>-task blastn -outfmt7.

Gene annotation

Both genomes were annotated by Ensembl using the Ensembl Genebuild pipeline as part of the Ensembl Rapid Release [47]. This Ensembl Genebuild pipeline consists of three different stages, where the first stage is Genome Preparation including assembly loading and quality control as well as repeat masking and simple feature annotation. The second stage is based upon four different pipelines: (1) Long Read Pipeline (align and correct reads, collapse redundant models), (2) RNA-seq Pipeline (mapping of short reads to the genome, intron localization and transcript model creation), (3) Protein Pipeline (alignment of known proteins to the genome, transcript model creation via alignments), (4) Projection Pipeline (LastZ pairwise alignment with reference genome, projection of coding exons to target species). The final stage is the Model Finalisation including model filtration and prioritization as well as final gene set creation. The small ncRNAs, Ig/TR genes, and pseudogenes were computed using separate pipelines [47].

Gene family analysis

Gene families were inferred from the proteomes of three horse genomes and one donkey genome using OrthoFinder v2.5.5 [48] with default options. The proteomes of Equus Asinus (Donkey; ASM607732v2) and Equus Caballus (EquCab3.0) were downloaded from Ensembl release 111. The proteomes of EquCab_Warmblood_WUR (GCA_964023255.1) and EquCab_Friesian_WUR (GCA_964023265.1), were downloaded from Ensembl Rapid Release (September 2024). For each coding gene, only the longest transcript was selected using the script included with OrthoFinder (primary_transcript.py). To investigate differences in the number of genes between the horse genomes, genome-specific gene families as well as gene families with a large difference in number of genes between EquCab3.0 and Friesian (N > 14) were further analysed by searching for homologous proteins in the NR database using BLASTP v2.16.0 [49].

Major histocompatibility complex

We manually assessed the structural accuracy and gene annotation of the horse MHC region in our Friesian and Warmblood genomes. The MHC is a highly gene-dense region known for its significant genomic complexity. It is traditionally organized into three regions: MHC class I, II, and III. Classical MHC class II molecules, including DR, DQ, and DP, are of particular interest because they encode heterodimeric glycoproteins displayed on the surface of cells responsible for presenting exogenous peptides to CD4 + T-helper cells, which in turn activate immune responses to pathogens. We manually assessed the MHC region of the Friesian and Warmblood genomes on (1) the number of annotated genes per MHC class, (2) the order of the genes (including their start and end position), and (3) the orientation of the genes (forward or reverse strand).

Structural variation

Structural variation between the two haplotype assemblies was detected using SyRI v1.6.3 [50]. First, the two haplotype assemblies were aligned using minimap2 with the settings -ax asm5 -eqx. Next, we used SyRi to identify structural variation using the minimap2 alignment. Then, the results were plotted using plotsr v1.1.1 [51]. Ensembl-VEP v112.0 [52] was run to determine genes involved in the structural variations as well as their effects. Structural variants were manually visualized in JBrowse (version 1.16.11) [53].

Results

Haplotype-resolved breed-specific reference genomes

We used the trio binning approach [26] to generate two breed-specific equine reference genomes, a haplotype-resolved assembly for the Friesian horse (EquCab_Friesian_WUR) and for the Dutch Warmblood horse (EquCab_Warmblood_WUR). Heterozygosity of the F1 was estimated to be 0.4% (Additional file 1: Figure S1). TrioCanu successfully assigned 91.1% of the F1 reads (97.3% of bases) to a parental haplotype, with 6,178,512 reads assigned to the maternal haplotype (Warmblood) and 5,805,245 reads assigned to the paternal haplotype (Friesian) (Fig. 1). The assigned parental reads were assembled using Flye v2.9 [28], resulting in an initial assembly comprising 2,288 contigs with an N50 of 36.6 Mb for the Friesian and 2,608 contigs with an N50 of 35.1 Mb for the Warmblood. Hence, we produced chromosome-arm level assemblies superior to EquCab3.0 (Table 1). The reference-guided scaffolded assemblies and LD information were congruent and no contigs nor scaffolds were re-ordered (Additional file 1: Figure S2). The consensus quality (QV) values of > Q40 (corresponding to > 99.99% accuracy) indicated high quality and completeness of the assemblies evaluated by Merqury [37] (Additional file 2: Table S1). Final assembly statistics for the Friesian and Warmblood as well as the equine reference genomes EquCab3.0 and T2T are presented in Table 1.

Repeat content and gene annotation

Repeat content

Repeats were found to cover 33.93% of the Friesian and 33.79% of the Warmblood genome. The most common repeats were long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), covering 16.98% of the Friesian and 16.80% of the Warmblood genome. Furthermore, 5.47% and 5.45% of the bases were long terminal repeats (LTRs), 4.83% and 4.79% were short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), 2.39% and 2.36% were DNA transposons, and 0.96% and 0.96% were low-complexity, simple or satellite repeats, for the Friesian and Warmblood genome respectively. The remaining 3.29% of the repeats in the Friesian and 3.42% of the repeats in the Warmblood remained unclassified (Additional file 2: Table S2; Table S3).

Centromeres and telomeres

All tandem repeats larger than 2,000 bp were visualized to assess centromeric and telomeric sequences (Fig. 2). Almost all metacentric chromosomes (Chr1 – Chr13, ChrX) had telomeric sequences (TTAGGG)n larger than 200 bp at the beginning and the end in both assemblies (Fig. 2). An exception was chromosome 11, for which no telomeric sequences were found at the end of the chromosome in both reference genomes. Telomeric sequences larger than 200 bp were not found on metacentric chromosome 2 in the Friesian and were only found at the end of metacentric chromosome 6 in the Warmblood. All acrocentric chromosomes (Chr14 – Chr31) had telomeric sequences larger than 200 bp at the end of the chromosomes in both assemblies, except for chromosome 24 in the Warmblood. Some of the acrocentric chromosomes had telomeric sequences at the beginning of the chromosomes (chromosome 14, 15, 17, 21, 23, 24, 29, 30 in the Warmblood and chromosome 15, 23, 29 in the Friesian) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, most chromosomes contained at least one known horse satellite DNA region (37cen, 2PI, or EC137), of which the 37cen sequence was found to be most abundant (Additional file 2: Table S4).

In addition, the Friesian and Warmblood genomes had 18.4% and 62.0% fewer non-coding genes compared to EquCab3, respectively (Table 3).

Gene families containing only genes from either the Friesian, Warmblood or EquCab3.0 assemblies were compared to the NR database (Additional file 2: Table S5-7). Interestingly, highly similar protein families were found for both the Friesian and Warmblood assemblies, namely those for cationic amino acid transporter 4, spermatogenesis-associated protein 31D4, actin, myosin, tubulin beta-6 and finally sodium channel protein type 2, 8 & 10 subunit alpha (Additional file: Table S5, Table S6). From those families, only the cationic amino acid transporter 4 was found in the twenty largest families specific for EquCab3.0, with over double the number of genes (27 in EquCab3.0 compared to 10 and 13 in the Friesian and Warmblood) (Additional file 2: Table S7). One genome specific (pseudo)transposase family (12 genes) was found for the Warmblood whereas none were found for the Friesian and six were found in the 20 largest families specific for EquCab3.0, in total consisting of 268 genes (Additional file 2: Table S5-7). Moreover, investigating the 19 gene families with the largest difference in the number of genes between EquCab3.0 and Friesian revealed six additional (pseudo)transposase families containing 415 genes in EquCab3.0 but only three in Friesian, eight in Warmblood and 15 in Donkey (Additional file 2: Table S8). Combining those families represent a difference of 680 and 663 fewer transposase genes in Friesian and Warmblood, respectively, compared to EquCab3.0. This difference in retrotransposons would explain 39.6% and 41.5% of the difference in the number of annotated coding genes between EquCab3.0 and the Friesian and Warmblood genome, respectively.

Major histocompatibility complex

We assessed the structural accuracy and gene annotation of the horse MHC region in our Friesian and Warmblood genomes. The assemblies and annotations were largely consistent between the two haplotypes, showing no major rearrangements across the MHC class I, II, and III regions on chromosome 20. This indicates a high degree of conservation in the structural organization and gene content of the MHC across these horse breeds (Table 5).

Structural variation

Both the Friesian and Warmblood scaffolded assemblies using the T2T assembly as reference, were completely colinear with the T2T assembly as well as with each other (Fig. 3), indicating no large (chromosomal) rearrangements.

Genome alignments.A The Friesian assembly aligned to T2T assembly. B The Warmblood assembly aligned to T2T assembly. C The Warmblood assembly aligned to the Friesian assembly

The number and cumulative length of each type of structural variation are presented in Table 6. Here, the reference genome refers to the Friesian assembly and the query genome refers to the Warmblood assembly. Interestingly, the number of duplications in the query (complementary to deletions in the Friesian) were 10-fold higher than the number of duplications in the reference (complementary to deletions in the Warmblood) (Table 6). Many deletions in the Friesian were found on chromosomes with larger repeated regions in Warmblood than Friesian (Fig. 2), resulting in some chromosomes being clearly longer in Warmblood (Additional file 1: Figure S3). Moreover, many structural variations were located in repeated regions (Fig. 2; Additional file 1: Figure S3). For example, for the acrocentric chromosomes 14, 15, 17, 23, 24, 26, 27, and 30, many structural variations were located at the beginning of the chromosome (Additional file 1: Figure S3) corresponding to repeated regions (Fig. 2). Furthermore, there were two large highly differentiated regions (HDR) between the two assemblies that were > 1 Mb (Additional file 3). These were located on Chr13 and Chr20 and were both located in highly repeated regions (Additional file 1: Figure S3). The HDR region on chromosome 20 partly overlaps with the MHC region.

In total, 722 large structural variations (> 10Kb) were identified between both genomes (Additional file 3). Of these, 14 affect the coding sequence of protein-coding genes (e.g. coding sequence variant, coding transcript variant, transcript amplification, 3’ UTR variant, or 5’ UTR variant). Most large structural variations overlap with centromeric or telomeric regions (Fig. 2; Additional file 1: Figure S3). Especially the large structural variations outside these repeated regions affecting the coding sequence are of interest, which involved the following genes: GTPase IMAP family member 5 (GIMAP5), ENSECAG00000063273, ENSECAG00000077493, melanoma-associated antigen D4-like (MAGED4), nuclear RNA export factor 2 and 3 (NXF2 and NXF3), and Sorting Nexin 6 (SNX6) involved in intracellular trafficking [54]. A full overview of the structural variation between the Friesian and Warmblood can be found in Additional file 3.

Discussion

Here, we present two novel breed-specific equine reference genomes for the Friesian horse (EquCab_Friesian_WUR) and Dutch Warmblood horse (EquCab_Warmblood_WUR). The trio binning approach resulted in two haplotype resolved genome assemblies. Both genomes are of higher quality than the previous equine genome, EquCab3.0 [7], but not as complete as the recently built equine T2T assembly (GCA_041296265.1) [17]. The developed breed-specific reference genomes will be highly valuable to map breed-specific sequence data, as well as for comparisons with other breeds.

In this study, we developed high quality genome assemblies outperforming the previous equine reference genome EquCab3. Namely, BUSCO scores of both genomes were comparable to EquCab3.0 but showed 50% fewer duplicate copies (Table 2). In addition, the Friesian and Warmblood genomes had ~ 24 fold higher contig N50 and were 2.1% and 3.1% longer, respectively. This means that EquCab3.0 contains the majority of genes, but the novel genomes have higher genome completeness regarding telomeric and centromeric regions. We found that the majority of the chromosomes in both the Friesian and Warmblood genome contained telomeric sequences and at least one known horse satellite DNA region (37cen, 2PI, or EC137) (Fig. 2, Additional file 2: Table S4). The 37cen sequence was found to be most abundant, which is in concordance with Piras et al. [55], who identified 37cen as the major satellite family in the domesticated horse being present on all chromosomes except chromosomes 2 and 11. The 2PI and EC137 satellites were found on some chromosomes, both metacentric and acrocentric (Additional file 2: Table S4), and the chromosomes for which they were identified were similar to previous analyses on the chromosomal distribution of DNA satellites [45, 55]. Moreover, for some chromosomes, we did not find all satellite DNA as previously described [45, 55] nor did we find any satellite DNA in either of the genomes for chromosome 2, 11, 16, 18, and 22. This was expected for chromosome 11, which is known to be satellite-free [6]. The lack of satellite DNA on the other chromosomes could be due to ONT alone not having the accuracy to span all repeat rich centromeres.

Notably, the number of annotated genes in the novel genomes were found to be lower than in EquCab3.0, but comparable to the recently published reference genome for the Finn horse (EquCab_Finn), which was assembled using PacBio and Omni-C data of a Finnhorse mare [16]. Six of the largest EquCab3.0 specific gene families were homologous to (pseudo) transposons, while only one of these families was found to be specific in the Warmblood and none in the Friesian. Hence, we hypothesize that the lower number of annotated genes in Friesian and Warmblood can partly be explained by a larger number of transposon genes that were annotated as protein coding in EquCab3.0.

The trio binning approach with long read data (R10.4, Q20+) of the F1 and scaffolding against the recently developed equine T2T assembly [17], yielded high quality haplotype-resolved genome assemblies. The heterozygosity level of 0.4% for the F1 horse resulted in successful haplotype separation of 91.1% of the F1 reads. Generally a high heterozygosity level of the F1 makes trio binning easier [26]. For practical reasons, we chose an already existing F1 horse (intraspecific hybrid) for this study, and therefore, no interspecific hybrid had to be generated, which would result in an even higher heterozygosity level. Still, the heterozygosity level of the F1 we obtained was higher than observed in studies for human (~ 0.1) [26], but lower than bovid (~ 0.9%) [26], Arabidopsis (~ 1.4%) [26], zebra finch (~ 1.6%) [56] and the wood tiger moth (~ 1.9%) [57].

Our assessment of the structural accuracy and gene annotation of the horse MHC region in the Friesian and Warmblood genomes highlights the complexity of this gene-dense locus. The results in our study are consistent with the organization of class-II genes in Villuma et al. [58]. Notably, both the Friesian and Warmblood genome have two Eqca-DRB1 and BRD2 genes annotated, whereas there is only one copy described in Viluma et al. [58]. Hence, comparing our results with the upcoming T2T annotation will be crucial for refining the MHC locus, particularly in MHC-class-II.

The sequenced F1 cross was a female horse, and therefore, we were able to capture the X-chromosomes for both genomes, which contains a substantial number of genes. Consequently, the Y-chromosome was not included in the genomes. However, in modern horses, variability on the Y-chromosome is very low [59], because of regulated breeding programs as well as low effective population size of males [60, 61]. Furthermore, an assembly of the modern horse Y-chromosome was published by Janečka et al. [62].

The Friesian and Warmblood genomes will make a valuable contribution to an equine pangenome which is currently underway. A pangenome encompasses the complete genetic diversity within a species by integrating multiple genomes. This approach is especially valuable for uncovering structural variants between breeds, such as insertions, deletions, and rearrangements that are often missed in conventional genome analyses. For Friesian and Warmblood horses, breeds known for their distinct traits, the equine pangenome could reveal unique structural variants that contribute to their specialized characteristics.

Conclusions

We present two novel breed-specific equine reference genomes for the Friesian horse (EquCab_Friesian_WUR) and Dutch Warmblood horse (EquCab_Warmblood_WUR). These genomes outperform the previous equine reference genome (EquCab3.0) and are valuable for future equine genetic analysis, breed conservation efforts as well as for the equine pangenome currently underway.
