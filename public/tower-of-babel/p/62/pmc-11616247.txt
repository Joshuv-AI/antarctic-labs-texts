Abandonment of paediatric peripheral intravenous catheter insertion in the emergency department: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zoe Clarke, Clayton Lam, Amy L Sweeny, Peter J Snelling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimChildren and their families have reported peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) insertion as the most stressful part of their emergency department (ED) encounter, with some enduring multiple attempts without a successful insertion. The purpose of this study was to identify factors associated with abandonment of paediatric PIVC insertion.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Gold Coast University Hospital. All patients 16 years of age and younger, presented in 2019 with a PIVC insertion attempted in the ED were eligible. The electronic medical records were screened by two reviewers to identify those who required a PIVC insertion. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess variables associated with PIVC insertion abandonment.ResultsOf 6394 records screened, 2401 (8.3%) had a PIVC insertion attempted, with 99 (4.1%) being abandoned. Age <12 months was the strongest predictor of PIVC abandonment at a rate of 11.3% (38/336), with a >10‐fold increased risk for infants less than 3 months old and 3–12 months old; adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) 12.4 (5.1–30.2) and 14.8 (5.8–37.4), respectively. Indications of ‘infection’ or ‘rehydration’ were associated with a decreased likelihood of abandonment when compared to ‘investigation only’ in multivariate modelling (odds ratio (95% confidence interval): 0.181 (0.099–0.332) and 0.262 (0.100–0.686), respectively).ConclusionsThis study suggests the rate of PIVC insertion abandonment in children is relatively infrequent. However, more than one in 10 children aged <12 months had PIVC attempts without successful insertion. PIVC abandonment was less likely when there was an indication that necessitated PIVC insertion, such as a serious bacterial infection.

Peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) insertion is the most frequently performed procedure in the paediatric emergency department (ED). 1 , 2 A PIVC is often inserted in children and adolescents to obtain a blood sample for pathology tests and provides a route for administration of intravenous medications and fluid. Over two‐thirds of hospitalised children require a PIVC during their hospital stay. 2 However, PIVC insertion in the paediatric population can be difficult, with first attempt PIVC insertion success rates reported as low as 31%. 3 , 4 Needless to say, many children and their families have reported PIVC insertion as the most stressful part of their health‐care encounter. 5 , 6

Paediatric PIVC insertion can be a challenging procedure for a variety of reasons. Patient factors for difficult intravenous access (DIVA) include having no visible or palpable veins, and younger age. 7 , 8 Potential for DIVA should be assessed through history, medical records and clinical assessment, with numerous validated tools available for use. 9 , 10 , 11 Clinician competence is also important, as increased confidence levels and success rates are associated with practice and the experience level of the clinician. 8 , 12 Additionally, dynamic ultrasound guidance has been shown to improve success of PIVC insertion compared with landmark technique in children with DIVA. 13

Although there have been multiple studies evaluating DIVA in children based on first‐pass failure, the factors associated with children in whom PIVC access was unable to be obtained (i.e. abandoned) after being attempted is currently unknown. This is of importance as knowledge of these factors may avoid unnecessary patient and family distress with early escalation to avoid delaying important treatment or reduce unnecessary PIVC insertions that may not have been indicated in the first instance. The objective of this study was to identify factors associated with abandonment of PIVC insertion, evaluated from a retrospective cohort of children and adolescents who presented to the ED.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted using data from the hospital electronic medical record (EMR) for an entire calendar year (1 January 2019 to 31 December 2019) at the Gold Coast University Hospital. The Gold Coast University Hospital is an academic hospital with a co‐located paediatric ED, providing tertiary level paediatric care for several subspecialities and critical care for the catchment area from Logan to Northern New South Wales. The Gold Coast Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee approved the study (LNR/2019/QGC/55041). The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 14

Selection of participants

EMR data on all ED presentations of patients 16 years of age or younger within the time period were exported into an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA). This spreadsheet data included presenting complaint, diagnosis, ED length of stay and disposition. Patients were eligible for the study if they had a PIVC insertion attempted in the ED, including if they were transferred from another hospital. Each spreadsheet entry was initially screened for the likelihood of having a PIVC insertion attempt by two independent reviewers experienced in paediatrics (ZC, PS). Inclusion criteria were admission to hospital, ED length of stay greater than 4 h, and/or presenting complaint/conditions that could potentially be associated with PIVC insertion (e.g. abdominal pain, cellulitis) (Table S1, Supporting Information). This initial overall list was generated by combining the lists generated by the reviewers.

The full EMR entries of included patients were then reviewed to determine whether a PIVC had been attempted in the ED, as per documentation in the doctor or nursing notes or as evidenced by the administration of intravenous medications as per the medication chart. Patients were excluded if they had a PIVC inserted pre‐hospital by paramedics, at another hospital prior to transfer, or during their hospital admission, but did not have a PIVC insertion attempted in the ED. Abandonment was defined as not obtaining PIVC access after at least one failed PIVC insertion attempt in the ED.

Data collection

Variables automatically extracted included age, sex, presenting complaint, Australasian Triage Scale (ATS) category, mode of arrival, discharge disposition, date and time of arrival and ED discharge and principal diagnosis. Variables manually extracted from the doctor or nursing notes and medication chart included the number of recorded attempts, designation of clinician initially attempting PIVC, escalation after failed attempt, use of sedation, use of adjuncts (e.g. ultrasound, vein finder, infrared), indication for insertion, final ED working diagnosis, the medical and perinatal history of the child, treatments administered and PIVC complications. Chronic or complex medical conditions were defined as either long term (i.e. greater than 12 months) or life‐long. Birth gestation data for prematurity was collected from all patients, which was assumed to be ‘not premature’ if not recorded in the EMR. The full hospital admission of patients with PIVC insertion abandonment in the ED was reviewed to capture additional information on their outcomes. The reason for abandonment was determined from review of the EMR, with consensus from the two reviewers.

Outcomes

The main outcome of the study was identifying factors associated with abandonment of PIVC insertion in paediatric patients in the ED. Secondary outcomes included describing an overview of PIVC insertion attempts and to determine any association between the use of ultrasound and successful PIVC insertion.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics produced included frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Normality of continuous variables (e.g. age) was assessed, and means or medians were produced as appropriate, along with standard deviations or interquartile ranges, respectively. Data elements with more than 20% of data missing were not considered for multivariable inferential analyses.

The χ 2 test was used to identify variables univariately significantly associated with abandonment; a P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Then, binomial logistic regression models were built considering the following variables: sex, age group (<3 months, 3 months to <1 year, 1 year to <3 years, 3 years to <6 years, 6–10 years and 12+ years), ATS (1, 2, 3 and 4/5), prematurity, chronic or complex history, and each indication versus all others. The age group categories were based on stages of child development: newborn, infant, toddler, pre‐school, school‐age and adolescence. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated. Variables univariately associated with abandonment were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model: only those variables that remained statistically significantly associated with PIVC abandonment were retained.

Results

There were 28 937 paediatric patients identified within the 12‐month period, with 6394 patients' records screened from the exported spreadsheet as potentially having had a PIVC insertion attempted. A total of 2401 patients were confirmed from EMR documentation to have had a PIVC insertion attempted in the ED. Of the 2401 attempted PIVC insertions in the ED, 95.9% (n = 2302) were eventually successful and 4.1% (n = 99) were abandoned after at least one initial attempt (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study from an entire calendar year, identified a small yet significant proportion of children and adolescents who had their PIVC insertion abandoned in the ED. PIVC insertion abandonment is important, as it represents a group of paediatric patients potentially enduring multiple failed PIVC insertion attempts. A subsection of this group may also be at the extreme end of the spectrum of DIVA, whereby despite multiple attempts, PIVC access could not be gained in these patients. There is currently no established data on PIVC insertion abandonment rates and subsequent management, particularly in the paediatric ED setting.

Two‐thirds of this group were younger than 3 years of age, with the strongest association in the under 12 months group. This is likely due to their smaller calibre veins being poorly visible and palpable, as well as being uncooperative and difficult to restrain. This is consistent with literature demonstrating children younger than 3 years of age are considered an independent variable for DIVA. 11 , 13 , 15 As the under 12‐month‐old age group is strongly associated with PIVC abandonment, additional resources, expertise or interventions should be considered when attempting PIVC insertion in this age group. Along with distraction, holding or other restraint techniques to maximise cooperativeness in this age group, there should also be consideration for the use of sedation, an experienced operator or ultrasound. 9 , 13 Additionally, this age group should have early involvement of senior decision‐making before PIVC insertion, so as to consider the indication or alternative routes for treatment administration.

There remains a variety of indications where PIVC insertion is deemed necessary in the paediatric ED, with PIVCs primarily utilised for fluid administration, intravenous antibiotics, analgesia and sedation. 16 In this study, children admitted with infection were least likely to have their PIVC abandoned. This potentially reflects the time critical nature of initiating intravenous antibiotics to prevent complications such as sepsis, or where oral antibiotic therapy had failed or was anticipated to fail. Ultimately, this meant abandonment was not an option regardless of PIVC difficulty. This contrasts with PIVC abandonment in the ED for a child going to the operating theatre, where PIVC insertion can be performed by an anaesthetist in a more controlled environment. It also was reported that more than a quarter of PIVCs inserted in the paediatric ED are not utilised, notwithstanding for pathology investigations. This highlights how early planning and consideration of the patient indication for PIVC insertion is necessary. 17

Early identification of patients with DIVA is crucial, as they may receive suboptimal care with delayed treatment or unnecessary pain and distress through repeated failed attempts. However, as our study demonstrated, there are other reasons for abandonment of PIVC insertion attempts. These include reassuring pathology results, utilisation of alternative forms of treatment administration or enlisting clinicians from another department for insertion. Reducing the number of inappropriate PIVC insertions in paediatric patients should become a clinical priority. Evidently, multiple attempts are painful and can lead to the development of needle phobia or long‐term vessel damage. 18 , 19 Ideally, clinicians should limit themselves to a maximum of two attempts before escalating to a more experienced clinician or use of an adjunct, such as ultrasound. This can potentially be formalised by utilising clinical prediction tools such as the DIVA score and establishing escalation pathways. 8 , 20 Furthermore, auditing and quality assurance of PIVC insertion practices could be improved by having a searchable PIVC entry in the EMR, which could include details of the operators, attempts and use of sedation or adjuncts.

Although ultrasound was used in a higher proportion of patients with successful insertion in this study, its use was statistically associated with abandonment in crude analysis. Given that ultrasound has been demonstrated to improve first and overall attempt success rates, particularly in children with DIVA, 13 the study findings could be related to any combination of factors. These could include poor documentation of its use in the successful group, inexperienced operators and highly uncooperative children who were inadequately restrained or sedated. However, it could be that this group represented a higher proportion of patients with DIVA at the extreme end of the spectrum of difficulty.

Limitations

The major limitation of this study was the retrospective study design, which likely resulted in information bias from poor documentation and subsequently having large amounts of missing data. For example, the use of inhaled nitrous oxide was poorly documented, so its use was under reported. Additionally, our methodology for initial and subsequent manual screening for inclusion of patients created a risk of selection bias. However, reviewing all the individual records from the entire cohort of patients was not possible or practical. This bias was mitigated by broad inclusion criteria applied by two reviewers with significant paediatric experience. The inclusion criteria was designed to be over‐representative of patients potentially having a PIVC inserted, so it is likely that the vast majority of patients who had a PIVC insertion attempted were captured through this process. Given that this study was single‐centred and conducted in a tertiary level paediatric ED, the findings may not be generalisable to other hospitals or departments. Strengths of the study included being able to collate data for a large cohort of patients across an entire calendar year, to allow the comparison of successful versus abandonment of paediatrics PIVC insertion attempts.

Conclusions

In this single‐centre, retrospective cohort study, we identified factors associated with PIVC insertion abandonment in children. Although the study was limited by poor documentation and missing data, it was shown that PIVC abandonment was strongly associated with younger age (<12 months) and lack of a robust indication for insertion. Reducing the rate of PIVC insertion attempts and abandonment should be a priority to limit patient distress and delays to treatment. Further research with a prospective observational study is now required to further evaluate this patient group.
