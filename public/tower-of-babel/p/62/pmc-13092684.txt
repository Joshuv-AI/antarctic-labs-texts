A bibliometric and thematic analysis of the 100 most cited articles on posterior shoulder instability
Authors: Michael Nocek, Alan D. Villegas Meza, Caroline B. Herrmann, Tyler J. Uppstrom, Nolan A. Kim, Aidan Sokup, Ayham Jaber, Lorenz Fritsch, Peter J. Millett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypothesis/BackgroundPosterior shoulder instability (PSI) is clinically significant but historically underrecognized, and the most influential studies shaping the field have not been systematically evaluated. The purpose of this study was to identify and analyze the 100 most cited articles on PSI, characterizing trends, contributors, and the frequency of themes within this citation-ranked sample.MethodsWeb of Science Core Collection was searched through June 2025 to identify the 100 most cited PSI articles. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full text. Extracted data included citation count/rate, year, journal, authorship, institution, level of evidence, clinical categorization, and thematic focus. Level of evidence was assigned by 2 reviewers, and journal impact was assessed using the 2023 Journal Citation Reports Impact Factor. Spearman correlation and analysis of variance were performed. Bibliometric visualization descriptively summarized collaboration patterns and keyword trends.ResultsThe 100 most-cited PSI articles were published from 1978 to 2021; citations ranged 28-160 (mean 60.3 ± 33.1). Most were published in the 2010s (45%) and originated from the United States (65%). The American Journal of Sports Medicine published the most articles (n = 32). Level IV was most common (52%); citation count did not differ by level of evidence, although level II studies more often appeared in higher-impact journals. Clinical entity stratification showed chronic/recurrent PSI predominated (65%), followed by mixed/unclear cohorts (19%) and locked posterior dislocation (12%); acute traumatic posterior dislocation was least represented (4%). Most represented institutions included Steadman Philippon Research Institute, Naval Medical Center San Diego (U.S. Navy), Burke & Bradley Orthopedics, and Sungkyunkwan University. James Bradley, Matthew Provencher, and Justin Arner were the most prolific authors. Most articles addressed surgical treatment and outcomes (67%). Recent trends emphasized glenoid bone loss, revision stabilization, and incidence-based epidemiology in high-demand populations.ConclusionThe top-cited PSI literature is dominated by surgical treatment/outcomes, is largely Level IV, and is concentrated in the United States and a small number of high-output institutions and authors. Clinical categorization shows it primarily reflects chronic/recurrent PSI, with locked posterior dislocation represented more than acute traumatic events. Recent highly cited work increasingly focuses on posterior glenoid bone loss, revision stabilization, and incidence-based epidemiology in high-risk populations. Although citation count is an imperfect proxy for influence, lower citation prominence was observed for diagnostic pathways, biomechanics/pathoanatomy, and population-based epidemiology. This pattern may reflect citation behavior or established consensus with incremental follow-on work, but may also indicate domains where additional high-quality prospective and population-based research remains valuable.

Posterior shoulder instability (PSI) comprises approximately 2%-10% of all shoulder instability cases and accounts for roughly 4% of shoulder dislocations.17,92,95,130 Unlike anterior instability, which is frequently associated with acute traumatic events, PSI is more commonly attributed to repetitive microtrauma and often presents with insidious, nonspecific clinical features, which makes diagnosis and management of PSI more challenging.9,108,116 As advancements in imaging modalities, surgical techniques, and pathophysiological understanding continue to evolve, there has been a corresponding rise in scholarly interest surrounding PSI.

Bibliometric analyses are used to evaluate research trends, identify key publications and contributors, and detect emerging themes.118 Insights gained from bibliometric analyses are beneficial because they are often used to guide future research directions.38,118 While bibliometric analyses have been conducted on other shoulder pathologies, such as anterior instability and rotator cuff tears, to our knowledge, no bibliometric study has exclusively focused on PSI.27,113

The objective of this study was to perform a bibliometric analysis of the 100 most cited articles on PSI. By examining publication trends, author and institutional contributions, and thematic focus, the study aimed to characterize the current state of PSI research.

Methods

Search strategy and study identification

A comprehensive search of the Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC) database was conducted through June 2025 to identify peer-reviewed articles related to PSI. WOSCC was selected because it provides standardized citation metrics and has been used in prior bibliometric analyses of orthopedic subspecialty literature. A Topic Search (TS) strategy was performed using the following query: TS = (“posterior shoulder instability” OR (“posterior instability” AND shoulder) OR (“posterior shoulder dislocation”) OR (“posterior labral tear”) OR (“posterior labrum” AND shoulder) OR (“reverse Bankart”) OR (“posterior capsulolabral” AND shoulder) OR (“posterior labral repair”) OR (“posterior glenohumeral instability”) OR (“posterior labrum tear”)). No date or language restrictions were applied. Search results were exported and sorted in descending order by total citation count.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Articles were eligible if they primarily examined PSI. Studies were included if they addressed posterior, posteroinferior, or locked posterior instability in terms of diagnosis, imaging, biomechanics (including glenoid version, posterior bone loss, or reverse Hill–Sachs lesions), epidemiology, management, or outcomes. Comparative studies were also eligible if they included posterior instability as a defined population of interest (for example, anterior vs. posterior or multidirectional with a symptomatic posterior component). Studies were excluded if anterior instability was the main pathology and posterior findings were incidental.

Two reviewers independently screened the same set of titles/abstracts and full texts, ranked from most to least cited, using prespecified eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by joint re-review and discussion; if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer was available to adjudicate. Interrater reliability for inclusion/exclusion decisions was assessed using percent agreement and Cohen κ, based on initial independent screening decisions prior to consensus.

Data extraction

For the top 100 most-cited articles that met inclusion criteria, we extracted total citation count, citation rate (citations per year since publication), publication year, journal title, journal impact factor (2023 Journal Citation Reports, Clarivate Analytics), corresponding author institution and country, and study keywords. We also recorded author- and institution-level productivity. Author productivity was defined as the number of included articles on which an author appeared in the byline (any authorship position) and the cumulative citations associated with those articles. Institutional productivity was defined as the number of included articles for which an institution was listed as the corresponding author affiliation, and the cumulative citations associated with those articles.

Level of evidence: level of evidence (LOE) was assigned independently by 2 reviewers according to the Oxford Center for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence (2011). Nonclinical studies (eg, cadaveric or biomechanical studies without patient outcomes) were assigned Level V by convention. Interrater reliability for LOE was assessed using initial independent ratings prior to consensus. Disagreements were resolved by discussion; if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer adjudicated.

Thematic focus: articles were categorized by thematic focus based on review of the stated study objectives and primary outcomes using predefined categories: (1) surgical treatment and outcomes; (2) biomechanics and injury mechanism; (3) imaging and clinical diagnosis; (4) population-specific risk groups; and (5) epidemiology and risk factors. Individual articles could be assigned to more than one thematic category.

Clinical entity categorization: each article was assigned to one of 4 mutually exclusive clinical-entity categories based on abstract and full-text review: acute traumatic posterior dislocation (single index event with acute presentation/management), locked posterior dislocation/fracture-dislocation (unreduced/neglected dislocation, often reverse Hill–Sachs–associated, and/or posterior fracture-dislocation), chronic/recurrent posterior instability (nonacute PSI with persistent symptoms and/or recurrent episodes), or mixed/unclear (cohorts spanning entities without separable reporting or insufficient detail).

Dual review and consensus: thematic and clinical-entity coding were performed independently by 2 reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion (with third-reviewer adjudication when required). Because thematic coding was qualitative and consensus-based, a discrete count of initial coding disagreements was not prospectively recorded.

Bibliometric mapping

VOSviewer (version 1.6.20; Leiden University, The Netherlands) was used for descriptive bibliometric visualization. Bibliographic coupling was performed at the institutional and author level (minimum 2 publications). Author-supplied keyword analysis was used to summarize broad thematic areas and temporal trends. A custom thesaurus standardized institutional names and merged synonymous keywords.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) with the SciPy and statsmodels libraries. Descriptive statistics were calculated as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen κ for binary screening decisions and quadratic-weighted Cohen κ for ordinal LOE assignments. Spearman rank correlation was used to assess associations between continuous variables (eg, journal impact factor and total citations, and journal impact factor and number of top 100 most-cited articles contributed per journal). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare mean citation counts and mean journal impact factors across levels of evidence (I-V). Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed prior to testing. When the omnibus ANOVA was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey Honest Significant Difference test. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Discussion

This bibliometric analysis of the 100 most-cited PSI publications shows that the PSI studies with the highest citation counts primarily address surgical treatment and outcomes, are largely Level IV, and are concentrated in the United States and a small number of high-output institutions and authors. Although the sample spans 1978-2021, publication volume clustered in the 2000s-2010s. Clinical-entity stratification showed these highly cited studies predominantly reflect chronic/recurrent PSI, with locked posterior dislocation represented more than acute traumatic events. Thematically, surgical treatment/outcomes comprised the largest share of highly cited work, whereas biomechanics/injury mechanisms and epidemiology/risk factors were least represented, indicating lower citation prominence of these domains within this citation-ranked dataset.

Citation dynamics and research trends

The top 100 cited PSI articles span from 1978 to 2021, with peak productivity the 2010s, coinciding with advances in imaging and arthroscopy and an increased understanding of PSI's subtle clinical manifestations.9,22,28,30,92 This surge in productivity is later than what is observed for other orthopedic topics and highlights PSI research as a developing field with strong future potential.1,22,27,62,113 The negative correlation between citation rate and publication age reflects expected citation aging patterns.20 Citation counts were lower than comparable literature on anterior instability or rotator cuff disorders, which is likely due to PSI's lower prevalence, diagnostic complexity, and historical under-recognition.9,27,94,113,131

The most cited article, Bradley et al (2006), was among the first large prospective studies evaluating arthroscopic capsulolabral reconstruction for unidirectional PSI.14,25 It demonstrated significant improvements in pain, function, and return to sport, and identified increased glenoid retroversion as a key anatomical risk factor.14 This work substantially influenced PSI management and coincided with a nationwide rise in arthroscopic shoulder stabilization from 71% to 89% between 2004 and 2009.25,133

Because total citation count can favor older publications, we also evaluated citation rate (citations/yr) as a complementary influence metric. Citation-rate ranking highlighted newer systematic reviews, contemporary clinical cohorts, and epidemiology/risk-factor studies, along with a prominent cluster on posterior glenoid bone loss/morphology and retroversion/biomechanics that accumulated citations rapidly despite lower cumulative citations. Together, total citations and citation rate provide complementary views of historical impact and contemporary relevance in the most-cited PSI literature.

Journal influence and methodological quality

High-impact journals accounted for much of the top 100 PSI literature. AJSM published nearly one third of the top 100, with the top 5 journals accounting for 65 articles. This concentration likely reflects the Bradford Effect, where a few journals account for the majority of articles on a specific subject.118 The positive correlation between journal impact factor and citation counts suggests a Matthew Effect, where publication in prestigious journals amplifies visibility independent of intrinsic study quality.19,59 Together, Bradford-type journal concentration and the Matthew effect can create a self-reinforcing citation loop: a small, prestigious core of journals publishes a disproportionate share of highly visible PSI articles; that visibility accelerates citations, which in turn further elevates both the articles' impact and the prominence of the same journal core. At the same time, high citation rates may also reflect the editorial selectivity and strong topical fit of these journals for PSI scholarship, given their focus on sports medicine, arthroscopy, and shoulder pathology.

Level IV studies predominated, mirroring trends in other orthopedic subspecialties and reflecting reliance on retrospective case series amid challenges conducting high-level evidence studies in surgical fields.78,80 Citation counts did not differ significantly by evidence level, suggesting clinical relevance and novelty may influence citations more than study design.104,115,122 Level II studies appeared more frequently in higher-impact journals than Levels III-V, suggesting an editorial preference for higher evidence-level studies in these publications. The rarity of Level I studies (only 2) limited statistical comparisons. These patterns highlight the complex interplay of journal prestige, study design, and research impact in PSI literature.

Geographic and institutional contributions

Twelve countries contributed to the top 100 most-cited PSI articles, with the United States accounting for 65%, consistent with broader shoulder research trends and reflective of the U.S.’s robust research infrastructure.109,111,134 The most frequently represented institutions by publication count, based on corresponding author affiliation, were primarily U.S.-based. These included centers such as the Steadman Philippon Research Institute and Naval Medical Center San Diego (U.S. Navy), which commonly manage collision athletes, military personnel, and revision instability cases, and have mature internal research pipelines. Only one non-U.S. institution ranked in the top 5 by publication count: Sungkyunkwan University (South Korea), driven in large part by the work of Seung-Ho Kim, who characterized the “Kim lesion” and described the Kim test for its clinical detection.51,52,55,56

Because bibliographic coupling reflects shared reference patterns rather than direct collaboration, these findings are best interpreted as intellectual alignment and shared research focus across centers. The concentration of highly cited PSI work within interconnected U.S. sports medicine and military programs, alongside a cohesive European cluster, suggests that the field has developed through a small number of closely related scholarly lineages.

Influential contributors and clinical impact

Author productivity and influence were calculated based on appearance anywhere on the byline, rather than restricting attribution to specific authorship positions. This approach was intended to capture an author's overall participation in highly cited PSI research, including collaborative and multicenter studies where substantial contributions may occur outside the lead or senior author roles. Within the 100 most cited PSI articles, James Bradley (University of Pittsburgh) and Matthew Provencher (U.S. Navy/Steadman Philippon Research Institute) were among the most prolific and most cited contributors, based on total appearances as authors on these studies. Their research focused primarily on arthroscopic stabilization techniques, surgical outcomes, return-to-sport, and risk factor identification in athletic and military populations.4,26,71,72,92,96 Seung-Ho Kim was another high-impact contributor; his group described the “Kim lesion” and introduced the Kim test, which remain central to the diagnosis and surgical planning of PSI.52,56 Collectively, these contributions influenced not only how posterior instability is surgically repaired, but also how it is recognized on exam, risk-stratified in high-demand patients, and counseled regarding return to sport and duty.

Research themes and trends

Keyword co-occurrence analysis identified “PSI” and “arthroscopy” as the most frequent terms, underscoring that the PSI literature has historically centered on arthroscopic management and surgical stabilization.22,96,108 More recent keywords—including “glenoid bone loss,” “posterior bone block,” “labral repair,” “throwers,” “injury prevention,” and “incidence”—appear toward the newer end of the timeline overlay (Fig. 4), suggesting increasing attention to bone loss–related pathology, revision-style reconstruction strategies, and population-level risk in high-demand groups.12,24,37,47,96,117,131 Rather than focusing solely on soft-tissue repair, current work is increasingly describing bony augmentation, capsulolabral reconstruction, and risk stratification in collision athletes and overhead throwers, reflecting an evolution toward more structurally complex and recurrent cases.24,47,96,117

Clinical-entity coding showed that the most-cited PSI literature is dominated by chronic/recurrent instability (65%), which is consistent with the broader clinical reality that many patients present with recurrent symptoms, rather than a single acute event. Although acute traumatic posterior dislocation is generally encountered more frequently in practice than locked posterior dislocation, locked dislocations comprised a larger share of the top-cited set than acute cases. One plausible explanation is that locked posterior dislocation represents a particularly distinct and memorable entity, with characteristic bony pathology (eg, reverse Hill–Sachs lesions), delayed recognition, and uniquely defined operative decision-making, which may preferentially drive citation of foundational or widely referenced reports. Finally, the presence of a meaningful mixed/unclear category (19%) is expected in a citation-ranked sample, as many influential reviews, technique articles, and cohort studies include heterogeneous posterior instability presentations or do not report separable results by clinical entity.

Thematic analysis revealed that 2 thirds of studies focused on surgical techniques and outcomes, underscoring an emphasis on operative management and outcome optimization.22,62 Comparatively, only one third investigated diagnostics and high-risk populations and less than a quarter looked at biomechanics, pathoanatomy, and epidemiology. These proportions do not establish a true “gap” in the overall PSI literature; rather, they indicate that these domains are less represented within the most-cited body of work and therefore have lower citation prominence in this citation-ranked sample. This pattern may reflect established consensus in some areas (with subsequent studies being more incremental and less frequently cited) and may also be influenced by citation behavior (eg, preferential citation among studies with similar objectives), which can amplify dominant themes in highly cited subsets. Within this context, topics such as diagnostics/early detection, risk assessment, biomechanics, and epidemiology appear less prominent in the top-cited literature and may be valuable areas for ongoing research attention.12,30,108,117,123

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the WOSCC is comprehensive and widely used for bibliometric analyses, reliance on a single citation index may have missed relevant PSI articles indexed elsewhere (eg, Scopus, PubMed, Embase, or Google Scholar), and citation counts and rankings can differ across databases. A database cross-check was performed, but analysis was limited given that citation-based ranking was not an option for other accessible databases. Second, selecting articles based on total citation count may exclude recent or emerging studies that have not yet accumulated citations, and citation frequency itself does not necessarily reflect methodological quality, LOE, or clinical impact. Accordingly, we also performed a secondary analysis using citation rate (citations/yr) to better contextualize contemporary influence. Third, focusing solely on the top 100 most-cited articles may introduce sampling bias, over-representing certain authors, institutions, or regions and limiting the generalizability of findings. Relatedly, thematic distribution in a citation-ranked dataset reflects citation prominence and may not accurately represent thematic emphasis across the full PSI literature. These proportions may also be shaped by citation behaviors; therefore, lower representation may reflect established consensus or fewer active controversies rather than an unmet research gap. Fourth, although LOE and thematic/clinical categories were predefined and independently assigned by 2 reviewers using established criteria, with disagreements resolved by consensus, these classifications still require judgment and some studies reasonably fit more than one category. Finally, bibliometric mapping (bibliographic coupling and keyword trend visualization) provides descriptive context about scholarly relationships and thematic evolution but has limited direct clinical applicability and may be influenced by citation behaviors (eg, self-citation or reciprocal citation).

Conclusion

In this bibliometric analysis of the 100 most-cited publications on PSI, the literature was dominated by studies on surgical treatment and outcomes, with most articles representing Level IV evidence and a strong concentration of contributions from the United States and a small number of high-output institutions and authors. Recent highly cited studies increasingly emphasize posterior glenoid bone loss, revision stabilization strategies, and incidence-based epidemiology in high-risk populations. Clinical-entity stratification showed that chronic/recurrent PSI predominates among the most-cited literature, consistent with typical clinical incidence, while locked posterior dislocation is disproportionately represented, likely reflecting its distinctive presentation and management. While citation count is an imperfect proxy for influence and does not capture the full PSI literature, this analysis maps indicates that diagnostic pathways, biomechanics/pathoanatomy, and population-based epidemiology are less prominent within this citation-ranked sample. This pattern may reflect citation behaviors and/or established consensus with more incremental follow-on work, but it may also indicate domains where additional high-quality prospective and population-based studies could still be valuable.
