Physiological neuroadaptations in prefrontal cortical regulation of abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking
Authors: Saurabh S Kokane, Chevin M Ray, Ayteria D Crow, James M Otis, Jacqueline F McGinty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiphasic molecular adaptations in pyramidal neurons (PNs) projecting from prelimbic (PL) cortex to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) (PL→NAc PNs) are hallmarks of early withdrawal vs. later abstinence from cocaine. However, whether biphasic electrophysiological changes accompany these molecular adaptations is unknown. Here we investigated sex-specific, ex vivo physiological neuroadaptations in Drd1 (PLD1)- and Drd2 (PLD2)-expressing PL→NAc PNs 2 hr or 7 days after cessation of cocaine self administration. Drd1- and Drd2-Cre+ transgenic male and female rats with virally labeled PL→NAc neurons were trained to self administer cocaine followed by two hours of withdrawal or one week of forced abstinence with or without a cue-induced cocaine- seeking test. Intrinsic excitability was increased selectively in PLD1→NAc PNs of males, but not females, after 2 hr of withdrawal and 7d of abstinence that was normalized by cue-induced relapse. Increased sEPSC frequency (measure of presynaptic glutamate release) in PLD1→NAc PNs and decreased AMPA/NMDA ratio (measurement of excitatory synaptic strength) in PLD2→NAc PNs in males, not females, was present during early withdrawal but normalized as abstinence progressed. In females, not males, an increased AMPA/NMDA ratio occurred after 7d of abstinence that was normalized by cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking. In contrast to findings in heroin-abstinent rats, PKA inhibition using Rp-cAMPs had no effect on augmented intrinsic excitability of PL→NAc PNs after 7d abstinence. However, RP-cAMPs reversed cocaine-augmented AMPA/NMDA ratio exclusively in PLD1→NAc PNs of females. These data reveal that cocaine abstinence-induced physiological neuroadaptations in PLD1→NAc PNs are normalized by cue-induced cocaine seeking in a sex-specific manner.Keywords: Dopamine receptors, Excitability, Synaptic plasticity, Cocaine, Nucleus accumbens, Prelimbic cortex, Drug seeking

Introduction

The pyramidal neurons (PNs) projecting from the prelimbic (PL) region of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) to the nucleus accumbens core (NAc) (PL→NAc PNs) have been frequently implicated in all major forms of drug seeking after abstinence and/or extinction in rodents [1-7]. PL→NAc PNs in rats differentially express dopamine (DA) receptors. Specifically, D1-expressing (PLD1→NAc) PNs are present in layers II-III and V, particularly abundant in layer Vb, whereas those expressing D2 (PLD2→NAc) are predominantly expressed in layer Va with very few in layers II-III and Vb [8-11]. Moreover, stimulation of D1 receptors in layer V PNs increases excitability of these neurons both in vitro [12,13] and in vivo [14] whereas D2 receptor stimulation decreases activity in layer V PNs in vivo [15,16]. Also, intra-PL infusions of DA receptor antagonists block cocaine- or cue-induced reinstatement of cocaine seeking [5,17,18], suggesting a DA-dependent effect on PL PNs underlying relapse to drug seeking. In addition, recent studies have demonstrated differences in intrinsic properties of PLD1→NAc vs PLD2→NAc PNs [9,19]. We recently showed that abstinence from heroin self-administration (SA) increased intrinsic excitability of PLD1 and PLD2→NAc PNs and synaptic plasticity in PLD1→NAc PNs that were reversed by cue-induced relapse to heroin-seeking [9]. However, a critical gap exists in our understanding of the cell type-specific, physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs that occur during early withdrawal and drive relapse to cocaine seeking after forced abstinence.

Our lab has demonstrated previously that a profound decrease in phosphorylation of plasticity-related proteins (PRPs; i.e., ERK MAP kinase, CREB) in medial PFC occurred within two hours (2 h) of the end of cocaine SA (defined herein as early withdrawal). The dephosphorylation of pERK and pCREB normalized within 24 hr. Interestingly, preventing dephosphorylation by intra-PL infusion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) immediately after the end of cocaine self administration, but not one week later, was pERK-dependent and decreased subsequent cocaine seeking [20,21]. As abstinence from cocaine progressed, pERK remained at normal levels but CREB was hyperphosphorylated [22]. Also, after 7 days of abstinence, when pERK levels were normal and CREB was hyper-phosphorylated, inhibiting PKA in the PL prevented CREB hyperphosphorylation and relapse to cocaine seeking [21]. In another study, the head diameter and GluA1- and GluA2-immunoreactivity (IR) in the largest apical dendritic spines, as well as nuclear pCREB-IR specifically in PL→NAc PNs decreased 2 h after the end of cocaine SA but increased after 7d of abstinence from cocaine vs. yoked-saline controls [22]. Taken together, these data demonstrate that biphasic neuroadaptations in the mPFC occur within the first week of cocaine withdrawal and abstinence that are not only differentially regulated but are critical for cocaine seeking. These findings led to our hypothesis that PL-NAc neurons are hypoactive during early withdrawal and hyperactive after one week of abstinence. However, there is little information about physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs, and none about PLD1 vs. PLD2→NAc PNs, during early withdrawal or abstinence from cocaine self administration with or without a cue-induced relapse test. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to identify cell type-specific physiological neuroadaptations in these projection neurons. We hypothesized that intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity in PLD1→NAc, but not PLD2→NAc, PNs would be attenuated during early withdrawal, shift to hyperexcitability as abstinence progressed, and normalized by cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking.

Materials and methods

Animals and surgery

A total of 63 Drd1-Cre (31 males, 32 females) and 50 Drd2-Cre (22 males, 28 females) rats on a Long Evans background (LE-TgDrd1a-iCre-3Ottc and LE-TgDrd2-iCre-1Ottc -initially generated as part of the National Institute on Drug Abuse Transgenic Rat Project) and characterized in Garcia-Keller et al. [23], were bred at the Medical University of South Carolina and used for all experiments. All rats were individually housed in a humidity/temperature-controlled colony room on a reverse 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 20:00 h) with ad libitum access to food and water. Rats were food-restricted to 20–30 g of rat chow during the SA procedure. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Medical University of South Carolina and were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. The design for all experiments conducted in this study is depicted in Fig. 1A and B. All rats underwent intravenous (i.v.) catheterization immediately followed by bilateral intracranial injections of AAVrg-hSyn-DIO-eGFP (pAAV-hSyn-DIO-eGFP was a gift from Bryan Roth - Addgene viral prep #50457-AAVrg; http://n2t.net/addgene:50457; RRID: Addgene_50457) or AAVrg-hSyn-DIO-mCherry (pAAV-hSyn-DIO-mCherry was a gift from Bryan Roth - Addgene viral prep # 50459-AAvrg; http://n2t.net/addgene:50459; RRID:Addgene_50459)) into the NAc (Fig. 1B-coordinates from bregma - AP: +1.6, ML: ±1.6, DV: −6.5 mm from dura) as previously described [9].

Cocaine-SA, abstinence, and cue-induced cocaine seeking test

Upon recovery, all rats were subjected to 2-hour daily cocaine HCl (0.2 mg/infusion; NIDA Drug Supply Program, NIH NIDA, Baltimore MD, USA; D1+ Cocaine group – N = 38: 19 males, 19 females; D2+ Cocaine group – N = 31: 14 males, 17 females) or yoked-saline sessions (D1+ Saline group – N = 25: 12 males, 13 females; D2+ Saline group – N = 19: 8 males, 11 females) in standard operant SA chambers (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT, USA) under a FR1 reinforcement schedule as previously described [1,9,20,21]. Pressing the active lever resulted in a cocaine infusion and activation of light and tone cues. Active and inactive lever presses, and number of cocaine infusions were recorded. Criteria for acquisition of cocaine SA was ≥ 10 i.v. infusions/session for 12 days with significant discrimination between active vs. inactive levers. Yoked-saline rats were run on a prerecorded program to receive 10 i.v. infusions of sterile saline at random intervals over 2 h. Active lever pressing in these rats resulted in the presentation of light and tone cues. Active and inactive lever presses were recorded. Following the last SA session, rats underwent home cage abstinence for 2 h or 7d. On the 7th day, a subset of cocaine SA rats underwent a 30 min cue-induced relapse test as previously described [9,21]. Briefly, rats were placed in the operant chamber for 30 min. The light and tone cues were presented when the active lever was pressed to associate renewed lever pressing with the previous availability of cocaine as an inducement to seek the drug although no drug was delivered. Active and inactive lever presses were recorded. Rats that pressed the active lever more than or equal to the number of average active lever presses within the first 30 min of the last three days of cocaine SA were considered to have relapsed to cocaine seeking.

Brain slice preparation and whole-cell electrophysiology

These procedures were modified from our previous publication [9]. Briefly, 2 h after the last cocaine SA session, on day 7 of homecage abstinence, or immediately after a cue-induced relapse test, rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated to obtain coronal brain sections containing the PFC (300 μm) in ice-cold, oxygenated high sucrose-containing artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF; 305±5 mOsm). Slices were immediately transferred to a holding chamber containing oxygenated normal aCSF (305±5 mOsm) and maintained at 34 °C. For whole-cell electrophysiology experiments, slices were transferred to the recording chamber which was continuously perfused with oxygenated aCSF containing pictrotoxin (100 μM; Biotechne Tocris, Minneapolis MN, USA). Picrotoxin was added to aCSF to block GABA contamination of recordings. eGFP- or mCherry-expressing layer V PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs were visually identified and patched. Intrinsic properties and spontaneous EPSCs (sEPSCs) were recorded with a potassium-based internal solution containing (in mM): 135 K-gluconate, 4 KCl, 10 HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis MO, USA), 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na3GTP, (Biotechne Tocris, Minneapolis MN, USA) and pH 7.35, 280–285 mOsm. AMPAR- and NMDAR-mediated evoked EPSCs (eEPSCs) were recorded using a cesium-based internal solution containing (in mM): 117 Cs-methanesulfonate (Biotechne Tocris, Minneapolis MN, USA), 20 HEPES, 0.4 EGTA, 2.8 NaCl, 5 TEA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis MO, USA), 4.92 Mg-ATP, 0.47 Na3GTP and pH 7.35, 280–285 mOsm. Standard recordings for rheobase and action potential (AP) firing were conducted under current-clamp mode. Synaptic properties were recorded under voltage-clamp mode (Vh = −70 mV). For sEPSCs, currents were recorded for 5 min. AMPAR- (5 sweeps, 1500 ms, at Vh = −70 mV) and NMDAR- (5 sweeps, 1500 ms, at Vh = +40 mV) mediated eEPSCs were evoked through presynaptic stimulation using a bipolar concentric electrode (FHC) placed approximately 250–500 μm proximal to the cell soma. Variability in the distance between cell soma and stimulating electrode led to variability in the amplitude of AMPAR- and NMDAR-mediated eEPSCs. eEPSC recordings at +40 mV were conducted after 5 min to allow stabilization of cell parameters. The amplitude of AMPAR-mediated eEPSCs was obtained by averaging the recorded traces and measured at the peak compared to prestimulation baseline. The amplitude of NMDAR-mediated eEPSCs was measured from the averaged NMDAR eEPSC trace at 50 ms after AMPAR eEPSC peak to prevent any potential AMPAR current contamination [24-26]. These measurements were used to calculate the AMPA/NMDA ratio for PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs. A subset of PFC slices from rats abstaining from cocaine or yoked-saline controls were incubated with aCSF containing 40 nM of a competitive antagonist of cAMP-induced activation of PKA, (R)-Adenosine, cyclic 3′,5′-(hydro-genphosphorothioate) triethylammonium (RP-cAMPs; Biotechne Tocris, Minneapolis MN, USA), for 1 h prior to conducting physiological recordings.

Verification of intracranial NAc viral injection sites

To verify NAc viral injection sites, 100 μm thick coronal sections containing the NAc were sliced in a cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis MO, USA) in PBS, mounted and imaged using a Leica Thunder microscope and imaging software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) for eGFP or mCherry expression. Intracranial injection placements were anatomically verified using a standard rat atlas [27].

Statistical analyses

Mixed-, multivariate- and univariate-ANOVAs were conducted using IBM SPSS (v30, New York NY, USA) for statistical analyses. A mixed-ANOVA was used to assess differences in lever pressing behavior and cocaine infusions for all rats across SA days. A multivariate-ANOVA was used to assess differences in active lever pressing during cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking vs. cocaine SA. A mixed-ANOVA was used to assess group differences in AP firing and multivariate-ANOVA was used to assess group differences between rheobase, sEPSC amplitude and frequency. Group differences in AMPA/NMDA ratios were assessed using a separate univariate-ANOVA. Pairwise comparisons of group differences were determined using a Bonferroni correction. Whenever tests of sphericity were violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected degrees of freedom are reported. GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 was used to graph the data.

Results

Cocaine self-administration and relapse to cue-induced cocaine seeking

After retrograde viral vectors were injected into the NAc, PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs were abundantly expressed in PFC slices (Fig. 1C top). Viral transfection in the NAc was approximately equal for both genotypes (Fig. 1C bottom). Injection site placements in NAc were verified to lie between AP +1.0 to +2.0 mm from bregma (Fig. 1D).

For both genotypes, the number of active lever presses was significantly greater than inactive lever presses across SA days for cocaine, but not saline-treated, rats (Fig. 1E and F; main effect of lever presses – F(1, 105)=164.66, p < 0.001, SA days – F(3.39, 355.89)=5.45, p < 0.001, and interaction between lever presses, SA days and drug condition – F(3.29, 345.28)=2.91, p = 0.030). Further, the number of active lever presses for cocaine was significantly greater than inactive lever presses in both genotypes (p < 0.001). There was no difference between active vs. inactive lever presses in saline-treated rats, indicating that increased active lever pressing was a consequence of cocaine-induced operant behavior. There were no sex or genotypic differences in lever-pressing behavior (interaction between sex, genotype and number of lever presses across SA days was not significant; F(3.29, 345.28)=0.22, p = 0.90) or the number of cocaine infusions across SA days (p = 0.79).

A subset of cocaine SA rats (D1+ Cocaine+Cue group – N = 15: 8 males, 7 females; D2+ Cocaine+Cue group – N = 13: 5 males, 8 females) underwent a 30 min cue-induced relapse test on day 7 of homecage abstinence (Fig. 1G). Both D1+ and D2+ rats had a significantly greater number of active lever presses during the 30 min cue-induced relapse test than the average active lever presses during the first 30 min of the last 3 SA days when SA maintenance was well established (main effect of lever presses – F(1, 24)=71.82, p < 0.001). The number of active lever presses did not significantly differ between D1+ and D2+ rats across sex and genotype (F(1, 24)=0.04, p = 0.85). Since active lever presses were accompanied by the presentation of cues, active lever pressing during the relapse test was indicative of cue-induced cocaine seeking. Unlike extinction operant behavior, where lever pressing is a result of absence of drug reinforcement, presentation of cues previously associated with cocaine and the resulting operant behavior reliably represent relapse to cocaine seeking [9]. Taken together, these data demonstrate that both male and female, D1+ and D2+ rats acquired and maintained cocaine SA and relapsed to cocaine seeking when presented with cues associated with the availability of cocaine.

Effects of early withdrawal from cocaine on intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity of PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs

Two hours following the last SA session, eGFP- or mCherry-positive layer V PL-PNs were patched. We found that the number of APs fired across increasing depolarization steps significantly differed by genotype and drug treatment (main effect of APs – F(2.16, 287.20)=547.78, p < 0.001 and genotype – F(2.16, 287.20)=4.99, p = 0.006). Specifically, in male rats, AP firing in PLD1→NAc (p = 0.025; Fig. 2A), but not PLD2→NAc (p = 0.755; Fig. 2B), PNs was significantly greater after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine vs. saline. However, rheobase remained unaffected in both PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs of males (Fig. 2C). In females, the number of APs fired in PLD1→NAc PNs tended to increase after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine vs. saline but did not reach significance (p = 0.080; Fig. 2D) and no difference in AP firing was detected in PLD2→NAc PNs (p = 0.737; Fig. 2E). In contrast, rheobase was significantly decreased in PLD2→NAc PNs (p = 0.034), but not in PLD1→NAc PNs (p = 0.161), of females (Fig. 2F) after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine vs. saline.

Divergent sex- and cell type-specific changes in synaptic properties of PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine were detected (significant interaction between sex and genotype on synaptic properties – F(4, 130)=2.89, p = 0.023). Analyses of simple effects demonstrated that this interaction effect was driven by significant sex- and cell type-specific differences in the frequency of sEPSCs and AMPA/NMDA ratio. In males, sEPSC amplitude of both PLD1 and PLD2→NAc PNs remained unaffected (Fig. 2G) but sEPSC frequency was significantly greater in PLD1→NAc PNs (p = 0.011; Fig. 2H) after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine. AMPA/NMDA ratio in male PLD1→NAc PNs remained unaffected after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine but was significantly less in PLD2→NAc PNs vs. saline-treated males (p = 0.04; Fig. 2I). In females, sEPSC amplitude for both PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs remained unchanged after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine vs. saline (Fig. 2J) whereas frequency was significantly decreased in PLD2→NAc (p = 0.018), but not PLD1→NAc, PNs (Fig. 2K). AMPA/NMDA ratio in females remained unchanged after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine (Fig. 2L).

Sex- and cell type-specific physiological adaptations in intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity after forced abstinence from cocaine and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking

After 7 days of abstinence or immediately after a cue-induced relapse test, eGFP- or mCherry expressing layer V PL-PNs were patched. The number of APs fired at increasing depolarization steps was significantly different after seven days of abstinence from cocaine across sex, genotype, and behavioral condition (abstinence vs. relapse) (main effect of APs – F(2.11, 395.98)=497.91, p < 0.001; interaction between APs and sex – F(2.11, 395.98)=3.801, p = 0.02; interaction between APs, genotype and behavioral condition – F(4.21, 395.98)=4.67, p < 0.009). Specifically, the number of APs fired in PLD1→NAc (p < 0.001; Fig. 3A), but not PLD2→NAc, PNs (Fig. 3B) was significantly greater after 7d of cocaine abstinence vs. saline-treated male rats. Additionally, the firing of PLD1→NAc (p = 0.029), but not PLD2→NAc, PN APs was significantly lower after cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking than that of abstinent males (Fig. 3A and B). Rheobase was also significantly altered by cocaine abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking between PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs (main effect of behavioral condition – F(2, 189)=4.67, p = 0.011, and genotype F(1, 189)=6.901, p = 0.009). Rheobase in PLD1→NAc, but not PLD2→NAc, PNs of males was significantly less after cocaine abstinence than after saline treatment (p = 0.034; Fig. 3C) and trended toward being reversed by cue-induced cocaine seeking but this effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.061; Fig. 3C). In females, the number of APs fired in PLD1→NAc (p = 0.095; Fig. 3D), but not PLD2→NAc (p = 0.855; Fig. 3E), PNs trended higher after 7d of cocaine abstinence vs. saline. The number of APs fired after cue-induced relapse to cocaine-seeking remained at saline levels in female PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs (Fig. 3D and E). In female PLD1→NAc PNs, rheobase tended to be lower after 7d of abstinence vs. saline (p = 0.097) and it was significantly lower than after cocaine abstinence alone (p = 0.008; Fig. 3F). There was no effect of abstinence or cues on the rheobase of PLD2→NAc PNs.

PKA regulation of physiological adaptations in intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity after forced abstinence from cocaine and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking

Based on our previous data implicating PKA in augmented PL→NAc excitability and plasticity after heroin abstinence [9], PL phosphoprotein expression in cocaine abstinence [20], and relapse to cocaine seeking [21], we investigated whether the PKA inhibitor, Rp-cAMPs, would affect the physiological properties of PL→NAc PNs after 7d abstinence. In contrast to our hypothesis, incubation of PFC slices with RP-cAMPs did not affect the number of APs fired in either PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs of either males or females (interaction between number of APs fired, genotype, sex and treatment was not significant; F(6.77, 412.80) = 0.818, p = 0.57; Fig. 4A and B, d-E). Interestingly, RP-cAMPs reduced the rheobase of PLD2→NAc, but not PLD1→NAc, PNs, of saline-treated males (p = 0.003; Fig. 4C). In females, RP-cAMPs significantly increased the rheobase of cocaine-abstinent PLD1→NAc (p = 0.026), but not PLD2→NAc, PNs (Fig. 4F). While RP-cAMPs did not affect sEPSC amplitude (Fig. 4G), it significantly lowered sEPSC frequency in PLD1→NAc (p = 0.012) and PLD2→NAc PNs (p = 0.014) of saline-treated males (Fig. 4H). However, RP-cAMPs did not alter the AMPA/NMDA ratio in either PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs of males (Fig. 4I). In females, RP-cAMPs did not alter sEPSC amplitude or frequency in either PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs (Fig. 4J-K). In contrast, RP-cAMPs normalized the cocaine abstinence-induced increase in AMPA/NMDA ratio in female PLD1→NAc PNs (p < 0.001; Fig. 4L).

Discussion

Augmented intrinsic excitability of PLD1→NAc PNs after cocaine SA and cocaine seeking

The most robust and consistent finding of this study is that intrinsic excitability of PLD1→NAc, but not PLD2→NAc, PNs increased after cocaine SA, persisted into abstinence, and was reversed by cue-induced cocaine seeking in male, but not female, rats. Thus, contrary to our hypothesis based on reduced CREB phosphorylation, dendritic spine morphology, and glutamate receptor expression in male PL→NAc PNs during early withdrawal from cocaine [22], when (artificially) stimulated, these neurons are more primed to fire than normal. Previous studies have indicated that there is increased intrinsic excitability in PL PNs 1–3 days after non-contingent application of cocaine [28-32] that persists for up to 45 days [29,33,34] but none have investigated physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs after cocaine SA or within hours of the end of cocaine exposure. Because increased firing probability begins as early as 2 h after the end of cocaine SA, it suggests that physiological neuroadaptations during early withdrawal may be acting as compensatory mechanisms to maintain the homeostatic balance within these neurons [35] that was perturbed by cocaine-induced hypoactivation of PL. Persistence of this augmented firing probability during abstinence is consistent with augmented spine dynamics and CREB phosphorylation in PL→NAc PNs [22]. Thus, increased PLD1→NAc PN activity during abstinence reflects the molecular neuroadaptations that emerge in PL by 7d of abstinence from cocaine [20-22]. However, unlike after heroin abstinence [9], and molecular neuroadaptations occurring in the PL after 7d abstinence from cocaine, augmentation of intrinsic excitability in PLD1→NAc PNs after cocaine abstinence is not PKA dependent. This finding indicates that molecular and physiological neuroadaptations occurring in these neurons during cocaine abstinence are mediated by distinct mechanisms. Indeed, studies in the NAc that have implicated PKA-independent mechanisms, such as sigma 1 receptor regulation of voltage-gated ion channels, influence intrinsic properties of neurons after cocaine exposure [35,36]. Future studies will be needed to identify PKA-independent mechanisms that underlie physiological neuroadaptations following cocaine abstinence in PL→NAc PNs.

Further, a key finding of our study was that cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking attenuated the abstinence-induced increase in intrinsic excitability of male PLD1→NAc, but not PLD2→NAc, PNs suggesting that exposure to cocaine-associated cues reduces the excitability of these neurons in a cell type-specific manner. In addition, we posit that these dynamic neuroadaptations in the firing probability of PLD1→NAc PNs have a strong potential to modulate NAc function. Indeed, increased intrinsic excitability in PL→NAc PNs has been directly shown to impact the strength of glutamatergic inputs to the NAc in a cocaine-induced behavioral sensitization model in mice [30]. Future studies implementing simultaneous double neuronal recordings from PL PNs and NAc MSNs are essential to determine the implications of these physiological neuroadaptations on NAc functionality and eventually on cocaine abstinence and cocaine seeking.

Synaptic plasticity of PLD1→NAc PNs after cocaine SA and seeking

Measurements of synaptic plasticity showed modest neuroadaptations across genotypes and sexes during early withdrawal and after abstinence. sEPSC frequency, but not amplitude or AMPA/NMDA ratio, was enhanced, in male PLD1→NAc PNs after 2 h withdrawal from cocaine whereas the only change in male PLD2→NAc PNs was a decrease in AMPA/NMDA ratio. These neuroadaptations suggest that divergent cell type-specific synaptic mechanisms are recruited during early withdrawal. Specifically, presynaptic glutamate release was increased in PLD1→NAc PNs and a depression of AMPAR-mediated currents in PLD2→NAc PNs. However, after 7d abstinence, increased sEPSC frequency during early withdrawal was normalized and AMPA/NMDA ratio was decreased selectively in PLD1→NAc PNs of males, indicating a decrease in overall synaptic plasticity. This decrease in synaptic plasticity persisted after re-exposure to cocaine-associated cues, indicating that unlike intrinsic properties, synaptic properties in these neurons remain unaffected after cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking.

In females, no measure of synaptic plasticity in PLD1→NAc PNs was altered after 2 h cocaine withdrawal. However, the sEPSC frequency was decreased in PLD2→NAc PNs with no change in AMPA/NMDA ratio. This suggests a decrease in presynaptic release probability in PLD2→NAc PNs. After 7d abstinence from cocaine, there was no effect on sEPSC frequency or amplitude in either PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs but there was a robust increase in the AMPA/NMDA ratio in female PLD1→NAc PNs that was reversed by cue-induced cocaine seeking, indicating a potentiation of AMPAR-mediated currents that is reversed after re-exposure to cues previously associated with cocaine. In fact, it has been demonstrated that home cage abstinence or extinction training after heroin SA followed by cue-induced relapse to heroin seeking causes downregulation of the GluR2 subunit of AMPA receptors in synaptic membranes of PFC neurons and a decrease in the AMPA/NMDA ratio in PFC slices recorded immediately after a 30-min cue test [37,38]. However, it must be noted that while the AMPA/NMDA ratio is suggestive of AMPAR-mediated synaptic changes in PL→NAc PNs, it does not definitively indicate the involvement of AMPARs or NMDARs. Regardless, these findings indicate changes in synaptic mechanisms are a key feature of cocaine abstinence and cue-induced relapse in PLD1→NAc PNs of female rats.

Sex-specific involvement of PKA inhibition on physiological neuroadaptations after cocaine abstinence

Previous research from our lab has demonstrated that PKA-mediated hyperphosphorylation of PRPs in the PL is a key feature of cocaine abstinence but not early withdrawal [20,21]. Additionally, we demonstrated that in vitro application of RP-cAMPs reversed heroin abstinence-induced physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs [9] and intra-PL infusion of RP-cAMPs attenuated relapse to cocaine- [20] or heroin-seeking [9]. Our findings in the current study indicate that PKA inhibition did not influence AP firing probability in either PLD1→NAc or PLD2→NAc PNs in males or females. Despite decreasing rheobase and sEPSC frequency in male saline control rats, RP-cAMPs had no effects in males after cocaine abstinence. Therefore, it is possible that cocaine abstinence makes the physiological properties of male PLD1→NAc and PLD2→NAc PNs resistant to the influence of PKA inhibition. In females, RP-cAMPs increased the rheobase but reversed the augmented AMPA/NMDA ratio of PLD1→NAc PNs selectively after cocaine abstinence. These effects suggest that some physiological neuroadaptations in females after cocaine abstinence may be mediated by PKA-dependent mechanisms.

Sex-differences in physiological neuroadaptations after abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking

A novel finding of our study was sex-specific influence of abstinence and cue-induced cocaine seeking on physiological neuroadaptations. Sex differences in subregion-specific, morphological, and physiological properties of PL PNs have been reported [39]. However, there have been no studies assessing sex-differences in cell type-specific, physiological neuroadaptations during cocaine abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking. Unlike males, there were no robust cell type-specific changes in intrinsic properties of PL→NAc PNs during abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking in females. Notably, a trend in augmentation of intrinsic excitability in PLD1→NAc PNs was observed in females during early withdrawal that persisted during abstinence from cocaine. This might indicate a slower or a less robust effect of cocaine abstinence on intrinsic excitability in PL→NAc PNs of females. It has been demonstrated that AP firing at more depolarized potentials in PL neurons of female mice increased after 14d of abstinence from remifentanil [40]. Therefore, it is possible that augmentation in intrinsic excitability occurs over longer abstinence periods. Additionally, sex hormones are also known to modulate physiological properties, in that estradiol facilitates neuronal excitability [41,42] and progesterone has inhibitory effects on glutamatergic transmission [43]. Our study did not control for the influence of sex hormones on physiological neuroadaptations after abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking. Therefore, it is possible that the trends in neuroadaptations in intrinsic excitability of PLD1→NAc PNs were confounded by hormonal modulation. However, we found increased excitatory synaptic strength in female, not male, PLD1→NAc PNs after 7 days of abstinence. Not only was this effect reversed by cue-induced cocaine seeking, but it was also reversed by in vitro PKA inhibition. Together, these data suggest that cocaine abstinence and cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking rapidly altered intrinsic excitability in the PL→NAc PNs of males but altered synaptic properties of females via a PKA-dependent mechanism.

Limitations associated with this study

A major limitation of our study is a lack of causal evidence between cell type-specific physiological neuroadaptations and behavioral outcomes that future studies will be needed to address. Evidence from studies in NAc have clearly demonstrated that decreasing MSN excitability via viral enhancement of inwardly rectifying K+ channel, Kir2.1, resulted in an enhanced locomotor response after non-contingent cocaine administration [44]. A recent study has demonstrated that repeated non-contingent cocaine injections increased intrinsic excitability of both PLD1- and PLD2→NAc PNs 24 h after the last cocaine injection and correlated with increased locomotor sensitization [30]. In the same study, intra-PL application of riluzole, an inhibitor of intrinsic excitability decreased cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization. While studies determining direct causality between physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs and cocaine abstinence and relapse to cocaine seeking are lacking, it is reasonable to hypothesize that altering physiological neuroadaptations in PL→NAc PNs during abstinence would influence behavioral outcomes associated with cocaine seeking.

In summary (Fig. 5), our results demonstrate that during early withdrawal from cocaine, AP firing and the probability of presynaptic glutamate release were increased in male PLD1→NAc PNs and AMPA/NMDA-mediated excitatory synaptic responses in PLD2→NAc PNs were decreased. As abstinence progressed, intrinsic excitability was strengthened but AMPA/NMDA ratio was decreased in male PLD1→NAc PNs. Unlike heroin [9], these mechanisms did not involve PKA. Further, cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking attenuated intrinsic excitability of PLD1→NAc PNs in cocaine-treated males but synaptic strength remained below saline control values. These data suggest that increased intrinsic excitability predominated in male PLD1→NAc PNs after cocaine SA at both timepoints. In contrast, there were no changes in female PLD1→NAc PN measurements during early withdrawal whereas rheobase and sEPSC frequency were decreased in female PLD2→NAc PNs. After 7d of abstinence from cocaine there was a marked increase in synaptic responses mediated by AMPA/NMDA receptors that was reversed by PKA inhibition selectively in female PLD1→NAc PNs. Cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking reversed the abstinence-induced changes in rheobase and AMPA/NMDA ratio of PLD1→NAc PNs. Overall, neuroadaptations in intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity, specifically in PLD1→NAc PNs, characterize cocaine abstinence and are reversed by cue-induced relapse to cocaine seeking. In males, neuroadaptations in intrinsic excitability were PKA-independent whereas in females, synaptic neuroadaptations were mediated by PKA. Taken together, this study firmly establishes that as abstinence from cocaine progresses, intrinsic and synaptic neuroadaptations, specifically in PLD1→NAc PNs, strengthen, further corroborating that increased physiological neuroadaptations in medial PFC are long-lasting hallmarks of abstinence from cocaine.
