Cost-Effectiveness of Highly Effective Glucose-Lowering Agents: Do Current Practices Optimize Clinical and Economic Outcomes?
Authors: Meredith Hoog, Alice Minghetti, William J. Valentine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAdvances in type 2 diabetes (T2D) treatment have expanded therapeutic options, but evidence on optimal treatment sequencing for long-term outcomes remains limited. This study evaluated six common T2D treatment pathways in the US using long-term modeling.MethodsA literature review of guidelines and published data identified 495 treatment sequences across 77 studies. The six most frequently reported pathways were selected for modeling analyses using the PRIME T2D Model. Metformin was first-line therapy in all modeled pathways. Empagliflozin 10 mg (EMPA) was added as second-line therapy in four pathways, followed by either tirzepatide 10 mg (TZP), liraglutide 1.8 mg (LIRA), semaglutide 1.0 mg (SEMA) or dulaglutide 3.0 mg (DULA) as third-line therapy. In two pathways, TZP or SEMA were introduced as second-line agents, followed by EMPA as the third-line therapy. Basal and basal-bolus insulin were fourth- and fifth-line therapies in all analyses. Treatment intensification was modeled every 3 years or when glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) exceeded 7.5%.ResultsWhen used as third-line treatment, more efficacious agents improved clinical outcomes and increased costs relative to comparators. As the most efficacious third-line therapy, TZP was associated with incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) of $50,545, $25,563, and $8,173 per quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained versus LIRA, SEMA and DULA, respectively, assuming intensification every 3 years. When used as second-line therapy, more efficacious agents further improved clinical outcomes but also contributed to higher direct costs over patients’ lifetimes. Second-line TZP was associated with ICERs of $59,434 per QALY gained versus second-line SEMA, when intensification at HbA1c 7.5% was applied.ConclusionsInitiating more efficacious therapies early in T2D treatment may improve long-term outcomes. Though associated with higher upfront costs, these therapies may reduce complication-related costs over time. Use of modern, efficacious therapies earlier in disease management may be a cost-effective strategy for healthcare payers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-026-01846-8.

Introduction

In the United States, type 2 diabetes is recognized as one of the most pressing public health challenges, with its prevalence steadily increasing over the past decades [1]. In 2004, approximately 6.3% (18.2 million) of Americans were diagnosed with the chronic condition, a figure that nearly doubled to 11.6% (38.4 million) in 2021 [2, 3]. As the number of people living with type 2 diabetes continues to increase, so does the associated burden on the healthcare system. National healthcare costs attributable to diabetes have increased by US $80 billion between 2012 and 2022, with annual costs associated with the disease reaching US $412.9 billion in 2024 [2, 4]. Direct medical costs represent the main source of the economic burden of type 2 diabetes, with US $306.6 billion being spent annually on disease-related complications and comorbidities [4]. An estimated 44.0% of patients with type 2 diabetes in the US are diagnosed with cardiovascular disease (CVD), which is the major cause of early mortality in this population, resulting in the death of at least 50% of patients [5, 6].

Despite robust evidence indicating that early, comprehensive management of glycemia, blood pressure, weight, and serum lipid levels can substantially reduce long-term diabetes-related complications, some clinical treatment strategies continue to emphasize gradual intensification [7, 8]. As a result, therapeutic escalation often occurs only after patients have experienced prolonged periods of suboptimal glycemic control. Clinical inertia, the failure to initiate or intensify therapy in a timely manner despite clear clinical indications, remains a barrier to optimal care [9, 10]. This challenge persists even within the framework of contemporary, patient-centered treatment approaches, highlighting the need for more proactive and timely intervention strategies in the management of type 2 diabetes [11, 12].

Newer classes of medications, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, have demonstrated significant efficacy in lowering glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and reducing body weight [13]. These agents offer additional cardiovascular benefits, reducing major adverse cardiovascular events in individuals with type 2 diabetes, regardless of CVD status [14]. More recently, tirzepatide, a once-weekly injectable dual agonist targeting both GLP-1 and GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) receptors, has shown improvements in glycemic control and weight reduction across a broad spectrum of patients with type 2 diabetes, including those at elevated CVD risk [15, 16].

Nevertheless, there is limited evidence on the long-term clinical benefits and cost-effectiveness of initiating the most effective glucose-lowering agents earlier in the type 2 diabetes treatment algorithm. While evidence has been published quantifying the potential benefits of early, intensive metabolic control, there is a paucity of data on the use of specific therapies and their placement in treatment pathways that could improve outcomes for patients with type 2 diabetes [17]. The aim of the present analysis is to examine the value of treatment sequencing in diabetes management by projecting long-term clinical and cost outcomes for six treatment pathways reflecting current management of type 2 diabetes in the US setting.

Methods

Selection of Treatment Pathways

A literature review was conducted to identify the most commonly recommended treatment pathways in the management of type 2 diabetes in the US. A total of 495 treatment sequences were identified across 77 articles published between 2018 and 2023, capturing the most recent therapeutic strategies, management guidelines, and supporting evidence. Across all studies, metformin consistently emerged as the preferred first-line therapy for individuals with type 2 diabetes, irrespective of the presence of additional complications. Analysis of second-line treatments indicated four main approaches of disease management: SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-IV) inhibitors, and sulfonylurea. Third-line options showed the most variation across all identified articles, with recommendations differing widely depending on the preceding treatment regimen. Notable consensus was found in the use of basal insulin and basal-bolus insulin as fourth- and fifth-line therapies, respectively.

Review of published guidelines from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE) similarly recommended metformin as the first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes, regardless of comorbid conditions [11, 18]. SGLT-2 inhibitors are commonly recommended as second-line therapy, followed by a range of drug classes for third-line treatment, including GLP-1receptor agonists, DPP-IV inhibitors, and SGLT-2 inhibitors [7, 11, 18]. Both basal insulin and basal-bolus insulin remain the most frequently prescribed options for fourth- and fifth-line therapies, respectively, regardless of comorbidity status [8, 18].

In alignment with the findings from the literature review, six distinct treatment pathways were identified for the present analysis. Metformin was selected as first-line therapy in line with published evidence and guideline recommendations. The SGLT-2 inhibitor, empagliflozin 10 mg, was added as second-line therapy in four pathways, followed by either the dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist tirzepatide 10 mg or, in comparator pathways, the GLP-1 receptor agonists liraglutide 1.8 mg, semaglutide 1.0 mg, or dulaglutide 3.0 mg. These agents were selected for third-line therapy based on their widespread use and superior efficacy relative to other potential third-line agents [19–22]. Two additional pathways were modeled to explore the use of high-efficacious therapies earlier in the treatment pathway. In these scenarios, second-line tirzepatide 10 mg or semaglutide 1.0 mg was added to first-line metformin treatment. In both pathways, empagliflozin 10 mg was added as third-line therapy. Across all six treatment pathways, basal insulin and basal-bolus insulin were applied as fourth- and fifth-line therapy, respectively. Modeling Analysis

Long-term projections of clinical and cost outcomes were made using the published and validated PRIME Type 2 Diabetes Model (PRIME T2D Model), a patient-level simulation model that has been shown in validation analysis to reliably reproduce real-life outcomes [23]. The PRIME T2D Model is capable of simulating treatment algorithms, risk factor progression, and projecting cumulative incidence of macrovascular and microvascular complications as well as hypoglycemic events. Model outputs included life expectancy (years), quality-adjusted life expectancy (measured in quality-adjusted life years, QALYs), incidence of diabetes-related complications, and direct medical costs. The model combines published risk equations (using a weighted model averaging approach for several complications) and Monte Carlo methods to evaluate the risk of mortality and diabetes-related complications based on simulated patient characteristics, risk factors and history of complications. The base-case analysis was run over a 50-year time horizon with future costs and clinical benefits discounted at 3% annually [24].

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Baseline Cohort Characteristics and Treatment Effects

The simulated population for the base-case analysis was based on cohort characteristics from the NHANES newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes cohort 2009–2018 [25]. Where values were not available from NHANES, estimates were retrieved from the first-line therapy cohort described in the National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) health economic report or from the SURPASS-2 trial population (see Supplementary Material Table S1) [26, 27]. Treatment effects for metformin were sourced from the review by Gnesin et al. [28]. For empagliflozin, treatment effects were sourced from three different review publications [22, 29, 30]. Data for both tirzepatide and semaglutide were taken from the published SURPASS-2 efficacy estimands and supplemented with data from the clinical study report [26]. Treatment effects for liraglutide were taken from the LEAD-6 trial, while the AWARD-11 trial outcomes were used for dulaglutide [31, 32]. Effects of basal and bolus insulin were derived using the Willis et al. formulae for HbA1c lowering on insulin rescue therapy for insulin-naïve patients [33]. HbA1c changes were applied in year 1 of the respective treatment line, after which HbA1c risk factor progression was based on the UKPDS Outcomes Model 2 equation published by Leal et al. [34]. For all other risk factors, changes were applied in year 1 of the respective treatment line and remained constant until the next intensification step through four lines of treatment. After switching to basal insulin, all risk factors, excluding estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and body mass index (BMI) returned to baseline levels and remained constant for the remainder of the simulation until year 50. BMI change was held constant throughout the entire simulation duration and did not return to baseline upon switching to fourth-line therapy. Long-term eGFR progression was based on data published by Zoppini et al. showing a progressive decrease in renal function over time [35]. Two different treatment intensification approaches were applied. In the first approach, intensification was assumed to occur every 3 years, with all patients intensifying to basal-bolus insulin in year 15. The second approach assumed intensification when HbA1c reached 7.5%, in line with American Diabetes Association (ADA) treatment goals [36]. Figures S1 and S2 in the Supplementary Material show the progression of all risk factors (HbA1c, BMI, systolic blood pressure, serum lipids, and eGFR) for all six pathways according to the intensification approach.

Costs and Quality-of-Life Utilities

Costs associated with diabetes-related complications were sourced primarily from Yang et al. and Yeaw et al. [37, 38]. All costs were inflated to 2023 USD using the Medical Care inflation indices from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (see Supplementary Material Table S2) [39]. Quality-adjusted life expectancy was evaluated in the modeling analysis combining published utility values using an additive approach. For each simulated patient, a base utility score of 0.80 was assigned in each year they were alive in the simulation based on the publication by Shao et al., and for each complication or adverse event experienced, disutilities were added to evaluate an annual quality-of-life utility score based on individual patient profiles (see Supplementary Material Table S3) [40].

Statistical Approach

For each simulation, 300,000 individual simulated patients were run through the model in each treatment arm in a first-order Monte Carlo simulation with sampling from distributions around patient characteristics and treatment effects. Mean cohort-level outcomes were reported for each treatment arm, and incremental values were calculated. The base-case analysis compared the pathway introducing tirzepatide as third-line therapy with the pathways introducing third-line liraglutide, semaglutide, or dulaglutide, while the pathway using second-line tirzepatide was compared with the second-line semaglutide pathway.

Results

Treatment Algorithms with 3-Year Intensification

Long-term projections with the PRIME T2D Model indicated that the treatment pathway with tirzepatide 10 mg as third-line therapy was associated with improvements in life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy over all three comparator pathways when treatment was intensified every 3 years (Table 2). Greatest clinical benefits for tirzepatide were found when compared with liraglutide 1.8 mg as third-line therapy (+ 0.093 QALYs), followed by dulaglutide 3.0 mg (+ 0.083 QALYs) and semaglutide 1.0 mg (+ 0.045 QALYs). Improvements in HbA1c and BMI associated with the tirzepatide treatment pathway as third-line treatment reduced the incidence of most micro- and macrovascular complications relative to comparators (Fig. 2). The greatest reductions were found for revascularization, congestive heart failure, and neuropathy across all three comparisons. The treatment pathway with tirzepatide as third-line therapy was associated with higher costs than the comparator pathways, with incremental costs ranging between $679 and $4,687. The treatment pathway incorporating tirzepatide as a second-line therapy resulted in improved life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy compared with semaglutide as second-line treatment (+ 0.057 QALYs) (Table 2). Clinical outcomes, including cumulative incidence of complications, were comparable between pathways applying tirzepatide and semaglutide as second- and third-line treatment (Fig. 2). Both second-line pathways were associated with higher costs than when applied as third-line treatments due to treatment time increasing by 3 years. Incremental costs ($7,123) and ICER ($125,516 per QALY gained) were correspondingly higher than second-line pathways (Table 2). Assuming intensification every 3 years, the clinical outcomes were broadly similar with the most efficacious treatments in second-line and third-line therapy. However, costs were higher in the second-line treatment scenario, due to a longer duration of treatment and corresponding pharmacy costs (without the persisting clinical benefits) of these agents.

Treatment Algorithms with Intensification at HbA1c 7.5%

Long-term projections with the PRIME T2D Model indicated that the active treatment pathway with tirzepatide 10 mg as third-line therapy was associated with improvements in life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy over all three comparator pathways when treatment intensification occurred at HbA1c 7.5% (Table 3). Highest clinical benefits for tirzepatide were found when compared with dulaglutide 3.0 mg as third-line therapy (+ 0.114 QALYs), followed by liraglutide 1.8 mg (+ 0.101 QALYs) and semaglutide 1.0 mg (+ 0.071 QALYs). Greater improvements in HbA1c and BMI associated with the treatment pathway applying tirzepatide as third-line treatment reduced the incidence of most micro- and macrovascular complications (Fig. 3). Greatest reductions were found for revascularization and congestive heart failure across all three comparisons. The treatment pathway with tirzepatide as third-line therapy was associated with higher costs than comparator pathways, with incremental costs ranging between $7,810 and $26,366. Higher costs in the pathway with tirzepatide were due to longer time spent on treatment. Mean time to fourth-line intensification in patients receiving tirzepatide was 12 years, while the three comparator arms reached fourth-line therapy after 11 years. Tirzepatide as a second-line therapy was associated with improved life expectancy and quality-adjusted life expectancy compared with semaglutide (+ 0.085 QALYs) (Table 3). Cumulative incidences of complications were comparable between the two analyzed second-line pathways (Fig. 3). Both second-line pathways showed greater QALY outcomes than their respective third-line pathway, with incremental benefits favoring tirzepatide as second-line treatment. Direct costs between second-line treatment pathways were comparable, with the tirzepatide pathway estimating incremental costs of $5,044 and an ICER of $59,343 per QALY gained compared with the semaglutide pathway (Table 3).

In the scenario assuming intensification when HbA1c exceeded 7.5%, patients were estimated to take tirzepatide for a mean duration of 10 years when it was introduced as second-line treatment versus 7 years when it was introduced as third-line treatment. Longer duration on therapy resulted in higher costs in the pathway introducing tirzepatide as second-line treatment ($180,690) compared with its third-line pathway ($176,849) (Table 3). Clinical outcomes were improved with the most efficacious treatments in second-line versus third-line therapy, driven by a longer duration of therapy with highly efficacious agents (due to their HbA1c-lowering effect). The increase in pharmacy costs (with second- versus third-line therapy) was partially offset by the reduced costs associated with diabetes-related complications.

Discussion

This is the first long-term modeling analysis examining the clinical and economic benefits associated with optimizing the sequence of treatments for the management of type 2 diabetes in the US. The findings highlight the nuanced trade-offs involved in the management of the chronic disease, particularly with respect to the choice and corresponding timing of pharmacological agents. Comparison of treatment pathways based on current practice and guidelines, and selecting the most efficacious of the commonly used agents for third-line therapy, showed that there is considerable potential to improve quality of life and reduce the risk of complications for patients with type 2 diabetes. While agents such as tirzepatide offer the most effective reductions in HbA1c and BMI, they are associated with higher costs than other treatments, particularly when compared with liraglutide. However, all of the ICERs reported from the modeling scenarios were in the range generally considered cost-effective in the US setting. These findings raise important considerations for clinicians, policymakers, and healthcare systems, as they strive to balance clinical outcomes with economic burden.

The choice of agent for third-line therapy was shown to be an important consideration for optimizing clinical outcomes for patients. By effectively reducing HbA1c levels and body weight relatively early in the disease progression, the overall quality of life for patients with type 2 diabetes could be improved, most notably by lowering the number of patients experiencing congestive heart failure, revascularization, neuropathy, and renal failure. Considering that cardiovascular disease remains the major cause of mortality and morbidity in type 2 diabetes populations, the reduction of such events suggests that tirzepatide may be particularly beneficial for patients who are at high risk, offering a valuable opportunity to mitigate the long-term burden of disease in earlier stages [5, 6]. For example, quality-of-life improvements associated with reductions in BMI on tirzepatide treatment in the analysis, likely a result of its activation of both GIP and GLP-1 receptors, are an important adjunct to glycemic control [41]. Given the close relationship between excess body weight and insulin resistance, interventions like tirzepatide may offer a more comprehensive approach to treating the multifactorial aspects of type 2 diabetes compared with other therapies that primarily target glucose control alone [16, 42].

The common practice of gradual intensification of therapy only after patients fall back into poor glycemic control may delay access to the benefits of newer agents like tirzepatide. Evidence from clinical trials has shown that early intervention with more effective treatments not only improves glycemic control but may also reduce the risk of long-term complications [43, 44]. Thus, there may be a missed opportunity to optimize patient outcomes and reduce the overall economic burden of diabetes by adhering to the traditional approach of waiting until later stages of disease progression before escalating therapy.

The analysis did not investigate the use of highly efficacious agents in first-line therapy in newly diagnosed patients, as the goal was to examine the long-term effects by applying it according to guideline recommendations and in line with current clinical practice patterns [7, 8, 11, 18]. The body of literature consistently shows metformin being prescribed as first-line therapy, with the majority of guidelines recommending SGLT-2, such as empagliflozin, as second-line therapy [7, 8]. Third-line therapy recommendations vary in the guidelines and literature examining medication prescription in the US, which is why the present analysis based the difference between the treatment pathways on third-line therapy. However, the analysis of second-line therapy with highly efficacious agents has the potential to further improve outcomes for patients with type 2 diabetes, albeit while incurring slightly higher pharmacy costs. The findings raise questions about the treatment paradigms for type 2 diabetes that emphasize gradual therapy escalation. The transition from a glucocentric approach to a more individualized, patient-centered treatment model incorporating weight and glycemic goals requires careful consideration of patient preferences, risk factors, and the availability of resources. The evidence presented here suggests that early introduction of highly efficacious therapies should become part of those considerations.

While the early introduction of highly efficacious therapies may result in greater upfront costs for the management of type 2 diabetes, therapies like tirzepatide could potentially reduce the healthcare expenditures over time by lowering the incidence of costly complications such as heart failure, revascularization, and renal failure. As the data on long-term cardiovascular safety of tirzepatide and other therapies in diverse patient populations continues to evolve, healthcare policymakers may need to assess whether the higher initial costs are justified by the potential for reduced complications and hospitalizations in the future. The present analysis may underestimate the potential for cost saving due to complications avoided in that regard [45].

The findings underscore the need for further research on the long-term cost-effectiveness of initiating newer therapies like tirzepatide in different stages of disease management. While the short-term clinical benefits are clear, long-term data on the impact of early treatment of diabetes-related complications and overall healthcare costs are essential to provide a comprehensive understanding of the true value of these newer therapies.

Limitations

In the 3-year intensification scenarios, a uniform intensification period across all patients was assumed to create a comparison of costs and effectiveness between treatment pathways. While this approach offers direct clarity, it may not fully reflect the variability seen in clinical practice. As a result, the estimated value or benefit of treatment could be either over- or underestimated, depending on whether individual treatment durations are shorter or longer than assumed.

The analysis used the same baseline population across all treatment pathways to ensure comparable outcomes. While this allows for an unbiased comparison of treatment effects, it does not reflect that, in real-world practice, certain therapies may be preferentially prescribed to patients with specific cardiovascular or renal risk profiles. As such, results should be interpreted as comparative assessments of treatment strategies under standardized conditions, rather than as direct predictions for specific patient subgroups.

The effects of the highest approved doses of the respective treatments were not evaluated. The intention was to estimate a representative cost and clinical benefit for each agent, which may reflect typical clinical use better but may also underestimate the maximum potential benefits of individual therapies. Finally, the analysis captured only the six most frequently recommended treatment pathways in the management of type 2 diabetes in the US; results from this analysis may not be generalizable to other treatment pathways.

Conclusions

Introducing highly effective agents such as tirzepatide earlier in the treatment pathway, as second-line therapy, offers the potential to significantly enhance glycemic control, promote weight loss, and reduce the long-term risk of diabetes-related complications compared with common treatment patterns and standard of care. Even when used as a third-line option, tirzepatide was projected to provide superior benefits over all examined comparators, underscoring its value across multiple stages of type 2 diabetes management.
