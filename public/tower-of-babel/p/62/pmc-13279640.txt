Artificial intelligence-driven real-time assistance in minimally invasive surgery: a technology-oriented narrative review
Authors: Jieun Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough minimally invasive surgery is a standard surgical approach because of its proven benefits to patient outcomes, it imposes increased cognitive demands on surgeons because of limited visualization, lack of tactile feedback, and constraints in instrument manipulation. These challenges have been partially mitigated by robot-assisted surgery, while recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have established intraoperative AI as a key technology enabling real-time support of surgical perception, decision-making, and instrument control. Here, we review the key AI technologies used during the intraoperative phase of endo-laparoscopic and robotic surgery, including anatomical and lesion recognition, instrument detection and tracking, surgical phase and workflow analysis, real-time tissue characterization, image-guided and augmented navigation, AI-assisted instrument control, and multimodal event detection. We also discuss key clinical integration challenges and future research directions focused on foundation and self-supervised learning paradigms, and human–AI collaborative system design.Keywords: Minimally invasive surgical procedures, Endoscopic surgery, Robot-assisted surgery, Artificial intelligence, Deep learning

INTRODUCTION

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS), which is standard practice in many surgical specialties, offers substantial clinical benefits like reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and lower infection rates by minimizing incision size and tissue disruption. It includes various techniques, e.g., laparoscopy, endoscopy, and robot-assisted surgery (RAS), aiming to achieve therapeutic goals with minimal invasiveness [1]. However, MIS poses significant challenges for surgeons, including the lack of direct operative field visualization, limited tactile feedback, and constrained degrees of freedom for surgical instruments. For instance, endo-laparoscopic surgery relies almost exclusively on two-dimensional (2D) or otherwise restricted visual input, making it difficult to interpret complex anatomical structures and execute precise maneuvers, as well as increasing the surgeon’s cognitive load.

RAS, which was developed in response to these limitations of MIS, extends the surgeon’s perceptual and motor capabilities within a minimally invasive environment. RAS can be viewed as a technological evolution that augments human dexterity and precision through enhanced visualization, tremor filtration, motion scaling, and intuitive instrument control, rather than as a mere subcategory of MIS [2]. Robotic systems contribute to improved clinical outcomes like reduced postoperative complications and shorter recovery times by enabling surgeons to perform complex procedures with greater precision and efficiency while maintaining low invasiveness [3,4]. Consequently, robotic platforms are a central component of modern MIS [5].

The da Vinci Surgical System, developed by Intuitive Surgical in the late 1990s and first introduced clinically in 1999, is the most prominent example of an RAS platform [6]. This system is widely adopted worldwide, and it integrates surgeon-controlled robotic arms, an ergonomic console, and high-definition three-dimensional (3D) endoscopic imaging to precisely replicate the surgeon’s hand movements with enhanced dexterity and tremor suppression [7,8]. By supporting meticulous tissue manipulation and suturing through small incisions, while providing a stable and immersive operative view, this configuration enables complex procedures to be performed with greater control and improved outcomes when compared with conventional laparoscopic techniques. Although the da Vinci system has dominated the robotic surgery market for more than two decades, with over 7,500 installations worldwide and more than 11 million procedures performed as of early 2023 [9,10], the expiration of key patents and growing demand for more flexible and cost-effective robotic solutions have accelerated the emergence of alternative platforms, including Hugo RAS, Versius, Senhance, Dexter, and MONARCH [11]. These next-generation systems increasingly incorporate modular architectures, enhanced data integration, haptic feedback, and artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled functionalities, reflecting ongoing efforts to improve flexibility, accessibility, and technological capabilities in robotic surgery [9,11]. The integration of AI into the intraoperative phase has built on these advances and is one of the most significant recent developments in MIS and RAS. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques, which have emerged as core components of this evolution, enable the analysis of large-scale, multimodal intraoperative data in real time [12–14]. AI-driven systems can support intraoperative decision-making through real-time visual perception, lesion and anatomical structure recognition, surgical instrument tracking, workflow and phase recognition, navigation assistance, safety validation, and semiautonomous instrument control [15–17]. Thus, the convergence of AI and robotic systems extends the surgeon’s capabilities beyond mechanical precision and can potentially enhance intraoperative safety, personalize surgical strategies, and improve overall surgical outcomes.

Currently, most clinically deployed robotic systems do not operate in fully autonomous surgery. Instead, they function as assistive technologies that preserve surgeon-led decision-making, while compensating for perception, dexterity, or experience limitations. Nevertheless, the increasing role of intraoperative AI as a foundational component of future surgical systems is underscored by the rapid growth of research on DL-based surgical video analysis and autonomous or semiautonomous robotic functions [18–21].

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the AI technologies used during the intraoperative phase of endo-laparoscopic and robotic surgery. We systematically examine major application domains based on diverse data modalities, including surgical video, robotic telemetry and kinematic logs, instrument and force sensors, and operating-room contextual and physiological signals. We also highlight persistent technical, clinical, and regulatory challenges that limit broader integration. This review aims to clarify the evolving role of intraoperative AI as a key driver of next-generation minimally invasive and robotic surgical practice by synthesizing current advances and outlining future research directions.

LITERATURE SEARCH STRATEGY

This review focuses on AI technologies applied during the intraoperative phase of endo-laparoscopic and robotic surgery. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in major scientific databases, including PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, and Google Scholar, to identify relevant clinical and engineering-oriented studies. The search covered articles published between January 2011 and December 2025, and the final search was performed on December 15, 2025. Only studies published in English were included. The search strategy combined keywords related to intraoperative AI and MIS, including “intraoperative AI,” “laparoscopic vision,” “robotic surgery,” “surgical navigation,” “augmented reality,” “surgical workflow recognition,” and “autonomous or semiautonomous surgical systems.”

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies focusing on endo-laparoscopic or robotic surgery; (2) studies in which AI methods were applied during surgery in real-time or near real-time; and (3) studies addressing intraoperative perception, decision support, guidance, control, or safety-related tasks. Studies limited to preoperative planning, simulation-only environments, or postoperative outcome prediction without intraoperative relevance were excluded. A total of 642 studies were initially identified, and 621 remained after removing duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 271 studies were assessed for full-text eligibility. Finally, 69 representative studies were included in this review based on their methodological relevance, clinical significance, and technical contribution.

Given the rapid evolution and heterogeneity of intraoperative AI research, this study adopts a technology-oriented narrative review approach rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. The selected studies were categorized into key technical domains to provide a structured overview of current methodologies, clinical applications, and future research directions.

INTRAOPERATIVE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE TECHNOLOGIES IN ENDO-LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY

Recent advances in AI have enabled a broad range of intraoperative applications in endo-laparoscopic and robotic surgery, including lesion and anatomical structure recognition, surgical tool detection, workflow analysis, tissue characterization, image-guided navigation, and autonomous or semiautonomous instrument control. These technologies aim to enhance surgical perception, decision-making, and procedural safety through the real-time analysis of intraoperative data. Although AI-enabled medical devices have achieved substantial clinical adoption in radiology and computer-aided diagnostic systems, the translation of similar technologies into real-time surgical assistance remains comparatively limited despite increasing research activity and technological progress [22,23].

Lesion and anatomical structure recognition

The foundation of intraoperative AI technologies lies in real-time visual perception and scene understanding of surgical videos. Endoscopic camera images are the sole source of intraoperative information in MIS. Therefore, AI-based visual recognition plays a critical role in compensating for the inherent limitations in human perception. However, frequent visual obstructions by smoke or bleeding, dynamic illumination changes, occlusion by surgical instruments, and continuous deformation of organs during manipulation make understanding surgical video streams challenging. Consequently, robust DL models capable of reliably recognizing lesions and anatomical structures are the cornerstone of the intraoperative AI pipeline [24].

Lesion recognition is the process of detecting, classifying, and visualizing clinically relevant abnormalities like tumors, metastatic lesions, inflammatory changes, and fibrosis or adhesions. Failing to identify subtle or atypical lesions can directly affect surgical outcomes and prognosis. Accordingly, to reduce observational errors by surgeons, recent studies have explored AI-based systems that automatically detect peritoneal surface metastases or intra-abdominal metastatic lesions from laparoscopic exploration videos [25]. While lesion recognition is often performed using conventional RGB images, integrating near-infrared fluorescence imaging can substantially enhance lesion-to-background contrast. In clinical practice, indocyanine green is the most widely used fluorescent agent, and it has been applied to tumor detection, margin delineation, anatomical liver segmentation, and biliary structure visualization during procedures like hepatectomy. AI techniques have been recently introduced to quantitatively analyze fluorescence signals or to automatically segment tumor regions in fluorescence-guided surgical images. As a result, AI convergence with fluorescence-guided surgery is increasingly regarded as a key extension of lesion recognition methodologies in intraoperative settings [26].

Lesion and organ recognition focuses on determining the presence of specific targets, while segmentation aims to precisely delineate their boundaries at the pixel level. Using DL to automatically segment organs and anatomical structures in laparoscopic images enables a wide range of real-time assistance functions, including overlaying anatomical contours onto the surgical field and highlighting regions that should be avoided to prevent iatrogenic injury. Early studies primarily evaluated the feasibility of convolutional neural network (CNN)-based models in automatically segmenting individual organs using selected laparoscopic video frames. Subsequent research expanded towards semantic segmentation approaches that comprehensively segment multiple organs and tissue types across the entire surgical scene [27,28]. Efforts have also been made to improve the stability and accuracy of organ boundary estimation, e.g., kidney edge detection, for reliable foundational information for image-guided navigation systems [29].

Surgical tool detection and segmentation

Surgical tools, the primary actuators that directly execute surgical interventions, are one of the most critical components in operative procedures. Therefore, accurate surgical instrument recognition and tracking are as essential as the identification of organs and lesions, constituting a core element of intraoperative AI systems [30]. Real-time estimation of the position, shape, pose, and temporal motion is required for reliable tracking of surgical tools within the surgical field. This information is foundational data for higher-level AI functionalities, including surgical navigation, automation, and safety assessment.

Surgical instrument detection aims to determine the presence of instruments within an image or to localize specific tools using bounding boxes or landmark representations. Early studies introduced CNN-based approaches to detect instruments and their location in laparoscopic or robot-assisted surgical videos, primarily focusing on demonstrating feasibility for real-time deployment [31]. Subsequent research extended these approaches to address precise manipulation requirements in robotic surgery via instrument-specific landmark detection or tool position estimation using stereo vision techniques [32]. Anchor-free CNN-based architectures have recently been proposed to enable faster and more robust real-time surgical instrument detection in robot-assisted surgical environments [33].

While instrument detection focuses on identifying tools or their approximate location, instrument segmentation aims to distinguish instrument regions from surrounding tissues and background, and to delineate their precise shapes at the pixel level. Surgical instruments can be segmented at different levels, including binary, semantic, and instance segmentation, where individual instruments are separately identified. Generally, detection corresponds to coarse-level localization centered on instrument position, whereas segmentation enables more precise surgical assistance by clearly defining boundaries between instruments and adjacent tissues. Numerous studies have reported CNN-based approaches for instrument segmentation in laparoscopic and robotic surgery videos, including methods combining real clinical images with synthetic data [34]. Unsupervised or weakly supervised segmentation techniques employing approaches like cycle-consistent adversarial networks and semantic diffusion have been proposed to mitigate the high cost of manual annotation [35,36]. Multiple instruments appear simultaneously and partially occlude or intersect with one another in real surgical scenes, necessitating multi-instrument instance segmentation to distinguish each tool as an independent object. The “mask-then-classify” strategy, a representative approach with demonstrated robust performance in complex multi-tool environments, provides the basis for higher-level analyses, such as instrument collision detection and tool-specific motion analysis [37]. Furthermore, modeling tool–tissue interactions to jointly identify the instrument, target tissue, and the surgical action being performed [38] is regarded as key to enabling technology for improved intraoperative safety and advancement towards semiautonomous and autonomous surgical systems.

Overall, CNNs are the most widely adopted DL models for surgical instrument detection and segmentation. Additionally, autoencoder-based architectures have been employed for dimensionality reduction and representation learning, particularly in segmentation tasks and unsupervised learning [36]. Collectively, these DL approaches improve the accuracy and robustness of surgical instrument perception and are essential building blocks for the next generation of intelligent surgical assistance and automation technologies.

Procedure and surgical phase recognition

Procedure and surgical phase recognition refers to techniques that automatically interpret the current stage and temporal context of an ongoing operation based on surgical video and instrument information. Surgical phase recognition enables classification of the current operative state by integrating visual features with the motion of surgical instruments, and it can be extended to real-time, context-aware assistance, including subsequent step anticipation and situation-aware decision support [39].

Early studies focused on classifying surgical phases using frame-level visual features extracted from surgical videos. Temporal models combining CNN-based spatial features, recurrent neural networks, and long short-term memory (LSTM) architectures have been widely adopted to better capture the continuity and the sequential structure of surgical workflows [40,41]. These approaches have evolved beyond coarse phase recognition towards the automatic segmentation and recognition of finer-grained surgical gestures and procedural steps within each phase [42].

An increasing number of studies have recently incorporated kinematic data, e.g., instrument position, velocity, and joint angles acquired from robotic surgical systems in conjunction with video data. Trajectory segmentation and motion analysis enable quantitative characterization of instrument movement patterns, contributing to learning from demonstration, skill analysis, and improved accuracy in surgical context recognition [43]. Such trajectory-based analyses have been extended to tasks like knot tying, path planning, and autonomous or semiautonomous tool manipulation, which are closely related to surgical automation [44].

In parallel, surgical phase recognition has progressed towards context-aware assistance that accounts for instrument usage sparsity and uncertainty, supporting early anomalous event detection and the prediction of surgical workflow evolution [45]. Procedure and surgical phase recognition technologies have collectively become fundamental building blocks for understanding surgical workflow, objective skill assessment, intelligent intraoperative assistance, and development of semiautonomous and autonomous surgical systems.

Real-time tissue characterization

Real-time tissue characterization, which is essential for optimal surgical outcomes while minimizing unintended tissue damage and complications, is a critical component of intraoperative AI. Because tissue morphology and spatial relationships continuously change during surgery, surgeons must make rapid decisions under constrained visual conditions with limited or no tactile feedback. In this context, DL/ML models play a crucial role by analyzing real-time video streams and intraoperatively acquired imaging data to promptly classify tissues and characterize their properties, thereby supporting intraoperative decision-making.

Real-time tissue characterization includes a range of tasks, including vessel segmentation, tissue boundary detection, classification of healthy versus pathological (e.g., cancerous) tissues, uncertainty-aware segmentation, and modeling tissue retraction and deformation. These systems can distinguish different tissue types, such as normal tissue, tumors, and vascular structures, based on their visual appearance, texture, and morphological features by leveraging advanced image processing techniques together with ML and DL models. Real-time identification and segmentation of high-risk structures, e.g., blood vessels, is critical for reducing intraoperative bleeding and defining safe operating regions. Real-time vessel segmentation and reconstruction techniques have been integrated with virtual fixtures to support safe instrument manipulation in microsurgical and robot-assisted surgical environments [46]. In parallel, DL-based approaches for automatic classification of healthy and pathological tissues have been investigated, further expanding the applicability of real-time tissue characterization in surgical assistance [47].

Among DL-based approaches, the U-Net architecture is the most widely adopted framework for real-time tissue segmentation and characterization. Spatiotemporal U-Net variants combined with LSTM modules have also been proposed to incorporate the temporal information inherent in surgical videos [48]. Additionally, generative adversarial networks and various CNN variants have been employed to improve segmentation accuracy and generalization performance. Beyond tissue recognition, these techniques have demonstrated the potential to enable autonomous functions, such as active tissue retraction and manipulation in robot-assisted MIS, highlighting their relevance for the development of semiautonomous and autonomous surgical systems [49].

Image-guided navigation and augmented guidance

Image-guided navigation is a representative intraoperative AI application aimed at enhancing the surgeon’s spatial awareness during surgery by registering intraoperative images with preoperative imaging data, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging, and intuitively presenting internal anatomical structures. Early approaches largely relied on manual processes to register preoperative 3D CT models with 2D laparoscopic images. DL-based methods have recently been introduced to automate key steps, including 3D model construction, structure identification in CT images, and corresponding structure detection in real-time surgical videos. These AI-driven 2D/3D registration techniques, which include image-to-image and image-to-patient registration, enable more robust and accurate alignment, even in realistic surgical environments characterized by organ deformation and motion [50,51]. The registered 3D anatomical information is typically presented as augmented reality (AR) overlays on the live surgical view, allowing surgeons to visualize critical anatomical structures, lesion locations, resection margins, and risk regions in real time. Navigation systems that integrate real-time 3D model registration with AR overlays improve intraoperative visualization, support precise target localization and access path planning, and reduce the operating surgeon’s cognitive load. Recent studies have proposed AR guidance systems that automatically generate patient-specific 3D models from DL-based organ and lesion segmentation and overlay them directly onto live endoscopic video streams [52,53].

Additionally, AI-based augmented guidance frameworks can integrate outputs from multiple intraoperative AI modules, including lesion and organ recognition and segmentation, surgical instrument segmentation and tracking, instrument trajectory analysis, tissue characterization, validation of the critical view of safety [54], and risk zone identification. This integrated information can be extended through virtual fixtures to target localization, optimal trajectory guidance, and the definition of safe operating regions, thereby simultaneously improving surgical precision and minimizing unintended tissue injury. Collectively, image-guided navigation and AR-based augmented guidance are core technologies that enable intelligent surgical navigation and the development of robot-assisted, semiautonomous, and autonomous surgical systems.

Artificial intelligence-assisted and semiautonomous instrument control

AI-based motion assistance aims to compensate for the surgeon’s physical and cognitive limitations by enhancing the stability and precision of instrument manipulation. Representative techniques include tremor reduction, motion scaling, and virtual fixtures, which are particularly effective in robot-assisted surgical environments. Tremor reduction and motion scaling stabilize fine instrument movements by attenuating involuntary hand motion and scaling the surgeon’s inputs, thereby enabling precise manipulation. These assistive control strategies preserve the surgeon’s intent while suppressing unintended instrument motions, thereby improving safety in micro-suturing and procedures performed near critical neurovascular structures. Furthermore, shared control and virtual fixture paradigms prevent inadvertent entry into risk zones and maintain stable tool paths by allowing AI systems to guide or constrain instrument motion based on predefined safe regions or optimal trajectories. Such approaches are regarded as collaborative assistance strategies, whereby AI supports the surgeon through human–AI collaboration, rather than replacing them.

Task automation, the most advanced application of intraoperative AI, focuses on well-defined subtasks, such as suturing, knot tying, cutting, and camera control. Most systems adopt a semiautonomous approach rather than full autonomy, with AI performing repetitive or standardized surgical actions under direct surgeon supervision. Reliable workflow and surgical phase recognition, together with objective skill assessment, are a fundamental prerequisite for such automation. CNN- and LSTM-based models have been widely used to recognize the current surgical phase and activity from operative videos, and to anticipate subsequent actions, thereby determining when automation can be safely applied and to what extent [55,56]. Autonomous workflow recognition plays a critical role in structurally understanding the flow of tasks and resources in the operating room and in enabling context-aware assistance [57–59].

Skill assessment functions as a safety-critical control layer that dynamically modulates the level of automation. AI systems can adapt the degree of automation or disengage automated functions when necessary, by quantifying the surgeon’s stability, consistency, and efficiency based on instrument tracking and trajectory analysis [60–63]. This paradigm enables human-in-the-loop automation, maintaining the surgeon’s decision-making authority while reducing their cognitive workload. Additionally, task automation is closely linked to surgical planning. For example, path-planning algorithms based on preoperative CT imaging can be used to recommend or partially automate instrument trajectories during specific surgical stages [64]. Collectively, safely governed by workflow recognition and skill assessment, task automation has emerged as a central component of semiautonomous surgical systems.

Event detection using multimodal signals

Multimodal signal-based event detection, a key extension of intraoperative AI beyond visual perception, aims to enable early identification of abnormal situations and potential complications during surgery. Recent studies have demonstrated that integrating surgical video and kinematic data from robotic systems, force/torque signals, audio streams, and physiological biosignals into DL frameworks can substantially improve the accuracy and reliability of intraoperative event recognition by capturing complementary aspects of the surgical process [65–67]. A representative experimental setup for multimodal signal acquisition in robotic surgery combines synchronized visual, kinematic, and force measurements during controlled tool–tissue interactions [68]. A da Vinci Research Kit is used in this setup to acquire camera images, robotic motion data, and contact force signals from an artificial silicone tissue mounted on a force-sensing platform. Multimodal datasets can be collected to model normal and abnormal interaction patterns by systematically varying instrument positions and configurations.

Physiological signals like electrocardiography and photoplethysmography, which reflect real-time changes in a patient’s systemic condition, can be leveraged to detect early signs of bleeding, pain responses, or cardiovascular instability. In parallel, kinematic data, including instrument position, velocity, acceleration, and joint angles, provide critical cues for identifying abnormal instrument manipulation, abrupt trajectory changes, or repetitive error patterns. These signals enable the detection of deviations from the expected surgical workflow when combined with surgical phase and workflow recognition. Time series-based anomaly detection models that fuse visual, kinematic, and physiological information can learn subtle temporal patterns associated with emerging adverse events, allowing warning issuance before such events are clinically evident to the surgeon. This facilitates timely intervention, mitigates the severity of complications, and contributes to improved patient outcomes.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

It is essential to address algorithmic performance, practical constraints, and the requirements of real surgical environments for clinical translation of intraoperative AI. One of the most critical challenges is real-time performance under resource constraints. Functions like AR overlays, surgical phase recognition, skill assessment, and closed-loop robotic assistance must operate with minimal latency to avoid disrupting surgical workflow. Even small delays can interfere with surgical flow and decision-making, particularly in applications involving semiautonomous actions like tissue retraction or instrument guidance. Therefore, achieving an appropriate balance between model complexity and real-time deployability remains an important problem [15,49].

Data availability, quality, and standardization are also fundamental challenges. High-quality, large-scale surgical datasets remain limited, and standardized protocols for data acquisition, annotation, and sharing across institutions are lacking [69]. However, some studies have constructed task-specific datasets for skill evaluation or adverse event analysis, often combining kinematic and procedural data [70]. Multimodal datasets integrating video, robotic kinematics, and physiological signals face additional obstacles, including heterogeneous data formats, sensor noise, incomplete metadata, and stringent anonymization requirements. Because of these limitations, models are frequently trained on small, non-representative datasets, which constrains their generalizability and clinical reliability.

Generalization and transferability across surgical procedures, platforms, and environments are also major challenges. Many DL models demonstrate performance degradation when applied to different endoscopic systems, unfamiliar tissue types, or new operating room conditions. Translation is further complicated by the gap between simulation-based training and real clinical deployment. Addressing these issues will require robust domain adaptation strategies, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning paradigms, and data augmentation techniques capable of capturing anatomical and procedural variability across patients and institutions [15].

Regulatory, ethical, and liability considerations play a decisive role in the adoption of intraoperative AI. AI-assisted and semiautonomous surgical systems can have serious consequences in case of errors, necessitating rigorous evaluation frameworks and clear accountability structures. Recent efforts, e.g., the IDEAL (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, and Long-term study) framework for surgical robotics, emphasize staged development, comparative evaluation, and long-term monitoring to ensure safety and effectiveness throughout the lifecycle of robotic surgical technologies [71]. Furthermore, data privacy issues become increasingly salient as advanced analyses incorporate physiological signals and detailed behavioral data [69].

Finally, human–AI trust and interpretability remain critical barriers to widespread clinical acceptance. The black box nature of many DL models limits the surgeon’s confidence and hinders meaningful integration into decision-making processes. Clinicians often require transparent, intuitive explanations to understand why a particular output or recommendation was generated, even when AI systems demonstrate strong predictive performance, e.g., in skill assessment or workflow analysis. Explainable AI and explainable robotics approaches that highlight contributing video frames, instrument trajectories, or decision constraints are therefore essential for fostering trust and enabling effective human–AI collaboration in the operating room [72].

Future research should prioritize foundation and self-supervised learning for surgical video representation, multimodal fusion of visual, kinematic, and physiological data, and human-centered AI system design that explicitly accounts for clinical safety, regulatory compliance, and the surgeon’s trust. It is crucial to address these challenges for intraoperative AI to evolve from experimental prototypes to reliable, interpretable, and widely adopted tools in routine surgical practice.

CONCLUSION

AI is fundamentally reshaping the paradigm of intraoperative decision-making by combining real-time visual perception, decision support, instrument assistance, and safety validation within the surgical workflow. AI-driven intraoperative assistance enables a more accurate recognition of anatomy and lesions, context-aware understanding of surgical workflow, and safer, more precise instrument manipulation in endo-laparoscopic and robotic surgery through motion assistance and semiautonomous functions. As they mature, these technologies hold the potential to simultaneously improve surgical accuracy, safety, and efficiency, while reducing the surgeon’s cognitive burden. Continued progress in real-time performance, data standardization, generalizability, regulatory readiness, and interpretability will be essential to ensure clinical adoption. This will position intraoperative AI as a core decision-support framework and a defining component of future surgical practice.
