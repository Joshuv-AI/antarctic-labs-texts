Intestinal ischemia: A rare and less common complication after laparoscopic cholecystectomy—A case report and literature review
Authors: Mohammad Javad Ghamari, Hamed Beyzaii, Behnoush Bahraminejad, Narges Mesbah, Ahmadreza Sobhani, Ali Mirsadeghi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey Clinical MessagePatients with a history of persistent mesenteric ischemia should not undergo laparoscopy. Mesenteric ischemia must be kept in mind for patients who develop nonspecific abdominal symptoms following laparoscopic surgeries.AbstractDuring laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), the rise in Intra‐Abdominal Pressure due to carbon dioxide insufflation can reduce blood flow through splanchnic vessels, potentially leading to intestinal ischemia. A 72‐year‐old woman with a history of diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and hyperlipidemia underwent LC. She was discharged but readmitted 4 days later due to peritonitis. Ischemic jejunum loops were found during surgery.Keywords: case report, cholecystectomy, intestinal ischemia, intra‐abdominal pressure, mesenteric ischemia, pneumoperitoneum

INTRODUCTION

The goal of laparoscopic surgery is to create an acceptable therapeutic result while minimizing trauma and metabolic effects. This results in benefits such as reduced postoperative pain, smaller wounds, lower rates of complications, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and lower healthcare costs. Using CO2 for pneumoperitoneum formation in laparoscopic surgery is cost‐effective and allows for better vision and mobility. However, it may lead to metabolic, immunological, and structural changes with potential clinical consequences. 1 , 2 Reduced mesenteric arterial blood flow, intestinal mucosal blood flow, and arterial perfusion of the stomach, duodenum, intestine, pancreas, and spleen are unpleasant effects of increased intra‐abdominal pressure (IAP). 3 Although rare, intestinal ischemia can occur after Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) with potentially life‐threatening consequences. Early detection and prompt treatment are crucial for a positive prognosis. 4

According to our search, our patient is the first case of mesenteric ischemia following LC that has been reported in Iran. Therefore, we would like to share our experience with this uncommon patient to better understand its varied aspects.

CASE HISTORY/EXAMINATION

A 72‐year‐old female was admitted to our hospital for elective LC due to a history of biliary colic. She had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and hyperlipidemia. She consumed Glibenclamide, Carvedilol, Atorvastatin, and Aspirin. She also mentioned using daily opium orally. On physical examination, vital signs were stable and, there was no tenderness, guarding, or positive Murphy's sign. Ultrasonography reported a gallstone with a 24 mm diameter and some small stones without any obstruction signs. Initial laboratory tests were normal. (Table 1) LC was performed with general anesthesia and the classic method without any problems. The surgery took about 30 min. The IAP was 14 mmHg. She then was admitted to CCU, and there wasn't any arrhythmia or any cardiac problems. The surgical drain had no biliary or blood drainage. The next day, after she had tolerated a soft meal, the drain was removed, and she was discharged from the hospital without any problems.

METHODS

She presented to the emergency department 4 days later with generalized abdominal pain. The pain had started right after she was discharged from the hospital and was progressive and constant. Associated symptoms included severe perspiration, nausea, and anorexia. At the time of arrival to the ED vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 108/69 mmHg, pulse rate 83/min, respiratory rate 18/min, temperature 36.8°C, and oxygen saturation was 92%. There was no sign of jaundice. The abdomen had generalized distention, tenderness, rebound tenderness, and guarding. Based on peritonitis evidence in the examination, she was considered for diagnostic laparoscopy. The preoperative laboratory tests are shown in Table 1. During the surgery, 2–3 ischemic loops of the jejunum middle part were detected. Ischemic patches reach about 30 cm in length. The intestinal wall was slightly edematous. This ischemia appeared to be due to venous thrombosis (Figure 1). The intestine was viable, and peristalsis was checked. Thus 10,000 IU intravenous Heparin was started immediately. No other pathology was found in the abdomen.

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW‐UP

After the surgery, she was admitted to the ICU for close observation. 1000 IU (per hour for a day) intravenous Heparin started, and 2 days later it was reduced to 625 IU per hour. The next day warfarin was added to the medications, and coagulation indices were assessed daily. Although coagulation studies including factor V Leiden, protein C, and antithrombin III were normal protein S activity was less than the normal range. The patient was NPO for 3 days postoperatively. Then liquid diet was initiated which was tolerable for the patient. Subsequently, the abdominal pain dramatically reduced, and the abdomen had no significant tenderness or guarding anymore. Besides, her defecation returned to normal. Seven days later, she was discharged from the hospital with a warfarin prescription.

DISCUSSION

Complications of LC can occur because of the pneumoperitoneum or the cholecystectomy itself. Complications of gallbladder removal include bleeding, bile leak, and damage to a major bile duct. Gas embolism, vagal reflex, arrhythmias, and hypercarbia are all known consequences of the pneumoperitoneum. 5 Porto‐mesenteric venous thrombosis (PVT) is rare but may be a fatal complication following laparoscopic surgeries. Venous stasis caused by increased IAP, intraoperative manipulation, or injury to the splanchnic endothelium, as well as systemic thrombophilic conditions, are all potential causative causes. Factor V Leiden mutations, prothrombin G20210A mutations, antithrombin, and protein S deficiencies III, have been found in 3%–30%, 3%–40%, 1%–29%, 7%–27%, and 2%–43% of patients, respectively. The most common symptoms of PVT after laparoscopic surgery are nonspecific abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea at first. Most individuals have been experiencing symptoms for more than 2 days before getting medical care. There may be leukocytosis and moderate increases in liver function tests, but metabolic acidosis is a common late symptom of intestinal infarction. 6

The celiac trunk, as well as the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries, supply blood to the splanchnic region. The arteriovenous pressure gradient and the vascular resistance of an organ dictate blood flow. CO2 insufflation raises venous pressure to a level near the IAP, reducing blood flow, furthermore makes the abdomen wall, particularly the superior and inferior epigastric arteries, stretch, potentially limiting the vessels' diameter and reducing blood flow. Increased IAP reduces mucosal blood flow and bacterial translocation, potentially leading to septic consequences and organ failure later. During laparoscopy, a modest reduction in mesenteric blood flow induces the release of systemically active vasoconstrictors such as vasopressin from the gut, which could develop into a vicious cycle producing additional gastrointestinal blood flow impairment. 7 The result of mean arterial blood pressure minus IAP is the intra‐abdominal perfusion pressure. A reduced mean arterial blood pressure due to the Trendelenburg posture and the pneumoperitoneum, as well as an elevated IAP due to the pneumoperitoneum, can predispose to splanchnic ischemia; this is especially true in long procedures. Extra CO2 in the circulation would damage the splanchnic circulation, leading to hypercapnia, respiratory acidosis, and an increase in systemic vascular resistance because of the hemodynamic stress response. “Acute intestinal distress syndrome” is a name for splanchnic hypoperfusion caused by physiological changes linked with laparoscopy. 1 Blood flow in the stomach, duodenum, jejunum, colon, liver, and parietal peritoneum reduced by about 40%, 11%, 32%, 44%, 39%, and 60%, respectively when the pressure increased from 10 to 15 mmHg. 8

In short, CO2 insufflation involves several risk factors: compression of the splanchnic vessels due to the IAP, reduced splanchnic blood flow with gradual rises in IAP, and a stepwise reduction in portal blood flow, elevated vasopressin levels in response to CO2, with concomitant effects on mesenteric vascular resistance, and reduced vena cava flow as a result of increased IAP from insufflation, and the patient's reverse Trendelenburg position. 9 These physiological alterations are well tolerated in healthy patients with normal cardiovascular and pulmonary function and have no clinical consequences. Atherosclerosis, for example, can predispose to mesenteric ischemia. As a result, patients should be carefully chosen for laparoscopic surgery, the surgeon should be competent and efficient, and prolonged procedures, particularly with the patient in the Trendelenburg position, should be avoided. High‐pressure pneumoperitoneum should also be avoided, particularly in patients with proven or suspected mesenteric vascular disease. 1 During LC, warming of the insufflated CO2 is linked to a lower peritoneal cytokine response. Insufflation should be initiated at standard pressure (12–15 mmHg) and gradually reduced as much as feasible without affecting visibility (dial‐down technique). Prepneumoperitoneum intravascular volume expansion can improve renal perfusion and lessen the severity of renal damage. It makes sense to Improve venous return in the lower limb using an intermittent sequential pneumatic compression device. 8

CONCLUSIONS

Patients with a history of persistent mesenteric ischemia should not undergo laparoscopy. In cases of nonspecific abdominal symptoms following laparoscopic surgeries, mesenteric ischemia should be considered in the differential diagnosis.
