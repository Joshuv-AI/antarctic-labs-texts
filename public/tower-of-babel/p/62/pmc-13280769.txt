Association of substance use with stroke in heart failure by community socioeconomic distress
Authors: Brian Witrick, Frank Annie, Timothy Dotson, Wes Kimble, George Sokos, Christopher Bianco, Sarah Rinehart, Megan Pate, Evonne Richards, Brian Hendricks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Substance use is common among individuals with heart failure (HF) and is linked to cardiotoxiceffects and adverse outcomes. Given the increased vulnerability of HF patients to ischemic stroke, understanding how substance use and community distress contribute to this risk is vital for optimizing clinical prevention strategies. This study examined whether substance use increases stroke risk among HF patients and whether this association differs by community distress level.Methods:We conducted a multicenter study using data from HF patients treated at WVU Medicine and Charleston Area Medical Center (CAMC) in West Virginia between 2017 and 2023. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the association between substance use and ischemic stroke, stratified by community distress level and adjusted for demographics and comorbidities.Results:Among 33,663 individuals with HF in the WVU Medicine cohort, 2651 (7.88 %) experienced a stroke. Among 8050 in the CAMC cohort, 900 (11.18 %) experienced a stroke. Overall, among both cohorts, patients with substance use disorder diagnoses had greater odds of stroke. WVU (aOR, 1.36; 95 % CI, 1.14–1.63) and CAMC (aOR, 3.50; 95 % CI, 2.85–4.55). In distressed communities, the association was stronger: WVU (aOR, 1.68; 95 % CI, 1.34–2.09) and CAMC (aOR, 3.70; 95 % CI, 3.03–4.52). In non-distressed communities, only the CAMC cohort showed a significant association (aOR, 1.80; 95 % CI, 1.63–2.09).Conclusions and relevance:Substance use is linked to higher stroke risk among HF patients, especially in socioeconomically distressed communities. Addressing both clinical and structural factors is essential for effective stroke prevention in this population.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome caused by structural or functional cardiac disorders that impair the heart’s ability to pump blood to meet the body’s demands (Malik et al., 2022). It remains a significant public health challenge, affecting an estimated 6 million individuals in the United States and over 64 million globally (Tsao et al., 2023; Vos et al., 2020). Despite advancements in treatment and care, heart failure continues to be associated with high morbidity and mortality ((Roger, 2021; Dunlay and Roger, 2014; Levy et al., 2002). Among older adults, it is the leading cause of hospitalization and carries a five-year mortality rate of approximately 50 % (Dunlay and Roger, 2014; Levy et al., 2002). The economic impactis also substantial, especially in the United States where annual medical costs exceed $30 billion and are projected to rise to $70 billion by 2030 (Patel et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2018).

Ischemic stroke is another major contributor to mortality and long-term disability, with over 300,000 incident cases recorded annually in the United States as of 2019 (Ding et al., 2022). Heart failure is an established independent risk factor for ischemic stroke, with affected individuals experiencing nearly a threefold increased risk compared to those without HF (Witt et al., 2006; Cuadrado-Godia et al., 2010). Stroke rates in HF patients increase over time, reaching as high as 47.4 events per 1000 person-years at five years, and correlate with the severity of heart failure (Witt et al., 2007; Barkhudaryan et al., 2021). Moreover, stroke outcomes among individuals with heart failure are often worse, including higher recurrence, disability, and mortality (Witt et al., 2006; Cuadrado-Godia et al., 2010; Hu et al., 2025).

Substance use disorders (SUDs) are highly prevalent in the United States, with over 19 million adults meeting diagnostic criteria in 2019 (Thyagaturu et al., 2022; Abuse, 2020). Cocaine, methamphetamines, opioids, cannabis, and other commonly-abused substances can have multifaceted effects on the cardiovascular system (Grubb et al., 2021; Nishimura et al., 2020; Hendricks et al., 2021; Gan et al., 2021; Thomas et al., 2020a). Cocaine, amphetamines, and heroin have been independently associated with increased risk of ischemic stroke, particularly in younger populations, underscoring their vascular toxicity (Cheng et al., 2016; Sheikh Andalibi et al., 2021). In the context of heart failure, substance use not only increases the risk of disease onset but also worsens its clinical course, leading to more frequent hospitalizations and poorer outcomes (Grubb et al., 2021; Nishimura et al., 2020; Thomas et al., 2020b).

The impactof both heart failure and stroke is further heightened by social determinants of health. Community-level socioeconomic status, such as poverty, unemployment, and education, has a profound effect on individual health outcomes (Kim et al., 2021; Ludwig et al., 2012). Individuals residing in economically distressed communities are more likely to develop incident heart failure (Akwo et al., 2018a; Ingelsson et al., 2006a). Furthermore, these individuals have significantly higher risks of hospitalization, 30-day readmission, and mortality following heart failure diagnoses (Patel et al., 2020; Mentias et al., 2022a). Similarly, neighborhood disadvantage is linked to higher stroke incidence, severity, and worse post-stroke outcomes (Mentias et al., 2022a; Weir et al., 2005; Aslanyan et al., 2003; Honjo et al., 2015).

While individual relationships between substance use, heart failure, and stroke are well documented, few studies have examined the intersection of these factors—particularly how substance use may influence stroke risk among individuals with heart failure, or how these associations vary by community distress level. The objective of this study is to determine if structural factors, like community economic distress, are an important effect modifying variable for stroke among clinical populations with co-occurring heart failure and substance use disorders. We utilize an innovative multi-center approach to prepare clinical cohorts from different geographic regions of our state. These are the largest two hospital systems in West Virginia, having at least one patient in over 87 % of WV zip codes. Results are expected to inform policies which encourage inter-professional care teams to help heart failure patients manage the complex social and clinical drivers of disease progression.

Methods

Study design and sample population

We conducted a nested case-control study using data from two separate cohorts of heart failure patients that were assembled from the West Virginia University Hospital Systems (WVU Medicine) and the Charleston Area Medical Center (CAMC). These health systems serve as major referral centers for cardiovascular care in rural and Appalachian populations, covering patients from over 87 % of ZIP codes in West Virginia (Fig. 1). Given the multi-center nature of the study, clinical data from each health system were standardized through a harmonization process to ensure consistency in variable definitions, coding structures, and data formats across sites. Diagnoses and procedural codes were aligned using common classification systems (e.g., ICD-10), and discrepancies were resolved through algorithmic cleaning and manual review. Due to data use agreements and institutional review board restrictions, patient-level data from WVU Medicine and CAMC could not be combined or analyzed jointly. Therefore, all analyses were conducted independently by cohort. However, the data harmonization process enabled consistent analytic approaches across both datasets, allowing for meaningful comparisons. Data within each cohort represents all inpatient hospitalizations for patients 18 years of age and older with an existing heart failure diagnosis (ICD 10 code I50) from January 2017 to June 2023. Each patient record includes a medical record number, admission date, age at visit, race/ethnicity, sex, zip code of residence, and comorbidities. This study was approved by the WVU (2303754016) and CAMC (17−348) Institutional Review Boards.

Study measures

Within each cohort, cases were identified as heart failure patients with a diagnosis of ischemic stroke. The primary exposure of interest was substance use, including cocaine, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, and stimulants. Alcohol use was included as a separate variable in the model. Potential comorbidities were identified a priori as binary variables indicating the presence or absence of the condition (Table 1). All study outcomes and comorbidities were identified using a list of ICD 10 codes (Supplemental Materials).

Community-level socioeconomic status was measured using the Distressed Communities Index (DCI). Developed and validated by the Economic Innovation Group, the DCI is a composite score that measures the socioeconomic distress of a community at the zip code level (Economic Innovation Group,). A DCI score is available for all zip codes with a population of at least 500 and captures 99 % of the US population (Economic Innovation Group,). The DCI is derived from publicly available data from the County Business Patterns and the American Community Survey and is designed to capture economic disadvantage at the local level for use by policymakers, researchers, and organizations (Economic Innovation Group,). There are seven economic metrics included in the DCI score: unemployment, education level, poverty rate, job growth, housing vacancies, business establishments, and median income (Supplemental Materials) (Economic Innovation Group,). Each variable is equally weighted, compared to its neighbors, and normalized to obtain a raw score that ranges from 0 (no distress) to 100 (severe distress) (Economic Innovation Group,). For this study, communities with a DCI score ≥ 75 were classified as distressed. The DCI has been previously applied in health services and policy research, including studies focused on cardiovascular disease, hospital readmission, mortality, and healthcare access, to highlight the role of structural and economic disadvantage in shaping health outcomes (Witrick et al., 2022; Mentias et al., 2022b; Charles et al., 2019; El Moheb et al., 2024; Hawkins et al., 2019; Weeks et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive summary statistics were calculated to compare demographic and clinical characteristics between case and control groups in each hospital cohort (Hayes-Larson et al., 2019). Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the association between substance use and stroke among heart failure patients for each hospital cohort separately. An interaction term was included between substance use and DCI to determine if the association differed based on community level socioeconomic status. Stratified analysis by DCI score was then conducted for each hospital cohort. Backward model selection with a statistical variable removal level of p < 0.15 was employed in each cohort to determine which covariates were included, such that the final models were the most parsimonious (Chowdhury and Turin, 2020). In the WVU Medicine cohort, the final model included the following covariates: age, race/ethnicity (White, African American, Other), sex, BMI (<18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, ≥30), insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, Private, Self-Pay/Other), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), end-stage renal disease (ESRD), peripheral artery disease (PAD), hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), venous thromboembolism (VTE), cancer, diabetes, and tobacco use. In the CAMC cohort, the final model included: age, race/ethnicity (White, African American, Other), sex, insurance type, CKD, COPD, ESRD, PAD, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, ASCVD, VTE, cancer, and diabetes.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with a p value < 0.05 set as statistically significant.

Results

WVU medicine

Between 2017 and 2023, there were 33,663 individuals with heart failure in the WVU Medicine cohort (Table 1). Overall, 7.88 % (n = 2651) of heart failure patients in this cohort suffered a stroke. Among individuals who suffered a stroke, 49.64 % were male, 94.53 % were white, 70.28 % were older than 65, and 85.97 % had Medicare insurance. Half of these individuals lived in distressed communities (50.28 %), 6.75 % had substance use, and 6.19 % had alcohol use. Common comorbid conditions in individuals who suffered a stroke include coronary artery disease (69.33 %), ESRD (61.11 %), PAD (57.33 %), hyperlipidemia (82.57 %), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (73.33 %). Individuals who did not experiencea stroke were more likely to be white, male, older than 65, and have Medicare insurance. Similarly, almost half of these individuals also lived in distressed communities (49.96 %), while 4.75 % and 5.41 % had substance use and alcohol use, respectively.

After adjusting for demographics and clinical comorbidities, individuals in the WVU Medicine cohort with substance use had 36 % (aOR: 1.36, 95 % CI: 1.14 – 1.63) higher odds of a stroke compared to individuals who did not have substance use (Fig. 2). Similarly, substance use was associated with 68 % (aOR: 1.68, 95 % CI: 1.34 – 2.09) higher odds of stroke among individuals who reside in distressed communities. However, the adjusted odds ratio between substance use and stroke was not statically significant for individuals with heart failure who reside in non-distressed communities (aOR: 1.02, 95 % CI: 0.78 – 1.35).

CAMC

In the CAMC cohort, 8050 individuals were identified as having heart failure between 2017 and 2023 (Table 2). Among these individuals, 11.18 % (900) suffered a stroke. Individuals who suffered a stroke were more likely to be white, male, have Medicare insurance, and had a mean age of 70.9 (11.9 Standard Deviation (SD)). Over half of these individuals resided in distressed communities (63 %), while 14.4 % had substance use, and 4 % had alcohol use. Common comorbidities among individuals with substance use included coronary artery disease (57 %), diabetes (52 %), hypertension (82 %), hyperlipidemia (63 %), ischemic cardiomyopathy (63 %), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (57 %). Among individuals who did not experiencea stroke, 52 % were male, 92.7 % were white, 72.5 % had Medicare, and the mean age was 70.2 (12 SD). Over half of individuals who did not have a stroke lived in distressed communities (57.5 %), while 9.79 % and 1.5 % had substance use and alcohol use, respectively.

After adjusting for demographics and clinical comorbidities, individuals in the CAMC cohort with substance use had 3.5 (95 % CI: 2.85 – 4.55) times the odds of stroke compared to individuals who did not have substance use (Fig. 2). Individuals who lived in distressed communities with substance use had an adjusted odds of stroke of 3.7 (95 % CI: 3.03 – 4.52) times the odds of individuals living in distressed communities who did not use substances. Similarly, the adjusted odds ratio comparing individuals with substance use to individuals without substance use in non-distressed communities was 1.8 (95 % CI: 1.63 – 2.09).

Discussion

In our multicenter study of over 40,000 individuals with heart failure from two health care systems, we found that substance use is associated with significantly increased odds of stroke after adjusting for demographic characteristics and clinical comorbidities. Importantly, this association was modified by the community-level socioeconomic status, as measured by the Distressed Communities Index (DCI), highlighting how structural and social determinants of health can exacerbate clinical risk. Across both health systems, residing in a distressed community consistently amplified the association between substance use and stroke. However, the strength of this relationship varied between cohorts. This stresses the importance of inter-professional clinical care teams to tackle the complexity of patient care management for heart failure patients in areas or populations where substance use disorders are highly prevalent. In the WVU Medicine cohort, the association between substance use and stroke was statistically significant only among individuals residing in distressed communities. In contrast, the CAMC cohort showed significantly elevated odds of stroke with substance use in both distressed and non-distressed communities, though the effect was markedly higher among those in distressed areas. These differences suggest that regional contextual factors may influence how community distress interacts with substance use to impact stroke risk.

Our findings align with previous literature demonstrating that substance use is a significant risk factor for worsening cardiovascular outcomes in individuals with heart failure. Although the exact biological mechanisms vary depending the specific substances used they each have well-documented cardiotoxic effects that worsen the effects of heart failure and further increase stroke risk (Grubb et al., 2021; Snow et al., 2019; Esse et al., 2011). For example, cocaine and other stimulates have been found to cause acute hypertension, vasospasm, endothelial injury, and arrhythmias, all of which increase thromboembolic potential and stroke risk (Havakuk et al., 2017; Kim and Park, 2019; Schürer et al., 2017). Additionally, opioid use is associated with increased arterial stiffness, reduced heart rate variability, and higher risk of atrial fibrillation, all of which are risk factors for stroke (Toska and Mayrovitz, 2023). Altogether, these adverse effects increase an individual’s risk of stroke while simultaneously worsening cardiac function and hemodynamic stability (Grubb et al., 2021; Snow et al., 2019; Esse et al., 2011). These adverse effects have been found to increase an individual’s health care utilization and contribute to their overall morbidity (Shetty et al., 2021). Individuals with heart failure who use substances are more likely to visit the emergency department, be admitted to the hospital, and be readmitted to the hospital within 30 days (Thyagaturu et al., 2022; Nishimura et al., 2020; Shetty et al., 2021). Furthermore, among individuals with heart failure, substance use can increase blood pressure and heart rate and increase the risk of blood clotting (Grubb et al., 2021). While much of the literature has focused on mortality as the primary adverse outcome, our study adds critical evidence on the association between substance use and stroke among individuals with heart failure—a group already at elevated baseline risk for stroke (Thyagaturu et al., 2022; Nishimura et al., 2020; Shetty et al., 2021). This is particularly important given the compounding morbidity that a stroke imposes on individuals already managing chronic heart failure (Witt et al., 2006, 2007; Cuadrado-Godia et al., 2010). It is imperative that clinicians and policy makers understand more about how substance use impacts stroke risk in heart failure populations so we can properly identify high risk groups and implement necessary interventions (Grubb et al., 2021).

We identified differences in the association between substance use and stroke among individuals with heart failure based on the socioeconomic status of where the individuals reside. Our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence about the importance of community-level socioeconomic status for individuals at risk for heart failure or those with heart failure (Patel et al., 2020; Thyagaturu et al., 2022; Mentias et al., 2022a; Akwo et al., 2018b; Ingelsson et al., 2006b). Previous studies found that living in a distress community independently predicts incident heart failure and that lower community-level socioeconomic status also increases the risk of hospital readmission and mortality among individuals with heart failure (Patel et al., 2020; Mentias et al., 2022a; Akwo et al., 2018b). There are several potential mechanisms that may explain the impact community-level socioeconomic status has on individuals with heart failure, especially those with substance use. Distressed communities often have less access to healthcare resources and community-level resources, such as healthy foods and outlets for physical activity, which can increase the risk of negative health outcomes (Gaskin et al., 2014; Powell et al., 2007). Furthermore, living in distressed communities can increase the level of stress and tension placed on individuals (Ewart and Suchday, 2002; Karriker-Jaffe, 2013). To cope with the increased levels of stress and tension, individuals may rely on substance use, including alcohol, drugs, or tobacco (Karriker-Jaffe, 2013).

Previous studies have found that individuals with heart failure who stop using substances can see improvement in cardiac function and overall heart failure symptoms (Thyagaturu et al., 2022; Grubb et al., 2021). More research is warranted to investigate the implementation of interventions for substance use cessation among this specific high-risk group. Traditional substance use treatment programs, such as outpatient addiction treatments, inpatient residential treatments, and detox facilities, have proven to be effective at reducing substance use (Prendergast et al., 2017). However, researchers should also investigate the use of technology-based interventions in the primary care setting (Tofighi et al., 2018). Furthermore, our findings suggest that interventions need to focus on individuals in distressed communities. A coordinated response-led by public health officials, primary care providers, and advanced practice nurses-is essential to improve access and continuity of care for individuals in distressed communities (Shetty et al., 2021). Overall, a multidisciplinary approach will be needed to address the increased risk of stroke among individuals with heart failure with substance use, especially those in distressed communities.

These findings have important implications for care delivery in cardiology and primary care settings. Clinicians should proactively assess for substance use in heart failure patients, as it can increase the risk of incidence heart failure and adverse outcomes, including stroke and mortality (Grubb et al., 2021; Nishimura et al., 2020; Thomas et al., 2020b; Parizad et al., 2025). Routine screening using validated tools such as the Tobacco, Alcohol, Prescription medication, and other Substance use (TAPS-1) tool and the Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment (SBIRT) model can aid in early detection and intervention (Babor et al., 2007; Mcneely et al., 2024). Additionally, individuals residing in socioeconomically distressed communities face compounded risks due to systemic barriers such as limited access to care, inadequate transportation, lower health literacy, and insufficient availability of specialty services (Mukhopadhyay et al., 2023; White-Williams et al., 2020; Pierce et al., 2025). As such, tailoring care strategies to address both clinical and contextual risk factors is essential for improving outcomes among heart failure patients with co-occurring substance use, particularly those in high-risk communities.

Throughout West Virginia, patients often face barriers in reaching healthcare institutions, which can delay care and increase disease severity by the time of presentation (Hong et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2025). However, the nature and magnitude of these barriers may differ by region. The observed differences in the strength of associations between cohorts may reflect the distinct economic, geographic, and healthcare landscapes in the two regions. WVU Medicine primarily serves northern and central West Virginia, which has historically been supported by manufacturing and industrial employment, and is characterized by rolling ridges and broader valleys, that are somewhat better connected by major transportation corridors. Conversely, CAMC serves southern West Virginia, a region more dependent on extraction industries such as coal mining, which has seen substantial decline in recent decades, and is marked by more rugged and deeply dissected terrains that create longer travel times and greater isolation (Hong et al., 2022). This economic and geographic divergence between regions has contributed to differences in healthcare access, availability of specialty services, and the prevalence of comorbid conditions (Hong et al., 2022). In the southern region, the more rugged and mountainous terrain may contribute to healthcare access limitations that are more evenly distributed across all communities, potentially contributing to the elevated stroke risk in both distressed and non-distressed groups. However, infrastructure initiatives such as West Virginia’s Roads to Prosperity Program aim to improve transportation networks, particularly in southern counties, and may help mitigate some of these structural barriers in the future (American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials AASHTO, 2024). Additionally, cultural factors, such as a strong sense of identity, strong reliance on family, and norms around health-seeking behavior, may differ between regions, and also contribute to the observed heterogeneity in stroke risks (Coyne et al., 2006). Although this paper aimed to address some of these regional differences by incorporating specific variables that reflect the unique patient comorbidity profiles of each region, significant differences in healthcare outcomes persisted. These findings underscore the deep-rooted and complex nature of these regional disparities within West Virginia’s healthcare landscape and highlight how geographic, economic, and healthcare system factors intersect to influence cardiovascular risk.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the use of administrative claims and EHR data introduces the potential for misclassification bias, particularly in the documentation of substance use, which may be underreported due to stigma, inconsistent screening, or variable documentation (Johnson and Nelson, 2013; McNeely et al., 2018). Second, cocaine, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, and stimulants were combined in our study to achieve a large enough sample size. As a result, we are unable to differentiate between the effects of each type of substance on stroke. Third, differences in clinical practices, resource availability, and documentation standards across the two healthcare systems may introduce information bias, particularly in how comorbidities and stroke events are captured (Schneeweiss and Avorn, 2005). Additionally, there may be limited overlap in patients receiving care at both institutions, which we could not fully assess. Fourth, although we adjusted for numerous clinical and demographic factors, we were unable to control for additional confounders such as ejection fraction, medication adherence, or the severity, duration, and patterns of substance use due to data limitations. These unmeasured factors could influence the likelihood of substance use and/or the risk of stroke, potentially resulting in residual confounding and affecting the magnitude of the observed associations. As such, our results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Future studies incorporating echocardiographic data, detailed substance use histories, and longitudinal follow-up would be valuable for clarifying temporal and mechanistic pathways. Finally, community-level socioeconomic distress was measured using the DCI, which, while widely used and validated, are only available for zip codes with > 500 individuals and is limited by its reliance on ZIP code–level aggregation. This may introduce ecological bias and obscure intra-community variability or individual-level socioeconomic status. Additionally, the DCI does not capture healthcare infrastructure, transportation access, or other non-economic social determinants of health (Economic Innovation Group,). We considered whether rurality indices or regional access-to-care metrics could further contextualize our findings. However, rural–urban classifications, which often function as crude proxies for healthcare access, and other traditional access-to-care metrics do not capture the complex barriers present in Appalachia, where geographic isolation, provider shortages, and transportation challenges may be present even in more urbanized areas (Hong et al., 2022).

Despite these limitations, our study has several notable strengths. One of the major strengths is its multicenter design. By leveraging data from two of the largest healthcare systems in West Virginia, we were able to evaluate patterns across different geographic and economic regions of the state. This allowed us to explore how regional variation in community distress, healthcare access, and sociodemographic composition may influence the relationship between substance use and stroke risk among individuals with heart failure in a rural context. The inclusion of two independent cohorts not only enhances the generalizability of our findings within rural populations but also offers a rare opportunity to assess how structural differences across healthcare systems can modify clinical outcomes. Another strength of the study is the inclusion of the DCI to measure the socioeconomic status of the community. The composite nature of DCI allows for a multidimensional view of socioeconomic distress that allows for a deeper understanding of the impact on the individual. Our findings add to a growing body of literature on the use of DCI in cardiovascular research by applying it in the context of substance use and stroke risk among individuals with heart failure, a population at the intersection of clinical vulnerability and socioeconomic disadvantage (Witrick et al., 2022; Charles et al., 2019; El Moheb et al., 2024; Hawkins et al., 2019). These findings further support the use of DCI in health research, particularly as a practical metric for identifying high-risk communities and guiding targeted public health interventions.

Conclusion

Our study provides a thorough investigation of West Virginia’s largest heart failure cohort, comprised of 40,000 patients and represented more then 87 % of our state’s zip codes. Here we conduct regression analyses and found a significant relationship between stroke and substance use disorder in heart failure patients, which was modified by community level factors, like community economic distress. This is a critical finding as it further emphasizes the importance of a coordinated care response to address the growing complexity of heart failure patient care, and need for inclusive health solutions which account for social drivers of disease progression in patients with chronic diseases.
