Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the Canadian healthcare workforce: a rapid evidence synthesis of key considerations, lessons learned, and promising practices to address the healthcare workforce crisis
Authors: Gail Tomblin Murphy, Tara Sampalli, Andrea Carson, Mark Embrett, Meaghan Sim, Caroline Chamberland-Rowe, Alyssa Indar, Marta MacInnis, Kaylee Murphy-Boyle, Janet Rigby, Julia Guk, Leah Boulos, Kristy Hancock, Jayden Altman-Prezioso, Shirin Mehrpooya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ongoing impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on Canada’s healthcare workforce and service delivery necessitate focused health system planning and delivery that prioritizes coordination, collaboration, and evidence-based strategies. A rapid evidence synthesis was commissioned by Health Canada to determine the impacts of the pandemic on the healthcare workforce and to identify promising strategies and innovations that mitigate these challenges.MethodsTwo, sequential rapid evidence syntheses were conducted between October 2022 and March 2023 using methodologies aligned with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses literature search extension (PRISMA-S) guidelines. The first review (October–November 2022) focused on the impacts of COVID-19 on Canadian healthcare workers and mitigation strategies, while the second (November 2022–March 2023) broadened the scope to international interventions. Findings were organized by impact level (individual, organizational, system). Quality assessment of sources was not performed.ResultsWe included 176 and 31 sources, respectively in the analysis. Sources identifying impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic described significant mental health impacts on healthcare workers, alongside changes in demand and supply of services, physical health challenges, and shifts in scopes of practice or care models. Interventions were primarily targeted at the individual or organizational level and included mental health support, training and upskilling, enhanced organizational communication and workforce planning initiatives. System-level interventions were less common, and most interventions lacked robust evaluation or evidence-informed design.ConclusionsThis review highlights a significant gap in literature regarding evaluated interventions to address healthcare workforce challenges during the pandemic. While numerous sources document the adverse impacts on healthcare workers, detailed reports on specific interventions are scarce. Most interventions focus on workforce planning, education, practice scopes, recruitment and technology integration. The research underscores the need for comprehensive recommendations addressing social and mental health support, workplace safety, organizational communication and pandemic preparedness. These recommendations are vital for developing future workforce strategies, thus enabling policymakers and healthcare leaders to effectively respond to current and future healthcare challenges. This strategic approach will enhance system resilience and improve healthcare delivery across Canada.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01395-9.Keywords: Healthcare workforce, COVID-19 pandemic, Rapid review, Nursing

Background

Long-standing health system gaps, which have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, continue to place pressure on healthcare workers (HCWs). For some, these pressures have resulted in decisions to leave their current positions resulting in long-term impacts on the healthcare system [1, 2]. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the healthcare workforce was challenging, with staff redeployment on a large scale, widespread overtime and HCWs under significant operational stress. Although specialist services and elective procedures were mostly halted, new operational strategies to accommodate ever changing health requirements had to be adopted [3]. Unfortunately, the negative effects on HCWs amplified workforce shortages and impacts on the health systems’ ability to deliver care and to meet population health needs [4]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare workers faced a surge in complex patient cases, particularly among the elderly with chronic conditions that could have been managed outside of hospitals under normal circumstances [5]. The combination of increased work demands, anxiety about contracting the virus and the fear of the unknown contributed to an environment where healthcare workers were overwhelmed [6–8]. This situation likely led to diminished care quality in some jurisdictions and exacerbated health outcomes for patients, as the system struggled to cope with the added pressures of the pandemic [9, 10].

Recent healthcare discourses underscore the need to adopt system-level frameworks that improve healthcare planning and delivery, by leveraging coordinated, collaborative and evidence-informed approaches. Frameworks such as the Quintuple Aim emphasize the need to attend the needs of HCWs as a critical component of building high performing healthcare systems [11–13]. To this end, it is imperative that we understand the impacts of the pandemic on HCWs and to identify lessons learned, promising strategies and innovations that address and mitigate these challenges. It is also critical to identify gaps in our understanding of COVID-19-related impacts on the healthcare workforce to identify future directions of research and inquiry to inform macro- and meso-level decision-making. To rebuild and support a more equitable and sustainable healthcare workforce post-pandemic, it is essential for Canadian health systems to: (1) understand and recognize the full breadth of impacts that the COVID-19 pandemic has had on the healthcare workforce, (2) identify promising practices that may serve to address these impacts, and (3) implement healthcare workforce planning approaches that will enable the design and implementation of a health system capable of meeting the Quintuple Aim.

Purpose and review questions

This rapid evidence synthesis was commissioned by Health Canada to inform the identification of priority areas and actions to mitigate the health workforce impacts of COVID-19. The objectives of this rapid evidence synthesis were to:

Identify the key impacts of COVID-19 on the Canadian healthcare workforce, including similarities and differences across professional groups and jurisdictions.

Identify the key impacts of COVID-19 on the Canadian healthcare workforce, including similarities and differences across professional groups and jurisdictions.

Describe interventions or strategies implemented in Canada, and internationally, to address the impact of COVID-19 on the healthcare workforce, including outcomes and evidence of effectiveness.

Describe interventions or strategies implemented in Canada, and internationally, to address the impact of COVID-19 on the healthcare workforce, including outcomes and evidence of effectiveness.

To outline promising practices that are best suited to address the key healthcare workforce challenges and knowledge gaps identified within the Canadian context, and policy recommendations that could serve to strengthen the sustainability and resilience of the Canadian healthcare workforce post-pandemic.

To outline promising practices that are best suited to address the key healthcare workforce challenges and knowledge gaps identified within the Canadian context, and policy recommendations that could serve to strengthen the sustainability and resilience of the Canadian healthcare workforce post-pandemic.

Methods

To address the overarching review objectives, we conducted the review in two sequential phases, informed by established review protocols [14, 15]. The reporting of the search strategy follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses literature search extension (PRISMA-S) guideline [16], which is intended to enhance transparency and reproducibility in systematic review reporting.

Phase 1 search (September–October 2022)

We completed a rapid evidence synthesis of academic and grey literature exploring the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on the Canadian healthcare workforce, describing challenges that the pandemic has generated or amplified, identifying approaches adopted that were intended to address these challenges and identifying gaps in our knowledge on this topic.

The search strategy was developed with two library scientists. All searches were conducted on 7 October 2022 using the following databases and interfaces: MEDLINE (Ovid MEDLINE(R) All) via Ovid, CINAHL with Full Text via EBSCOhost, and Embase (preprints only) via Elsevier Embase.com. No language limits and no study design limits were placed on the search. The following databases were included: (1) Medline, (2) CINAHL (full-text), and (3) Embase (pre-prints only). Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) (previously known as CADTH) search filters for COVID-19 and Canada were applied to the searches. Grey literature searches for phase 1 were conducted from 6 to 11 October 2022 used a targeted search strategy that included Pan-Canadian Health Organizations such as Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) and Canada Health Infoway, the Canadian Health Workforce Network, COVID-19 hubs, provincial/territorial ministries of health and provincial/territorial health authorities (see File 1 for full search strategy).

Phase 2 search (November 2022–March 2023)

In phase 2, we (1) conducted a targeted expansion of the review to identify international literature to address key evidence gaps identified through phase 1, (2) identified any additional Canadian literature that may not have been found in phase 1 (i.e. published between the end of phase 1 and beginning of phase 2), and (3) identified promising practices that could address healthcare workforce challenges.

The search strategy for phase 2 was developed with support from library scientists. All academic searches for phase 2 were conducted on 18 November 2022, and results were limited to those from 1 January 2019 to the present. No language limits and no study design limits were placed on the overall search. The following databases were included: (1) Medline, (2) CINAHL (full-text) and (3) Embase (pre-prints only). Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) (previously known as CADTH) search filters for COVID-19 and Canada were applied to the searches. Grey literature searches for phase 2 were conducted from 17 to 25 November 2022, using a targeted search strategy that included government, health authority, non-governmental organizations, and think-tank websites for targeted countries. Google advanced searching was also conducted for each included jurisdiction (i.e. provinces, territories, countries), with the first five to six pages of Google queries being reviewed for eligible sources (see Supplementary File 2 for full search strategy).

The international search was targeted to countries with broadly comparable health system structures, workforce organization, and policy contexts to Canada. Specifically, we included the United Kingdom (England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales), Australia and New Zealand. These countries were selected because they operate publicly funded, universal healthcare systems with nursing workforce structures and regulatory environments similar to those in Canada, allowing for more meaningful policy comparisons.

Screening and data extraction: phases 1 and 2

We imported academic and grey literature sources into Covidence Systematic Review Software (Veritas Health Innovation, 2022) for title/abstract and full text screening based on source inclusion/exclusion criteria (see Supplementary File 3 for phase 1 and 2 inclusion and exclusion criteria). Owing to the rapid turnaround required for this work, a single-review approach to title/abstract screening and full text screening was adopted. All members of the research team participated in screening. While this approach enabled timely completion of the review, it introduces a methodological limitation that may increase the risk of selection bias.

Phase 1 data were extracted using the following endpoints: (1) study characteristics (jurisdiction, dates, aim, design and methods), (2) health profession, (3) impact (e.g. mental health, recruitment and quality of care), (4) interventions (e.g. regulatory/licensure, recruitment, education and training) and (5) evidence of outcomes.

Phase 2 data was extracted using the following endpoints: (1) study characteristics (jurisdiction, dates, aim, design, methods), (2) health professional focus, (3) health sector, (4) type of intervention, (5) reason for intervention, (6) description of the intervention or strategy, (7) evidence of effectiveness and (8) summary of key findings.

The extracted data were reviewed for duplicates and errors/omissions by senior members of the research team (M.E., A.C. and M.S.).

No formal quality assessment of included sources was performed. As a result, the strength and reliability of the evidence base could not be formally appraised, and findings should be interpreted with caution.

Results

We identified 1794 unique sources from the phase 1 search, focusing on impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on the Canadian workforce. After comparison with inclusion/exclusion criteria, 176 sources were eligible for extraction (n = 122 peer-reviewed sources and n = 54 grey literature sources). Please see Supplementary File 4 for phase 1 PRISMA, and Supplementary File 5 for a summary of the sources identified in the phase 1 search.

From the phase 2 search, which focused on interventions to address impacts to HCWs across international jurisdictions as well as an expanded Canadian search, a total of 1209 unique sources from the academic databases were identified, combined with 71 sources identified through the grey literature search. After comparison with inclusion and exclusion criteria, 31 sources were identified as eligible for extraction (n = 28 academic sources and n = 3 grey literature source) (see Supplementary File 6 for phase 2 PRISMA). Please see Supplementary File 7 for a summary of the sources identified in the phase 2 search.

Most sources used qualitative and quantitative designs, including both primary and secondary research, followed by mixed methods approaches. Many sources focused on the broader healthcare workforce, meaning that the source focused on two or more healthcare workforce roles and did not distinguish between these roles in the source’s analysis/results. “Other” represents roles that only appeared once as the focus of a source. Sources that focused on a specific healthcare workforce role were mainly on nurses [licensed/registered practical nurses (LPN), registered nurses (RN), nurse practitioners (NP)] or physicians and surgeons.

We organized interventions into the following sections: (1) individual-level interventions (i.e. interventions targeting healthcare workers as individuals, often requiring them to opt-in or seek out these supports), (2) organization-level interventions (i.e. interventions targeting groups, teams and/or organizations of healthcare workers) and (3) system-level interventions (i.e. interventions targeting system-wide changes through the mobilization of policy levers, or through cross-sectoral collaboration).

Pandemic impacts on the Canadian health workforce

We observed that mental health, stress, or burnout (psychological impacts) were most frequently identified within the sources as impacts to Canadian HCWs (n = 97). More than half of the sources (55%) focused on the mental health impacts to Canadian HCWs, with the two top manifestations of mental health impacts being: (a) (di)stress, depression and anxiety; and (b) burnout. This was followed by impacts related to workforce demand (requirements and needs) (n = 46 sources), impacts related to workforce supply (shortages and staffing levels) (n = 44 sources), impacts on physical health (n = 39 sources) and impacts on scopes of practice and models of care (n = 32). Sources frequently identified more than one impact on HCWs, suggesting the intersectional nature of the pandemic’s effects on HCWs. Recommendations to address impacts to the health workforce

From phase 1, we identified 95 recommendations made by authors to support the health workforce across 92 sources. These recommendations appeared across a variety of healthcare settings and targeting diverse healthcare roles (see Supplementary File 8 for a summary of recommendations). Recommendations were multi-faceted and are not mutually exclusive (Table 4). Below we present a narrative summary of the top five recommendations.

Recommendations to address healthcare workforce challenges from source authors

Offering formal social and mental health supports (n = 17 sources)

Authors most frequently recommended offering formal social and/or mental health supports for HCWs. This corresponds with the most cited impact of the pandemic on the healthcare workforce being mental health, stress and burnout (see Table 3). In acknowledgement of the complexity of the practice environment, authors suggested that social and psychological support services (e.g. mental health interventions and associated resources) be offered in addition to prioritizing the wellbeing of HCW through reducing stressors that included having sufficient personal protective equipment (PPE) supply, appropriate and manageable workloads, consistent communication with management and leadership, and policies to address health and safety [17, 18]. Some sources highlighted the vulnerability of certain groups of HCWs; for example, women and non-binary HCWs experienced higher levels of burnout and anxiety than male HCWs [19], due to the intersection of gender with stress due to the demands of familial and household responsibilities [20]. Thus, several sources recommended that policies and mental health supports should be developed in family-centred ways, which account for the unique lived experiences of those in under-served communities, including racialized populations [21, 22].

Enhancing workplace health and safety policies (n = 16 sources)

The second category of recommendations concerns the implementation of health and safety policies related to outbreak and infection control procedures. Such policies and procedures support the physical safety of HCWs and patients, and when implemented consistently and communicated to employees in clear and transparent ways, can mitigate burnout and stress amongst the healthcare workforce [23–25, 111]. Several sources discussed the need for health and safety policies that mitigate workload burdens and subsequent mental health impacts, such as managing workload by placing a limit on the total hours employees can work in a week [18, 26, 27].

Strengthening organizational communication (n = 11 sources)

Recommended strategies for improving organizational communication to benefit HCWs at work included a clear, regular communication strategy from institutions and clinical leaders [111], as well as frequent opportunities to debrief with employees and management to keep staff up to date on the latest COVID-19 policies, guidelines or directives, and their implications for workflow and patient care [18, 28, 29]. Source authors identified the importance for organizations to facilitate safe places for staff to enable them to have discussions about pandemic-related challenges and concerns [26]. Authors suggested that regular discussions or debriefs may facilitate confidence in HCWs to implement new policies, which is important during pandemics when policies and directives are ever-changing and HCWs are navigating uncertainty [30, 111]. Formal feedback mechanisms for suggestions and questions with consistent follow-up processes may strengthen communication between leadership/management and front-line staff [31].

Improving workplace culture (n = 10 sources)

Facilitating a workplace culture in alignment with staff and organizational values and needs was identified as a strategy to mitigate the negative impacts of COVID-19 on the healthcare workforce, and especially the mental health impacts on HCWs. The specific characteristics of a workplace culture are unique to the setting and organizational and staff values; however, common elements which enhance workplace culture include teamwork and collaboration, morale, communication and relationships between staff and leadership, recognition of HCWs’ efforts, support, engagement and autonomy [24, 28, 31]. An effective workplace culture may facilitate HCWs’ trust in the organization and teams and contribute to resilience amongst HCWs during healthcare crises.

Developing more robust pandemic planning (n = 6 sources)

Finally, sources emphasized the importance of appropriate pandemic planning, such as a unified pandemic response plan, to ensure preparedness of the healthcare workforce and to mitigate its potentially negative effects on HCWs. Lessons can be derived from various pandemics and epidemics (e.g. SARS, H1N1 and COVID-19). Education and training are key components of pandemic planning and may include integrating outbreak management/pandemic preparedness strategies during health professions education [30, 112]. Education and training may also necessitate, for example, shadowing opportunities for nurses, and other care team members in areas outside of their practice setting to better prepare the workforce for potential future crises when redeployment occurs [135].

Implemented approaches to address healthcare workforce challenges

From both phase 1 and 2, we identified 111 sources that described interventions that were implemented to address healthcare workforce challenges caused by the COVID-19 pandemic; all interventions were instituted in reaction to the pandemic crisis. Some sources described multiple interventions at different levels, so the combined total for each level is greater than the total number of sources. Interventions were described within 82 Canadian sources, and 30 global sources. Of these, 43 sources indicated an evaluation component; 68 did not describe an evaluation.

The following section describes the interventions identified within the sources. Several sources included more than one policy intervention; however, many had a focus on a single intervention. Of the evaluated work, interventions were focused on a range of strategies to mitigate HCW impacts, including mental health and wellness supports, education and training, scopes of practice and models of care, and recruitment interventions. The use of technology was frequently leveraged within interventions. The evidence for technology use aligned with public health measures related to physical distancing in place during the immediate COVID-19 crisis.

A total of 49 sources described interventions that were implemented to address healthcare workforce planning (at an organizational or system-level) to increase the healthcare workforce capacity. Strategies such as active recruitment [33], the rapid shift to virtual care [136] and staff deployment [113] were implemented.

We organized findings into three levels of interventions: (1) individual, (2) organizational and (3) system. We found most interventions were focused on addressing HCW impacts at an individual or organizational level, rather than interventions at a system level or integrated between levels. It is important to note that many individual-level interventions were initiated or offered by healthcare organizations but were intended for individual use, and were opt-in initiatives, meaning participation was optional.

Individual-level interventions (n = 16)

Most individual-level interventions were implemented to address the mental health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic through self-management approaches (e.g. wellness hubs, apps, psychological supports) or through peer support structures. These were primarily opt-in approaches (rather than mandatory) to address the needs of HCWs. However, in some instances, a multi-layered strategy was employed that focused on improving the organizational culture around mental health of HCWs.

Three mental health and support initiatives were identified in Australia [185–187]. Two of these initiatives were organization-led and included a peer support element [186, 187]. Sanatkar et al. investigated the use and effectiveness of “Shift”, a smartphone app that provided mental health resources to junior doctors in Australia, on depression and anxiety symptoms, as well as work and social functioning, COVID-19 safety concerns and help seeking. Hand in Hand (HnH) was an organization providing peer support to HCWs not meeting criteria for mental illness through an interdisciplinary panel of volunteers [186]. The “SEED” wellness programme [187] used a strengths-based approach to determine the needs of staff and implement wellbeing initiatives to support the resilience and recovery process during the pandemic. Example SEED initiatives that were implemented across the local health district sites included: (1) coffee buddies – coffee break with a nominated staff member as a form of collective care, (2) quiet room – dedicated room within the hospital for staff to take time for quiet reflection, (3) 24/7 wellness – weekly wellness sessions to promote staff well-being and (4) (s)crap book – communal journal to share/reflect on stressful experiences during the pandemic.

Unique to the interventions for HCWs was the preparation of healthy, hot meals by a hospital food service retailer in Australia for purchase by HCWs and their families during the initial waves of the pandemic [188]. Approximately 300 meals were purchased within the first 3 months of offering. The authors noted the popularity of this initiative, based on anecdotal feedback.

One rapid review looked at evidence-based mental health resources supporting front-line HCWs also enrolled in post-licensure university programmes (advanced certification, graduate training) [189]. Over 137 strategies were identified, addressing a variety of mental health issues (and frequently, more than one) – wellbeing (n = 58 sources), stress (n = 50 sources), anxiety (n = 37), resilience (n = 32), physical function (n = 21), depressive symptoms (n = 15), insomnia (n = 12) and post-traumatic stress disorder (n = 4). Overall, 25 strategies to support mental health were identified from these resources; the most frequently cited strategies were those focused on self-care initiatives such as meditation (n = 21, 15.3%), mindfulness (n = 16, 11.7%), virtual scenic tours (n = 15, 10.9%), physical exercise (n = 13, 9.5%), yoga (n = 12, 8.8%) and relaxation therapy (n = 10, 7.3%). The authors noted that under a quarter of resources (n = 24, 17.5%) indicated an evaluation or the development of strategies based on evidence.

For sources that described an evaluation component, most provided descriptive or anecdotal evidence on utilization, satisfaction or experience, frequently captured through feedback surveys. Users indicated being generally satisfied with the initiatives to support their mental health or wellness or shared positive experiences about them [32, 185, 186, 188, 190, 191]. Less frequent were sources that described a more robust approach to evaluation of the intervention. In addition to use and satisfaction, Santakar et al. explored alleviation of depressive and anxiety symptoms related to the use of the app [185]. The authors found that there was high usage and time spent on the app. Depressive and anxiety symptoms significantly decreased pre- and post-assessment. The authors found no significant relationship between mental health symptoms and app use (e.g. number of logins, total activity time). Olcon et al. conducted focus group or individual interviews with 33 HCWs who participated in the SEED initiative [187]. In their findings, the authors describe that SEED enabled personal-level participation and engagement to enhance HCW recovery from the mental health impacts of the COVID-19 crisis. Blake and colleagues conducted an evaluation that explored differences between users and non-users of the COVID-19 respite centres [191]. They found no significant differences in perceived job stressfulness, job satisfaction, presenteeism or turnover intentions between users and non-users. However, participants who had accessed a respite centre were more likely to report higher work engagement. Staff who accessed support from centres were highly satisfied; however, the authors recognized that the service model required a lot of resources to maintain. The authors provided other considerations for future staff wellness centres on the basis of this evaluation: (1) ensuring multiple, dedicated timeout/rest spaces that are separate from clinical environments; (2) addressing the needs of diverse groups of staff (e.g. equity groups or those in roles outside of front-line care); (3) advocacy and the visibility of this type of support to leadership; (4) line manager training and support for mental health and wellness; (5) offering multiple types of supports to staff; and (6) making retention an important strategy for the healthcare organization.

Organizational-level interventions (n = 72)

Organizational-level interventions were the largest group of implemented interventions identified from the sources. Many interventions focused on providing training, education and upskilling of HCWs to provide COVID-19-related care. Frequently these interventions focused on developing or enhancing critical care skills. Other interventions included those focused on organizational communication to address the needs of HCWs, infection prevention and control initiatives (often embedded within COVID-19-related care training), and health workforce planning and capacity. Like the individual-level interventions, many organizational-level interventions leveraged technology to support implementation. Not all interventions had a reported evaluation component. Organizational-level interventions are described more fully over the following paragraphs, organized by broad category of focus.

We identified three sources that described organizational-led interventions to support the mental health and wellbeing within the National Health Service (NHS) workforce (England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland – known as Health and Social Care). These initiatives identified were: respite centres for clinical and other front-line staff that included the presence of psychological first-aid-trained buddies [191]; implementation of a stepped-care model and psychological support framework built from learnings from previous public health emergencies [178]; provision of a psychological health e-package supporting HCWs [192]; a staff support helpline with tailored supports and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy [190]; and a multi-level strategy to support the health and wellbeing of HCWs through building a supportive mental health culture, normalizing mental health within the organization and reducing barriers to mental health care and supports [193]. Two of these sources described the evidence that was used to inform their development of their intervention [178, 192].

Just over half of the interventions (n = 37) focused on health workforce planning and capacity, and changes to service models. Interventions included the development of a pandemic operations command and support structure for physicians to provide care to non-ventilated COVID-19 inpatients [114], the development of outbreak protocols for long-term care (LTC) facilities with clear team management supports (e.g. rewards and recognition structure and communications) [161] and surge capacity planning and redeployment pathways with the addition of new staffing roles (CCAs and student nurse orderlies) [135]. Gregory and Austin (2020) described how scheduling staff with the same consistent co-workers in a line-based system, along with private space to work that accommodated for social distancing allowed for greater focus on tasks, use of technology implemented before the pandemic for day-to-day tasks [115]. Telecare was an approach described within two sources to reduce pandemic related stress HCWs [116, 165]. Southey and Stoddart (2021) also found that monthly professional practice team calls also remedied the anxiety and challenges faced by clinicians [165]. Schultz et al. (2021) describe the development of a virtual service delivery model (virtual ward) to remotely support COVID-19-positive patients (symptomatic but stable) in their homes [194]. The authors demonstrated that in a pandemic crisis, virtual ward can be a useful technique for lowering hospital demand and maintaining capacity. The success of virtual wards in a pandemic environment depended on a strong executive commitment, flexible leadership and a readiness from important stakeholders to quickly adopt and accept novel service delivery models. Overall, both the staff and the patients expressed great levels of satisfaction. In their narrative review, Young identified several service changes in Australia to support the health workforce: (1) telehealth, (2) “Hospital in Home”, (3) skill sharing (e.g. transition to care program), (4) student-led telehealth and (5) workforce planning (e.g. changes to team structure, work from home) [195]. Few details of these interventions were described. A sole source evaluated the implementation of a COVID-19 testing centre for essential service providers (fire, police, paramedicine) to enable staff to receive test results in an expedited manner. The authors concluded that paramedics are capable to run such a centre as it was shown to reduce the time front-line staff spend off work self-isolating due to the rapid turnaround for results.

There were 23 sources that described the provision of training, education and upskilling interventions to support HCWs – 16 sources were from Canada and 9 sources described training interventions from other countries. Education and training interventions were focused on building capacity for healthcare workers to provide COVID-19-related care (frequently critical care) during the pandemic [33, 34, 117, 166, 196–201], to prepare pre-registration professionals for mass emergency events [202] and to help prepare HCWs for redeployment [201, 203, 204]. Technology was also leveraged to support training and dissemination [35, 117, 173, 199–201, 205].

Some sources described educational programmes and guidance that was available for broad dissemination to HCWs – locally and globally [198, 201, 205]. For initiatives that reported an evaluation component, there was positive uptake and high satisfaction with the available content. For example, Island Health (2021) used COVID-19 related simulations conducted at the Royal Jubilee Hospital in Victoria, British Columbia, to develop the COVID-19 simulation guide; at the time of publication, the guide was downloaded 378 times across 32 countries. Jarden (2021) describes the development, implementation and evaluation of the evidenced-based “Critical Care Essentials COVID-19” online programme to provide foundational knowledge to RNs to safely assess and care for patients with acute respiratory failure due to COVID-19 [67]. The evidence-based programme was designed by an interdisciplinary team and offered free-of-charge to RNs in Victoria, Australia, with continuing professional development credits. In one month, 2875 students had accessed the course. Overall, 23.6% (n = 678) completed all modules. Most survey respondents (n = 395) found the content useful and relevant to practice, engaging in its content (especially the interactive design), easily accessible and would recommend the course to colleagues. Similarly, Contreras and colleagues (2021) describe a simulation-based approach for training HCWs from University Hospital Galway (UHG), Ireland, to prepare for a public health outbreak. At the UHG, the team applied a Plan–Do–Study–Act cycle to the implementation of simulation training, focused on the following educational objectives: (1) personal protective equipment (PPE) application, (2) reducing the generation of aerosols and (3) crisis resource management (CRM) concepts. Based on findings from the initial 4 weeks of training, at least 750 learners representing target service areas (critical care and anesthesia) completed simulation training across one or multiple scenarios. The authors reported that leadership and collaboration was foundational to the success of the training.

As many clinicians were unfamiliar with virtual care technology, resulting in many using a telephone as an alternative to virtual, Kwok et al. describe how group discussions were held to exchange resources and ideas to inform and empower HCWs [171]. Practice sessions also improved the ability of clinicians to perform their tasks virtually, handle technological errors and ease into virtual care. The interventions were successful, as the clinicians became more comfortable with the medium and felt a sense of achievement after acquiring new skills. They also saw virtual care as sustainable, with the desire to incorporate it into certain aspects of their practice.

Nine sources describe an intervention focused on organizational-level communications. The Imperial COVID Communications Network (ICON), which was developed during the pandemic to improve communication and collaboration between senior and junior doctors, was the focus of another evaluation study [206]. ICON targeted several levels of communication: (1) leadership to junior doctors (vertical – top-down), (2) peer-to-peer (horizonal) and (3) junior doctors to leadership (vertical – bottom-up). While a more robust evaluation is needed, the authors argued that ICON was effective in improving collaboration between senior leadership and junior doctors. ICON’s WhatsApp group had high engagement with over 750 members and good retention over time. Strong et al. describe a communication intervention to address employee uncertainty during the pandemic and changing guidelines and protocols [207]. Twenty “VIDCASTS” were held, whereby an expert panel (leadership representation, infectious disease consultant, infection control nurse) provided COVID-related information and updates, as well as responses to staff questions. Evaluation found strong uptake of the live videos with high levels of satisfaction and perceived value with the communication approach. Respondents reported that the live video sessions helped alleviate some of their anxieties around COVID-19 and enabled them to feel connected and supported during the crisis.

Two sources focused on quality improvement initiatives – both from the United Kingdom. Seamark and colleagues used exemplar cases to highlight quality improvement interventions adopted within NHS Trusts (n = 30) [208]. Interventions were organized under three themes: (1) practice interventions, (2) people interventions and (3) planning interventions. Not all interventions targeted HCWs; additionally, not all interventions were explicitly evaluated. Qualitative interviews suggested that interventions strengthened relationships and interdisciplinary collaboration within the local health and care system. In another quality improvement intervention, Smith and colleagues described the adoption of a Lean quality improvement approach within an NHS trust to address patient backlogs, but also to improve work processes caused by the pressures of the COVID-19 pandemic [36]. The trust used a compassionate communication approach throughout all the rapid improvements. Authors noted that adopting the Lean approach reduced wait lists and wait times. Staff outcomes were assessed through qualitative feedback and findings suggest that the changes relieved pressure for both administrative and clinical staff.

System-level interventions (n = 37 sources)

System-level interventions focused on policy levers (regulatory, legislative, financing) to support HCWs. Changes to scopes of practice [137], emergency registration/licensure [37], relaxation of registration/licensure criteria [154] and reinstatement of retirees [138] were all identified within the Canadian sources. Other interventions (n = 3) focused on funding, financing and remuneration, including the introduction of financial incentives for recruitment and retention to address critical shortages [139], increases in remuneration, or “pandemic pay”, during the response period [209], and “base financial compensation” to provide income stability to fee-for-service physicians affected by the COVID-19 pandemic [210]. Only one evaluated Canadian regulatory intervention was identified. Bishop and Rosenburg-Yunger conducted a qualitative evaluation of how pharmacists responded to Health Canada’s short-term class exemption [subsection 56(1)] to allow pharmacists to “extend, transfer, receive verbal orders, and deliver controlled substances” [118]. Their findings indicate that this exemption allowed pharmacists the opportunity to provide continuity of care to those experiencing opioid use disorder during the acute period of the pandemic.

Other interventions focused on building cross-sectoral networks to support communication [119, 211], or the organization and delivery of communication [119, 212, 213]. In doing so, these system-level interventions provided support to address impacts to the health workforce. Patanwala et al. describe the implementation of a population-level mass COVID-19 vaccination rollout through a partnership between the health system and universities (health professional schools) [212]. The intervention augmented the health workforce without depleting existing staff resources by leveraging “pharmacist extenders”. Multi-sector support was also a foundational component of the Enhanced Universal Support Offer for United Kingdom care homes [213], whereby practitioners’ expertise (through partner organizations) was leveraged to provide support and guidance [213].

Finally, Adams and Wannamaker analysed policies in Canada, the United Kingdom and Australia that were revised during the COVID-19 pandemic with the goal of increasing workforce capacity and flexibility for regulated healthcare professions during the first wave of the pandemic [214]. The reason for these policy changes and regulations was to ensure that countries had enough healthcare personnel and resources to address the pandemic demands, as many nations were not sufficiently prepared. The three case countries all introduced regulatory changes during the COVID-19 pandemic to increase the capacity of the healthcare workforce and/or flexibility. The changes affected mostly all health professionals, while other policy changes impacted specific professions (medicine, nursing, and pharmacy). Canada differed (compared with Australia and the United Kingdom) in flexibility around scopes of practice, where temporary orders/by-laws allowed for an expansion of tasks; Australia and the United Kingdom observed less altered roles as there are few regulated health professions in the United Kingdom and none in Australia. The regulatory structures of the United Kingdom and Australia allowed for greater self-discipline and flexibility within scope, whereas in Canada, scopes of practice were more rigid.

Synthesis of findings

From the rapid evidence synthesis, we found most interventions that focused on addressing impacts to HCWs were at an individual or organizational level, rather than interventions at a system or integrated level. Further, most of these interventions, even if led by an organization, were aimed at addressing mental health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic through HCWs self-care approaches (e.g. wellness hubs, apps, psychological supports). Other initiatives were focused on addressing communication needs of HCWs during a time of crisis, as well as training, upskilling and education of clinical (frequently, critical care) skills or infection prevention and control (IPAC) to prepare HCWs for pandemic response. In total, 37 of the included sources identified a system approach to addressing impacts to HCWs. None of the sources identified suggested a proactive or preparatory approach but rather were instituted in reaction to the pandemic crisis.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic has presented unprecedented challenges to healthcare systems worldwide, with HCWs facing immense pressure and strain on multiple fronts. Our findings highlight the profound psychological toll of the pandemic on Canadian healthcare workers, with mental health, stress and burnout emerging as prevalent concerns. It underscores the complex intersectional nature of these impacts, emphasizing the need for comprehensive support strategies tailored to address diverse challenges faced by HCWs. The literature also reveals challenges related to workforce demand, supply shortages and health and safety considerations, underscoring the importance of resource allocation and adaptation to evolving healthcare practices amidst the pandemic. In response, various interventions were implemented mostly targeting individuals, with voluntary capacity, with less evidence for interventions at the organizational and system levels to structurally support the wellbeing and resilience of HCWs. As our synthesis shows, individual-focused interventions tended to be short-term, with evaluations reporting only immediate satisfaction or symptom relief, rather than durable impacts on workforce resilience. Without addressing structural determinants such as workload, staffing or organizational culture, these initiatives risk being piecemeal. This gap highlights the importance of complementing individual supports with system-level interventions.

Implications for healthcare workers

Individual-level interventions predominantly focused on self-management approaches to address the mental health impacts of the pandemic on HCWs. Initiatives such as smartphone apps, peer support programmes, and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy aimed to provide resources and support directly to HCWs. Although needed, health systems’ focus on individual lifestyle change in policies to address HCWs wellbeing can be limiting due to several weaknesses. Firstly, such an approach often neglects the underlying structural determinants of health, such as social, economic and environmental factors, which significantly influence health behaviours and outcomes. Individual-focused interventions may have limited effectiveness in creating sustainable, population-wide health improvements, as they fail to address the systemic issues that shape health behaviours. Inadequate policy responses that solely target individual behaviours without addressing root causes can result in short-term solutions that do not address the underlying systemic issues contributing to public health problems.

Several organizational-level interventions were found within healthcare systems, particularly in the United Kingdom (UK) and Australia; however, less frequently than individual level interventions. Initiatives emphasized creating supportive environments and resources for HCWs. Wellbeing centres, rest areas and psychological support frameworks were established to provide HCWs with opportunities for rest, socialization and access to mental health resources. The provision of restorative supervision and wellbeing centres not only supported HCWs’ mental health but also contributed to higher work engagement and satisfaction.

Our review found that organizational approaches to supporting health workforce wellbeing demonstrated measurable positive outcomes. For example, NHS respite centres and stepped-care psychological support frameworks were associated with higher work engagement, reduced anxiety and strong user satisfaction. Similarly, simulation-based training in British Columbia and Ireland, and the “Critical Care Essentials COVID-19” programme in Australia, improved preparedness, confidence and uptake among HCWs. These organizational-level strategies go beyond individual self-care by embedding supports in the workplace, and thus offer a more comprehensive and impactful approach. Our findings align with broader evidence showing that organizational interventions can improve wellbeing, engagement and retention among HCWs [114, 215].

These findings align with broader literature advocating organizational-level approaches as critical for sustainable workforce resilience (e.g. Kock and Latham, 2020; Hunt et al., 2021) [216]. By implementing standardized assessments and responding to individual patient needs, organizations can better support both patients and HCWs. Moreover, effective communication and support from the team are essential for quality improvement champions in healthcare settings [217]. However, challenges exist, including inconclusive evidence on reducing stress and improving retention, complex implementation processes for some interventions and limited effects on distal outcomes such as health and retention.

System-level interventions focused on policy and structural changes aimed at enhancing the resilience and capacity of healthcare systems to support HCWs during the pandemic. Examples include the Enhanced Universal Support Offer (EUSO) in the NHS, which provided additional support to care homes, and the implementation of Communities of Practice (COPs) in New South Wales (NSW) to facilitate collaboration and resource-sharing among healthcare professionals [211]. These interventions reflect a coordinated effort to address systemic challenges and ensure the effective mobilization of resources and support for HCWs.

Evidence supports the importance of implementing whole-system healthy workplace interventions in healthcare settings to address the high rates of sickness absence, burnout and distress among healthcare professionals [215, 218]. There is a growing body of research supporting the implementation of whole-system approaches to enhance the health and wellbeing of healthcare workers, ultimately aiming to improve healthcare delivery and staff experience in healthcare settings. Implementing whole-system approaches to enhance the health and wellbeing of HCWs is crucial for improving healthcare delivery and staff experience in healthcare settings, especially in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Research by Kock and Latham advocates for “whole organization” approaches to address the mental health impact on HCWs, emphasizing the need for a balanced view of psychological interventions and systemic support to tackle this significant public health challenge [215]. This highlights the importance of systemic interventions that go beyond individual-focused strategies to create supportive work environments and promote staff wellbeing.

Implications for policy

Decision-makers should consider the implementation of multi-level approaches that combine individual, organizational and system-level interventions to comprehensively address the needs of HCWs. Interventions that integrate mental health support, peer assistance and organizational resources are likely to be more effective in promoting resilience and wellbeing among HCWs. To effectively support HCWs, it is essential to implement multi-level strategies that encompass individual, organizational and system-level interventions. Greenberg et al. emphasize the significance of addressing mental health challenges encountered by HCWs during the COVID-19 pandemic and underscore the potential harm of using single-session psychological debriefing approaches [219]. In addition, Walton et al. highlights the importance of providing mental health care for medical staff and affiliated healthcare workers as part of the public health response to the pandemic [220] This underscores the necessity of integrating mental health support into interventions aimed at enhancing resilience and wellbeing among HCWs.

It is critical to recognize of the interplay among individual, organizational and systemic factors that influence HCWs’ wellbeing and resilience. Interventions that address structural inequities, promote supportive work environments and foster a culture of psychological safety are crucial for mitigating the impact of stress and burnout among HCWs. Addressing the wellbeing and resilience of HCWs requires interventions that consider the interplay between individual, organizational and systemic factors. Research by Hunt et al. emphasizes the importance of psychological safety and supportive work environments in enhancing team performance and wellbeing [221]. These studies highlight the need for interventions that promote psychological safety at individual, team and organizational levels to foster a culture where HCWs feel safe to speak up and voice their concerns. Furthermore, Paccoud et al. and Stajduhar et al. shed light on the impact of structural inequities, racial discrimination and socioeconomic factors on healthcare navigation and access [222, 223]. These studies underscore the importance of addressing systemic barriers to healthcare and promoting equity to ensure that all individuals, including HCWs, have equal access to care.

Implications for research

There is a need for ongoing evaluation and adaptation of interventions to ensure their relevance and effectiveness in dynamic and evolving contexts. Most of the interventions identified from this review had limited evaluation data. While many interventions demonstrated positive outcomes in initial evaluations, continuous monitoring and feedback mechanisms are essential to identify areas for improvement and sustain long-term impact. Proctor et al. emphasize the importance of continuous evaluation and propose the use of the “Dynamic Adaptation Process” framework, which may be useful for health systems [224]. The framework highlights the need for ongoing assessment of interventions to ensure they remain effective in changing environments, such as the health workforce during a public health emergency. This framework suggests that interventions should be continuously monitored, and adaptations should be made on the basis of feedback from stakeholders and data analysis. It argues against having a short-term perspective on interventions aimed to improve health systems. Furthermore, the work of Stirman et al. underscores the significance of feedback mechanisms in the implementation of interventions [225]. They argue that feedback loops are essential for identifying areas of improvement and making necessary adaptations to interventions. By collecting feedback from various health workforce stakeholders, organizations can gain valuable insights into the effectiveness of their interventions and make informed decisions about necessary changes.

Beyond the immediate context of the COVID-19 pandemic, these findings have broader implications for the long-term resilience and sustainability of healthcare systems. The challenges and gaps identified – particularly the reliance on short-term, individual-level interventions and the relative scarcity of structural and system-wide responses – reflect persistent vulnerabilities in workforce planning, governance and organizational culture. Strengthening health workforce supports at multiple levels is not only critical during emergencies but also essential for addressing ongoing pressures such as demographic shifts, chronic disease burdens, health inequities and the integration of new technologies. Embedding multi-level, system-wide approaches to workforce wellbeing into routine health system policy and planning can therefore help build a more adaptive, equitable and sustainable healthcare system capable of withstanding both acute shocks and long-term transformations.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this rapid evidence synthesis lies in its comprehensive and methodical approach, adhering to established guidelines from PRISMA-S and leveraging insights from both academic and grey literature. The study’s two-phased, sequential approach allowed for a thorough examination of the immediate impacts of COVID-19 on the healthcare workforce, followed by an expansion into international literature to identify effective interventions. This not only enriched the understanding of the challenges faced but also enabled a nuanced comparison across different jurisdictions and healthcare settings. Moreover, the synthesis benefitted from a multidisciplinary team and the expertise of library scientists, ensuring the rigour and breadth of the search strategies employed, which enhanced the validity and applicability of the findings for decision-makers.

The study does include several limitations that could impact the generalizability and applicability of its findings. The rapid nature of the review necessitated single-reviewer screening and data extraction processes, which, despite efficiency, may have introduced bias and may have resulted in missed sources and reduced the reliability of findings. Furthermore, the lack of language and study design limits in the search strategy, while inclusive, could have resulted in an overwhelming diversity of data, complicating the synthesis and interpretation processes. The study also primarily focused on published and accessible literature, potentially overlooking unpublished studies or non-English works that might hold relevant insights into the pandemic’s effects on healthcare workers or the effectiveness of certain interventions. These factors might limit the depth and comprehensiveness of the conclusions drawn from this synthesis.

The international search was intentionally restricted to countries with broadly comparable health system structures, workforce organization and policy contexts to Canada, specifically the United Kingdom (England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales), Australia and New Zealand. While this targeted approach strengthened the relevance of policy comparisons, it may limit the generalizability of findings to jurisdictions with different health system models or workforce structures.

Conclusions

The findings of this rapid evidence synthesis suggest that compared with sources reporting on pandemic impacts on HCWs, there were fewer sources that reported interventions to address workforce challenges during the pandemic, with fewer still reporting interventions that were implemented and evaluated. Of the interventions that were identified in the sources, most focused on workforce planning, education and training, scopes of practice and models of care, recruitment interventions and technology. Furthermore, many sources identified multi-faceted recommendations to address healthcare workforce challenges. The top recommendations included: (1) offering formal social and mental health supports, (2) enhancing workplace health and safety policies: health and safety policies to protect the physical safety of HCWs and patients and mitigate burnout and stress amongst HCWs, (3) strengthening organizational communication, (4) improving workplace culture and (5) developing more robust pandemic planning. Lessons can be taken from recommendations to inform future strategies and interventions. The evidence and resulting recommendations from these sources, complemented by integrated and contextualized workforce planning, could inform the development of future strategies and interventions to meet the current and future needs of the Canadian healthcare workforce. Importantly, the impact of healthcare workforce interventions extends beyond the workforce itself, as strengthening recruitment, retention, wellbeing and preparedness directly contributes to resilient health systems, improved quality of care, and sustained capacity to respond to future public health crises. By embedding workforce-centred strategies into broader health system planning, policymakers can ensure long-term system sustainability and equity.
