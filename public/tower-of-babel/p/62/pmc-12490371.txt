Real-world treatment patterns of Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors in mantle cell lymphoma in a community oncology setting
Authors: Bijal D Shah, Mei Xue, Wesley Furnback, Keri Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study examines United States real-world Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) treatment patterns, duration, and adherence in mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) patients.Materials & methodsA retrospective analysis of electronic medical records for patients with MCL who initiated a BTKi between January 2019 and November 2021 was conducted. Patients were followed ≥ 6 months, examining baseline characteristics and outcomes including treatment duration and adherence.Results402 patients initiated acalabrutinib (n = 161), ibrutinib (n = 197), or zanubrutinib (n = 44). The zanubrutinib group demonstrated significantly longer median treatment duration (292 days) compared with acalabrutinib (259 days) and ibrutinib (149 days; P < 0.01) and displayed significantly higher adherence to treatment.ConclusionsIn this study, zanubrutinib group experienced significantly longer treatment durations and higher adherence compared with the acalabrutinib and ibrutinib groups.KEYWORDS: Mantle cell lymphoma, Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, BTKi, treatment duration, adherence, treatment discontinuation

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a rare and aggressive subtype of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), typically characterized by the malignant transformation of B-cells in the mantle zone of lymphoid follicles. It accounts for approximately 2–10% of all cases of NHL [1–3]. Due to its aggressive nature, MCL often presents with advanced-stage disease and can be challenging to treat, requiring specialized therapeutic strategies [1–3]. There are two main subgroups of MCL, which are classified based on clinical presentation and molecular features, including nodal MCL (the most common) and leukemic non-nodal MCL [4]. Nodal MCL has an aggressive disease course and can include symptoms such as lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and gastrointestinal infiltration [4,5]. Leukemic non-nodal MCL typically has an indolent disease course with few symptoms [6].

The age-adjusted annual incidence of MCL in the United States (US) is estimated to be about 1 per 100,000 people and has been increasing over time [7,8]. MCL is diagnosed more often in males than females (3:1) and the risk of the disease has been shown to increase with age [8]. MCL is currently considered incurable, and the prognosis for patients diagnosed with this lymphoma is generally poor, with a median overall survival of less than 5 years [7].

Approximately three-quarters of patients diagnosed with MCL present with aggressive disease requiring immediate treatment [9,10]. First-line (1 L) treatment of MCL most often includes chemoimmunotherapy with the most utilized being bendamustine plus rituximab (BR) and a combination of rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) [11]. Treatment options for MCL patients have advanced beyond chemotherapy, leading to reported improvements in survival rates. Studies comparing survival during different time periods (ie before and after the introduction of novel treatments) have shown a significant reduction in the risk of death with newer therapies compared with chemotherapy alone [9]. However, most patients will eventually relapse or become refractory to treatment (R/R), with about 20% of patients experiencing this within 2 years of their initial therapy [12].

Treatment options for MCL in later lines are still limited but have seen advancement and significant shifts over the last 2 decades [13]. Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi) are the most frequently utilized treatments in the second line upon their approvals in MCL [13]. Ibrutinib, a first-generation BTKi, was the first BTKi approved for MCL in 2013 [14]; however, it was withdrawn in 2023 [15]. Following this, acalabrutinib was approved in 2017 [16], followed by zanubrutinib, which received approval in 2019 [17]. In 2023, pirtobrutinib, a non-covalent BTKi, received approval by the FDA for use in MCL after at least two lines of systemic therapy, which must include a BTKi [18].

The objective of this retrospective study was to report the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of MCL patients receiving BTKi-based therapeutic regimens as well as the treatment duration, discontinuation rates, and adherence of those regimens in a real-world setting.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study used the Integra Connect PrecisionQ real-world database to select adult MCL patients receiving a BTKi-based treatment regimen between 1 January 2019 and 30 November 2021. The Integra Connect PrecisionQ database includes electronic medical records (EMRs) from 17 community oncology network accounts that span across more than 1,900 providers in the US.

Study design

A retrospective, observational, cohort study was conducted using data from 1 October 2018 through 31 May 2022. An index period between 1 January 2019 and 30 November 2021 was used to identify adult MCL patients initiating a BTKi-based regimen. BTKi included acalabrutinib, ibrutinib, and zanubrutinib. Of the patients with a BTKi-based regimen, MCL patients were identified if they had one or more claims with a diagnosis of MCL on or before the date of the initiation of the BTKi-based regimen. The initiation date of the BTKi-based regimen served as the index date.

Patients were required to have data availability during a pre-index period, which included the 3 months immediately prior to the index date. Patients were also required to have at least 6 months of data availability following the index date. Data availability was defined as one or more valid medical or prescription claims during the pre-index and post-index period. Patients were followed from their index date until the end of the study period (31 May 2022) or they were lost to follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Study population

To be enrolled in this study, patients were required to fulfill the following criteria: (1) initiation of a new BTKi-based regimen (monotherapy or combination therapy) during the index period, and (2) one or more diagnoses of MCL on the date of the first BTKi claim or during the pre-index period. Patients were excluded from the study if they fit any of the following criteria: (1) were < 18 years of age at index, (2) did not have a valid medical or prescription claim in the 3-month period prior to the index date, or (3) did not have a valid medical or prescription claim in the 6-month period after the index date.

Enrolled patients were divided into three mutually exclusive cohorts (zanubrutinib, acalabrutinib, or ibrutinib) based on the BTKi-based regimen initiated during the index period. If patients initiated more than one BTKi-based regimen, a hierarchical approach was taken. If patients initiated zanubrutinib as a BTKi-based regimen, they were stratified into the zanubrutinib cohort and the date of zanubrutinib initiation was their index date. If patients initiated acalabrutinib as a BTKi-based regimen, but not zanubrutinib, they were stratified into the acalabrutinib cohort, and the date of acalabrutinib initiation was their index date. The remaining patients initiated an ibrutinib-based regimen and were included in the ibrutinib cohort with an index date of the date of ibrutinib initiation.

Study measures

Baseline demographics, including age, gender, race, ethnicity, and payer, were measured at the index for patients in the acalabrutinib, ibrutinib, and zanubrutinib cohorts. Comorbidities were measured during the pre-index period and included atrial fibrillation, cardiac arrhythmias (other than atrial fibrillation), cardiovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), hypertension, and renal disease.

Clinical events, treatment duration, discontinuation, and adherence were measured during the follow-up period. Clinical events were evaluated during the mandatory 180-day follow-up period and included anemia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, bleeding, dyspnea, fever, general fatigue, headache, hypertension, infection, myalgia, myocardial infarction, neutropenia, and weight loss.

Treatment duration, discontinuation, and adherence were evaluated throughout the follow-up period. Treatment duration was calculated from the index date (date of BTKi initiation) to the discontinuation date. Patients were considered to have discontinued if there was a more than 60-day gap in the BTKi’s supply. Discontinuation rationales including toxicity, no response, disease progression, or worsened comorbidities were reported when the data were available.

The adherence rate was calculated as the ratio of the actual number of days’ supply over the possible number of days’ supply for each BTKi at different intervals during the follow-up period. These intervals included 30 days, 60 days, 90 days, 180 days, and 360 days.

Statistical analysis

Demographics were measured at index and reported using means, standard deviations (SDs), and medians for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Baseline comorbidities were measured during the pre-index period and were reported as frequencies. Clinical events were measured during the 180-day mandatory follow-up period and were reported as frequencies. The median treatment duration over the follow-up period and the frequency of adherent patients at each of the pre-defined intervals were reported. P values for comparisons between the treatment regimens were calculated using ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Treatment duration was plotted via Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves using the event rates of discontinuation for each of the cohorts. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 402 MCL patients initiating either acalabrutinib, ibrutinib, or zanubrutinib were enrolled in the study. Of these patients, 93% had received at least one line of systematic therapy for MCL. 161 patients were included in the acalabrutinib group, 197 in the ibrutinib group, and 44 in the zanubrutinib group. Half (22/44) of zanubrutinib patients had prior use of a BTKi before initiating zanubrutinib, compared with 29.2% (47/161) of acalabrutinib patients. Per the study design, there were no ibrutinib patients with prior BTKi use. Furthermore, of the 22 zanubrutinib patients with prior BTKi use, 63.6% (14/22) had received the previous BTKi within 60 days of zanubrutinib initiation. In the acalabrutinib cohorts, 57.4% (27/47) of the 47 patients with previous BTKi utilization had received it within 60 days of the initiation of acalabrutinib.

The median age (range) at BTKi therapy start was 75 (56–89) years in zanubrutinib, 76 (36–89) in acalabrutinib, and 72 (36–89) in ibrutinib (P < 0.01); the percentage of patients 65 years of age and older was similar between the cohorts. There were no significant differences across the cohorts relating to gender, race, ethnicity, or payer type.

The prevalence of chronic pulmonary disease during the pre-index period was significantly different across the three cohorts (P = 0.04). Chronic pulmonary disease was prevalent in 25.0% (11/44) of zanubrutinib patients during the pre-index period, compared with 16.8% (27/161) and 12.7% (25/197) of acalabrutinib and ibrutinib patients, respectively. There were no significant differences across the cohorts in the prevalence of the other measured comorbidities during the pre-index period. However, rates were numerically higher for the zanubrutinib cohort compared with the acalabrutinib and ibrutinib cohorts for atrial fibrillation, cardiac arrhythmias, diabetes, GERD, and renal disease. Cardiovascular disease and hypertension were numerically highest for the acalabrutinib cohort.

Clinical events

There were no significant differences across the cohorts in the rates of clinical events during the 180-day follow-up period (Table 2). The zanubrutinib cohort had the lowest rate of atrial fibrillation (9.1%; n = 4/44) and no cases of atrial flutter during the follow-up period. The incidence of fever in the zanubrutinib cohort was 4.5% (2/44) compared with 12.4% (20/161) and 16.8% (33/197) for the acalabrutinib and ibrutinib cohorts, respectively. Myocardial infarction occurred in 4.3% (7/161) of acalabrutinib patients, 4.5% (2/44) of zanubrutinib patients, and 7.6% (15/197) of ibrutinib patients during follow-up.

Treatment duration

The average follow-up time was highest for ibrutinib (746 days), followed by acalabrutinib (701 days) and zanubrutinib (493 days). Median treatment duration was significantly (P < 0.01) longer for zanubrutinib (292 days) compared with acalabrutinib (259 days) and ibrutinib (149 days; Figure 1).

Treatment discontinuation

Zanubrutinib demonstrated the lowest discontinuation rate during the follow-up period with 43.2% (19/44) discontinuing, compared with 45.2% (89/197) for ibrutinib and 51.6% (83/161) for acalabrutinib (Table 3). Discontinuation due to toxicity was lower and similar for ibrutinib (13.2%; 26/197) and zanubrutinib (13.6%; 6/44), compared with acalabrutinib (17.4%; 28/161). Discontinuation due to no response, disease progression, or worsened comorbidities was lowest for zanubrutinib (18.2%; 8/44) followed by acalabrutinib (23.0%; 37/161) and ibrutinib (24.9%; 49/197).

Discussion

This real-world study evaluated the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of patients with MCL receiving BTKi-based regimens including acalabrutinib, ibrutinib, and zanubrutinib. Overall, there were no significant differences between the BTKi cohorts in clinical events over the 180-day follow-up period. However, the acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib cohorts generally had numerically lower rates of clinical events than the ibrutinib cohort. Treatment duration was found to be significantly longer for patients in the zanubrutinib cohort (median: 493 days) compared with the acalabrutinib (median: 259 days) and ibrutinib (median: 149 days) cohorts. Adherence rates for the measured intervals between 60 and 360 days were also significantly higher for zanubrutinib compared with those for acalabrutinib and ibrutinib. Lastly, patients on zanubrutinib had the lowest discontinuation rate during the follow-up period.

There have been few real-world studies comparing the outcomes of all three covalent BTKi with sufficient sample size in MCL patients [19]. Recent studies have primarily focused on examining outcomes in the post-BTKi setting [20,21]. Of the studies that have directly examined BTKi, there has been a low sample size for zanubrutinib due to its more recent approval [11,22–25]. The real-world studies examining BTKi primarily included cohorts in which ibrutinib was received by the majority of patients [11,24,25]. These studies have reported relatively short time to next treatment (TTNT) durations for these ibrutinib-dominated cohorts [11,25].

A real-world analysis of the Flatiron database in the US reported the TTNT for patients receiving BTKi in MCL according to treatment line [11]. Median TTNT was 17 months in 1 L, 13 months in second line (2 L), and 9 months in third line. The majority (78%) of patients in this analysis received ibrutinib. A study of MCL patients in a US claims database also reported median TTNTs of 30 days in the 1 L cohort, 55 days in 2 L, and 70 days in third line among patients receiving BTKi (62% ibrutinib) [25]. An international, retrospective, observational study reported a median BTKi treatment duration of 9 months in 2 L for R/R MCL patients after receiving rituximab-based chemotherapy in 1 L [24].

One recent study examined treatment duration individually for ibrutinib (n = 50) and acalabrutinib (n = 48) in R/R MCL patients in the US [22]. The analysis of R/R MCL patients receiving ibrutinib or acalabrutinib with at least 15 months of follow-up reported median treatment durations (time to discontinuation) of 8.9 months for ibrutinib and 6.9 months for acalabrutinib [22]. Given the relatively small sample size of the ibrutinib and acalabrutinib cohorts, the difference in treatment duration was not significant. In our study, zanubrutinib and acalabrutinib reported higher median treatment durations of 9.6 months and 8.5 months. These results suggest that as the data matures, there could be an increase in TTNT and treatment duration for MCL patients receiving zanubrutinib or acalabrutinib-based treatment regimens.

A recent retrospective chart review included 300 MCL patients receiving either acalabrutinib (n = 100), ibrutinib (n = 100), or zanubrutinib (n = 100) and examined response and adverse events [26]. The review reported a trend favoring zanubrutinib over acalabrutinib and ibrutinib, even with the zanubrutinib patients generally being older and more complex. A claims-based study in the US examined compliance rates for 1,242 patients receiving ibrutinib, 485 patients receiving acalabrutinib, and 67 patients receiving zanubrutinib [27]. In this study, the majority (68.4%) of ibrutinib use was in 1 L treatment, while acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib were used in the R/R setting in 68.9% and 80.6% of cases, respectively. During the 1-year follow-up period, the compliance rate was highest for zanubrutinib (65%) followed by acalabrutinib (62%) and ibrutinib (59%). The trends in both of these studies involving all three covalent BTKi were consistent with the findings of our study, in which zanubrutinib had more favorable outcomes compared with acalabrutinib and ibrutinib.

These dire outcomes reinforce the need for patients to receive a covalent BTKi that can maximize their time on treatment through clinical effectiveness and manageable side effects. However, the results of this analysis also show strong outcomes for zanubrutinib in the post-BTKi setting, as half of the patients in the zanubrutinib cohort had a history of prior BTKi use. Due to the study design, patients in the acalabrutinib and ibrutinib cohorts had lower rates of prior BTKi exposure, with 29% of the acalabrutinib cohort and no patients in the ibrutinib cohort with a history of BTKi use.

There are several limitations of this study. First, this is an analysis of real-world data using EMRs, which makes it subject to miscoding, inconsistent documentation and reporting, missing data, and potential inaccuracies. Second, 93% of the overall study cohort had received at least one line of systematic therapy for MCL; however, due to the limitation of real-world EMR data, it was challenging to accurately determine the precise line of therapy. The design of this study may have led to differences in the number of prior lines of therapy and/or the number of prior BTKi between the cohorts at baseline. Therefore, we focused on the BTKi-based treatment cohort instead of defining the line of therapy. In addition, some clinical information, including staging, biomarkers, and the determination of line of treatment, could not be accurately assessed in the database as it could be subject to missing data and treatments received outside of the records included in the database. As the line of therapy is an important indicator for outcomes, it could have provided some insight regarding the differences in outcomes between the included BTKi. Third, a large period of this study took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have impacted patterns of care, interactions with healthcare providers, and outcomes. Lastly, the Integra Connect PrecisionQ dataset is not a nationally representative dataset and only includes data from community hospitals in the US. The results of this analysis may not be generalizable to some populations and may not represent the management of MCL patients in institutions not included in the database.

Conclusion

This real-world study using EMR data from US community-based oncology practices shows a significantly longer treatment duration, increased adherence, and lower rates of discontinuation for zanubrutinib compared with acalabrutinib and ibrutinib in patients with MCL. Further analyses using more recent data and a longer time horizon are needed to validate these outcomes.
