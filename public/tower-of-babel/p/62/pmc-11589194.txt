Targeting glioblastoma with a brain-penetrant drug that impairs brain tumor stem cells via NLE1-Notch1 complex
Authors: Audrey Burban, Ahmad Sharanek, Aldo Hernandez-Corchado, Hamed S. Najafabadi, Vahab D. Soleimani, Arezu Jahani-Asl
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBrain tumor stem cells (BTSCs) are a population of self-renewing malignant stem cells that play an important role in glioblastoma tumor hierarchy and contribute to tumor growth, therapeutic resistance, and tumor relapse. Thus, targeting of BTSCs within the bulk of tumors represents a crucial therapeutic strategy. Here, we report that edaravone is a potent drug that impairs BTSCs in glioblastoma. We show that edaravone inhibits the self-renewal and growth of BTSCs harboring a diverse range of oncogenic mutations without affecting non-oncogenic neural stem cells. Global gene expression analysis revealed that edaravone significantly alters BTSC transcriptome and attenuates the expression of a large panel of genes involved in cell cycle progression, stemness, and DNA repair mechanisms. Mechanistically, we discovered that edaravone directly targets Notchless homolog 1 (NLE1) and impairs Notch signaling pathway, alters the expression of stem cell markers, and sensitizes BTSC response to ionizing radiation (IR)-induced cell death. Importantly, we show that edaravone treatment in preclinical models delays glioblastoma tumorigenesis, sensitizes their response to IR, and prolongs the lifespan of animals. Our data suggest that repurposing of edaravone is a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with glioblastoma.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive primary tumor in the adult brain. The present standard of care for GB includes maximal surgical excision of the tumor followed by ionizing radiation (IR) and chemotherapy (Stupp et al., 2005). Despite multimodal therapy, prognosis for patients with GB is still dismal with a median survival rate of less than 22 months following diagnosis (Gilbert et al., 2014; Stupp et al., 2017). Multiple factors contribute to this poor outcome including intra-tumoral heterogeneity, tumor resistance to therapy, and a high rate of tumor recurrence (Gimple et al., 2019; Neftel et al., 2019; Patel et al., 2014), mainly due to the presence of a population of tumor cells with stem-like properties, the brain tumor stem cells (BTSCs).

BTSCs sustain self-renewal, exhibit enhanced proliferation and tumorigenic potential, and are able to recapitulate the original tumor (Singh et al., 2004). In addition, BTSCs can evade therapy and contribute to tumor relapse via multiple mechanisms (Bao et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2012; Cheng et al., 2011). Developing novel strategies to target BTSCs is at the forefront of efforts to combat GB.

Edaravone (3-methyl-1-phenyl-2-pyrazolin-5-one, MCI-186, Radicava), is a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drug which has been successfully used to treat patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (Rothstein, 2017). This synthetic pyrazolone derivative is a free radical scavenger and an anti-inflammatory drug (Fujisawa and Yamamoto, 2016; Tsuji et al., 2005). Edaravone also functions as a modulator of transcription factors, such as nuclear factor κB (Kokura et al., 2005; Tsuji et al., 2005) and PHOX2B (Li et al., 2022). Importantly, in the context of organ injuries to the brain (Liu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2005) and liver (Tsuji et al., 2005; Yoshida et al., 2008), edaravone has been shown to reduce the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), a key regulator of BTSC growth and maintenance (Eyler et al., 2011).

In view of our current knowledge that edaravone suppresses iNOS signaling and iNOS plays important roles in BTSC and GB tumorigenesis (Eyler et al., 2011), we asked whether edaravone can target cancer stem cells in GB. In the present study, we report for the first time that edaravone impairs stemness in patient-derived BTSCs harboring different oncogenic mutations without inducing toxicity to non-oncogenic neural stem cells. We found that edaravone suppresses iNOS expression modestly prompting us to investigate its precise mechanism of action. Follow-up studies led to the discovery of Notchless homolog 1 (NLE1) as a direct target of edaravone in BTSCs. Via binding to NLE1, edaravone alters Notch signaling pathway and BTSC fate. Importantly, edaravone sensitizes GB response to IR therapy and prolongs lifespan in preclinical models.

Results

Edaravone is a potent drug that specifically targets the BTSCs

Previous studies have shown that edaravone regulates iNOS expression levels by inhibiting its promoter transactivation and mRNA stability (Yoshida et al., 2008). iNOS is a direct transcriptional target gene of STAT3, which is highly upregulated in GB tumor cells downstream of EGFR/EGFRvIII (Puram et al., 2012). We, thus, examined the effect of edaravone on multiple patient-derived BTSCs. We subjected BTSCs to edaravone at different concentrations and assessed cell viability using PrestoBlue assay. Our results revealed a significant impairment of cell growth in all the BTSC tested following 3 days of treatment with edaravone and a more pronounced effect with lower growth inhibition 50 (GI50) values upon increasing the duration of treatments to 7 days (Figure S1A). Since in vitro stem cell culture supplements are saturated with proteins, and edaravone is shown to have high binding affinity to proteins (FDA report, 2022), we asked whether drug-protein binding could minimize the effect of the drug in vitro leading to higher GI50. We conducted additional experiments in media with lower B27 supplement. Notably, following 3 days of treatment, we observed a striking decrease in average GI50 from 491 to 83 μM in BTSC73 (Figures S1A–S1C), indicating that the high protein levels present in culture media in vitro capture a large fraction of the edaravone. Given the significance of edaravone on cell viability, we next evaluated the effect of edaravone on BTSC proliferation as well as cell death. We employed a 5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay and KI67 staining and found a significant reduction in cell proliferation (Figures 1A–1E). Our results suggest that edaravone treatment results in a dose- and time-dependent inhibition of cell proliferation in all the BTSCs. In parallel, we conducted flow cytometry analysis of BTSCs for annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) following 7 days of edaravone treatment with concentrations up to 500 μM. Our results revealed no cell death (Figures 1F–1H), confirming that the decrease in cell viability at 500 μM of edaravone is not due to the induction of cell death but rather attributed to the inhibition of proliferation or self-renewal. Next, we examined the highest concentration of edaravone that could be tolerated by BTSCs in stem cell media, without inducing toxicity or cell death. We found a ∼15%–25% of cells positive for PI at 750–1,000 μM of edaravone, respectively (Figure S1D), and a significant 50% increase of early apoptotic cells in BTSC73, BTSC147, BTSC68, and BTSC172 upon treatment with 1,000 μM of edaravone (Figure S1E). These results raised the question of whether the tolerated dose of 500 μM by BTSCs induces potential toxicity to the non-oncogenic stem cells. To address this question, we employed wild-type neural stem cells (NSCs) in parallel experiments. Strikingly, no significant induction of cell death was observed in NSCs, even with the highest concentration of 1,000 μM (Figures S1F and S1G). Edaravone impairs Notch signaling and stem cell signature in BTSCs

Edaravone has been shown to suppress the expression of iNOS in the brain (Liu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2005) and liver (Tsuji et al., 2005; Yoshida et al., 2008), and iNOS is a central player in BTSC maintenance and GB tumorigenesis (Eyler et al., 2011; Jahani-Asl and Bonni, 2013). We, thus, examined whether edaravone impairs BTSCs via altering iNOS. Surprisingly, treatment of BTSCs with edaravone for 3 days attenuated the iNOS mRNA expression modestly and did not have an impact on iNOS protein expression level (Figures S2A and S2B) or on levels of nitrite (NO2−), a stable byproduct of nitric oxide (Figure S2C). We, thus, reasoned that edaravone may function via alternate mechanisms in BTSCs. To gain a broader knowledge of how edaravone impacts BTSCs, we subjected BTSC73 to mRNA sequencing analyses in the absence and presence of edaravone (Figure S3). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed the downregulation of pathways related to cell cycle progression in edaravone-treated BTSCs (Figures S4A–S4E). Validation of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data by quantitative real-time PCR analysis (real-time qPCR), and cell cycle profiling analysis on edaravone-treated cells revealed that edaravone alters gene panels related to cell cycle (Figure S4F) and leads to deregulated cell cycle progression, characterized by a decrease in the percentages of cells in G1 and an accumulation of cells in the S phase (Figure S4G). Interestingly, in addition to changes in cell cycle progression, GSEA revealed a significant downregulation of stem cell markers and signaling pathways related to BTSC maintenance including Notch and Hedgehog signaling (Clement et al., 2007; Rajakulendran et al., 2019) (Figures 2A–2C). To validate these results, real-time qPCR analysis was conducted whereby we found significant downregulation in the expression of NOTCH1, LFNG, MAML2, DTX4, HEY1, and WNT5 (Figure 2D). Furthermore, immunoblotting for cleaved Notch1 revealed a strong reduction of the active (cleaved) Notch1 protein level in BTSCs treated with edaravone compared to vehicle (DMSO)-treated cells (Figures 2E–2J). Notch signaling plays crucial roles in controlling BTSC stemness and self-renewal (El-Sehemy et al., 2020; Park et al., 2017; Rajakulendran et al., 2019). We, therefore, assessed BTSC self-renewal by extreme limiting dilution assay (ELDA) and expanded on our analysis of additional stem cell markers that were deregulated in the RNA-seq data. mRNA and protein expression analyses showed significant downregulation of SOX2, SOX9, Olig2, Nestin, ID3, and ID4 mRNA and a marked decrease in SOX2 and Nestin protein expression levels in edaravone-treated BTSCs (Figures 2K–2Q). Via subjecting different patient-derived BTSCs to ELDA, we established that edaravone treatment robustly attenuated self-renewal in a dose-dependent manner in any of the BTSCs that were examined (Figures 2R–2T; Figures S5A–S5D). Next, we asked whether the effect of edaravone on stemness is restricted to cancer stem cells. ELDA analysis was conducted on wild-type NSCs. Strikingly, at concentrations that are effective to inhibit BTSC self-renewal, treatment of wild-type NSCs with 100 and 250 μM of edaravone had no significant impact on NSC self-renewal (Figure S5E). A decrease in stem cell frequency was, however, observed at a higher concentration of edaravone (500 μM) in NSCs (Figure S5E). Edaravone targets NLE1-Notch1 signaling

To identify the direct targets of edaravone, we next employed the drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay followed by proteomic analysis using mass spectrometry of the bands that were protected from proteolytic digestion in the presence of edaravone. Strikingly, this analysis led to the identification of the NLE1, a regulator of Notch signaling, among the edaravone binding partners, raising the question of whether edaravone regulates Notch1 signaling via NLE1. To address this question, we conducted DARTS followed by immunoblotting to confirm the interaction of edaravone with NLE1. Whole BTSC lysates treated with edaravone or vehicle control were subjected to protein digestion by pronase. Western blot analysis revealed that while NLE1 was digested by pronase in vehicle control (Figures 3A and 3B), it was protected in a dose-dependent manner in the edaravone-treated samples even at high pronase concentration (45 ng/μL) (Figures 3A and 3B). In contrast, vinculin that does not interact with edaravone was digested in control and edaravone-treated samples (Figures 3A and 3B). NLE1 is shown to bind to the cytoplasmic domain of Notch and serves as a direct regulator of Notch signaling activity in Drosophila and Xenopus embryos (Royet et al., 1998). Thus, we investigated whether NLE1 binds to Notch1 in BTSCs. We employed endogenous co-immunoprecipitation experiments to evaluate protein-protein interaction in patient-derived BTSCs. Strikingly, we found that NLE1 physically interacts with Notch1 in BTSC73 (Figure 3C) and BTSC147 (Figure 3D). Next, to examine the functional consequence of this interaction, we induced genetic knockdown (KD) of NLE1 and asked whether NLE1 KD phenocopies the impact of edaravone. Our data showed that silencing of NLE1 decreases the expression of active/cleaved Notch1 in multiple patient-derived BTSCs (Figures 3E and 3F) and this decrease was accompanied by attenuated expression of stemness markers SOX2 and Olig2 (Figures 3E and 3F). In further support, ELDA analysis of NLE1 KD BTSCs revealed 70% decrease in self-renewal capacity compared to BTSC control cells (Figures 3G and 3H). Altogether, these data reveal that genetic KD of NLE1 phenocopies edaravone effects in BTSCs inducing Notch1 deregulation and BTSC impairment.

Edaravone delays GB tumorigenesis and extends animal lifespan

Next, we set out to assess the impact of edaravone on tumor growth using patient-derived xenograft model. To begin with, we subcutaneously injected BTSC73 into the flank of 8-week-old severe combined immunodeficient (SCID) mice and assessed tumor growth by bioluminescent imaging of luciferase expression using in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Three weeks following injection, all mice in the vehicle-treated group harbored ulcerated tumors at the site of injection. Strikingly, edaravone decreased tumor growth by 80% as quantified using the luciferase signal (Figures 4A and 4B) and tumor mass (Figure 4C). Previous studies have demonstrated that edaravone is able to penetrate through the blood-brain barrier (Fang et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015). We, therefore, investigated whether edaravone could suppress tumorigenesis in the brain using BTSC-derived orthotopic xenografts. We transplanted BTSC73 into the brains of SCID mice (Figure 4D). Tumor formation was monitored by IVIS (Figure 4E). When tumor became detectable, after 6 days, the animals were randomized to receive edaravone or vehicle control intraperitoneally. At 22 days following surgery, vehicle-treated mice xenografted with BTSC73 formed malignant brain tumors (Figures 4E and 4F), while a decrease was observed in brain tumor growth in edaravone-treated mice (Figures 4E and 4F). Furthermore, edaravone treatment prolonged the lifespan of mice-bearing BTSC73 xenografts (Figure 4G). To confirm these results, we run parallel experiments with another patient-derived BTSC147, as described for BTSC73. 39 days following surgical implantation of BTSCs in the brain, mice receiving vehicle control formed malignant brain tumors while mice treated with edaravone exhibited harbored smaller tumors (Figure 4H) and exhibited extended lifespan (Figure 4I). Having established that edaravone impairs stemness and Notch1 pathway in vitro, we asked whether it alters stemness in brain tumors in vivo. Brain tumor sections stained with SOX2 and cleaved Notch1 revealed a strong reduction in SOX2 (Figure 4J) and cleaved Notch1 expression (Figure 4K) in brain tumor of edaravone-treated mice relative to the vehicle control. Edaravone treatment sensitizes BTSCs and GB tumors to IR-induced cell death

Notch signaling promotes radioresistance in BTSCs (Wang et al., 2010), and upregulation of DNA repair capacity is among the mechanisms by which BTSCs resist IR-induced DNA damage and associated DNA-breaks-induced apoptosis (Bao et al., 2006). Our data revealed that edaravone downregulates DNA repair pathway (Figure 5A) and Notch signaling, leading to the question of whether edaravone treatment in combination with IR provides a survival advantage. First, we conducted immunofluorescence staining of phosphorylated histone H2A variant (p-H2AX), a DNA damage marker, and observed an increase in γ-H2AX-positive cells in edaravone-treated BTSC73 xenografts (Figures 5B and 5C; Figures S6A and S6B). Next, we evaluated how edaravone impacts the response of BTSCs to IR. We employed cell viability assays on multiple patient-derived BTSCs following treatment with edaravone alone, IR alone (2 and 4 Gy), or combined treatment with edaravone and IR. Compared to untreated cells, a∼2-fold decrease in live cell number was observed in edaravone-treated or irradiated BTSCs. Strikingly, a drastic decrease between 8- and 9-fold in cell viability was observed in BTSCs receiving the combination of edaravone and IR (Figures 5D–5H). Via employing annexin V/PI flow cytometry assay, we also assessed the impact of the combined IR and edaravone treatment on cell death in BTSCs. While treatment with 500 μM edaravone did not induce cell death, a dose of 4 Gy of IR resulted in 12% PI-positive cells. Importantly, the combined treatment of edaravone and IR resulted in a significant induction of cell death with 33% of cells positive for PI (Figures 5I and 5J; Figure S6C). Next, we assessed IR-edaravone combination in vivo, by transplanting luciferase-expressing BTSC73 into the brain of immunodeficient mice, followed by treatment with vehicle control, edaravone (50 mg/kg), irradiation (2 cycles of 2 Gy), or a combination of edaravone and IR. Edaravone alone and IR alone delayed tumor formation, with ∼5-fold decrease in luciferase activity compared to control mice. Interestingly, a significant major reduction in tumor size was observed in irradiated edaravone-treated mice with ∼18-fold decrease in luciferase activity compared to control and ∼3.5-fold decrease compared to edaravone or IR alone (Figures 5K and 5L). Importantly, the survival of animals receiving the combination of IR and edaravone was significantly extended compared to the animals that received IR or edaravone alone (Figure 5M). In summary, we present data that edaravone sensitizes BTSCs and GB tumors to IR-induced cell death. These results suggest that combination therapy with edaravone and IR is a promising strategy for GB tumors.

Discussion

In the present study, we establish for the first time that edaravone is a promising drug for targeting of cancer stem cells in GB. Edaravone suppresses BTSCs via suppressing NLE1-Notch signaling and impairs cell cycle progression and DNA repair mechanisms. Importantly, edaravone sensitizes GB response to IR and expands lifespan in preclinical patient-derived xenograft models. Our data suggest that repurposing of edaravone in combination with IR could prove effective in combating GB.

Edaravone was initially approved for the treatment of patients with ALS. In pre-clinical animal models, edaravone is suggested to exert a potential therapeutic effect against organ injuries (Doi et al., 2004; Tsuji et al., 2005) and neurological diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (Jiao et al., 2015), epilepsy (Liu et al., 2018), and motor neuron disease (Ikeda and Iwasaki, 2015). However, prior to our study, the impact of edaravone on BTSCs and GB tumorigenesis was not investigated.

Notch signaling plays diverse roles in nervous system development, including maintenance of self-renewal and regulation of fate decisions in neural and glial lineages (Louvi and Artavanis-Tsakonas, 2006; Yoon and Gaiano, 2005). In GB, Notch signaling is aberrantly activated and is associated with poor prognosis of patients with GB (Dell’albani et al., 2014; Hai et al., 2018). Furthermore, Notch signaling is shown to be highly active in BTSCs and it contributes to their tumorigenicity and clonogenic potential (El-Sehemy et al., 2020; Rajakulendran et al., 2019). Recently, Notch1 has been shown to upregulate the transcription of SOX2 via the downstream Notch-induced transcription factor SOX9 to control BTSC invasion and is correlated with a poor prognosis of glioma subjects (Wang et al., 2019). We show that edaravone suppresses Notch signaling via binding to NLE1. Although NLE1 was discovered as a direct regulator of Notch signaling activity in Drosophila and Xenopus embryos (Royet et al., 1998), its role in cancer has remained poorly understood. Two recent studies showed that NLE1 appears to be involved in the progression of melanoma (Ren et al., 2021) and non-small cell lung cancer (Xu et al., 2022). In the present study, we provide evidence that NLE1 forms a complex with Notch1 in patient-derived BTSCs, and its targeting by edaravone leads to BTSC and GB impairment. Given the central role of Notch signaling in BTSCs and GB malignancy, and in view of our findings that edaravone significantly alters Notch signaling, we provide proof of principle for consideration of edaravone for future GB treatment.

In our study, we found that high concentration of edaravone is required to suppress BTSCs in vitro. We thus conducted additional experiments in which we maintained the BTSCs in low-supplement media and showed that the presence of high amount of proteins in the stem cell culture media supplements neutralizes a large fraction of the drug. In support of our findings, drug-protein binding is shown to be an issue for in vitro studies, as this could minimize the effect of the drug (Bowman and Benet, 2018; Gardner et al., 2022; Hestermann et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2016). Several studies highlight that higher concentrations of drugs are required in the culture medium to induce a cell response compared to the Cmax that causes effects in vivo. In support of this, our experiments performed in media with reduced proteins revealed 5-fold decrease in the concentration of edaravone required to induce 50% growth inhibition. Although drug-protein binding impacts drug concentration in vitro, in clinic, data for most compounds have revealed that plasma proteins have minimal effects on the efficacy of the compounds (Smith et al., 2010). Noteworthy, the Cmax of edaravone in patients could attain 55 μM (Shimizu et al., 2021), which is comparable to the GI50 in reduced protein condition in vitro.

Edaravone is a lipophilic molecule with a low molecular weight that can pass the blood-brain barrier (Fang et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015). However, edaravone has a short circulation half-life and limited penetration of the blood-brain barrier, which may attenuate its therapeutic efficacy in the brain. According to the FDA pharmacological review of edaravone and based on 14C-edaravone distribution studies in rats, only 6% of plasma edaravone levels were detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (FDA report, 2017). In the past 5 years, different strategies have been developed to improve edaravone brain delivery and stability, such as encapsulation of edaravone in micelle (Jin et al., 2017), plasma exosomes (Guo et al., 2021), alginate-based nanocomposite hydrogel (Fan et al., 2019), co-treatment with compounds to enhance its blood-brain barrier penetration (Fang et al., 2014), and intranasal administration of edaravone nanoparticles (Lu et al., 2023). Given that edaravone is already an FDA-approved drug and substantial trials to improve its stability and delivery to the brain are ongoing, the feasibility of edaravone repurposing for GB treatment is warranted in near future.

Experimental procedures

Patient-derived BTSC cultures

BTSCs were generated following surgery with informed consent of adult patients with GB. All cells were characterized for major mutations in GB including EGFRvIII, p53, PTEN, and IDH1 status (Table S1). See supplementary materials and methods for details.

BTSC treatment with edaravone

BTSCs were dissociated to single-cell suspension using Accumax (Innovative Cell Technologies, #AM105) and treated with edaravone (Sigma, #M70800) or vehicle control (DMSO 0.1%) at the time of plating, at the indicated concentration and time points. To determine the impact of protein binding on edaravone efficiency, BTSCs were treated in media containing 0.1% B27 supplement.

Cell proliferation and determination of GI50

BTSCs were dissociated to single-cell suspension using Accumax. Cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells/well, in low-attachment 96-well plates (Sarstedt, #83.3924.500). Cell number was evaluated 3 and 7 days post plating using PrestoBlue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A13261) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Dose-response curves were plotted, and growth inhibition 50 (GI50) values were calculated using nonlinear regression analyses with the GraphPad Prism software.

Cell death

Cell death was determined using TACS annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (R&D systems, #4830-01-K) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and as detailed in the supplementary materials and methods.

IR

For measurement of cell proliferation and cell death, BTSCs were dissociated to single-cell suspension using Accumax. BTSCs were treated with edaravone or vehicle control at the time of plating, and then irradiated with 2 or 4 Gy using the X-ray Irradiation System (Faxitron MultiRad 225). Cell proliferation was assessed 7 days post irradiation using PrestoBlue assay, and cell death was measured using annexin/PI assay 4 days post irradiation.

ELDA

Decreasing numbers of BTSCs per well (dose: 100, 50, 25, 12, 6, 3, and 1) were plated in a 96-well plate, with a minimum of 12 wells/dose and treated with edaravone or vehicle control at the time of plating. Seven days after plating, the presence of spheres in each well was recorded and analysis was performed using software available at http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/(Hu and Smyth, 2009) to define the percentage of stem cell frequency.

EdU proliferation assay

EdU assay was performed using the Click-iT EdU cell proliferation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C10340). Briefly, 3 days following treatment with edaravone, EdU was added to the culture for 1 h. BTSCs were then dissociated using Accumax and 7 × 104 cells were plated on 24-well-treated plate in media containing 10% FBS, for 30 min. Cells were then washed with DPBS, fixed, permeabilized, and stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. See supplementary materials and methods for details.

DARTS assay

DARTS assay was performed according to Lomenick et al. (2009). See supplementary materials and methods for details.

Protein immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitations were performed on whole BTSC lysates as previously described (Sharanek et al., 2020) and detailed in the supplementary materials and methods.

Stereotaxic injections

Luciferase-expressing BTSCs were stereotactically implanted into the right striata (0.8 mm lateral to the bregma, 1 mm dorsal and 2.5 mm from the pial surface) of 8-week-old male SCID mice. Six days post BTSC injection, mice were administered intraperitoneally with 50 mg/kg edaravone, as previously used (Feng et al., 2021), or vehicle control every day until mice were sacrificed. For Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival plots, mice were collected when they showed signs of tumor-related illness. See supplementary materials and methods for details.

Statistical analysis

Two-tailed and unpaired t tests were used to compare two conditions. One-way and two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc analyses were used for analyzing multiple groups. Data are shown as mean with standard error of mean (mean ± SEM). The log rank test was used for statistical analysis in the Kaplan-Meier survival plot. p Values of equal or less than 0.05 were considered significant.
