Vascular‐targeted photodynamic therapy with glucose‐conjugated chlorin e6 for dogs with spontaneously occurring tumours
Authors: Tomohiro Osaki, Shota Hibino, Yusuke Murahata, Takao Amaha, Inoru Yokoe, Masamichi Yamashita, Akihiro Nomoto, Shigenobu Yano, Mamoru Tanaka, Hiromi Kataoka, Yoshiharu Okamoto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhotodynamic therapy (PDT) using photosensitisers is a minimally invasive treatment for malignant tumours. However, ideal photosensitisers are not yet established. Recently, we developed a new photosensitiser, glucose‐conjugated chlorin e6 (G‐Ce6).ObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical efficacy of vascular‐targeted PDT (VTP), a type of PDT utilising a short drug‐light interval, using G‐Ce6 to treat spontaneously occurring tumours in dogs.MethodsFive dogs with spontaneously occurring tumours (malignant melanoma: three; haemangiopericytoma: two; and squamous cell carcinoma: one) were subjected to VTP. These dogs were intravenously injected with G‐Ce6 at doses of 1–3 mg/kg 5 min before laser irradiation. Tumours were superficially or interstitially irradiated using a 677‐nm diode laser.ResultsRepeated VTP decreased tumour size, yielding complete remission in three dogs. Complications such as oedema surrounding normal tissues and fistulae were observed, and the oedema was self‐limiting. The fistula was cured by debriding the necrotic tissues formed after VTP.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that VTP using G‐Ce6 had antitumour effects in dogs with spontaneously occurring tumours.

INTRODUCTION

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a minimally invasive treatment for malignant tumours. PDT is based on the preferential tumour‐specific accumulation of a photosensitiser, followed by local irradiation of the lesion with light of an appropriate wavelength. The interaction between light and the administered photosensitiser induces a photochemical reaction that generates highly reactive species, termed singlet oxygen (1O2) or radicals (van Straten et al., 2017; Gunaydin et al., 2021). In veterinary medicine, there have been several reports on the use of PDT (Dobson et al., 2018; Guimarães et al., 2022). Although PDT is safe and effective for a variety of tumours in dogs and cats, it does not always ensure an effective therapeutic outcome. Therefore, development of optimal photosensitisers characterised by low skin photosensitivity and water solubility, a short drug‐light interval (DLI), a high absorption peak of 600−800 nm, and a high quantum yield for singlet oxygen is required (Gunaydin et al., 2021). Although various photosensitisers have been developed to improve the effects of PDT and decrease side effects (van Straten et al., 2017), an ideal photosensitiser for PDT is yet to be developed.

Porphyrins and chlorins are the most commonly used photosensitisers (van Straten et al., 2017). Chlorin e6 (Ce6), a second‐generation photosensitiser, has a high efficiency in singlet oxygen generation and shows an absorption peak at approximately 670 nm, which is favourable for deeper tissue penetration. Mono‐L‐aspartyl chlorin e6 (NPe6) has been clinically approved for PDT of lung and oesophageal cancers and other malignant tumours (Ikeda et al., 2011; Kobayashi et al., 2022; Yano et al., 2021). However, the clinical use of Ce6 is limited because of its hydrophobicity. Various studies have assessed the clinical use of Ce6 in PDT. Although chlorin e6‐conjugated poly(ethylene glycol)‐poly(d,l‐lactide) nanoparticles, Ce6‐coated superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, and gelatin‐Ce6 have been developed, their use remains limited to in vivo experiments (Amirshaghaghi et al., 2019; Kumari et al., 2019; Son et al., 2019).

Recently, we developed a new photosensitiser, glucose‐conjugated chlorin e6 (G‐Ce6) (Kataoka et al., 2021; Nomoto et al., 2021; Osaki et al., 2019). It is relatively water‐soluble and rapidly excreted from the body. The half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of NPe6 and G‐Ce6 against SNP cells, primary canine mammary gland tumour cells, are 30.6 and 1.7 μg/mL at a light dose of 15 J/cm2, respectively (Osaki et al., 2019). When a photosensitiser is administered intravenously, it is initially present in the vascular system. After a few hours to days, the photosensitiser accumulates in the tumour tissues (Mashayekhi et al., 2019). The biological target of PDT depends on the DLI. Conventional tumour cell‐targeted PDT preferentially damages tumour cells using a long DLI photosensitiser, as the photosensitiser preferentially accumulates in the tumour cells. Alternatively, VTP preferentially damages tumour vessels using a short DLI photosensitiser, as the photosensitiser remains localised within the vasculature at the peak plasma concentration (Mashayekhi et al., 2019; Nogueira et al., 2020; Osaki et al., 2009). VTP, a PDT type involving short DLI, using G‐Ce6, has been effective against mice bearing canine mammary gland tumours (Osaki et al., 2019). Furthermore, G‐Ce6 at a dose of <5 mg/kg was reportedly well tolerated by healthy adult Beagle dogs (Osaki et al., 2019). However, the efficacy of PDT with G‐Ce6 has not yet been established in dogs with tumours. In the present study, we evaluated the clinical efficacy of VTP using G‐Ce6 to treat spontaneously occurring tumours in dogs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects and tumour

VTP was initially used to treat five client‐owned dogs with naturally occurring tumours (malignant melanoma, three; haemangiopericytoma, two; and SCC, one). No evidence of metastasis was observed on radiography or computed tomography (CT). Each tumour was diagnosed via histopathological examination of biopsy samples (Table 1). As mandibulectomy or amputation could affect the mobility and functionality of the body, the owners decided against surgery. After obtaining written informed consent from the owners, dogs were treated with VTP using G‐Ce6. Before each treatment, tumour size was measured using a Vernier calliper (VC‐20 Silver M model slide Caliper; Niigata Seiki Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan). The local response to VTP was evaluated using response evaluation criteria for solid tumours in dogs (v1.0) (Nguyen et al., 2015). Survival time was measured starting from the first PDT session.

Photosensitiser

Methyl (7S,8S)−18‐ethyl‐5‐(2‐methoxy‐2‐oxoethyl)−7‐(3‐methoxy‐3‐oxopropyl)−2,8,12,17‐tetramethyl‐13‐(1‐(3‐(((2S,3R,4S,5S,6R)−3,4,5‐trihydroxy‐6‐(hydroxymethyl) tetrahydro‐2H‐pyran‐2‐yl)thio)propoxy) ethyl)−7H,8H‐porphyrin‐3‐carboxylate (G‐Ce6, MW: 893.06) was synthesised as previously described (Nomoto et al., 2021). For intravenous administration in dogs, G‐Ce6 was dissolved in 2.5% Kolliphor (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 97.5% saline. The volume of G‐Ce6 required to achieve the initial desired dose of 1 mg/kg (up to 3 mg/kg, depending on the therapeutic effect on the tumour) was diluted using 0.5% saline to obtain a total injection volume of 10−20 mL, depending on body weight. The G‐Ce6 solution was sterilised using a syringe filter (Millex‐HA Syringe Filter Unit 0.45 μM; Merk Millipore Ltd., Cork, Ireland) with a pore size of 0.45 μm. The entire injection volume was administered intravenously over 10 min using an appropriate syringe pump.

Vascular‐targeted PDT using G‐Ce6

Five dogs were intravenously injected with G‐Ce6 5 min before continuous wave laser irradiation. The dogs were treated under sedation or general anaesthesia. For sedation, a combination of medetomidine (0.03 mg/kg; Dorbene, Kyoritsu Seiyaku, Co., Tokyo, Japan) and midazolam (0.15 mg/kg; Dormicum Injection 10 mg; Maruishi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Osaka, Japan) was injected intramuscularly. For general anaesthesia, butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg; Vetorphale; Meiji Animal Health Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and midazolam (0.15 mg/kg) were injected as preanaesthetics. General anaesthesia was induced using intravenous propofol (4 mg/kg; Propoflo 28; Zoetis Japan Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) and maintained using isoflurane and oxygen after tracheal intubation. Robenacoxib (1 mg/kg; Onsior; Elanco Japan, Tokyo, Japan) was administered subcutaneously for analgesia.

A flat‐cut quartz fibre was used for superficial irradiation. Light was scattered from the surface into the tissues. Interstitial PDT can be used to treat locally advanced tumours of thickness >10 mm (Shafirstein et al., 2017). For interstitial irradiation, one or more 14‐gauge over‐the‐needle intravenous catheters were inserted into the lesion for treatment. A quartz fibre with a 1‐ or 2.5‐cm cylindrical light diffuser (Medlight SA, Ecublens, Switzerland) was inserted through the external cylinder of the catheter that was placed to support the fibre. The tumour was irradiated using a 677‐nm diode laser (Osada Electric Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The VTP protocol using G‐Ce6 is shown in Table 2. Light intensity and dose were determined to be able to irradiate the entire tumour while maintaining the effective blood concentration of G‐Ce6.

Robenacoxib was administered for 2 days after each treatment for analgesia. The PDT protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No.: H28‐004).

CT analysis of tumours

As previously mentioned, each tumour was imaged with the dogs under sedation or anaesthesia using a 16‐row multidetector CT scanner (ECLOS; Hitachi Medical Co., Tokyo, Japan). Angiographic CT images were acquired 1 min after intravenous injection of a nonionic contrast medium (iopamidol, 300 mg injection; Fuji Pharma Co., Ltd., Toyama, Japan). CT enhancement values before VTP using G‐Ce6 were calculated as follows: three regions of interest (ROIs) were set at the same position as the solid component of the tumours before and after administering the contrast medium, and the vascular structure and necrotic area were avoided. The difference in CT values of each ROI between the unenhanced and contrast‐enhanced CT images was defined as the CT enhancement value.

Side effects

The side effects induced by VTP were evaluated according to the Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group‐Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 2 (LeBlanc et al., 2021).

RESULTS

3.1 Efficacy of vascular‐targeted PDT

In dog 1, the mass was located in the centre of the left mandible (Figure 1a). The CT images showed that the left mandibular bone was absorbed, and the tumour showed contrast enhancement (Figure 1d). No evidence of metastasis was observed on CT. Histopathological examination of the mass revealed malignant melanoma. The tumour was interstitially irradiated using a quartz fibre with a 1‐cm cylindrical light diffuser (200 mW/cm, 100 J/cm) (Figure 1b) and superficially irradiated using a flat‐cut fibre (200 mW/cm2, 100 J/cm2). One day after the first PDT session, the CT images did not show contrast enhancement or swelling (Figure 1e). Necrotic tissues were debrided, and a second PDT session was performed 14 days after the first treatment. The CT images did not show contrast enhancement 28 days after the second PDT session (Figure 1f). A fistula formed 7 days after the second PDT session; however, it resolved within 1 week. CT imaging and enucleation were performed because of the enlargement of the right eyeball 160 days after the first PDT session. Although histopathological examination of the right eyeball revealed malignant melanoma, malignant melanoma of the mandible was not observed 171 days after the first PDT session (Figure 1c).

In dog 2, an ulcerated mass was located on the third digit of the right forelimb (Figure 2a). CT imaging was not performed. No evidence of metastasis was observed on radiography. Histopathological examination of the mass revealed haemangiopericytoma. The tumour was irradiated using a quartz fibre with a 2.5‐cm cylindrical light diffuser (160 mW/cm, 80 J/cm; Figure 2b). The centre of the irradiated tumour became necrotic 8 days after the first VTP (Figure 2c). Crust formation on the tumour was observed 7 days after the second PDT session (Figure 2d), as the tumour size gradually decreased (Figures 2E and 3). The tumour disappeared 28 days after the sixth treatment (Figure 2f). Although dog 2 died of an unrelated cause 642 days after the first PDT session, the tumour did not recur.

In dog 3, the masses were located on the right caudal side of the mandible. The CT images showed that the right mandibular bone was not absorbed, and the tumour showed mild contrast enhancement. No evidence of metastasis was observed on CT. Discospondylitis was observed from C6 to C7. Histopathological examination of the masses revealed a malignant melanoma and SCC. The tumour was irradiated using a quartz fibre with a 2.5‐cm cylindrical light diffuser (200 mW/cm, 90 J/cm). Furthermore, the dog was treated 14 and 51 days after the first PDT session. Although tumour progression was not observed, the dog's general condition worsened because of discospondylitis, and the subsequent treatment was discontinued.

In dog 4, which had a history of epilepsy, the mass was located in the caudal part of the left maxilla and mandible. The CT images showed that the left mandibular bone was absorbed, and the tumour showed contrast enhancement. No evidence of metastasis was observed on CT. Intracranial lesions were not observed. Histopathological examination of the mass revealed malignant melanoma. The tumour was superficially irradiated using flat‐cut fibres (300 mW/cm2, 100 J/cm2) at three locations. Seizures were observed during the second injection of G‐Ce6, 14 days after the first PDT session; thus, the second PDT session was postponed and performed 2 days later. Upon examination of the oral cavity before the second PDT session, dropout of necrotic tumour tissues and partial defects in the maxilla were observed. No seizures were observed after the third G‐Ce6 injection. Lung metastasis was observed 93 days after the first PDT session. Owing to the gradual infiltration of tumour cells into the surrounding normal tissues, the dog was treated 17 times. The tumour infiltrated caudally, and the dog died of distant metastasis 229 days after the first PDT session.

In dog 5, an ulcerated mass was present in the lateral aspect of the left tarsus (Figure 4a). The CT images showed no absorption of left tarsus bone, and the tumour showed contrast enhancement. No evidence of metastasis was observed on CT. Histopathological examination of the mass revealed haemangiopericytoma. The tumour was irradiated using two quartz fibres with a 2.5‐cm cylindrical light diffuser (200 mW/cm, 150 J/cm; Figure 4b). The tumour became necrotic after VTP (Figure 4c), and the necrotic tissues were debrided 6 days after the first treatment. Although temporary lameness and mild anorexia were observed after treatment, they were self‐limiting. Tumour size gradually decreased. The tumour surface was ulcerated 7 days after the fourth PDT session (Figure 4d), and the tumour size decreased 15 days after the sixth PDT session (Figure 4e). The treated area was almost completely covered with normal skin 79 days after the first treatment (Figure 4f). The dog was in remission at the last follow‐up 254 days after the first treatment.

Side effects

In all dogs, except dog 4, temporary skin or oral mucosal oedema (grade 1) was observed around the irradiated area a few days after VTP. Dogs 1, 4, and 5 showed slight lethargy for a few days after VTP. Oedema and decreased activity were self‐limiting without medication. In dog 1, a fistula developed after PDT on the tumour‐infiltrated skin (grade 2). The fistula resolved spontaneously following natural sloughing of the necrotic tissue. Mild mature neutrophilia was observed in dogs 1, 2 and 4.

DISCUSSION

VTP leads to tumour vascular shutdown via irreversible vascular collapse and blood coagulation (Nogueira et al., 2020; Olivo et al., 2010). Consequently, treated tumours become necrotic owing to the interrupted blood supply. A previous study showed that VTP using the benzoporphyrin derivative monoacid ring‐A (BPD‐MA) delayed tumour growth more effectively than conventional PDT using BPD‐MA (Osaki et al., 2007) and helped effectively treat oral and nasal tumours (Osaki et al., 2009).

The target of PDT depends on the properties of photosensitisers. Hydrophilic photosensitisers are easier to administer. However, they do not accumulate in tumour cells and are rapidly excreted from the body. A previous study showed that the half‐life of G‐Ce6 at a 2 mg/kg dose was 3.02 ± 0.58 h (Osaki et al., 2019). The half‐life of BPD‐MA in dogs with naturally occurring tumours at a 0.5 mg/kg dose was reportedly 8.14 ± 5.34 h. The risk of skin phototoxicity of G‐Ce6 may be lower than that of BPD‐MA (Osaki et al., 2006). The rapid excretion of G‐Ce6 suggests that VTP using G‐Ce6 can be used to target the tumour vasculature by shortening DLI. A previous in vivo study showed that VTP using G‐Ce6 suppressed tumour growth more effectively than conventional PDT using G‐Ce6 (Osaki et al., 2019). In the present study, the tumour size in all dogs was relatively large and required multiple irradiations. Given the short half‐life of G‐Ce6, light intensity must be increased to achieve a desirable intensity before a rapid drop in the blood concentration of G‐Ce6 can be realised, which has the advantage of a short treatment time. Although irradiance of >150 mW/cm2 could induce thermal effects in external PDT, tumour volume at >60°C does not significantly differ between 150 and 200 mW/cm in interstitial PDT (Shafirstein et al., 2018). In external PDT, tumours are irradiated while changing the irradiation position to reduce the risk of thermal effects. Notably, repeated VTP can be performed for residual tumours. In the present study, tumour size decreased with repeated treatments, yielding complete remission in three cases.

In two cases of haemangiopericytoma, the treatment was limb‐sparing, and excellent cosmetic results were achieved. Usually, digit or limb amputation is necessary to achieve complete resection of appendicular haemangiopericytoma (Wouters et al., 2022). To avoid amputation, reconstructive surgery, such as axial pattern flaps or skin grafts, is required to close the postresection defects (MacPhail & Fossum, 2019). In dog 5, multiple irradiations were performed to cover the entire tumour. Facilitating preserved the function of the affected limb. In dog 3, stable disease was achieved. In a previous study, the circulating G‐Ce6 concentration peaked immediately after intravenous administration and decreased rapidly (Osaki et al., 2019). Therefore, the efficacy of VTP may depend on the concentration of the photosensitiser in blood circulation because the photosensitiser could be in direct contact with the vascular endothelial cells in the tumour tissues. The CT enhancement values for the effective cases (dogs 1, 4 and 5) were over 50 HU; in contrast, the value for the nonresponsive case (dog 3) was <30 HU. Therefore, it was considered that applicable cases could be selected using contrast‐enhanced CT.

Owing to its increased hydrophilicity, G‐Ce6 has the following advantages: the risk of skin photosensitivity is remarkably reduced, and the period between the administration of the photosensitiser and irradiation of the tumour site is significantly reduced. However, adverse effects such as oedema and fistulae were observed in the surrounding healthy tissues. Oedema surrounding the target tissues occurs owing to circulating photosensitisers in the vasculature in nontargeted surrounding tissues (Mashayekhi et al., 2019). In a previous study, fibrin thrombus formation within the vasculature was observed in the surrounding normal tissues (Osaki et al., 2019). The oedema was self‐limiting and did not require any special treatment. In some cases, fistulae occurred a few days after VTP using G‐Ce6 because of tumour infiltration into the surrounding normal tissues. VTP‐induced necrosis of tumour tissues can result in skin defects. However, in our patients, the treated tissues healed completely, with scar formation. Necrotic tumour tissues resulting from VTP were debrided to promote wound healing. After debridement, excellent tissue healing was observed, presumably because, unlike radiotherapy, PDT did not affect cell cycle distribution (Allman et al., 2000). This observation also suggests that repeated VTP using G‐Ce6 and debridement could help eradicate the tumour. In dog 4, seizures were observed during the injection of G‐Ce6 14 days after the first PDT session. However, as the symptoms were the same as those in dogs previously diagnosed with idiopathic epilepsy, we concluded that seizures were unlikely to be associated with G‐Ce6 administration.

After the first VTP with G‐Ce6, tumour size temporally increased and then markedly decreased (Figure 3). Tumour enlargement may be related to oedema induced by the vascular shutdown and inflammation induced by tumour necrosis. In contrast, subsequent tumour shrinkage might be related to the relief of oedema, loss of necrotic tissues and reduced inflammation. Temporally, a slight decrease in activity was observed after VTP with G‐Ce6. The decrease in activity improved after a few days. Considering these healing processes, we mostly performed VTP using G‐Ce6 every 2 weeks.

The main limitation of this study was its small sample size. Further evaluation with a larger number of clinical cases to confirm the usefulness of VTP using G‐Ce6 in canine tumours is warranted to standardise treatment conditions.

CONCLUSIONS

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in which repeated VTP was performed using G‐Ce6, achieving complete remission in three of the five cases. The VTP performed using G‐Ce6 exerted antitumour effects in dogs with spontaneously occurring tumours. Overall, our results may serve as a reference for future translational research on the application of PDT in human patients.
