Comparison of coronary computed tomography angiography to invasive coronary angiography for coronary allograft vasculopathy surveillance in patients with orthotopic heart transplant
Authors: Kevin Tayon, Ramez Barsoom, Mohamed Ibrahim, Goyal Umadat, Brian Shapiro, Jose Nativi Nicolau, Pragnesh Parikh, Juan Leoni, Suhny Abbara, Michael Gharacholou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvasive coronary angiography (ICA) is considered the gold standard for diagnosing coronary allograft vasculopathy (CAV) in post orthotopic heart transplant (OHT) patients. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has been used as an alternative modality, but outcome studies comparing CCTA to ICA are limited.MethodsWe studied consecutive OHT patients who underwent CCTA (n = 38) from January 2010 to January 2024 and compared them to an age- and sex-matched cohort who underwent ICA (n = 76). The outcomes of interest were clinical events (death, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke) and testing–related adverse events or complications at 1 year. Secondary outcomes included test characteristics of CCTA in patients who also underwent ICA within 90 days (paired subgroup, n = 20).ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between CCTA and ICA groups. CAV presence was similar between groups (CCTA: 8/38 [21%], ICA: 20/76 [26%], p = 0.54). There were 4 adverse events in the ICA group (coronary spasm [n = 1], access-site bleeding [n = 2], and radial artery spasm [n = 1]) and none in the CCTA group. Clinical events occurred in 16% (6/38) of the CCTA group vs 7% (5/76) of the ICA group (OR 2.74, 95% CI, 0.77-10.13, p = 0.12). In the paired subgroup, CCTA demonstrated adequate diagnostic capabilities for the detection of CAV (accuracy 95%, sensitivity 75%, specificity 100%, positive predictive value 100%, negative predictive value 94%).ConclusionsCCTA showed strong agreement with ICA for CAV detection and severity assessment. CCTA may emerge as a safer, noninvasive alternative to ICA for CAV assessment.KEYWORDS: coronary allograft vasculopathy, orthotopic heart transplantation, cardiac computed tomography angiography, invasive coronary angiography, cardiac imaging

Background

Coronary allograft vasculopathy (CAV) is a major determinant of long-term survival after orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT) and is the most common cause of retransplantation.1 Unlike native coronary artery disease, which is typically focal and eccentric, CAV manifests as a diffuse, concentric intimal thickening initially affecting small distal vessels and causing graft dysfunction.2 Due to cardiac denervation, patients with CAV are often asymptomatic, making routine CAV surveillance essential.3, 4

Invasive coronary angiography (ICA) is the gold standard for CAV diagnosis, but needs to be coupled with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) to detect intimal thickening in early CAV.5 Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) offers a noninvasive alternative, capable of high-resolution assessment of coronary anatomy and disease. Prior studies have demonstrated strong diagnostic agreement of CCTA for CAV with ICA and IVUS.6, 7, 8 Indeed, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) 2023 guidelines for OHT recipients give CCTA a Class IIa recommendation to exclude CAV in ≥2 mm vessels.4

The major benefit of CCTA resides in its noninvasive nature, which leads to a very low rate of serious adverse events.9 Although ICA carries a very small (<0.1%) risk of serious complications (death, myocardial infarction, pericardial tamponade, stroke, and emergent bypass surgery),10 rates of nonserious (e.g., vascular access–site injury) complications related to the procedure are more frequent. Additionally, when compared to ICA, CCTA is cost-effective,11 is preferred by patients due to comfort,12 and eliminates radiation exposure to lab personnel.13 Although prospective ECG-gated protocols can use less radiation than ICA, retrospective ECG-gated protocols typically use higher radiation doses than ICA.12, 14 Outcome data comparing CCTA and ICA as CAV surveillance strategies are limited. In this study, we compared modality-related events and 1-year outcomes between patients undergoing CCTA and ICA for CAV surveillance and examined the diagnostic accuracy of CCTA in the subgroup of patients who underwent both modalities, with CCTA as the comparator and ICA as the reference standard.

Methods

Study design and participants

We retrospectively reviewed all OHT recipients undergoing CCTA (n = 40) at Mayo Clinic sites in Arizona, Florida, and Minnesota between January 2010 and May 2024. All adult (≥18 years) participants (n = 38) were included. Patients were selected for CCTA and ICA based on clinician discretion (Table 3). A comparator group of patients undergoing ICA was selected in a 2-to-1 fashion matched for age and sex (ICA group, n = 76). Among the CCTA group, 20 patients also underwent ICA within 90 days before or after CCTA, allowing assessment of CCTA accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values (CCTA subanalysis group).

Image acquisition protocol

CCTA image acquisition was performed using ≥128-slice multidetector CT scanners (Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash, GE Revolution, or Philips iCT), with retrospective ECG-gated acquisition per institutional protocol. Tube voltage (typically 100-120 kVp) and tube current (mA) were adjusted according to patient body habitus. Iodinated contrast (typically 60-80 ml) was administered via antecubital vein at 5 to 6 ml/s, followed by a saline flush. Image acquisition was performed during a single breath-hold. Before CCTA, beta-blockers were administered to achieve a heart rate of ≤60 bpm when feasible, and sublingual nitroglycerin (0.4-0.8 mg) was administered, unless contraindicated per institutional protocol. Images were reconstructed using guideline-consistent protocols (<1 mm slice thickness with standard kernels). Coronary assessment was limited to segments ≥2 mm in diameter and stented segments ≥3.0 mm in diameter, consistent with contemporary SCCT guidelines.15, 16, 17 Multiplanar and curved multiplanar reformations were used for evaluation of luminal stenosis. Image quality was assessed qualitatively at the time of clinical interpretation, and all studies were deemed diagnostically interpretable for analysis.

Reporting and interpretation

The presence of CAV was determined by luminal stenosis, assessed using a combination of quantitative measurements and visual estimation, without direct assessment of intimal thickening. Coronary allograft stenosis was graded on CCTA and ICA using the ISHLT score: CAV 0: no CAV; CAV 1: <50% left main (LM) stenosis, <70% single primary or single branch stenosis without allograft dysfunction; CAV 2: <50% LM stenosis, single primary vessel with ≥70% stenosis, branch of 2 systems with ≥70% stenosis without allograft dysfunction; CAV 3: ≥50% LM stenosis, 2 or more primary vessels with ≥70% stenosis, isolated branch stenosis in all 3 systems or ISHLT CAV 1 or 2 with allograft dysfunction (ejection fraction <45% with or without regional wall motion abnormalities or restrictive physiology).18, 19 Per-vessel analyses were performed across the 3 major epicardial coronary allografts (left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries).

ICA protocol

ICA was performed by the standard technique via radial or femoral arterial access. Decisions regarding IVUS use were made prospectively and not performed systematically. IVUS was performed in 38 of 76 (50%) of the total ICA cohort and 8 of 20 of the CCTA subanalysis group.

Outcomes

Events of interest were modality–related adverse events and clinical events within 1 year of imaging, identified through chart review. Adverse events were defined as complications occurring during or immediately after the procedure and directly attributable to the imaging modality. Clinical events included all-cause death, cardiovascular death, heart failure–related hospitalization, myocardial infarction, and stroke. Diagnostic test performance of CCTA was determined using ICA as the reference standard in patients who underwent both modalities. Test accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Effective dose (mSv) was estimated for CCTA by converting the measured dose-length product using a chest–specific conversion factor of 0.014 mSv/mGy·cm and for ICA by converting the measured dose-area product using a conversion factor of 0.0022 mSv/Gy·cm².20, 21

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean values with standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between imaging modality and events at 1 year. Models were performed unadjusted and then adjusted individually for age at screening, sex, and time from OHT to imaging to account for potential confounding. Diagnostic accuracy statistics were calculated with Wilson confidence intervals. Analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Mean age at imaging was similar in the CCTA and ICA cohorts (CCTA: 48 ± 15 years, ICA: 49 ± 15 years, p = 0.68) (Table 1). Male sex comprised 66% of the CCTA group and 64% of the ICA group. Rates of obesity, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and smoking were similar between groups (p > 0.05). Patients in the CCTA cohort were imaged later post-OHT (CCTA: 5.17 ± 6.53 years vs ICA: 1.00 ± 0.11 years, p = 0.0002). Presence of prior percutaneous coronary intervention was higher in CCTA than ICA (CCTA: 3/38 vs ICA 0/76, p = 0.01).

Modality–related adverse events

Adverse events were observed exclusively in the ICA group (Table 2). Four patients (5%) experienced complications, including coronary artery spasm (n = 1), vascular access–site bleeding (n = 2), and radial artery spasm (n = 1). No patients undergoing CCTA experienced imaging modality–related complications (i.e., contrast-induced nephropathy, hemodynamic instability, hypersensitivity reactions, or arrhythmia). No serious complications such as death, myocardial infarction, stroke, pericardial tamponade, or emergent coronary bypass surgery occurred in either group. CCTA led to increased doses of effective radiation compared to ICA (CCTA: 13.9 ± 8.0 mSv, ICA: 4.9 ± 4.0 mSv, p < 0.001).

Clinical outcomes

Within 1 year of the index imaging, the composite clinical end-point of all-cause death, cardiovascular death, heart failure–related hospitalization, myocardial infarction, or cerebrovascular accident occurred in 6 patients (16%) in the CCTA group and 5 patients (7%) in the ICA group. This corresponded to an unadjusted odds ratio of 2.74 (95% CI, 0.77-10.13, p = 0.12). When adjusted for potential confounders, the findings were consistent with the unadjusted analysis. After adjustment for age at screening, the odds ratio for the 1-year composite end-point was 2.71 (95% CI, 0.76-10.09, p = 0.12). Adjustment for sex yielded an odds ratio of 2.73 (95% CI, 0.77-10.12, p = 0.12). Adjustment for years from OHT to screening yielded a slightly higher odds ratio of 3.02 (95% CI, 0.75-12.15, p = 0.11). Concordance indices ranged from 0.62 to 0.66, indicating limited discriminatory power.

In the CCTA cohort, four deaths occurred during follow-up, including malignancy (n = 2), pneumonia (n = 1), and lung transplant rejection (n = 1). Two CCTA patients were hospitalized for heart failure due to transplant rejection (n=1) and severe tricuspid regurgitation (n=1).

In the ICA cohort, 3 deaths occurred within 1 year, attributable to pneumonia (n = 1), renal failure (n = 1), and respiratory failure (n = 1). Two patients were hospitalized for heart failure due to transplant rejection (n = 1) and renal failure (n = 1). No cardiovascular deaths, myocardial infarctions, or cerebrovascular accidents occurred in either group.

Diagnostic performance of CCTA

All CCTA studies (38/38) were of diagnostic image quality with 112 of 114 (98%) interpretable segments and 2 of 114 (2%) of segments excluded from analysis due to <3 mm cardiac stents. Heart rate at image acquisition was 82 ± 12 beats per minute. Thirty-one of 38 (82%) patients received nitroglycerin and 35 of 38 (92%) of patients received beta-blockers. The presence of CAV by CCTA (8/38, 21%) and ICA with or without IVUS (20/76, 26%) was not significantly different (p = 0.54). Mean ISHLT CAV scores were similar between modalities (CCTA: 0.37 ± 0.79, ICA: 0.30 ± 0.46, p = 0.58). Among the 20 patients who underwent both CCTA and ICA within a 90-day window, CAV detection was similar (CCTA: 3/20 [15%], ICA: 4/20 [20%], p = 0.68) with comparable severity (CCTA: 0.35 ± 0.81, ICA: 0.45 ± 0.99, p = 0.73). There was agreement on CAV presence and severity in 19 of 20 (95%) participants and 55 of 58 (95%) of analyzed segments. As summarized in Table 4, for detection of CAV, CCTA had a sensitivity of 75%, specificity of 100%, PPV of 100%, NPV of 94%, and accuracy of 95% compared to ICA. There was a single participant in whom CCTA and ICA findings were discordant, in which CCTA did not identify CAV detected by ICA, with IVUS performed after CCTA.

Discussion

In this multicenter, retrospective study of OHT recipients, we compared outcomes among patients undergoing CCTA vs ICA for CAV detection. The principal findings were (1) adverse events were observed only in the ICA cohort, highlighting the safety advantage of a noninvasive approach; and (2) there were no statistically significant differences in 1-year outcomes between groups, even after adjustment for age, sex, and years after transplantation. In the subgroup that underwent both modalities within 90 days, CCTA showed strong diagnostic concordance with ICA with an accuracy of 95%. These results corroborate prior studies6, 7, 8 and extend them by adding safety and outcomes data. Collectively, these findings support CCTA as a potentially safe and reliable alternative to ICA for CAV surveillance in selected patients. However, given the limited number of events and low CAV burden, outcomes should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Adverse events

Adverse events were observed in 5% of ICA patients and in no CCTA patients. Although observed complications were minor, such events highlight the inherent risks of invasive procedures, which may be amplified in OHT recipients given challenging vascular access and distorted anatomy.

In comparison, no CCTA-related complications were observed in this study, aligning with large registry data showing that CCTA complications, such as arrhythmias, are rare and self-limited.9 This reinforces the procedural safety of a noninvasive surveillance strategy, especially for patients undergoing repeated lifetime surveillance studies. However, CCTA led to an increased effective dose of radiation compared to ICA, due to the usage of retrospective ECG-gated protocols.14 While prospective ECG-gated protocols effectively reduce radiation to levels modestly below ICA, assessment of myocardial function, a recommended component of CAV assessment, cannot be performed.6 Despite increased radiation to patients, CCTA completely avoids occupational radiation exposure for staff, a system-level benefit.13

Clinical outcomes

At 1 year, the composite end-point occurred in 16% of CCTA patients and 7% of ICA patients. Although unadjusted and adjusted models suggested a numerically higher event rate with CCTA, the differences were not statistically significant, and confidence intervals were wide, reflecting the small number of events. Several factors should be considered when interpreting this finding.

First, all deaths in the CCTA group were not attributable to CAV (i.e., malignancy, pneumonia, and lung transplant rejection). This pattern is consistent with the broader literature on late post-transplant mortality, where infection and malignancy dominate, while CAV remains the leading cause of graft loss.4

Second, CCTA patients were imaged significantly later after transplant (mean 5.2 years) compared with those undergoing ICA (mean 1.0 year). The later timing of CCTA inherently places patients at higher baseline risk for clinical events, as supported by registry data showing that morbidity and mortality rise with increasing time from transplantation.1, 4 Thus, the numerical difference in outcomes may reflect underlying risk differences rather than harm attributable to CAV surveillance modality.

Third, adjustment for age, sex, and years from OHT to screening did not materially alter the findings, suggesting no causal effect. Instead, they are consistent with the limitations of small sample size, selection bias, and potential confounding from medical comorbidities.

Diagnostic performance characteristics

In the subgroup with paired imaging, CCTA detected CAV at a similar rate and severity as in ICA, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy (95%), specificity (100%), NPV (94%), and PPV (100%). These findings align with prior studies supporting the reliability of CCTA for CAV detection.6, 7, 8 Sensitivity in our cohort was lower (75%) than previously reported, potentially reflecting the limited number of positive cases. Given the progressive nature of CAV, a higher prevalence would be expected among patients imaged later post-transplant. Therefore, the similar disease burden observed across CCTA and ICA groups may reflect selection bias or limited sensitivity for early, distal CAV. However, CAV classification was discordant in only 1 patient (40-year-old female, 16 months post-OHT), in whom CCTA preceded ICA with IVUS by 84 days. While this finding could represent interval CAV progression as a subset of transplant recipients develop accelerated CAV progression,22 a false-negative CCTA cannot be excluded.

The high accuracy and NPV of CCTA for CAV demonstrate it can reliably exclude significant disease, making it valuable for longitudinal surveillance, especially in patients with known CAV or challenging vascular access (Figure 1). Additionally, CT allows retrospective assessment of myocardial function, plaque characterization, and CT-derived fractional flow reserve without additional radiation or contrast, whereas these evaluations require additional invasive procedures in the catheterization laboratory, which may further enhance the clinical utility of CCTA in CAV assessment.

There are several technical limitations for CCTA in CAV assessment. Limitations attributable to spatial resolution and partial volume averaging (“blooming”) artifacts preclude quantitative stenosis assessment in small (<2 mm) vessels and small (<3 mm) coronary stents.17 This is especially challenging to the assessment of early CAV as it frequently affects small distal vessels. Although CCTA techniques to assess intimal thickening increase sensitivity for CAV detection and may aid in the assessment of early CAV, it is not routinely performed and suffers from the same limitations in distal vessel assessment as angiographic stenosis assessments.17, 23 The diagnostic quality of CCTA decreases as heart rate increases.16 In post-OHT patients, however, rate-controlling therapy can significantly reduce heart rate from a baseline of 96.5 ± 7 to 84.4 ± 8.8 bpm with beta-blockers and 76.2 ± 8.9 bpm with If channel antagonists.24 While above the recommended heart rate goal of <60 bpm, previous studies with similar heart rates have reported excellent rates of diagnostic quality images (e.g., 92% excellent or good with heart rate 83 ± 14 bpm in6).

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations merit consideration. The retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Although ICA patients were matched 2:1 with CCTA patients on age and sex, other factors likely influenced modality choice. The later time from transplant to imaging in the CCTA group highlights this possibility, as clinicians may have preferentially selected CCTA for patients further out from OHT.

The small sample size and low number of outcome events limited statistical power, resulting in wide confidence intervals around odds ratios. Larger cohorts with longer-term follow-up are needed to determine whether CCTA and ICA yield equivalent clinical outcomes. The inconsistent use of IVUS in the ICA group is another limitation. As IVUS is the most sensitive tool for detecting early intimal thickening, its absence in half of ICA patients in our cohort may have underestimated the prevalence of early CAV. Additionally, because donor data were not consistently available in our study, we cannot fully distinguish CAV from donor–derived coronary atherosclerosis, and some degree of misclassification may be present.

Finally, our results reflect the experience of high–volume transplant centers with dedicated cardiac CT programs, which may limit generalizability. Implementation at lower-volume centers or in settings without dedicated CT expertise may produce different outcomes.

Future studies should address these limitations directly. Prospective, multicenter investigations with systematic IVUS use and longer-term follow-up are needed to establish whether surveillance modality influences clinical outcomes. Studies should also incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses, patient-reported outcomes, and advanced CCTA techniques (e.g., intimal thickening assessments, fractional flow reserve, plaque quantification, and plaque characterization) to provide a comprehensive evaluation of surveillance strategies.

Conclusions

CCTA provides high diagnostic accuracy for CAV detection and eliminates procedural risks associated with ICA. The strong diagnostic performance and favorable safety profile of CCTA support its integration as a noninvasive alternative for surveillance in select patients. Larger prospective studies are warranted to define the optimal role of CCTA in CAV surveillance strategies.
