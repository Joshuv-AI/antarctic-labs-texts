Biohybrid corneal stromal tissue formation using keratocytes encapsulated in supramolecular microgels
Authors: Maritza M Rovers, Annika F Vrehen, Patricia YW Dankers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompared to bulk hydrogels, microgels offer distinct advantages for biomedical applications. Their increased modularity and heterogeneity compared to hydrogels, combined with their small size and reversible dynamic bonding, enhance their suitability for minimally invasive cell delivery. Additionally, microgels offer greater control over porosity, resulting in the formation of intricate porous microstructures. In this work, keratocytes encapsulated in ureidopyrimidinone (UPy) supramolecular microgels functionalized with UPy-cRGD were fabricated to generate a micro stromal tissue in vitro. Both the human corneal keratocyte cell line (HCK) and primary keratocytes (PK) demonstrated effective cell-cell and cell-matrix mediated microgel assembly, resulting in the formation of self-generated scaffolds. The void spaces between the assembled microgels facilitate migration and infiltration of the cells through the biohybrid stromal tissue construct. The retention time of the cells within the microgels can be controlled by altering the microgel composition. Immunohistochemical analyses of PKs assembled tissues demonstrated the formation of stromal micro tissues, cellular extracellular matrix deposition and substantial upregulation of nuclear yes-associated-protein (YAP) during culture. This work highlights a novel supramolecular approach with promising potential for minimally invasive therapies aimed at treating corneal defects in clinical settings.Keywords: Microgels, Supramolecular chemistry, Droplet-based microfluidics, Stromal construct, Injectability, Granular hydrogel

Introduction

Tissue engineering integrates mechanical, chemical, and biological components to engineer materials capable of mimicking the natural microenvironment of cells, known as the extracellular matrix (ECM), which offers structural support, and biochemical, biophysical, and mechanical cues to cells. Hydrogels have been extensively researched as substrates mimicking the ECM in regenerative medicine, for instance as injectable carriers loaded with cells to deliver them precisely to the desired location for local tissue regeneration [1]. However, due to the typical macroscopic size of these hydrogels, the encapsulated cells often suffer from poor nutrient, oxygen and waste exchange, and limited cell migration due to nanoscale pores [2,3]. To overcome these challenges, bulk hydrogels can be miniaturized into microgels. Their small size allows for the non-invasive injection of microgels in vivo, which can be used as 1) delivery vehicles for single cell-laden microgels, and as 2) scaffolds that provide structural support for tissue healing and regeneration [4,5]. Therefore, microgels represent interesting materials for advanced clinical applications [6].

The advantage of cell-laden microgels is that they prevent cell damage during delivery and treatment, offering a supportive growth environment while shielding the cells from the body's immune response [7]. The Mooney lab for instance encapsulated single cells in thin ∼6 μm layered alginate microgels [8]. These cells encapsulated in microgels prolonged the in vivo residence time of cells compared to injected cells with empty microgels. Similarly, single mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were encapsulated within poly(ethylene glycol) norbornene (PEGNB) microgels that crosslink via photo-photopolymerization to promote an anti-inflammatory response [9]. After injection into a mouse model with induced inflammation, the singly encapsulated MSCs demonstrate a high retention rate in vivo.

Furthermore, single cells encapsulated in microgels can serve as a platform for the assembly into small-scale tissues [4]. This is driven by the creation of a microporous scaffold with interconnected pores, which enhances cell migration and infiltration, independent of hydrogel degradation. The Anseth lab used microgel building blocks based on PEG with clickable surface groups based on azide or alkyne moieties for the bottom-up fabrication of porous cell-laden scaffolds [10]. Furthermore, the Burdick lab formulated cell laden hyaluronic acid-based microgels with programmable degradation to fabricate programmable and dynamic granular composites that control compaction and tissue formation over time [11].

The approach of cell encapsulation in microgels is particularly advantageous for regenerative therapies requiring minimal invasiveness [12]. For example, in the field of regenerative medicine in ophthalmology as means for treating eye disease, like corneal blindness [13].

The cornea is the outermost part of the eye and has two main functions: protection of the inner parts of the eye and refraction of light towards the retina [14]. When the cornea is damaged or diseased, its ability to focus light on the retina and minimize light scattering is compromised, leading to visual impairment. Corneal blindness is one of the most common causes of vision loss worldwide, affecting millions of people [15]. While hydrogels are being studied to engineer living constructs that can support the self-regeneration of native tissue, microgels have not yet been extensively explored as building blocks for stromal constructs to regenerate the cornea [16]. However, they have already shown potential as drug delivery vehicles [17,18].

Here, we propose using cells encapsulated in microgels as a minimally invasive treatment of corneal defects. This approach would enable the localized and safe delivery of cells via injection, allowing them to egress from the microgels and actively form corneal tissue in situ, with the microgels providing supportive building blocks (Fig. 1A). Our goal is to create a biohybrid stromal tissue using keratocytes encapsulated in supramolecular microgels. We focus on encapsulating cells that can egress and form a tissue via cell-cell and cell-microgel interactions. This approach could be advantageous for a minimally invasive in vivo treatment to support the regeneration of corneal defects. For this, we would employ a supramolecular approach based on ureidopyrimidinone (UPy) moieties, which has recently demonstrated the development of an injectable supramolecular synthetic bulk hydrogel that provided a stable and biocompatible microenvironment for encapsulated primary stromal keratocytes [19,20]. Furthermore, using a droplet-based microfluidics setup, microgels can be formulated with similar UPy-monomers, which has proven to have bioactive cell adhesive properties [21]. Compared to other microgel systems, the modularity of the UPy-system provides precise control over microgel properties and their resulting functionality [21,22]. This modular approach enables the formulation of stable microgels that can be strategically used in a bottom-up tissue engineering approach, facilitating the assembly of complex tissues from these building blocks. Previous studies have demonstrated the use of UPy-functionalized microgels as a bioactive culture platform for normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs), where cells assembled the microgels into a granular scaffold through cell–cell and cell–material interactions [21]. In that approach, cells were seeded on top of the microgels, which may limit retention during delivery applications due to potential cell detachment. To address this, we aim to encapsulate keratocytes directly within the microgels, enabling improved cell integration and stability for potential minimally invasive delivery to the cornea.

First, cells encapsulated in microgels using B- and M-type UPy-monomers functionalized with a UPy-cRGD additive were formulated by trapping two variations of keratocytes within supramolecular microgels: the I. robust SV40-immortalised human corneal keratocyte cell line (HCK) and II. human donor derived primary keratocytes (PK) cultured towards a) stromal fibroblasts (SFs) and b) corneal stromal keratocytes (CSKs) (Fig. 1B). Next, the mechanism of cell egress from HCK encapsulated in microgels was investigated by varying UPy-cRGD concentrations and microgel stiffness to understand how bioactivity and mechanical properties influence tissue formation. After encapsulation, the cells were monitored during culture to observe microgel assembly driven by cell-cell interactions. Subsequently, PKs were employed as a more representative cell line to study stromal tissue constructs in greater detail and were extensively analyzed using various immunohistochemical techniques.

Results and discussion

Formation of supramolecular microgels and cell encapsulation

The core of the microgels comprised of a monovalent UPy-Glycine M-type hydrogelator and a bivalent B-type UPy2-PEG10k crosslinker, both containing hydrogen bonding UPy-groups as supramolecular moieties (Fig. 1C). The integrin-binding UPy-cRGD ligand was introduced into the microgel network to allow cell adhesion, cellular infiltration and cell migration after microgel encapsulation. The B- and M-type were dissolved separately in aqueous solution, and both solutions were mixed with culture medium to provide the cells with some nutrients during gelation. To allow cell encapsulation, cells were supplemented to the dissolved B-type molecules. This choice is because the B-type monomer is dissolved in PBS and diluted in medium, which is compatible with cell viability. This method aligns with established protocols for creating bulk hydrogels from the same M- and B-type monomers, where cells are consistently co-dissolved in the B-type solution before mixing with the M-type solution to trigger gelation [19,20,23].

Both solutions were drawn separately into a pipette-tip based droplet microfluidic setup. Within this setup, the B-type solution was directed to flow from the middle inlet, while the M-type solution was loaded in the inner inlet (Fig. S1). At the point of intersection, both hydrogelators mix in a 1:1 volume ratio where the faster oil phase shears microdroplets from the aqueous polymer stream. In this droplet cells are encapsulated and the hydrogelators crosslink into a transient networks via supramolecular interactions. This process produced a mix of empty microgels, as well as microgels containing single or multiple cells. The distribution of cells per microgel follows a Poisson model, which depends on the cell concentration and droplet volume (Table 1 and Fig. S2) [24]. Encapsulation of HCK cells closely followed the expected Poisson distribution. In contrast, PK cells showed a similar pattern but with fewer single-cell microgels than predicted. The differences in encapsulation efficiency across microgel conditions and cell types are most likely due to specific properties of both the microgels and the cells. For example, an increase in w/v% can increase viscosity, which may hinder uniform droplet formation or cell distribution. Furthermore, it is known in general that different cell types follow different Poisson distributions, which can be due to the presensence of sticky cell surface proteins, cell shape and size [24].

Encapsulation of HCK cells in various microgel compositions

Microgel composition dictates the egress speed of cells and tissue compaction

HCK cells were encapsulated in 3 microgel compositions: soft 1.25 w/v% (+ 1.5 mM or 3 mM UPy-cRGD) and stiff 2.5 w/v% (+ 3 mM UPy-cRGD microgels) (Fig. 2A–B). The 2.5 w/v% + 3 mM UPy-cRGD condition serves as a well-characterized baseline, previously studied in bulk hydrogels for both mechanical properties and cell behavior [19]. To examine the effect of stiffness, we included the softer 1.25 w/v% microgels with 3 mM UPy-cRGD. The previous study by Rovers et al. has shown a significant difference in mechanical properties between 1.25 w/v% and 2.5 w/v% microgels [21]. Additionally, based on findings from Rijns et al. that effective ligand concentration impacts cell behavior, we incorporated the 1.25 w/v% microgels with 1.5 mM UPy-cRGD, matching the effective ligand concentration of the 2.5 w/v% with 3 mM UPy-cRGD condition [25].

Once the microgels had gelated properly, brightfield images confirmed the successful encapsulation of cells within the microgel (Fig. 2C). The diameter of the microgels was slightly affected by the weight percentage, with the stiffer 2.5 w/v% microgels having a larger diameter compared to the softer 1.25 w/v% microgels (Fig. 2D). The different conditions were cultured over a period of 9 days, and brightfield images were taken of the cells to monitor their growth and development (Fig. 2E). On day 2, 3D stacked confocal images confirmed the cells being encapsulated within the microgels (Video 1; white arrowhead). A part of the cell population began to egress from their microgels leaving an empty cavity in the microgel (Fig. 2A; white dotted circle, and Video 1; yellow arrowhead), curling around the microgel surfaces and linking neighboring microgels through cell-cell and cell-material interactions. Cells spread largely on the soft microgels, but remain round and small on the stiffer microgels at day 2 (Fig. S3).

This cell egress is likely driven by a combination of the reversible nature of the supramolecular network in combination with supramolecular erosion and possibly mild degradation processes. Our previous work showed that microgels remain structurally stable over time under physiological conditions (37 °C and pH 7.4) as their diameter did not change during the study period [21]. However, from a previous study on bulk hydrogels it is known that the reversible, non-covalent interactions within the hydrogel network make the hydrogel inherently adaptable. This allows for matrix remodeling and cellular activities, such as spreading and migration, without requiring bluk degradation or enlarged pore sizes, which are often necesarry in conventional covalent hydrogel systems [23]. Nevertheless, Diba et al. did observe slight erosion in the bulk hydrogels (∼10 % mass loss for 1.25 and 2.5 w/v% hydrogel formulations) by day 7. This erosion likely results from the erosion of the B-type crosslinker. We expect that a similar degradation process occurs at the microgel level, which could facilitate cell egress and migration from the microgels.

By day 4, the 1.25 w/v% + 1.5 mM microgels had started to compact into a tissue, a phenomenon also observed, though to a lesser extent, in the 1.25 w/v% + 3.0 mM condition. However, this effect was not seen in the stiffer microgels. This effect is not driven by softer microgels degrading faster than stiffer microgels, as both conditions show the presence of the microgels in the granular scaffolds formed by the cells (Fig. S3). By day 7, the 1.25 w/v% + 1.5 and 3.0 mM microgels had fully compacted into dense tissues. The 2.5 w/v% conditions also began to compact, with this process becoming more evident by day 9.

This phenomenon indicates that the cellular egress and tissue formation can be tuned based on the microgel composition. In this case, cells compacted their tissue more rapidly being egressed from softer microgels compared to stiffer ones. Similar observations were noted for alginate microgels, where the fraction of egressed cells was significantly higher in 54 kDa and 48 kDa alginate microgels compared to those made from 139 kDa to 232 kDa alginate [8]. This may be due to the fact that cells in higher w/v% microgels are less able to quickly rearrange their local environment. Another explanation could be the off-center position of the cell in the microgel, which causes cells to escape from their niche [26]. However, in our three conditions, all cells were randomly encapsulated in the microgel. Regarding bioactivity, the amount of bioactive UPy-cRGD ligand also affected the compaction speed. We compared two scenarios: 1) microgels with similar stiffness (1.25 w/v%) but different UPy-cRGD concentrations (1.5 vs. 3 mM) and 2) microgels with equal effective ligand concentrations (1.25 w/v% + 1.5 mM vs. 2.5 w/v% + 3 mM). In both cases, cells in the microgels with 1.5 mM UPy-cRGD formed and compacted their tissue more quickly than those with 3.0 mM. We hypothesize that this effect is due to the need for cells to adhere to RGD peptides to bind to the material. When RGD is present in abundance, cells can adhere without extending long protrusions. However, if RGD is present at lower concentrations, cells must extend further to bind to the ligand, which may stimulate faster migration. This observation is consistent with the faster compaction seen in the 1.5 mM UPy-cRGD microgels compared to the 3 mM microgels. Similar findings were observed with RGD-functionalized aligned microgels in fibrin hydrogels, where higher RGD concentrations did not necessarily yield better outcomes. Dorsal root ganglions (DRGs) cultured in these hydrogels showed no significant differences in neurite growth between GRGDS-coupled and bioinert microgels [27].

Additional immunohistochemical staining revealed a consistent effect of tissue compaction over 4, 7, and 9 days of culture (Fig. 3A). A significant increase in cell number, as indicated by the nuclei, was observed compared to the initial cell count immediately after gelation, suggesting high tissue proliferation for all conditions (Fig. 2C). Zoomed-in images showed microgels in the 2.5 w/v% condition appearing as light cyan in the background, whereas these microgels were no longer visible in the lower 1.25 w/v% conditions. In the 2.5 w/v% condition, the round shape of the microgels was evident, with the cytoskeleton (red) covering the surface, while in the 1.25 w/v% conditions, the shapes were irregular and appeared more squeezed or compressed, likely due to the high cell density compacting the tissue scaffold.

Next, tissue compaction was quantified by measuring the tissue area, which decreased over the culture period in all cases (Fig. 3B). This indicates that the cells are effectively compacting the tissue. Consistent with the microscopy images, the 1.25 w/v% + 1.5 mM condition demonstrated the fastest compaction of its scaffold, as evidenced by the smallest tissue area at day 4. The circularity of the tissue was also determined, where 0.0 represents a flat line and 1.0 a perfect circle (Fig. 3C). In the case of the 1.25 w/v% microgels, the tissue became more circular after compaction, while the 2.5 w/v% microgels retained a more irregular shape, as the tissue had not fully compacted by day 9.

Mechanotransduction and ECM-protein secretion

Mechanotransduction is a process initiated by cell-material interactions that enables cells to sense and respond to external forces by remodeling the cytoskeleton or activating specific gene pathways [28]. Mechanical signals are initially sensed by cell surface receptors like integrins or cadherins, which then trigger and regulate multiple signaling pathways. One of these pathways is the Hippo signaling pathway, which regulates tissue growth among other biological processes. The mechanosensitive transcriptional regulator yes-associated protein (YAP) belongs to the Hippo pathway and is involved in mechanotransduction pathways [[29], [30], [31]]. It is regulated by mechanical cues such as cell-cell contact, ECM rigidity, flow, cell shape and cell tension.

To investigate cell-material interactions within the microgel-formed tissue, the cellular location of YAP was quantified for the 1.25 w/v% + 1.5 mM and 2.5 w/v% + 3 mM microgels at day 2, 4, and 7. It is known that YAP translocates to the nucleus on stiff substrates while remaining mostly in the cytoplasm on soft substrates. A custom-made CellProfiler pipeline was used to quantify the intensity of YAP expression inside the cellular nuclei and cytoplasm. The ratio of YAP expression between the nuclei and cytoplasm was above 1 for all conditions and at all time points, suggesting that cells sensed mechanotransduction on both the soft and stiff microgels (Fig. 4A–B), while usually stiff substrate promote YAP nuclear localization. However, for the UPy-hydrogels it is known that the mechanical properties of soft and stiff hydrogels fall within a relatively narrow range (approximately 1000–2000 Pa) [25]. These moderate differences in stiffness may not be sufficient to induce differential YAP signaling.

Encapsulation of primary keratocytes within supramolecular microgels

Previous experiments involving the encapsulation of HCK cells demonstrated that microgel composition influences the egress speed of HCK cells, as well as tissue formation and compaction. Since HCK cells represent a simplified cell model, we have now progressed to using the more representative PK cells, which are isolated from human donor corneal stroma and expanded in vitro. PKs offer a more accurate representation and can be treated to mimic either metabolically active or quiescent native keratocytes, known as a) stromal fibroblasts (SFs) and b) corneal stromal keratocytes (CSKs), respectively. For this purpose, we chose to continue with a single microgel condition: 2.5 w/v% + 3.0 mM UPy-cRGD, which has already been proven to successfully support PK cells in bulk hydrogels in vitro [19,20]. Single-cell encapsulation within supramolecular microgels was achieved, but microgels with multiple encapsulated cells were also created to a lesser extent (Fig. 5A and Table 1).

An increase in the number of cells per well was observed on the third culture day, the PKs started to migrate from their microgel and initiated microgel assembly by pulling the microgels together via both cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions (Video 2–6). From PK encapsulation within microgels towards tissue formation

At day 11 of culture, the tissue formed by the cell-microgels interactions were fixated and various immunohistochemical analyses were performed. The PKs treated towards SFs formed dense tissues of approximately ∼1–2 mm in diameter (Fig. 7A). The results demonstrate intracellular, granular deposition of the ECM protein collagen type VI by the SFs within the tissue, as well as the intracellular expression of neural differentiation marker tubulin β3 (Fig. 5D and Fig. S6). Corneal keratocytes possess the ability to heal the cornea throughout life. This property could be related to the origin of the keratocytes, being neural-crest derived mesenchymal cells [[33], [34], [35]]. Associated to this specific cell characteristic, some groups studied the differentiation behavior of corneal keratocytes and concluded that corneal keratocytes are not terminally differentiated but maintain a certain multipotency, which can contribute to the differentiation into neural crest derivatives [[35], [36], [37]]. The presence of tubulin β3 positive cells within the formed tissue could indicate that some of the PKs activated towards SFs still exhibit a multipotent differentiation potential. While the multipotent differentiation capacity of PKs differentiated towards CSKs is diminished, resulting in a less pronounced expression of tubulin β3 (Fig. 6B).

Additional immunohistochemical staining showed that the PKs activated towards SFs expressed cytoplasmic alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) with no specific architecture and no colocalization with the actin fibers (Fig. 7A and Fig. S7A). The upregulated expression of αSMA was expected, and is supported by several studies where the keratocyte-fibroblast transition has been related to an upregulation of αSMA due to the presence of serum products within the culture media [32,[38], [39], [40]].

Furthermore, a major challenge in generating engineered tissues is gaining control over cell proliferation, more specifically, having cell division when more cells are needed or arresting them to avoid overgrowth [31]. Proliferation marker Ki-67 is widely used and is highly expressed by cells during mitosis [41]. Small dots of Ki-67 are present in the nuclei as the cells exit the mitosis and enter the early G1 phase, followed by merged dots when the cells go through the mid G1 and late G1 phase [42]. Ki-67 is stable for ∼ 36 h, whereafter it will be degraded [43]. The obtained results showed the presence of Ki-67 positive nuclei on the edges and within the core of the formed tissue, suggesting the cells are still proliferating even within the core of the biohybrid tissue (Fig. 7C and Fig. S7B–C). Despite the already dense network of clustered cells and microgels, the presence of Ki-67 cells suggest that the cell number is still increasing within the core of the cluster.

Upregulation of YAP translocation in PKs encapsulated within microgels

Recent reports have pointed YAP as a master regulator of cell-ECM interactions and nuclear translocation of YAP can be the result of various biological processes, including cell proliferation and tissue homeostasis [30]. Over-proliferation and excessive ECM deposition by cells can stiffen the matrix surrounding them and eventually trigger a fibrotic response, relating YAP activation to fibroblast activation and cell proliferation [31]. If the cells reach a high cell confluency this will cause a reduction of material adhesive sites and hence an altered cell shape. In contrast, soft matrices reduce stress fiber formation and thus inactivates YAP [29,31]. The analysis of YAP expression within the formed tissue is believed to aid in a better understanding of the ongoing mechanobiological interactions within the tissue over time [44].

Here, the translocation of YAP was quantified and studied at day 5, 7, 14 and 21 during cell culture as an indicator of cellular mechanotransduction in response to the microgel culture platform (Fig. 8A). The confocal images of YAP expression showed a difference in cytoplasmic and nuclear YAP over time. A custom-made cell profiler pipeline was used to quantify the intensity of YAP expression inside the cytoplasm and inside the nuclei. Furthermore, The nuclei eccentricity values were quantified and were within the same range of ∼0.7–0.8 for all the timepoints, suggesting that the nuclei are oval-shaped instead of round-shaped (Fig. 8B). The ratio between the nuclear YAP intensity and YAP intensity within the cytoplasm of the PKs are slightly above ∼1 at day 5 (Fig. 8C). During culture, YAP activation is upregulated and the nuclear YAP translocation increases significantly from just above ∼1 at day 5 to just above ∼6 at day 21. At the end of culture, at day 21, all tissue showed expression of YAP solely within the nuclei of the cells, indicating a distinct nuclear translocation of YAP (Fig. 8D). Interestingly, previous research by Vrehen et al., using the same microgel components in bulk hydrogels, showed a YAP ratio of approximately ∼1.4 at day 17 or of ∼2.5 when hydrogels with cells were injected at day 7 [19]. Although the injected cells exhibited a higher nuclear YAP translocation, most likely attributed to shear stress during injection, both values were significantly lower than those observed in microgel cultures. This enhanced YAP translocation in the microgels is probably caused by an interplay of various microgel physical properties, e.g. pore size by void spaced. Like, the rapid proliferation of cells, potentially driven by larger pore sizes and improved nutrient diffusion in the microgel network, along with collective cell-generated forces exerted on the surrounding microgels and neighboring cells, contributing to the observed YAP response.

Here, the results indicate that the interaction between the cells and the microgels act as the key factor in activating nuclear YAP, specifically the spreading and bridging of cells between the microgels. Live imaging of PK cultures reveals that cells can sometimes spread across up to five microgels, covering distances exceeding 300 μm (Video 3). It is possible that this caused the increased YAP activation, as Caliari et al. have already showed that YAP nuclear translocation was greater in spreading cells [45]. Furthermore, immunohistochemical stainings of the tissues have shown a fibrotic response associated with ECM collagen VI deposition, excessive cell proliferation and tissue contraction by cells. All these observations may have contributed to activation of the YAP/TAZ mechanosensing complex.

Microgel injectability

To explore the potential of microgels for biomedical applications, we focused on their clinical use. As proof of concept, we studied the injectability of cell-free microgels using a 0.225 mm diameter needle. Microgels were fluorescently labeled as described previously with a UPy-Cy3 dye for visualization with confocal microscopy after injection through a pipette tip and needle (Fig. 9A) [21]. In both cases, the microgels retained their round shape, as confirmed by unchanged eccentricity (Fig. 9B). Additionally, their perimeter remained constant, indicating structural integrity during injection (Fig. 9C). This proof of concept confirms the feasibility of injecting microgels as a minimally invasive therapy. While this study used cell-free microgels, earlier experiments demonstrated that cells embedded in bulk hydrogels made of the same monomers tolerate injection-induced shear stress. Therefore, we anticipate that including cells during injection will not pose an issue [19].

Conclusion

Corneal cells, being keratocytes, were encapsulated in supramolecular microgels through combining droplet-based microfluidics with a supramolecular approach. Unlike cells encapsulated in bulk hydrogels, microgels enabled the formation of a dense, interconnected cell network within a microporous construct of cells and microgels. Both a robust keratocyte cell line and primary isolated keratocytes demonstrated cell-cell and cell-matrix mediated microgel assembly, ultimately leading to the formation of their own scaffold. The interconnected micropores and the dynamic behavior of the microgels enhanced cell migration and infiltration. By adjusting the microgel composition in terms of bioactivity and mechanical properties, we were able to fine-tune cell egress and tissue compaction. Cellular expression of tubulin β3 suggests that the primary keratocytes retain multipotent differentiation potential, while collagen type VI deposition and intracellular αSMA expression indicate a transition from keratocytes to fibroblasts. The combined effects of a fibrotic response, ECM deposition, and reduced microgel dynamics due to cell-mediated assembly are thought to increase matrix stiffness perceived by the cells. This assumption was supported by the significant upregulation of nuclear YAP activation during culture.

Towards the future, the small size of individual microgels and their dynamic bonding properties offer enhanced possibilities for injection. The supramolecular microgel system presented here has proven to be a promising tool for biomedical applications, particularly in minimally invasive therapies. Encapsulating cells in microgels provides protection during injection, and the retention time of the cells within the microgels can be controlled by altering the microgel composition.

Experimental section/methods

Microfluidic device production

Droplet-based microfluidics was used to generate single-cell laden supramolecular microgels, based on the published protocol of Sinha and coworkers who developed a pipette-tip based method for single cell encapsulation within droplets to enable cell isolation and perform single-cell analysis [24]. This system was adapted towards the formation of single-cell laden supramolecular microgels. Microfluidic devices were produced from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molds using soft lithography. Photomasks for soft photolithography were ordered from CAD/Art Services, Inc. (Bandon, Oregon). PDMS molds were produced by spin-coating wafers with SU-8 3000 photoresist (Micro Resist Technology) according to manufacturer's protocol to obtain 30 μm of channel height. Microfluidic devices were fabricated by mixing PDMS base and curing agent (Sylgard) at a ratio of 10:1 w/w%. After air bubble removing with vacuum, the mixture was poured onto a master silicon wafer containing the device layout and the mixture was cured at 65 °C for 3 h. After curing, the PDMS was carefully removed from the wafer and 1 mm holes were punched for the inlets and outlet. The PDMS was bonded channels-down to glass slides to yield closed microchannels via OH-terminated by exposure to plasma (Emitech K1050X), whereafter the devices were incubated at 65 °C for 1 h to increase binding. Finally, channels were treated with 5 v/v% silane in fluorinated HFE-7500 oil (Fluorchem UK, 3M Novec engineered fluid), incubated for 1 h at 65 °C, flushed with HFE-7500, and incubated overnight at 65 °C.

Human corneal keratocytes (HCK) cell culture

The human corneal keratocytes (HCK) were cultured under standard culture conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 4.5 g/L D-glucose, L-glutamine, 25 mM Hepes, 10 v/v% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Greiner Bio-one), 1 v/v% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Invitrogen). Cells were passaged at 80% confluency. Trypsin (1:250) with 1 mM EDTA (Invitrogen) was used to detach the cells from the culture flask and harvest them for experiments.

Primary keratocyte (PK) cell culture

Primary human keratocytes were isolated from leftover human corneoscleral transplant material from Descemet Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty surgery, which were obtained from the Cornea Department of the ETB-BISLIFE Multi-Tissue Center (Beverwijk, the Netherlands). The keratocytes were cultured in expansion medium at 37 °C, 21% O2 and 5% CO2 until ±80 % confluency. Since the medium contained FBS, keratocytes were considered to be activated matrix-producing cells, referred to as stromal fibroblasts (SFs). To initiate cell re-differentiation towards corneal stromal keratocytes, differentiation medium was used. Cells were cultured with medium changes every 3 days, keratocytes from multiple donors were used up to passage #3. TrypLE Express Enzyme (1x), no phenol red (12604013, Gibco) was used to detach the cells from the culture flask to use them for experiments. Table S1 shows the various media reagents to culture.

Preparation of hydrogelators

Pre-solutions of B- and M-type UPy-molecules were prepared separately (Table S2). M-type UPy-compound (UPy-Glycine + UPy-cRGD) was dissolved at twice the desired final concentration at 70 °C for 20 min under stirring conditions in an alkaline solution (i.e., 80 or 160 mM NaOH in PBS if the pre-solution was <5 w/v% or ≥ 5 w/v%, respectively). The resulting solution was neutralized by the addition of acid (i.e., 1 M HCl or 2 M HCl, respectively to the amount of base) and then diluted once with complete medium. The B-type molecule was dissolved at three times the desired end concentration at 70 °C in PBS for 1 h. Afterwards, both M- and B-type solutions were transferred from a glass vial to a sterile Eppendorf tube. From this step onwards a safety cabinet is used to guarantee a sterile work environment. The solutions were disinfected by exposing them to UV-light for 20 min. The cells needed for encapsulation were suspended in the correct amount of medium and this cell suspension was added to the B-type solution with a cell concentration of 4000 cells/μL. Thereby, the B-type molecules were diluted by the cell suspension to the desired end concentration.

Microgel formation

Single cell laden microgels based on supramolecular monomers were generated using a tip-loading approach as previously described in Sinha et al. [24] All the components needed were disinfected by exposing them to UV-light for 20 min. Supramolecular hydrogels pre-solutions of B- and M-type UPy-molecules with primary keratocytes or HKC cells were prepared separately as described previously. The pre-solutions were separately drawn into a 200 μL pipet tip and loaded according to Fig. S1 to the inlets in the PDMS chip. Syringes and tubing filled with mineral oil (Sigma Aldrich; M8410) were used as hydraulic system to dispense the liquids from the syringes driven by computer-controlled pumps (neMESYS microfluidic pump, Cetoni). HFE-7500 with 2.5 v/v% Pico-Surf (PS; Sphere Fluidics) was flushed into the oil inlet to form microgels. Microgel production was started with oil flow at a speed of 10 μL/min to prefill the channels of the microfluidic device. The flow of the hydrogel pre-solutions was started simultaneously at 5 μL/min. The flow of the oil was increased to 30 μL/min once the hydrogel pre-solutions started to mix at the droplet formation point. The microgels were collected in an Eppendorf tube from the outlet and medium was poured on top of the emulsion to prevent evaporation of the HFE oil. Single cell laden microgels were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C to allow gelation of the microgels. Thereafter, the microgels were isolated by demulsification with 20 v/v% 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl-triethoxysilane (PFO; Sigma) in HFE-7500 oil. Microgels were collected, counted with a hemocytometer and resuspended in fresh medium. Next, the fraction of the number of cells encapsulated in one microgel was determine. Mixtures of microgels with and without encapsulated cells were homogenously divided over the wells of a non-adhesive 96-well plate (Fisher Scientific, Nunclon Sphera-Treated, U-Shaped-Bottom plate 15227905) and additional cell culture medium to top of the well was added. The plates were placed in the incubator at 37 °C, 21% O2 and 5% CO2. After 3 days the cell culture medium which surrounds the microgels was refreshed. For the PKs treated towards CSKs, the expansion medium is replaced by the differentiation medium. During culture, medium was partly refreshed every 2–3 days.

Live imaging of primary keratocytes encapsulated in microgels

Time-lapse imaging of primary keratocytes encapsulated in 2.5 w/v% + 3 mM UPy-cRGD was performed in an incubator at 37 °C using a CytoSMART Lux3 FL (CytoSMART Technologies B.V., Eindhoven, The Netherlands). Images were acquired at 20x digital zoom every 5 min for 21 days in brightfield mode.

F NMR to check for fluorine residuals during cell culture

NMR measurements of either medium after 5 days of culture, or the discarded oil layer containing HFE with PFO and PS in HFE were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz (AVANCE III HD) at 25 °C (376 MHz for 19F NMR) (Fig. S8).

Cell fixation, staining and imaging

Before immunohistochemical stainings were carried out, the microgels were washed 3x with PBS (5 min per wash). All cells encapsulated within the microgels and tissues were fixated for 20 min at room temperature using 3.7 % paraformaldehyde (formalin 37% in PBS, 104033.1000, Merck). After washing with PBS, samples were permeabilized for 15 min with 0.5 % Triton X-100 in PBS. Followed by adding a blocking solution of 10 % donkey or goat serum in 0.05 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. Next, the cells were incubated with the primary antibodies diluted in 2% serum in 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS overnight at 4 °C. Thereafter, the cells were washed thoroughly with 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS, including wash waiting steps of 5–10 min. Next, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibodies and phalloidin at room temperature for 2 h. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI at a dilution of 1:250 for 10 min and washed thoroughly with PBS (including wash waiting steps of 5–10 min). During imaging, microgels were place on a thin coverslip (24 × 69 mm, VWR 631–1575) immerged in mowiol 4–88 (Sigma Aldrich, 81381) and imaged using Leica TCS SP8 X inverted confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems using HC PL APO CS2 objectives; 20×/0.75, 40x/0.95). Images were processed in ImageJ to create a max-projection image of the original z-stack. See Table S3 for the dyes, and primary- and secondary antibodies used within this study.

Cell image analysis

Brightfield images were analyzed using ImageJ Fiji to measure the area and circularity of HCK-encapsulated microgel compacted tissues. The other image analyses were analyzed from maximum-intensity z-projections of confocal image stacks. To determine the localization of YAP, z-projections of images were obtained from channels corresponding to the DAPI (nuclei), phalloidin (F-actin) and anti-YAP antibody staining (YAP). Afterwards CellProfiler™ (cell image analysis software) was used to design a pipeline which determined the nuclear areas and cytoplasmic areas of YAP. The YAP mean intensity was determined in those respective areas and the ratio between the intensities present in nuclear and cytoplasmic regions of the cell was then used as a measure of YAP nuclear translocation. The same pipeline was also used to determine the nuclear eccentricity.

3D height indication cell images

A custom MatLab script was written to pair a specific height of the cell within the obtained z-stack image to a specific color. Image stacks obtained from channels corresponding to the DAPI (nuclei) and phalloidin (F-actin) were used to create these 3D height indication cell images. LAS X Life Science Microscope Software was used to calculate the original μm distance in the z-direction from the original lif file.

Microgel injection

Fluorescently labeled microgels (2.5 w/v%) with a UPy-Cy3 dye (0.1 mM) were prepared as described previously [21]. To investigated if the microgels can be injected without any damage, they were suspended in PBS and loaded into a syringe with a 30-gauge needle. The mixture was then injected into a well of a glass-bottom chamberslide. As a control, microgels were pipetted directly into a well. The samples were imaged using a confocal microscope, and a pipeline was created in CellProfiler™ to measure the perimeter and eccentricity of the microgels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software, with all data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the microgel diameter experiment, normality was assessed using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Since these conditions did not follow a normal distribution, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to identify statistically significant differences between groups. For the HCK tissue area and circularity, normality was assed using a Shapiro Wilk test. An ordinary one-way ANOVA was performed with a Tukey's multiple comparison test for the circularity data and an unpaired t-test for the area data. For the YAP expression in HCK cells, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. For the YAP expression in PK, a Kruskal-Wallis test was performed with an additional Dunn's multiple comparisons test. For the microgel injection, a Shapiro Wilk test was used to assess normality, and thereafter a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used.
