Sultams as Dual Inhibitors of Human Carbonic Anhydrase and Thioredoxin Reductase
Authors: Aleksandrs Pustenko, Raitis Bobrovs, Venkatesan Saravanan, Kathir Avan, Simone Carradori, Antons Sizovs, Andrea Angeli, Claudiu T. Supuran, Raivis Žalubovskis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality, often complicated by drug resistance arising from single‐target therapies. Dual inhibitors that simultaneously target multiple cancer‐related defense mechanisms offer a promising strategy to overcome these limitations. Herein, we report the design, synthesis, and biological evaluation of a series of sultam‐based N‐acyl derivatives (8a–k and 9a–k) as potential dual inhibitors of thioredoxin reductase (TrxR1) and tumor‐associated carbonic anhydrases (CA IX and XII). TrxR1 screening identified a few active compounds, with inhibitory potency strongly influenced by the type and orientation of substituents. CA inhibition assays revealed pronounced selectivity for CA IX/XII, while off‐target isoforms CA I and CA II remained unaffected. These findings highlight the potential of sultam derivatives as dual‐targeting anticancer agents and provide a foundation for further optimization toward selective, multi‐pathway cancer therapy.

Introduction

Diseases caused by cancer are complex, life‐threatening and one of the main factors in reducing the average life expectancy. The global number of new cancer cases and mortality from cancer increases every year. This indicates a strong need to develop new medications for cancer. An innovative approach is to use of dual inhibitors [1]. A single inhibitor can inhibit a specific target, e.g., attack cancer cells, yet exclude the normal cells. However, clinics face the problem that repeated administration of the drug often leads to drug resistance in cancer patients [2]. This occurs due to the activation of alternative receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) pathways. RTKs not only regulate normal cell processes but also play a critical role in cancer development [3].

To address this, clinics use multidrug therapy. However, this approach may lead to drug–drug interactions and dose‐limiting toxicities [2]. Therefore, a single drug with the ability to target two targets simultaneously (a dual inhibitor) serves as an effective strategy in cancer treatment [4, 5].

Enzymes thioredoxin reductase (TrxR, EC 1.8.1.9) and carbonic anhydrase (CA, EC 4.2.1.1) are defined as anticancer drug targets [6, 7]. Currently, many TrxR inhibitors are in preclinical or early‐stage clinical trials, demonstrating that TrxR inhibition is a major direction in anticancer research [8] and one (SLC‐0111 [9]) CA inhibitor in clinical trials.

Combining dual targeting of TrxR and cancer‐related CA isoforms (CA IX and CA XII) represents a powerful anticancer strategy by simultaneously disrupting two fundamental defense mechanisms in tumor cells—protection against reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pH regulation [10].

Human TrxR is a selenocysteine‐containing enzyme responsible for maintaining redox homeostasis and oxygen homeostasis and preventing oxidative stress [11, 13]. Three isoforms of TrxR have been identified so far: cytoplasmic and nuclear protein (TrxR1), mitochondrial protein (TrxR2), and testis‐specific protein (TrxR3), of which TrxR1 is the best characterized and widely expressed in many different tissues [14, 15]. Cancer cells naturally express several‐fold increased TrxR levels compared to normal cells due to their uncontrolled proliferation and intense metabolism [6, 16, 17]. Overexpression of TrxR and Trx (thioredoxin) contributes to cancer progression through either the promotion of cell growth or the inhibition of cell death. High levels of Trx are also associated with greater invasive and metastatic potential [17, 18]. Also, TrxR is upregulated in cancer cells as a major defense mechanism against oxidative stress, tumor necrosis, and the respiratory burst of immune cells [19]. Additionally, increased levels of Trx are related to the development of resistance to various chemotherapeutic agents, including cisplatin and docetaxel [20, 21].

Recent studies on thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) inhibition have focused on natural products and their synthetic derivatives as potential TrxR inhibitors [22, 25]. These compounds generally interact with TrxR through noncovalent binding, thereby minimizing potential off‐target toxicity compared with covalent inhibitors. Moreover, many of these molecules exhibit a high degree of selectivity toward TrxR, making them promising candidates for the development of targeted anticancer therapies.

As for the carbonic anhydrases, they are ubiquitous zinc‐containing metalloenzymes that catalyze the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide and regulate pH in virtually all living organisms [26].

Today, at least eight genetic families of CA are known, of which α‐CA are the most widely studied class because they are found in mammals [27]. There are 15 known isoforms of human α‐CA; among them, the membrane‐bound CA IX and XII isoform expression is limited in normal tissues (noncancerous). However, CA IX and CA XII are overexpressed in many solid tumors under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, patients with this type of tumors have a poor prognosis due to resistance to chemo‐ and radiotherapy [28, 29]. It has been shown that CA IX inhibitors have strong anticancer potency in the case of metastatic breast cancer [13]. CA IX and XII play a major role in regulating the extracellular pH in tumor cells and have been shown to be abundantly expressed in many types of advanced solid metastatic tumors located in a variety of organs (kidneys, lung, breast, colon, liver, prostate, melanoma, etc.) [30].

Results and Discussion

The concept of dual inhibition of TrxR and CA is known in literature. Previously, it has been successfully validated with sulfocoumarin [31] and coumarin prodrugs [32, 33]; these chemotypes often face challenges regarding isoform selectivity among the carbon anhydrases [34, 35]. To address this limitation and break away from established core structures, we introduce a novel series based on unsaturated cyclic sulfonamides (sultams), specifically 2,3‐dihydroisothiazole 1,1‐dioxide (1) and 3,4‐dihydro‐2H‐1,2‐thiazine 1,1‐dioxide (2) frameworks as potential dual inhibitors of TrxR and CA. Historically, sultams have been explored as antibacterial agents, but more recently, they have been explored as potential anticancer, anti‐inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antiviral agents [36]. Notably, sultams exhibit certain CA inhibition activities [37].

Distinct from traditional scaffolds, this structural design eliminates the primary sulfonamide group—frequently associated with off‐target liabilities—and features an embedded (α,β)‐unsaturated bond system. Remarkably, this structural modification shifts the inhibition profile entirely away from the off‐target cytosolic isoforms hCA I and hCA II, yielding selective inhibitors of the tumor‐associated hCA IX and hCA XII isoforms. Furthermore, the rigid cyclic sulfonamide core presents a well‐positioned electrophilic center that serves as a potential Michael acceptor for targeting the accessible C‐terminal active site of TrxR.

Sultams 1 and 2 contain a Michael acceptor moiety (Figure 1 colored blue), and studies show that compounds with a Michael acceptor moiety can be used as potential anticancer agents due to their ability to bind to nucleophilic sites in biological molecules, thereby disrupting cancer cell function and inducing apoptosis [38]. The Michael acceptor group is desirable in the TrxR inhibition [10, 31, 32, 39]. As for CA inhibition motif (Figure 1 colored green), secondary and tertiary sulfonamides may bind at the entrance of the active site, thus ensuring CA inhibition and good isoform selectivity [40].

TrxR1 Inhibitory Activity

The inhibitory effects of the target compounds 8a–k and 9a–k were examined using a 5,5′‐dithiol‐bis‐2‐nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) reduction assay [41, 42]. First, the initial screening of TrxR1 inhibitors was performed at 200 μM concentration by incubation with DTNB, recombinant rat TrxR1 (rTrxR1), and NADPH (Table 1). Based on the inhibitory potential of sultam derivatives that showed more than 50% TrxR1 inhibition rate at 200 μM concentration, we then determined their IC50 values. The derivatives that failed to reach 50% inhibition benchmark were excluded from further evaluation due to insufficient inhibition potential.

The structure–activity relationship (SAR) evaluation revealed that most of the synthesized derivatives exhibited no significant inhibitory activity, with inhibition values generally below 50% at 200 μM; however, we managed to identify potent compounds. It must be noted that highly potent TrxR inhibitors are not desirable because they are causing excessive and irreversible oxidative stress (ROS buildup) that leads to severe cytotoxicity, potential off‐target effects, and high toxicity to healthy cells, not just cancer cells [42, 45].

In general, there is no significant difference between sultam 8 and sultam 9 derivatives. Among the sultam 8 derivatives, compound 8b, bearing a para‐fluoro substituent, and compound 8g, containing a meta‐bromo substituent, exhibited measurable TrxR inhibitory activity. Notably, other para‐substituted analogues within the series did not demonstrate TrxR inhibition. Likewise, derivatives incorporating alternative halogens (8e and 8f), electron‐donating (8d), or electron‐withdrawing groups (8c) were inactive. Furthermore, heteroaryl‐substituted analogue 8i, as well as the alkyl‐substituted derivatives 8j and 8k, showed no detectable TrxR inhibitory activity. In the case of sultam 9 derivatives, we can observe a similar pattern as in the case of sultam 8 derivatives, heteroaryl‐substituted analogue 9i, as well as the alkyl‐substituted derivatives 9j and 9k, showed no detectable TrxR inhibitory activity. Among the sultam 9 derivatives, compound 9c, bearing a para‐nitro substituent, and compound 9g, containing a meta‐bromo substituent, exhibited measurable TrxR inhibitory activity. Other para‐substituted analogues within the series did not demonstrate TrxR inhibition; derivatives incorporating halogens (9b, 8e, and 8f) and electron‐donating (8d) groups were inactive. Notably, compounds 8g and 9g emerged as the most active analogues within their respective series, implying that their specific substitution patterns enhance favorable interactions within the enzyme's active site. However, compounds 8h and 9h, which possess substituents at the same position, did not reach the inhibition threshold. This suggests that both substituent electronic nature and location in aromatic ring are crucial for effective TrxR inhibition. Collectively, these findings suggest that rational optimization of substituent type and position could be an effective strategy to enhance the efficacy and selectivity of TrxR inhibitors.

The inhibition profile of the synthesized compounds against four human carbonic anhydrase isoforms (hCA I, II, IX, and XII) revealed a clear pattern of isoform selectivity. All derivatives showed no inhibitory activity toward the off‐target CA isoforms CA I and CA II (K i > 100 μM), while exhibiting notable inhibition profiles against cancer‐associated isoforms CA IX and CA XII. In cancer treatment involving CA inhibitors, this characteristic is advantageous for minimizing potential side effects, as the CA I and CA II isoforms are widely distributed across various tissues in the body [45, 47].

All synthesized compounds 1–2 and 8–9 exhibited inhibitory activity against tumor‐associated CA IX isoform, with K i in the micromolar range. Among the sultam 8 derivatives, 8e emerged as the most potent inhibitor (K i = 5.0 μM), whereas 9c was the most active member of the sultam 9 series (K i = 3.3 μM). Regardless of whether they are sultam 8 or 9 derivatives, the CA IX inhibitory activities are similar. No clear SAR was evident with respect to the electronic characteristics or positional variation of aryl substituents, as these modifications did not significantly alter CA IX inhibition. Similarly, replacement of the aromatic ring with heteroaryl or alkyl substituents did not produce measurable changes in inhibitory activity.

As for the second cancer‐related CA isoform CA XII, compounds have a different inhibition profile compared to that of CA IX. Compounds 1–2 and sultam derivatives 8b, 8c, 8d, 8j, 8k, and 9j were inactive against CA XII (K i > 100 μM). Interestingly, among the para‐substituted analogues, compound 8b bearing a fluorine substituent was inactive, whereas 8e (para‐chloro) and 8f (para‐iodo) exhibited activity. Compound 8d, containing a methoxy group in the para position, is not active, while 8h, containing a methoxy group in the meta position, is active. These findings suggest that the position of the substituent on the benzene ring influences biological activity. In contrast, the alkyl‐substituted derivatives 8j and 8k displayed no activity. As for the sultam 9 derivatives, only the alkyl analogue 9j was inactive; all other derivatives demonstrated comparable activity regardless of the electronic properties or positional variation of the substituents. Notably, the thiophene‐containing derivatives 8i and 9i were the most active members of their respective series.

The difference in the CA IX and CA XII inhibition could be related to the variations in the amino acid composition of the “selective pocket” located near the active site, resulting in a more sterically restricted environment in CA XII [46, 48].

Several examples of secondary cyclic sulfonamides have been reported in the literature as carbonic anhydrase (CA) inhibitors. Analogues of the antiepileptic drug sulthiame have been reported to exhibit potent carbonic anhydrase (CA) inhibitory activity in the nanomolar range [47, 49]. However, despite their high potency, these compounds generally lack isoform selectivity, inhibiting off‐target CA isoforms with comparable efficacy to cancer‐associated isoforms. Nevertheless, a greater degree of selectivity has been observed toward the CA VII isoform, suggesting potential applications beyond cancer‐related targets. In contrast, Saccharin‐derived compounds [37, 50] have demonstrated potent and selective inhibition of cancer‐associated CA isoforms. Similar to the sultams described in this study, these compounds exhibit high isoform selectivity and show minimal inhibitory activity against off‐target CA isoforms.

Because the synthesized sultam derivatives contain a Michael acceptor fragment, preliminary cytotoxicity studies were performed for the selected active compounds 8g and 9g in a panel of human cancer cell lines. Compound 8g showed moderate micromolar cytotoxicity (Table 3). The data suggest that the compounds do not exhibit highly potent nonspecific cytotoxicity under the tested conditions. Together with the clear SAR observed in the TrxR1 assay and the lack of inhibition of hCA I and hCA II, these results do not support indiscriminate promiscuous activity as the sole explanation for the observed biological profile. Nevertheless, selectivity over nonmalignant human cells has not yet been established and will be addressed in future optimization studies.

Computational Studies

To better understand lead compound 8g and 9g binding with the enzymes, computational studies were performed.

TrxR Computional Studies

The expected binding mode of selected compound 9g was estimated using molecular docking. TrxR1 contains two spatially separated redox centers—one positioned deep within the N‐terminal region of the enzyme (Cys59 and Cys64) and the other residing on a flexible, highly accessible C‐terminal arm (Cys497 and Sec498). Structural analysis of the human TrxR1–Trx1 complex indicates that redox‐driven structural changes are localized primarily to the mobile C‐terminal arm [48, 51]. Consequently, this arm mediates electron transfer to the substrate through the formation of an intermolecular disulfide bond between Sec498 of TrxR1 and Cys32 of Trx1. Because the electron transfer process requires multiple conformational states of the C‐terminal arm, molecular docking was performed using a structural model in the “reduced waiting position,” in which Cys497 and Sec498 are in their reduced form (PDB: 3EAN). This conformation is thought to represent the substrate‐binding state. The predicted binding of the compounds 8g and 9g is depicted in Figure 2.

Docked poses of compounds 8g and 9g in complex with rat TrxR1 (PDB ID: 3EAN). Inhibitor and key amino acid residues are shown as sticks. The teal cartoon indicates the flexible C‐terminal arm. Yellow and blue dashed lines indicate hydrogen and halogen bonding, respectively. Nonpolar hydrogens are omitted for clarity.

Compounds 8g and 9g are expected to form a covalent bond with the catalytic Sec498 residue via a Michael addition. The compounds bind under the flexible C‐terminal arm, and the calculated binding energies of the top‐scoring poses are 3.48 and −3.21 kcal/mol for 8g and 9g, respectively. The top‐scoring binding modes for both compounds are the same, with the sulfonyl group hydrogen bonding to the Lys29 side chain and the bromphenyl group binding in a predominantly hydrophobic subpocket. The bromine group is forming halogen bonds with Ser483 and Val484 main chain amide groups.

CA Computional Studies

Molecular docking studies were performed to better understand the binding affinity and binding modes of the synthesized compounds against carbonic anhydrase IX and XII isoforms; only the top binding posture and affinities of 32 conformers per ligand were discussed. Out of the five ligand compounds, 8g was found to be the best candidate with the best affinity toward both isozymes; for hCA IX −7.4 kcal/mol, because of its optimal fit near the catalytic domain, the sulfonamide moiety likely forms a secondary interactions with zinc ion (Zn301) at the active‐site entrance and strong hydrogen bond with Thr199, and the tail fits deep into the hydrophobic pockets, forming Van der Walls interactions with Leu91 and Val121. In contrast to hCA IX, hCA XII has a lower binding affinity (−6.9 kcal/mol), but the contacts are robust and intact, with identical coordination connections with Zn901 and Thr200. Predicted binding conformations of compound 8g within the active site of hCA IX (top right) and within the active site of hCA XII (bottom right).

To further investigate the experimentally observed selectivity toward tumor‐associated carbonic anhydrase isoforms, compounds 8d and 9j, which exhibited potent inhibition of hCA IX (K i = 5.0 and 3.4 μM, respectively) but no measurable inhibition of hCA II (K i > 100 μM), were docked into the active site of hCA II and compared with their corresponding binding modes in hCA IX. In hCA IX, compound 8d established hydrogen bond interactions with Thr200 and occupied the hydrophobic pocket lined by residues Leu141, Val121, Val143, Leu198, and Trp209. Similarly, compound 9j adopted a favorable pose within the hCA IX cavity, forming a hydrogen bond with Asn67 while being stabilized by extensive hydrophobic contacts. In contrast, both compounds displayed less favorable binding modes in hCA II, remaining closer to the entrance of the active‐site cavity and forming only limited interactions, mainly involving Gln92 and water‐mediated contacts. Furthermore, neither compound established productive interactions with the catalytic zinc‐containing region. The reduced interaction network observed in hCA II provides a structural explanation for the experimentally observed lack of inhibition of this cytosolic isoform, while the more extensive interaction pattern in hCA IX is consistent with their selective inhibitory activity.

To examine the stability and dynamic behavior of 8g in both isoforms of hCA IX and hCA XII, the complex produced by the docking experiments is exported separately and submitted to molecular dynamics simulations. The complex 8g–hCA IX reached equilibrium early in the trajectory, as demonstrated by the root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis of Figure 4A. The protein backbone fluctuations stabilized between 1.8 and 2.4 Å, and the ligand RMSD remained remarkably consistent, indicating that the compound remained firmly attached to the active‐site binding pockets. Residues such as His94, His96, and Thr199 showed very low RMSF, confirming that the binding interactions predicted during the docking were maintained throughout the dynamic simulation. The root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) in Figure 4B further confirm the overall stability of the active site. The fluctuations below 1.0 Å, which is comparable to RMSD, confirm a rigid and stable binding environment. The residue contacts that led to this stability are summarized by the protein–ligand contact histogram and 2D interactions displayed in Figure 4C,D. Because of the regular ionic interactions which involve zinc (Zn301), His94, His96, and His119 with 100% occupancy, stability is highly obtained in the case of complex 8g–hCA IX.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a series of sultam‐based N‐acyl derivatives (8a–k and 9a–k) were designed, synthesized, and evaluated as potential dual inhibitors of TrxR and CA. TrxR1 screening identified some active compounds, notably 8b, 8g, 9c, and 9g. CA inhibition assays revealed pronounced selectivity toward tumor‐associated isoforms CA IX and CA XII, while off‐target isoforms CA I and CA II were unaffected. Compared to the reference inhibitor AAZ, which inhibits all isoforms in the low nanomolar range, the synthesized derivatives display pronounced selectivity for tumor‐associated CA isoforms, highlighting their potential as selective CA IX/XII inhibitors. SAR analysis highlighted that both electronic properties and substitution patterns play an important role in modulating activity and isoform selectivity.

Computational studies provided mechanistic insights into the binding behavior of compounds 8g and 9g against TrxR1 and the tumor‐associated CA isoforms. Docking results suggest that 8g and 9g can effectively occupy the TrxR1 active site, with a predicted covalent interaction at Sec498 supported by hydrogen and halogen bonding interactions. Among the evaluated compounds, 8g demonstrated the most favorable binding affinities toward hCA IX and hCA XII, driven by stable hydrogen bonding networks. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the structural stability of the 8g–hCA IX complex over 100 ns, with maintained catalytic‐site interactions and minimal backbone fluctuations. Collectively, these findings identify 8g as a promising dual‐inhibitor candidate.

Overall, we tend to think that the disruption of two cancer defense mechanisms (pH regulation and protection against oxidative stress) is a promising strategy for cancer treatment. These findings provide a strong foundation for the further optimization of sultam derivatives as selective anticancer agents and support the continued development of dual TrxR/CA inhibitors for targeted cancer therapy.
